## 14. Sela Descends

I had been a warden for nineteen years, and in all that time I had operated on exactly one article of faith: magic is a fire that pretends to be a hearth. It warms you until it doesn't. Every arrest I'd made, every writ I'd signed, every night I'd spent walking Greyhollow's walls listening for the hum of forbidden things—that faith was the spine of me. You can break a person's back and they'll crawl. Break the spine of what they believe, and they don't even know which direction the floor is.

Here's how it broke.

It started, as most of my disasters do, with paperwork. Prophecy-traffic through the black-market dealers had tripled in a month—not scattered purchases but a pattern, a corridor of banned books moving through the city like blood through a vein, all of it flowing toward one destination. The mountain.

We don't have a mountain. That's what I would have said, three months ago. We have the Grey Spine, a ridge of dead rock north of the city that every survey, every expedition, every warden's map agreed was solid granite all the way down. No caves. No gates. Certainly no courts. Greyhollow was founded on the principle that there was nothing under the world worth worshipping or fearing, and our whole legal architecture—every anti-magic statute, every exile protocol—was built on that foundation stone.

The foundation stone was lying to us.

* * *

I stood at the mouth of the gate at dawn with forty wardens behind me and a stolen ledger open in my hands, and I will admit—I laughed. Not because anything was funny. Because the gate was *carved*, and the carving was older than the city, older than the statutes, and someone had gone to enormous trouble to make sure we never found it. There were wards on the approach road, subtle ones, woven so that any warden walking this path would feel a sudden conviction that they'd forgotten something at home. A wife. A kettle. A pressing appointment. I'd walked this road twice before in my career. Both times I'd turned back, certain I'd left my stove lit.

Forty years of my life, and I'd been turned around twice by the magical equivalent of a note pinned to a door saying *nothing to see here.*

"Chief?" Vess, my second, at my shoulder. She's twenty-six, sharp as a paper cut, and she'd followed me into worse than this already. "Say the word and we go home."

"The word," I said, "is *down*."

The gate didn't resist us. That should have been my first warning. Doors that resist you want something from you; doors that open smoothly are doors you've been *expected* at. We descended through galleries of pale stone shot through with veins of light—not torchlight, not glowstone, but something organic, pulsing slow, like the mountain was breathing through its bones. Bone-light, the old smugglers' stories called it, and the smugglers, it turned out, had been the only honest historians in Greyhollow.

Vess kept count of the galleries aloud, her voice steady in the way of people who need it to be. "Twelve. Thirteen." At the fourteenth gallery we saw them: lanterns. Hundreds of them, thousands, hanging in the air without hooks or chains, each one flickering with a light that wasn't quite light. And each one, when you got close enough, had a face.

I have seen souls before. Not many—a storm-caller's execution, once, when the condemned woman's power tore loose of her body and stood screaming in the square for eleven seconds before the suppression teams burned it out. Eleven seconds, and I still hear it some nights. Here there were thousands, and they weren't screaming. They were hanging. Organized. Catalogued.

They were *filed.*

"Chief," Vess whispered. "These are people."

"These are evidence," I said, and heard how hollow it sounded, and hated myself for reaching for it. Nineteen years of doctrine dies hard. It dies ugly, too.

* * *

By the twentieth gallery my certainty had stopped bleeding and started hemorrhaging. The evidence kept arriving whether I wanted it or not. Warden records—the ones from the purge, the Night of Broken Thunder, the great righteous cleansing that built my career—were quoted in the Registry's own documents, verbatim, with annotations. Our exile protocols hadn't been designed by Greyhollow's founders out of wisdom. They'd been *drafted.* Supplied. Handed down to us complete, like furniture from a catalog, by clerks who wanted a steady harvest of souls above ground and knew better than to come collect them personally.

There was a memo. An actual memo, in a clerk's immaculate hand, three hundred years old, discussing the efficacy of "the Greyhollow arrangement" and recommending its renewal. It used the phrase *self-policing stock.* I read it four times. My hands didn't shake, which surprised me. I think somewhere around gallery seventeen I'd passed clean through shaking into a cold, level clarity that felt less like composure than like being newly born with no idea what anything was.

Because here was the arithmetic that undid me: I had spent my career protecting Greyhollow from fire. And the entire time, the institution I served had been running a farm.

"Chief Investigator Sela."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, dry as ledger pages turning. The corridor ahead filled—not with soldiers, thank whatever gods the Registry also pretended not to believe in, but with clerks. Pale figures in grey robes, faces smooth as unscribed vellum, eyes like ink waiting to be written with. The one who'd spoken carried a ledger identical in binding to mine. Stolen property, technically. Mine was the copy; theirs was the original; and looking at it I understood with perfect clarity that everything I had ever confiscated, every banned book burned in Greyhollow's squares, had been a cull of their inventory. We weren't suppressing prophecy. We were managing circulation.

"You're trespassing," the clerk observed, without heat.

"So are you," I said. "You're trespassing on my whole life. I'd like it back."

A pause. If vellum could blink, that was a blink.

"You are Chief Investigator Sela Mandrel. Author of the Third Purge Protocols. Your conviction index is exemplary." The clerk tilted its head, studying me the way I'd studied a thousand suspects. "And yet you came armed only with questions. Curious. The last warden delegation arrived armed with fire."

"That was two hundred years ago. What did your people do with them?"

"They were filed."

Vess made a small sound beside me. Forty wardens behind us, and I could feel all of them doing the same math at once: *we are inside the thing we don't believe in, and it has receipts.*

"I'll make you an offer," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "I'm going to walk these halls. I'm going to see what you do here. And then I'm going to decide whether Greyhollow's treaty with you—written or unwritten, known or unknown—survives contact with daylight. You can help me or you can file me. Those are the options."

Another pause. Then the clerk did something I will think about for the rest of my life, however long the Registry allows it to be: it smiled. Thinly. Like a crack in porcelain.

"Curious," it repeated. "Come, then. But know that what you find will require you to choose, and the Registry has observed that investigators choose poorly. Attachment," it added, as though citing precedent, "is a documented inefficiency."

"Then document this," I said. "Attachment is the only reason anyone does anything worth the ink."

* * *

They took us deeper. Of course they did. And somewhere around the thirtieth gallery I stopped being an investigator and became something rawer—a witness.

We saw the vaults where memories hang like cured hides. We saw a chamber where a debt was being renegotiated over a soul that flickered like a guttering lamp, and the soul had a young man's face, and the clerk handling him consulted his terms the way a butcher consults a cut sheet. Vess vomited in a corner. I didn't. I want credit for that. I wanted to, badly.

And then, in a gallery hung with storm-light—actual storm-light, the blue-white blaze I'd spent nineteen years hunting—we found the record of the Night of Broken Thunder. The true record. Not the purge version we taught recruits, the one where a rogue storm-caller nearly destroyed Greyhollow out of malice or madness.

The truth was smaller and infinitely worse. A storm-caller had saved the city. Called down a tempest that should have shattered her and didn't, because she paid for it—with her soul, sold on the spot to spare the city reprisal, her memory cut away so she could survive the loss without drowning. The Registry's annotation in the margin, in that same immaculate hand, read: *Specimen of note. Prophecy-relevant. Retain.*

Prophecy-relevant. Astrid Whitlock. The charm-seller from the night market whose trail I'd been following for a month, the fugitive whose arrest warrant sat in my satchel at that very moment, three floors of mountain above my head.

She hadn't fled justice. She'd been *delivered* to it. Her whole exile—my whole pursuit—was a delivery route.

"Chief," Vess said quietly. She'd recovered, mostly, and had that look she gets when she's about to say something insubordinate and correct. "The warrant. If any of this is true—"

"It's true," I said. "All of it. That's the problem. I built my life on a lie so well-constructed it had *footnotes.*"

I looked down the long bone-lit corridor, toward whatever heart this place had, toward the eclipse that every smuggler's whisper said was coming, toward the two figures the prophecies kept insisting would walk into this mountain together. One leaves alone, the books said. For a century the Registry had arranged the world to make that sentence come true, and now the sentence was due, and the instruments of its fulfillment included a stolen soul, a charming stranger I'd have arrested on sight six months ago, and me.

Me. Nineteen years of faithful service, and my reward was discovering I'd been a sheepdog guarding a slaughterhouse, proud of how well I kept the flock contained.

"Chief Investigator." The lead clerk had returned, gliding, patient. "The eclipse approaches. Terms will soon be enforced. You may withdraw with your wardens unharmed—the Registry honors its arrangements, even with those ignorant of them. Or you may proceed downward and become involved. We caution you: involvement is irreversible."

Down below us, faint through miles of stone, I heard thunder. Real thunder, caged and furious, the sound of a storm-caller deciding something. And beneath the thunder, almost too soft to catch, another sound—stone breaking, a whistle cracking, someone choosing.

Nineteen years, and I finally recognized the feeling in my chest. It wasn't doubt. Doubt I knew. This was its opposite: the sudden, total absence of it.

"We're proceeding," I told the clerk, and drew my warden's blade—not to fight, I suspected it would be useless for fighting—but because Greyhollow steel was forged above ground, under an open sky, and I wanted something of the sky in my hand where I was going. "And when this is over, Investigator Vess is going to start a list of every arrangement your Registry has ever made with my city. Bring your ledgers. You won't be needing them for much longer."

The clerk regarded me for a long moment, ink-dark eyes unreadable.

"Noted," it said. "Filed under: complication."

From below, the thunder swelled, and the mountain's bone-light began to stutter—and far off in the dark, the first freed soul rose past us like weather given wings, and I swear on every statute I ever enforced, it looked *happy.*
