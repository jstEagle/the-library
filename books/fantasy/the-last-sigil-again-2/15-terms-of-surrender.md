## 15. Terms of Surrender

The Pale Registry did not have chairs.

I noticed this about four minutes into what was unmistakably a negotiation, standing on cold bone-flagstone in a chamber lit by the soft glow of my own stolen years, while three clerks in robes the color of unwritten paper regarded me with the patience of people who had outlasted empires and intended to outlast me too. No chairs. No table. Nothing to lean against. I understood then that this was deliberate. The Undercourt wanted you uncomfortable. Discomfort made people agree to things.

"Restate your understanding of the debt," said the middle clerk. They all looked alike—tall, grey-eyed, faces like unfinished portraits—and they all spoke with the same unhurried cadence, as though words were currency being counted out one at a time.

"You hold my soul," I said. "Collateral against a debt incurred three years ago. Payment of which bought Greyhollow its continued existence. The contract expires at the coming eclipse."

"Correct." The clerk turned a page in a ledger so vast it required a second clerk to steady it. "And you are aware that the bearer of the collection sigil has been identified."

"I'm aware Wren's been having a very bad day."

Something flickered across the clerk's face—not amusement, nothing so warm as that, but perhaps its fossil. "The heir Wren Vale, styled Heir of the Deep, raised above ground for the sole purpose of locating and delivering the soul-bearer. Yes. That individual."

*Individual.* As if Wren were a shipment. As if the date at Marrow's noodle stall, the terrible poetry, the way Wren laughed with their whole head tipped back—all of it were line items.

I kept my voice level. Three years of selling luck-knots to tourists had taught me how to smile through anything; apparently it also worked in reverse. "And here's where you make your offer, because clerks don't summon debtors for status updates."

The lead clerk—the one whose ledger I'd been staring at—closed it with a sound like a coffin lid deciding to be optimistic. "Two terms are available to you, Astrid Whitlock. We will state them plainly, because the Registry does not deal in riddles. Riddles are for prophecy."

That got a breath of a laugh out of me. Even now. Especially now.

"The first term: your soul is restored to you, whole, tonight. The sigil binding it is dissolved by our own hand, cleanly, without storm or damage. In exchange, the memories we return with it will exclude entirely the person Wren Vale. Every hour. Every touch. The name itself will mean nothing to you. You will walk out of this mountain free, whole, and unburdened, and you will never know what you paid to forget."

The chamber was very quiet. Somewhere far off, water dripped onto stone with the regularity of a metronome counting down something.

"And the second term," I said, because my mouth had apparently decided to keep functioning without consulting the rest of me.

"The second term: you retain everything. Every memory, including those restored from the vault—including him. You live them fully until the eclipse. And at the eclipse, the sigil completes, and your soul is collected in full. You remain here." A pause, delicately weighted. "Alive, if it comforts you. The Registry has no interest in cruelty. Only in payment."

"There it is," I said softly. "Forget them or lose yourself. Those are the options."

"Those are the *terms*. Options imply preference on our part. We have none."

"Lucky you."

I thought about Wren. That was the trap, of course—I could think of nothing else. I stood in a hall built of other people's sold pasts and all I could see was a night market and a stranger buying a charm neither of us needed, saying *for luck, obviously,* with a crooked smile, and me thinking: *oh no.* That first oh-no of attraction, the small fatal click of a lock turning. I'd felt it before a dinner date, before a prophecy book, before any of this. Love had gotten to me first and told me none of the facts.

If I took the first term, some future Astrid would walk out of this mountain light as a cloud, and somewhere above, a person with storm-grey eyes would wait for someone who would never come, and never know why. Wren would remember everything. Wren would carry both halves of us.

If I took the second term, I'd get to keep every single minute—but the minutes would end at eclipse, and then the Undercourt would own the only part of me that mattered, forever, and Wren would have delivered me exactly as scripted, exactly as the last page of that cursed book demanded. One enters the mountain. One leaves alone.

Both roads ran through Wren's grief. The Registry had designed them that way.

"You've done this before," I said. "Offered these choices. To others."

"We offer terms according to contract," the clerk said, which was not an answer, which was an answer.

"How many took the forgetting?"

The three clerks exchanged glances—or rather, failed to, their faces too smooth for it, but something passed between them anyway. "Sufficient numbers," the lead one said finally. "Grief is heavy, Astrid Whitlock. Most souls set it down when offered the chance."

"And how many chose to keep everything?" I asked. "How many walked out of here whole?"

The silence stretched long enough that the dripping water got bored of it.

"That number," the clerk said, "is zero."

* * *

They left me alone after that. Alone, in a niche gallery off the audience chamber, guarded by nothing visible—which in the Undercourt meant guarded by everything invisible. The walls here were veined with pale light, and pressed close I could hear them: souls in storage, murmuring. Not screaming. Murmuring. Like a city heard through a wall, like rain on a roof over strangers. Sold people. Three years ago I'd been one of the items on these shelves, and I still didn't know what I'd bought with the price.

I sat down against the wall—it was warm, faintly, body-warm, and I hated that I noticed—and put my head in my hands.

*Take the deal. Take the first deal.*

The thought arrived in Wren's voice, and that was the worst part, because I knew—they knew—that's what they'd say. Wren Vale, who had spent their entire manufactured life following orders, would stand in front of me with tears they'd deny later and say *take the forgetting, Astrid, please, live, forget me, it's the better trade,* and they would believe it, because duty was the only scripture they'd ever been given and self-erasure read to them like mercy.

And I would refuse. Because I was, as established repeatedly and catastrophically, me.

Footsteps. Soft ones, hesitant ones—Wren's, I knew their walk now, the way they moved like someone perpetually apologizing to the floor. I looked up.

They stood at the mouth of the gallery in court clothes someone else had dressed them in—deep blue, formal, wrong, like a bird stuffed and mounted—and they looked worse than the clothes. Their eyes were red-rimmed and furious about it. Behind them, at a distance, two Registry sentinels made of folded shadow waited with the patience of furniture.

"They're making you enforce it," I said. It wasn't a question.

"At eclipse." Wren's voice came out scraped. "The heir completes the collection. That's—" A breath. "That's the whole reason I exist, apparently. They raised me for it. Planted me in Greyhollow, gave me the whistle, gave me the mission, gave me—" They gestured at me, at the space between us, and their hand shook. "You were the assignment. Do you understand? Everything. The market, the charm, the dinner. All of it was written before I ever saw your face."

"I know." I'd had hours with that truth now, and it still went through me like cold water, but less cold than it would have yesterday. "And?"

Wren blinked. "And—what?"

"And yet here you are, off-script, in a hallway, instead of in the chamber rehearsing your big collection speech. So. And?"

Their jaw worked. "I asked them to let you take the forgetting. I offered—" A laugh with no humor in it whatsoever. "I offered to take extra term. More service. Anything. They said the contract doesn't renegotiate. They said the heir doesn't ask. They said—" Wren stopped. Pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes. When they spoke again, the words came quiet and level, which from Wren was more frightening than shouting. "They said I was made for this. Made, Astrid. Like the charms you used to sell. Something crafted to look real."

I crossed the gallery and took their hand off their face and held it. Their fingers were cold. Mountain-cold. Court-cold.

"Listen to me," I said. "When you bought that stupid luck-knot in the market, I watched you haggle. Badly. You paid double out of politeness, and you blushed when I called you on it. The Registry didn't script that. Scripts don't blush." I squeezed their hand. "Whatever they made you for—you've been doing something else with the materials for months. That's yours. They can order you to collect me at eclipse. They cannot order you to be the person who does it gladly."

"Astrid—"

"No. My turn." I hadn't planned the speech. It assembled itself out of three years of small weather and one week of catastrophe. "Here's what I've decided, since everyone keeps asking me to choose. I won't take the forgetting. I won't hand myself over either. Both of your elegant terms assume I accept the shape of the choice, and I've read that prophecy book, and I am *done* letting other people write my pages. So tell them. Tell the paper-faced accountants of the underworld that Astrid Whitlock declines both terms. Declines the menu. Declines the restaurant."

Wren stared at me. "They'll collect you by force."

"Then they'll find out what force costs against a storm-caller with nothing left to lose and excellent reasons to be angry." I smiled, and I felt it come out crooked, and I let it. "Besides. There's a third thing they haven't accounted for."

"What third thing?"

"You," I said. "The heir who haggles badly and blushes. The heir who broke the script once already in those halls upstairs. They think duty owns you. Duty's about to learn otherwise, and I intend to be there when it happens."

For one moment—one suspended, bone-lit moment—Wren almost smiled back. Almost. Then the shadow-sentinels shifted behind them, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

"It's nearly midnight," Wren said quietly. "Eclipse is tomorrow at dusk. They'll come for your answer at dawn."

"Then give them mine tonight. Word for word." I lifted their cold hand, pressed my lips to the knuckles, and said it clearly enough for the walls of sold souls to carry it wherever such walls carry things:

"*Neither. Never. Find another debtor.*"

Far off down the galleries, something vast and patient turned a page.

* * *

They locked me in a vault that night, which the Registry probably considered a courtesy—a guest suite of one's own memories. The ceiling glowed with fragments of my missing year, drifting like weather seen from underwater: a tower in lightning, my own hands raised, the taste of ozone and terror. I lay on the warm stone floor and watched my lost self call down the storm to save a city that would exile her, and I practiced, quietly, methodically, the way I'd once practiced knot-work in a market stall.

Not reclaiming. Not surrendering.

Something else. Something with thunder in it.

Dawn was four hours away when the lights in the veins of the walls began, one shelf at a time, to flicker—and beneath the murmur of stored souls, I heard footsteps descending that did not belong to the Registry. Many boots. Disciplined ones. And cutting through the deep stone, unmistakable even here, even now:

A warden's whistle.
