## 16. The Whistle Breaks

I have been carrying a bone whistle against my heart for twenty-six years.

That's not a metaphor. It sits in a leather sheath sewn into the left breast of my coat, positioned precisely over the place where the clerks told me my heart would be, as if they'd wanted to make sure I never forgot which of the two was more important. The whistle is carved from the femur of some ancestor whose name I was never given, and it is warm even in winter, and when I hold it too long I can feel it *listening*—the way a door listens, the way a ledger listens, patient and cold and absolutely certain of what will be written next.

Astrid Whitlock is asleep three feet away from me, curled against the wall of a side gallery where the bone-lights burn low and amber instead of that surgical white the Registry favors for its audience chambers. Their breathing has the ragged catch in it of someone who cried themselves unconscious. They learned today what they sold. What they paid. Who they asked to cut it out of them.

And in an hour, maybe two, the eclipse begins, and I am supposed to walk them down to the Pale Registry and hand them over like a parcel with excellent posture.

So. Let's talk about the whistle.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about being raised by a court of dead accountants: they are very good at love. Not *feeling* it—they don't feel much of anything below the collarbones—but at *structuring* it. At building it into you like load-bearing walls. The first thing they taught me, before letters, before walking, was this: *You are the heir. The heir is not a person. The heir is a function.* And functions don't get lonely, because loneliness implies a gap between what you are and what you're for, and I was made seamless. I was made to fit.

They raised me above ground, in Greyhollow, in rented rooms and respectable anonymity, with tutors who taught me wardens' patrol routes the way other children learned nursery rhymes. I knew every blind spot in that city's anti-magic lattice by the time I was nine. I could tell you which drains the scrying can't see down, which market stalls sit in the shadow of a broken ward-stone, which hour the night watch changes and how long the gap yawns open. When I was fourteen I reported a hedge-witch selling fever-cures out of a cellar, because that was the assignment, and I remember standing outside her door afterward feeling something I didn't have vocabulary for yet. It took me years to name it. It wasn't guilt exactly. It was the sound a seam makes just before it splits.

The whistle works like this: it binds me to the Registry's claim. As long as it's whole, they can find me anywhere, call me anywhere, *compel* me anywhere—the last one only twice a year, but once is plenty, believe me. As long as it's whole, I am their function wearing a charming smile. And there's a clause—I've read the registry-terms so many times I could recite them drunk, and on several occasions have—a clause stating that the heir's claim lapses upon destruction of the instrument of binding. Lapse. Not transfer. *Lapse.* Which means the moment I break it, I stop being the heir of the Deep and become, in the eyes of the Pale Registry, nothing at all. A vacancy. An embarrassment.

Which also means the moment I break it, Astrid's collection order transfers from *the heir shall deliver* to *any means necessary.*

I've done the arithmetic about forty times since we came down here. Every version of it ends badly for someone. If I keep the whistle, I keep enough standing to negotiate—maybe—to soften the terms of what happens at eclipse. If I break it, I lose every ounce of leverage I have, and the clerks stop needing to be polite to either of us.

If I keep it, I stay the person who hands over the person I—

Well. There it is. That word again. It keeps showing up uninvited, like weather.

* * *

Astrid wakes the way they do everything: abruptly, defensively, one hand already half-curled toward a storm that isn't there. Three years of hiding your own hands from yourself teaches you reflexes. Then their eyes find mine in the bone-light and the defensiveness goes soft at the edges, and I hate how good that looks on them, how much I want to be worthy of it, how thoroughly I am not.

"Hey," they say. Voice scraped raw.

"Hey," I say. "How are we feeling? Scale of one to 'sold my soul to subterranean bureaucrats,' where are we landing?"

A laugh comes out of them sideways, wet at the corners. "That's the joke, isn't it. You've been making jokes since the night market. I thought it was charm." They sit up, press their back to the wall, look at me with those storm-grey eyes that the prophecy book drew far too kindly. "It was cover."

"Astrid—"

"The date was cover. The luck-knot. The dinner at Ferro's where you said—" and here they go pale all over again, remembering, "—you said *the thief comes smiling*. Word for word. Before I'd ever opened the book. You were quoting scripture at me over roasted quail and I thought you were flirting."

"I was flirting," I say. "That's the horrible part. Both things were true at once. That's how they build us down here—no, above ground. That's how they built *me*." I pull my knees up, mirror their posture, because apparently I'm going to do this sitting down like a civilized coward. "You want the whole confession? Because I'm about to give you the whole confession, and once I start I don't think I'll be able to stop, and some of it is going to hurt you, and all of it is going to hurt me, and I need you to know that none of it—not one single day of it—was fake."

Their jaw sets. "Start with the whistle."

Of course they know about the whistle. Of course. They've been watching my hands for days; they watched me touch my chest in the galleries when the clerks spoke; they're an exile who survived a purge by noticing everything.

I reach into the sheath and draw it out. In the amber light it looks obscene—yellowed, jointed, a thing that used to be somebody's leg and is now somebody's leash. Astrid flinches from it like it's a snake.

"This is the heir's seal," I say. "This is what makes me theirs. Twenty-six years ago the Pale Registry took a child out of the deep courts and planted him in Greyhollow like a seed, and everything that grew from that planting had one purpose: grow close to the soul-bearer. Be likable. Be *charming*. Learn their habits, their haunts, their weaknesses. And when the eclipse came, walk them through the mountain gate and deliver them to the Registry, complete and intact, so the debt closes and the prophecy closes and the books balance." I turn the whistle over in my fingers. My hands are shaking. I let them see that too. "There was a file on you, Astrid. I've had a file on you since before I could read it myself. Your name was on my first birthday gift. Not a toy. A *dossier*."

Silence. The bone-light hums.

"Say the rest," Astrid says quietly.

"The charm you sold me the first night. I didn't need a luck-knot. I needed a pretext. The date, the dinners, the walk along the Graycanal where you told me about the Night of Broken Thunder without knowing you were telling me—you thought you were making conversation and you were completing my assignment for me, section by section." My throat closes. I push through it. "And then somewhere around the third week—and I want you to understand that I fought this, I genuinely, stupidly fought it—I stopped doing the mission and started doing the *thing*, and the thing was you. I liked you. I like you. Past tense, present tense, whatever tense they don't have a word for down there, because down there they only conjugate *ownership*."

Astrid is very still. "And the last page," they say. "Of the book. You've seen it."

Here it is. The bottom of the well.

"I've seen it," I say. "Two figures enter the mountain. One leaves alone." I look at them straight, because they deserve that much and so much more than that. "The illustration shows who leaves. It's me, Astrid. It's always been me. The heir ascends, the soul remains. That's the ending they wrote, and they wrote it before either of us could walk, and every step we've taken since has been downhill toward it."

Astrid exhales slowly. I brace for rage. For tears. For the door.

What I get instead is: "Do you believe it?"

"What?"

"The page. Do you *believe* it?" Their eyes are dry now, and bright, and terrible in the way that weather is terrible right before it decides. "Because I've spent three years believing a book about myself, and it nearly killed me twice. So I'm asking. Forget the clerks. Forget the prophecy. Forget the script. Do you believe that's how we end?"

And here's the thing about raw honesty—it doesn't leave you anywhere to hide, including behind your own despair.

"No," I say. "I don't. I haven't believed it since the gallery of sold souls, when I watched you put your hand against a stranger's memory-lantern like it was a wounded bird. Functions don't do that. Heirs don't do that. Whatever I am, I'm not the thing on that page anymore, and I think—I think I stopped being it somewhere between the night market and now, and the only reason I haven't said so is that saying so has a price."

"Which price?"

I hold up the whistle.

* * *

I explain the terms quickly, because the hour is getting late and the mountain is starting to breathe differently—the deep percussion under the floor speeding up like a pulse before exertion. Eclipse approaches. The Registry does not wait politely.

"If I break it, I'm free of them, but so is the order. It stops being *the heir delivers the bearer gently* and becomes *collect by any means*. They'll send things. They'll flood the halls with reclaimed souls, use them, wear them if they have to. Sela Coss is probably already breaching the upper gates with warden steel, though gods help me, that might actually work in our favor." I weigh the whistle in my palm. It feels heavier than bone should. It feels like every year of my life. "If I keep it, I can still walk into that chamber as their heir and argue terms. Maybe buy you better conditions. A softer cage."

"And?"

"And if I keep it," I say, "I'm still holding the leash. And I have finally, at the age of twenty-six, met someone I would rather bite than hand over." I laugh, and it comes out cracked. "That's the whole speech. That's the confession, complete with footnotes. I was sent to collect you and I fell in love with you instead, and I am about to commit treason against the only family I've ever known, and I would like to note for the record that I did the math and chose badly on purpose."

Astrid reaches across the space between us and puts their hand over mine. Over the whistle. Their palms are warm—storm-warm, always a few degrees past human, the heat they've been hiding for three years under salves and knots and careful mundane craft.

"Wren," they say. "Break it."

"You understand what it costs."

"I understand what it *buys*." Their grip tightens. "I spent three years living inside a contract I couldn't remember signing. I am not going to watch you live inside one you signed as an infant. Whatever comes through those doors tonight—we meet it as two people, or we don't meet it at all."

For a moment neither of us moves. The bone-lights flicker. Somewhere far above, stone shifts with a sound like a held breath releasing.

Then I raise the whistle, and I say—for the clerks, for the ancestor whose leg this was, for the child in the rented rooms memorizing patrol routes—what I should have said years ago:

"My name is Wren Vale. I am not a function. I decline."

And I snap it across my knee.

* * *

The sound it makes isn't a crack. It's a *note*—one clear, high, dying tone that rolls down the galleries like a bell rung in a cathedral of the dead, and everywhere it touches, the bone-lights gutter. For three full seconds the mountain goes dark. In the dark I feel something leave me—not painfully, not like a wound, more like a coat taken off after a lifetime of being told I'd catch my death without it. The claim lifts. The listening stops. For the first time since I was born, no one knows exactly where I am.

Then the lights come back up, redder than before, and the deep pulse beneath our feet stumbles, recovers, and begins to accelerate.

"They felt that," Astrid says, already on their feet, pulling me up.

"Oh, they felt it. Somewhere down there a clerk just dropped his pen, and another one is opening a drawer labeled *contingencies*, and I promise you the drawer is full of things with teeth." I drop the two halves of the whistle on the stone and grind them under my heel for the sheer childish satisfaction of it. Bone dust. That's all the heir of the Deep amounts to, in the end. "We have until the eclipse peaks. Maybe less. The order transferred the moment it broke—there will be collectors in these halls within minutes, and they won't knock."

Astrid's hand finds mine. Storm-warm. Certain.

"So we run," they say.

"No," I say, and I'm smiling, and it's not the assignment's smile, not the charming one, not the thief's—it's mine, brand new, never worn. "Down is the only direction, remember? We don't run from the Registry, Astrid. We run *at* it. You and I are going to walk into that chamber before they come to us, and you're going to tell the Pale Registry exactly what you told me: that contracts written over sleeping people don't count. That souls aren't line items. That the books—" and here my voice breaks, gloriously, unforgivably, and I let it, "—the books don't balance, and never did."

Far above us, muffled by a mile of stone, the sky begins to darken.

The eclipse has begun.
