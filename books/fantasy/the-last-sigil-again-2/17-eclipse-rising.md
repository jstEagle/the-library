## 17. Eclipse Rising

**Astrid**

The moon began to eat the sun at the exact moment I stopped being afraid.

I want to be honest about that, because everyone always imagines courage arrives first and fear politely steps aside. It doesn't. Fear stays. It just stops mattering. Somewhere between the Pale Registry's terms—*the soul, or the memories; choose, little storm*—and the moment the light over the bone-lit halls turned the color of an old bruise, my fear ran out of room to live in me. Wren's hand was in mine. The clerks were smiling their paper smiles. And I thought: *they want me to choose. So the worst thing I can do is refuse to play.*

But that decision comes later. First comes the eclipse, and everything it dragged up out of the dark.

The Undercourt had gone quiet in the way a held breath is quiet. The galleries of sold souls—the lanterns of other people's stolen years—had been flickering all day like candles in a draft, and now they flared. All at once. Every soul the Registry had ever filed away woke up and *noticed* us.

"They know," Wren said. Their voice was steady. Their hand was not. "The eclipse opens the ledger. Everything owed gets collected tonight."

"Everything," I repeated. "Meaning me."

"Meaning you." They turned to face me, and in the bruise-light I could see how tired they were—not body-tired but *assignment*-tired, three decades of being someone else's instrument worn down to the wire. "Astrid. When this starts, whatever happens—"

"Don't do the dramatic farewell thing."

"I was going to say *stay behind me*, actually."

"You're the heir to a court of soul-clerks, not a wall."

"I'm also the only person here who knows where the Registry keeps its weaknesses, and one of them is standing right next to you pretending to be annoyed." A ghost of their market smile. "You bought a luck-knot from me once. Did it work?"

"It got me kidnapped by prophecy," I said. "Ask me again after the apocalypse."

The first bell rang then—a sound with no source, coming out of the walls themselves, out of the bones the halls were built from. Deep. Patient. The sound of an account being opened.

And the souls came off their hooks.

That's the image I'll never get out of my head, so you may as well have it too: hundreds of lights peeling free of the gallery walls, each one a stolen memory or a sold year or a name somebody traded away, streaming through the corridors like weather looking for a sky. Not running. *Filing.* The Registry wasn't freeing them—it was arming them. Reclaimed souls, pressed into service for one last night of collections. Lanterns made into soldiers.

"Where are they going?" I asked, though I already knew.

"The gate," Wren said. "Where your warden friend is holding the door."

"Sela's going to love this."

"She's going to hate it so much she wins," Wren said. "I've read her file. She's exactly stubborn enough."

"You've read her *file*?"

"Astrid." They squeezed my hand, gentle, urgent. "I've read *your* file. Let's survive first and feel violated later."

* * *

**Wren**

Here is what they don't tell heirs of the deep: you spend your whole above-ground life learning to pass as human, and then the hour comes when passing is the most dangerous thing you can do.

My mother—the Registry calls her First Clerk Vael, and I have never once called her mother—stood at the head of the Long Ledger Hall as the eclipse deepened, and around her the clerks moved like pens writing themselves. She did not look at me. That was the message. Heirs who hesitate get unmade quietly; heirs who make scenes get unmade loudly, as examples. I had watched it happen twice before I was old enough to understand what I was watching.

Tonight I intended to make the largest scene in the history of the Undercourt. But timing is everything, and the eclipse had its own schedule.

"Collection proceeds," Vael said to the assembled clerks, her voice the sound a contract makes when it tears slightly. "The storm-caller's sigil holds until totality. At totality, the bearer walks to the Registry floor under escort, and the debt closes. The Vale line completes its purpose." A pause, thin as a knife. "Both purposes."

*Both purposes.* Meaning: collect the soul, and retire the heir. I'd known it since I was six years old and found my own name written in the margin of my own assignment, in ink that predated my birth. You don't forget a thing like that. You just learn to fold it small enough to carry.

I looked across the hall at Astrid, standing between two clerks who thought they were guards and were about to find out otherwise, and I folded the last of my obedience down to nothing.

There was one piece of business first. The whistle.

It hung at my throat on its cord of woven hair—bone-white, older than the city above, tuned to the Registry's claim on me. One blow and the court would come to me. One blow and I could summon aid, authority, the full weight of my inheritance. It was, Vael had told me at nineteen, *everything you will ever need.*

She'd been almost right. It was everything I would ever need to remain theirs.

Not tonight. Tonight it stayed around my neck, intact, because a broken whistle announced itself and a silent one still opened doors—and there was one more door I needed it to open. The vault. Astrid's missing years. If we were going to shatter anything tonight, Astrid deserved to know exactly what they were shattering, and why it had cost them three years of forgetting.

I moved along the edge of the hall while the clerks chanted the collection liturgy, and I let the whistle warm against my skin, and I lied to myself one final time—that I could do this cleanly, that no one else had to burn—for practice. Because after tonight I wanted to be very good at telling the truth.

**Sela**

I had been a warden for twenty-two years, and in twenty-two years I had learned exactly one rule I trusted: magic lies. Magic promises rain and brings floods. Magic promises salvation and takes your sister. So when the mountain gate in front of me swung open onto a corridor lined with burning souls, and my own wardens turned to me with their faces saying *Chief Investigator, please tell us what to believe*, I gave them the only honest answer I had left.

"I don't know anymore," I said. "Hold the line anyway."

Twelve days ago I had breached this mountain to arrest a charm-seller. Twelve days ago I had believed, absolutely, in the clean geometry of my life: Greyhollow was magicless because magic was outlawed, magic was outlawed because magic destroys, and I was the person who kept the equation balanced. Then I had walked down into the Undercourt and found out who wrote the equation. The Registry. The same cold clerks whose agents had spent a century feeding Greyhollow just enough manufactured "magic crime" to keep the wardens fat, frightened, and blind—blind to the real traffic, the soul-traffic, the centuries of exiles marched down into these halls and filed away like overdue letters.

My crusade hadn't been wrong. It had been *rented.*

"Hold the line," I said again, because repetition is a kind of prayer for people like me, and took my place at the gate's mouth with my iron baton—Iron. Iron! The one material both courts respected, which should have been my first clue that the world was stranger than my manuals—and waited for whatever the eclipse sent up the stairs.

What it sent was the souls.

They came up the bone-lit corridor in a river of pale fire, hundreds of them, and I braced for horror and got something worse: recognition. Faces flickered inside the lights. Faces I'd processed. An exile I'd escorted to the city limits in my third year, weeping, begging me not to make him go. A woman whose "unlicensed divination" case I'd closed with a note in the margin that said *no further inquiry*. They streamed past us toward the open air, and every single one of them paused—one heartbeat, no more—to look at me.

Not accusingly. That was the unbearable part. They looked at me the way you look at weather. Like I was a condition they had survived.

"Warden!" Corporal Ives, young, terrified, holding the left flank with a shaking baton. "Do we stop them? Is stopping them—we're supposed to stop things, aren't we?"

I looked at the river of the stolen dead-or-worse flowing past my iron line, up toward a sky I had defended my whole career without ever once asking who owned it.

"No," I said, and felt twenty-two years come loose in my chest like a tooth. "Let them go. Anything leaving this mountain tonight leaves free. That's the new law. I'm writing it right now."

"And if something comes *down*?" Ives asked. "Something from up top? From the Registry?"

I settled my grip. Above us, far above, the eclipse was reaching totality, and even down here the light had gone strange and bruised.

"Then we're the door," I said. "And doors don't care whose side the storm is on."

* * *

**Astrid**

They came for me at the last minute before totality, which tells you everything about how bureaucracies fight. Not early, not with strategy—at the deadline, with escorts.

Six clerks, their robes rustling like turning pages, their faces smooth as vellum. Behind them, the Long Ledger Hall blazed with gathered soul-light, and at its center stood First Clerk Vael, and beside her—arriving late, breathing hard, eyes finding mine with a look I couldn't read and didn't trust—Wren.

"There has been a change," Vael said. "The heir will conduct the collection personally. Fitting. The Vale line began this account; the Vale line closes it." Her gaze slid to Wren. "Blow the whistle, child. Call the escort. Do your one purpose."

And Wren reached up, and took the whistle in hand, and I saw the moment—the whole world narrowed to it—where they could have done the obedient thing. Where the cord was already lifting over their head, where six clerks and a First Clerk and three thousand years of written fate were all leaning on that one small motion.

They lifted the cord over their head.

And threw the whistle down onto the Registry floor, and ground it under their heel until the bone cracked, and the crack went through the hall like the first thunder of my entire caged life.

"My purpose," Wren said, in a voice I had never heard them use—market-charm Wren, date-Wren, mission-Wren, all of them burned away and something rawer standing in the ash—"was never yours to assign."

Vael's composure did not break. That was the terrifying thing. She simply looked at the shattered whistle, then at her child, and said, "Then the account closes by force. Totality is now."

And the eclipse peaked, and every lantern-soul in the Undercourt turned toward me at once, and the sigil on my chest—the binding I'd carried for three years without knowing its shape—lit up like a brand.

I had maybe one breath left as myself-as-I-had-been. Wren was shoving through the clerks toward me. Sela's wardens were somewhere above, holding a door. The sky outside was dark at noon.

One breath. Enough for one choice.

I reached for the storm.

* * *

**Sela**

Later—if there was a later—people would ask me what the moment of totality looked like from the gate, and I would tell them the truth: it looked like the sky deciding it had been patient long enough.

The mountain shook. Not the tremor of settling stone but the shrug of something enormous waking up, and from the depths below us rose a sound I knew from reports, from purge records, from the Night of Broken Thunder that had started my whole career: a storm-caller, uncaged.

"Chief—" Ives started.

"I know," I said. "Stand fast."

Because whatever came next, whoever won down there in the dark, my orders were the ones I'd invented twelve minutes ago: *anything leaving leaves free.* Even if what came up those stairs wearing a storm was the same criminal I'd crossed a city to arrest. Even if it broke every rule I had left.

Especially then. Rules that had been rented deserved breaking.

The light beneath us turned white.
