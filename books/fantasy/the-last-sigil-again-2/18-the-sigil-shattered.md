## 18. The Sigil Shattered

Here is the thing nobody tells you about having your soul held in escrow: the paperwork is immaculate.

The Pale Registry had produced it for me an hour ago—three years of ledgers, clauses, and small cold print—and laid it across a lectern of white bone like a merchant presenting an invoice. *One soul, storm-tempered, lightly used. Payment received in full against damages to the city of Greyhollow. Collection at eclipse.* They'd even offered me terms. Terms! As if I were negotiating a lease. Take back the soul and forget Wren Vale's face entirely. Or keep every memory I'd clawed back tonight and surrender everything else when the sky went dark.

Either way, the ledger balanced. That was the Registry's whole religion: balance.

I looked at the sigil.

It sat over my sternum where it had sat for three years without my knowing—a knot of pale light beneath my skin, visible now only because the vault of my own memories had taught me how to see it. It was beautiful, the way a well-made cage is beautiful. Fine work. Someone down here was proud of it.

"Astrid." Wren's voice came from somewhere behind me, stripped of all its market-day charm. Just a name in the dark, said by someone who loved me and had lied to me in the same breath for months. "There has to be another way."

"There isn't," said the eldest clerk, whose name might have been Quiescence or might have been nothing at all. Its face was the color of old paper and about as expressive. "The contract is sound. The bearer is present. The heir will enforce collection, as written."

"The heir," Wren repeated, and something cracked in the middle of the word.

I turned around. The bone-lit hall was full of movement now—the eclipse had begun above us, I could feel it like a hand pressing down on the mountain, and the Registry had opened its lanterns. Sold souls flickered along the galleries, hundreds of them, thousands, each one a stolen year wearing someone's shape. Somewhere beyond the gate, Sela and her wardens were holding the line against things that used to be people, because the world had gotten strange enough tonight that Sela Voss was on my side and I still hadn't fully processed that.

But mostly I looked at Wren.

They stood between me and the clerks with their hands half-raised, the broken whistle's shards still glinting in their fist, and they looked terrified in a way no prophecy had bothered to illustrate. The book had shown two figures entering the mountain and one leaving alone. It had not shown what either of their faces did at this part.

"Hey," I said. "Remember the night market? You bought a luck-knot you didn't need and haggled anyway. Worst customer I ever had."

"Second worst," Wren said hoarsely. "You hadn't met yourself yet."

That's the thing about Wren Vale. Even mid-apocalypse, even as the court's lost heir with a shattered whistle and a throne waiting below, they could make me laugh. Which was exactly why I couldn't do what the Registry wanted, or what the prophecy wanted, or even—gods help me—what the sensible part of me wanted, which was to take the soul, take Wren's hand, forget nothing, and run until the mountains ran out.

Because there was no running from a contract written into your bones. There was only renegotiation.

And I am, if nothing else, very good at weather.

* * *

Let me tell you what three years of small weather actually means.

It doesn't mean I stopped being a storm-caller. It means I got better at lying. Every luck-knot I sold in Greyhollow had a thread of caged thunder in its knotwork—not enough to trip the wardens' senses, just enough to keep the pressure down, like a kettle singing instead of screaming. Every salve, every charm, every little honest fraud of mine had been a pressure valve. For three years I had been one continuous held breath, and I had told myself it was discipline. Craft. The careful mundane life of a careful mundane woman.

It wasn't. It was a dam, and I had built it myself, and I knew exactly where the weak point was because I'd put it there on purpose.

You can't unmake a soul-contract by reclaiming the collateral. The clerks had made that beautifully clear. Take the soul back and the debt transfers—Greyhollow owes again, and the Registry collects however it likes. Refuse, and they take me at eclipse, and Wren's whole existence gets filed under *mission complete*. The trap had two doors and both doors were theirs.

So don't use a door. Use the storm.

The sigil binding my soul wasn't separate from me—that was the elegance of it. It grew out of the debt, and the debt grew out of the storm I'd called on the Night of Broken Thunder, and the storm—

The storm was still mine. They could hold my soul in escrow. Nobody, in three hundred pages of immaculate ledger-work, had thought to foreclose on the lightning.

* * *

"You're doing the thing," Wren said quietly.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you go calm right before you do something catastrophic. I've watched you haggle. I know your tells."

"I have no idea what you mean," I said, rolling my shoulders, feeling for the seams.

Quiescence drifted forward, robes whispering like turning pages. "Bearer. The eclipse is underway. Choose."

"Oh, I'm choosing," I said. "I've never chosen anything harder. That's rather the point."

I reached into my chest—not metaphorically, though it felt like it, though my fingers came away smelling of ozone and rain—and found the first seam. Three years of knotted thunder, wound tight as a luck-knot, and I had sold charms to half the city that could unravel it. All I had to do was pull.

Understand what I was pulling. My soul was bound to the sigil; the sigil was bound to the debt; the debt was soaked through with storm. If I called the storm up through the contract, I wouldn't be taking my soul back. I'd be striking the knot that held the whole arrangement together—contract, sigil, debt, and quite possibly the part of me that had signed it. The clerks had offered me a choice between forgetting Wren and losing myself. I was proposing a third option: lose the version of me that agreed to any of this, and see who walked out of the wreckage.

Self-destruction as self-authorship. I'd have put it on a charm tag if anyone would've bought it.

"If I'm wrong," I said to Wren, "tell me I was brave. Don't tell me I was stupid. I'll be in no position to argue but I want the record straight."

"Astrid—"

"Wren." I smiled at them, and it hurt, and I let it hurt, because the last three years of not-feeling had been the real lie. "Whatever page we ended up on—I picked this ending. Not the book. Not the court. Me."

Then I pulled the seam.

* * *

Three years of small weather came out of me all at once.

It didn't look like lightning, at first. It looked like memory—which makes sense, because that's what it was made of. The Night of Broken Thunder tore open inside the bone-lit hall: rain falling upward, the taste of ash on the wind, Greyhollow's rooftops lit blue-white while I stood on the tower with my arms full of sky and my heart full of terror, calling the storm down to smother the fire the Registry itself had set. My storm. My crime. My sacrifice, folded up small and carried in knots and salves for a thousand nights.

The hall filled with it. The galleries of sold souls flickered wildly—every stolen memory in the mountain recognizing the weather of its own making. Clerks scattered like papers in wind. And the sigil over my sternum blazed white as the storm ran down the contract's fine print, clause by clause, eating the debt the way floodwater eats a ledger.

"No—" Quiescence's paper face finally moved, which I counted as a professional triumph. "Bearer, the contract cannot be—"

"Read it again," I said, teeth gritted, hair standing on end, laughing or sobbing or both. "I think you'll find the terms have changed."

The strike, when it came, was simple. Everything complicated had already happened—the exile, the sale, the forgetting, the charming liar who bought a charm he didn't need. All that remained was the oldest magic I knew: aim, and let go.

Lightning hit the sigil dead center.

The world went white, then went true.

I felt the contract shatter the way you feel a tooth crack—from the inside, all at once, with a strange clean finality. Not reclaimed. Destroyed. The debt died mid-sentence. The sigil broke apart into light and blew outward through the halls of the Undercourt like dandelion seeds, and everywhere a fragment touched, a sold soul flared free—one leaving alone, ten leaving alone, a thousand leaving alone, the prophecy fulfilling itself so fast and so broadly that it stopped meaning anything at all.

And the memory—the missing year, the scar where I'd cut myself open—came home.

I remembered asking for the knife. I remembered the words I'd said to the Registry, kneeling in the ruins-smoke: *Take the debt out of the city. Put it in me. And take the memory too—I won't survive it otherwise.* I remembered choosing to forget, so I could live in Greyhollow without drowning in what I'd paid. Grief for a self I couldn't mourn—except now I could, and did, all of it arriving in one tide: the terror, the arithmetic of one soul against a city, the terrible mercy I'd done myself.

Memory, soul, and sky collided. The mountain shook. Above us, through a hundred fathoms of stone, I felt the eclipse break its stride—as if the sky itself had paused to read the new terms.

The last thing I registered was the floor tilting, the freed souls streaming past me toward the surface like weather going home, and Wren's arms catching me before the dark took the rest.

"Got you," they said. "I've got you. One leaves alone—fine. But nobody said which one, and nobody said I'd leave you behind."

Good answer, I meant to say. Worst customer I ever had.

But the storm had spent me down to the wick, and the mountain was coming apart around its emptied halls, and my eyes were already closing—so whatever I said next, the dark heard it before Wren did.
