## 19. One Leaves Alone

The mountain began to die before Astrid did.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is all I have left. The sigil didn't shatter and then the halls fell. It was simultaneous — the contract breaking and the architecture breaking, as if the Registry had built its whole court on the assumption that Astrid Whitlock's soul would stay sold forever, and when that assumption collapsed, everything resting on it collapsed too. Bone-lit galleries going dark gallery by gallery. Staircases unspooling like ribbon cut at both ends. Somewhere below us, the Pale Registry screaming in a language made entirely of ledgers.

And Astrid, in my arms, eyes closed, breathing.

I checked. I'm not ashamed of how many times I checked. Fingers under the jaw, ear against the chest, the small warm proof of it repeated like a prayer I'd only just learned: *breathing, breathing, breathing.* They had called down a storm inside a mountain and struck their own soul loose from its binding, and then they had folded up like a coat taken off a hook, and now they were mine to carry, which was the one duty I had ever wanted and the last one I could bear.

Because here is what the prophecy said. Here is what I knew, standing in the shaking dark with the person I love unconscious against my shoulder: *two enter the mountain, one leaves alone.*

I had read that page so many times it lived behind my eyelids. In the early weeks, back when Wren Vale was still mostly a mission wearing a charming face, I'd studied the illustration the way you study an enemy's map. Two figures at a gate. One walking out into light. And I had assumed — this is important, I need you to know I assumed — that the one who left would be me. That I'd deliver Astrid, complete the collection, walk out into Greyhollow's gray morning having done the thing I was made for, hollow and promoted. That's how I'd understood *alone.* Alone as in victorious. Alone as in *the job is done.*

I never once imagined *alone* would mean carrying them.

* * *

The first stairway went while I was still deciding which way was up.

That sounds stupid. I'd walked down those stairs; surely I could walk up them. But the Undercourt wasn't arranged the way above-ground places are arranged. It was arranged the way memory is arranged — by weight, by wound, by whatever the clerks had needed nearest. With the Registry dying, the arrangement was dying with it, and the route we'd descended had become a rumor of a route.

Astrid shifted against my shoulder and murmured something. Not words. Just the sound a storm makes when it's deciding.

"Stay with me," I told them. "You've done the hard part. All I have to do is walk uphill, and I've been lying my way uphill my entire life."

They didn't answer. Their hand had found the front of my shirt at some point and held on, knuckles pale, like even asleep they were making sure I wouldn't do anything noble without supervision.

I took the stairs three at a time.

Let me tell you what the halls looked like while they came apart, because someone should remember it and Sela was still holding the gate and the clerks were still screaming and there was no one else. The lantern-souls — the sold ones, the flickering ones, the thousands of small captive lights that had lit our descent like a city's worth of streetlamps — were coming free. The Registry's grip on them had been part of the same great binding, and Astrid's strike hadn't just broken one contract. It had cracked the ledger itself.

So they rose. That's the only word for it. They rose off their hooks and shelves and alcoves like heat off summer stone, and they went *up,* streaming through the collapsing galleries past me and my burden, and each one brushed against me on the way — not deliberately, I don't think, but the way rain brushes against you, the way weather does, because they were weather now, or becoming it —

— and one of them stopped.

Just for a moment. A soul the color of lamplight through honey, hovering at the level of my eyes, and I swear to every god whose name I'd ever used falsely that it *looked* at Astrid. At the sleeping face over my shoulder. And something passed between them, some recognition too old and too large for me to witness, and the soul made a sound I can only describe as a sigh of relief so profound it moved my hair.

Then it went up, and took a piece of the ceiling with it.

* * *

I don't remember most of the climb. I want to be honest about that too. Grief does strange bookkeeping; it keeps receipts for nothing and loses the things you'd pay anything to keep. I remember fragments:

A bridge of vertebrae the length of a cathedral, cracking under us, and me running it flat-out with Astrid's head bouncing gently against my collarbone, apologizing aloud — *sorry, sorry, sorry* — as if apology could soften the ride.

A chamber where the walls had been lined with jars of names, and the jars were emptying, and one name rolled to my feet like a dropped coin. I stepped on it. I don't know whose it was. It crumbled to dust and I felt that dust for the next thousand steps, certain I'd crushed someone's whole self under my boot.

Sela. Sela at the gate, bloodied, one arm hanging wrong, shouting orders at wardens who had followed her into a fairy tale and were only now understanding what they'd followed her into. She saw me coming with Astrid and her face did something complicated — the righteous certainty souring into doubt, and then the doubt souring into something like respect, and then all of it wiped clean by the simple emergency of *move.*

"The gate's failing," she yelled. "Whatever you're going to do, do it faster!"

"I'm doing it as fast as two legs allow!"

"Then grow more legs! You people are supposed to be magic!"

"We're not *you people*, we're—" and then a section of ceiling where the conversation had been, and both of us diving different directions, and me coming up with Astrid somehow still secure, because apparently the body will negotiate any bargain except dropping the beloved. Good to know. Terrible way to learn it.

The freed souls were pouring toward the gate by then, a river of them, upward-bound, and the wardens had stopped trying to fight anything and were just — pointing. Directing traffic. Sela's anti-magic crusade, her life's work, reduced to a woman waving lanterns toward the sky, and the look on her face suggested she'd already begun the long process of finding that funny instead of devastating. Or possibly both. Both is usually correct.

And then the gate itself — the bone archway, the threshold where the prophecy's final page had drawn its two figures — and beyond it, impossibly far and impossibly close, the grey light of a Greyhollow dawn.

I ran for it with everything I had left, which was not much. My lungs were full of bone-dust. My legs had filed a formal complaint several floors ago. And Astrid — Astrid weighed almost nothing. That frightened me more than the collapse. Storm-callers should weigh like thunderclouds. Mine weighed like a bundle of laundry, like a promise kept past the point of keeping.

*Two enter. One leaves.*

I crossed the threshold carrying them, and I heard it — felt it — the prophecy closing around us like a book snapping shut. Because the letter was satisfied, wasn't it? I was leaving alone. Astrid wasn't leaving. Astrid was luggage, cargo, the beloved dead-weight of a fulfilled verse. The words didn't care that they were breathing. Words never care. That's the whole horror of prophecy: it counts bodies, not souls.

I got us fifty yards clear — maybe less — before the mountain finished its argument.

* * *

The sound it made was not a sound. It was a decision the world unmade.

I turned — because of course I turned, because some animal part of me needed to see it — and watched the Undercourt come down. Not dramatically, not cinematically. Quietly, almost politely, the way a held breath releases. Galleries folding into galleries. The Pale Registry's last scream cutting off mid-ledger. And above it all, rising from the shattered crown of the mountain, the souls — thousands upon thousands of them, every stolen light of three centuries — ascending into the eclipse-darkened sky like a storm deciding to become stars.

The eclipse was ending. I registered that distantly. The moon releasing the sun, the sun remembering its job, and the first true light of morning catching the rising souls and scattering them — east, west, north, everywhere, weather with nowhere left to be bound.

Somewhere in that scatter was the fragment of memory I'd once glimpsed in the outer galleries — a woman laughing in a kitchen, bread burning, unimportant, irreplaceable. Somewhere in it were pieces of everyone the Registry had ever bought. Going home, maybe. Going everywhere. Becoming rain over other people's roofs.

And I stood there holding Astrid, who had done all of it, who had chosen all of it, and I wept like the mountain owed me money.

Not gracefully. I want that on the record. Wren Vale, heir of the deep, raised above ground to shepherd a soul-bearer home, wept in the dirt at the foot of a collapsing mountain, snot and all, while his cargo slept through the entire apocalypse he'd helped cause. If any of the wardens saw, they had the decency to say nothing. Sela probably saw. Sela says nothing with tremendous eloquence.

*One leaves alone,* the page whispered in the back of my skull.

"Yes," I told it. "I noticed."

Because that was the joke, wasn't it? The prophecy had gotten its letter exactly right, and missed its spirit by a mile. It said one leaves alone, and I did — no heir, no court, no throne waiting below, no Registry to report to, no mission, no script. Everything that had ever written a single line of me had just been buried under a mountain. I had never been more alone in my life.

And I had never been less lonely. Because the person who made alone mean something different was breathing against my chest, and the mountain that made me was rubble, and the future had no pages left in it at all.

Alone, yes. Empty-handed, no. I carried the whole rest of it down in my arms.

* * *

Greyhollow was waking when I reached its edge.

I don't know how long the walk took. Hours. The sun climbed properly, indifferent and golden, warming the backs of souls-turned-weather as they scattered overhead. People in the streets pointed up at the strange lights. Someone would make up a story about it by nightfall. Someone would make up ten.

I carried Astrid through streets that had exiled them, past windows that had shuttered against them, and no one stopped me, because I looked like a man carrying a sick friend home and Greyhollow has always known how to look away from exactly the right things.

Their apartment was above the charm shop. Of course it was. I'd known the building since my second week in the city, back when knowing it was surveillance and knowing it now was devotion and the knowledge itself hadn't changed shape at all, just its name.

The door was locked. I put my forehead against it and laughed, once, wetly.

"Astrid," I said to the sleeping weight on my shoulder. "Your key is in your pocket and your pocket is currently my problem."

I found it. Of course I found it. I'd spent months learning this person for professional reasons and the muscle memory outlived the profession. Inside: the narrow stairs, the small rooms, the salves and knot-work and half-finished charms of a life built carefully over a wound. I laid them down on their own bed with the tenderness of someone defusing something, which, in fairness, they were.

And then I sat on the floor beside the bed, because my legs had finally submitted their resignation, and I waited.

The light moved across the floor. Noon-ish. The souls overhead had finished scattering; the sky was just sky again, scandalously ordinary. Down in the market, faintly, someone was arguing about the price of fish. The world, continuing. It seemed rude, frankly.

I watched Astrid breathe and I did the accounting I'd been avoiding the whole climb. What I'd lost: a court, a birthright, a father-shaped absence, a purpose I'd never asked for, a whistle, a name. What I'd gained: a chair on a floor, a view of a sleeping person, and absolutely no idea what happened next. No page existed for this. The book was ash somewhere under the mountain, along with its author and its arithmetic.

For the first time since the whistle, since the assignment, since the beginning — nobody was coming to collect me either.

It was terrifying. I recommend it.

Astrid's fingers twitched. Then curled. Then their brow furrowed in the specific way it does when a thought is being assembled from expensive parts, and I leaned forward, heart hammering like I was seventeen and caught somewhere forbidden, and waited for their eyes to open.

When they did — grey as the city, wide as the weather — they looked at me for a long, long moment. And the first thing Astrid Whitlock said, voice scraped raw, whole soul newly returned and visibly still settling into place like furniture after a move, was:

"...Did you carry me the *whole* way?"

"Every step."

"And the stairs?"

"Especially the stairs."

Their eyes filled. Not storm-grey anymore — just wet, just human, just theirs. "Wren. What did you *do*?"

What did I do. I broke a dynasty, buried a court, burned a script, and walked out of a prophecy carrying the only thing in the world I couldn't bear to lose. What did I do.

I smiled, because smiling is the first language I ever learned and the only honest one I have left.

"I left alone," I said. "Exactly like it said."
