## 20. Aftermath of Weather

The mountain stopped screaming at dawn.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was the last thing I had left. The screaming wasn't a sound, exactly—it was pressure, the kind you feel in your back teeth before a summer storm, except it went on for six hours and it came from underground and every warden in Greyhollow heard it through the soles of their boots. Then, somewhere in the grey hour when the night shift wants to go home and the morning shift hasn't arrived, it stopped. And what rose out of the mountain instead was weather.

Real weather. Weather with intention in it.

Clouds poured out of the peak like water out of a tipped jug—clouds that had no business existing inside solid stone—and they unspooled across the sky in ribbons, gold-shot, restless, alive. Rain fell upward for eleven minutes. I know because I stood in the courtyard and counted, because counting is what I do instead of panicking, and I have never once in twenty years needed to panic as badly as I did that morning.

"Chief Investigator." Corporal Vess came up beside me, helmet under her arm, hair plastered flat by the impossible rain. She's a good soldier. Steady. The kind of officer who follows orders without needing to understand them, which until recently I considered her finest quality. "Orders?"

I looked at my wardens. Four hundred of them ringed around the mountain's foot, armed with iron filings and prayer-certsificates and the absolute certainty that magic was a rumor told by criminals. Half of them were staring at the sky with their mouths open. The other half were staring at the souls.

Because the souls were visible now. That was the thing nobody could look away from. They'd come out of the mountain with the weather—a thousand points of soft light rising through the rain, drifting over the valley like lanterns released at a festival, scattering toward the horizon in every direction. Some of them, I swear on the writ itself, paused. Hovered near people. Near *specific* people. I watched one settle over old Mother Quist at the market gate, the woman who sells turnips and complains about the tax, and I watched her drop her basket and press both hands to her mouth and weep for reasons she will never be able to explain to anyone.

"That's not possible," Vess said quietly.

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

We stood there while the impossible rained on us.

* * *

Let me tell you what I believed yesterday, because I think a person ought to be able to account for their beliefs the way an accountant accounts for coin. I believed magic was real but rare, and that rarity made it dangerous, and that danger justified everything the Wardens did—the purges, the writs, the exile lists, the Night of Broken Thunder three years ago when we drove every storm-touched soul out of Greyhollow or into the ground. I believed the city's anti-magic wards were a shield built by wise founders. I believed I was the shield's best servant.

I believed the Undercourt was a smuggler's fable. A story exiles told each other to make their exile sound like a pilgrimage.

Here is my accounting now: the wards are hollow. They always were. I've spent two days going through the archive vaults beneath the Wardenhouse—vaults I have clearance to, vaults I've walked past my whole career—and I found the founding contracts. Actual parchment. Registry seals. Greyhollow's "magic-free" charter was purchased from the court beneath the mountain, paid for in souls, renewed every generation by men who then built careers policing the very magic their own signatures kept alive. The purge lists weren't protection. They were *harvest*. Every storm-caller we drove out of the city walked into the dark carrying exactly what the Registry wanted.

And me? I was the efficient little clerk of the harvest. Twenty years of service. Commendations. A commendation plaque, gods help me, still hanging in my office.

Vess found me in the vaults on the second evening, holding the oldest contract with hands that would not stop shaking.

"Chief," she said. "There's a crowd at the mountain gate."

"What kind of crowd."

"The kind that's waiting for someone to come out."

* * *

Two figures came down off the mountain on the third day.

I saw them from four hundred yards through the gate-arch, and even at that distance I knew one of them. Astrid Whitlock. Storm-caller. Exile writ number 114, my own signature at the bottom, dated three years and two months ago—the arrest warrant I'd been proudest of, once, because Whitlock had been the most dangerous name on the Broken Thunder list. The most powerful. The one who got away clean.

Except they hadn't gotten away clean, had they. That was the joke the mountain had been keeping for me. They'd gotten away *paid for*. Sold. Their soul sitting in a bone-lit ledger under a mile of stone while I slept soundly in my warden's quarters believing justice had been served.

They walked slowly. One leaning on the other—I couldn't tell which was holding which up. Behind them the freed lights of the scattered souls drifted thinner, fewer, the last of them climbing into a sky that had finally remembered how to behave. The mountain behind them had a new shape. I didn't ask what had happened to it. Some questions you can see the answer to from far away: half its face had come down, and where the great sealed gate had been there was just a wound of daylight, open, ordinary, empty.

Wren Vale walked beside them. The heir. The Registry's own lost child, raised above ground as a shepherd, sent down to bring home the flock. I'd read the file fragments the archivists pulled from the flooded lower halls—when the sigil broke, the Registry's records drowned in released memory, clerks wandering the galleries unable to remember their own names, ledgers rewriting themselves mid-page. An institution built on perfect record-keeping, and now its records are weather too. There is a poetry in that I refuse to enjoy. Enjoying it would mean I'm not angry yet, and I intend to stay angry for a good long while. Anger is load-bearing, right now. It's the only wall still standing.

"Chief Investigator Sela," said the young warden at the checkpoint, when the two figures came within thirty paces. He had his hand on his baton. He looked at me for the order. They all looked at me for the order. Four hundred wardens, and every single one of them waiting for Chief Investigator Sela to do what Chief Investigator Sela has always done.

Astrid Whitlock stopped at the line in the stones where Greyhollow's authority officially began. They looked worse than any storm-caller should—grey-faced, hollow-eyed, moving like someone whose body had been borrowed and recently returned. But their eyes were clear. Clearer than mine, probably.

"Sela," they said. My name, no title. Exiles don't owe titles.

"Whitlock." I stepped forward. The crowd behind them—market folk, mostly, drawn by the lights and the falling sky and three days of rumors that grew stranger with every retelling—went quiet in the way crowds do when they sense history deciding something.

I had the writ. Of course I had the writ; I carry the active warrants on my person, always have, folded in oilcloth against my heart like a second set of ribs. Exile writ 114. All I had to do was unfold it, read the charges, and give the order, and twenty years of institutional muscle would carry the moment for me the way it always had. Arrest the storm-caller. Restore order. Pretend the sky had not rained upward.

My hand went to my breast pocket.

And here is the thing I will remember on my deathbed, whatever deathbed I eventually earn: Astrid Whitlock watched my hand move, and did not flinch. Did not run. Did not raise so much as a spark of the power I knew was coiled in them—they'd shattered a soul-contract with a called storm two days ago; the air around them still smelled faintly of ozone and burnt parchment. They just stood there, swaying slightly on their feet, and waited to see what kind of person I was.

That's the part that undid me. Not mercy. Not argument. Just the *waiting*. As if they'd already decided I might be worth the benefit of the doubt, and were willing to stand there, exhausted, at the end of everything, while I proved them right or wrong.

Twenty years, and no one had ever simply waited for me before. Everyone salutes or runs. Nobody waits.

I unfolded the oilcloth. Took out writ 114. Held it up so the crowd could see the seal, the signature, the word EXILE stamped across it in the red ink we reserve for sentences that can't be appealed.

Then I turned to Vess.

"Corporal. Fire."

She blinked. "Chief?"

"A brazier. Now." And when she still hesitated, because good soldiers hesitate at new orders, I said the rest of it loud enough for the whole gate to hear: "The mandate is void. The charter was bought and paid for with stolen souls, and every writ signed under it—including every writ signed by me—is paper from a dead institution. We're burning them. All of them. Starting with this one."

I put writ 114 into the brazier myself.

It caught quickly. Red ink curls up beautifully when it burns, if you've never had occasion to notice. The seal bubbled and went black. My signature—the neat, upright hand of a woman who believed she was a shield—warped, browned, and came apart into ash that the wind took east, toward the mountain, as if returning it.

The crowd made a sound. Not cheering, exactly. Something rawer than cheering. The sound of a city exhaling after holding its breath for a hundred years.

Behind me, Vess was already shouting for the archive carts. "Bring out the exile rolls! The purge ledgers! Everything with a Registry countersign!" Good soldiers, it turns out, don't need to understand an order. They just need to believe the person giving it has finally stopped lying to herself.

Astrid Whitlock watched the ash rise. When they spoke again, their voice was rough as gravel. "Why?"

I thought about saying something dignified. Something about law and conscience and the difference between following orders and being ordered around. All true. None of it sufficient.

"Because you waited," I said. "Three years ago I put my signature on your life and I never once asked what it cost you. You could have hated me. Instead you stood there and gave me room to be better than I've been. That's more grace than the Wardens ever earned, and I'm not arrogant enough to waste it twice."

They nodded slowly, like a person accepting terms of trade. Then their knees buckled—not dramatically, just the quiet collapse of someone whose account had run dry days ago—and their companion caught them, arms around them, lowering them gently to sit on the milestone at the gate. Wren Vale. The heir who refused the throne. I looked at that pale, sharp-boned face, at the way they cradled a former enemy of the state like something breakable and precious, and I revised my files one more time: threat assessment, none. Loyalty assessment, total.

"We need a healer," Vale said. Not asking me. Telling me, with the particular calm of someone accustomed to being obeyed—which, fair enough, they'd earned it in the deep places if nowhere else.

"Healer's coming," I said. "So is tea, if you can stomach Wardenhouse tea. It's an acquired taste. Most things are, apparently. I only learned that Tuesday."

Something that might have been a laugh moved through Astrid Whitlock's exhaustion. They leaned their head against Vale's shoulder and closed their eyes.

* * *

By nightfall the brazier outside the gate had become a bonfire, and the bonfire had become a queue.

Exiles came down from the hills—actual exiles, the ones who'd survived three years in the wild country beyond our patrols, thin and wind-hardened and suspicious as cats. They came to the fire one at a time and gave their names, and my wardens checked the rolls, and the writs came out, and burned. Writ 117. Writ 203. Writ 88, which made an old man named Corvus sink to his knees in front of the flames. Forty-one writs by midnight. I stayed until the last one, because it seemed important that the same hand that signed them watch them burn. Justice delayed is injustice compounded; justice reversed, at least, is arithmetic done honestly.

Mother Quist brought the wardens turnips. I choose not to interpret this as mockery.

Near dawn, I sat on the milestone where Whitlock had collapsed and drank terrible tea and talked with the only other person still awake—Vess, wrapped in a blanket, watching the embers.

"Chief," she said, after a long silence. "What happens now? To us. To the city. There's no charter. No mandate. Half the archive's drowned and the other half proves we were the bad guys. What do wardens even *do*, tomorrow?"

I considered the question seriously, because she deserved that. Below us, Greyhollow lay spread out under a washed-clean sky, lamps lit, market stalls already being dragged out for a day of commerce that would include, for the first time in living memory, charms sold openly in the night market and possibly lightning bugs doing tricks. Above us, the mountain wore its wound of daylight like a scar that had stopped hurting.

"Now?" I said. "Now we find out whether we were ever anything besides the Registry's broom. If there's a self left under the uniform, tomorrow's when it learns to walk." I turned my cup in my hands. "I signed away forty-one lives, Vess. I don't get to be finished. I get to be useful. There's a difference, and I intend to spend the rest of my career learning it."

She nodded slowly. Then: "There's one more thing. Courier came in an hour ago. From the mountain."

My stomach dropped. The mountain was supposed to be done with us. The mountain was supposed to be a hole and a lesson and nothing more.

"From the mountain," I repeated.

"Not the mountain exactly." She held out a slip of paper. "One of the Registry clerks. The ones who wandered out yesterday—remembering their names, most of them, or close enough. This one says he remembers a *schedule*. Says before the sigil broke, the Registry had standing instructions. Contingencies." Her voice went carefully flat, the way it does when she's frightened and hiding it. "Says the eclipse collection wasn't the only harvest planned. Says there are other courts. Other registries. Other cities with bought charters—" she met my eyes— "and that one of those cities is due for renewal within the year."

I looked at the paper. I didn't read it immediately. First I looked east, toward the mountain, where at the foot of the broken gate a small fire still burned—two figures beside it, close together, one asleep against the other's shoulder, keeping their own watch under a sky with nothing written on it anymore.

Other cities. Other ledgers. Other Selas, somewhere, still signing.

"Well," I said, standing, folding the slip into my breast pocket where the writs used to live. "Then somebody had better warn them."

I picked up my coat. For the first time in twenty years, it felt less like a uniform and more like clothing—something a person could take off, or wear wherever she chose.

"Corporal Vess," I said. "Wake the archive team. And put the kettle on. We're going to need a lot of tea."
