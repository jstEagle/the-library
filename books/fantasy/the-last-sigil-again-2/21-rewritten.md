## 21. Rewritten

I woke to the smell of rain and the taste of my own name.

That second part is not a sentence I expected to write, and if you'd told me a month ago that I'd ever describe waking up as *tasting*, I'd have sold you a luck-knot and quietly moved my stall across the market. But there it was. My name—Astrid Whitlock, storm-caller, exile, fool, liar, lover—sat on my tongue like copper after lightning, and beneath it, stranger still: silence. The good kind. The kind that comes after a storm has finished shouting and the sky is wrung out and honest.

I was lying in wet grass at the foot of the mountain, staring up at a dawn the color of hammered tin, and I could remember everything.

Everything.

The Night of Broken Thunder. Marrow's ledger with my name inked in bone-white script. The vault where my missing year had hung like a coat I'd forgotten I owned. The sigil breaking under my own hand, my own storm, my own choice—and Wren screaming my name as the world came apart around us in ribbons of light and memory and three years of weather let out of its cage all at once.

And before all of it, the oldest memory, the one they'd cut out of me so cleanly I'd built a life on the scar: standing in the Registry's cold gallery, trading my soul for Greyhollow's skin, asking them—*asking*—to take the memory too, because I couldn't have lived one more day knowing what I'd given up.

They say the Registry keeps souls like lanterns. Mine had burned for three years in those bone-lit halls, and now here I was, lying in the grass with the whole of myself poured back in, and do you know what the first feeling was? Not triumph. Not relief.

Embarrassment.

Because I remembered being twenty-four years old and signing my own damnation with a flourish, because I'd read too much poetry about noble sacrifice, because I'd thought the kindest thing the Undercourt could do for me was make me forget how much it cost. I remembered the clerk's face—that patient, papery smile—as they slid the memory away like a waiter clearing a plate I hadn't finished. *You'll be happier,* the clerk had said, and the worst part, the truly unforgivable part, was that they'd been right. For three years I *had* been happier. A smaller happiness, a rented happiness, but I'd furnished it nicely with charm-craft and market banter and a loneliness I mistook for contentment.

Well. The rent was due, and I had paid it in full, and the apartment was mine again, dust and all.

I sat up. My body reported in with a full inventory: ribs bruised from falling masonry, palms scorched from channeling a storm I'd been strangling since the solstice, throat raw from screaming a name I won't repeat here because you already know whose it was. My hair was full of grit. Somewhere in the collapse, I'd lost one boot. One. Which meant somewhere on the mountainside there was a single boot sitting in the rubble like a monument to everything I'd failed to plan for.

And my chest—

My chest was *full*. That's the only word for it. For three years I'd been walking around as four-fifths of a person and calling it a personality. Now every breath went all the way down and came back up carrying things with it: grief for the exiles whose memories I'd seen flickering in those halls, terror at how close I'd come to letting the prophecy finish its arithmetic, and underneath it all, stubborn and banked and refusing to go out, love—the real kind, the kind that had looked at a script written a century ago and said *no, thank you, we'll write our own.*

The mountain behind me was quiet. Not collapsed—not entirely—but changed. The gate stood open, which gates are not supposed to do, and from inside came a sound like wind moving through an empty house. The freed souls were gone. All of them. Scattered upward through the rock like weather finding the sky, off to whatever happens to a soul when nobody owns the deed anymore. I hoped it was something good. I hoped it was something like this morning.

Which reminded me.

"Wren," I said, out loud, to no one, because saying it made it true, and I had spent enough of my life letting other people decide what was true. "Wren."

No answer. Just the mountain breathing its empty-house wind.

Right. Time to find out whether the last page of that wretched book had gotten the final word after all.

---

Walking is underrated as a dramatic device. Everyone talks about the storms and the sigils and the courts of clerks who file your soul between *Pending* and *Paid In Full*, but nobody warns you about the walk. Down the mountain's lower slopes, through scrub pine that bent in winds that were, for once, just winds. Past the boundary stones where Greyhollow's anti-magic wards had stood for a century, humming their flat gray note against anything that wasn't mundane.

Except.

I stopped at the first ward-stone and put my hand near it, ready for the familiar deadening pressure, the sensation of my power going muffled and distant like a voice through a wall.

Nothing happened. Or rather—something didn't happen. The stone hummed its note and my storm answered it, easily, the way two instruments tune to each other. The wards weren't broken. They were *irrelevant*, suddenly, the way a fence is irrelevant to someone who's learned the gate was never locked, only painted shut. Whatever I'd done when I shattered the sigil, whatever flood of unbound soul and released memory had roared through both courts—I'd rewritten more than a contract. The whole ledger was smudged. The Registry's careful accounting, Sela's righteous certainty, Greyhollow's comfortable lie that magic was a problem that happened elsewhere—all of it smeared into something new and illegible and terrifyingly open.

I flexed my fingers and small blue light gathered in my palm like water finding a cup. It didn't hurt. That was the miracle, honestly. Three years of caging it, and it came to my hand gentle as a tame thing, or maybe just tired of fighting me, or maybe—we had never actually been enemies, my magic and I. We'd just been badly managed. By me.

"You're leaking," I told it. "We'll work on that."

It pulsed, which I chose to interpret as agreement, because a person can choose to interpret things, and I had recently learned that choosing was the entire point.

I walked on. The sun climbed. Somewhere above the tree line, birds were conducting their usual business of being loudly alive, and I found myself cataloguing details with the desperate attention of someone who has recently learned that memory can be taken and wants to press every flower flat in the book before anyone comes with scissors. The way frost still clung to the north-facing hollows though it was nearly midsummer. The smell of pine sap warming. The specific squeak of my one remaining boot, which I was already emotionally attached to and would defend with violence.

And threaded through all of it, the loose feeling. That's the honest word for it. *Loose.* Like a tooth. Like a knot retied. Everything I'd been—exile, criminal, victim, hero, the girl who sold herself, the woman who burned the contract—every identity I'd worn fit differently now, the way clothes fit differently after you've been ill. None of them were wrong, exactly. None of them were *right*, either. They were options. A wardrobe instead of a uniform.

I had spent my whole life being assigned things. Assigned a talent by bloodline. Assigned a crime by panic. Assigned a punishment dressed up as a purchase, assigned a forgetting dressed up as mercy, assigned a role in a prophecy written by clerks who thought narrative was just another form of bookkeeping. Even my rebellion had been scripted—one enters the mountain, one leaves alone, thank you for playing, your participation has been recorded in the permanent record.

But the permanent record was smudged now. And the pen, as far as I could tell, was in my hand.

I crested a rise and stopped.

---

Wren was sitting at the mountain's foot on a fallen boulder, exactly where the treeline gave up and the scree began, looking like a painting of somebody waiting. Knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, chin resting on top. Watching the path down from the gate with an expression I recognized from our first date—the charming mask, the easy smile—except the mask was off and lying somewhere in the rubble, and what was underneath was so nakedly hopeful and so terrified of hoping that it hurt to look at, the way the sun hurts when you've been underground.

They saw me and stood up so fast they nearly went over on the scree. Then they did the most Wren thing possible: caught themselves, straightened their jacket, and tried to arrange their face into composure, and got about halfway, and the other half stayed wrecked and open, and I loved them so hard in that moment that my chest genuinely ached around the new-whole weight of my soul.

"You're awake," they said. Their voice cracked down the middle. "You've been—you were out for almost two days. I carried you out. The prophecy said—" They stopped. Swallowed. Started over, and this time they didn't reach for any script at all. "You were heavy. You weigh more than you look. I'm telling you all the wrong things. Hi. Hello. Are you—are you *you*?"

"Every embarrassing piece," I said. "Including the part where I signed my soul away and asked them to take the receipt too."

Wren flinched. "Astrid—"

"No, let me finish, I've had two days of unconsciousness to compose remarks and I intend to deliver them." I came down the slope toward them, boot squeaking, storm-light flickering off my fingertips because apparently my body had decided that hiding was over and I hadn't yet learned to argue with it. "I remember everything. I remember the debt, and the sale, and the vault, and I remember *you*—the whistle, the orders, the heir's crest sewn into your lining that you thought I never noticed. I remember you overhearing the clerks plan to keep me anyway. I remember the whistle breaking." I stopped an arm's length away. Close enough to touch. Far enough that touching would be a choice, which was, I was learning, the only way I wanted anything done anymore. "I remember all of it. And I'm still here."

"So am I," Wren said, and then, quieter, with the particular steadiness of someone setting down something very heavy very carefully: "I refused it. The throne. The Registry's gone sideways—the clerks who are left are arguing over ledgers with nothing left to collect, and the seat's empty, and they sent word that it's mine if I want it. Heir of the Deep. All I have to do is come back down and start writing people into books again."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them the position had been eliminated." A ghost of the old charm moved across their face—not the mask, just the humor that had always lived underneath it. "Actually, what I told them was less polite, and involved several words I learned from a certain charm-seller in a night market, but the gist was that I'm retired."

"And the court?"

"Will survive without an heir or won't. Not my department anymore." They looked at their hands—at the faint deep-light still clinging to the fingers, heir's light, court light, fading by degrees like dye coming out in the wash. "I keep expecting it to hurt more, giving it up. It doesn't. That's either growth or a character flaw. Possibly both."

I should tell you what I did next, and I want it noted that it was a choice. Not fate, not prophecy, not the momentum of a story some clerk had entered into the rolls a hundred years before either of us was born. The book was ash; I'd watched the pages burn in the aftermath with a satisfaction I intended to savor for decades. Nothing about us was written anywhere except in the wet grass and the mountain air and the space between two people deciding, deliberately, in full possession of every terrible fact, to close the distance.

So I closed it.

Wren made a sound against my mouth—half laugh, half sob, entirely undone—and their arms came around me and we held onto each other in the shadow of the ruined mountain like two survivors of a shipwreck checking that the other one was solid. They were shaking. So was I. My storm rose around us in a slow spiral of blue-white light, unbidden, and instead of suppressing it I let it, and it curled around us both like a cat settling in, and Wren laughed into my hair, wet and disbelieving.

"It likes me," they said. "Your storm."

"It's got taste. It always did." I pulled back far enough to look at them properly. "For the record: I choose you. Knowing everything. Especially knowing everything. The whistle, the mission, the crown you didn't want. All of it. I'm not doing forgiveness-as-forgetting again—I did that once and it cost me my soul, literally. This is forgiveness-with-full-records-retained. It's a rarer product. Costs more."

"What's the price?" Wren asked, smiling through the wreckage.

"You never lie to me again. Not kindly, not protectively, not for the mission. If the truth is ugly, you hand it over ugly, and I'll do the same. Deal?"

"Deal." They said it like an oath, which, given everything oaths had cost us lately, felt right. Then their expression shifted—still soft, but with something new moving underneath, and I'd learned to read that look as *incoming complication*. "There's one more thing. About the throne I refused."

"Wren."

"They didn't accept the refusal." They reached into their jacket and drew out a piece of paper, bone-pale, covered in the Registry's cramped clerical hand. "This came up the mountain this morning. Delivered by a clerk who looked like he'd been crying, which I didn't know they could do, so that's new. Apparently when I severed the claim, the succession law did something none of their ledgers predicted. The inheritance didn't die. It *transferred*."

"Transferred to whom?"

Wren turned the paper around. My own name sat in the center of it, written in that bone-white ink I knew too well, surrounded by formal language I only half understood and one line I understood completely:

*By severance of the prior claim, the estate of the Pale Registry passes to Astrid Whitlock, soul restored, account settled, balance owing: one (1) court.*

I read it twice. Three times. The storm-light around my hands guttered and flared.

"I destroyed their contract," I said slowly. "I broke the sigil, I flooded their halls, I set every soul they owned free into the sky, and their legal response is to give me *the building*?"

"The whole court," Wren confirmed. "The halls, the vaults, the ledgers, the lantern-gallery. There's a great deal of it, and apparently it doesn't function without an owner, and you're the owner, and the clerks are downstairs right now holding an emergency meeting about whether they're allowed to unionize." A pause. "One of them voted yes already. I think his name was Pellum. He seemed excited."

I stood there at the foot of the mountain, whole for the first time in three years, freshly chosen and freshly choosing, holding title to the underworld.

"Well," I said. "I suppose I should go inform my staff."
