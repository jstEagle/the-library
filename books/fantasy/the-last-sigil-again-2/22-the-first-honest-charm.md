## 22. The First Honest Charm

**Astrid**

There is a particular kind of terror in setting up a market stall when you have nothing left to hide.

I'd spent three years perfecting the art of the mundane: luck-knots tied with exactly the right number of knots and not one more, weather-salves that worked through beeswax and stubbornness alone, charms that were only charms because people believed hard enough to make them true. Every item a small lie of omission. Every sale an act of survival.

Now I spread my wares across the same scarred plank table in Greyhollow's night market, and in the center of it—dead center, where no one could miss it—I set out a jar of storm-light.

Actual storm-light. Bottled the honest way, from my own palms, which had been doing a fair impression of twin thunderheads since the mountain gave us back. It glowed blue-white against the lantern-glow of the market like a confession nobody had asked for.

"You're going to cause a riot," Wren said, arranging the knot-cords beside it with the fussy precision of someone who had once been raised to inherit a court and had instead decided to become extremely good at merchandising.

"Good. Riots are good for business."

"Riots are *bad* for business. Riots are what happens *after* business stops."

"That's quitter talk."

Wren laughed—that laugh that had undone me at a dinner table a lifetime and one shattered sigil ago—and I felt the old familiar pull in my chest, except now it wasn't at war with anything. That was the strange part. The part I kept having to relearn. For three years every feeling I'd had came with a footnote: *this too may be scripted, this too may be assigned.* Now the page was blank, and blank pages, it turns out, are terrifying.

Also, they're mine.

* * *

**Wren**

Here is something they do not tell you about refusing an empty throne: word travels.

By our third night back in the market, we'd had visitors. A delegation from the Ashvale covens, come to look at Astrid's palms the way pilgrims look at relics. Two of my father's former clerks—the ones who hadn't been dissolved along with the Registry's ledgers—standing at the edge of the lamplight with their bone-pale faces turned up like moths, waiting to see if I would change my mind. Sela Crowsdottir, twice now, arms crossed, watching the crowd with the expression of a woman who has burned her own institution's paperwork and is still deciding how she feels about it.

And once, a child. Small, gap-toothed, clutching a copper coin she'd clearly had to argue someone for. She didn't want a charm. She wanted to see the storm-light up close.

Astrid crouched down to her level and opened their hands, and the light pooled there, gentle as a held breath, and the child's whole face went wide and holy.

"Does it hurt?" the girl asked.

"No," Astrid said, and then, after a pause in which I watched them decide to be truthful, "It used to. Not anymore."

"Why not?"

"Because I stopped pretending it was someone else's job to hold it."

The girl nodded with the tremendous gravity of the very young, paid her coin for a knot she almost certainly couldn't tie, and left. And Astrid stood there in the market's noise with light still cupped in their palms, looking slightly stunned, as though they'd only just heard what they'd said.

*That,* I thought. *That's why I was sent. Not to collect them. Whatever the clerks intended—some part of whatever made me was sent to find the one person alive who could teach me that.*

I didn't say any of this. I just handed Astrid the coin box and let my shoulder rest against theirs, which in the language we'd developed over two courts' worth of wreckage meant roughly everything.

* * *

**Astrid**

The book came out of my coat around midnight, when the market had thinned to stragglers and the lantern-sellers were counting their take.

You know the book. Black cover, cracked spine, the banned prophecy codex I'd stolen off Marrow's black-market shelf back when stealing it seemed like the bravest thing I'd ever do. The one with the illustration that had turned my blood to sleet: two figures entering the mountain, one leaving alone. The one whose last page had hung over every kiss, every campfire, every moment of trust like a knife balanced on a doorframe.

One leaves alone. That's how it ended. That's how it was always supposed to end.

Except here's the thing about prophecies that nobody puts in the banned books: they're contracts, and contracts can be broken. I'd proven that with the most spectacular act of contractual violation in the history of two courts. The Registry was rubble. The sigil was ash. The eclipse had come and gone and taken the whole architecture of *must* with it.

But the letter of it had still come true. Wren had carried me out of the collapsing dark, alone. I hadn't been awake to feel it, but Wren told me about it sometimes, late, in the flat careful voice of someone describing a wound that's stopped bleeding but hasn't forgotten how. How the halls fell behind them step by step. How the freed souls streamed past like weather going somewhere better. How they walked up and up and out into ordinary starlight carrying me, and how the prophecy's last line had burned itself satisfied into the air above the gate:

*One leaves alone.*

"We obeyed it," Wren had said. "Even breaking it, we obeyed it."

So tonight I took out the book, and Wren looked at me, and I looked at Wren, and neither of us said anything, because some conversations you've already finished; you're just waiting for the ceremony.

There was a brazier at the end of our row—an old woman selling roasted chestnuts who had, in three nights, progressed from wary to fiercely protective of "her storm-sellers." I bought a paper cone of chestnuts as tribute. She nodded like a queen granting a favor.

Then I held the book over her coals.

Funny thing about burning a prophecy: it doesn't go quickly. The pages fought. They curled and blackened and the ink glowed a moment before it died, and I swear—for a heartbeat—I saw the final illustration flare: the two figures, the mountain, the one walking away alone. Then it was just paper. Just smoke. Just an old liar finally telling the truth, which is nothing.

"One leaves alone," Wren murmured, watching the last of it go. Their voice did something complicated in the middle of the sentence and came out the other side steady.

"Not anymore," I said. "Now it's just a book that's wrong."

"It was never wrong."

"No," I admitted. "That was the problem. It was right all the way down to the letter." I threaded my fingers through theirs, right there over the coals, in front of the chestnut woman and the stragglers and the whole indifferent turning world. "But letters aren't the only thing books are for."

Wren squeezed back. Above us, Greyhollow's sky—the magicless, wardens'-patrolled, thoroughly ordinary sky—flickered with heat lightning that everyone in the market knew perfectly well came from the stall at the end of the row, and nobody minded, and that was its own kind of miracle.

* * *

**Wren**

I want to record, for whoever finds this—which is probably no one, because we keep our records in knots and bottled light these days, in things that have to be held to be read—what it was actually like, the first week.

It was terrible. It was wonderful. Mostly it was small.

Learning to sleep without listening for a whistle. I still wake reaching for it sometimes, my hand closing on nothing, and Astrid—half-asleep, unerring—puts their palm flat against my sternum until my heart remembers whose it is.

Learning that my name is mine. *Wren.* Not heir, not shepherd, not the Registry's long investment come due. A bird's name. A small brown bird that builds wherever it likes.

Learning Astrid's new name—not the exile name, not the storm-caller name the purge files used, but the one they chose themselves, testing it aloud for days before committing, the way you test ice before you trust it with your weight. They sell under it now. Painted on the stall's little sign in Astrid's own handwriting, crooked and proud:

*WEATHER & WEATHER. Charms, salves, small mercies. No futures told. Nothing here is written.*

Customers ask, sometimes, about the storm-light. Whether it's safe. Whether it's legal. Whether the stories are true—the mountain, the eclipse, the court beneath, the heir who broke a whistle and a dynasty in the same breath.

Astrid gives different answers depending on the customer. I've started keeping tally. To children: *true, and better than the stories.* To the nervous: *safe as rain.* To the one warden who still comes by on his rounds, awkward, curious: *ask Sela, she was there.*

To me, when the stall is closed and the lanterns are guttering and it's just us and the cooling coals: nothing at all. They just open their hands and show me. That's the whole answer. That's always been the whole answer.

* * *

**Astrid**

On the seventh night, Sela came back a third time, and this time she didn't stand at the edge of the lamplight. She marched up to the stall like she was serving papers, dropped a folded square of leather on the table, and stood there radiating the particular discomfort of a righteous woman performing an unrighteous-feeling kindness.

"What's this?" I asked, though I already knew. You know your own exile writ when you see it. Even burned.

"Evidence," Sela said. "Of a crime that no longer has a statute. I'm told the correct procedure is disposal." Her jaw worked. "Consider it disposed. Formally. In front of witnesses."

"You could've just thrown it in a fire yourself," Wren said gently.

"I could have." She looked at the storm-light jar, at the sign, at the two of us standing close together in the smoke and the market noise, and something in her face—the certainty that had driven her through years of purges and raids and wrongness dressed as duty—finally, fully, settled into something quieter. Something that might, given time, grow into peace. "But I owed it to you to watch it burn properly. Both times."

She meant the writ. She also meant the world. I didn't ask which; some debts between exiles and their hunters don't get named out loud. They just get paid.

She bought a luck-knot on her way out. Paid full price. Refused the discount. Tied it to her belt where the wardens' insignia used to be.

* * *

**Wren**

Later—much later, when the market had packed itself away and the chestnut woman had banked her coals and the only light left was ours—we sat on the loading dock behind the chandler's shop with our boots hanging over the canal, sharing the last of the wine, and Astrid said the thing.

They'd been circling it all evening. I'd learned their circling patterns the way you learn weather: the joke that lands slightly wide, the silence a beat too long, the hands busy with nothing.

"The old me," they said finally, to the water. "The one who sold their soul to save a city and cut the memory out so they wouldn't have to live with the weight of it. Everyone keeps asking if I miss them. If I feel like the same person. And the honest answer is—" they turned the bottle in their fingers, "—no. I don't. The person who made that bargain was so tired, Wren. So lonely they thought loneliness was just what they were made of. I'm not them. I remember being them the way you remember a story you read once. And I keep waiting for that to feel like a loss, and it doesn't. It feels like—" they searched, "—like taking off shoes that never fit."

"And what does fit?"

They looked at me. Storm-light moved in their eyes even with their hands empty, which is apparently just how they are now, and which I will never be calm about, ever, and they should know that by now.

"This," they said. "This exact stupid dock. This cheap wine. You." A pause, and then, wry as ever, because they are who they are: "Don't let it go to your head. You were also assigned to me once. I like to think I'd have picked you anyway, but the sample size is compromised."

"You'd have picked me anyway," I said. "I've seen you pick. You picked me when every rational fact pointed the other direction. You shattered a binding rather than choose either of the options you were given. You don't follow scripts, Astrid Whitlock. You never did. That was the whole problem the courts had with you."

"*Former* Whitlock," they corrected, and smiled, and the smile was the freest thing I had ever seen. "New name, new person, remember? Nothing assigned. Everything chosen."

"Everything chosen," I agreed.

Below us the canal carried the market's reflected lights out toward the sea, little flames going somewhere we'd never follow, and above us the sky did its soft flickering thing, and somewhere in my chest, where a whistle used to sit, there was nothing at all—just room. Just the enormous, unwritten room of tomorrow.

"So," Astrid said, leaning their head against my shoulder. "No prophecy. No debt. No court, no registry, no script, no duty. Tomorrow's completely blank." They tilted their face up toward mine, eyes full of borrowed lightning and entirely their own. "What do you want to do with it?"

I considered the question with the gravity it deserved—the first truly open question either of us had ever been asked, the first one with no trap in it, no terms, no fine print, no last page already drawn.

"Everything," I said. "In no particular order. Starting with breakfast."

Astrid laughed, and the storm-light rose off their skin like weather deciding to be kind, and far away over the mountains where a court used to be, the first real rain in a hundred years began to fall on ground that was finally allowed to feel it.

Nothing about tomorrow was written.

We'd make sure of it ourselves.
