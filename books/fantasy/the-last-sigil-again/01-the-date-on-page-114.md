## 1. The Date on Page 114

The light in Vellharrow died the way it always did — slowly, grudgingly, as if the sky were reluctant to admit that a city without magic still deserved a sunset. Odile Eriksen watched it go from behind his stall's crooked awning, folding copper charms into squares of wool with the practiced tenderness of a man handling something alive.

They were not alive. That was the point of them.

He had spent seven years learning to make beautiful things that did absolutely nothing. Love-knots of braided tin that held no binding spell. Small glass eyes that saw nothing and protected no one. A hanging bell he'd tuned by hand so precisely that its silence seemed almost like enchantment — though if a Warden ever struck it, they'd hear only good metalwork, and good metalwork was legal. Barely. The Wardens of Vellharrow burned anything that hummed, and so Odile had taught himself to make things that only whispered, and then things that only breathed, and finally things that kept their mouths shut entirely.

"Evening, Odie." Old Sefa from the pie cart two stalls down was packing up her trays. "You're staying late."

"Big night." He heard his own voice and winced at how much it gave away. "Dinner. With someone."

Sefa's eyebrows climbed toward her kerchief. "The archivist? The tall one who comes by Thursdays and pretends he isn't buying charms for himself?"

"He doesn't pretend. He genuinely believes he's researching folk practices." Odile tucked the last charm into its cloth. "For three months I have sold that man eleven love-knots, and he has never once acknowledged what they're for."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight he asked me to dinner. An actual dinner. At an actual table. With plates."

Sefa laughed, a wheezing, delighted sound. "Seven years in this market and I've never seen you eat with anyone. Go. Before you find another reason to hide behind your little table." She paused, her expression softening into something older. "You deserve an evening, Odie. Whatever else is true about you."

Whatever else is true about you. He smiled at her and did not let the smile reach anything underneath. Seven years, and even kindness in Vellharrow came with a small unopened door in it.

He closed the shutters, locked the stall with a key that turned twice because the lock was cheap, and went home to change his shirt.

* * *

Tomas Vey was waiting outside the Lamplighter's Rest, and the sight of him stopped Odile half a street away, which was embarrassing and true.

Tomas had one of those faces that seemed lit from within by some private, patient lamp — not handsome exactly, or not only, but *present*, the way a hearth is present in a cold room. He wore his archivist's coat brushed clean, and his hair was doing something ambitious near his ear, and when he saw Odile he didn't wave or call out; he just stood there and let his whole face change, quietly, the way weather changes over water.

"You're staring," Odile said, arriving.

"I'm confirming you're real. I've imagined this evening enough times that I wanted evidence." Tomas offered his arm, formal and slightly ridiculous. "May I?"

"You may not. It's too early for that. Save the arm for dessert."

Inside, the Rest smelled of rosemary and tallow smoke, and their corner table had an actual candle — real beeswax, which cost more than either of them should have spent. They ordered wine neither could name. Tomas told a story about a flooded archive sub-basement and a clerk who had tried to dry a century of municipal records in front of the council fire, and Odile laughed until his ribs hurt, genuinely, stupidly, the laugh of a man who had forgotten the muscle existed.

And somewhere between the second cup and the bread, Odile realized he was happy.

It came on him like vertigo. He set his hand flat on the table to steady against it. Happiness, for Odile Eriksen, was a thing that arrived with an invoice attached. He had learned to distrust it the way sailors distrust calm water.

"You've gone somewhere," Tomas said gently.

"Nowhere. Here." He turned his cup. "I'm here. Tell me about the letter you found. You wrote me about it — the collection you're cataloguing. The old private library off Tanner's Row."

Something flickered across Tomas's face — there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "Ah. Yes. That."

"That sounds ominous."

"It's nothing. It's—" He laughed at himself. "I keep deciding to tell you and then deciding dinner first. But we're at dinner now, aren't we. We're at dinner, and I've run out of reasons."

"Tomas."

"I bought you something." He reached into his coat and set a slim parcel on the table, wrapped in brown paper and tied with plain string. "From the collection. It was in a crate of books nobody wanted — moldy psalters, tax rolls, a treatise on drainage. And this."

Odile untied the string slowly. Inside was a book bound in grey leather gone soft with age, its spine cracked, its title stamped in faded silver: *THE TURNING OF THE WHEEL: A CENTURY OF PORTENTS, GATHERED AND SET DOWN BY THE HAND OF MARGIT ERIKSEN.*

The wine in Odile's mouth turned to iron.

His mother's name. His mother's *book*.

"Margit Eriksen," Tomas said quietly. "I looked up the name in the civic registers after I found it. There's no record. No birth, no death, no household. As if the person never existed in Vellharrow at all." He hesitated. "I thought perhaps you might know the family. Given the shop, the charm-work — names travel through trades, sometimes."

"I know the name," Odile said. His voice came out level, which took everything he had. "Where exactly did you find it, Tomas? Which crate? Who owned the collection?"

"A recluse named Aldous Wren. Dead eight years. The city's been liquidating his estate in stages." Tomas leaned forward. "Odile, I've read parts of it. It's strange. Not mad-strange — *precise*-strange. She recorded dates. Weather. Small portents. And every few pages, longer entries. Prophecies, if you want to call them that. Written decades before events that—"

"—that happened," Odile finished. He knew. He knew exactly how precise his mother's hand could be, because he had grown up watching it move across pages just like these, and he had left that house before he was old enough to understand what she was writing down, and he had never once dared open the copies she kept locked in the cedar chest, and he had crossed a border into a magic-free city partly — though he'd never admitted this even to himself — because he did not want to know what she saw coming.

He should have thrown the book in the Rest's hearth. Right then. That would have been the wise act, the exile's act, the act of a man committed to being nobody.

Instead he said, "May I look at it tonight?" and heard himself add, "I'll bring it back tomorrow. To the archive. Properly catalogued."

"Of course." Tomas's smile returned, warm and relieved, as if Odile had accepted something far larger than a book. Perhaps he had. "Though — one thing. Before you go. Page hundred and fourteen. I flagged it with a slip. It's the reason I bought the book instead of leaving it in the crate, honestly. It's the reason I asked you to dinner, if I'm being fully honest, which apparently I'm incapable of being tonight."

"Why? What's on page hundred and fourteen?"

"Read it," Tomas said, "and tell me what you see."

* * *

Odile walked home through streets gone quiet and blue with evening. Vellharrow had no lamps but oil and no stars worth mentioning — the mountain, Mount Sarn, stood east of the city like a closed door, eating half the sky, and the sky it left was thin and washed-out. He carried the ledger inside his coat, against his chest, and he could feel it there the way you feel a stone in your boot: small, insistent, impossible to ignore.

His rooms above the dyer's shop were one candle, one bed, one window facing west. He lit the candle. He sat. For a long moment he simply held the book and listened to the building settle around him.

*She's dead,* he reminded himself. *She has been dead for years. Whatever is in this book cannot reach you.*

Then he opened it to page 114.

The slip Tomas had placed there marked a single entry, dated — and Odile's breath stopped in his throat before his eyes even reached the words beneath it, because the date itself was impossible.

It was today's date. Not approximately. Not the right season, the right month. The exact day. The day-month-year that had begun this morning with him burning porridge and ended tonight with wine and a candle and a man whose face changed like weather over water.

His hands were shaking. He made himself read.

*On this date, in the year the river fails,* his mother had written, in her tight, slanted hand, *the debt comes due in the low city. Two men sit at one table. One is the thief who returns, though he does not yet know what he stole. The other is owed, and will be collected. The court beneath the mountain keeps its ledgers better than any archivist, and it does not forget a bargain signed in blood and butter.*

*The thief will go down after it. That is written too. What he brings back —*

The entry broke off there. Not faded. Broken off, mid-sentence, the ink stopping as though her hand had lifted from the page and never come back down. Below it, in a different ink, darker, newer-looking than anything else in the book, someone had added a single line:

*TWO MEN. ONE DEBT. PAID ON THIS DATE.*

Odile read it four times. The candle burned steadily. The building settled. Somewhere below, the dyer's dog dreamed and whined.

*Two men sit at one table.* He and Tomas, at the corner table with the beeswax candle. Tonight.

*One is owed, and will be collected.* Tomas. The word *collected* sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

*The thief who returns, though he does not yet know what he stole.* Odile read that phrase again and again, hunting for another meaning in it, some other reading, some way it pointed anywhere other than at the exiled storm-caller selling mute charms seven years in a city that burned anything that hummed. A thief. Him. He had stolen nothing in his life except — and here a door in his mind slammed shut, hard, the way it always did, the way it had since the trial, since the rite, since the border crossing — except something he could not remember stealing.

That was the cruelty of the stripping. They hadn't taken the memory itself. They'd taken the path to it. He knew there was a room in his past with the door bricked over; he could feel the shape of the room through the wall. Every time he pressed his ear against the brick, he heard only weather.

"The court beneath the mountain," he said aloud, testing the words. Mount Sarn. The dead mountain that ate half the sky, that no one mined because no one could say why, that the oldest residents wouldn't look at directly. Children's rhymes about it. *Under Sarn, under Sarn, the quiet ones keep what's sworn.*

His mother had believed in the quiet ones. Had he ever known that, or was he inventing it now?

He got up and paced, and the pacing became frantic, and he caught himself at the window with his palm already raised toward the eastern dark — the old instinct, the forbidden geometry, fingers beginning to arrange themselves into a calling shape before he strangled the gesture and shoved his hand into his pocket like a boy caught reaching for sweets.

No storms. No workings. No humming. Seven years of discipline, and a book had nearly undone it in an hour.

Think. Think like a man with no magic, because that is what you are.

Facts. One: his mother had written prophecies that came true. Two: she had written one for tonight. Three: it concerned two men at a table — and Tomas had found this exact book, in a dead stranger's crates, and brought it to Odile specifically, and flagged this specific page, and asked him to dinner on this specific date. Four—

Four. Odile went very still.

Tomas had said: *it's the reason I asked you to dinner.*

Not: I found the book and thought of you. Not: coincidence, chance, accident. He had *flagged the page*. He had chosen the date. Odile turned the thought over slowly, the way you turn a key you've found in the street, wondering what it opens.

"Tell me what you see," Tomas had said. As if he knew what was on page 114. As if he'd read it long before tonight.

As if he'd been *counting toward it*.

Odile looked down at the ledger in his hands, at his mother's broken-off sentence — *what he brings back* — and felt wonder curdle into dread so completely that the two became indistinguishable, a single taste in his mouth like coins and rain.

Because there was a fifth fact, wasn't there. The one under all the others. The one the brick wall hummed against when the wind blew from the east.

Somewhere behind it, in the bricked-over room, something in him already knew what he had stolen.

And the mountain, according to his dead mother, was coming tonight to collect.

A knock at his door — three soft raps, unhurried, familiar — made him spin around with the ledger clutched to his chest like a shield.

Through the wood, gentle as the weather over water: "Odile? I couldn't stand letting the evening end yet. Do you have a minute?"

Tomas's voice. Warm. Patient.

And beneath it, so faint Odile might have invented it, something else — a sound like frost forming, like paper turning, like a very large ledger opening somewhere far beneath the earth.
