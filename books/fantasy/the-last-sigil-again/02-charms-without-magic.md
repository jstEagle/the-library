## 2. Charms Without Magic

The ledger sat in Odile's lap like a sleeping animal.

He had carried it home from the dinner — Tomas had insisted, pressing it back into his hands at the door with a look that said *you were meant to have this* — and now he sat on the narrow bed above his stall with the candle guttering and page 114 still open, because closing it felt like agreeing to something.

Tonight's date. Tonight's exact date, inscribed a hundred years before either of them was born. Two men and a debt. He had read the entry eleven times. It did not change. It did not soften. The ink had gone brown with age, but the words beneath it were as sharp as the day some dead hand had set them down: *the thief who returns*, and a name beside it that made his chest go strange and tight every time he looked at it, because it was Tomas's name, written in a book older than Tomas's grandmother, waiting for him like a trap waits for a footstep.

Odile Eriksen had spent seven years learning not to be anyone worth writing down. And here he was, written down.

He turned the page over. He turned it back. He held it to the light as if the paper might confess to being ordinary — cheap rag stock, watermarked with nothing, the kind of ledger any merchant in Vellharrow might use for grain tallies. But merchants' ledgers didn't do this. Merchants' ledgers didn't know things that hadn't happened yet.

And then he stopped looking at what the book said and started looking at how it said it.

The handwriting was cramped, practical, slanted hard to the right — a hand trained for speed, for recording, for getting the truth down before the truth could be disputed. But it was the spellings that caught him. The old inflected endings, kept long after the rest of the world had sanded them smooth. *Wæter* for water. *Gast-bond* instead of the common *soul-deed*. A genitive construction that no one south of the Sarnfells had used in living memory, doubled up the way mountain people did when they wanted a thing unmistakably possessed: *the court's-own hall.*

Odile went very still.

He knew that dialect. He had grown up inside it. It was the language of his mother's kitchen, of her curses and her lullabies, the speech she slipped into when she was tired or angry or afraid — which, in the last years, had been most of the time. It was the dialect of the high valleys under Mount Sarn, where the wind came off the icefields and everything people owned they owned in grammar first, because the courts took everything else.

His mother had written this book.

No. His mother had been barely literate in the trade-tongue; she'd signed her name with a laborious, beautiful care, like a woman carving it into stone she expected to outlast her. This hand was fluent, fast, professional. Not hers. But it was her tongue, her valley, her people. Someone from home. Someone who spoke the way she spoke when the doors were shut.

This book came from Mount Sarn.

He set it down on the blanket as though it had grown warm. Then he picked it up again, because of course he did; you don't put down the only letter you've ever gotten from home, even if the letter is a threat.

Seven years. Seven years since he had heard that dialect spoken aloud by anything other than his own memory. Vellharrow was magic-free by law and nearly so by fact — a gray, decent, stubborn city built on trade routes that had once run through lands where wonders were common, until the Wardens came and burned the wonder out. Now Vellharrow sold rope and salt and honest iron, and its one law above all others was simple enough for a child to recite:

Nothing hums.

Anything that hums burns.

* * *

He had learned the rule his first week, watching from the market square with a bundle of unsold charms under his arm and no idea yet how to sell charms that weren't.

The Wardens worked in threes. Gray coats, ash-colored hoods, faces kept carefully forgettable — that was official policy, Odile would later learn; Warden recruits were chosen partly for faces you couldn't hold onto. They moved down the stalls of the Charmers' Row, which was a joke of a name if ever there was one, because nothing on that row had worked in years. Every seller there dealt in inert trinkets: knots tied in patterns that used to mean protection, glass beads pressed in molds that used to hold luck, copper rings wound with wire in spirals that used to sing.

Used to. That was the whole economy of the Row — nostalgia sold as ornament. People bought the shape of magic the way you buy a painting of a place you can never go back to.

A woman three stalls down from where Odile stood had a bird. A wren, small and brown, perched on her shoulder, and it was singing — except that it wasn't a song, not really. There was a thread of something underneath the notes, a frequency that made the teeth ache and the candle flames lean. Old weather-magic, half-feral, living in the bird's throat the way lice live in a coat.

The Wardens heard it before anyone else did. They always heard it first; that was the other thing about their forgettable faces, the thing people whispered about — that whatever they'd traded for their work, hearing wasn't part of the price.

They didn't argue with the woman. They didn't give her a chance to plead, though she tried, holding the wren out toward them, saying it was just a bird, just an old bird, it had been in her family for —

They burned it in a bronze brazier they carried between them, right there in the square, while the whole Row watched and pretended to arrange their goods. The wren sang louder as it burned, and the song got stranger, wilder, full of storm-sounds, and Odile stood in the crowd with his fists clenched around his own bundle of trinkets and understood two things at once, with terrible clarity.

First: the wren had sounded like home.

Second: he could never, ever make anything here hum.

So he hadn't. Seven years of discipline. Seven years of selling knots that were only knots, beads that were only glass, and being very, very careful never to let his craft wake up. A storm-caller who never called storms. A maker whose hands stayed deliberately stupid. Some nights the effort of it sat on his chest like a wet wool coat, this constant, exhausting pretending-not-to-be, this life spent at half a voice. But the alternative was a brazier in the square.

He had told himself, all these years, that exile was exile. That home had cast him out and home was gone and the dialect was gone with it, locked away in the attic of his skull where he kept everything else from before.

Now the attic had mailed him a letter.

* * *

He read the ledger until the candle drowned in its own wax, and then he lit another and read more, because sleep was not going to come anyway, not with tonight's date sitting on page 114 like a stone on a grave.

It wasn't only the entry. It was the margins. Whoever had written this book — this record of prophecies, this accounting of things to come — had annotated it the way a merchant annotates prices. Little notes in that northern hand, in that northern tongue. *Confirmed twice.* *Watch the third winter.* Beside one entry near the front: *the court does not forgive deferral.* Beside another: *ask the stair.*

Ask the stair. Ask what stair?

And threaded through everything, recurring like a refrain in a dirge, one word he knew too well: *Eriksen.*

His own name, scattered through a century-old prophecy like seeds through flour. Once as a signature. Once as a warning. Once, on a page he had to read four times before his eyes would accept it, in a phrase that translated itself into his mother's voice whether he wanted it to or not:

*What the Eriksons bought, the Eriksons will pay for.*

Bought. Bought what? Bought when? His family had been poor as frost-poor could be — poor the way mountain families were poor, rich in weather and nothing else. What could an Eriksen possibly have bought from an under-court beneath Mount Sarn, and what could the payment possibly be, that a book written before his birth already knew his name?

He thought of his mother's hands. He thought of the last time he'd seen her — the gate, the rain, her mouth opening around words she couldn't say in front of the marshals, words that had come out wrong, flattened into the trade-tongue, stripped even of her own grammar. As if the exile had taken the dialect too. As if they had salted the earth of her speech behind him.

She had said: *Go, and don't write.*

He had obeyed. Seven years, no letters, no word north, nothing. Because writing would have been a thread, and threads get followed, and everything he loved lived at the end of them.

But someone from home had written. Someone had filled a ledger with tomorrow, bound it, and somehow — he turned to the flyleaf, and there it was, the thing he'd been avoiding looking at directly all night, a bookseller's mark stamped faintly in the corner, a mountain goat standing on a crag above three initials worn almost to nothing — someone had sent it south. Into Vellharrow. Into the one city where such a book, humming as it plainly did with futures, should have been fed to a brazier within a day of crossing the walls.

Unless the Wardens couldn't hear it. Unless a book of prophecies hums at a pitch only certain ears catch.

Unless, said a cold small voice in the back of his head, it was sent to exactly one pair of ears.

He looked at the entry again. *The thief who returns.* He thought about the word *returns*. You could only return to a place you had left. The prophecy assumed he would go back. Back north. Back to the valleys, back under the mountain, back into the grammar of his childhood.

He had sworn on the gate, with the rain coming down and his name being struck from the rolls behind him, that he would die in Vellharrow rather than look at those peaks again.

The candle flickered. Outside, Vellharrow lay silent under its usual starless haze, decent and gray and safe, seven years of safety stacked around him like sandbags against a flood.

And underneath the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth because he had never quite been able to throw it away, sat the one object from his old life he'd allowed himself to keep: a charm of his own making, the last thing his hands had ever sung into being, inert for seven years, dormant, obedient, harmless.

As he sat there with the ledger open on his knees, in the deep dead hour past midnight, Odile heard — faint, impossible, unmistakable — a sound from beneath the floor.

A hum.

* * *

He was down the ladder with the candle before he'd decided to move, heart going like a drum in a procession, and he knelt on the cold boards of the dark shop and pulled up the loose plank with fingers that shook and didn't care that they shook.

The oilcloth was damp. The charm inside was a small thing, palm-sized, a knot of braided silver wire around a core of mountain stone, made in the shape his mother had taught him, the pattern for calling rain to a dry field. His masterpiece, once. His shame, now. He had made it the week before everything ended, and he had never been able to burn it himself, and so it had lain under his floor through seven years of silence like a held breath.

It was humming.

Softly. Barely audible. A note so low he felt it in his back teeth more than heard it, a thread of sound rising out of the stone like heat off summer road. And as he held it in his cupped hands in the dark, the hum changed — steadied, gathered, became almost structured, almost like —

Almost like a word.

He knew the pattern. Rain-calling was the first working his mother had ever taught him; you learned it young in the valleys, because a dry year up there killed faster than any war. The braid, the spiral, the stone — it was a question asked of the sky. *Come. Fall. Feed us.*

Except he was indoors, underground, in a magic-free city, asking nothing of anybody.

Which meant the question was coming from the other direction. Something was asking the sky to come to *him*. Or something was using his own old craft, his own old pattern, his own forgotten, buried, contraband little prayer, as a bell — ringing it to see where he was.

The hum climbed. The candle flame leaned toward the charm, then bent away from it, confused.

And Odile Eriksen, who had spent seven years being no one, who had unlearned his own name syllable by syllable until he could walk through Vellharrow without leaving a ripple, sat on the floor of his dark shop holding the last piece of who he had been, and listened to it call across a hundred miles of distance to whatever waited at the other end.

He should smash it. He should feed it to the stove right now, plank and all, and sweep the ash out with the morning dust, and tomorrow he would tell Tomas the dinner was pleasant but there was nothing between them, better this way, and he would go back to selling knots that were only knots until the day he died safe and small and unwritten in any book.

The hum rose another fraction. Underneath it now, so faint he might have invented it, something answered from far away. A second note. A third. Like voices finding each other across a valley.

Like a choir tuning.

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a dog began to bark, and then another, and then the whole city's dogs, all at once, all in the same direction — north, toward the black mass of Mount Sarn just visible beyond the walls, sitting under its stars like a closed door.

Odile wrapped the charm back in its oilcloth with hands that had gone steady, the way hands do when the moment for shaking has passed. He slid the plank home. He climbed the ladder. He put the ledger in the strongbox under the bed and lay down on top of it, clothed, boots on, the way soldiers sleep.

Tomorrow, he would find Tomas and ask him the only question that mattered: why him, why now, why this book, why this date.

Because somewhere under the floor, muffled by oak and earth and seven careful years, the last sigil of Odile Eriksen was still singing.

And somewhere far to the north, something had begun to sing back.
