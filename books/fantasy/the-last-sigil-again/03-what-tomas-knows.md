## 3. What Tomas Knows

The restaurant was called The Copper Kettle, which Tomas had always thought a dishonest name, because the only copper in it was a dented kettle on a high shelf that nobody had used in living memory. Everything else was tin and iron and the gray light of Vellharrow's oil lamps. But it had a corner table away from the door, and it had bread that arrived warm if you timed your order to the baker's second batch, and tonight it had Odile sitting across from him with his sleeves rolled up and flour from his own stall still dusted along one forearm like he hadn't noticed it.

Tomas had been rehearsing this for eleven days.

He knew how it sounded. He had turned the sentence over so many times it had gone smooth as river stone: *I didn't find you by accident.* Eleven days of walking past the charm stall without stopping, then stopping, then buying a luck-charm he did not believe in for two coppers he could not spare, then coming back the next day because Odile had laughed at something a customer's child said and Tomas had felt the sound land somewhere beneath his ribs and stay there. Eleven days of telling himself he would explain before things went any further than bread and conversation.

And now they had gone further than bread and conversation — further than he'd planned, faster than he'd planned — because Odile had looked at him across this table twenty minutes ago with an expression Tomas couldn't name and said, quietly, "Ask me why I came to Vellharrow," and Tomas had heard the loneliness under it and wanted, badly, stupidly, to answer honestly.

The letter was in his inside pocket. It had been there all evening, a small rectangular weight against his heart, and every time he shifted in his chair he felt it like a confession waiting to be made.

"You're doing the thing again," Odile said. He was tearing a piece of bread with great concentration. "Where you look at me and then decide not to say whatever you were about to say."

"Do I do that?"

"Constantly. It's becoming a whole personality." But he smiled when he said it, and the smile made it worse, because Tomas liked him — genuinely, helplessly liked him — and what he had to say might end this before it had properly begun.

The kettle on its shelf caught the lamplight. Somewhere in the kitchen a pan clattered, and a woman's voice called out an order, and outside the window the market was closing down stall by stall, awnings folding like wings, the day being put away. An ordinary evening. That was the thing about ordinary evenings; you never knew you were standing in the last one until you were standing in a different kind entirely.

"My grandmother died in the spring," Tomas said.

Odile set down the bread. "I'm sorry."

"She raised me. My parents died when I was small — fever year, the year the wells went bad — so it was her. She was..." He searched for it. Everyone reached for *strict* or *kind* and she had been neither, exactly. "She was precise. She believed the world kept accounts. Debts and payments and interest accruing in the dark while you slept. I thought it was just how old people talked. She'd grown up north, near Mount Sarn, before she came south, and she still had the accent, and she still—" He stopped.

"Still what?"

"Touched iron before she'd say anything important. Knocked on wood twice. Little superstitions, except she never treated them as superstitions. She treated them as procedure." He drew a breath. "When she was dying, she gave me a letter. Sealed. She said, *When the mountain writes to you, open this.* And I said, Grandmother, mountains don't write, and she looked at me — you know how she could look, like she was measuring you for something — and she said, *They will.*"

Odile had gone very still. Not dramatically still, not a performer's stillness. Just — the way water goes still before it freezes. Tomas watched it happen and hated himself for what he was about to do and did it anyway, because he had promised himself honesty before anything else, and because some part of him, the part that had grown up under a precise old woman who touched iron before speaking, understood that the truth here was load-bearing.

He took the letter from his pocket and laid it on the table between them.

It was heavy paper, cream-colored, gone soft at the folds from years of being carried. The wax seal had been broken carefully, hinge-cracked rather than shattered, so the seal itself remained whole: a circle crossed by three descending lines, like rain falling through a ring.

He watched Odile look at it. Watched him go from stillness into something beyond stillness — a man listening to a sound no one else can hear.

"Where did you get this seal," Odile said. His voice was very level, the way voices get when they're holding something heavy at arm's length. "This is a wax impression. Someone pressed their actual signet into this."

"It was hers. Or — she had it. A ring she never wore. She kept it wrapped in cloth at the bottom of her sewing box, and once, when I was nine, I found it and asked whose it was, and she took it from me and put it away and didn't speak to me for a full day. First and only time." Tomas turned the letter so it faced him across the table. "I opened the letter six weeks ago. The night after our third dinner. I don't know why that night. It just felt — due."

"What does it say?"

Tomas had read it so many times he could recite it with his eyes closed, but he unfolded it anyway, smoothed it flat beside the bread and the wine, because some things needed to be seen in their own handwriting. The ink had faded to the color of dried lavender.

*To my grandson, if the mountain comes for him.*

*You will have been told all your life that magic is a rumor and Vellharrow is safe. Both are true the way a locked door is true. I paid a great deal to keep that door shut behind us, and I am sorry for what the payment will cost you, because debts of this kind do not die with the debtor. They wait. They mature like fruit, patient and sweet-seeming, and then they fall.*

*If frost comes to your window in shapes, if you dream of stairs going down, if a ledger opens in your hands — do not run south. Running is a payment plan and the mountain accepts payment plans gladly. Go instead to the market district and find the charm-seller with the crooked awning. Do not tell him first. Let him come to trust you, because he trusts no one, and he is right not to.*

*His name is Eriksen, though he will not know it, and he is the storm-caller, though he cannot call storms anymore, and he is the only person alive who has stood in the under-court of Mount Sarn and walked out again. He owes our family a debt older than your life. Ask him for it.*

*Forgive me. Forgive him. Forgive me twice, because I am asking both of you to pay for my arithmetic.*

*Your grandmother,*

*Astrid, who signed more than her name*

Tomas finished reading aloud and looked up.

The silence at the table had changed texture. It wasn't the comfortable silence of two men sharing bread anymore. It was the silence of a room where something has been said that cannot be unsaid.

Odile was looking at the letter the way a man looks at a wound he thought had healed years ago, newly opened, still bleeding after all.

"The crooked awning," Tomas said softly. "That's yours."

"I know that's mine." Odile's hand had moved — Tomas saw it happen, saw him catch himself — halfway toward the letter, then withdrawn to rest flat on the table instead, fingers spread, pressing down as if the table might buckle. "Astrid. Her name was Astrid." He said it strangely. Testing it. Like a word in a language he'd been told he'd forgotten. "Eriksen," he said next, and flinched at his own voice saying it.

"You didn't know?" And then, because the question was absurd the moment it left him: "No. Of course you didn't know. You can't have known. You would never have let me—"

"Let you what." Not a question. A blade laid flat on the table.

"Sit with you. Talk to you. Come here." Tomas's own voice was rising and he pulled it down. "Odile. I sought you out. Before I ever spoke to you I knew your stall, your hours, the way you price things too low for strangers and too high for people who look like they can afford it. I chose you. Every dinner, every walk home, every — everything — started with a letter and a debt I didn't understand and a dead woman's instructions."

He had expected anger. He had braced for it, rehearsed for it, built little walls in his mind for it. What he had not prepared for was the way Odile simply closed his eyes for a moment, the way a man does when a blow lands somewhere he'd already been struck.

"The date," Odile said.

"What?"

"The date we met. The eleventh." His eyes opened. "Show me the letter again. The fold. Where she dated it, if she dated it—"

"There's no date. Why does the—" Tomas stopped. Because he remembered now, remembered with a cold sliding feeling, the ledger he'd glimpsed once in Odile's stall, the old book the charm-seller handled like something venomous, the page Odile had snapped shut when Tomas leaned too close. He had assumed it was inventory records. Accounts. A man like Odile would keep accounts.

"Eleven days ago," Odile said, mostly to himself. "I bought this dinner a week ago. I planned it. I planned it around the date because I'd already seen the date, written in a hundred-year-old book, in handwriting I recognized, describing two men and a debt coming due. I invited you here knowing. I've spent eleven days letting you—" His jaw worked. "I thought I was walking into a prophecy. And the whole time, you were walking into a letter. We were both summoned, Tomas. By different hands, to the same table."

"That's not—"

"Astrid signed more than her name." Odile picked up the letter at last, held it to the lamp, tilted it. The light caught something Tomas had never seen in six weeks of reading it — faint impressions in the paper above the signature, the ghost of a second writing pressed through from another sheet once laid beneath. "There's a co-signature. There was a contract. This isn't a grandmother's goodbye, it's a receipt." His thumb moved over the seal, over the three descending lines through the ring, and something crossed his face too fast and too old to be surprise. Grief, maybe. Recognition. "Rain through a ring. That's a storm-sign. That's a northern storm-sign, the kind the Wardens burn people for, and your grandmother wore it hidden in a sewing box in a city where nothing is allowed to hum."

"She was protecting me."

"She was *paying* with you." It came out harsher than Odile perhaps intended — Tomas saw him hear it, see it land — but he didn't take it back. Instead he set the letter down between them with terrible gentleness, the gentleness of a man handling something that might detonate. "Tomas. Listen to me, because I need to say this while I still can. Whatever you feel about these dinners — whatever this has been — I need you to know that I wasn't pretending. Even knowing the date, even expecting doom at the end of it. The last eleven days have been the first time in seven years that I haven't felt like furniture in my own life."

Tomas's throat was tight. "Mine either."

"So I'm going to be angry about the letter later. Possibly at you, possibly at your grandmother, possibly at the mountain. But right now I need you to answer one question, and I need you to think hard, because everything depends on it." Odile leaned forward. The lamplight carved his face into planes and shadow. "Has anything strange happened? Frost. Dreams. Stairs. A feeling of being *filed*, like a document someone's about to retrieve."

Tomas laughed — a short, broken sound, because it was that or scream. "Three nights ago I dreamed of a hall. Underground. Enormous. Shelves going up farther than I could see, and every shelf full of... I want to say books. Papers. And a clerk made of ash asked me to state my name for the record, and when I woke up my window was frosted. From the inside. In June." He swallowed. "It spelled something. I thought I imagined it."

"What did it spell?"

"*Received.*"

Odile sat back slowly. Outside, the last stall in the market folded its awning, and the streetlamps buzzed, and somewhere a dog began barking at nothing, at the dark, at the mountain far to the north that no one in Vellharrow ever looked at directly, the way you don't look at a creditor.

"Then it's begun," Odile said quietly. "The mountain has received you." He gathered the letter, folded it along its worn creases, and — Tomas would remember this detail forever, the smallness of it — slid it back across the table to Tomas rather than keeping it. "Finish your wine. Then we're going back to my stall, because there's a book you need to see, and a date on page one hundred fourteen that I've been dreading alone for a week, and I think—" He stopped. Started again, steadier. "I think your grandmother and my family made a bargain together once, and I think we're the invoice."

Tomas drained the glass. His hand shook; the wine trembled like a thing alive.

"One more question," he said, as Odile dropped coins on the table and rose. "The prophecy. The one in your book. When it named the two men and the debt — did it say what happens to us?"

Odile paused at the edge of the lamplight, half in, half out, and for a moment he looked every year of his exile, every mile of the road that had brought him to a crooked awning in a city without magic.

"No," he said. "But it named me 'the thief who returns.' And Tomas — I don't steal. I never have." His eyes went, briefly, involuntarily, to the north window, to the black shape of Mount Sarn against the stars. "Which means sometime soon, I'm going to learn."
