## 4. The Warden's Knock

The knock came at the wrong hour.

That was the first thing Odile noticed — before the shouting, before the smell of pitch and burning hemp threading through the evening air of the Nightmarket. Wardens kept schedules. Vellharrow's Wardens were creatures of paperwork and ritual, and their raids happened the way tides happened: predictable, announced in their way, always at dawn when the stalls stood empty and nothing flammable was within arm's reach of anything else. A dawn raid was bureaucracy. A raid at dusk, with the market full and the lanterns lit, was something else entirely.

He had been folding his awning when he heard it. Three slow knocks on the market's eastern gatepost, spaced like a heartbeat that had learned patience. Every charm-seller in Vellharrow knew that rhythm. It meant *we are not hurrying for you*. It meant *you are already caught*.

Odile's hands went still on the canvas.

Around him the market did what markets do, which is nothing at first — that terrible held-breath pause where two hundred people pretend simultaneously that they have not heard what they heard. A fishwife mid-haggle let her voice trail off into the dusk. A child stopped chasing a paper windmill. Somewhere near the fountain, a fiddle player drew one last note out of her bow and then, wisely, put the instrument away.

Then the second knock came, and the market broke apart like a dropped plate.

* * *

Seven years in this city had taught Odile exactly three things worth knowing, and the first was this: never run. Running marked you. The Wardens had boys on the rooftops whose whole trade was watching for the ones who ran, and they were paid per catch. So Odile did what he had practiced in his head a hundred times, in the grey hours before opening, lying awake in his rented room above the dye-shop: he finished folding his awning. He stacked his trays. He closed his stall the way he closed it every night, with the small mechanical tenderness of a man who loved his work, because the man who loved his work was the only man he was allowed to be.

Inside, under the counter, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a false bottom that smelled of dried lavender, lay a book.

He had told himself all day that he would get rid of it. All through dinner — the long table by the window at the Saltcellar, Tomas across from him turning his cup in circles, the conversation moving so carefully around the thing between them that it barely left room for the food — Odile had been composing the sentence. *I found a book. It's old. It might be nothing.* And then, later, after Tomas had shown him the letter with the Eriksen seal and the world had tilted on its axis: *I need to burn this, and I need to burn it now.*

He hadn't burned it. That was the truth he would carry into whatever came next. When the moment came — standing in the alley behind the Saltcellar with the ledger heavy under his coat, a brazier right there, an easy ten seconds from ashes — he had looked at the fire and thought about page 114, about the two names written in his mother's dialect, about the word *debtor*, and his hands had refused. His hands had tucked the book back against his ribs like a child hides stolen bread, and walked home through streets that suddenly felt mapped.

Now the Wardens were coming down the market row, and the book was under the floorboard of his stall, and the false bottom smelled of lavender, and Odile Eriksen stood in front of his little kingdom of inert trinkets and tried to look like a man with nothing to hide.

There were six of them. He counted out of habit, the way other men counted exits. Six Wardens in their slate-grey coats with the ash-white cuffs, moving down the row in the loose formation of men who have done this often enough to stop caring how it looks. At their center walked a tall woman with a ledger-chain at her belt — an Auditor, then, not just muscle. Auditors didn't come for humming charms. Auditors came for histories.

"Warden's business," called the nearest Warden, in the flat tone of a man reciting weather. "All vendors remain at your stalls. All goods remain visible. Anyone who moves will be moved."

The market arranged itself into silence.

They started at the far end. Odile watched them work the way he imagined a condemned man watches the headsman sharpen his blade: with a horrible professional interest. The procedure was old and well-oiled. A junior Warden would lift each item from a stall while the vendor stood with hands visible; the Auditor would pass her palm above it, close enough not to touch; if her palm stayed quiet, the item went back. If her palm *sang* —

He'd seen a singing once, years ago, his first winter in Vellharrow. A glass-blower down on Tallow Street had a kiln-charm that had woken up somehow, some residual heat of making that no one had intended, and it hummed when the Auditor passed. Just hummed. A note, thin as wire. They had taken the kiln out into the square and broken it with hammers while the glass-blower stood there holding his apron in both fists like a man holding his own throat. Then they took the apron too, because everything the maker touched became evidence. The glass-blower left the city the next week. Odile had never learned where people went when they left Vellharrow that way. There wasn't really a *where*. There was just away.

His own stall would pass. That wasn't arrogance; it was seven years of craft. Nothing on his tables hummed. Nothing on his tables had ever hummed. He made love-charms out of carved bone that carried nothing but the shape of wanting. He made road-luck tokens from river stones that had never known a current but water. He sold protection knots tied from ordinary string with ordinary patience, and if they worked — and people swore they did — it was only because a frightened person carrying something made carefully by human hands is a person who walks a little taller. That was the whole secret of his trade, and he had almost come to believe in it himself, which was its own kind of magic, the harmless kind, the kind Vellharrow permitted.

So his stall would pass. But they weren't here for his stall.

He understood that fully when he saw which direction the Auditor's chain pointed.

* * *

The woman with the ledger-chain stopped three stalls down, in front of Ivet Sarno's candle shop.

Odile's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world.

Ivet was sixty if she was a day, a broad-shouldered woman with flour-white hands and a laugh you could hear from the fountain. She rendered her own wax, poured her own tapers, and sold them cheap to every household on the row. Her candles were famous for one thing only: they burned evenly, without guttering, all the way down. Customers said it like a joke. *Even Ivet's candles behave.* Odile had bought them for years. He had stood at her stall not three nights ago, buying a dozen for the dinner he'd been so nervous about, and Ivet had pressed an extra taper into the bag and said, "For the handsome one. First dinners matter."

She had never sold magic in her life. She wouldn't have known a working if it bit her.

But her candles burned evenly. And in a city where the Wardens' doctrine held that any effect without an obvious cause was a cause suppressed, *evenly* was a word that could kill you.

"No," Ivet was saying, as the junior Warden lifted a taper off her rack. "No, wait — those are wax, that's all, that's just good wax, I strain it twice, ask anyone, ask anyone on the row —"

"Hands visible, mistress."

"I'm not hiding anything. Look at me. Look at my hands." She held them up, flour-pale, trembling now. "Thirty years I've had this stall. Thirty years. My husband poured candles beside me until he died, and his father before him, and not once —"

The Auditor passed her palm over the taper.

From three stalls away, Odile saw it happen. Saw the Auditor's hand go still in the air above the candle. Saw her tilt her head, the small precise motion of a woman listening to something very far away or very deep. Saw her fingers spread, and the faintest shimmer rise off her skin like heat off summer stone.

"Humming," the Auditor said. "Grade two. Residual working, embedded in the medium." She turned to the junior. "Mark the stock."

"That's impossible," Ivet said. Her voice cracked in the middle and kept going anyway, the way voices do when they've spent thirty years being cheerful and don't know another register. "That's — I render my own wax, I strain it twice, there's nothing in it, there's never been anything in it —"

"Ma'am."

"There's *nothing in it*."

"Ma'am, step back from the stock."

And here was the thing Odile would remember afterward, in the long white hours when memory has nothing better to do than replay its cruelest footage: the thing he would remember was not the fire. It was Ivet's face in the moment before the fire — the exact second she stopped arguing and understood. Not understood *what was happening*. Understood *who*. Her eyes came up from the candles, past the Wardens, past the frozen row of vendors, and found Odile across the distance, and for half a heartbeat she looked at him the way you look at a doctor who is reading your results in silence.

Then she looked away, because she was kind, and she did not want him to carry it.

"Clear the row," said the senior Warden.

* * *

They burned the stock in the fountain square, because the square's stones were charmed against spread — the one enchantment Vellharrow allowed itself, the irony of which was lost on no one but spoken of by no one either. The Wardens piled two hundred candles into a wire cage built for exactly this purpose, doused them in something that stank of minerals, and set them alight in front of everyone, because doctrine held that a cleansing witnessed was a cleansing complete.

Two hundred candles burn fast. It should have been over in minutes.

It was not over in minutes.

Because the flame found the wax, and the wax — strained twice, rendered by hand, poured by a dead man's daughter beside a living one — remembered, somehow, some residue of care that even an Auditor's grade-two classification couldn't quite name, and the fire turned colors it should not have turned. Green at the base. A blue so deep it was nearly violet at the tips. The smoke came off it in ribbons that rose straight up in windless air, perfectly vertical, like pillars, like something deliberate, and the crowd made a sound Odile had never heard a crowd make — not screaming, not cheering, something lower, a collective exhale with grief inside it.

Ivet stood at the edge of the square with her arms crossed hard over her chest, watching thirty years go up in colors. She did not cry. Odile wished she would cry. Crying would have been easier to stand next to.

When it was done, a junior Warden handed her a paper — the confiscation notice, the bill for the cleansing, because Vellharrow made you pay for your own execution of property — and Ivet took it, and folded it once, precisely, and put it in her apron pocket the way she'd fold a receipt for flour. Then she picked up her empty crate, and her empty racks, and walked back down the row past her darkened stall, and past Odile's, without looking at him at all.

That was worse. He wanted her to spit at him. Spitting would have assigned guilt somewhere outside his own chest, and God knew he needed it assigned anywhere else.

* * *

He closed the stall properly. He did not know why he bothered — some animal instinct about being seen doing normal things — but he locked the shutters and hung the little wooden sign with its painted sun, the sign he'd whittled himself his first month in the city, and he walked home the long way, past the fountain square, where the colored smoke still ghosted above the stones though the fire had been cold for an hour.

The safe city was never safe. He had known that the way you know the floor will hold you: abstractly, constantly, uselessly. Seven years he had lived inside Vellharrow's doctrine of *no magic*, and he had mistaken the doctrine's silence for peace. But silence is what a held breath sounds like. Every stall on that row was one Auditor's palm away from ruin, and everyone knew it, and everyone smiled at each other over the counters anyway, because what else was there — and underneath all that smiling, apparently, there had always been the informing. Someone had told the Wardens something. Not about Ivet. About him.

He was sure of it now, and the certainty arrived with the specific taste of betrayal, which is metallic and familiar. The raid had started three stalls down from his — close enough to be aimed at him, far enough to be deniable. The Auditor had not touched his stock yet; they'd been interrupted by the crowd gathering at the burning, and the senior Warden had called it, saying they'd return tomorrow with "a full audit of the row," which was a phrase that meant *you especially*. But Odile had seen the Auditor's ledger-chain. Seen the tag hanging off it. Tags on that chain listed targets, and targets had descriptions, and when she'd turned to mark Ivet's stock he had glimpsed the topmost tag swinging free of the clasp, ink-side out.

It had read: *charm-seller, east row, male, 30s. Suspected concealment. Verify prior report re: humming item.*

A humming item. His stall held nothing that hummed. Nothing had ever hummed. Which meant the report was false — or meant that whoever had reported him had reported something they shouldn't have known existed, something that *did* hum, something old and paper-bound and written in a dialect only one family still spoke, something currently wrapped in oilcloth under a lavender-scented false bottom three feet from where the Auditor would stand tomorrow morning.

Someone knew about the ledger. And they hadn't gone to Odile. They'd gone to the Wardens.

He climbed the stairs to his room above the dye-shop with his heart going like a trapped bird. The room was cold. He lit no lamp — lamplight in windows drew rooftop eyes on nights like this — and stood in the dark listening to the city settle, the distant fiddle starting up again near the fountain because markets heal fast or pretend to, and below it, fainter, the sound of Ivet's shutter closing, one last time, on a stall that would stand empty by morning.

Maren, he thought. Or someone like Maren. Someone close. You didn't learn about a hidden ledger in a false-bottomed drawer by passing a stall. You learned it by being trusted. By being let behind the counter. By being the one friend in seven years that a man with no history allowed himself.

He sat down on the edge of the bed in the dark and put his head in his hands and let himself feel it for exactly as long as it took the feeling to become useless. Fear first — cold, complete, the fear of a man whose seven-year architecture of safety had shown itself to be a house of cards standing in a draft. Then guilt, arriving behind the fear like a debt collector, whispering its arithmetic: *Ivet's candles burned tonight because of you. She'll pay the confiscation fee because of you. She looked at you like a doctor reading results, and she looked away to spare you, and you will never be able to repay that, and she knows it, and that's why she won't look at you again.*

And then, last, quieter than either, a third thing that frightened him more than the Wardens did.

Because when he lifted his head, his wrist was aching. Not the ache of strain or cold. A deep, structural ache, like a bone remembering a break. He pushed back his sleeve in the dark, knowing what he would find and finding it anyway: nothing. Bare skin. The sigil had been stripped seven years ago along with everything else; the rite had seen to that; there was nothing on his wrist but the small pale shine of old scarlessness.

And yet it burned. Faintly, under the skin, at the edge of perception — a warmth shaped like a circle, like a seal, like a signature — and with it, drifting up through the ache like bubbles through dark water, a smell that had no business in a rented room above a dye-shop in a city where nothing magical survived the night.

Ozone. The smell of a storm deciding.

Outside his window, high over Vellharrow, above the roofline, above the smoke-stained square where Ivet's candles had burned in colors, the clouds had begun to gather.
