## 5. Beneath the Mountain, A Ledger of Souls

Tomas had never dreamed in color before.

That was the first wrong thing. His dreams, when they came at all, were the gray murk of an ordinary mind tidying itself — fragments of the ferry docks where he'd worked as a boy, his grandmother's kitchen with its perpetually boiling pot, faces he'd half-forgotten saying things that meant nothing. Gray, all of it, like Vellharrow itself under its permanent lid of cloud.

But tonight the dream opened in green.

Not the green of grass or leaves. The green of deep water, of light strained through fathoms of it, and he was walking — he understood without being told that he had always been walking, that the walking predated him somehow — down a corridor so tall its ceiling vanished into that drowned emerald dark. The walls were not stone. They were paper. He could see it now as he passed: strata of vellum pressed together like sedimentary rock, a hundred thousand years of layered documents, and written on every exposed surface, in ink of varying ages and colors, were names.

He stopped before one.

*Vellharrow Ferryman's Guild, roll 12, entry 441: Aldric Mott. Term of service: forty years. Status: paid in full.*

He didn't know any Aldric Mott. But reading the entry did something to him anyway — a small cold click behind his sternum, like a lock testing a key. The names on the walls weren't decorations. They were *accounts*. He felt that the way you feel a stare on the back of your neck: not through the eyes but through the skin.

The corridor ended at doors.

They were enormous — each one carved from a single slab of black stone shot through with veins of something that glowed faintly gold, like ore remembering fire — and they stood open, which struck him even in the dream as a terrible courtesy. Things like this were not supposed to be open. Things like this were supposed to be shut forever, and the fact that they weren't meant someone wanted him inside.

He went in. Of course he went in. It was his dream; where else would he go?

*

The hall beyond was a court, though no court Tomas had ever seen described in any of Vellharrow's civic histories. There was no dais, no throne. Instead the space opened upward into darkness and outward into rows upon rows of shelving — great galleries of them, rising in tiers like the amphitheater he'd once visited in Corvane as a child, except these tiers held no seats. They held ledgers. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each spine labeled in script too small and too old to read from the floor, and between the shelves moved figures.

They were made of ash.

That was the only word for it. Not burned — *ashen*, the way a thing looks after fire has taken everything except the shape of its having been there. They were tall and narrow and they drifted rather than walked, their feet never quite disturbing the dustless floor, and their faces were smooth and featureless except for a single horizontal line where a mouth would be, which did not move when they spoke.

And they spoke constantly. Not aloud. The hall hummed with a sound like wind through a reed bed, like pages turning in an infinite room, and Tomas understood that this was their speech — a language of rustle and pressure that arrived in his mind already translated:

*—entry received—*

*—term expired, notice served—*

*—the Eriksen account matures at season's turn—*

He flinched at the last phrase, though he couldn't have said why. It slid past him the way a fish slides past your leg in murky water: there and gone, but leaving the water feeling different.

In the center of the hall stood a desk. Just one, modest, oak-dark, absurdly human amid all that vastness — and behind it sat the only figure in the room that was not ash. It was shaped like a person the way a coat left on a hook is shaped like a person. Its surface was the same layered vellum as the walls, and its face was blank parchment, and when it looked up at him — he knew it looked up at him, the whole hall seemed to tilt slightly toward him when it did — Tomas felt a sensation he would spend the rest of his life failing to describe correctly.

It was not fear. It was *being read*.

"Ah," said the clerk-thing, and its voice was the voice of someone reciting a familiar passage. "The next generation."

"I'm dreaming," Tomas said. His voice sounded small and wet in all that dryness. "This is a dream. I know how dreams work. You're not real."

"Real," the clerk repeated, tasting the word. One of its hands — long, precise, tipped with something like bone — turned toward the shelves behind it, and a ledger detached itself from the row and floated down to land open on the desk. "A common filing error. Dreams are filed under the dreamer. This is filed under the contract." It smoothed the page with one fingertip, gently, the way you'd touch something valuable. "You are simply located here for the duration of the notice period."

"Notice of what?"

The clerk turned the book around so he could see it.

The page showed a name — *Eriksen* — and beneath it columns of figures in that shifting, unreadable script, except that now, looking directly at it, Tomas found he could read it after all. That was worse. Numbers resolved themselves: sums owed, sums deferred, interest accruing across decades like frost spreading over glass. And at the bottom, in fresher ink, still faintly glistening as though written moments ago:

*Maturity date: seven days hence. Payable: one soul, next generation of the co-signatory line.*

"The account has been dormant for sixty years," the clerk said, with something that in a human face might have been satisfaction, or might have been only the absence of anything else. "Dormancy does not forgive. Dormancy accumulates. Your grandmother knew the terms. She initialed each clause herself."

"I never signed anything."

"No," the clerk agreed. "You inherited."

Around him, the ash-figures had stopped moving. All of them, everywhere in those towering galleries, had gone still and silent, their blank faces angled toward the desk, toward him, the way iron filings align around a magnet. The hall held its breath — if a place like this could be said to breathe, which Tomas suddenly suspected it could not, which suspected instead that places like this *inhaled*.

"Why me?" he heard himself ask. "Why now?"

"Because the deferral ends." The clerk rose. It was taller standing than sitting, much taller, and its parchment face caught the green light and gave nothing back. "Because the original debtor's exile term concludes. Because accounts come due when the guarantor can no longer run." A pause, and then, with terrible mildness: "Because someone finally read the book."

*The book.* The word landed in him with a weight out of proportion to its syllables, and he thought — distantly, the way you think things in dreams, where thoughts arrive pre-loaded with meanings you'll only unpack later — of Odile's stall, of the battered ledger the charm-seller kept wrapped in oilcloth beneath his counter, of the way Odile's hands had gone still on their dinner table tonight when Tomas mentioned his grandmother's letter.

"You know him," Tomas said.

"We hold his signature," said the clerk. "We have held it for seven years. We hold many signatures. It is what we are *for*." It extended one bone-tipped finger toward him, not touching, hovering an inch from his chest, and through that inch Tomas felt a cold that had nothing to do with temperature — a cold like arithmetic, like a sum being checked. "But this notice is not about him. Not yet. This notice concerns the collateral."

The finger withdrew. The clerk sat back down, and the hall exhaled — shelves rustling, ash-figures resuming their slow circuits, the reed-wind of voices picking up mid-sentence as though it had never paused.

"You may go," the clerk said. "The summons will follow you. It always follows."

"Tomas."

He hadn't said his own name. He realized that a half-second too late — realized that the voice had come from everywhere and nowhere, from the walls themselves, from the ten thousand ledgers at once, and that every name in this place was spoken the way his had just been spoken: once, precisely, by the architecture that owned it.

"Tomas Vael," said the hall, trying the name against some interior scale. "Filed."

*

He woke with his heart slamming and the taste of dust in his mouth, real dust, gritty between his teeth, though his room held no dust — he'd swept the evening before, he remembered sweeping, he remembered coming home from dinner with Odile and standing in the doorway a moment too long, wanting to say something and saying instead only *goodnight*, safe words, gray words, words from the gray life he'd been living before tonight.

Then he saw the window.

Frost covered the glass. Not the diffuse silvering of a cold night — a design, deliberate, fine-lined, drawn in whorls and barbs like the illumination in an old manuscript. And across the center of it, rendered in strokes so sharp they might have been cut with a knife, was a sentence in a script he had never learned and could nevertheless read perfectly, the way he'd read the ledger in the dream:

*TOMAS VAEL. NOTICE OF MATURITY. PRESENT YOURSELF OR BE COLLECTED. SEVEN DAYS.*

Below the words, in smaller characters, was a seal — a mountain above three descending lines, like a sigil falling into depth — and beneath the seal, almost an afterthought, almost gentle:

*The court thanks you for your attention.*

Tomas sat on the edge of his bed for a long time, watching his breath fog and fade against the frosted glass, unable to look away from it and unable, equally, to stop seeing what was underneath the frost-script, visible only because the candle on his table guttered just then and threw its light sideways across the pane:

The frost was growing. Slowly, patiently, in filigree tendrils reaching inward from the edges of the window, spreading along the frame, down the wall, across the floorboards toward the foot of his bed — writing as it went, tiny letters, hundreds of them, thousands, entries in a ledger that had decided to start keeping records of him in his own room.

He thought of Odile's face at dinner. Of the letter in his coat pocket, sealed with that same falling sigil. Of the way the charm-seller's hands had trembled — not with weakness, Tomas understood now, but with recognition.

Seven days.

He got dressed in the dark. He took the letter from his coat and held it a while without opening it, because opening it felt like signing something, and he had learned tonight exactly what it cost to sign things you didn't understand.

Then he dressed fully, pocketed the letter, blew out the candle, and went out into the sleeping city to find the man who had sold charms that didn't work, and who had known — Tomas was certain of it now, certain with the cold certainty of the frost still creeping across his floor behind him — exactly who Tomas Vael was before they'd ever met.

* * *

The knock, when it came to Odile's door above the shuttered stall, was soft. Almost apologetic.

"Tomas?" Odile's voice, rough with interrupted sleep, through the wood. A pause. Then, sharper, awake all at once: "What happened? What's wrong?"

Tomas stood in the stairwell holding the letter against his chest like a shield, and heard himself say, in a voice steadier than he felt:

"It knows my name. Whatever's underneath the mountain — it wrote to me. In frost. On my own window." He swallowed. "And I dreamed about your ledger, Odile. Before I ever saw it. I dreamed about the court that owns it, and the clerk called me *the next generation*, and it said—" his breath caught, "—it said someone finally read the book. Seven days. That's what I have. Seven days before they collect."

Silence from behind the door. Long enough that Tomas thought, absurdly, that Odile might simply not answer — might pretend no one was knocking, the way he'd pretended for seven years to be no one at all.

Then the bolt drew back, and Odile stood there in the lamplight with the oilcloth-wrapped ledger already under his arm, as though he'd been holding it while he slept.

"I know," Odile said quietly. "I've known since dinner. Come inside. We need to talk about your grandmother — and about what my family did to yours."

Behind them, unnoticed by either man, the first fine tenders of frost had followed Tomas up the stairs — and were now, patient as interest accruing, spreading silently across the threshold of Odile's door.
