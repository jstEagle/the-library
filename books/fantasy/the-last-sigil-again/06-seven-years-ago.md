## 6. Seven Years Ago

The rain had a taste in those last days before the trial — iron and ash, like biting a coin that had passed through a fire. Odile remembered that. He remembered it more clearly than he remembered his own mother's face, which was the first cruelty of memory: it kept what hurt and lost what loved.

He had stood on the trial-stone of Hjertvass for six hours while the elders argued over him as though he were weather they could negotiate with.

* * *

The hall had been built to hold storms. That was the joke of it, though no one laughed anymore. The Vault of Judgement sat at the crest of Mount Sarn's shoulder, its walls ribbed like the inside of a shell, its ceiling open to the sky through a ring of stone so that whatever was judged there was judged under the actual weather — because in Hjertvass, weather was testimony. A clear sky above an accused man meant innocence. Thunder meant guilt. It had never once been wrong, the elders said, in four hundred years of saying it.

Above Odile, on the morning of his trial, the sky did nothing at all. It hung grey and patient, holding its breath, and every hour it refused to break was another hour the elders could not finish their arithmetic.

Six hours. He counted them by his own pulse.

They had taken everything from him before the trial even began — his storm-glass, his rings of knotted silver, the long coat stitched with sigils down the inner lining so that from the outside he looked like any fisherman's son. They took the charms out of his pockets one by one and laid them on a table where they lay dead and small, like insects pinned for study. But they could not take what was already in him. A storm-caller's craft lived in the marrow, in the way his blood moved, in the particular frequency at which his grief resonated. They knew that. Everyone knew that. Which was why the trial was not really about whether he had called the storm.

Everyone knew he had called the storm. The storm had drowned three fields, cracked the ferry bridge at Vold, and put the river through the lower quarter of Hjertvass to its second floor. Forty-one houses. No dead — that was the only mercy in the ledger, and it was a mercy he would spend the rest of his life being unable to explain, because no one wanted to hear that a storm-caller could drown a town and aim the water around every sleeping body in it. Skill was indistinguishable from intent, the elders said. Or rather: skill was worse than intent. Intent might have failed. Skill did not.

So the question before the court was not *did you do this* but *what are we permitted to do with someone who can.*

Odile stood on the trial-stone with his bare feet going numb against the granite and listened to them circle it.

"Stripping," said Elder Rask, who was old enough to remember the last stripping and spoke of it the way other men spoke of famines. "Full rite. Name, craft, record. Let him go into the world as no one."

"Exile follows stripping anyway," said Elder Maren-then-not-his-Maren, some other Maren, a hard-faced woman with frost in her eyebrows. "A man without a name cannot hold land or kin or credit. Exile is just stripping with geography."

"Then why strip him at all?" This from the youngest of them, Elder Sef, whose voice still carried river-country vowels. "Strip him and keep him. A storm-caller without a name is still a storm-caller. We'd be fools to send that away and idiots to keep it angry."

"The Wardens won't have him."

"The Wardens don't have to—"

"He called a storm into a town full of sleeping people," Rask said, flat, final, the voice of a door closing. "Whatever else is true, that is true. There are places for men like that. Places with walls."

Odile said nothing. Speaking was permitted; he had been told his voice was his own until the rite took it. But every word he might offer was a word they would weigh against the drowned fields, and there were no words heavy enough. So he stood, and he watched the sky refuse to confess for him, and somewhere in the sixth hour he stopped listening to the elders and started listening to the sound beneath the trial-stone — a low, patient hum, like a hive, like held breath, like something underneath the mountain turning pages.

Later — much later, after exile, after seven years of selling inert trinkets to people forbidden to believe in them — he would understand that he had heard the under-court that day. That day it had been listening to him too.

* * *

The verdict came at dusk, when the sky finally made up its mind and broke — not into thunder but into a soft, relentless drizzle, which the elders read as sorrow rather than guilt, though Odile always suspected the sky had simply run out of ways to say nothing.

Guilty of calling. Sentence: stripping of name and craft in full rite, followed by perpetual exile beyond the Wardens' border. Effective immediately, because the court feared him even in chains, and fear makes for efficient paperwork.

What none of the sentence recorded — what appeared nowhere in the public reading, the town crier's proclamation, the letter sent to the coastal registries — was what happened in the two hours between verdict and rite. Those two hours belonged to a side chamber below the Vault, lit by one guttering lamp, and they contained three people: Odile, Elder Rask, and a document.

"You should know," Rask said, "that the rite takes everything. Not the surface memories. Everything that touches the thing removed. We will take your name, and with it every moment your name was spoken. We will take your craft, and with it every lesson, every teacher, every night you spent learning to listen to cloud-fronts. You will walk out of here knowing you are exiled and unable to remember why. That is the design. It is considered merciful." His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Mercy has always been our most expensive product."

"I understand," Odile said.

"You don't. No one does, until afterward, and afterward there is no telling anyone." Rask laid the document on the table and turned it to face him. "Which is why I am showing you this now. Read it. You have perhaps ten minutes before they come for you, and I am apparently the last honest coward in Hjertvass."

It was a contract. Odile had seen contracts before — every charm-seller's apprentice learned the shape of obligation, the grammar of consideration given and received — but he had never seen one written like this. The language was older than the court's legal hand, older than the common script, running in two columns that mirrored each other like a road and its reflection in water. One column was the court's. The other column bore a signature he knew better than his own heartbeat, because it was the signature that had taught him his letters: **Eriksen**, in the old style, the E drawn like a lightning stroke frozen mid-fall.

His mother's hand. His mother's seal. Dated nine years ago.

"This isn't mine," he said.

"No," Rask agreed. "It's yours now. That's how inheritance works with these things. Your mother signed it when you were fourteen, and she signed it *for* the family, and the family means whoever carries the blood and the name." He tapped the page. "Read the consideration clause."

Odile read. The words were cold in a way paper shouldn't be, cold the way the stone beneath the trial-stone had been cold, humming. The contract was a purchase. The court — the under-court, the quiet magistrates beneath Mount Sarn whom the storm-callers paid tribute to and never named aloud — had sold something. A life bought back. A soul reclaimed from a ledger and returned to a body. And the price, deferred, transferable, compounding quietly for nine years like a debt of snow:

One name, fully surrendered. One craft, fully surrendered. One self, wholly and irrevocably rendered unto the court, *payable upon demand or upon the maturation of any related instrument.*

"It was my sister," Odile said. He didn't remember deciding to say it. The words came up from somewhere below memory, below the place the rite would dig. "The fever year. She died. They said she died."

"She did," Rask said. "For eleven hours. Your mother went down the mountain with a lantern and came back up without the lantern and with her daughter breathing. The court does not do charity. It does commerce. And your mother, gods rest her stubborn bones, negotiated terms so brutal that the court has apparently been waiting ever since for the chance to collect on something worth the trouble." He folded his hands. "You are worth the trouble, Eriksen. A stripped storm-caller is the finest payment this court has received in a century. They've been patient. Patience is the one thing they have in infinite supply."

Odile stared at the signature. His mother had been dead two years. The fever year felt like a story someone had told him — no. It didn't feel like anything. He searched for it and found a smooth blank place, a patch of ice over water, and understood with a lurch that his mother had done more than sign the contract. She had paid a little extra — a memory here, a season there, small installments — to blur it. To make sure her son would never lie awake wondering how his sister had come back from the dead, and at what interest.

She had hidden the purchase inside him. And now the whole bill had come due, and the court had chosen collection day, and collection day was today.

"There's an alternative," Rask said quietly. "There always is, with contracts. Look at the substitution clause."

There was a substitution clause. Of course there was. The court's documents were machines with a thousand moving parts, and somewhere in the mirrored columns was a provision that let the debtor designate a substitute asset of equivalent value — *equivalent,* the word repeated itself in Odile's skull with the patience of dripping water — payable in place of the primary instrument, provided the debtor executed the surrender voluntarily, knowingly, and with full understanding of what was surrendered.

"They want me to choose it," Odile said. "They want it on record that I chose."

"The court collects better from willing hands. Don't ask me why; I've stopped asking why about anything with a seal on it." Rask pushed a pen across the table. It was black bone, and it was warm. "I'll tell you what happens if you refuse. The rite proceeds as sentenced. Stripping and exile — and the exile buys the court nothing, because exile is the state's punishment, not the court's payment. The contract stays live. It matures again, someday, maybe in ten years, maybe in fifty. Maybe on a child of yours. Maybe on a child of hers." He paused. "You do have a sister. Returned souls stay returned, mostly. Mostly is a word I'd think about."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above them, the drizzle went on testifying to nothing.

Odile thought about the fever year he couldn't remember. He thought about a girl he must have taught to whistle up wind, whose name was already dissolving in his mouth like sugar, because the blurring his mother bought had eaten deeper than she intended. He thought: *she is alive somewhere in this town and I cannot even grieve her properly.* He thought: *if I pay, she keeps whatever life she was given back. If I don't, the debt rolls downhill, and downhill from here is always toward the children.*

He thought, finally, with terrible clarity: *the prophecy-ledgers all say the same thing about men in my position, and every one of them is wrong about what gets stolen.*

He picked up the pen.

"I want it noted," he said, and his voice did not shake, which surprised him, "that I am doing this for her. Put it in the margin. In my own words. However many years from now, whenever someone reads this — I want them to know it wasn't the sentence. It wasn't the court. It was me."

Rask looked at him for a long moment. Then he took a second pen, finer, and wrote in the margin himself, in the small precise hand of a man who had spent forty years recording other people's ruin: *Debtor surrenders voluntarily. Stated cause: the sister.* He underlined it twice. "There," he said. "That's the most honest line ever entered in this building. Try not to make it the last one."

Then the doors opened, and the rite-priests came in smelling of cold ash, and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

The rite was not pain. That was the horror of it. Pain would have been simple — pain leaves scars, and scars are a kind of record. The rite was absence moving through him like a tide going out. He felt his name go first: not the sound of it but the weight, the way *Odile* had always sat behind his sternum like a second heart, gone, and the priests caught it in a vessel of smoked glass and sealed it, and somewhere in the mountain it became an object, an asset, a coin.

Then the craft. Every dawn he had ever spent learning to taste the pressure-change on the air. Every storm he had ever ridden, every one he had ever turned aside, every one he had ever — the courts said — aimed. Gone like water down a drain, and the strange thing was that he could feel the shapes of the holes they left, the way a missing tooth bothers the tongue. He knew he had known things. He could point to the empty places and name nothing in them.

Last, per the contract, the designated memories — the fever year, the sister, the signing, the side chamber, the old man's kindness. Rask's face was already going soft and unfamiliar in his memory even as the priests worked, and Odile had time for one final coherent thought, which he aimed like a nail driven deep into the last board standing:

*I chose this. Whatever I forget: I chose this. Someone was worth it.*

The thought survived. Barely. It survived the way a coal survives under ash — no light, no shape, only heat, only a stubborn warmth at the center of a man who walked out of Hjertvass at midnight carrying nothing, knowing nothing, owning nothing but a single mark on his wrist where the priests had pressed the court's receipt into his skin.

They told him it was the brand of a criminal. The Wardens, in later years, would treat it as contraband. Seven years of hiding it under leather cuffs and sleeve-linings, seven years of flinching whenever it ached before weather — and he had believed them, because he had no reason not to, because the truth had been sold and filed and locked beneath a mountain in a column of mirrored text.

But the receipt remembered what he could not. On certain nights — nights when the frost crept wrong, when the air over Vellharrow tasted faintly of iron and held breath — the mark on his wrist would warm, patient as a creditor, and some animal part of him would wake in the dark knowing, without words, without reasons, that somewhere below the world, a page had his name on it.

And that tonight, in a city that forbade magic, over dinner with a man who smiled like an answered question, the account had begun to come due.

* * *

Seven years later, in the back room above a charm stall in Vellharrow, Odile Eriksen woke with the taste of iron rain in his mouth and the ghost of his sister's name dissolving on his lips — a name shaped like *V-* something, like *Vess* or *Vey*, already slipping — and the sigil on his wrist burning like a coal uncovered at last.

On the table beside him, closed, the prophecy-ledger waited. He had not dared open it since the dinner. He did not dare leave it closed either, now.

Outside, frost was creeping up the window glass in fine, deliberate lines — and they were not random. They were script.
