## 7. The Bargain of Eriksen

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Tomas finally laid his grandmother's letter flat on Odile's table between them.

He had carried it for eleven years — through two moves, one marriage that failed, and seven years of telling himself it was nothing but an old woman's riddle, a game she'd played with him because her hands were too stiff for cards. He had read it a hundred times. He had never once understood it. And now, watching Odile lean over the paper with the ledger open beside it, Tomas understood that he had been carrying a live coal in his pocket all this while, and had simply never noticed the burning.

"You said she wrote it the winter before she died," Odile said. His voice had gone quiet in a way Tomas hadn't heard before — not softness exactly, but the stillness of a man standing at the edge of something deep.

"The winter of the great frost. Everyone said she was confused by then." Tomas turned the letter so the light caught the ink. "She told my father to keep it until 'the mountain came asking.' He thought that was grief talking. She'd lost my grandfather that same year."

"And then your father died, and you kept it."

"I kept it because I couldn't throw away her handwriting." It sounded foolish said aloud. It had never felt foolish in his chest.

Odile didn't laugh. He pulled the candle closer, and Tomas watched the charm-seller become something else — someone reading a language the way other men read their own names. The letter was short. Nine lines in a spidery, slanted hand, the letters crowded against each other as though the writer feared running out of paper, which perhaps she had. Half the words were not words at all, or not any kind of Vellharrow tongue. They looped and broke in patterns that had always looked to Tomas like decoration, like the flourishes an old woman put on a page to make a plain letter beautiful.

"They're not flourishes," Odile murmured. He wasn't touching the paper. His fingers hovered above it, close enough that Tomas could see them tremble — very slightly, very briefly, before they stilled. "Tomas. These are storm-marks. Phonetic bindings. She wrote half this letter in a cipher that only works if you already know how to hear weather."

"My grandmother was a laundress."

"She was a laundress *here*." Odile looked up, and there was something terrible in his face — not horror, worse, recognition. "Where did she come from?"

Tomas had answered this question at every dinner party and every market stall registration for forty years. *The eastern valleys. Before the border closed.* It was the answer everyone gave, the answer that ended conversations politely. But sitting here, with the ledger open to its marked pages and the candle guttering, he heard himself give the true answer instead.

"She never said. She had an accent she trained out of herself. She wouldn't sing. Ever — not lullabies, not at weddings, nothing. And once a year, on the same night every year, she would stand at the north window with her hand flat against the glass, facing Mount Sarn, for exactly as long as it took a candle to burn down. My father asked her once what she was doing." Tomas's throat tightened around the memory. "She said she was paying attention. That's all. *Paying attention.*"

* * *

Odile worked through the cipher aloud, and Tomas learned what it was to watch a man translate his own childhood.

The first three lines were simple once the key turned — greetings, instructions, love, the ordinary freight of a dying woman's last letter. *Keep this from the Wardens. Keep this from the priests. Give it to no one but the one it describes.* Then the fourth line bent into the storm-marks, and Odile went silent for so long that Tomas rose twice to check the shutters, certain he'd heard footsteps in the lane.

"What does it say?" he finally asked, the third time.

"It says," Odile began, and stopped, and pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. When he lowered it, he looked older. "It says: *To the child of my child. The court beneath Sarn holds a debt with our name on it. I signed so that my son would breathe. The price deferred is not the price forgiven. When the frost comes to your window and writes to you, go to the storm-caller. He will hate me, and he will be right to. Tell him the seal remembers what the man forgot.*"

The room was very still. Somewhere outside, the night market was packing up; a cart wheel squeaked past the shuttered window, and neither of them moved.

"'Our name,'" Tomas said. His voice came out strange, flattened. "Whose name?"

"That's the thing." Odile drew the ledger toward him, turning pages with a care that bordered on ritual, until he reached the entry they had both been circling since dinner — the one dated tonight, naming two men and a debt. He set it beside the letter. "Look at how she signs the cipher section. Not at the bottom. In the middle of the text, where a witness would sign a contract rather than where a writer signs a letter."

Tomas looked. There, threaded into the looping storm-marks, was a signature he had never registered as a signature — he had taken it for a knot of decoration, a flourish like the others. Now, with Odile's finger tracing beneath it, he saw the shape inside the shape.

A stylized E, crossed by three horizontal strokes, crowned with a small mark like a lightning bolt seen sideways.

The blood left his head so quickly he had to grip the table.

"That's—" He couldn't finish. He didn't need to. Odile rolled back his sleeve without being asked, and there on his wrist sat the sigil, faded to a ghost-gray scar after seven years, but unmistakable: the same E, the same three strokes, the same sideways lightning.

"The seal of House Eriksen," Odile said. "What's left of it. What's left of me."

* * *

They worked until the candle was a puddle and Tomas had lit two more from the stub without noticing he'd done it.

Piece by piece, the letter surrendered. Odile read the storm-marks; Tomas supplied what his grandmother's silences had taught him — why she flinched at hail, why she never let him sled on the mountain's lower slopes no matter how the other children taunted him, why she had wept once, horribly, silently, at his christening when the priest asked the family to speak the child's name aloud.

And slowly, line by line, the shape of it emerged, and Tomas wished with everything in him that it hadn't.

His grandmother had come from the mountain country. From the scattered holdings under Mount Sarn, where the old families still dealt with the under-court the way merchants dealt with banks — except the currency was different, and the interest compounded in ways no honest ledger showed. She had married out, fled east, changed her name twice. And then his father — her only son, Tomas's father — had fallen ill in his ninth year with the lung-rot that took half the valley children that decade, and there had been no medicine, and no money, and one door left.

"She bought his life," Odile said quietly. It was nearly midnight now, and he had not raised his voice in an hour. That was somehow worse than shouting. "From the court beneath Sarn. The lung-rot reversed itself in nine days. Your grandmother told everyone it was prayer."

"It was prayer," Tomas said automatically, and then hated how young he sounded.

"It was a contract." Odile tapped the ledger, the open entry, the date that was today. "And look at the terms. This isn't her handwriting in the ledger — this is the court's copy, filed a century ago, which means either she renewed an old bargain or she entered one that was always going to land here. Either way. Read the collateral clause."

Tomas read it. The words seemed to rearrange themselves as he read, the way a nightmare does, each pass making it more definite:

*Debt secured against the line of the co-signatory. Principal payable upon maturity in the soul of the next generation entire. Maturity: the third dawn following the seventh year of the exile of the debtor.*

"Next generation entire," Tomas repeated. "That means—"

"It means the contract doesn't care which grandchild. It means the court files souls the way Vellharrow files tax deeds — by household, by line, by whatever name the debt is anchored to. It means your grandmother saved your father's life by promising the court a soul from your generation, and the bill has just come due, and the anchor—" Odile's jaw worked. "The anchor of the whole arrangement is an Eriksen signature. Mine. My house's. Co-signed, the way a poor borrower needs a richer friend to guarantee the loan. Except in this case the richer friend was a storm-lineage, and the guarantee was written into the mountain itself."

Tomas stood up. He didn't remember deciding to stand. His chair scraped back and he was on his feet with his palms flat on the table, staring down at the letter, at the neat spidery loops of the woman who had taught him to whittle, who had hidden sweets in her sleeve well into her nineties, who had held his hand at his father's funeral and said, *he's out of reach of them now, love, that's what matters.*

"Out of reach of them," he said. "That's what she said. At the funeral. I thought she meant God."

"She meant the court." Odile hadn't moved. "And your father wasn't out of reach. None of us ever are. That's what these contracts *are*, Tomas — they're not debts, they're leashes with very long rope. You live your whole life at the end of it and call the slack freedom."

"Then she knew." The anger arrived all at once, hot and total, seven years of grief finding a target at last. "She knew what she'd signed. She knew it would come due — she wrote *when the frost comes to your window*, Odile, *when*, not *if* — and she hid it in a cipher and left it to a boy to find, and she didn't even warn him properly. She made it a *riddle*. A treasure hunt. While she was dying warm in her bed, I was—"

He stopped, because his voice had cracked, and because Odile had risen too, and there was something in the other man's face that stopped the words in Tomas's mouth — not pity. Something rawer.

"She did warn him," Odile said. "She warned him in the only language the court can't read. That's not a riddle, Tomas. That's armor. Every word of that cipher is a word the under-court cannot parse without a storm-speaker to voice it. She wrapped the truth in weather because weather was the one thing they couldn't touch." His hand drifted, involuntary, toward his wrist, toward the gray sigil. "She spent her whole life making sure her family's business stayed illegible to them. And she sent it to you with instructions to find the one person alive whose name was on the guarantee."

"The one person it describes," Tomas whispered. His grandmother's phrase. He had thought it meant himself. Sitting here, looking at the sigil on Odile's wrist, he understood she had meant both of them — that the letter had always been addressed to two men across a gap of years, and he had merely been the courier.

"Why?" he asked. "Why would she trust you? She fled the mountain country before you were born. Did she know you? Know your mother?"

"I don't know." For the first time all night, Odile sounded uncertain, and it frightened Tomas more than the contract had. "My mother kept correspondence with the old holdings. Refugees, scattered families. If your grandmother wrote to her—" He broke off. His eyes had gone distant, reaching for something, and Tomas watched him grasp and miss. "There's something I should remember. About her letters. There's something I—"

He frowned. Pressed two fingers to his temple. And then the frown smoothed over, the way water closes over a dropped stone, and he blinked and said, mildly: "It doesn't matter. The point is the debt. The point is what happens at the third dawn."

"The third dawn," Tomas said. "Three days."

"Three days. After which the court collects a soul from your line, entire, and files it beneath the mountain alongside every other promise this city's grandparents ever made and hoped their grandchildren would never have to honor." Odile gathered the letter with careful hands and held it out. "I'm sorry. I know you wanted it to be something else. An inheritance. A recipe. Anything."

Tomas took the letter. His hands were shaking. Eleven years he had carried it — folded into a coat pocket through winters, tucked into a book during the marriage, kept in a tin under the floorboards through the worst year of his life — and the whole time it had been a knife, wrapped in love, pointed forward through time directly at him.

"She should have told me," he said. "Plainly. In plain words. She should have sat me down and said *the mountain owns a piece of you, boy, here's what it costs.*"

"Yes," Odile said simply. "She should have. They all should have. That's the cruelty of the old bargains — the people who make them convince themselves that silence is mercy." He looked down at the ledger, at his own family's name anchoring another family's ruin, and his expression did something complicated and closed. "My house co-signed your family into this. Whatever else happens in the next three days, that part is mine to answer for."

"You were a child."

"The sigil wasn't. The sigil was old when I was born, and it signed anyway." Odile smiled, and it was the loneliest smile Tomas had ever seen. "That's the thing about inherited contracts. Nobody living agreed to them. Everybody living pays."

* * *

It was past two in the morning when Tomas finally rose to leave, letter restored to its oilcloth, ledger closed between them like a patient on a table. Odile walked him to the door, and in the narrow doorway, with the cold lane breathing in around them, Tomas found he could not make his feet move.

"Odile. The letter said — *tell him the seal remembers what the man forgot.*" He searched the other man's face in the thin starlight. "What did you forget?"

For a long moment Odile said nothing. The wind moved his hair. Behind him the candle had gone out entirely, and the little shop of inert charms stood dark, a room full of beautiful things that hummed for no one.

"I don't know," he said at last, and it was the most honest sentence spoken all night. "But I think the mountain does."

Tomas stepped out into the lane. And it was there, pausing at the corner to look back one last time at the dark shopfront, that he saw it — faint on Odile's doorframe, silvered like breath on winter glass, though the night was merely cool and dry:

A curling line of frost, writing itself in slow, deliberate strokes across the wood.

Writing, he realized with a cold that had nothing to do with temperature, in the shape of his own name.
