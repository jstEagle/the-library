## 8. Running Out of Sky

The frost came to Vellharrow the way rumors came: quietly at first, from the edges, and then all at once, everywhere, and no one could say who had started it.

Odile noticed it on his shutters before he noticed it anywhere else. He was rinsing his tea cup at the basin in the grey hour after dawn when the light through the window caught something wrong — a filigree of white creeping along the bottom rail of the wood, fine as lacework, deliberate as handwriting. He set the cup down and did not touch it for a long moment.

Vellharrow did not frost. Not really. The city sat in a bowl of warm stone and older bargains, and its winters were famously mild, famously dull, the kind of weather that made travelers complain about how little there was to say about it. Seven years, and he had never once scraped ice from glass here. Seven years, and the sky had not wept once — that was part of what the city bought with its magic-free peace: a sky kept smooth as a held breath.

He touched the frost. It bit his fingertip with a cold that felt *addressed* — cold with intent behind it, cold like a letter opener.

And it wasn't random lacework at all. Up close, the pattern resolved into strokes, curves, a script he knew too well. His mother's dialect. The old high-country hand that wrote right to left and hung its vowels like laundry between consonants.

He read it without wanting to.

*SEVEN DAYS BECOME SIX.*

He stood very still while the water went cold in the basin behind him.

* * *

By midmorning, half the market had seen the frost on their own doors and windows, and the other half had heard about it, and both halves were talking too loudly, which was how Vellharrow expressed fear. A charm-seller's livelihood depended on reading crowds, and Odile read this one the way you'd read a rising river: the fishmonger's wife pressing her palm flat against her frosted window as though she could hold the cold back by touch; two porters arguing in low urgent voices over whether frost was weather or omen; a child dragging her mother by the sleeve to look at the ferning white on a drainpipe, delighted, while the mother's face said *Wardens*.

Because that was the question now, wasn't it. Weather was nobody's business. But anything that *behaved* like it meant something — anything that hummed, glowed, whispered, or wrote — that belonged to the Wardens, and the Wardens answered to no one but the mountain's shadow and their own certainty.

Odile opened his stall anyway. Closing would draw more attention than staying; a man who panicked was a man worth investigating. So he laid out his trinkets — the brass sun-catches, the knotted cords sold as "worry-untiers" (inert, all of them, inert as gravel), the little painted stones labeled *for safe travel*, which worked exactly as well as any rock you might carry in your pocket — and he smiled his market smile and haggled over nothing, and all the while he watched the frost spread.

It moved through Vellharrow like ink through water. By noon it was on the well-houses. By early afternoon it had climbed the bell tower of the Chantry of Quiet Hours, tracing the stone in patterns the priests were already calling natural, loudly, the way people call things natural when they are praying they are. And always, always, it was writing. He caught fragments as he passed: *THE ACCOUNT STIRS. THE ACCOUNT STIRS. THE NEXT OF THE SIGNATURE LINE.* Some of the script was his mother's dialect. Some of it was older than that — court-hand, under-mountain, the cramped patient lettering of entities that never needed to raise their voices because they never needed anyone to hear them twice.

Tomas found him at the stall just past the third bell, moving fast, coat unbuttoned, looking less like a cartographer and more like a man whose map had started bleeding.

"You've seen it." It wasn't a question. Tomas planted both hands on the stall's counter, and Odile saw the frost-light reflected in his eyes even here, even in the shade of the awning — faint white crawling up the inside of Tomas's own front window, he said, spelling his name. Not *a* name. *His*. Tomas Vael, in full, in the old hand, lettered across his bedroom glass like a label on a drawer.

"It knows where you live," Odile said quietly.

"It knows where everyone lives." Tomas laughed, and the laugh had a crack down the middle of it. "Half the city is iced over with its own name. There's a woman on Lantern Row who woke up and found her late husband's name on her mirror, and she's been sitting in front of it crying since dawn because she thinks it's a message from beyond, and maybe it *is*, I don't—" He stopped. Pressed his palms against his eyes. "I'm sorry. I haven't slept. Every time I close my eyes I'm back in that hall, that dream-hall, with the shelves."

"The ledger," Odile said. They'd hidden it together two nights ago, wrapped in oilcloth beneath the false bottom of his stall's strongbox, because keeping it at either of their homes felt like sleeping beside an open mouth. "You think the frost is the collection notice."

"I think the frost is the collection notice," Tomas agreed, "and I think we have five days, maybe six, before whatever signs those notices comes to collect, and I think—" His voice dropped. Around them the market churned on, oblivious or determinedly oblivious. "I think we should burn it. The ledger. Both ledgers if there are others. Burn every page and scatter the ash in the canal and let the debt go to hell unpaid."

"And the court simply forgets you exist?"

"Maybe it does. Maybe debts die with their paperwork. That's how it works everywhere else in the world."

"That's how it works everywhere else in the world," Odile said, "because everywhere else in the world, paper can be burned. This isn't paper. You dreamed the hall, Tomas. You saw how they file things. The book isn't the record. The book is a *copy* of the record. We'd be burning a map because we don't like the country."

Tomas stared at him. "Then what do we do?"

Odile looked out at the frosted city. At the bell tower wearing its white script like a shroud being fitted. At the child on Lantern Row who would, if the ledger's arithmetic held, be safe — her name was not in the Eriksen line, not yet, not unless someone signed again — and at the hundreds of others who were only collateral, only weather-touched, only frightened, and none of it their doing.

"We slow it down," he said.

* * *

He waited until full dark, until the Wardens' patrol lamps had swung past Copperlane on their rounds and the market stood empty except for the wind worrying the awnings. Then he unlocked the strongbox, unwrapped the ledger, and set it on his worktable under a single candle.

Up close, the frost outside his own window had thickened since morning. Through the glass he could see his stall's name-board furred white, *ERIKSEN CHARMS & CURIOSITIES* — the name he'd built out of nothing, a dead woman's surname bought off a registry list for three silver marks, a shell around a hollow — and beneath it, in fresh deep-cut script:

*SIX DAYS BECOME FIVE.*

Faster than noon. The intervals were shortening. Whatever schedule governed the collection, it was accelerating, pulling its deadline toward itself like a fisherman hauling line.

"All right," Odile murmured to the room, to the ledger, to the mountain he couldn't see but could suddenly feel the way you feel a storm front behind hills — pressure in the ears, taste of metal. "Let's see your terms."

He was not supposed to be able to do what he was about to attempt. That was the whole architecture of his exile: the rite had taken his name, and with the name, the craft. A storm-caller without his name was a riverbed without a river — the shape remained, the capacity gone. For seven years he had honored that emptiness the way a eunuch honors a vow, and told himself it was the same thing.

But a small working — a slowing, a turning-aside, a *not-yet* — that was not calling storms. That was barely craft at all. That was the magical equivalent of holding a door against a draft. And he was so tired, and the deadline was shrinking, and Tomas's name was written in ice on his own bedroom glass, and Odile had spent seven years learning exactly one lesson thoroughly: *what it costs to stand still and watch.*

He rolled back his left sleeve. The sigil sat on the inside of his wrist where it had sat for seven years — the rite's residue, the brand the Wardens called the exile's mark, a closed spiral with a single break in it, inert, scar-dark, dumb as a knot in wood. Every Warden in Vellharrow knew it on sight. Every Warden had looked at his wrist at least once over the years, in the early days, and found only what they expected: a marked man, emptied, harmless.

He pressed two fingers of his right hand over the sigil and reached for the smallest thing he knew how to reach for.

Not power. He had none. But habit — habit has its own deep channels, cut by years, and water remembers its bed even in drought. He shaped the working the way his mother had taught him before he was ten: breath first, then boundary, then the wordless *ask*. Slow it. Turn it. Buy an hour, buy a day, buy Tomas six days instead of five. A door held against a draft.

For a heartbeat, nothing. The candle flame leaned in the wind from the window gap. The frost ticked softly against the glass, growing, patient, punctual.

Then the sigil *woke*.

Pain went up his arm like a struck bell — not the pain of burning but the pain of a locked joint being forced open, a door in his own body kicked inward — and the closed spiral on his wrist broke open along its single fault-line and began, slowly, horribly, to *turn*. Light seeped out of it, blue-white and cold as the writing on the glass, and the spiral rotated, and with each degree it turned Odile felt something pouring out of him that he had not known was still in there: not power, not craft, but *recognition*. The working he'd attempted hadn't failed. It had been *answered* — received, logged, matched against a file — somewhere far below the stone, by something patient.

"No," he breathed. He tried to close his fist over it, smother it, and the light shone through the gaps of his fingers. "No no no—"

The frost on his window stopped growing.

Then it reversed. The white script pulled backward out of its letters, gathered itself, and rewrote — fast, fluid, eager, a clerk amending a document in real time:

*HE TRIES TO PAY EARLY.*
*NOTED WITH APPROVAL.*
*FIVE DAYS BECOME FOUR.*

The light died. The sigil settled back into stillness, scar-dark again, closed again — but different. He could feel it differently now, the way you feel a tooth after the dentist's probe finds the crack. It had never been a brand. In that moment of forced opening, he had understood it whole and instantly, the understanding already sliding away like a dream at waking, but the shape of it stayed: *it was a receipt. It had always been a receipt. Proof of purchase, proof of payment — his family's currency, minted in flesh, spendable—*

Spendable *once more*, said the sliding ghost of the knowledge, and then it was gone.

Odile sat down hard on his stool. The candle had guttered out at some point; he didn't remember when. Moonless dark, and the only light in the room was the frost-glow from the window, illuminating the new words in soft corpse-blue.

Four days. He had meant to buy time and instead he had *announced* himself — announced not his presence, which the mountain had clearly never lost track of, but his willingness. *He tries to pay early. Noted with approval.* He had knocked on the door of the under-court and offered to settle up ahead of schedule, and the court, which never shouted, had written back in ice on his own window: *delighted. come sooner.*

And worse — worse than the deadline, worse than the pain — was the simpler arithmetic sitting in his lap like a stone. The sigil was visible now. Not merely present, not merely the faded mark the Wardens glanced past — it had *flared*, blue-white, unmistakable, in a room facing Copperlane's main walk, and flared workings left residue the way gunpowder left smell. Any Warden walking past tonight with a detection lens would read the walls of this stall like a shout. *A working was performed here. An exile performed it. The exile's mark is active.*

Hiding was over. Not ending — over, already finished, sometime between his first whispered *ask* and the frost's approving reply, and he had simply been the last to know.

He rewrapped the ledger with hands that mostly didn't shake, slid it back under the strongbox's false bottom, and stood a long moment in the dark listening to the city breathe around him. Somewhere north, the bell tower wore its white script. Somewhere south, Tomas slept — or didn't — beneath his own name written in ice. Somewhere below everything, patient as arithmetic, the court amended its files.

*Four days,* he thought. *Or fewer. Or none, if the Wardens reach me first.*

He was reaching for the lamp to relight it when he heard footsteps in the walkway outside — quick, light, familiar, wrong-hour footsteps — and a soft double knock on his shutter, the old signal, the one only one person in Vellharrow had ever used.

Maren's voice came low through the wood, tight with something he'd never heard in it before.

"Odile. Open the door. I need to tell you what I did."
