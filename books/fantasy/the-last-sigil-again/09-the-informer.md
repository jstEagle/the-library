## 9. The Informer

Tomas had never been good at lying, which was why he'd built his whole life around jobs that didn't require it. He mended clocks. Clocks did not ask where you'd been last night or why your hands smelled of frost and candle smoke. For three days since the summons bloomed on his window, he'd told no one, said nothing, walked through Vellharrow like a man carrying a cracked bell — careful of every step, waiting for it to ring.

Now he was about to lie to Odile's only friend.

"She'll know," he'd said that morning, standing in the back room of Odile's stall while frost crept along the seams of the shutters outside. "Maren. She'll look at me and she'll know something's wrong."

"Then don't tell her," Odile said. He was grinding charm-powder with a mortar he kept for show; nothing he sold worked, and both of them knew it now, and somehow that made the ritual more desperate than fraudulent. His wrist was wrapped in linen. Beneath the linen, the sigil had flared visible two nights ago, silver as a scar lit from inside. "Ask her about weather. Ask her about her daughter. Keep her talking."

"You think I haven't done this before?" Tomas had said, and Odile had looked at him with an expression Tomas couldn't read — half apology, half the particular grief of a man watching someone learn to lie on his behalf.

* * *

Maren Solvik kept a dye-shop off Cordwainer's Row, two doors from the tanner, which meant her whole street smelled of vinegar and rendered fat and, beneath it, the sharp mineral bite of her own trade: indigo, madder, weld. Tomas had known her for years the way you know everyone in Vellharrow — by nodding distance. She was broad-shouldered and quick-eyed and laughed like she meant it, and she had been the first person in the city, seven years ago, to buy one of the new charm-seller's trinkets without haggling him down to insult.

She was at her counter when he came in, sorting skeins by lamplight, and she smiled when she saw him.

"Tomas. The clock-man." She set down a twist of blue wool. "Come to finally let me fix that coat? I've been meaning to say — the dye's gone uneven at the shoulder. It makes you look haunted."

"I am a little haunted," he said, and heard how true it came out, and watched her smile change.

For a moment neither of them spoke. A cart rattled past outside. Somewhere down the row a dog barked twice and gave up.

"Sit," Maren said quietly. "I'll make tea."

He sat. Her shop was small and warm and utterly ordinary, and that ordinariness pressed against him like a weight. On the wall behind her hung a child's drawing — a house, a tree, two stick figures holding hands under a scribbled sun. *Lotte*, he thought. Her daughter was named Lotte. He knew that much from years of market talk, and he knew, from the ledger hidden under floorboards in Odile's back room, that the name *Lotte Solvik* appeared on page 118 in his grandmother's cramped hand, in the same column that held *Tomas Vael*, in the same column that had held tonight's date.

The debt of Eriksen did not stop at one name. That was what he and Odile had understood only yesterday, turning pages by candlelight with their breath held: the bargain had been drawn wide. A soul for a life, and interest accruing across generations, and the court beneath Mount Sarn collecting wherever the ledger pointed. Tomas was simply due first.

Or so they'd thought. Until the Wardens came to the market two nights ago, and burned old Piet's stall over a humming charm, and asked their quiet questions with their eyes moving toward the charm-seller's corner — and Tomas had begun to wonder whether the mountain was working alone.

Maren brought the tea. She poured his, then hers, then sat across from him with her hands wrapped around the cup, and she did not drink.

"You've come to ask me something," she said. "So ask."

"Maren—"

"You've got your grandmother's face, you know. I met her once. Before you were born, maybe when you were small. She came into this shop wanting indigo for a mourning ribbon and she talked my ear off about mountains." Maren's eyes were steady and very tired. "You have the way she had of sitting like the chair owes you money. Ask me, Tomas."

The lie he'd rehearsed dissolved somewhere between his chest and his mouth. What came out instead was:

"The frost-signs. In the market. Someone reported a charm to the Wardens — a humming one. And the Warden captain asked after Odile by description. Not by stall. By *description*."

Maren looked down into her cup.

"And I keep thinking," Tomas went on, because now that the dam had cracked there was no stopping it, "who would know Odile well enough to describe him? Who would know his stall well enough to say what might be hidden there? And every answer comes back to the same short list of people in this city who actually love him, and that's the part I can't—" His voice caught. He set the cup down because his hands weren't steady enough to hold it. "That's the part I can't make fit."

For a long moment the shop was silent except for the tick of the regulator clock in the window — one of his own repairs, he realized distantly, keeping its honest time above whatever was about to happen here.

"Lotte's name is in it too," Maren said.

Not a question. Tomas felt the floor tilt gently beneath his feet. "You've seen the ledger?"

"I've seen a *page*." She stood up abruptly, crossed to the door, and bolted it. Then she pulled down the shade. When she turned around, her composure had gone the way of frost in sunlight. "Three weeks ago. Odile bought a book off a peddler — you knew that? Big thing, vellum, older than the city walls. He brought it here because he was afraid to open it alone, and I'll tell you what that tells you about a man, that his fear has a shape he trusts one person with. We read it by candlelight like children reading ghost stories." She laughed, a terrible small sound. "And then we found the dates. Past ones. Dates that matched things that happened — the bridge collapse, the fever year. And future ones. Blank entries with dates and names and debts, waiting like empty graves."

"He told you?" Tomas said. "He showed you?"

"He showed me everything. Because that's what friends do." Maren's voice frayed. "And then I found her name."

"Lotte."

"My daughter's name, written in a dead woman's handwriting, in a book full of debts owed to a court under a mountain, dated four years from now." She braced both hands on the counter as if the shop were pitching in a swell. "Do you understand what I read that night, Tomas? I read that my girl belongs to something that files souls like deeds of property. And I read that the only man in Vellharrow who might understand any of it — the storm-caller, the exile, whatever he was before he sold charms that don't work — was going to be taken, or run, or burn, and take the ledger's secrets with him. And if the Wardens took him, the book would go into an evidence vault and rot. And if he ran, we'd never see him again. Either way, the names stay in the book. Either way, nobody learns how to strike them out."

"So you informed on him." Saying it aloud made Tomas feel sick. "You gave the Wardens a reason to search his stall. To seize the ledger. So it wouldn't disappear."

"I gave them a *humming charm*," Maren snapped. "A fake. I salted his display bin myself, three nights ago, with a bit of wire and resin that sings when the wind hits it right. I thought — I thought they'd take the book, catalog it, some clerk would file it away, and it would *exist*, Tomas. Safe. Findable. Because I can't fight a mountain, but I know where the Warden archive keeps its paper." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "I thought I could steal it back later."

"And Piet's stall?"

Her face went gray. "Piet wasn't supposed to be next door to a fire. I didn't—" She stopped. Started again. "I checked the wind direction. I swear to every god that doesn't listen anymore, I checked."

Tomas sat with it. He wanted to be furious — some clean, simple fury that he could aim like a tool — but what rose in him instead was recognition, cold and total, the same feeling he'd had reading his grandmother's letter: *everyone in this story is trying to save someone with one hand while signing something away with the other.* His grandmother had bought her son's life and mortgaged her grandson's soul. Odile had signed something seven years ago that cost him his name. And Maren, who laughed like she meant it, had fed a neighbor to the Wardens' fire hoping to hide a book from a mountain.

"We're all collateral," he said quietly. "Every one of us. That's how the debt works. It doesn't care who pays."

Maren slid down until she was sitting on the floor behind her counter, skirts pooled, back against the shelving, a dyewoman's strong hands hanging loose between her knees. She looked suddenly much older and much younger at once.

"How bad is it?" she asked. "You know something. You've known for days — it's all over you. The frost on your windows, the way Odile watches doors now. How bad, Tomas?"

He should have lied. He was, by his own long assessment, bad at it anyway.

"There's a summons on my window," he said. "Written in frost. My name is in the ledger, and the debt matures soon, and the court under Mount Sarn intends to collect my soul as payment for a bargain my grandmother made before I was born." He paused. "Odile's family co-signed it."

Maren closed her eyes. "Gods below. He knew. He must have known when he showed me the book — his own seal in it —"

"He didn't know what it cost. Neither did she, maybe. Nobody ever knows what these things cost until the invoice arrives." Tomas got up, went around the counter, and sat down on the floor beside her, because it seemed suddenly absurd to conduct this conversation at different altitudes. Up close he could see the dye stains on her fingers, blue-black, permanent — the mark of a trade soaked in past the skin. Everyone in this city wore their marks somewhere.

"The Wardens are going to find the sigil on his wrist," Tomas said. "Maybe tomorrow, maybe tonight. When they do, they won't file him in an archive, Maren. They'll burn him in the square. Whatever you thought you were protecting him from — it's worse now, because of what you did."

"I know." Her voice cracked clean through. "I've known since the fire. I've been walking past his stall for two days pretending my legs work properly." She dragged a sleeve across her eyes, hard, like a woman doing fieldwork on her own grief. "Tell me what to do. Tell me how to fix it. I'll carry the book myself. I'll stand in front of the Wardens. I'll walk up that cursed mountain barefoot if someone points me at the road."

"You'd go down there?" Tomas stared at her. "Knowing what it holds? Knowing Lotte's name is in it?"

"Knowing my name will probably end up in it too, sooner or later." She laughed again, wet and fierce. "Everything I love is already in that book, clock-man. Down is just where the paperwork lives."

Tomas thought of the frost-summons on his window, the elegant spiraling script that had melted at dawn and left the glass cold to the touch for hours. He thought of Odile wrapping his wrist each morning now, sleeves buttoned to the knuckle, smiling at customers with the expression of a man memorizing faces. He thought of the stair he had dreamed — though he hadn't told anyone about the dream yet, the hall of filed souls, the shelves that went down farther than light — and of the fact that in the dream, one shelf had borne a small brass plate reading *SOLVIK*.

There was no version of this that stayed above ground. He understood it the way you understand a clock's mechanism: not all at once, but completely.

"Tomorrow night," he said. "After the stalls close. Come to the back room. Bring nothing — especially bring nothing the Wardens could want. And Maren." He waited until she looked at him. "He forgives you. He doesn't know yet that he does, because he doesn't know yet that it was you. But he will forgive you, and it'll be easier if you're not the one making it hard."

Maren nodded. Then, because she was still herself underneath everything, she reached over and straightened his collar with two dye-stained fingers, brisk as a sister.

"That coat," she said. "Bring it tomorrow. If we're going to die in a hole in a mountain, you're not doing it looking haunted."

* * *

He told Odile that evening, in the back room, by the one candle they allowed themselves. He laid it out plainly: Maren, the salted charm, Piet's fire, the archive plan, the confession on the dye-shop floor. Odile listened without moving, wrists wrapped, face unreadable, the ledger closed between them on the table like a sleeping animal.

When Tomas finished, Odile was silent for a long time.

"She checked the wind," he said at last. It wasn't forgiveness. It wasn't condemnation. It was the voice of a man adding a column. "Of course she did. She'd have stood in the square and counted the smoke if she thought it would help." He put one wrapped wrist flat on the table, beside the ledger, and Tomas saw that the linen had begun to glow faintly through the weave, silver bleeding through like dawn through a shutter. "It doesn't matter now. None of the reasons matter. The Wardens are coming for me whether Maren pointed them or the mountain did, and the mountain is coming for you regardless, and there is exactly one door out of this city that isn't watched."

Tomas felt the cold arrive before he understood the words. "Odile. No."

"Page 114," Odile said quietly. "Tonight's date. Two men and a debt. I spent three days hoping the prophecy was wrong, and then I spent today learning that everyone I love is collateral in it, and Tomas—" He looked up, and the candlelight caught the silver glow at his wrist, and his eyes were calm in the way water is calm over a drop. "The prophecy says *the thief who returns*. I've been asking myself all week what I'm supposed to steal." He drew the ledger toward himself. "I think I finally know."

Outside, the first frost-signs were climbing the window glass, delicate as fern, patient as ink drying — and far off, under the dark shoulder of Mount Sarn, something vast turned a page.
