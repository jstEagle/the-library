## 10. Down Is the Only Door

The frost-signs had reached the well by morning.

Odile stood at his window with a cup of barley tea going cold in his hands and watched the patterns crawl across Vellharrow like something writing itself. They were on doorposts now, on shutters, on the flanks of oxen dozing in their traces — thin white script that no one else seemed able to read, though half the city paused to frown at it, rub at it, decide it was salt bloom or old paint or nothing. Only Odile could read it, and what it said was simple, and said everywhere, in every hand:

*Counting.*

The mountain was counting.

He had not slept. He had spent the night with the ledger open on his table, page 114 facing the ceiling, and had read the entry so many times the words had worn smooth as river stones. *Two men. A debt maturing. The thief who returns.* His mother's dialect curled through the prophecy like smoke through a keyhole, and every hour he understood more of it, the way you understand a language by drowning in it. The entry was not a warning. Warnings were addressed to someone. This was an invoice.

Below, in the market square, the frost-script on the well's rim pulsed faintly, once, like a pulse taken at the wrist of the world. Then it moved on to the bakery. Then the tannery. Counting.

Odile set down the tea and began packing.

* * *

He packed the way he imagined other men planned escapes: quickly, ruthlessly, holding each object up to the light of *will I need this where I am going* and discarding most of them. He had done this exercise before, seven years ago, when exile had given him one bag and one hour. But then the question had been *what can I carry*, and now it was inverted, and the inversion changed everything.

He took the ledger first. Wrapped it in oilcloth, bound it with cord, and then — after a moment's thought — unwrapped it and tore out three pages. Maren's daughter's page went into his breast pocket, against his heart, where it sat like a coal. Tomas's letter went beside it. The remaining pages stayed whole; a weapon broken before battle is only kindling.

Then the charms. Seven years of inventory, and every piece of it dead wood — carved birds that would never lift, glass beads that held no weather, copper rings cooled past any warmth but body heat. In Vellharrow they sold because people wanted to believe in something small and harmless. Odile had told himself for years that this was honest work: he sold hope at a price anyone could pay, and the Wardens left him alone because his wares were exactly as empty as they appeared.

Except they weren't. Not all of them. There was one box he had never opened since the day he'd nailed it shut, and it sat at the back of the shelf behind the dried lavender, and his hands were shaking before he even touched it.

Inside, on a bed of grey felt, lay a storm-caller's kit. His mother's. The whistle of black birchwood that raised wind out of still air. The knotted cords, one knot per mile of sky. And beneath them, wrapped separately because she had always kept it separate, the needle of cold iron with which a caller stitched themselves into a storm so the storm would know its own.

He did not take the kit. He sat looking at it for a long time, and the frost crept another inch down the window glass, and finally he closed the box and put it in the bag anyway, because a man walking toward a mountain should carry the tools of whoever he used to be, whether or not he intends to use them. Some things you bring not for their function but for their testimony.

Last, he took the small brass scale from the counter — the one he used to weigh charms for customers, pans balanced on a knife-edge of honesty. It was worth almost nothing. He took it because the ledger spoke in debts and equivalents, and he had a sudden superstitious conviction that in the place he was going, everything would be weighed, and he wanted to be the one holding the instrument.

By the time he finished, the light through the window had gone strange and silver, and the frost-script had reached his own doorpost. He watched it write itself across the wood, patient as ivy, and it wrote his address, and then it wrote a number, and the number was two.

Two days. Perhaps less. The counting was nearly done.

* * *

Tomas arrived before noon, which meant he had not slept either, which meant the frost had found him too.

Odile saw him coming down the market lane and had time to notice how wrong he looked — not ill, precisely, but *located*, the way a man looks when something has fixed its attention on him. Frost-script ringed his left wrist above the glove line like a bracelet of fine white wire. He had tried to scrub it. The skin around it was raw.

"It doesn't come off," Tomas said, without greeting, without sitting. "I tried. Salt, lye, prayer — I'm not a praying man but I tried that too." He held his wrist out over the table, and Odile looked, and the script rearranged itself under his gaze into a word he knew: *held.*

"I know," Odile said. "Mine says *debtor*."

"Charming."

"Tomas—"

"No." Tomas pulled out a chair and sat, hard, the way men sit when standing has become a performance they can no longer afford. "No, let me go first, because I've been rehearsing this since dawn and if you interrupt me I'll lose my nerve entirely. You're planning something. I've known you three months and I've seen your face make this exact expression twice — once when the Wardens came through the market and once when I mentioned my grandmother — and both times it was the face of a man deciding which risk to take rather than whether to take one. So whatever you're planning, I have two pieces of information for you." He raised one finger. "One: the frost is on me now, which means whatever clock you're watching, mine is shorter. Two—" the second finger, and here his voice did something complicated, steadying itself against his will— "two, I dreamed about the inside of that mountain last night, and I don't intend to describe it, except to say that there was a hall, and there were shelves, and somewhere on those shelves is a file with my name on it, and I woke up with frost on the *inside* of the window, Odile. Inside."

The room was very quiet. Outside, a cart rattled past, ordinary as bread.

"You dreamed the under-court," Odile said slowly. "Before we ever deciphered the letter."

"I dreamed it before I ever met *you*. When I was nine." Tomas laughed, a short ugly sound. "I thought it was a nightmare about the cellar. It took me thirty years to realize the cellar was just the nearest thing my mind could find." He leaned forward. "So. Whatever you're planning. I'm coming."

"You're not."

"I insist."

"And I refuse. Tomas, listen to me—" Odile came around the table, and the words came faster than he intended, spilling out of the place where dread lived. "You are the payment. That's what the ledger says, that's what the letter says, that's what the frost on your wrist says. If you walk toward Mount Sarn, you aren't a rescuer, you're a *delivery*. Everything in that court will part for you like water. The stair will want to carry you down. Every step will feel like coming home. That's what collection feels like, I'd wager — not terror, but *welcome* — and by the time you noticed the difference it would be too late, because welcome is the trap that works."

"Then it's fortunate," Tomas said, "that I'll have you with me, and you seem constitutionally incapable of feeling welcome anywhere."

Despite everything — despite the frost and the ledger and the number two written on his own door — Odile felt his mouth twitch. "This isn't a joke."

"No. It's arithmetic." Tomas stood, and now he was close, close enough that Odile could see the exhaustion under the defiance, the fear underneath the exhaustion, and beneath *that*, bedrock: the thing that didn't move. "Here is my arithmetic. Option one: I stay in Vellharrow and wait for the mountain to collect me. I know how that ends. I've read the same page you have. Option two: I run. And the frost follows me, because it's already on my wrist and it knows my name, and I die tired in a ditch somewhere with a debt unpaid and my daughter's inheritance of bad luck undiminished. Option three—" he tapped the ledger, still lying open on the table— "I go down with the one man in the world whose family signed the contract, carrying the one book that seems to know how the court thinks, and we contest this thing instead of submitting to it. Three options, Odile. Two of them are just ways of dying politely."

"And the third is a way of dying impolitely."

"The third," Tomas said, "is a way of *choosing*. I spent forty years not choosing anything — I married who suggested, I worked what offered, I came to your stall because a dead woman's letter told me to. Well, the letter's been read. The debt's been named. From here on, everything I do is mine." He held out his marked wrist between them like proof of purchase. "You don't get to protect me out of this one. Protection stopped being possible the moment page 114 got written. All you get to choose is whether I face it alone."

And there it was, Odile thought. The thing he had been circling all night. Because the truth was that he had already decided — had decided somewhere between tearing out Maren's daughter's page and nailing the storm-kit into his bag — and all his refusals since had been theater, the last performances of a man who had spent seven years playing a character called Nobody Important. The ledger called him *the thief who returns*. Fine. Thieves chose their jobs. Thieves picked the door.

"Alone is worse than dead," Odile said quietly. "I learned that seven years ago, and I paid a great deal to learn it, and I won't waste the tuition." He looked at Tomas's wrist, then at his own, where the sigil slept under the bandage he'd wound there that morning — visible now, faintly, even through linen, a thread of pale light like lightning remembering itself. "We go tonight. After dark, when the Wardens change shift. There's an old survey road on the mountain's south flank — miners cut it a century ago and the court let them, which means the court *wanted* a door, and I intend to find out why."

"You've thought about doors."

"I've thought about nothing else." He turned to the ledger and laid his palm flat on page 114. "Look at it again. Really look. This book came out of that mountain — my mother's dialect, the Eriksen seal on your grandmother's letter, the same hand, I'd swear it now. Which means the ledger isn't just prophecy. It's *correspondence*. Someone down there wrote this and sent it up, a hundred years ago, addressed to whoever would eventually need it. A map is a message from someone who wants you to arrive." He tapped the page. "And a weapon is a message from someone who wants you to win. I think it might be both. I think someone down there has been waiting a long time for an Eriksen to come back and read properly."

"Your mother?"

"My mother is twenty years dead." But his voice caught on the words, snagged like cloth on a nail, and Tomas heard it and, wisely, said nothing. "Someone. We'll find out. Bring the letter. Bring warm clothes and no iron — iron offends them, and I'd rather not begin a negotiation by offending the magistrates."

"And what do we call this negotiation? When they ask what we've come for?"

Odile closed the ledger and slung the bag over his shoulder, and felt the storm-kit shift inside it, heavy as a confession.

"We tell them the truth," he said. "We've come to steal."

* * *

They left Vellharrow at moonrise, by the drover's gate, where the wall was old and the watch was bored and the frost-script on the stones glowed faintly enough to light their way, which Odile chose to interpret as courtesy and refused to examine further.

The city fell away behind them — the market, the well, the burned scar where Halvard's stall had been, the little house with the charm-shop sign that some other fool would hang his hopes on within the season. Odile did not look back. Looking back was for men who believed they might return as themselves.

The south flank road was exactly as the old surveys promised: a shelf of cut stone switchbacking up through pine and scree, built by miners who had gone into the mountain and, according to every record, never come out. The air grew colder with each turn, and not winter-cold — *courthouse*-cold, the cold of a place that keeps its temperature the way a magistrate keeps his voice. Frost-script thickened on the rock walls until the whole road ran white, and Odile read as he walked, because he could not help it, and what the walls said was the same thing the ledger said, said larger, said in letters ten feet tall:

*Debtor returns. Debtor returns. Debtor returns.*

"They knew," Tomas said at the fifth switchback. His breath smoked. "They knew before we decided. The frost, the dreams, the ledger — they've known for a hundred years that we'd be walking up this road tonight."

"Yes."

"Doesn't that frighten you? Walking into a trap that was set before your grandmother was born?"

Odile considered the question honestly, the way he considered all questions now, because honesty was the one luxury exile had left him. Ahead, the road ended at a doorway cut into living rock — not grand, not guarded, just a rectangle of perfect darkness with the frost-script ringing it like a signature awaiting ink. Cold air flowed out of it, steady as breath.

"It frightens me," he said. "But here's what I know about traps. A trap is set for the person the setter expects. And everyone who set this one expected a debtor — a frightened man, a guilty man, a man who comes to beg or to pay." He touched the ledger in his bag, and the sigil on his wrist answered with a flicker of pale light, and far below them, so deep it was more felt than heard, something in the mountain shifted its weight, like a clerk turning a page.

"But that's not who's coming," Odile said. "Coming is a thief. And thieves," he stepped through the doorway, into the dark, and the mountain closed around them like a held breath, "are expected to take things."

The stair went down.
