## 12. Court of Quiet Things

The stair stopped being a stair an hour before we reached the bottom.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was all I had left by then. A stair has a top and a bottom and the promise of arriving somewhere else. What we were walking down had become something more like a throat — smooth-walled, warm in a way stone should not be warm, and it swallowed our footsteps so completely that I kept checking that Odile was still beside me. Not by looking. By listening for his breath.

He breathed like a man counting.

"You're doing the arithmetic again," I said.

"I'm always doing arithmetic."

"Not out loud you aren't." My voice came back to me flattened, as if the mountain had opinions about volume. "You go quiet when the numbers are bad."

Odile didn't answer for three steps. In the dark of the descent, lit only by the ledger — because yes, he carried the book open now, and its pages gave off a light like the last coals of a fire nobody remembered lighting — his face was half shadow and half amber, and the shadow did most of the talking.

"The stair is shorter than it should be," he said finally. "We've descended maybe four hundred feet. Mount Sarn is a mile of rock if it's an inch. Either the court doesn't sit under the mountain—" he turned a page, and the light shivered across the walls, "—or distance works differently down here, and I'd rather not learn which."

"Say it's the first one."

"Why?"

"Because then there's a floor under me made of something honest."

That earned me the ghost of a smile, gone before it finished arriving. He closed the ledger partway, dimming us both. "Stay close to my voice," he said. "If anything speaks to you down here — anything — don't answer with your whole name. First name only. Middle name if pressed."

"What happens if I give it everything?"

"Then it's theirs to file."

* * *

The hall announced itself not with grandeur but with temperature.

One moment we were in the warm dark; the next, the air turned cold in the specific way of rooms that have been cold for longer than anyone can remember — not the bite of winter but the patience of it, the cold of cellars and crypts and the inside of banks after closing. The walls opened around us without a doorway. There was simply, abruptly, room.

And light — but wrong. It came from everywhere and nowhere, a grey luminescence like the sky on a day when snow is deciding. Under that light the hall resolved itself into something that stole the breath right out of my chest, and I am going to describe it exactly, because I have decided that someone who wasn't there should know.

It looked like a chancellery. That was the horror of it. Not a cavern, not a hellscape, not any of the shapes fear had drawn for me over seven sleepless years since Grandmother died. It looked like the land-registry office in Vellharrow where I once paid a fee to dispute a fence line: long counters of dark wood, shelves rising into the grey haze, ledgers upon ledgers upon ledgers bound in every shade of leather and none of them leather at all, I understood that instantly, none of them leather at all. Bells hung above the counters — small handbells, hundreds of them — and they rang occasionally, each ring producing not sound but a soft displacement, a feeling in the teeth, and wherever the ring fell, a page somewhere turned itself.

There were clerks. That is the word my mind supplied and I have never found a better one. They stood at the counters or moved along the aisles, and they were made of ash — not wearing ash, not covered in it, *made* of it, dense-packed grey that held a shape the way smoke holds a shape when smoke has decided to be patient. Their faces were smooth. Where features should have been there were suggestions: a crease where attention lived, a hollow where a mouth would be if a mouth were necessary.

They did not speak. They did not need to. When one of them wanted something moved, it moved. When one of them wanted a record consulted, the shelf delivered it into its hands like a dog bringing a stick.

"Tomas." Odile's voice, very low. His hand found my sleeve. "Don't stare at their hands."

I had been staring at their hands.

Their fingers ended in nibs. Each clerk's fingertips tapered into pen-points, black and fine, and as they worked — stamping, turning, entering — the points left faint lines of light on the pages, and I realized with a lurch that the writing in every book in that hall was being written continuously, even now, even in books already full. The past being rewritten in real time. Or recorded. With these creatures there was no difference.

A bell rang somewhere high in the haze. Every clerk in the hall stopped.

Every head — every smooth, featureless suggestion of a head — turned toward us.

Not fast. That was the worst part. They turned at the speed of a page falling, unhurried, inevitable, and the silence that accompanied the turn had weight, had grain, pressed against my eardrums like deep water. I felt my own heartbeat become loud enough to be embarrassing. Beside me Odile had gone very still in the way I'd learned meant he was terrified and refusing to spend movement on it.

Then, as one, the clerks resumed. The bell's summons had been received, acknowledged, filed.

Three of them detached from the counters and came toward us. They did not walk so much as *arrive progressively*, ash robes whispering with a sound like sand poured on paper. The foremost stopped six feet away — I would later understand that six feet was a formal distance, a legal distance — and raised one pen-tipped finger.

Above us, from nothing, words appeared in the air. Grey letters, hanging like frost-breath:

**NAME OF THE DEBTOR.**

"They know why we're here," Odile murmured. "They've known since the door in the market stall. Answer it. First name only."

My mouth was dry as the clerks themselves. "Tomas," I said.

**TOMAS,** the air wrote, and then, beneath it, smaller: **OF THE LINE OF HALVORSEN. CONFIRMED AGAINST RECORD 9,114-C. THE DEBT MATURES AT MOONRISE TONIGHT. THE COURT NOTES THE DEBTOR HAS PRESENTED HIMSELF VOLUNTARILY. THIS IS IRREGULAR AND WILL BE ENTERED.**

"It will be entered," one of the clerks repeated aloud — and its voice nearly buckled my knees, because it was Grandmother's voice. Not imitated. *Hers*, the exact grain and warmth of it, lifted from some recording the world had never known it made. "Everything is entered, Tomas Halvorson. Nothing spoken below the mountain goes unrecorded. Speak accordingly."

"That's her voice," I said. Anger got there before fear, which is probably a family trait. "You're using my grandmother's voice."

**THE COURT HOLDS MANY VOICES IN TRUST,** the air wrote. **ALL VOICES ARE EVENTUALLY HELD IN TRUST.**

Beside me, Odile exhaled through his nose. "Charming," he said, and stepped forward, putting himself slightly between me and the pen-fingered thing, which took either courage or a complete misunderstanding of what ash does to a living man. "We've come about the contract. Eriksen co-signature, maturing tonight. We want to see the original instrument."

**REQUESTS ARE HEARD BY MAGISTRATES ONLY,** wrote the air. **CLERKS FILE. MAGISTRATES JUDGE.**

"Then fetch your magistrates."

The clerk with Grandmother's voice inclined its smooth head — a gesture of such perfect bureaucratic courtesy that it circled all the way around to menace — and turned. The other two flanked us. And we were walked.

* * *

They walked us down the length of the hall, past counters where I made the mistake of reading.

The open ledgers lay face-up everywhere, and the writing in them moved. Names, mostly. Names with dates of acquisition, dates of maturity, columns of terms rendered in that same grey light. I saw a name I recognized — Old Piet from the tannery row, dead nine years — listed not under deaths but under *holdings*. I saw names with no dates at all, just the word PENDING beside them, some pending so long the ink around them had yellowed while the names themselves stayed fresh, renewed, maintained.

Pending. Like my name would say, if I could find it. Pending until moonrise.

"Don't read them," Odile said quietly.

"How do you know what I'm—"

"Because I'm doing it too, and it's making me sick."

At the hall's far end the counters gave way to a dais — modest, almost apologetic, three steps of black stone — and behind it, seated at a single plain desk as though they had been sitting there since before the concept of furniture, were the magistrates.

There were five of them. They were larger than the clerks, or perhaps denser; the eye slid off the question. Their ash was darker, packed harder, and where the clerks were smooth the magistrates had been given faces — real ones, or the memory of real ones, carved in that packed grey with the care of people who understood that judgment requires something to look at. But the faces did not move. The eyes were closed. All ten of them, closed, serene, and I understood that they had never needed sight, because they had never needed to search for anything. Everything came to them. Everything was filed.

None of them spoke. One of them — the center magistrate, whose carved face might once have been a woman's, or a river's — simply extended a hand, palm up, and waited with the composure of a creature that had waited out empires.

The clerk bearing Grandmother's voice leaned close to Odile. "The ledger," it said. "The court requests the item you have been carrying like a weapon."

Odile's arm curled around the book. For a moment I thought he'd refuse — I had watched this man lie to Wardens with a smile, watch him talk his way past two inspections of his stall, watch him charm a customs officer into forgetting a crate entirely. But then he glanced at me, and whatever he saw in my face made the decision for him, and he laid the prophecy ledger on the magistrate's open palm.

The book weighed nothing in that hand. It rose, drifting, and opened itself to page 114 — our page, our date, the entry that had started all of this — and hung there between us and the dais like an accusation presented for counter-signature.

The magistrates read it. I knew they read it because the frost-light of the pages reflected in nothing, touched nothing, and yet I felt the reading happen, felt it pass over us the way weather passes over a field.

Then the center magistrate spoke.

Its voice was not Grandmother's. Its voice was not anyone's. It was the voice a room makes when everyone leaves it — the settling, the cooling, the quiet that rushes in behind departure — and it said:

**"Two debtors present themselves. One named, one named. One contract, matured. The court acknowledges the irregularity of voluntary arrival and sets it against nothing. Sentiment is not currency here. Begin."**

"Begin what?" I asked. My voice cracked on the second word and I hated myself for it.

**"Terms. All debts are settled by terms. The debtor Tomas Halvorson owes the court one soul, matured at moonrise, per contract 9,114-C, co-signed under the Eriksen mark. The court offers the standard election."** The magistrate's closed face tilted, courteous as a bank teller. **"Election the first: payment in full. The soul of Tomas Halvorson enters the holdings at moonrise. The experience is brief and the filing is gentle. Most debtors report the transition resembles falling asleep in a warm room while someone reads aloud."**

"Most debtors," I said. "How many is most?"

**"The court does not compile satisfaction surveys."**

"And the second election?" Odile asked.

The hall changed. I cannot describe how, except that every bell in that vast grey ceiling went silent at once — silent in the way of held breath — and the clerks at the farthest counters turned their smooth heads toward the dais, and the temperature dropped another degree, and the magistrate's next words arrived with the weight of something that had been waiting a hundred years to be said:

**"Election the second: substitution. The contract bears a co-signature. The co-signature constitutes collateral. The court may accept, in place of the debtor's soul, the equivalent already owed."**

The words hung there. Frost-breath. Fading slowly.

I turned to Odile — because of course I turned to Odile, because he was the one who read things, who understood dialects and clauses and the grammar of old bargains — and I watched his face do something I had never seen it do in seven years of knowing him.

It went white. Not pale — white, drained to the bone, the color of the clerks themselves, and his eyes fixed on the drifting ledger, on page 114, and his lips moved silently, re-reading the entry, and I saw him arrive at the same sentence I had missed.

I grabbed the book out of the air. My hands shook. I found the line.

*The debt falls due at moonrise, payable in the soul of the younger line — or in the equivalent already owed.*

Equivalent. Already owed. As though it had always been sitting there in the sentence, patient as the clerks, waiting for someone to read it properly.

"Tomas," Odile said. His voice had gone very careful, the way it went when he was pricing something he couldn't afford. "Put the book down."

"The equivalent," I said. "What equivalent? What's already owed?"

The center magistrate answered without moving, without opening its eyes, in that voice of empty rooms:

**"The court holds contract 3,201-E. Signed one hundred years ago. Signed in exchange for a life. The signer deferred payment at time of exile. Deferral accrues. The equivalent already owed—"** and here, for the first time, the carved face did move, the grey lips curving into something too gentle to be called a smile, **"—is the signer."**

The hall waited. The bells waited. Somewhere in the shelves, a page turned itself.

Odile did not look at me. He looked at his own wrist, at the place beneath his sleeve where the sigil sat — the mark he never spoke of, the brand he'd told me once, drunk on cheap plum wine, that he'd earned rather than suffered. His thumb moved over it, back and forth, like a man touching a scar to confirm it's real.

"Tomas," he said again, softer now. "Whatever happens in the next hour — and something is going to happen, because I have never in my life let a contract stand that I could break — I need you to remember one thing."

"What?"

"That I read page 114 first. Before dinner. Before any of it." Finally he looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and terribly calm. "I knew what tonight was when I sat down across from you. So don't you dare let them tell you that you were the trap. I walked in with my eyes open. Both times."

The magistrate's hand turned palm-up again, patient as stone.

**"The court awaits the debtor's election. Choose, storm-caller. The moon is early tonight."**
