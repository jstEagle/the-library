## 14. Maren Descends

Maren had told herself the lie so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone: *I did what I had to do. I did what any mother would do.*

She repeated it now, in the blue hour before dawn, standing at the mouth of the old quarry road with her daughter's ledger page folded into a square small enough to hide in her palm. The paper was cold. It had been cold for three days — ever since the frost-signs began crawling across Vellharrow like veins of silver under the skin of the world — and no hearth, no pocket, no pressed hand could warm it.

*Ilse,* the page said, in that spidery script that predated the city itself. *Ilse Voss. One soul. Held against the debt of Eriksen.*

Her daughter's name. Filed away in a century-old book like a deed to a house, like a lien on a field, like something that could be collected the way the grain-tithe was collected — and Maren had learned this week that it could be, that it *was being*, that the mountain below them kept accounts older than any kingdom and settled them without mercy or hurry.

Odile Eriksen was going down to settle his half. She had watched him make the decision from across the market square, watched him fold the ledger under his coat with the careful motions of a man hiding a wound, watched Tomas Vane — quiet, kind Tomas, who bought charms he surely knew were only wood and wire — take Odile's arm as if they were simply two men going out for an evening walk and not walking into the throat of a legend.

And she had gone home and packed bread, a flint, her good boots, and Ilse's page.

Because here was the truth she could not wear smooth: she had not reported Odile to protect the city. She had not even reported him to protect Ilse, not really, not in any way that mattered. She had reported him because the Wardens had come asking questions with their ledgers of their own, because they had looked at her stall and then at her face and asked if she knew anything about humming goods, and because she had seen — in one terrible flash — the frost climbing Ilse's window and understood that the mountain's reach went all the way up into the free city, into the clean streets, into the rooms where children slept.

So she had given them Odile. A stranger's safety weighed against her daughter's breath, and she had not hesitated long enough to be proud of it, and she had not hesitated short enough to be forgiven.

*I did what I had to do.* The stone again. Smooth. Cold as the page.

The quarry road wound down between shoulders of black rock, and at its end — where every child in Vellharrow was taught there was nothing but collapsed shafts and bad air — the mountain stood open.

Not broken open. Not blasted. Simply *open*, the way a mouth opens, the way a ledger opens when the clerk decides your turn has come. A seam of darkness in the living rock, tall as a church door, breathing out air that smelled of iron and old paper and, beneath both, something sweetish and wrong, like flowers left too long in a sealed room.

They had gone in an hour ahead of her. She had made sure of it — hung back at the market, trailed them through the evening streets, let them get far enough ahead that they wouldn't see her and, worse, wouldn't stop her. Because her plan, the plan she had rehearsed on the walk over, was simple and shameful and hers: she would follow them down, find whatever court held these debts, and bargain alone.

*My daughter's name for theirs.* That was the shape of it. She'd hand over the page, point to the two men who had come down voluntarily, offer the court a trade — take them instead, take the storm-caller whose family signed the wretched contract in the first place, take either of them, take both — and walk out with Ilse's soul unclaimed.

They would never know. Or if they knew, what of it? What was Odile to her? Seven years of shared tea and borrowed sugar and the particular companionship of two people who sold beautiful useless things in a city afraid of magic — seven years, and she had already spent that coin once, hadn't she? She had spent it with the Wardens, and the sky had not fallen. Only his stall. Only his neighbor's stall. Only a little smoke, a little screaming, a little ash that the morning rain — when rain finally came — would wash away.

*I did what I had to do.*

She stepped through the door in the rock.

* * *

The dark swallowed her by degrees. First the grey light of the world above, then the grey light of her own eyes adjusting, and then nothing but the sound of her boots on stone that had been cut — she realized after fifty paces, after a hundred — cut deliberately, each step worn concave by feet that had come down here across more years than Vellharrow had existed.

There were lanterns. She didn't understand the lanterns. They hung at intervals along the wall, globes of pale glass with no flame inside, glowing the way glowworms glowed, the way deep water glowed, and their light was the color of the frost-signs, and she understood with a lurch that the cold creeping through Vellharrow and the light down here were the same substance, the same *handwriting*, and that everything written in it belonged to the mountain.

Voices echoed down the stair ahead of her — two voices, low, one of them Odile's, pitched in that careful way he used when he was frightened and refusing to say so. She slowed. Kept the distance. Told herself it was tactics.

It was cowardice wearing tactics' coat, and she knew it, and she walked on anyway.

The stair changed. That was the first thing that truly frightened her, though she would spend a long time afterward trying to pretend otherwise: the steps began to bear names.

Carved into the riser of each step, worn shallow by centuries of descent, letters caught the lantern-light. She bent once to look, brushing grit from the stone with her thumb, and read:

*HALLVARD SOLEM. SURRENDERED FREELY.*

And the next step: *GRETA SOLEM. TAKEN AGAINST THE DEBT OF HER HUSBAND.*

And the next, and the next, and the next — names stacked under names going down past counting, whole staircases of them, generations of them, and somewhere among those thousands of carved words there was a step that said *ILSE VOSS*, and Maren stood on the stair of names with her hand pressed flat over her heart as if she could hold her daughter's soul in place by pressure alone.

*Freely,* said the first name. *Taken,* said the second. The mountain distinguished. The mountain *kept records*. And she thought, with a nausea that came up from somewhere deeper than her stomach: it will know which kind mine is. It will know I came to sell other people.

She heard Odile cry out somewhere below — a short, cracked sound, the sound of a man reading something about himself — and her first instinct, God help her, her first true instinct was to freeze. To press herself into the alcove of a landing and wait until the danger passed, the way she had waited out the Wardens' footsteps outside her shuttered stall, the way she had waited out every hard thing in seven years of exile-adjacent life: small, silent, survivable.

She waited three breaths. Then she made herself move, because Ilse was upstairs in Vellharrow sleeping under a window full of frost, and mothers who froze on stairs did not get daughters back.

* * *

The stair ended without warning.

One moment Maren was descending through rock, and the next the walls fell away and she was standing at the edge of a gallery so vast that her mind refused it, slid off it, tried again. Lanterns by the ten thousand hung in the dark above like a sky of cold stars, and beneath that false heaven stretched hall upon hall of shelving — shelves of grey stone rising higher than the cathedral spires of the capital, receding farther than sight could follow, and every shelf was filled with books.

Not books. She corrected herself, gripping the rail of the gallery. *Files.* Folders of pale vellum, boxes of dark wood, scrolls bound in ribbon, ledgers thick as paving stones — and the sweetish smell she'd noticed at the door was everywhere down here, the smell of paper and dust and time, and underneath it, faintly, unmistakably, the smell of people. Of breath. Of warmth. As if each document remembered the body it concerned.

Souls filed like deeds. She had known it — Tomas had dreamed it, the story had come back to them with frost on the window — but knowing a horror and standing inside it were different countries, and the border crossing cost her something she didn't get back.

Somewhere in that archive was a folder with Ilse's name on it. Somewhere in that endless cold starlight was a box containing everything her daughter was — the gap tooth, the fear of thunder, the way she sang when she thought no one listened — reduced to a record, held against a debt signed before the girl was born by people who were dust.

And the clerks.

Maren saw them then, down on the archive floor: figures moving between the shelves with the unhurried patience of tide. They were made of ash, or dressed in it, robes and faces the grey of long-dead fires, and they did not speak to one another. They didn't need to. When one reached a shelf, the right file seemed simply to present itself; when one turned, the aisle arranged itself around the turning. They moved like the interior of a machine that had been running since before language, and their silence was worse than shouting, because shouting meant passion and passion meant negotiable, and nothing in this place was negotiable. This place *kept terms*.

She crouched behind a pillar at the gallery's edge and watched, and the plan she had carried down the stair — *my daughter's name for theirs* — sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Because here was what she hadn't let herself imagine: scale. In Vellharrow, her sin had been containable. One tip to the Wardens, one stall burned, one man's life inconvenienced, maybe ruined — a small cruelty in a small city, deniable, survivable, forgivable someday perhaps by someone kinder than herself. But down here, in this lightless country of paperwork, she could feel the arithmetic changing. Her betrayal wasn't a single act; it was a line item in a system that had been running lines for a thousand years. Every cowardice fed it. Every mother who traded a neighbor to keep her child, every debtor who signed a name that wasn't his, every small survival — the mountain *ate* them, filed them, drew interest on them, and waited.

She was not bargaining with a monster. She was bargaining with a filing cabinet the size of the world, and filing cabinets did not accept trades. They accepted *payments*, rendered exactly, in full.

Ahead and below, near the center of the great floor, the halls opened onto a space of bare stone — a court, she realized, a literal court, with a bench of black rock and three shapes seated behind it, ash-grey and motionless as the shelves. Two small figures were approaching the bench. One walked like a man carrying his own execution order. One walked like Odile.

Maren took out Ilse's page. Unfolded it. Refolded it. Her hands were shaking badly enough that the creases tore, and a tiny triangle of the paper fluttered down through the lantern-light and was gone.

This was the moment. The plan said: go down, interrupt, offer the trade, save Ilse. The plan said: use them, the way she had used them with the Wardens, the way she had been using everyone for seven years, because love was a currency and she had always spent it where it bought the most.

But she kept seeing the step on the stair. *Greta Solem. Taken against the debt of her husband.* Taken — because someone else's bargain, someone else's cowardice, had rippled down a generation and taken her. If Maren stood up on this gallery and offered two men in exchange for her daughter, she would not be saving Ilse. She would be carving another step. *Taken against the debt of her mother.* Some future person would descend these stairs and run a thumb over her daughter's name and learn what Maren had done tonight, and the mountain would have gotten exactly what it always got: another small human cruelty, compounding.

Her legs would not carry her down the last flight. She told herself it was fear. It was, partly. But under the fear, cracking open like ice under weight, was something she recognized with horror as conscience — arriving, as conscience always did, precisely too late to be convenient.

Below, the middle magistrate spoke. No voice carried; the words simply arrived, the way the frost-signs arrived, written directly into the hearing. Maren couldn't make out the terms. But she saw Odile go rigid, and she saw Tomas put his hand on Odile's arm, and she saw the magistrates sit in their perfect patience while two lives were discussed in the third person, and something in her chest broke along a line she'd been pretending wasn't there for seven years.

"You came to sell them," said a voice beside her. "Don't bother denying it. I can smell intent down here."

Maren spun, page clutched to her chest.

A woman stood at the gallery rail. Ash-grey robes. A face Maren knew — older, harder, lined with decades that should not have passed since the last time Maren had seen it, but known. Known from a photograph on a charm-seller's shelf. Known from the way Odile's jaw set whenever anyone asked about his family.

"You're his sister," Maren whispered.

"I'm the record-keeper." The woman's eyes were the color of the lanterns. "And you are the friend who informed on him. Yes, we know. The mountain knows everything that happens under its weather." She tilted her head toward the court below, where the magistrates waited with their terrible patience. "So. You've seen the scale of it now. Tell me honestly, since lying is wasted breath down here — do you still think your daughter's soul is worth more than theirs?"

Maren opened her mouth. The old answer — *yes, yes, yes, any mother's answer* — rose up smooth as the river stone, ready to be spoken.

And faltered.

Because below them, Odile had stepped forward, alone, toward the bench, and even from this height Maren could see him draw himself up the way he did behind his stall when the Wardens walked the market — chin level, spine straight, a man deciding exactly how much of himself he was willing to lose.

"No," Maren said. Her voice cracked on it. "No. I don't." She looked down at the torn page in her hands — her daughter's name, her daughter's whole self, folded and refolded until it broke — and felt the cowardice crack with it, clean through. "But I don't know what else to give. Tell me what else there is to give."

The sister — the record-keeper — studied her for a long moment. Then she held out her grey hand.

"Come down," she said. "Both of you are about to refuse the terms. Someone ought to be there to witness it." A pause, and something almost like warmth crossed the ash of her face. "Someone *human*."
