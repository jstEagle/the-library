## 16. The Reading of the Prophecy

The magistrates did not shout. That was the worst of it, Tomas decided — that they never raised their voices, not once, in all the hours he and Odile had been held beneath Mount Sarn. The court spoke the way frost speaks: quietly, evenly, with the absolute certainty that you would still be there when it finished.

They sat at a table of black stone that had not been there a moment before. Or perhaps it had been there always, and only became visible when the court wished to negotiate. Everything in the under-court worked like that — furniture that assembled itself from shadow and patience, corridors that were longer on the way back than the way down, light that came from nowhere and cast no shadows at all, so that every face in the hall looked flat as a coin.

The magistrates themselves were ash given posture. They stood — or occupied standing-shaped spaces — along the far wall, tall figures the color of old fire, their edges softening and re-forming like smoke deciding, over centuries, what shape to take. Their faces, where faces could be found, were smooth and patient. One of them held the ledger.

Not Odile's ledger. Another one.

Tomas saw it first as a gray rectangle cradled in the magistrate's long hands, and his heart performed a small, stupid hop of recognition before his mind caught up: *it's a book, it's just a book.* But nothing in this place was just anything. The binding was vellum gone the color of fog, stitched with thread that might have been silver or might have been hair. It was older than Odile's copy by any measure he could guess. Older than Vellharrow. Perhaps older than the mountain's patience, which seemed to be the oldest thing in the world.

"You brought a copy," Tomas said, because someone had to speak, and Odile had gone very still beside him in the way he went still before storms — though there were no storms here, and hadn't been for seven years, and would be none ever again if Tomas had anything to say about it.

"We do not bring," said the magistrate holding the book. Its voice was dry paper turning. "We keep. Your copy is an echo. This is the voice."

The other magistrates did not move. They never moved unless moving was required, and then they moved all at once, like a single creature wearing many robes. Tomas had counted nine of them. He suspected the number changed when he wasn't looking.

"The terms stand as offered," the magistrate continued. "The soul of Tomas Aldervane, tendered against the debt of Eriksen, matured this season. Or: the equivalent already owed." Its smooth face turned toward Odile, and though the face had no eyes to speak of — only two shallow hollows where eyes might have settled and thought better of it — Tomas felt the weight of its attention pass over him like a hand over a flame, checking whether he still burned. "You have until this reading concludes to choose."

"And if we refuse?" Odile asked. His voice was steady. Tomas knew that steadiness now, knew what it cost. He'd learned to read Odile the way sailors read weather — the set of his shoulders, the stillness of his hands. Right now his hands were quiet on the table, but his thumb was pressed hard against his wrist, against the place where the sigil had flared visible two nights ago and ended seven years of hiding. As if he could cover it. As if covering it had ever worked.

"Refusal is a word," the magistrate said. "Debt is a fact. We will collect the fact regardless of your vocabulary."

*Charming,* Tomas thought, and hated himself for thinking it, because humor was the last coin he had left and he was spending it too fast.

"Then read," Odile said. "If the reading matters enough to delay collection, let us hear it."

The magistrate inclined its head — or the ash that served it as a head — and opened the book.

* * *

The sound of the pages turning filled the hall the way water fills a cellar: slowly, from below, rising. Each page was thick as bark and pale as bone, and the writing upon it was not ink. Tomas leaned forward despite himself. The letters seemed to sit slightly above the surface of the page, the way breath fogs glass — as if the words were written in cold air and the page merely remembered them.

"This text was set down one hundred and twelve years past," the magistrate said, "by a seer who traded her sight for the seeing. She wrote in the dialect of the northern coast. We have kept her grammar intact. Translation would be mercy, and this is not a merciful room."

"It's Eriksen dialect," Odile said quietly. "My mother's."

"Your mother transcribed a portion of it," the magistrate agreed. "A century ago. Her hand appears on eleven pages. You may find that instructive later. For now — the relevant passage."

The hall went quiet in a way that was not silence. Silence is empty. This was full — full of attention, full of the weight of a hundred years of waiting, as if the whole mountain had been holding its breath since the seer dipped her pen and could finally, finally exhale.

The magistrate read:

*"In the hundred-and-twelfth year, when the debt comes due and the city forgets the sky, the debtor's line shall gather beneath the stone. Two men will sit at the mountain's table: the one who is owed, and the thief who returns."*

Tomas's mouth went dry. *The one who is owed.* That was him. That was his name written a century before his grandmother's granddaughter's letter had ever reached a charm-seller's stall — the soul filed away as payment, the man sitting at the table because the table had been set for him before he was born.

He looked at Odile. Odile was watching the magistrate's hands, not the book, and his face had the particular blankness he wore when he was feeling too much to afford showing any of it.

"The thief who returns," the magistrate repeated, letting the phrase hang. "Hear how she continues."

*"The thief who returns will open the door that was closed for his sake. The thief who returns will stand before the Quiet Things and speak against the grain of his own name. And when the weighing is done—"*

The magistrate paused.

It paused deliberately. Tomas understood that immediately, the way you understand a held note in music — the pause was part of the composition, a silence the seer had built into the sentence a hundred years ago, knowing someone would someday need a moment to brace themselves. Knowing exactly who would be sitting here. Knowing he would want to run, and making sure the words caught him first.

"—the thief who returns will leave with nothing of himself."

The words fell into the hall and did not echo. Nothing echoed here; the walls drank sound the way sand drinks rain. So the sentence simply arrived and simply stayed, sitting on the black table between the three of them, patient as everything else in this place.

"That's it?" Tomas heard himself say. His voice sounded wrong in his own ears, too loud, too alive. "'Leave with nothing' — that's a riddle, not a sentence. What does it mean? Nothing how?"

"The seer was precise," the magistrate said. "She was precise because precision was all she had left."

"Then be precise with me. Does he die? Is that what 'nothing of himself' means? Is that the price?"

Beside him, Odile had not moved. Had not breathed, Tomas realized. He put his hand over Odile's on the table — a small rebellion, a human thing done in a room designed to make human things impossible — and felt the fine tremor running through the storm-caller's fingers, the tremor of a man holding something back with both hands.

"The passage does not say *dies*," the magistrate said.

Tomas waited. The wait stretched. The magistrate regarded him with its eyeless patience, and Tomas understood, with a slow cold horror, that it was going to make him ask.

"What does it say, then?"

"It says *forgets*."

* * *

Afterward, Tomas could never reconstruct the next few minutes properly. Memory gave them back to him in pieces, the way a dropped plate gives back shards.

He remembered the word landing. *Forgets.* Such a small word. A schoolroom word. A word for old men and lost keys and the names of neighbors' children. It should not have been able to hold what it held.

He remembered the magistrate continuing to read, because the court was thorough even when it was cruel — especially when it was cruel. *"Nothing of himself,"* the seer had written, and then, in smaller script crowded into the margin like a woman hurrying to get it all down before her last light failed: *"not his life, not his blood, but his name and the knowledge of his name; the thief will pay himself and walk out empty, and the empty man will not know what he bought, and the one he bought it for will carry the knowing alone."*

Alone.

That was the word that broke something in Tomas. Not the forgetting. The *alone*. Because he understood it instantly, completely, in the marrow-way one understands terrible things: whoever walked out of this mountain beside the thief would remember everything. Every bargain. Every stair of names. Every moment of sacrifice. And the man beside him would remember nothing at all, and would have to be told, or — worse — never told, and live out his days a stranger to his own heroism while the person who loved him carried the whole story like a stone in his chest.

"He doesn't die," Tomas said. His voice came out strange. Flat. "He just — stops being himself. And I'm supposed to watch. That's the prophecy. That's what your precious book says happens."

"The prophecy does not command," the magistrate said. "It observes. There is a difference, though mortals rarely find comfort in it."

"There's another option." Odile spoke at last, and his voice was worse than Tomas's — it was calm, terribly calm, the calm of a man who has stopped arguing with gravity. "Your terms. Tomas's soul, or mine. The equivalent already owed."

"Yes."

"If I pay the equivalent, what precisely is collected?"

"All that you are. The contract names it: name, craft, memory, and the sigil that binds the three. You would leave this mountain alive, whole of body, and vacant of self. The seer saw truly. She always saw truly. It is why she sold her sight — she could not bear to keep seeing."

Tomas turned in his chair. "No."

"Tomas—"

"*No.*" Louder this time, and the ash-magistrates along the wall all shifted at once, a rustle like a fire settling, and Tomas discovered he did not care. Let them hear him. Let the whole mountain hear him. "You don't get to decide this. Both of you. You sit there—" he pointed at Odile, whose face was doing that awful blank thing again, "—and you're already saying goodbye. I can see it. You've been saying goodbye since the frost came through my window, maybe since the day we met, and I am telling you that I did not drag myself down a thousand stairs into the dark to trade one version of losing you for another."

"You'd rather lose me entirely?" Odile asked softly. "Death is also a leaving, Tomas. This way you'd have more of me. Most of me. Nearly all."

"Nearly all is not the man I—"

He stopped. The word had come up his throat before he'd authorized it, and now it stood there in the air between them, unspoken and yet somehow spoken anyway, because in this hall of quiet things perhaps nothing needed to be said aloud to be heard.

Odile looked at him. For one moment the blankness cracked, and underneath it Tomas saw the thing he'd fallen for across a charm stall in a magic-free city — the warmth, the grief, the terrible tenderness of a man who had spent seven years selling inert trinkets and pretending that was all he'd ever wanted to be.

Then the crack sealed over, and that frightened Tomas more than the magistrates had.

"There is a third path," Odile said, and turned back to the court, and his thumb was pressing hard against the sigil on his wrist again, pressing like a man testing a coin to see if it was real. "But I'll need to see my contract. All of it. Not the summary. Not the terms. The original, signed and sealed, every clause."

The lead magistrate went still — stiller than still, stiller than the others, a silence inside a silence. When it spoke, its dry-paper voice had acquired something new. Not alarm. Ash does not alarm. But interest, perhaps. The interest of a ledger-keeper who has kept accounts for ten thousand years and has seen, perhaps twice, a debtor reach for a clause nobody remembered was there.

"The archive will produce it," the magistrate said. "Though I will observe, debtor, that those who read their contracts whole rarely thank us afterward."

"I've never thanked anyone in my life," Odile said. "Why start underground?"

* * *

They were escorted — *herded*, Tomas would have called it — through corridors that bent like river bends toward the archive. The magistrates walked ahead in their silent file, and the gray book stayed behind on the black table, open to the final page, as if it expected them back. As if it knew how stories ended and was content to wait for this one to catch up.

Tomas walked close to Odile and kept his voice low. "What clause? What are you looking for?"

"I don't know yet." Odile's eyes tracked the walls, the seams in the stone, the way a man studies a lock. "But my mother transcribed eleven pages of that prophecy a century ago. She didn't do it out of piety. My mother didn't have a pious bone. She did it because she was planning something, and she left the plan where I'd eventually find it, even if it took me seven years and a dinner date to get there."

"Dinner date," Tomas repeated. "Is that what we're calling the night we met?"

"I'm workshopping it." But there was no humor in it, and neither of them smiled.

Ahead, the corridor opened into a vast dark where shelves rose out of sight like cliffs, and somewhere in that dark a bell tolled once — a deep, mineral sound, the sound a mountain makes clearing its throat.

And in that moment, walking into the archive where his whole life lay filed and catalogued, Odile said, almost too quietly for Tomas to hear:

"When we get in there — whatever I find, whatever I decide — don't let me do it alone. Whatever the prophecy says about carrying things alone. Promise me you'll argue."

Tomas took his hand in the dark.

"That," he said, "is the first thing you've asked of me that I can actually give."
