## 17. Terms Refused

The magistrates waited.

That was the thing about them, Odile was learning. They did not press. They did not lean forward or let their ash-stitched faces soften with something like hunger. They simply waited, the way stone waits, the way a ledger waits, and they made their patience into a pressure that filled the hall from floor to unseen ceiling until Odile felt it in his teeth like the hush before lightning.

*Your soul,* the eldest of them had said — not aloud, never aloud, but in the way a page turns, in the way frost writes on glass — *or his debt discharged whole. These are the terms.*

Two offers. One generous by the standards of courts beneath mountains: give up everything he was, every memory, every hour of the seven years he'd spent selling dead charms to people who didn't know what magic felt like, and walk out of here beside Tomas with nothing missing from him but himself. The other offer had been simpler still. Pay with the boy. Let the bargain of Eriksen close as it was written, a century ago, in ink that smelled even now of pine resin and cold iron.

Odile stood between them with the ledger under his arm and his sister's name caught in his throat like a fishhook.

He thought about saying yes.

That was the shame of it, and he would carry the shape of that temptation even if he never carried anything else: for one long breath he had looked at the offered mercy — *forget, and be free* — and wanted it. Wanted the erasure the way a drowning man wants the water to close over his head. Seven years of exile, of humming charms that hummed with nothing, of flinching at Warden boots on cobblestones. Seven years of being no one in particular already. What would he lose, truly? Some storms. A name he hadn't spoken since the trial. A sister's face he'd taught himself not to see when he closed his eyes.

And then he'd looked at Tomas.

Tomas stood a pace behind him and slightly left, which was where he always seemed to stand, as though Odile were a fire worth warming his hands at. His jaw was set in the particular way it set when he was frightened and refusing to say so. He had come down a mountain for a man he'd known one autumn. He'd put his grandmother's letter on a table like evidence and said, *I'm sorry it took me so long to find you.* He had not once asked Odile to pay for him.

If Odile forgot, someone would have to tell him who he'd been. And the only someone left would be a man he'd chosen an hour ago, in a city above, to have dinner with.

No. That wasn't a price. That was just a slower way of losing everything.

"Neither," Odile said.

The word fell into the court and did not echo. Halls like this swallowed sound the way they swallowed everything else — filed it, stamped it, made it theirs. But the magistrates turned, all of them, a slow grinding motion like pages being squared against a desk, and the eldest's attention settled on him with the weight of a full archive.

*The terms are fair,* it said. *Fairer than most are given.*

"I don't doubt it." Odile's voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. Somewhere below his fear there was an old muscle flexing, one he hadn't used in seven years — the muscle of a man standing in front of a tribunal and refusing to be small. "You've been fair for a hundred years. Fair is what you're made of. But I've read contracts my whole life — sold enough false ones, charm-sellers learn the trade — and I've learned that a fair term on a rotten instrument is still rot."

Silence. Not empty silence; *assessing* silence. He pressed on before it could crush him.

"The contract you hold. The Bargain of Eriksen. Grandmother signs for her son's life, co-signed under an Eriksen seal, debt deferred one generation, payable in the next soul of the line. I've read it in your archive — I've held the page in my hands." He lifted the ledger slightly, an accusation made of paper. "You want me to believe this court does nothing but honor what was written. Very well. Then honor all of it. Honor the *law* of it."

*State your claim.* The words came down like a gavel made of snow.

"A debt signed under a false name," Odile said, "is void."

For the first time since he had entered Mount Sarn, something moved in the hall that was not scheduled to move. It was small. A tremor along the ash-threaded robes of the junior magistrates. A flicker in the frost-light, like a candle reconsidering. The eldest went very still, and its stillness was different now — not patience but *attention*, total and terrible, the attention of a thing that had not been contradicted in a century.

*That clause,* it said at last, *was struck from practice four hundred years ago.*

"Struck from practice," Odile agreed. "Not from the law. You keep everything here — I've seen your shelves. Souls filed like deeds, names carved into stairs, a prophecy copied out in triplicate because you couldn't bear to trust a single record. A court that keeps *everything* cannot claim one clause has simply ceased to exist. It's there. It's binding. And it applies."

*Prove it.* No anger in the voice. Worse than anger: procedure. *Name the signatory who signed falsely, and the instrument under seal, and we will examine the claim according to the old forms. If the clause holds, the contract is void, and every obligation drawn from it dissolves.* A pause, delicate and dreadful. *If it does not hold, you will have wasted the court's time. The court does not forgive waste. And the original terms will return to the table — with the deferral expired.*

Tomas's hand found the back of Odile's coat and gripped it. Not pulling him back. Anchoring him. Odile covered the hand with his own, briefly, and then stepped forward alone.

Here it was, then. The door he had walked toward for seven years without knowing it, and now that he stood before it he understood why he had always, always kept his back to it.

Because proving the clause meant naming the false signature. And the false signature was not his mother's. Not his father's. Not any ancestor gone comfortably into the ground beyond the reach of under-courts.

It was his sister's name.

Wren Eriksen, who at nine years old had been dying of the gray fever while the storm-callers argued over whether her life merited a working that costly. Who had been saved because her older brother — sixteen, furious, talented past sense — had done the unforgivable arithmetic. The under-court sold lives, but it required a signature of equal weight, a soul pledged against a soul, and Odile had none of weight yet. So he had done what no one was supposed to know could be done. He had signed *as* her. Taken her name onto his own hand, forged the one signature in the world he knew better than his own, because he had traced it ten thousand times signing letters for her when she was small and her hands were tired. He had bought her life with a lie written in her name, and the sigil that bloomed across his wrist afterward — the mark everyone believed branded him a criminal, a storm-caller punished for hubris — was never a brand at all.

It was a receipt. And receipts, unlike brands, could be examined.

"Wren," Odile said.

The name came out of him like a tooth. Like something rooted that had to be torn. He had not spoken it aloud in seven years — the rite of exile had seen to that, had scooped the memory soft as marrow out of him, leaving only the bone of the fact: *you did something, it cost you everything, do not look too closely at what.* He had lived around the hole in himself the way a man lives around a burned room, keeping the door shut, keeping the furniture of his mind arranged so no one ever had cause to open it.

Now the door was open, and the court was looking through it.

*Clarify,* said the eldest.

"She signed nothing." His voice cracked and he let it. Let them hear it. Let it be entered into whatever record this place kept of him, which he suspected was more complete than he could imagine. "My sister signed nothing. She was nine years old and she was dying and she never agreed to any bargain, because there was no bargain for her to agree to. Her life was purchased under her name by another hand. Mine." He raised his wrist, and the sigil flared beneath his sleeve, visible now to everyone, the light of it the color of a sky deciding on thunder. "I wrote her name. I wrote it well — well enough to fool your clerks, well enough to buy her twenty-two years of breathing. And a debt executed by forgery, under a false name, per the clause this court itself preserves in its own archive—" he nodded toward the endless dark shelves, "—is void. Not renegotiated. Not deferred. *Void.*"

The hall did not gasp. Halls like this didn't gasp. But every magistrate turned at once, fully, and the frost-light guttered, and somewhere deep in the walls there was a sound like a vast ledger being opened to a page no one had visited in four hundred years.

*Forgery,* the eldest said, tasting the word. *Claimed by the forger himself.*

"Yes."

*You understand what you confess. The penalty for signature-theft, under the same law you invoke, is forfeiture of the stolen name and all names bound to it. If your claim fails, you have confessed to a crime whose sentence is total. If it succeeds, the confession stands in the record forever, and the record,* a pause like the closing of a drawer, *does not forget.*

"I've spent seven years being forgotten," Odile said. "On purpose. By choice, and by rite, and by my own cowardice, all three. I can survive being remembered. What I can't survive—" and here his voice finally failed him, and he heard Tomas breathe in sharply behind him, "—is standing in this hall and letting you collect a soul that was never yours, when the only reason you hold a claim on it is a lie I told to save someone else. Take the lie apart, then. Take all of it apart. Void the contract."

*And the sister?*

The question landed like ice slid under a door.

*The record-keeper of this court,* the eldest said, with the mildness of a blade being sheathed rather than drawn, *has served under the name Wren Eriksen for twenty-two years. If the contract is void, her purchased service ends. She is released. She walks free.* Another pause. *But the confession binds the confessor to examination. You will be brought before the archive. Your claim will be tested against the original instruments. And you should understand, storm-caller, before you speak again — the testing is not gentle. The archive reads what a man is. All of it. Everything he has hidden, even from himself.*

Odile thought of the stair of names outside, each one carved and worn, his own half-eroded, *debtor* cut beneath it in a clerk's patient hand. He thought of Maren somewhere above him in the freezing dark of Vellharrow, bargaining with her daughter's page. He thought of his mother's dialect in the margins of a prophecy book, and of a letter sealed in wax that smelled like home, and of a man who had crossed a city to sit across from him and become, in a single evening, the reason for all of this.

He thought of Wren. Nine years old, fever-bright, asking him to tell her a story about storms until she slept. Twenty-two years a prisoner in a court of quiet things, filing souls in the belief that her brother had forgotten her — because the rite had been designed to make forgetting true, and everyone, even the mountain, had assumed it worked.

"It doesn't matter what it costs me," he said. "Test it."

*You may withdraw,* the eldest offered, almost gently, which was the most frightening thing it had said yet. *The terms remain open. Mercy is still on the table.*

"Then take mercy off it." Odile set the ledger down on the black stone floor between them, spine up, like a challenge laid at a duelist's feet. "I refuse both terms. I contest the instrument. Bring your archive. Bring the original signatures. Bring whatever four-hundred-year-old clause you buried hoping no one would need it." He drew a breath that shook only a little, and that little was for Wren, and he let the rest go hard. "And bring my sister into this hall, because if we're going to tear a hundred-year contract apart on the strength of her name, she's the one person in this mountain who has the right to watch."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for Odile to count three separate ways he might die in it.

Then the eldest magistrate rose — and Odile saw for the first time that it was not sitting at all, had never been sitting, but had merely been folded, the way a letter is folded, the way a contract is folded — and unfolded to a height the frost-light could not reach.

*So be it,* it said. *The archive is opened. The claim is entered. Summon the co-signatory.*

Far away, deeper than deep, a bell rang once — a sound like the first crack in a frozen lake — and down every corridor of the mountain, on every stair carved with surrendered names, the frost began to move. Not melting. Traveling. Streaming inward, toward the hall, carrying with it the sound of footsteps that Odile knew better than his own heartbeat, though he had not heard them in seven years and had forbidden himself even the memory.

Tomas's hand tightened on his shoulder.

"Odile," he whispered. "What did you just do?"

Odile watched the far doorway, where the dark was gathering itself into the shape of someone coming, and found that his mouth had gone dry around the answer.

"I called her," he said. "After twenty-two years — I finally called her."

The footsteps grew closer.
