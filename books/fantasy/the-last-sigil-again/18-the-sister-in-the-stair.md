## 18. The Sister in the Stair

The summons came as a sound.

Not a voice — the court did not use voices when a bell would do — but a single tone that rose out of the floor of the Court of Quiet Things and passed through Tomas's ribs like a finger through water. The magistrates of ash did not move. They never moved; they only *were*, columns of grey patience arranged around the hall like furniture that had learned to think. But their attention shifted, all of it at once, the way a field of wheat turns toward wind.

"The co-signatory is summoned," said the magistrate who had spoken before, the one whose face was a smooth oval of cinder with two places where eyes might once have been considered. "The record-keeper will attend."

"Record-keeper," Odile repeated beside him. His voice had gone thin. Since he'd read his own contract in the archive — since he'd learned what the sigil on his wrist actually *was* — he'd been speaking like a man reading his own obituary in a language he almost knew. "The court has a record-keeper?"

"The court has had seven," the magistrate said. "The current one has served nineteen years of her term. She is efficient." A pause, long enough to be contemptuous or reverent; with ash for a face, it was impossible to say which. "She is also your sister."

Tomas felt Odile stop breathing. Not gasp — there was no drama in it. He simply stopped, the way a clock stops, mid-tick, and for three full seconds the hall was quieter than it had any right to be, given that they were standing inside a mountain full of filed souls.

"That's not possible," Odile said finally. "My sister died. Or — no. No, she didn't die. She was *taken*. She was—" His hand went to his wrist, to the sigil that had burned visible for days now, a brand of pale gold that the Wardens above could not yet see but the whole mountain could. "The trial. The rite. I signed—"

"You signed," the magistrate agreed. "What you signed *for* is recorded elsewhere. As is what she signed in turn." The oval face tilted, a movement like a page turning. "You have read your contract. Have you read hers?"

"I didn't know she *had* one—"

"All debts have two ends," the magistrate said. "You are one of them."

* * *

They were made to wait, because the court dealt in waiting the way other courts dealt in torture. It was more effective. Tomas sat on a bench of black stone that was warm in the way a living thing is warm, which he tried very hard not to think about, while Odile stood at the base of the Stair of Names and stared upward into the dark where the steps spiraled away toward a light that wasn't quite light.

Tomas watched him instead of the dark. He had gotten good at watching Odile these past days — at reading the small weather of him, the way his jaw set when he was frightened and went loose when he'd decided to be brave about it. Right now the jaw was loose. That scared Tomas more than anything the magistrates had said.

"You knew her," Tomas said quietly. "Before."

"I knew her." Odile's thumb moved over the sigil, back and forth, a habit Tomas had come to recognize as the gesture of a man touching a scar to confirm the wound was real. "Vessa. She was eleven when—" He stopped. Started again. "There's a hole where she should be. In my head. I can remember our mother's kitchen, remember the smell of it, and there was always a second bowl drying by the fire, and I cannot for my life tell you who ate from it." His laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Seven years I thought the exile took everything. It left me just enough to ache."

Tomas reached over and put his hand on Odile's shoulder. It was the first time he'd touched him since the frost-summons, since the descent, since the stair — and Odile leaned into it like a wall leaning against a wall, briefly, and then straightened, because the mountain does not permit comfort for long.

Above them, the not-light brightened.

Footsteps began to descend the Stair of Names.

Tomas heard them before he saw her: a slow, even tread, unhurried, the walk of someone who has made this climb ten thousand times and expects to make it ten thousand more. Then she rounded the spiral and came into view, and Tomas understood that every story Odile had failed to remember had been standing in front of them the whole time, wearing the court's grey.

She looked like Odile. That was the first thing — obscene, almost, how much she looked like him. The same sharp architecture of cheekbone. The same storm-grey eyes, though hers had a stillness his didn't, the stillness of water that has been held too long behind a dam. She wore a scribe's harness of bone clasps and grey cord, and strapped across her chest was a ledger so large it was nearly a door: the master volume, Tomas realized, the book from which all the others were copied, the book in which his own name was currently filed under *outstanding*.

Her eyes found Odile immediately. Of course they did. Nineteen years of servitude, and she had known this moment was written somewhere in the volume she carried.

"Well," she said.

Her voice was steady. Her hands, wrapped around the great ledger's spine, were not.

* * *

"Vessa," Odile said.

"Don't." One word, quiet, and it landed on him like a blow. She descended the last steps and stopped three away — close enough to touch, far enough that touching would be a choice. "Don't say it like you mean it. Don't stand there with your face doing that."

"Doing what?"

"*Remembering.*" The word tore something on its way out of her. "That's the cruelty of it, do you understand? Do you have any idea? I have spent nineteen years filing souls in this hall, and every single day I have known exactly what I traded and who I traded it for, and you—" She stopped. Breathed. The magistrates watched with the terrible patience of things that measure time in centuries. "The rite took your memories, yes. But they told me — the clerks told me, when I arrived — that the exile would take the rest. That within a year you wouldn't know you'd ever had a sister. And I thought, good. Good, let it take it, let him be free of it, let him sell charms in some clean city and never wake at night wondering if I'm cold." Her knuckles whitened on the ledger. "But then I'd see the entries. Your file. It comes across my desk every year for review — the Eriksen contracts, all of them, and yours is the thickest — and every year there's a note appended. *Subject retains residual affect. Subject retains dreams.* You *dreamed*, Odile. For seven years, you dreamed of a kitchen, and you didn't know why, and I knew why, and neither of us got to forget."

"Vessa." Odile stepped forward and she stepped back, and the sound he made then was small and broken and Tomas would hear it in his sleep for years. "I didn't choose to forget you. I would never—"

"You chose *everything else*." She laughed, and it was his laugh, the same one Tomas had fallen for across a charm stall at dusk, except flensed of all warmth. "You chose to sign. You chose to let them think it was your name on the debt. You chose a rite that carved you hollow rather than tell anyone — rather than tell *me* — that my brother was buying my life with his. Did you think that was mercy? Did you think I wanted to be a secret?" Her voice dropped, and somehow that was worse. "I was eleven. I was eleven years old, and I woke up in this hall with a clerk explaining compound interest to me, and the only thing that kept me sane was knowing my brother had done something so enormous, so *impossible*, that someday — someday — he'd come down those stairs and we'd finally talk about it. Nineteen years, Odile. I kept your file in order. I corrected the clerks' errors in it myself. I memorized your handwriting from the signature pages. And now you're here, and you don't know me, and you're looking at me like I'm a stranger who happens to be wearing your mother's eyes."

"I know your eyes," Odile whispered.

"No," Vessa said. "You know a feeling. There's a difference."

And then, because she was her brother's sister and made of the same stubborn weather, she turned away from him — deliberately, the way one closes a door — and faced Tomas.

"You," she said. "The soul on page 114. The maturing claim." She looked him up and down with the flat appraisal of someone who has read ten thousand lives and buried most of them. "He came down for you."

"He insisted," Tomas said.

"They usually do. It's in the prophecy, you know. We keep a copy." Something flickered in her face then — not softness, nothing so kind, but a fatigue so old it had worn grooves. "Do you know what the worst part of serving this court is? It isn't the years. It's that the work is *good*. Every soul I file is a life someone thought was worth a bargain. I hold the evidence of how much people love each other. Thousands of entries. Mothers, brothers, lovers, strangers. All of it love, and all of it priced." She adjusted the ledger on its strap, a small motion, housewifely, heartbreaking. "Your entry is well-made, at least. Your grandmother negotiated terms I've rarely seen bettered. She loved you very precisely."

"She never told me any of it," Tomas said. "I found the letter after she died."

"They never tell. Telling makes the debt contestable." Vessa glanced back at Odile — one glance, quick, stolen, and Tomas saw in it the whole shape of what she would not let herself feel. "He taught me that. Or I taught him. At eleven, you learn the law faster than you learn grief."

* * *

It was Odile who broke the silence, and he did it the way he seemed to do everything now — badly, honestly, with no plan.

"I'm going to fix it," he said. "All of it. Your contract, mine, Tomas's, the girl Maren — the daughter, the one whose name is in the ledger. I found the clause. Debts signed under false names are void, and mine was signed under—" His hand closed over the sigil. "I'm going to spend it, Vessa. Everything. The name, the craft, the memories, whatever the sigil holds. I'll pay the whole price and void the contract and walk out of here with nothing, and that includes—" his voice caught, steadied, "—that includes forgetting you again. Properly, this time. Permanently."

The hall absorbed the words. Somewhere far above, water dripped onto stone, counting seconds in the mountain's own currency.

Vessa stared at him. And then she did the thing that undid Tomas completely: she set the great ledger down. On the floor of the Court of Quiet Things, with deliberate care, spine aligned to the seam of the stones, she set down the book she had carried for nineteen years — the book that was her sentence and her duty and, Tomas suspected, the only companion she'd been permitted — and she crossed the three steps between herself and her brother and took his face in both hands.

"You absolute fool," she said, and her composure finally, finally split. "You think that's a sacrifice? You think forgetting is the cost?" Tears cut clean lines through the fine ash dust on her cheeks. "I have spent nineteen years remembering for both of us. Do you know what I wouldn't give — what I would trade *every year of this servitude* — to put it down? To hand it to someone else and be free of it?" Her forehead came to rest against his. "You're not paying a price, Odile. You're asking me to keep carrying it alone. Again. Forever. Because after tonight there won't even be a file to check. There'll be nothing in your ledger at all."

"Then remember loudly," Odile said. His hands came up and gripped her wrists — not pulling her away, holding on. "If I can't carry it, you carry it. Tell the clerks. Write it in margins. Be insufferable about it. Make them hate the name Eriksen for generations." A wet, ruined half-laugh. "You always were louder than me."

"I was *better* than you," Vessa said, "at everything," and then the two of them were holding each other in the middle of the hall of ash and patience, nineteen years collapsing into an embrace that could not possibly hold all of it, and Tomas looked away, because some griefs are private even in public, and some reunions are wounds being cleaned rather than healed.

When he looked back, Vessa had stepped away, wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and become a record-keeper again — but changed. Looser at the shoulders. Like a woman who had set down something heavy and discovered her spine still worked.

"So," she said briskly, in the tone of someone refusing to allow a moment to become sacred. "The clause. False name. Show me the language."

Odile unrolled the copy of his contract he'd taken from the archive, and the siblings bent over it together, heads inclined, identical profiles lit by the not-light, and for a moment they looked like children doing homework by a kitchen fire, and Tomas had to press his knuckles to his mouth.

"There," Odile said. "*Executed under seal of identity.* If the seal-name differs from the legal person—"

"It doesn't matter if it differs," Vessa said sharply. "It matters if you can *prove* it differs, before the bench, with testimony." She straightened. Looked at the magistrates. Looked at the stairs. And then she said the thing that turned Tomas's blood to ice water:

"And there's exactly one witness in existence who knows what name you were born under, little brother. Me. And my testimony is worthless — I'm court property. Anything I say is a footnote, not evidence." Her jaw tightened. "Unless."

"Unless what?" Tomas asked.

Vessa looked at her brother for a long moment. Whatever passed between them needed no words; it was the oldest language either of them spoke.

"Unless," she said, turning to the bench of ash, "the court itself produces its original registry — the true roll, the pre-contract record of the Eriksen births — and reads it aloud into evidence. Which it will refuse to do. Unless compelled." Her chin lifted. "And there is one party present with standing to compel it. One party whose soul is currently held as collateral under the very contract in question."

The nearest magistrate's oval face rotated toward Tomas with the slow inevitability of a door opening.

"The soul on page 114," it said.

"Me," Tomas said. His mouth was dry. "What do I have to do?"

"You may demand discovery," the magistrate said. "Under the old forms. You may compel the court to open its original rolls and read the true name into the record — which, if it matches not the debtor's seal, voids the contract entire, releases all souls held beneath Eriksen signatures, and dissolves nineteen years of servitude in an afternoon." The oval tilted. "You may also fail. And a claimant who invokes discovery and fails forfeits all future standing. There will be no second petition. No appeal. No further negotiation, ever. Whatever the roll says becomes the final truth of every Eriksen, forever."

Silence. Even the dripping water above seemed to wait.

Tomas looked at Odile — at the man he'd met over inert charms and bad wine, whose name he'd learned was already a lie, whose whole self was mortgaged — and found, to his complete lack of surprise, that there had never really been a decision to make.

"Do it," Tomas said. "Open the roll. Read the name."

Vessa picked her ledger back up and hugged it to her chest like a shield, and her eyes — her mother's eyes, their mother's eyes — shone with something that was terror and hope in equal, ruinous measure.

"Then listen carefully," she said, "all of you. Because whatever comes out of that roll next decides whether my brother walks out of this mountain a man—" her voice caught, and she pushed through it, "—or a blank page. And I have waited nineteen years to find out which."

Far above them, at the top of the Stair of Names, the not-light began, slowly, to descend.
