## 21. A Man Without a Name

There is a window.

That is the first thing. Not a name, not a place, not a self — a window, with light coming through it the color of weak tea, and dust turning slowly in the light like things that have nowhere to be. He watches the dust for a long time because watching is easier than thinking, and because when he tries to think, he finds the shelves of his mind half-empty, and the emptiness has a shape he does not want to look at yet.

His hands are on top of the blanket. He looks at them as if they belong to a stranger who has been introduced to him badly — *this is Odile*, someone should say, *Odile, these are your hands* — but no one says anything, and the hands stay strange. There is a mark on his left wrist. A pale circle, smooth as river stone, like a scar where something used to be inked and was not so much removed as *spent*. He rubs it with his thumb. The skin is warm there, faintly, the way a coin stays warm in a pocket long after you've forgotten putting it there.

He cannot remember what it was.

He cannot remember most things, he realizes, in the slow way one realizes a room is cold — not all at once but by degrees, by the parts that keep going numb. He knows what bread is called. He knows what rain sounds like, though he can't say how he knows, since he seems also to know that rain hasn't fallen here in years. He knows the word *Vellharrow* the way you know the word for a door: it opens onto something, he's sure of it, but right now all he gets is wood grain and a latch.

And there is a man asleep in a chair beside the bed, with his head tipped back and his mouth slightly open, and when Odile sees him something happens in his chest that has no word attached to it at all. It isn't recognition exactly. Recognition would come with facts. This comes without any — just a pull, low and certain, like the tide knowing the moon. His chest hurts around it. He thinks: *I don't know you.* He thinks, a half-breath later, quieter: *I would know you anywhere.*

The man wakes as if Odile's looking had weight. His eyes find Odile's and go wide and wet very fast, and then he smiles, and the smile is doing a great deal of work — holding up grief, holding down joy, holding everything in the middle perfectly still.

"Hello," the man says. His voice is rough with sleep and something else. "You're awake."

"Yes," Odile says. His own voice surprises him — hoarse, unfamiliar, like hearing a letter read aloud by someone else. "I'm sorry. I don't—" He stops. Starts again. The words feel borrowed. "I don't remember you."

"I know." The man leans forward, elbows on his knees, and doesn't touch him, though every line of him wants to; Odile can see the want sitting on him like weather. "Tomas. My name is Tomas."

"Tomas," Odile repeats, and the chest-thing pulls again, harder, and he puts a hand flat over his sternum as if to hold a door shut against it. "Why do I— why does my heart do that? When I say your name?"

Tomas's smile wobbles. He recovers it. "Because you've had a fever," he says. "A bad one. You were out for four days. The healer said memory might be... patchy. For a while."

"Patchy," Odile says. He tests the word and finds it too small. He looks around the room instead — small, clean, one candle burned down to a puddle, a kettle, two chairs, a coat hung on a peg that he feels, absurdly, that he owns. On the table beside Tomas's elbow sits a book. Thick. Old. Closed, with a strap around it, and Tomas's hand rests on the cover the way a hand rests on a sleeping animal it doesn't entirely trust.

"What's that?" Odile asks.

"Nothing," Tomas says, too quickly. Then, gentler, more honestly: "Not nothing. But not tonight. Tonight you should eat something. Can you sit up?"

He can. The room tilts once and settles. Tomas moves around him with an ease that suggests practice — days of practice, Odile thinks, days of this chair and this vigil and this face arranged into calm — and brings bread and broth, and Odile eats while Tomas watches him with an expression he can't parse, hunger and guilt braided together, like a man feeding someone he has wronged or been wronged by, or both.

"You keep looking at me like I'm going to break," Odile says.

"You did break," Tomas says softly. "Or — no. That's not fair. You didn't break. You were *spent*. Like a coin." He catches himself, jaw tightening. "Forget that. Eat your bread."

But Odile's thumb has already gone back to his wrist, circling the smooth pale place, and this time the warmth answers under his skin like something knocking from inside a wall.

* * *

Sleep takes him in pieces after that, and each piece is a different country.

In one, there is a stair. He is walking down it — or up it; the dream won't commit — and every step has a word carved into it, worn nearly smooth, and he understands that the words are names, and that some of them have been walked on so long they've become nothing but texture, and he is crying in the dream without any sadness he can locate, the way stone weeps groundwater, just seeping.

In another, there is snow falling upward, and a hall where nobody speaks because speaking would be redundant, and a figure behind a desk made of ash who looks at him with infinite patience and says, in a voice like a ledger closing: *Paid in full.* And in the dream he feels relief so vast it frightens him awake.

He surfaces gasping once, deep in the night, and Tomas is there immediately, one hand hovering over his shoulder — not landing, always hovering, always asking permission with the space between them.

"The mountain," Odile says. "I dreamed about a mountain."

"You dream about a lot of things," Tomas says. "Dreams aren't records. Go back to sleep."

But Odile lies in the dark listening to Tomas breathe, and thinks: *You're lying to me. You're lying to me kindly, the way you'd bandage a wound, but you're lying,* and finds, strangely, that he doesn't mind. That trust arrives in him ahead of evidence, unearned and total, like water finding a channel that was carved long ago. Whoever this man is to me, Odile thinks, drifting, whatever happened — he carried me somewhere. I can see it in his shoulders. People carry things in their shoulders, even when their arms are empty.

* * *

Morning comes gray and soft, and with it, rain.

It begins while Odile is propped in the window seat with tea he can't taste properly, a fine silvering against the glass, then faster, then the sound of it on the street below — and people come out of doorways to stand in it. He watches them. They stand in the rain with their faces turned up and their eyes shut, and several of them are crying, openly, unashamed, letting the sky do part of their weeping for them, and Odile's throat closes around nothing.

"Why are they crying?" he asks. "It's only rain."

Tomas stands beside him, close enough that their sleeves touch. "It hasn't rained here in seven years," he says. "Seven years of dry sky. People forgot it could."

"That's not possible," Odile says. "Weather doesn't work like that. Weather doesn't *wait*."

"No," Tomas agrees, and there is something complicated in his voice, something held carefully between pride and mourning. "Usually it doesn't."

Odile watches a boy in the street below spin with his arms out, mouth open to catch it, and feels a homesickness for a place he is standing in. It's the worst feeling he has ever had, and he has the sense — fleeting, like a fish glimpsed under ice — that he has had worse. Many worse. That worse is back there somewhere, in the empty shelves, wearing his handwriting.

"There's a storm coming," he hears himself say. "A real one. Off the mountains. Tomorrow, maybe the day after."

Tomas goes very still. "How do you know that?"

Odile frowns. "I don't know. My knees know it. My teeth." He laughs, embarrassed, and rubs the smooth place on his wrist. "That's ridiculous. Ignore me. What do I know about storms?"

"More than anyone," Tomas says quietly, and then, when Odile looks at him: "Nothing. You know nothing about storms. Forget I said that."

"You keep telling me to forget things," Odile says. "You realize that's the one thing I'm already doing constantly. You don't have to help."

It lands harder than he means it to. Tomas turns away toward the window and stands there a moment with his hand pressed flat against his own sternum, mirroring without knowing it the gesture Odile made yesterday, and Odile understands suddenly that this man is carrying something enormous and has decided, deliberately, to carry it alone.

"Tell me one thing," Odile says. "Not everything. One thing. Whatever you think I can hold."

Tomas is quiet for a long time. Rain writes and rewrites itself on the glass. When he finally speaks, his voice is steady the way a rope bridge is steady — engineered, deliberate, holding.

"You're kind," he says. "Whatever else you've forgotten — and it's a lot, it's more than a lot — you're kind, and you're stubborn, and you make terrible jokes when you're scared, and you once told me that belonging isn't something you remember your way into. It's something you choose. Every day. Like getting up." He turns, and his eyes are red-rimmed but level. "So. Get up. Come downstairs. The stall needs opening."

"*Stall?*" Odile says.

"Your stall. Charms." A ghost of a smile. "They don't work. Nobody minds. You sell them anyway, mostly because people like being wished well by you."

"That's the most absurd thing I've ever heard," Odile says, "and I believe every word of it," and Tomas laughs — a cracked, startled laugh, like ice giving way over living water — and something in the room eases by a degree neither of them will name.

* * *

The city, when they walk out into it, is drenched and transformed.

Rain has gotten into everything overnight — into the mortar of the walls, the seams of the awnings, the cracks in the flagstones where green shoots have apparently been waiting seven years for permission. The market smells of wet canvas and woodsmoke and mud, which Odile learns is called *petrichor* by people who like words, and he likes words, he discovers, he likes them enormously, and he wonders briefly if that was always true or if it's new, a compensation, a coin given in exchange for something else.

People stop them in the lane. That's the thing he wasn't prepared for. A woman with flour on her forearms crosses the street to grip his arm and say, "You came back," and a man outside the chandlery touches his hat and says, "We heard, we heard," and an old woman simply takes his hand in both of hers and holds it and cries a little and says thank you, thank you, thank you, without explaining what for.

"Do they think I did something?" Odile murmurs, when the crowd thins.

"Yes," Tomas says.

"What?"

"Ask me again in a year," Tomas says. "Maybe ten."

Odile lets it go. He is learning, in these first hours of himself, that his ignorance has a texture — that some absences ache (there is a sister-shaped hole somewhere in him, he keeps finding edges of it, a lullaby he half-knows, a name that starts with a sound like wind through a doorway) and others are merely quiet, rooms with the furniture gone but the floor still solid. He decides, with the stubbornness Tomas apparently predicted, to walk on the solid floors first. The aching doors can wait. They'll still be there. Absence, he suspects, is very patient.

At the stall — his stall, absurd, wonderful, a folding table of trinkets that hum with nothing but good intentions — there is a letter wedged under the scale.

Tomas picks it up, reads the front, and goes white. He holds it out without a word.

The paper is expensive, travel-worn, sealed with plain wax. The hand on the front is careful and unfamiliar:

*For the charm-seller of Vellharrow — who will not remember me, and does not need to.*

Odile breaks the seal. Inside, in that same careful hand, is a single page. He reads it standing in the rain, because the rain seems to insist on it, and Tomas reads it over his shoulder, breathing audibly.

*Little brother.*

*I kept the ledgers. All of them, every year, every name. It was mine to keep and I kept it well, and now the keeping is done, and I am coming up the long stair to see the sky, which I am told has remembered how to rain.*

*I am told you spent everything. Of course you did. You always paid more than the price. Mother said you'd give away your own name someday and she laughed when she said it, and look.*

*Don't try to remember me. Remembering was the payment; let it stay paid. Just leave a lamp in your window. I'll know the house by the light, and you'll know me by — well. You'll know me. Some things survive even the best bookkeeping.*

*I'm bringing bread. Yours was always better, but mine will do.*

*— V*

Odile reads it twice. Three times. The rain runs off his chin onto the page, and he tilts it away to protect the ink without deciding to, some reflex older than memory, and Tomas makes a small wounded sound beside him and puts a hand on his shoulder at last — finally, finally landing — and Odile covers it with his own.

"I don't know who this is," Odile says. His voice comes out wrecked. "I don't remember a sister. I don't remember *anything*, Tomas, there's just this — shape, this V-shaped absence, and I can hear her laughing at something our mother said, and I don't know why I'm crying."

"You're not crying because you remember," Tomas says. "You're crying because you *kept* something anyway. Whatever they took, they couldn't get all of it. Look at you. You're soaked through and you're smiling."

"I am not," Odile says, and then discovers that he is, helplessly, enormously, tears and grin arriving together like weather from opposite fronts.

Below the signature, in different ink, smaller, added later in a hand less careful — hurried, joyful, human — someone has written one more line:

*P.S. Leave the lamp lit. Also: I never liked your charms. They never worked. Keep making them anyway.*

Odile folds the letter along its old creases and tucks it inside his coat, over his heart, where paper goes when it matters. Then he lifts his head and looks down the long gray lane, where the rain is still coming down on a city that spent seven years learning drought, and far off — past the rooftops, past the walls, past everything — he can just see the mountain, hooded in cloud, its crown dissolving weather into the sky like a debt finally forgiven.

Something in him salutes it. He doesn't know what, and he doesn't ask.

"Lamp," he says to Tomas. "Tonight. In the window. We should find the brightest one in Vellharrow."

"We?" Tomas says, and the word cracks open in the middle, full of everything he isn't saying — the closed ledger under his arm, the four days in the chair, the whole buried mountain of it.

"We," Odile confirms. He doesn't know why the word is so easy. He doesn't need to. Some knowledge lives lower than memory, in the wrists and the sternum and the tide-pull of the chest, and it tells him plainly: *this one stayed; this one carried you; build forward from here.*

He shakes the rain out of his hair like a dog, laughs at Tomas's protest, and turns toward home — toward a stall that sells wishes that don't work, a window waiting for its lamp, and a stranger with his sister's laugh somewhere on the road, bringing bread.
