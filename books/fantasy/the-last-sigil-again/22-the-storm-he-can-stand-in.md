## 22. The Storm He Can Stand In

**Odile**

The first drop hit the awning of his stall like a knuckle rapped on a door.

Odile looked up from the charm he was wrapping — a tin bird with no magic in it at all, sold to children who liked the way it caught light — and watched the canvas darken in spreading blooms. A second drop struck the cobbles. A third. Then the sky simply gave up pretending and opened, and rain came down on Vellharrow for the first time in seven years.

The market did not scatter. It did the opposite. Stall-keepers stepped out from under their awnings and stood with their faces tipped back like sunflowers, mouths open, eyes shut. Old Maren's empty booth — she hadn't set up in days — collected puddles in its corners, and no one minded. Somewhere down the row, a woman began to laugh, high and helpless, and then others joined her until the whole street sounded like something being born.

Odile stayed where he was, one hand still on the wrapped charm, and felt something move through him that had no name.

It wasn't wonder. Wonder was what the others wore, the bright astonishment of people seeing an impossible thing. This was older than that. The rain ran down the back of his neck and he shivered — not with cold but with recognition, the way you shiver when someone says your name in a crowd and it turns out they were calling someone else, but for half a heartbeat you were *known*.

*Home,* said some voice in him that was not quite his own. *This is what home sounds like.*

He had never heard that voice before. Or — and this thought arrived quietly, without alarm, the way most true things arrive — perhaps he had heard it all his life and only now had the silence around it broken enough to notice.

He stepped out from behind the stall.

The rain soaked him through in moments. His shirt clung to his shoulders; water found the seam of his collar and traced his spine. He should have gone inside. Any sensible merchant would have gone inside. But his feet carried him out into the middle of the street instead, into the deepest part of the downpour, and he stood there with his arms slightly away from his body, palms open, letting it strike them.

He didn't try to do anything. That was the strange part, the part he would turn over later and turn over again. Seven years in Vellharrow, seven years of dry skies and Wardens and charms that were only tin and glass, and not once — not once in any of it — had he looked at a cloud and wanted anything from it. He knew that about himself the way you know you've never committed a crime: as an absence, a clean record. And yet standing under the breaking sky, he felt less like a man witnessing weather and more like a man returning to a house he'd been born in, running his hand along the banister in the dark and finding every worn place exactly where his memory said it should be.

Except he had no memory saying so. That was the joke of it. He laughed, alone in the rain in the middle of the emptying street, and the laugh came out cracked and startled him more than the thunder did.

Thunder rolled eastward, toward Mount Sarn, and the mountain took it and gave back an echo that went on longer than any echo should, deep and vast, like the mountain clearing its throat after a long silence. Odile turned to look at it. The peak wore a collar of storm-cloud, and lightning moved inside the cloud in slow, deliberate veins, and he felt — he could not say how else to put it — *acknowledged*. As if the mountain had looked at the thin man standing soaked in the market street, and inclined its head.

"Madman," someone said fondly as they hurried past. "You'll catch your death."

"I don't think I will," Odile said, and found that he meant it in a way he couldn't explain.

His wrist ached. It had ached for three days now, ever since the morning he'd woken in his own bed with no idea why the window was shattered inward rather than outward, no idea why Tomas sat beside him with red-rimmed eyes and a closed ledger on his knees, no idea why his wrist bore a pale scar shaped like nothing at all — a mark faded almost to nothing, a receipt after the purchase has been spent. He'd asked Tomas about it. Tomas had kissed his knuckles and said, *You fell*, and Odile, examining the man's face with a stranger's careful attention, had decided that some mercies were worth accepting unexamined.

There were gaps in him. He knew it the way an amputee knows weather in the missing limb. Whole corridors of himself had been walked through and left bare: he could remember the taste of every tea he'd drunk in Vellharrow but not whether there had been anyone, anywhere, who drank tea with him before. He remembered learning to wrap charms, but not learning to make anything else. He remembered being afraid of the Wardens, but not why fear had once felt like a language he was fluent in.

And underneath all the gaps, like bedrock under topsoil, two things remained that he could not account for.

One: he loved Tomas. Not the small growing love of seven months' courtship — or so he believed; the love he felt was load-bearing, structural, the kind that takes decades to build. When Tomas entered a room, something in Odile's chest settled, the way a house settles at night. He had stopped asking where such love came from. It was simply true, and truth needs no provenance.

Two: the rain. The rain felt like forgiveness.

He stood in it until his fingers pruned, until the market had emptied and the gutters ran brown and singing, and then, because the world insisted on continuing, he went back to his stall and began pulling the oilcloth over the charms — though who would buy a tin bird in a flood, he couldn't imagine, and he found himself grinning at the absurdity of it, grinning like a fool into the downpour.

That was when he saw her.

A woman stood at the mouth of the lane, half-hidden by rain-haze, watching him. She was travel-worn and mountain-worn both, mud to her knees, hair plastered flat, a satchel slung across her body the way soldiers carry dispatches. She had the look of someone who has walked a very long way to stand in a very specific spot, and having arrived, has suddenly forgotten what she came to say.

She was also, Odile realized with a jolt that had nothing to do with surprise and everything to do with the ache in his wrist, *familiar*. Not known — familiar, which is a different organ of recognition entirely. Familiar is the body remembering what the mind cannot. The way she pushed wet hair from her face with the back of her wrist. The stubborn set of her shoulders, squared against weather and everything else. Something in the architecture of her face that made him think, absurdly, of his own reflection, of looking into a mirror angled slightly wrong.

She started toward him. Stopped. Started again.

Odile wiped his hands on his apron — a useless gesture, he was drenched — and waited.

"You're the charm-seller," she said, when she was near enough that she didn't have to raise her voice against the rain, though she raised it anyway, as if the words needed force to leave her. "Eriksen's stall."

"That's me." He hesitated. "Well. Odile Eriksen. Though I don't know how you'd know the name. I don't think we've met."

Something crossed her face then — a flinch, quick and devastating, like a bird striking glass — and was mastered. He watched her master it. Watched her take a breath and fold whatever had just happened inside her away, the way you fold a letter you'll read later, in private, when your hands can be allowed to shake.

"No," she said. "We haven't met." Her eyes went to his wrist, to the faded scar, and stayed there a long moment. When she looked up again they were wet, and he was courteous enough to blame the rain. "I'm told you sell charms. Inert ones. Nothing that hums."

"Nothing that hums," he agreed. "Warden-safe. Mostly they're for luck. Which is to say, they do nothing, and people feel better holding them, and I've stopped feeling dishonest about it."

"Do you believe in luck?" she asked. It was a strange question. There was something beneath it, something urgent, and she was watching him with an intensity that made the rain feel warm by comparison.

Odile considered. He considered the gaps in himself. The corridor-bare rooms. The love with no provenance. The scar like a spent receipt. The way a mountain had inclined its head to him in the thunder.

"I believe," he said slowly, "that some things are owed to us. And that most of us go our whole lives without collecting." He shrugged, sheepish at his own gravity. "I don't know why I believe that. But I do. Strongly. It's one of the few strong beliefs I have."

The woman made a sound — half laugh, half wound — and pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

"Are you all right?" he asked.

"Ask me again tomorrow," she said. "Ask me again every day for a while, if you don't mind. I've come a long way, and—" She stopped herself. Started over, choosing each word like a footfall on ice. "I'm new to the city. I need work. I'm good with records, with ledgers, with keeping accounts straight. And I was told — I was *told* — that the charm-seller might be hiring."

He wasn't hiring. He had never hired anyone in his life; he barely made enough to feed himself and Tomas on the nights Tomas stayed. But he heard himself say, "I can't pay much."

"I don't need much."

"And I'd want references."

"You won't find any," she said, "but you'll find I don't lie to you. Whatever else happens. However strange the questions get. I'll never lie to you, Odile Eriksen. That's my reference, and it's the only one I've got."

*However strange the questions get.* He turned the phrase over. It sounded like a promise made in advance of crimes neither of them had described yet. It sounded like the opening chapter of something. He found, to his mild alarm, that he wanted the work of it — the strangeness, the questions, the slow archaeology of another person. He had spent what felt like his whole life selling small comforts to strangers. Perhaps it was time to buy something back.

"All right," he said. "Monday. Come Monday, and bring your ledgers, and we'll see what we can build." He extended his hand. "Welcome to Vellharrow. It rains now, apparently. Nobody will stop talking about it for weeks."

She took his hand. Her grip was fierce and brief, and her eyes closed, and for one moment she held his hand the way you hold onto the edge of something when you've nearly drowned.

Then she let go, and stepped back, and became merely a stranger seeking employment on a rainy day.

"Monday," she repeated. Her voice was steady. Her eyes were not, but he had decided, generously, to keep blaming the weather. "I'll be here."

She turned and walked off down the glistening street, and Odile watched her go, and the ache in his wrist sang like a plucked string, and he did not know why he raised his other hand in farewell to a person he had never seen before in his life.

But he did.

**Tomas**

Tomas watched from the doorway of the shuttered bookshop across the square, ledger hugged to his chest, and let the rain decide what his face was doing.

He had come to walk Odile home. That was all. Seven days since the mountain, seven days since he'd woken on the mountainside with frost melting off his coat and Odile asleep against his shoulder, breathing, alive, blank as new snow — seven days of telling him nothing, showing him nothing, carrying the whole burning weight of it alone so that the man he loved could be spared even the shape of what he'd paid. The ledger rode under Tomas's arm everywhere he went now. He slept with it nearer than his pillow. He had promised himself he would never open it again, and had already broken that promise twice, reading by candlelight the entries in his grandmother's dialect, the debt, the dates, the name signed twice in two different hands.

*The thief who returns will leave with nothing of himself.*

The prophecy had been right, in the way prophecies are right — technically, cruelly. Odile had returned. Odile had left with nothing of himself. What the prophecy had never bothered to say was whether *nothing* could grow back.

Tomas intended to find out.

From across the square he watched a mud-spattered woman approach the stall, watched Odile speak with her, shake her hand, watch her walk away. He recognized her at forty paces and his heart did a complicated thing — grief and relief braided tight, because he knew who she was now; he'd pieced it together from the stair of names, from the record-keeper's cell with its thousand years of files, from the way the court's archivists had bowed to her as she passed, freed among the freed, walking up out of the mountain with her brother's name held carefully in her mouth like a coal she didn't dare set down.

She had chosen not to tell him. Tomas understood that too. Some truths, delivered too early, are just wounds with paperwork.

Now she had come anyway. To stand near him. To be lied to gently, every day, by omission, while she rebuilt whatever could be rebuilt. Tomas thought: *that is either the cruelest thing I have ever witnessed or the bravest,* and knew it was both, and knew it was none of his business, and felt his throat close anyway.

The rain intensified. Across the square, Odile abandoned the pretense of closing the stall and simply stood in it, arms out, head back, laughing at the sky — and Tomas forgot the ledger, forgot the mountain, forgot the court and its patient ash-faced magistrates, because there was the man he loved standing in the first rain in seven years looking like a man being *reminded* of something wonderful.

He crossed the square without deciding to.

"Tomas!" Odile called when he saw him, and the delight in it was uncomplicated, total. "Look at it! Look at the sky! Seven years of dust and the moment I finally—"

"The moment you finally what?"

"I don't know." Odile laughed again, shaking water from his hair like a dog, boyish and impossible. "The sentence keeps starting itself and then losing the end. Isn't that strange? It's like the rain knows something about me that I've forgotten."

Tomas reached him and took his face in both hands, and Odile went quiet, surprised, pleased.

"You're soaked," Tomas said.

"You're soaked."

"We're idiots."

"We're idiots," Odile agreed happily, and kissed him in the middle of the flooded market square, in front of God and the mountain and the last few stragglers hurrying home, and if any of them noticed that the kiss had the desperate thoroughness of a reunion rather than a greeting, none of them said so.

When they broke apart, Odile rested his forehead against Tomas's. "I met someone," he said. "A woman. Just now. She wants work."

Tomas kept his breathing level. "Did she say her name?"

"She didn't." A pause. Odile's eyes narrowed slightly, working at something. "Which is odd, isn't it? People always say their names. It's the first coin anyone spends." He shook his head and the puzzlement slid off him like the rain. "Anyway. Monday. She seems honest. You get a feel for these things, selling trinkets."

"You get a feel for lots of things," Tomas said.

"Mm." Odile studied him. There it was again — that careful, patient attention, the stranger's gaze he sometimes turned on Tomas when he thought Tomas wasn't looking, cataloguing, wondering, choosing not to ask. Tomas braced for the question. *Who am I to you, really? Why do I love you like a house? What happened to my wrist? What's in the ledger?*

Instead Odile smiled and said, "Walk me home?"

"Always."

They gathered the stall together, Odile chattering about rain and gutters and whether tin birds rusted, and started up the hill toward the little rooms above the chandler's. The mountain loomed over the city's eastern shoulder, wearing its storm like a crown, and lightning walked its spine in slow strides, and Tomas noticed that Odile glanced at it once — only once — with an expression Tomas couldn't read. Not fear. Not longing. Something like two old colleagues passing on opposite sides of a street, acknowledging each other without stopping.

"Big place," Odile remarked.

"It is."

"Feels like it's watching us."

"It's done watching," Tomas said. "I think now it's just... listening."

"To what?"

"To names." Tomas squeezed his hand. "People saying their own names. Out loud. It likes that, I hear."

Odile looked at him sideways. "You've become strange since the storm started, you know. All these things you 'hear.' You used to be a respectable clerk."

"I was never a respectable clerk. I was fired from respectable clerking."

"Thank the gods for that, or we'd never have met." They walked in silence a moment, boots splashing, the city around them coming alive in the rain — shutters thrown open, children shrieking in the flooded lanes, someone beating a drum on a balcony for no reason at all. Then, quieter, almost shy, Odile said: "Do you ever feel like you've missed something? Like there was a version of your life where you learned things you can't remember learning? I keep having this feeling, lately. Like I paid for something. Not money. Bigger than money. And the payment cleared, and the goods arrived, and I just—" he gestured vaguely at the rain, at Tomas, at himself, at all of it, "—can't see what they were."

Tomas stopped walking.

The rain drummed on the awning of the chandler's shop below them. Somewhere a child screamed with laughter. Lightning flickered far off over Sarn, patient as a lighthouse.

"What do you see," Tomas asked carefully, "when you look at your life?"

Odile considered it honestly, head tilted, water dripping from his nose. "A stall," he said. "Trinkets. A city that doesn't trust magic, which suits me fine, because I don't seem to have any. A man I love so much it scares me, and I don't know why it scares me, except that it feels like loving something I was *given* rather than something I earned, and I hate debts." He huffed a laugh. "Listen to me. Debts. Where does a charm-seller get opinions about debts?"

"Maybe everyone does," Tomas said. "Maybe debts are just the interest everyone pays for being alive."

"Spoken like a former clerk." Odile bumped his shoulder. "Come on. I'm freezing, and if we're lucky the chandler's wife has left soup on our step again, and if we're luckier she's left bread with it."

They climbed the stairs. Inside, the room was dim and smelled of tallow and old paper, and Tomas lit the lamp while Odile wrung out his shirt, complaining cheerfully, alive, ordinary, gloriously unremarkable. The ledger sat on the table where Tomas had left it. Odile glanced at it — he always glanced at it; Tomas had learned to read the glances, the faint snag of curiosity that never became a question — and then looked away, and hung his shirt by the fire, and said something about soup.

Later, much later, lying in the dark with rain still silvering the window and thunder muttering its slow retreat eastward, Odile spoke into the quiet.

"Tomas. Are we happy?"

The question hung there, guileless and enormous.

Tomas stared at the ceiling. He thought about a hall beneath a mountain where souls were filed like deeds. About a sister who had served a hundred years among ash magistrates to save a brother who no longer knew her face. About a page torn from a ledger, and a daughter whose name was spoken freely in the streets again. About the word *void* stamped across an ancient contract in ink that smoked. About a man who had owned one sigil and spent it all, and gotten in exchange exactly this: a damp room, a bowl of cold soup, a stranger who loved him, rain.

"Yes," Tomas said. "We are."

"Good." A pause. Odile's hand found his in the dark and held it, thumb tracing the knuckles, unhurried, certain. "Because it feels like the beginning of something. I can't tell you why. It just does. Like we've been given the start of a story, and the rest of it hasn't been written yet."

Tomas turned his head. In the grey wash of storm-light through the window, Odile's face was calm, open, waiting — a man with no past and therefore every possible future, a man who had chosen, in the only way that matters, to be whoever he was becoming.

"No," Tomas said, and kissed his temple, and felt the last cold stone in his chest finally, fully melt. "It's been written. We're just the ones who get to live in it now."

Outside, the rain went on falling on Vellharrow, soft and steady and forgiving, and on the mountain where the stair of names ran up into the light, and on the market square where a woman with mud on her boots lay awake in a rented room rehearsing, for the hundredth time, the ordinary words she would say on Monday:

*Hello. I'm here. My name is — well. We'll get to that.*

*My name is Eriksen.*
