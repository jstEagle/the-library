## 1. Charms and Small Mercies

The rats of Vaelharrow were terrible gossips.

Coral Nystrom learned this within her first week of selling charms at the Lantern Row night market, when a one-eared rat named Sootkin informed her — through the long, slow conversation of whisker-twitch and posture that passed between them — that the pie-seller three stalls down watered his filling with river water and hoped no one noticed. Coral had thanked him with a crust of cheese and told exactly no one, because telling people things was how you got noticed, and being noticed in Vaelharrow was how you disappeared.

Instead she used it. When a customer hesitated over which luck-charm to buy, Coral would glance toward the pie stall and say, "Perhaps not the meat pies tonight, mistress. The oyster cakes look fresher." And when the customer's husband spent the next morning hunched over a chamber pot, cursing the pies, he came back to her stall and bought three charms instead of one, and told everyone on Lantern Row that Nystrom's charms *worked*.

They did work. That was the trouble. They just didn't work the way anyone thought they did.

"Charms!" she called now, into the amber wash of lantern-light, keeping her voice at the cheerful pitch of a woman with nothing to hide. "Luck-charms, road-charms, charms against damp and disappointment! Baked fresh this morning!"

Nobody laughed, but somebody always smiled, and that was enough. A charm-seller was allowed to be a little funny. It made her harmless.

Her stall was a fold-down table wedged between a candle-dipper's cart and a man who sold secondhand boots by holding them up against people's feet without asking permission first. On the table lay her wares: knotted cords dyed madder-red, little cloth bundles stitched shut with thread pulled from her own hem, smooth river stones painted with symbols that meant nothing at all — because meaning things was illegal, and Coral Nystrom had been very carefully meaningless for eleven years.

The knots were real work. She'd learned them from her grandmother before the old woman died, back when the family still pretended to be ordinary cord-makers. Grandmother Maren had taught her the knots for patience, for safe passage, for a door that stays shut and a heart that stays open. What Maren had never taught her — what Coral had taught herself, in cellars and alleys, in whispers so quiet even she could barely hear them — was the other half of the trade.

Because here was the secret of Nystrom's luck-charms: a knot tied while a pigeon promised to roost above a gambler's window all night was worth ten knots tied by any honest hand. A bundle sewn while a rat swore — and rats took their oaths seriously, they were a solemn people — to lead a merchant's rival astray through the maze of the tannery district would do more for a man's fortune than a year of prayer. The charms themselves were theater. The animals were the magic.

And the magic itself was, officially, nothing. Because there was no such thing as talking to beasts. The Sigil Law had seen to that.

Coral arranged a row of stones with the ease of long practice, angling them so the candlelight caught their paint. Around her, the market breathed: frying onions and tallow smoke, the cry of the eel-man, two children chasing each other between stalls while their mother pretended not to see. Somewhere a fiddler played badly and nobody minded. This was the Vaelharrow that people believed in — warm, loud, safely ordinary — and Coral loved it the way you love a house built on top of something buried.

She knew what was underneath. Everyone knew, the way everyone knows the floor will hold. You just don't look down.

*Evening, small-fry,* said a voice at her ankle.

"Evening, Duchess," Coral murmured, barely moving her lips, smiling at her customers as though greeting an old friend who happened to be invisible. Which, technically. Duchess was a rat of considerable age and imperious bearing, gray gone silver at the muzzle, who considered herself the proprietor of the entire eastern quarter of the market and permitted Coral to operate there out of what she described as *sentiment*.

*Business good?*

"Steady." Coral handed a red knot-cord across the table to a young fishwife, took her coin, wished her calm seas. "You've been busy?"

*A matter of some urgency.* Duchess sat up on her haunches in the shadow beneath the table, tail curled like a punctuation mark. *The pigeons are upset.*

"The pigeons are always upset."

*About the bells tonight. The white tower rang nine after dusk. Nine is not the market bell. Nine is the* other *bell.*

Coral's hands went still over her stones. She knew about nine bells. Everyone in Vaelharrow knew about nine bells the way they knew about the Sigil Law — abstractly, gratefully, and with a firm refusal to imagine the details. Nine bells meant the Wardens of the Seal had found someone. Someone marked, someone suspected, someone whose name would be chalked off the census rolls by morning and spoken of afterward only in the lowered voices people used for the dead and the disgraced.

"When?" Coral asked.

*An hour past,* said Duchess. *Near Chandler's Walk. A woman. The pigeons say she kept birds — songbirds, finches, canaries — and she talked to them.*

A cold thread slid down Coral's spine. "Talked to them how? People talk to their birds all the time. 'Pretty Polly,' and all that."

*Talking,* said Duchess, with the delicacy of a creature who understood the difference better than most judges, *and* listening.

Across the market, the fiddler finished his tune. In the gap where the music had been, Coral heard the ordinary sounds of the night — coins, laughter, grease spitting in a pan — and under them, faint as a pulse, the great murmuring conversation of the city's animals: pigeons arguing over roost rights on the temple eaves, cats filing their evening reports, dogs complaining about the cold. A whole country within the country, invisible, unsanctioned, and utterly unaware that its chief interpreter stood behind a folding table selling painted rocks.

Eleven years since the Law. Eleven years since the day the sigils went up on every gate and lintel in Vaelharrow — pale geometric marks burned into stone and wood, humming faintly if you touched them, though touching them was also forbidden — and the priests of the Concordance announced that magic was dead, that the gods were dead, that the age of wonders had ended forever and good riddance. Eleven years since Coral, twelve years old and weeping in a cellar while soldiers marched past outside, pressed her face into her grandmother's shoulder and whispered *I can hear them, I can still hear them, why can I still hear them* — and felt her grandmother's arms go tight and terrified around her, and heard Maren say, in the voice of a woman memorizing a sentence for later use:

*"Then you will never, ever say so."*

So she hadn't. Not to the census-takers who came each spring with their ink and their questions. (*Any unusual affinities in the household? Any beasts that behave oddly around you?*) Not to the neighbors. Not, God forgive her, to anyone. She'd folded the gift up small, like cloth in a chest, and fed it scraps in the dark: a word to a stray dog here, a bargain with a gull there. Enough to keep the thing alive. Never enough to let it grow.

That was the arithmetic of survival in Vaelharrow, and Coral had gotten very good at it. A gift you must starve is still a gift; it just learns to eat quietly.

*The Warden wagons went east on the river road,* Duchess reported. *Toward the Sealhouse. The birds say the woman did not struggle. She asked them to feed her finches.*

Coral closed her eyes for one breath. Just one. Then she opened them, straightened her shawl, and smiled at the next customer — a broad-shouldered carter wanting a charm for his horses' health.

"Health-charm, master? Tied at dawn, best luck I've—"

He bought two. He always bought two; he was superstitious about odd numbers. Coral counted his coppers into her tin and did not think about the woman on Chandler's Walk, except that she thought of nothing else for the rest of the hour, the way you don't think about a splinter.

* * *

The market thinned as the night deepened. The eel-man packed up his trays. The boot-seller vanished mid-haggle, as he did every night around the ninth hour, leaving customers holding boots and questions. Lanterns guttered one by one until Lantern Row was a string of dim orange beads.

Coral packed her stones into their padded box, coiled her cords, and folded the table with the practiced snap of eleven years' routine. Duchess emerged from the shadows and sat on the cobbles beside her, ostentatiously grooming, in the manner of a rat who wished it known she was accompanying Coral by choice and not because of any sentiment, obviously.

"You could come home with me," Coral said. "It's cold tonight."

*I have a committee meeting under the granary,* said Duchess. *Fiscal matters. But walk me to the corner. The cats are restless, and one does not travel unescorted.*

They walked. The streets of Vaelharrow at night were canals of shadow between islands of lamplight, and Coral navigated them the way she navigated everything: smoothly, modestly, along routes chosen years ago for their lack of witnesses. Her rooms were above a chandler's shop on Bellwether Lane — one room, one window, one kettle, and a lock she'd paid a locksmith extra to make complicated, then never locked, because locks suggested you had something to protect.

Halfway down Bellwether Lane, Duchess stopped.

*Small-fry.*

"What?"

*The woman on Chandler's Walk.* The rat's whiskers trembled in the lamplight. *The pigeons sent a runner. She had children. Three. The youngest is sick — fever, the bad kind, the kind that burns. The neighbors are saying the mother took the child to every herb-wife in the quarter and none of them would touch her, because a fever-child is bad luck to treat and besides—* Duchess paused, and for once the imperial rat sounded simply old. *Besides, they're saying the mother has gone strange with grief. Saying she's asking everyone. Anyone. Saying she'll pay anything for a miracle.*

Coral stood very still in the middle of the lane.

"No," she said.

*You haven't heard my question yet.*

"I know your question. No."

*Small-fry—*

"She's grieving and frightened and desperate, and desperate people talk, and if she talks to the wrong person—" Coral started walking again, faster. "Duchess, I sell *painted rocks*. That's what I do. That's all I do."

*You sold the cooper's wife a charm last month and her husband's cough left him in two days. Half the market thinks you're blessed.*

"Then half the market can go to confession about it." Her voice cracked on the word *blessed*, and she hated that. "I'm not. I'm nothing. That's the whole point. Nothing is safe."

They reached the corner where their ways parted. Duchess sat back and looked up at her for a long moment, and Coral had the uncomfortable sensation of being measured by several ounces of rodent and found wanting.

*My grandmother,* said the rat at last, *lived under the floor of a baker's shop for six years. Every morning she could smell the bread. Every morning she starved on sawdust rather than risk the trap.* A pause. *I have always wondered whether she was wise or merely afraid.*

"She was wise," said Coral.

*Mm,* said Duchess, unconvinced. *Goodnight, small-fry. Feed your finches.*

"I don't have finches."

*No,* said Duchess, turning away into the dark. *But you might, someday. If you stopped being so careful.*

Coral climbed the stairs to her room, lit her lamp, put the kettle on. Through the window she could see a sliver of Vaelharrow rooftops, the distant white gleam of the Sealhouse tower where the nine bells had rung, and beyond it the dark line of the river, moving the way rivers move — patiently, endlessly, like something waiting.

She drank her tea. She banked the fire. She lay down in the dark and stared at the ceiling and performed the nightly ritual of forgetting: the woman on Chandler's Walk, the fever-child, the sound a gift makes when it starves.

She was almost asleep when someone knocked at her door.

Three soft knocks. Then, after a silence, a woman's voice — raw, scraped hollow, the voice of someone who had been knocking on doors all night and been turned away from all of them:

"Please. The charm-seller from the market. They said — please, I have coin, I have anything — my daughter is dying, and I need someone who *listens*."

Coral lay in the dark, heart slamming, and did not answer.

The knock came again. Softer. Almost a prayer.
