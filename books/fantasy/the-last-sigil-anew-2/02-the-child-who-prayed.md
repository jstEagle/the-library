## 2. The Child Who Prayed

The knock came at the worst possible hour, which was to say, any hour at all.

Coral Nystrom had a system for knocks. Three quick raps meant a customer wanting a luck-charm, and she could handle that through the door with her eyes closed. A slow, heavy pounding meant the Watch, and she had a whole routine of sleepy confusion prepared for that. But this knock — this was two soft taps, then nothing, then two more soft taps, like someone who couldn't quite bring themselves to ask for what they'd come for.

She knew before she opened it. She always knew, because the pigeons on her roofline told her things, and tonight they'd been restless in a way that had nothing to do with weather.

Marta Senn stood on her step holding a lantern that had nearly burned itself out, as if even the flame was too tired to keep going. She was a washerwoman with forearms like ship rope and a face that had forgotten how to do anything but worry. Behind her, half-hidden in the doorway shadow across the lane, waited a boy of maybe ten — her nephew, sent along because she hadn't wanted to walk alone.

"Coral." Marta's voice cracked on the second syllable. "Coral, I need— I don't know what I need. That's the truth of it. I don't know."

"You need tea," Coral said, because it was easier than saying *you need a miracle and I can't give you one*. "Come in before you wake the whole street. Tam, you too. There's honey cake if you promise not to tell your aunt I spoil you."

Tam grinned despite everything, the way children do, and slipped inside. Marta followed more slowly, and Coral noticed how she looked up and down the lane first — a habit everyone in Vaelharrow had learned without ever being taught. The Sigil Law didn't post notices anymore. It didn't need to. People watched their own streets for free.

Inside, the shop smelled of dried lavender and candle wax and the particular dustiness of birdseed, because Coral sold charms and remedies and, unofficially, seed for people who liked feeding pigeons, which was itself a small act of rebellion nobody had thought to outlaw yet. She put the kettle on and got out three cups, and Marta sat with her hands wrapped around her elbows like she was holding herself together with friction.

"It's Lissie," Marta said finally. "My girl. The fever came four days ago and it won't break. Physician came, physician went. He gave me tinctures and a bill and a look, that last one being the only thing he gave me for free." Her laugh came out wrong, bent in the middle. "He said to prepare myself. Prepare myself. What does that even mean? How does a person *prepare*?"

"Nobody knows," Coral said quietly. "They just say it."

Tam had found the honey cake and was eating it in the careful way of a child who understood that treats during bad times were fragile things. The kettle began its low complaint toward boiling, and Coral poured, and for a moment the little shop held nothing but steam and silence.

Then Marta set down her cup, untouched, and looked at Coral with an expression that stripped away every polite fiction between them.

"I've heard things," she said. "About you. About your gran, rest her. About the rats in this quarter never touching grain from your bins. About how old Hesta's cat came home after nine days missing, the very morning Hesta bought a charm off you. About how your pigeons—" She stopped. Started again. "I'm not asking you to admit anything. I know what happens to people who admit things. I'm just asking. On my knees if I have to. Is there anything — *anything* — you can do?"

"Marta—"

"They say the Law took the magic out of the world," Marta pressed on, and her voice had gone fierce now, the fierceness of a mother with nothing left to lose. "Fine. Then explain my Lissie burning up like a lamp with no oil left. Explain why prayer doesn't work anymore except when it does, once in a hundred years, for somebody else's child. I prayed, Coral. I prayed until my knees bled on those chapel stones, and the god they say is dead didn't so much as twitch. So either the gods are gone, or they're *choosing*, and either way I'm standing in the shop of the only woman in Vaelharrow the animals follow around like a parade."

Coral's hands had gone cold around her cup.

This was the moment. This was the exact moment she had spent eleven years building walls against. Every harmless charm, every whispered word to a rat about where not to scurry, every careful nothing she'd made of her life — all of it existed so that no one would ever sit across from her and say *is there anything you can do*.

Because the answer was yes. And yes was the most dangerous word in Vaelharrow.

"The Law says blessings are sedition," Coral said. Her voice came out flat and practiced. "You know what happened to the Fenner woman. You were at the vigil."

"I lit candles at that vigil," Marta said. "And I watched them take her away for leaving bread on a sick neighbor's sill and calling it blessed. And I thought, well, that's madness, but it's far-off madness. It's not my madness." She leaned forward. "It's mine now. My madness is upstairs in a bed sweating through the sheets and talking to people who aren't there. So I'll ask you plain, Coral Nystrom, and then I'll never speak of it again: are you going to let my daughter die because you're scared?"

The question hung there like a blade balanced on its point.

Tam had stopped chewing.

Coral looked at the boy, and something in his face — that awful adult comprehension children get too young — undid her faster than any argument could have. He wasn't looking at her like a charm-seller. He was looking at her like a person who might be able to fix the unfixable, and he was trying very hard not to hope, because hoping hurt.

"I'll come and sit with her," Coral said. "That's all I'm promising. I'll come and sit."

"That's all I'm asking," said Marta, who was lying through her teeth, and they both knew it.

* * *

The Senn house was one room above a cooper's shop, warm from a stove that was working too hard, and it smelled of sickness — that sweetish, wrong smell that Coral had hoped never to learn but recognized instantly anyway.

Lissie lay in the bed like something already halfway elsewhere. She was seven years old, small for it, with dark hair plastered to her forehead and lips gone grey at the edges. Her breathing came fast and shallow, each one a small negotiation her body kept losing. Her eyes moved under closed lids, chasing something.

"She sees them," Marta whispered. "People. Or... shapes. She talks to the corner of the room. Yesterday she said, 'the tall lady says it's almost time.' " Marta's jaw tightened. "I told her there's no tall lady. I told her she's going to be fine."

"What did she say back?"

Marta's voice failed her for a moment. "She said, 'I know, Mama. I'm just being polite.'"

Coral sat down beside the bed. Up close, the fever had a texture she could almost feel against her skin, a shimmering wrongness like heat off summer cobbles. And beneath it — beneath the sickness — she felt the other thing. The thing she had spent her life pretending not to feel.

Lissie was praying.

Not out loud. Not in words, exactly. Children didn't pray the way adults did, with formatted requests and transactional bargaining. Lissie prayed the way animals spoke — in pure want, unashamed and unshaped: *don't want to go, don't want Mama to be sad, want to see spring, want to see spring, want to see spring.*

Coral had spent eleven years starving her gift. She had learned to keep her listening shallow, to skim the surface noise of rats and sparrows and never, ever dip deeper. Because deep listening was a door, and doors opened both ways, and somewhere past the edge of the world were things that noticed when a door opened.

But a child was praying into the dark, and the dark wasn't answering, and Coral was apparently the only door left.

"Oh, you foolish woman," she murmured to herself.

She took Lissie's small hand. It was furnace-hot. And instead of doing the sensible thing — the thing that would have kept her invisible, her name off ledgers, her door unmarked — Coral reached down through the fever with everything she had, down past the skin and blood and heat, to the sickness itself.

Because here was the secret nobody understood about her gift: she didn't command beasts. She *spoke* to them. And a sickness, in its way, was a beast — a small blind animal of heat and hunger, burrowed into a child, doing what beasts do.

*Hello,* Coral said to it, the way she'd say hello to a rat in a wall. *Hello, little hunger. I see you.*

The fever stirred. Noticed her. And — this was the part that had always unsettled her most about her gift — it answered. Not in words. In sensation: a vast, mindless contentment. *Warm. Full. Growing. Good.*

*You're killing her,* Coral said. *The one who feeds you. Beasts don't eat their own den.*

Confusion. A flicker of the animal logic beneath: *small den-mate, weak den-mate, food—*

*No.* Coral pushed gentleness into it, the way she'd calm a spooked horse, the way she'd once talked a terrified dog off a bridge with nothing but patience and a stolen sausage. *Not food. Not prey. Listen. There's a bigger warmth. A warmer den. If you leave her now, quiet, easy, I'll show you where—*

And that was when the floor of the world opened under her.

Something answered from *below* her voice. Below the sickness. Below the city, below the river, below whatever bottom the world pretended to have. It didn't speak so much as arrive — a presence so vast that Coral's mind could only render it as pressure, as depth, as the feeling of standing at the shore of an ocean that had just become aware of the beach.

*—and I will carry it out,* said the vastness, in a voice that used hers like a borrowed coat. *Little sister of thresholds. Little keeper of doors. You knocked. I answer.*

Coral tried to let go of Lissie's hand. She couldn't. Her arm wouldn't obey. The presence flowed *through* her, down her arm, into the small hot hand, and she felt it move through the child's body the way dawn moves across a valley — not fighting the fever, not killing it, simply arriving, and the fever retreating before it like night birds scattering.

The vastness sighed through her mouth, and the sigh came out in a language with no living speakers, and the candles in the room all leaned sideways though there was no wind.

Then it was gone. Withdrawn. Gone the way a wave goes — not vanished, merely returned to wherever such things wait.

Lissie's breathing slowed. Deepened. Color crept back into her lips, pink as sunrise. Her eyelids fluttered and, for a heartbeat, opened — and she looked directly at Coral, directly *through* Coral, at something standing behind her chair.

"She's pretty," Lissie said, clear-voiced and dreamy. "The tall lady says thank you. And she says—" the little brow furrowed, retrieving it, "—she says you owe her a door."

Then the child slept, genuinely slept, chest rising and falling in long easy swells.

Coral sat very still. Her hand was still in Lissie's. Somewhere outside, every pigeon in Vaelharrow had begun to sing — at midnight, at midnight, in the dead cold dark — a low rolling chorus of coos that should not have been happening, and did not stop for a full hour.

Marta wept into her nephew's shoulder and called it a physician's error and a strong heart and the grace of whatever saint she could name safely. Coral let her. Coral helped change the soaked sheets and drank the celebratory tea and smiled the right smiles and left before dawn with coins pressed into her pocket that she would secretly return through Tam next week.

She walked home through empty streets feeling like a woman who has just heard ice crack under her feet and hasn't dared stop moving.

At her door, she stopped.

There was a mark on the wood. Small, at the height of a child's reach — no, lower, the height of a kneeling woman. A moth, drawn in lines of faint silver light, wings folded, patient as a signature. It was beautiful. It was delicate. It was glowing softly in the pre-dawn dark where anyone — a neighbor drawing water, a Watchman on rounds, an inquisitor walking his territory — could not fail to see it.

Coral raised her hand to wipe it away and found her fingers wouldn't touch it, sliding off like the mark was made of cold glass.

Behind her, from the gutter, a rat regarded her with small black eyes, and in the oldest language she knew — the one without words — it said:

*They see you now.*

Above the rooftops, far off toward the river, a bell tolled once. Not an alarm. Not a service hour. Just a single note, hung in the frozen air like a signature on a page — and Coral understood, with the certainty of a woman watching her own careful life catch fire, that somewhere in Vaelharrow, someone had been waiting a very long time for exactly this light.

She stood in the doorway a moment longer, then went inside and barred the door, and did not sleep, and the moth glowed on.
