## 3. Ash on the Ledger

The report arrived at dawn, as all the worst reports did, carried by a boy who knocked on Dain Ferrow's door with the particular rhythm of someone who had been told *do not wait for an answer*.

Dain knew the rhythm. He also knew that the boy—Pell, fourteen, ambitious, and blessed with the unshakable conviction that inquisitors were glamorous—would wait anyway, hovering on the step like a moth at a lamp, hoping to be invited in for tea and stories about heretic-burning. Dain had never burned anyone. He suspected this was the single greatest disappointment of Pell's young life.

He opened the door just wide enough to accept the sealed folio.

"Anything exciting?" Pell asked, craning.

"A ledger audit from the Salt Ward."

"Ah." Pell's face performed disappointment with the subtlety of a dropped plate. "Well. Good morning, Inquisitor Ferrow."

"Good morning, Pell. Eat something green today."

He shut the door, set the folio on his kitchen table beside the teapot and yesterday's cold toast, and looked at it for a long moment without touching it.

Ledger audits came wrapped in gray wax. This seal was red—the color reserved for confirmed acts. And beneath the red wax, pressed into the paper itself, was the sigil that had made the courier run through the pre-dawn streets instead of waiting for office hours: a circle bisected by a vertical line, the mark the Office used when magic had been witnessed by more than one reliable source and could no longer be filed under *mass hysteria, dietary, or weather*.

Confirmed divine manifestation. First in eleven years.

Dain put the kettle on first. There was no point reading anything of consequence without tea; his old mentor Vessa had taught him that, back when she was teaching him everything else. *The work will still be terrible in ten minutes, Dain. Be kind to yourself in the interval.* She'd been dead nine years now, and he still heard her voice most mornings, usually while doing something mundane, which was either comfort or haunting, depending on how honest he was feeling.

He poured, sat, broke the seal, and read.

---

The facts were these, laid out in the cramped hand of a ward-clerk named Osk who dotted his i's like knife-points:

On the night of the ninth, in the Tallowmarket district, a child named Merrin Sable, age six, presented with marsh-fever in its final stage. The mother had sent for every physiker within reach and been refused by all of them—too far gone, too late, go home and pray. The mother had done precisely that, but not at any lawful shrine. She had gone to a charm-seller in the night market. A woman. Unnamed in this report, because the witnesses could not agree on her name or even, remarkably, her face.

Witness one: a fishmonger two stalls down, who swore the charm-woman "said words over the fever like it was a person she was arguing with."

Witness two: the physiker who finally attended at dawn, expecting a corpse, and found instead a sleeping child with a clear brow, whose fever had broken "as though it had been *escorted out*, Inquisitor, I don't have another word for it, I've practiced thirty years and I've never seen sickness leave politely."

Witness three: the neighbor, old Marta Quill, who had seen, on the charm-woman's door at first light, a mark that had not been there before. Not painted. Not chalked. It was simply *there*, faint as breath on glass: a moth made of light, wings spread, and it had stayed visible for the better part of an hour while half the street stood in the lane staring at it and pretending very hard they were not staring at it, because everyone knows what happens to people who are caught staring.

Osk concluded: divine intercession, minor classification, source unidentified. Recommend immediate investigation. Recommend discretion. Recommend—

And here Osk's handwriting changed for one line, went looser, almost human:

*Recommend whoever you send is gentle. The mother has lost nothing. Please do not let us be the ones to give her something to lose.*

Then the clerk-hand clamped back down again, formal, finished.

Dain read the whole thing twice, then sat with his cooling tea and looked out the window at the gray roofs of Vaelharrow, and thought about moths.

---

He had been seven years old, in a village called Emberlow that did not appear on maps anymore—not because it had been erased, but because it had been renamed, twice, until nobody remembered what it had originally been called, which amounted to the same thing. He had been seven years old, and his sister Ilsa had been five, and she had fallen into the mill-race in early spring when the water was high enough to drown a grown man in four minutes.

She had been under for longer than four minutes.

Dain remembered standing on the bank screaming—not praying, he hadn't known how to pray yet, the village shrine-keeper had been arrested the previous autumn and nobody had replaced her—and remembering, later, only fragments. The water going strange. Still, somehow, in the middle of the race, like a hand cupping around her. Ilsa coughing up half the river onto the stones, alive, sobbing, alive.

And on the flat rock beside where she'd washed up, glowing faintly in the morning light, fading slow as breath off glass: a moth.

Their father had covered it with his coat. Their father, who understood things, had covered it with his coat and said, "We saw nothing," and taken them home, and that evening the Office riders came through asking after miracles, and their father said, "Nothing here but a near-drowning, thank the Law the child lives," and gave them bread and directions and watched them out of sight.

Three weeks later the family moved to the city. Dain had never learned whether his father had been warned, or warned himself, or simply understood the arithmetic of the world they lived in: that a saved child was a debt the Sigil Law always collected, with interest.

Ilsa was alive today. Married, three children, living in the Hillward districts, sending him letters about her garden that he answered promptly and cheerfully, and neither of them ever spoke about the moth. It lived between them like a stone in a shoe—small, always there, felt with every step.

Dain drank his cold tea and thought: *eleven years.* Eleven years since the last confirmed manifestation, and now one in Tallowmarket of all places, in a night market, over a fever-child, marked with a moth.

The Law said the gods were dead. Killed, ended, finished—a century ago, in the founding, when the Signatories had drawn the new borders and written the new names and declared, officially and permanently, that the divine was closed for business. That was the whole architecture of the country. Take away the death of the gods and you took away the floor.

So what, Dain wondered, looking at Osk's knife-point i's, does an institution do when the floor moves?

What do *I* do?

* * *

---

The Office of Inquiry occupied a former granary in the government quarter, which Dain had always found fitting: a building built to store the sustenance of the city, repurposed to store its fears. His desk was on the third floor between a window that stuck and the office of High Inquisitor Sarel Vane, which had no windows at all, on the principle—Vane had once explained to a new recruit, with the patience of a gardener explaining pruning—that hope was a draft.

At midmorning, Dain climbed the stairs to that windowless room, folio in hand, and knocked.

"Enter."

Sarel Vane had been old when Dain was recruited fifteen years ago and appeared, infuriatingly, no older now. She was a narrow woman, silver-haired, dressed in the slate-gray of her rank with the single white cord of seniority knotted at her shoulder. She did not look up from her writing when he entered. She never did. It was a technique, and like all her techniques, it worked.

"You've read it," she said.

"I have."

"The moth."

"The moth," Dain agreed.

Now she looked up. Her eyes were pale, unhurried, the eyes of someone who had spent fifty years watching people decide whether to lie to her, and had rarely been disappointed.

"In your assessment, Ferrow. As the officer assigned. What are we looking at?"

Dain had prepared an answer on the walk over. He was good at answers; it was the quiet part of his craft, the part nobody praised. Fifteen years in inquiry and his file contained exactly one confirmed manifestation—this one—and a great many entries that read *investigated, no evidence substantiated, case closed*. A great many cases in which a woman had cured her son's croup with words, and Dain had interviewed her and written *folk remedy, coincidence*, and gone home and slept poorly and slept anyway.

"The evidence is consistent," he said carefully, "with a skilled charlatan exploiting a coincidental recovery. Fever breaks on its own. Grieving mothers see patterns. The moth sigil—" *was on a rock by the river the day my sister didn't die,* said a voice in his chest, which he stepped on firmly— "could be chalk dust and lamplight. I'd recommend a routine interview of the charm-seller, low profile, and filing under unresolved."

Vane listened to all of it with the expression of a woman watching rain.

"And the fact," she said, when he finished, "that the physiker described the fever leaving *politely*?"

"Fever patients often rally. Terminal rallies are well documented."

"And the fact that the mark persisted for an hour in open air?"

"Lingering phosphorescence. Market lantern-oils vary in quality."

"Dain." She said his name the way she said few things: plainly. "You are the best falsifier I employ."

His stomach dropped through three floors and into the granary's foundations. "High Inquisitor—"

"Do not insult us both by performing shock." She rose, crossed to a cabinet, and drew out a second folio—thin, older, its corners soft with handling. "You write beautiful reports of nothing, Ferrow. You have written them for fifteen years. Do you imagine I cannot read the shape of a suppressed finding? Every time your investigations conclude in *coincidence*, the coincidences cluster. A lame man walks in Copperlane, seven years ago—your report says *misdiagnosis*. A drowned girl revives in the river wards, four years ago—your report says *fishing net, fortuitous*. You have spent a decade and a half standing on the scale with your thumb underneath it."

Dain said nothing. His mouth had gone dry as ash.

"I have never minded," Vane continued, returning to her desk. "Mercy is a lubricant. An office that arrests every hedge-charm and folk-cure drowns in paperwork and makes enemies of every grandmother in the city. Your little mercies keep the peace cheaply, and you file them honestly enough that I can count them, which means I can tolerate them. But this—" she tapped the red-sealed folio— "is not yours to soften. This has been confirmed by three independent sources and witnessed by forty. If I permit you to bury it, and it grows—as such things grow—then it grows unwatched, unnamed, and free. So."

She pushed the older folio across the desk toward him.

"You will investigate the charm-seller. Thoroughly. You will find her, you will assess her, and you will report to me directly—no clerks, no intermediaries. And if she is merely a fraud who got lucky, Ferrow, then I expect you to lose her in the crowds like you always do, and I will sign off on whatever beautiful nothing you bring me. But if she is what the moth suggests she is—"

She paused. For just a moment, in the windowless room, Dain would have sworn the High Inquisitor looked tired. Deeply, anciently tired, like a wall that had held against the sea for fifty years and knew exactly how thin it was getting.

"—then you will remember what we are for. Not what we do. What we are *for*."

"What are we for, High Inquisitor?"

"Vaelharrow," she said simply. "All of it. Every soul inside the line, including the foolish ones, including the guilty ones, including the ones who pray to dead gods and get answers. Open the other folio when you're alone. Dismissed."

---

* * *

---

He waited until he was home, that evening, door bolted, lamp trimmed low. The old folio was thinner than it looked because half of it was blank—pages removed, he realized, deliberately, long ago. What remained were fragments. Field notes in a hand he didn't recognize. And drawings.

Drawings of sigils. Marks of light reported across ninety years, cataloged in a careful clerk's hand: the moth, recurring, again and again and again, always associated with water, with drowning, with fever, with children pulled back from edges. Beside each entry, in the margin, someone had written classifications. Dain read them slowly.

*Moth — mercy-class. Answered prayer. No recorded harm. Response latency: immediate.*

Underneath, in different ink, older: *They were always gentlest. Why did we fear them most?*

And below that, in a third hand, sharp as a blade: *Because gentleness is how they get in.*

Dain closed the folio and sat in his small kitchen with his small lamp, and outside his window the city of Vaelharrow lay under its lawful dark, a million people sleeping soundly in a country that had killed its gods a hundred years ago, officially, permanently, and filed the deed under *settled matters*.

He thought about Osk's note. *Recommend whoever you send is gentle.*

He thought about a fever leaving politely. About a moth on a door, wings spread, patient as dawn.

He thought about Ilsa, coughing riverwater onto the stones, alive, alive, alive.

Tomorrow he would go to Tallowmarket. Tomorrow he would begin the hunt for a woman whose own witnesses couldn't describe her face—which meant, Dain thought with a tired flicker of humor, that she was either genuinely invisible, powerfully warded, or so ordinary that the city itself declined to notice her, which in his experience was the most dangerous kind of person there was.

He turned the lamp down to a coal.

One question circled him as he lay awake, patient as a moth at glass: if the gods were truly dead—if the Law had killed them clean—then who had spent the last hundred years answering the prayers of drowning children?

And why did every answer look like mercy, when the Office's oldest files insisted that gentleness was how they got in?

Somewhere south of the city, beneath the river, something that did not exist shifted in its sleep.

And in the Tallowmarket night market, behind a stall of harmless charms, a woman with no memorable face was about to discover that her careful life had already ended—she simply didn't know it yet.
