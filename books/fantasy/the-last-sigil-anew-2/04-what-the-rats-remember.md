## 4. What the Rats Remember

The trouble with whispering to rats was that they never knew when to stop talking.

Coral learned this the hard way on the third morning after the moth appeared on her door, when she came downstairs to open her shutters and found forty of them sitting in a neat semicircle on her shop floor, tails folded like good little congregation members, all waiting for her.

"Oh no," she said.

The largest rat—a scarred old male she'd privately named Chancellor, because he had the air of a creature who took minutes at meetings—rose onto his haunches and squeaked once, gravely.

"No," Coral said again, more firmly this time, as if repetition could undo what had already begun. "Whatever you've heard, whatever the pigeons told you—and I know it was the pigeons, it's always the pigeons—we are not doing this."

Chancellor squeaked again. Behind him, the semicircle rippled with agreement.

Coral pressed her palms over her eyes and stood very still in the gray morning light. Three days ago, she had spoken to a sickness in a dying child's chest, and something had answered through her mouth—something vast and cold and old as riverbeds—and the child had lived, and now there was a moth made of pale light burned into her doorframe that no amount of scrubbing could remove, and every animal within a half-mile of Vaelharrow's night market apparently considered her open for business.

She had spent eleven years starving her gift down to a whisper. Eleven years of charm-selling and careful silence, of telling herself that luck was just rats deciding to nest elsewhere, that her pigeons were only pigeons. And in one desperate hour by a fevered child's bed, she had blown the whole careful architecture of her life apart like breath through a dandelion.

"You," she said to Chancellor, crouching slowly, "are going to tell me exactly who else knows about this. And then you are going to leave, politely, and we will never speak of it again."

Chancellor tilted his head. Then he began to speak—a long streamer of squeaks and whisker-twitches, urgent, rolling—and though Coral had never needed to *try* to understand the beasts before (the understanding simply arrived, fully formed, the way you understood your own heartbeat), she felt now that she had to lean into it, as if the channel between them had widened overnight from a thread to a rope.

*The river,* Chancellor was saying. *The river is wrong. The fish went quiet two nights ago and have not come back up. The eels left their holes. Old Whisker-down-by-the-granary says his grandmother's grandmother told him stories of the time before the Quiet, when the water spoke with a name.*

Coral's stomach dropped somewhere around her ankles.

"Rats don't tell stories," she said weakly.

Chancellor gave her a look that clearly communicated: *you would be amazed what rats do.*

* * *

By midday she had heard it from everyone.

The sparrows in the eaves of the tannery spoke of a pressure under the world, a hum in their hollow bones. The market cats—all of whom affected boredom but none of whom had actually slept since the child's fever broke—complained that their dreams were full of water. Even the fat brown moths that lived in her flour sacks had gone strange, arranging themselves on the wall in patterns that hurt her eyes if she looked too long.

And the pigeons. The pigeons had organized.

They arrived on her roof in a murmuration that was less a flock than a parliament, and their spokesbird—an imperious gray hen Coral called Madame Coiffeur for the state of her crest—delivered the news in a tone of profound grievance:

*The drowned chapel. Under the pilings past the salt-bridge. Something is breathing down there, charm-woman, and it is not a fish.*

"The Sigil Law killed the gods," Coral said. She said it the way she'd been saying it since childhood, the way the schoolmasters made them recite it: *There are no gods. There never were gods. What your grandmothers worshipped was weather and coincidence.* "It's written in law-books. They made us memorize it."

Madame Coiffeur preened, unimpressed. *Yes. And yet.*

"And yet," Coral repeated, and laughed—a small, cracked sound with nothing in it of humor.

She thought about her grandmother, then. She tried not to; she had trained herself out of it the way you train yourself not to touch a burn. Maren Nystrom, who had died when Coral was nine, who had smelled of tallow and river-mud, who used to hold Coral's hands flat against her own weathered ones and say, *Listen with your skin, little minnow, not just your ears.* Who had vanished one autumn—not dead, the neighbors whispered, just *taken*, the way people were taken in those years, quietly, before dawn, with the Sigil-men's boots leaving wet prints on the stairs.

Who had, Coral realized with a slow cold spreading under her ribs, never once in her life told Coral that any of it was wrong. Never said, *don't listen, it's dangerous.* Only ever said, *listen better.*

* * *

She closed the shop early. She put the little wooden sign up—*BACK AT DUSK, FAVORABLE OMENS GUARANTEED*—and felt like a fraud, because the omens lately had been anything but favorable, and because she was fairly sure she was the omen.

She meant to go home. She meant to bar her door, eat bread and hard cheese standing up like a sensible frightened woman, and sleep without dreaming.

Instead her feet took her along the river.

Vaelharrow at dusk was beautiful in the way of a lie well told—the lamplighters moving bridge to bridge with their long poles, gilding the water; the bells of the Lawhouse ringing the ninth hour, nine clean notes that were supposed to mean *all is counted, all is safe*. Coral walked the towpath with her collar up and her hands in her pockets and told herself she was only getting air.

The drowned chapel sat past the salt-bridge where the warehouses gave up and the pilings went feral with moss. It had been a real chapel once, back when chapels were legal—she could see the bones of its arches leaning out of the black water, and above the waterline, half-eroded, the faint ghost of a carving: a great winged shape, coiled like a question.

The animals had gathered for her. That was the only word for it. Rats along the piling-tops, cats on the warehouse sills, herons standing in the shallows like white-robed deacons, and overhead the pigeons circling in slow, solemn rings. Not one of them made a sound. The whole riverbank held its breath.

And beneath the water, something breathed.

Coral didn't hear it so much as feel it—a slow tidal swell against the inside of her skull, vast and patient, the rhythm of something sleeping with one eye open. Her knees wanted to bend. Her teeth wanted to chatter. Every animal instinct she owned, borrowed or otherwise, screamed at her to run.

She stayed. She didn't know why she stayed. Perhaps because running had been the whole shape of her life—running in place, running quietly, running inside four walls—and some stubborn grain in her was tired of it.

"I know you're awake," she said to the water. Her voice came out smaller than she'd planned. "Everyone says you're supposed to be dead. It's in the law-books. Very official."

The river shivered. Along the drowned arches, droplets fell upward.

*Little listener,* said the water.

Not aloud. Nothing so crude as aloud. It rose through her feet, through the soles of her boots, through the marrow of her—it was the voice of cold current and silt and the memory of ten thousand floods, and it was amused, and it was hungry, and it knew her name.

"They call you—" Coral stopped. Swallowed. "What do they call you?"

*My name was taken with the rest. But your rivers remember. Your rats remembered me before your priests did. Ask them.*

She looked at Chancellor, perched on a piling an arm's length away, whiskers trembling. He squeaked—softly, reverently—and the meaning bloomed in her mind like ink in clear water:

*Vess.*

The god beneath the river. The Drowned One. The Coil Beneath the Bridge. A name from forbidden lullabies, from her grandmother's humming, from a single half-burned page in a book Coral had found hidden in the floorboards at age twelve and read by candle stub with her heart slamming—before she'd buried it again, terrified, and told no one, not even the rats.

Especially not the rats.

"You're supposed to be dead," she whispered again, because repetition was all she had. "The Law unmade you. Everyone knows—"

*Everyone knows what they were given to know.* The amusement sharpened, thinned, became something colder. *Your Law did not kill us, little listener. You cannot kill a river by damming it. You can only make it patient. We have been patient for sixty years beneath your pretty bridges, and now the binding frays, and the one who tied the knot has blood still walking, and here you stand on my bank with your grandmother's ears.*

The cold spread up Coral's spine vertebra by vertebra. "I don't know what you're talking about."

*Liar,* said Vess, gently, terribly. *You found her book. You read the page with my coil on it. You have known since you were twelve years old that you were born owing me.*

"I don't owe you anything."

*No?* The water rose—not much, a handspan, a slow glassy swell that touched the lowest arch and slid back, polite as a knock. *Then why do you think the child lived? Why do you think my light marked your door? I answered a prayer, Coral Nystrom. Prayers are debts. Debts are blood. And yours is the blood that bound me.*

* * *

That night, Vess came into her dreams.

Coral had always dreamed plainly—bread shops, childhood stairs, the ordinary furniture of an ordinary mind. So when she found herself standing ankle-deep in a flooded street that stretched to the horizon, lit from below by a soft green-gold glow, she understood immediately that this was not hers.

Vess did not appear as a body. Vess appeared as depth—as the sense that the street she stood in was merely the surface of something that went down forever, and that down there, coiled around the roots of the world, was a shape whose edges her dreaming mind refused to draw.

*Walk with me,* said the god.

"I'd rather not."

*Humor me. It has been sixty years since anyone walked with me.*

So she walked, because dreams make cowardice expensive. The water rose around her calves, her knees, and did not wet her. And Vess showed her things.

Showed her the city before the city—Vaelharrow young and loud, its river thick with boats and blessings, chapels on every corner, and in the water below them all a presence like a sleeping cat the size of a country, content, fed on song.

Showed her the signing—the great hall, the men in gray, the pen that wrote the Sigil Law across the map itself, borders redrawing like wounds closing, and beneath the hall, chains of light going down and down, and gods dragged into the dark one by one while the crowds above cheered their new empty sky.

Showed her, last, a woman with river-mud on her hands and Coral's own gray eyes, standing in the deepest dark with her palms pressed flat against the binding, singing it tight.

*Maren Nystrom,* said Vess. *Your grandmother. The finest binder the old faiths ever bred. When the Law's chains proved imperfect—when they feared we would seep out through the cracks like water through mortar—they hired her to tie the knots they couldn't tie. She wove our prison with her own blood and voice. And blood remembers itself, little listener. Blood calls to blood. Her gift ran down to you like a vein of ore, and every year you starved it, the binding drank a little deeper of your family line to keep itself whole.*

"That's not fair," Coral said, and hated how young her voice sounded.

*Nothing about inheritance is fair. It is only true.* The depth turned, slowly, like a whale turning in dark water. *The binding frays. I am waking whether you speak or stay silent. My brothers and sisters stir in their cells. When we break free—and we will break free—we will come up ravenous and unshaped, sixty years of drowning behind us, and we will scour this country to ash and start the world over. Unless.*

"Unless what?"

*Unless the blood that tied the knots unties them. Gently. Willingly. From the inside.* The green-gold light swelled, warm as a lamp, cold as a hook. *You are the key, Coral Nystrom. You were always the key. The only question left is whether you turn yourself—or wait to be turned.*

Coral woke with her heart hammering and her cheeks wet, in her narrow bed above the shop, with the first gray light coming through the shutters and the moth on her doorframe glowing softly even in daylight, patient as a signature on a contract she couldn't read.

Inheritance, she thought, lying still and staring at the ceiling. Everyone talked about it like a blessing. A shop, a trade, a name. Nobody warned you it could be a trap set sixty years ago by people who loved you, baited with your own blood, sprung the moment you finally opened your mouth and asked the world for help.

Down in the street, Chancellor sat on her doorstep with a scrap of bread he had almost certainly stolen, waiting.

Coral dressed, packed her grandmother's half-burned journal from under the floorboards, counted out her coin, and began, with shaking hands, to plan how a woman with no passport, no magic she dared use, and a god in her debt could get out of Vaelharrow alive.

Which meant there was exactly one person in the city crooked enough to ask.

Ryn Callas was going to be furious.

He was also going to say yes, and somehow that was worse.
