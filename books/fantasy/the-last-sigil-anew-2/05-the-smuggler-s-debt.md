## 5. The Smuggler's Debt

The Saltwife's Rest sat at the crooked end of Brackwater Lane like a tooth that had given up on the mouth around it. Coral had walked past it a hundred times in the last three years, and every one of those hundred times she had told herself she was only passing by.

Tonight she stopped at the door.

Inside, the tavern smelled of tar, fry-grease, and the particular sourness of men drinking away money they'd sworn they'd spend on their families. A one-eared cat regarded her from atop a barrel with an expression of profound professional disappointment, and Coral sent it the smallest thread of *good evening* along the old channels before she could stop herself. The cat blinked slowly, mollified, and went back to judging everyone.

She shouldn't have done that. She was trying to be careful. She had been trying to be careful for three years, ever since the moth of light had appeared over her door and the whole careful architecture of her invisible life had begun to come down around her ears like a badly built shed.

But habits of kindness are harder to kill than magic is. Everyone in Vaelharrow knew that. It was practically the city motto.

Ryn Callas was not at the bar. He was at the corner table by the window that didn't quite close, playing cards with three dockhands who were losing to him with the cheerful resignation of men who knew the game was rigged and had decided to enjoy the rigging anyway. He looked—Coral's chest did something complicated—exactly as he had always looked, which was unfair. Three years should have been allowed to do *something*. He should have gone grey at the temples, or grown a beard, or developed a paunch, or some other visible consequence of her absence. Instead he was all long lines and quick hands, dark hair tied back with a bit of red cord he'd probably stolen, laughing at something one of the dockhands said in a way that made the whole table laugh too.

He always could do that. Make rooms warmer just by standing in them.

Coral took a breath, crossed the floor, and pulled out the chair across from him.

"Seat's taken," said Ryn, without looking up from his cards.

"It's not."

"Strongly felt to be taken." He gathered the pot, tipped the dockhands a wink—"same time next week, gentlemen, bring your daughters' dowries"—and only then lifted his eyes.

Whatever he'd prepared to say when he finally saw her, she watched him lose hold of it. His face did a small, complicated thing, there and gone, like a fish under ice. Then it settled into something flat and unreadable, which was worse.

"Coral Nystrom," he said. "I thought you were a rumor."

"I thought you were in Ironharrow."

"I was. I got better." He stacked his winnings with neat, economical movements. "You look well. That's a lie. You look like someone who hasn't slept since the last frost, but you're wearing it with a certain style."

"Ryn—"

"Brandy?" He raised two fingers at the barkeep without waiting for her answer. "Sit. You clearly came here to ask me for something, and I've found that conversation goes easier with brandy. Yours especially."

She sat. The chair wobbled. Everything in this establishment wobbled; it was a wonder the building stayed upright out of sheer stubbornness.

"How did you find me?" he asked. "Actually, don't answer. Let me guess. The rats told you."

Her stomach dropped through the wobbling floor.

Ryn watched her face do whatever her face did, and sighed, and put the cards down. "Relax. I'm not going to sell you to the Inquisition. For one thing, you couldn't afford me. For another—" he paused, choosing, "—a gull landed on my rail two months ago and screamed at me for ten minutes straight until I let it have my breakfast, and I've thought about that gull more than any man reasonably should. So. Rats told you where I drink. Fine. What do you want?"

"You're going east," Coral said. "Downriver, past the toll-stations. I've heard it from—" she gestured vaguely, "—around."

"From around. Of course." He leaned back. "And you want passage."

"And discretion."

"Passage I can sell you. Discretion costs extra, and you've already spent most of what you had with me."

There it was. Three years of carefully not thinking about it, laid on the table between the brandy glasses like a third player joining the hand.

"Ryn." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "I need you to know—"

"Don't."

"Let me say it. I've practiced saying it."

"That's somehow worse." But he waved his hand: go on.

"I gave your route to the harbormaster's clerk," Coral said. "I told them where the *Kestrel's Fancy* would be running silk, because I'd found out that Ferro Vance had bought the patrol ledgers, and if the Inquisition boarded that ship they wouldn't be looking for contraband. They'd be looking for unregistered passengers. There was a family in your hold, Ryn. Six of them. The youngest had a mark behind her ear."

The tavern noise went on around them—the clink of glasses, the lie and chatter—but their table might have been under glass.

"They were going to burn that child," Coral said. "So I burned your route instead. Your ship, your cargo, your name on the clerk's list. Two years in Ironharrow for smuggling. And I never came to tell you why, because explaining felt like asking forgiveness, and I hadn't earned the right to ask."

She ran out of words. She'd rehearsed more, on the walk over—a whole speech, actually, quite moving, she'd gotten a little choked up practicing it into a pigeon—but the pigeon wasn't here now, and neither was the speech.

Ryn turned his glass slowly on the table, watching the ring of moisture it left.

"Ferro Vance," he said at last. "That snake was selling passenger manifests to Vane's people. I'd wondered how they kept finding the families." He looked up. "Six people."

"Six."

"The girl with the mark."

"She's eleven now. Living in Tarnholt, last I heard. Her mother sends word through the tanners' guild."

Another silence. Then Ryn laughed—one short, disbelieving breath of it—and dragged both hands down his face.

"You absolute *idiot*, Coral. You could have told me. You could have walked into this tavern and said 'Ferro's selling manifests, change your route,' and I would have changed the route, because I am not a *monster*, I just play one at cards—"

"You'd have asked who told me. And I'd have lied badly. And then Ferro would have wondered why a charm-seller knew his business, and started pulling threads, and the threads would have led to the child anyway. Or to me. Or to you twice over." She shook her head. "It had to look like betrayal. Clean, stupid, greedy betrayal. Nobody investigates a woman who sells out her lover for coin."

"Nobody investigates," Ryn repeated softly. "Except you investigated yourself every day for three years, I'd wager."

Coral looked at the brandy she hadn't touched.

"Yeah," she said. "A bit."

Ryn was quiet a moment longer. Then he reached across and turned her untouched glass so it sat square in front of her, a small domestic gesture so familiar it hurt.

"Drink," he said. "You're going to need it, because I'm about to negotiate, and you're terrible at negotiating when you're dry."

* * *

The negotiation went, as predicted, badly for her.

"East passage, one traveler, discreet," Ryn said, counting on his fingers. "That's a berth in the grain wagon, bread and dried apples, and my personal guarantee that no one asks your name. Normally I'd charge forty marks."

"I have eleven."

"Eleven marks and one enormous favor, yes. Here's the favor." He leaned forward, and the tavern light caught the scar on his jaw—the one from the Kestrel's Fancy, though he didn't know she knew that. "Two nights from now I move a cargo out of the tannery warehouses. Live cargo. And before you make that face—"

"What face?"

"That face. The one that says *caged beasts, Ryn, really.* It's griffhawks. Eleven of them. Sold south by the aviaries at Greywatch, bound for some margrave's mews who wants hunting birds with pedigree. They're worth more than this entire street, and they are also—" he rubbed his jaw, "—driving my handlers half mad."

"They're terrified," Coral said quietly.

Ryn blinked. "You know that, or you're guessing?"

"I know that." She wrapped her hands around the brandy glass. "Griffhawks aren't like hawks. They pair-bond for life. Those birds were taken from Greywatch in autumn, which is bonding season, which means somewhere on that cliff there are eleven birds sitting on cold nests wondering where their mates went. And they can smell fear on the handlers, and the handlers are afraid of them, and it's a circle that only gets tighter. By the time they reach the margrave, half will be self-maiming. The other half will be dead."

Ryn stared at her.

"That," he said, "is a great deal more than I knew, and I've had them four days." He drummed his fingers once on the table—a habit she remembered, thinking-money. "Here's the shape of it. Caged griffhawks don't travel quiet. They scream at night, they batter the cages, and a wagon full of screaming birds draws exactly the kind of attention a smuggler pays good money to avoid. My usual trick is drugging the meat, but it makes them sick and sick birds die, and dead birds mean I owe the buyer nine thousand marks I do not have. So."

"So you want me to talk to them."

"I want you to *sit with them*. Tell them—whatever you tell them. That the ride will be smooth. That no one will hurt them. That screaming is counterproductive. Whatever works. You do that for two nights and one morning, and you ride east in the grain wagon with my personal guarantee." He spread his hands. "That's the price. That's the whole price. No coin required."

Coral looked at him for a long moment.

"You understand what you're asking," she said. "After everything—the Law, the moths, whatever you've heard. You understand that using it is the dangerous part. Not having it. Having it is survivable. Using it is how neighbors disappear."

"I understand that a neighbor of mine disappeared last month for suspected blessing," Ryn said, "and that the same week, a gull made me feed it breakfast, and I've been turning something over in my head ever since." His voice stayed easy, but his eyes didn't. "You spent three years starving that thing, Coral. I watched you do it—I just didn't know what I was watching. You'd flinch when a dog barked near you. You'd cross the street rather than pass the pigeons on the granary steps. I thought you hated animals." A short, rueful breath. "You were fasting. I know what fasting looks like. I grew up Lenten."

The image landed harder than she expected: herself from the outside, swerving wide around a sparrow, and Ryn watching her do it and filing it away with all the other small observations he'd never mentioned.

"If anyone sees," she began.

"No one sees. The tannery yard after midnight is emptier than a tax collector's heart. One handler—Tam, he's deaf in one ear and loyal in all the rest, and he owes me more than I'll ever call in." Ryn stood, dropping coins on the table with a flourish. "Two nights from now, moonrise. Brackwater gate, the tannery side. Wear something you can kneel in."

He got three steps toward the door, then stopped, and came back, and stood over her with his hands in his pockets, looking younger suddenly, or older—she couldn't decide which.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the thing I hated most about Ironharrow wasn't the walls. It was doing the arithmetic. Two years in a cell against six lives, and knowing the sum was right and feeling wrong about it anyway. That's the arithmetic you've been doing alone for three years." He touched the back of her chair, briefly, not her shoulder—he was careful about that, always had been. "Don't do it alone anymore. It rots you. Ask me how I know."

Then he was gone, out into the lamplit lane, whistling something off-key, leaving Coral alone with two brandies and a heart that wouldn't sit still.

* * *

The tannery yard at moonrise smelled exactly as advertised, and worse.

The cages stood in a row under the lean-to, tall as coffins, barred with greywatch-iron that hummed faintly with suppression-work—legal binding-craft, licensed and stamped, the kind the Law permitted because it was aimed at beasts instead of people. Inside each cage a griffhawk hunched, feathers flat, eyes like struck flint. Eleven birds. Eleven hearts beating fast enough that Coral could feel them from the gate, a drumming in her teeth.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, you poor things."

The nearest bird's head snapped around. For a long moment it simply stared, the way wild things stare when the world says one thing and something underneath says another. Then, very slowly, it rose, stepped to the bars, and made a sound Coral had never heard a griffhawk make—not the scream, not the hunting cry, but a low, broken warble, high and questioning.

*Are you—* the sound meant. *Are you one of the voices? Are you real?*

"I'm real," Coral whispered, kneeling in the straw-smelling dirt, and opened every door inside herself at once.

What came back nearly knocked her flat. Eleven minds, starved of speech for a season, all speaking at once—fear and grief and rage and, underneath it all, a question repeated like a heartbeat: *where, where, where is the mate, where is the cliff, where is home.* And beneath even that, threading through all eleven like a root through soil, something else. Something vast and patient and wet, rising from the direction of the river with a pull she felt in her spine, in her grandmother's blood, in the debt she owed—

One of the birds shrieked—a raw, human-sounding sound of terror—and its cry bent strangely in the air, and for half a heartbeat Coral could swear it shaped a word:

*Vess.*

From the shadows by the gate, Ryn's voice, steady and low: "Coral. What did it just say?"
