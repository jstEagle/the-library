## 6. Nine Bells for a Burning

The report sat on Dain Ferrow's desk for two days before he touched it.

It wasn't a long report. It didn't need to be. A confirmed act of divine magic in the Lantern Quarter — a child pulled back from the fever's edge by a voice that was not the mother's, witnessed twice, corroborated by a physician who had signed his name in the careful, frightened hand of a man hoping the Inquisition would leave him alone. The sigil observed at the site: a moth, luminous, wings spread.

Dain read it four times. Each time, the same line snagged him like a nail in a floorboard.

*Witnessed twice.*

Which meant it hadn't been hidden well. Which meant whoever did it was either very new at this or very desperate. He'd spent nineteen years reading reports of miracles, and almost every one of them was fraud, hysteria, or a trick of candlelight that some overeager junior had promoted into heresy out of ambition or fear. The Sigil Law had been thorough. That was its whole point. The Law had not merely outlawed magic; it had *edited* it, struck it from the ledgers and the maps and the mouths of grandmothers until the country of Vaelharrow could no longer remember what it had been called before, only that it had a name now, stamped and official, and that names were safety.

And yet. Twice witnessed. A moth made of light.

Dain dipped his pen, wrote the date at the top of a fresh page, and then sat there while the ink dried on nothing.

"You're doing the thing," said Brannic from the doorway. "The thing where you stare at paper like it insulted your mother."

Brannic Oss was twenty-six, broad as a doorframe, and possessed of the unshakable conviction that all problems could be solved by either paperwork or walking briskly. He had been assigned to Dain for six years, which in Dain's opinion was five years longer than the young man deserved and about right for how long it took anyone to learn the job properly: slowly, and mostly by being lied to.

"The Lantern Quarter case," Dain said.

"Ah." Brannic came in, helped himself to the chair across the desk without asking, because he never asked, and leaned forward with the eagerness of a man who still believed the work mattered. "I heard. Real one, they're saying. Vane herself took the file."

"I know."

"Confirmed manifestation. First in—" Brannic counted on his fingers, gave up. "Years. Maybe ever. Some people in Records are calling it the first *ever*, since the Law went down. Isn't that something? We might actually see—"

"See what?"

Brannic blinked. "A god, sir. Or near enough. Isn't that what we're for?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Nineteen years, and Dain had never once managed to answer it honestly, even to himself. Officially, the Inquisition existed to enforce the Sigil Law: to find the residue of magic wherever it pooled, to record it, to erase it, to keep the ledger clean. Unofficially — in the stairwells and the wine shops and the low voices after third bell — everyone knew the Law was less a broom than a lid. Something underneath the country had been nailed down, and the Inquisition were the nails' caretakers, checking each one, tapping each head, replacing the ones that rusted through.

"We're for the ledger," Dain said. "We write what happened so it can be closed."

"Right." Brannic nodded. Then, quieter: "You've got that face again. The one where you're deciding whether to lie on a form."

"I don't lie on forms."

"No, sir," Brannic agreed solemnly. "You *round*. There's a witness who saw too much, you round them down to a drunk. A blessing worked too well, you round it up to good medicine. It's why nobody downstairs will room with you at conferences. They say your reports have a smell."

Dain looked at the blank page. Nineteen years of rounding. A drunk here. Good medicine there. A fire attributed to a careless tallow-chandler rather than to whatever had actually happened in the house on Coriander Street when Dain Ferrow was nine years old and prayed, once, with his whole heart, and something answered — but that was not a thought he permitted himself before noon, and sometimes not at all.

"A moth," he said aloud. "Witnessed at the scene."

Brannic frowned. "Significance?"

"It's an old marking. Pre-Law. Households used to draw it over doors when someone inside was sick — a petition-sign, asking for intercession." Dain turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "My grandmother drew it. Over our door, every winter. I asked her once what it meant and she told me it was against moths getting into the flour."

"And was it?"

"It was," Dain said, "against everything getting in."

He signed the page — *assigned, Ferrow, D., senior inquisitor, Lantern Quarter inquiry* — and felt the small familiar click of a decision becoming a fact. He would go. He would investigate. And somewhere between here and the Lantern Quarter, he would decide what the truth weighed, and whether it could be rounded.

* * *

The shop was called Charms and Small Mercies, and it was exactly the kind of place Dain had spent his career pretending not to love.

It sat crooked on its street like an old dog leaning against a leg, windows crowded with luck-knots and river-glass strung on twine, dried lavender bundles, little carved birds whose purpose no one could legally state but which everyone understood to be *for something*. A hand-lettered sign promised CHARMS FOR LUCK, LOVE, AND QUIET NIGHTS — a masterpiece of deniability. Luck was commerce. Love was sentiment. Quiet nights were health. Nothing there broke the Law, because nothing there claimed to *work*.

Dain stood outside it for a long moment, hat in hand like a mourner, and let Brannic do the talking, because Brannic loved the talking.

Inside smelled of beeswax and dried herbs. Behind the counter was a woman perhaps thirty, brown-skinned, dark-haired, with ink stains on her fingers and the particular stillness of a small animal deciding whether you were weather or predator. Her eyes moved to the grey-and-silver badge on Dain's coat — the scales-and-quill of the Office of Ledger Integrity — and something behind them shuttered, politely, like curtains drawn on a dull afternoon.

"Inquisitors," she said. "To what do I owe the honor? If it's about the awning, I've filed the repair request twice."

"Ms. Nystrom?" Brannic consulted his notes with theatrical diligence. "Coral Nystrom?"

"That's the shop name too, yes. Very efficient neighborhood."

"We're making inquiries regarding the household of Maren Odile, Weaver's Lane," Dain said. "Her daughter. The fever."

The stillness deepened. Dain watched it happen — watched a woman who had clearly rehearsed this moment her entire life execute the rehearsal. A tilt of the head. Mild puzzlement, calibrated to be neither helpful nor obstructive. "Terrible business. I heard the girl took ill. And then didn't die, thank the— " a fractional pause, expertly measured, "—thank goodness. I sell charms, sirs. Lavender for sleep. Knots for luck. I'm afraid I haven't any stock in fevers."

"You visited the house," Dain said. Not accusingly. Almost apologetically. "Three days before the child improved. A neighbor saw you."

"I deliver orders." She spread her ink-stained hands. "Lavender keeps. People like it delivered."

"And did you speak, at the house? Say anything? Any words over the child, however informal?"

Here it was. Dain knew the shape of this moment the way a carpenter knows a joint. She would deny; he would press gently; she would deny better; and then it came down to whether he rounded the report toward mercy or toward the ledger, and both directions cost something, every time, forever.

But Coral Nystrom surprised him. She held his gaze — steady, unafraid in a way that was itself alarming, because everyone was afraid of the Inquisition, fear was practically a civic duty — and she said, softly:

"If I could do what you're asking me about, inquisitor, do you truly think I'd be selling luck-knots for two coppers?"

It was a perfect answer. It was also, Dain thought, the most honest sentence spoken in the room, including anything he himself said afterward. Because it wasn't a denial. It was a confession wearing denial's coat — *yes, and look how much good it has done me, none, look at my crooked shop and my twine and my two coppers* — offered to him like a coin across a counter, trusting him to know its value.

He knew its value. That was the trouble with him. Everyone in the Office said it: Ferrow sees too much, Ferrow hears the second sentence under the first. It had kept him mediocre for nineteen years, and mediocrity had kept a great many people alive.

"Thank you for your cooperation," he said, and heard his own voice go flat and official, the drawer closing. "If you recall anything further, the Office is at your disposal."

"Of course." She walked them to the door. As Dain crossed the threshold, she added, quietly, just for him: "The lavender really does help with sleep, you know. No law against a good night's rest."

"No," Dain agreed. "No law against that."

Outside, Brannic exhaled. "Well. She's hiding something, obviously. But I'll be honest, sir — I bought a luck-knot. Two coppers. For my sister's wedding."

"Did you tell her it was for the wedding?"

"Course not. Said it was for quiet nights." Brannic patted his pocket. "She winked at me, sir. Do you think winking counts as a manifestation? Should I report myself?"

"Go write your report," Dain said. "Round yourself down to a customer."

* * *

Nine bells rang that evening, which was wrong, because executions were always announced by nine.

Dain stood in the crowd on Candlemas Square because standing in crowds was part of the job — presence, visibility, the quiet arithmetic of terror done publicly so that it needn't be done privately. Beside him, Brannic was pale and silent, holding his notebook upside down without noticing.

They brought out a woman named Petta Vosse. Dain knew the file: forty-one, laundress, widow. Accused by her own boarder of "healing practices" — poultices, mostly, and a certain way of murmuring over wounds that the boarder had found unsettling after she cured his infected hand when the physicians couldn't. No confirmed manifestation. No sigil. No evidence whatsoever beyond a cured hand and a nervous man. By every rule of the ledger Dain served, Petta Vosse was innocent.

By every rule of the moment, she was necessary.

High Inquisitor Sarel Vane stood on the platform above the pyre, and the crowd hushed the way water hushes before ice. Vane was sixty and straight-backed and silver-haired, and she had the rarest gift Dain knew: she made cruelty sound like tidying. She surveyed the square with the expression of a woman checking a storeroom for damp.

"Citizens of Vaelharrow," she said, and her voice carried without effort, the way it always did, as if the city itself were listening and leaning in. "Three nights ago, something old stirred in the Lantern Quarter. Something the Law buried. And some among you felt hope." A pause, surgical. "Hope is the vector. Hope is how the old rot gets back into the walls. So tonight we perform a small surgery."

Petta Vosse was weeping and saying the Our-Father-of-the-Law, the prayer they taught children first, the one with no gods in it, only rules. Dain mouthed along without meaning to. He had known every word since before Coriander Street. Before the night he'd knelt by his bed with scraped knees and prayed — actually *prayed*, the forbidden way, the old way — for his father's cough to end. And it had ended. And three weeks later men in grey coats had come to the village asking questions with a patience that was worse than shouting, and the house next door had burned, and his grandmother had gripped his shoulder hard enough to bruise and said *never again, boy, whatever you have, starve it*, and he had obeyed her for thirty years, and here he stood, mouthing prayers to a Law that existed because of boys like him.

The torch went down. The straw caught. The smoke rose straight up in the windless evening, a black column stitched into the sky, and nine bells rang out from the Basilica tower — nine for a burning, the old count, older than the Law itself if anyone had bothered to ask, and no one ever bothered to ask.

Beside him, Brannic whispered, barely audible: "She didn't do anything. Sir. She *didn't do anything*."

"I know," Dain said.

"What do we — what does one *do*—"

"One stands," Dain said. "One is seen standing. One goes home and sleeps, if one can, and files one's reports tomorrow, and rounds where rounding is needed, and tells oneself it keeps worse things from happening. That is what one does." His voice sounded, to his own ears, like a description of drowning delivered by a swimming instructor. "It has worked for nineteen years."

"Has it?"

Dain didn't answer. Across the square, Sarel Vane's gaze swept the crowd — cataloguing faces, reading grief, hunting for the particular quality of attention that marked a believer — and passed over Dain, and paused, and came back.

She looked at him for a long moment across two hundred heads. Then she inclined her head slightly, the smallest acknowledgment: *good, you're watching, learn.* And turned away to the warmth of the fire.

Dain's faith in the Law did not crack all at once, that night. Faiths rarely break dramatically; they erode, quietly, the way a riverbank goes, one grain insisting it doesn't count. But standing in the smoke-smell with a luck-knot in his colleague's pocket and a woman's ashes rising straight as a plumb line, Dain understood something with terrible clarity:

There was a moth over a door in the Lantern Quarter. A woman with ink-stained hands had confessed to him without confessing, trusted him without knowing why, and gone back to her two-copper charms. And he had a report to file, and a choice shaped like a blank page, and a grandmother's voice in his ear saying *starve it, starve it* — except that the starving had never saved anyone, had it? It had only made the hunger patient.

He walked home the long way, past the Basilica, past the bell tower counting ten now, eleven, twelve, ordinary bells for an ordinary night. At his own door he stopped. Old habit. He glanced up, the way he did every winter, at the lintel where thirty years ago a grandmother's chalk mark had faded to nothing.

Then he went inside, lit one candle, and began to draft the report on Coral Nystrom.

He wrote: *Inquiry conducted. Subject cooperative. No evidence of unlawful practice found. Shop inventory consistent with licensed charm-trade. Recommend closure of file.*

He looked at the words for a long time. Somewhere across the city, a moth was presumably flying toward a crooked little shop that smelled of beeswax, and something vast was dreaming under the river, and High Inquisitor Sarel Vane had already assigned the Nystrom file to her personal detail — Dain had seen the assignment slip on her desk that afternoon, seen his own name already inked upon it, before he had even knocked on the charm-shop door.

She had known. She had sent him to test the woman, or to find her, or to fail — and whichever it was, the file was no longer his to round. It was hers to burn.

Dain blew out the candle and sat in the dark, complicit in a silence that had lasted thirty years, and wondered what a man owed to the truth when the truth had teeth.
