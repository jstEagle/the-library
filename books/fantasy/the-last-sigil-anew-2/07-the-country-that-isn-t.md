## 7. The Country That Isn't

The caravan left Vaelharrow before dawn, when the city was still the color of drowned pewter and the watchmen at the Salt Gate were more interested in their breakfast than in counting wagons.

Coral rode in the third wagon, wedged between a crate of pickled eels and a cage of sleeping quail, and tried very hard to be cargo.

"You're sitting on my manifest," Ryn said from the driver's bench, not turning around. He hadn't turned around once since they'd left his warehouse, and Coral wasn't sure whether that was kindness or cowardice. With Ryn Callas it had always been difficult to tell the difference.

"I'm not sitting on your manifest."

"You're absolutely sitting on my manifest. It's under your left hip. If the harbor inspectors stop us, I need it to look like we're carrying salt cod and prayer flags, and you are currently flattening my prayer flags."

"Prayer flags," Coral repeated flatly. "You smuggle prayer flags."

"They're *decorative* prayer flags. Perfectly legal. Devoutly popular. Nobody looks twice at a wagon full of piety." He finally glanced back over his shoulder, and there it was—that crooked half-smile that had gotten her into trouble at seventeen and would probably get her killed at thirty-one. "Besides, you're the one fleeing divine persecution. Maybe some of it will rub off."

"Ryn."

"Cargo doesn't talk," he said, facing forward again. "Cargo especially doesn't say my name like that."

So Coral shut up and sat on the manifest and watched Vaelharrow shrink behind them, its chimneys bleeding smoke into a sky gone pale as milk. Somewhere in those streets was her shop with its little brass sign—*Nystrom's Charms & Curios, Luck Reasonably Priced*—and somewhere on that door was a moth drawn in light that three days of rain had failed to wash away.

Somewhere behind them, too, was an inquisitor named Ferrow who had stood in her empty shop for a long time, according to the pigeons, and then left without arresting anyone.

She didn't know what to make of that. The pigeons didn't either. Pigeons were terrible gossips—all cooing and no substance—but they'd been unanimous on one point: the man had touched her grandmother's worktable very gently, like it might apologize to him.

The quail shifted against her knee, feathers rustling.

*Small ones,* the eldest quail murmured sleepily. *Warm. Not for eating?*

"Not for eating," Coral whispered. "Just ride along."

*Good. Riding is nice.* A pause. *Is the big dark still following us?*

Coral's stomach dropped. "What big dark?"

*Under the road. Under everything. It hums when we sleep. It says names.*

She looked out at the passing countryside—hedgerows, turnip fields, a shepherd moving his flock along a stony lane—and felt, for the first time since leaving the city, the thing underneath it. Not sound exactly. More like the vibration you feel through the floor of a house when a wagon passes on the far side of the street, except the wagon never stopped passing, and the house was the whole world.

It said names.

She decided, for the moment, not to ask whose.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow ringed by old yew trees, the kind of place where smugglers felt safe because honest people found it gloomy. There were eleven in the company: Ryn, four drivers, two brothers who handled the beasts, a cook named Marta who could make soup out of boot leather and make you thank her for it, and four guards whose job, as far as Coral could tell, was to lose at dice loudly enough to drown out suspicious noises.

Coral helped Marta with the pot, mostly because helping meant being near the fire and away from the quail, who had begun murmuring about the big dark again in voices like dry leaves.

"Eat," Marta said, pressing a bowl into her hands. "Fugitives who don't eat get caught. It's a fact of the trade."

"Is it?"

"Mm. Also, Ryn hasn't eaten. Take him a bowl. He gets stupid when he's hungry, and he's already—" Marta paused, choosing her words with the care of a woman defusing something. "He's been walking past your wagon all day pretending to check the axle. You've got one axle per wagon, girl. He's checked yours nine times."

"He hates me," Coral said.

"Obviously," Marta agreed. "Take him the bowl."

Coral found Ryn at the edge of camp, standing where the yews thinned out, looking north. She held out the bowl. He took it without looking at her, which took skill.

"The horses won't graze on the north side," he said. "Been like that since noon. They'll drink, but they won't put their heads down facing that way."

Coral followed his gaze. The land rose gently toward a ridge, black under the stars, unremarkable in every way she could see. But when she opened that other sense—the one she'd spent her whole life keeping folded up small, like wings in a narrow box—she felt it clearly: a lean in the world. As if every living thing on the plain were a compass needle and the north end of the magnet lay just beyond the ridge.

"It's pulling," she said quietly.

"You feel it too, then." He said it without surprise, and that surprised *her*. "Thought so. The mules have been fighting the traces since the ford. Old Tam says it's bog gas." He ate a spoonful of stew. "Old Tam also thinks the moon follows him personally, so."

"Ryn. What do you know about it?"

He was quiet long enough that the fire sounded loud behind them. Then he set the bowl down on a root, which for Ryn Callas—the man who once inventoried a stolen shipment of candlesticks twice because he couldn't bear uncertainty—was roughly equivalent to dropping it off a cliff.

"When I was fourteen," he said, "my father ran this same route. North circuit. And one night near the border stones, we stopped for the night and a stag came into camp."

"A stag."

"Big old thing, gray about the muzzle. Walked right through the middle of us, past the fire, past me close enough I could've touched it. And it walked up to the border stone—the first one, the one with the sigil cut into it—and it stood there and it *wept*." He rubbed his jaw. "Not bleeding, not hurt. Wept. Big silent tears, like a person. My father shot it. Said later it was sick, that sickness does that to deer. But I saw its eyes before it dropped, and Coral, that animal looked at that stone the way you look at a locked door when you're standing outside it in the rain."

Coral hugged her arms around herself. The pull to the north hummed faintly through her boots, patient as tide.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because yesterday you talked a griffhawk out of tearing my throat open, and then you asked me to teach you nothing and told me things no charm-seller knows, and now the whole countryside is leaning toward something only the animals seem able to hear." He finally turned and looked at her straight on. In the firelight his face was older than she remembered, and kinder, which was worse. "I'm telling you because I think you already know what's under the road. And I think if you know, then I'd rather hear it from you than spend another ten years telling myself my father shot a sick deer."

Coral looked at the fire. At the yew trees. At the north.

"My grandmother bound the gods," she said. It came out small. "That's what the dreams say. Vess says. The god under the river. It says my grandmother was one of the ones who wrote the Law, and that the binding runs in our blood, and that I'm—I'm the key." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it. "I don't even know what that means. I sell luck-charms. Half of them don't work. The other half work because I apologize to rats."

To his enormous credit, Ryn did not laugh, though she saw the laugh arrive and get wrestled down.

"And the rest?" he said gently. "The Law. What they taught us in school. That the gods were destroyed in the Reckoning, that magic burned out of the world like a fever breaking, and the Sigil Line keeps the ash from catching again?"

"That's what they taught us."

"That's what they taught everyone." He picked up the bowl again, holding it like an anchor. "My father heard different, from men who ran the northern route before the borders closed hard. Coral—the gods weren't killed. Killing a god isn't a thing you can do. They were *put away*. The whole country is the box."

The ground did not literally move. But Coral felt it move anyway—a slow dissolution, like watching ice go out of a river, the solid thing she'd stood on all her life quietly becoming water.

"The borders," she said. "The Sigil Line. It's not keeping ash in. It's keeping *them* in."

"Drawn in blood, my father's friends said. Signed in blood. Every border stone a nail in the lid." He shrugged, helpless. "I don't know how much of that's true and how much is smugglers' talk. Smugglers' talk is usually forty percent true and sixty percent excuse for the price of passage. But I know the stag wept at a stone. And I know my horses won't graze north. And I know you."

"I'm not a key," Coral said. "I'm a shopkeeper with a rat problem."

"You're a woman the god under the river calls by name," Ryn said, "who just fled the capital because a miracle happened over her head, riding in a wagon of prayer flags because I thought piety would hide you." He almost smiled. "If that's not a key, it's doing a remarkable impression of one."

* * *

Two days north, the animals began to change.

It started small. A hawk over the ford flew in perfect circles for an hour, tightening, tightening, like water going down a drain, until it dropped exhausted into a field and had to be left behind. Then the caravan's own dogs—one of them started howling at noon, facing north, and would not stop, and when the beast-hand cuffed it, it looked at the man with such clear human reproach that he sat down in the road afterward and wouldn't speak for an hour.

On the fourth night, the wolves came. Not to attack—they stayed at the edge of the firelight, six of them, sitting in a row like a congregation, and they *sang*. Not wolf-song. Something older, with harmonies in it that made the hair stand up on Coral's arms and made Marta drop a ladle and swear in three languages.

And the quail, huddled in their cage beside Coral's bedroll, whispered:

*It's closer. The big dark. It knows you're coming. It's happy.*

"Quail," Coral whispered back, "what does happy look like, on something that size?"

A long pause. Quails were not philosophers, but they were honest.

*Like the moment before the coop door opens,* the eldest said at last. *When everybody's pushing and nobody knows if outside is grass or a hawk.*

"Yes," Coral said. "That's exactly it. That's exactly the problem."

She lay awake after that, listening to the wolves sing themselves hoarse, feeling the pull strengthen hour by hour until it was less like a hum and more like a hand resting lightly between her shoulder blades—not pushing yet. Just reminding her it was there.

Vess spoke that night, in the space between sleeping and not.

*You travel well, granddaughter,* the god said, in a voice like the river under ice. *Your grandmother sulked the whole way to the signing hall. You have better manners.*

"What do you want from me?" she asked the dark. Around her, the caravan slept; above her, the wolves had gone quiet, all of them facing north even in sleep.

*Want?* A ripple that might have been amusement. *I want what was taken. I want the sky without a lid. I want my rivers to remember their names. And I want you to stop flinching every time I say the word debt, child. A debt can be paid in many currencies. I am not particular about coin.*

"You burned a chapel full of light into my door. You sent moths. You woke a sickness and made me talk to it. Forgive me if I keep my purse closed."

*The moth was a message, not a mark. Though I confess I enjoy that it frightened them.* The voice grew quieter, deeper, and Coral felt it in her teeth. *Ask your smuggler what lies past the border stones, Coral Nystrom. Ask him why no map printed in fifty years shows anything north of Greywater Reach. Ask him what country you are standing in right now.*

"We're in Vessaria," she said, using the schoolbook name. "The lawful territory of—"

*Say its true name.*

"I don't know its true name."

*No,* Vess said, and the sadness in it was vast and slow and old as riverbeds. *None of you do. That was the first theft, child. Before the gods, before the magic—they stole the name. A country that forgets what it is called can be told it has always been whatever you say it is.* A pause, and then, softer, almost tender: *Sleep, granddaughter. Tomorrow the road bends east, and tomorrow you will see the first stone. And tomorrow, I think, your smuggler will finally tell you the rest of what he knows—because he has been carrying it since he was fourteen years old, and a man can only hold a locked door shut for so long before his arms tire.*

* * *

The border stone stood alone in a field of wet grass, and Coral understood immediately why the maps stopped here.

It was perhaps nine feet tall, granite gone green with moss except where the sigil was cut—a deep-carved spiral of lines that hurt to look at, that seemed to rearrange slightly whenever she blinked, that her eyes kept trying to file as *just weathering, just lichen, nothing to see here*. The Law's own signature, written into the land itself. And around its base, worn into the soil by centuries of paws and hooves and feet, was a path. A circle. Worn smooth by countless creatures pacing round and round the stone, unable to cross, unwilling to leave.

Ryn stood beside her. Behind them, the drivers had unloaded the mules and were pretending, very badly, to inspect harnesses. Everyone knew what the stone was. Nobody had ever said.

"My father's friends," Ryn said quietly, "called the country something else. The old name. The one that got signed away." He looked at her sideways. "You want to hear it?"

The pull at her back had become a pressure now, warm and immense and waiting. The quail murmured in their cage. Far off, a single wolf began to sing, and the note it chose was one Coral somehow knew, though she'd never heard it—a note like the first syllable of a word the whole world had been forced to forget.

"Yes," she said.

Ryn took a breath, and told her the country's real name—and as he said it, the border stone shivered from base to crown, moss sliding loose, and beneath the carved sigil something else began to glow through the granite like a brand through paper.

Something answering to its name.

From the north, unhurried, patient as tide, the humming rose to meet them—and this time, Coral could hear words in it.

Her name among them.
