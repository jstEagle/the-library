## 8. Cages

The griffhawk had been screaming since dusk.

Coral heard it through the canvas wall of her tent in Ryn's caravan camp—three wagons pulled off the salt road into a stand of wind-bent pines—and she did her best not to hear it. That was the trick of the last six days on the road: hearing everything and pretending to hear nothing.

*Let me out,* said a fox from the second wagon, in the flat gray way foxes talk, all edges and hunger. *Small one. Let me out and I will owe you a debt of teeth.*

*I can't,* Coral murmured into her bedroll.

*Liar,* said the fox, without malice. Foxes didn't have malice. They had appetite, and appetite was honest in a way people weren't.

The griffhawk screamed again—a sound like an eagle's cry dragged through something older and sadder—and then it stopped screaming, which was worse. Animals that screamed were still fighting. Animals that went quiet had started doing sums about their own worth, and animals always arrived at the same answer when they were caged long enough: *not enough.*

Coral lay in the dark and stared at the canvas ceiling and thought about Ryn Callas asleep twenty feet away, his hat over his eyes, his boots by the fire where he always put them so he could find them barefoot if trouble came. She thought about the debt between them—the old one, the ugly one, the one shaped like a night in Vaelharrow seven years ago when she'd sold his route to a customs officer because the alternative was watching them hang him for something worse than smuggling, and he'd taken the lash instead and never once asked her why.

He hadn't asked her why now, either. When she'd come to him at the Salt Lantern with her hood up and her hands shaking, he'd listened to maybe ten words—*they're hunting me, I need out*—and set down his drink, and that had been that. The price came later, quiet as a knife going back in its sheath: *You'll talk my cargo calm. Half my beasts die of fright every run. You stop that, we're square.*

She'd agreed before she understood what the cargo was.

She understood now. Sixty cages under tarps and slatted boards: foxes, marsh-cats, a moon-pale pair of river otters who chattered to each other all night in a language like water over stones, a blind old boar-hound who had stopped asking questions years ago. And the griffhawk, the crown of the shipment, bound for some collector in Corvess with more coin than conscience. Griffhawks mated for life. This one's mate was somewhere behind it, south, getting smaller with every mile.

*Not enough,* the griffhawk had said that first day, looking at Coral through the bars with one gold eye. *I know what you are. I smelled it on you in Vaelharrow, before the city got loud. You're the listening one. So listen: either kill me or open this cage, because I will not arrive.*

Coral had told it she couldn't do either. It had turned its head away, which is what hawks do instead of spitting.

Now it was midnight, and the camp was quiet except for the fire ticking, Ryn's slow breathing, the creak of rope, and the sum the griffhawk was doing again, patiently, the way you do a sum you already know the answer to.

Coral sat up.

"You're awake," said the otter nearest the end of the second wagon, delighted. Otters were always delighted; it was their entire philosophy. "We're awake too. We're always awake. Did you know the stars are holes? We think they're holes."

"Holes in what?"

"That's the fun part," said the otter. "Nobody agrees."

"Shh," said the other otter, who was the sensible one.

Coral pulled her coat on and stepped out into the cold. Frost silvered the grass. The pines leaned together overhead like conspirators. She walked past the dying fire, past Ryn's wagon—his breathing changed for half a heartbeat when her boots crunched, and then steadied; he slept light but he trusted his camp, and that trust sat in her chest like a swallowed stone—and stood in front of the griffhawk's cage.

It was a beautiful animal even hunched and filthy, feathers going gray at the wingtips, forelegs folded under it like a falcon's patience wearing a lion's body. Up close, in the dark, it smelled of iron and pine sap and old grief.

"I'm not opening your cage," Coral whispered. "I want you to understand that. I'm telling you so you don't waste hope on me."

The griffhawk opened its eye.

"But," Coral said, and hated herself, "if a cage were to happen to be badly latched—I've noticed the latch on your door is rusted, anyone could see it's rusted—if it happened to swing loose in the night, and if you happened to fly south until the mountains, no one in this caravan would chase you. We'd tell the buyer wolves got into the cargo. Wolves are plausible. Everyone believes wolves."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the third wagon the blind hound sighed, the sigh of a creature who has given up on miracles and is only noting, for the record, that the air smells different tonight.

"You would betray him," the griffhawk said. Not accusing. Just placing the fact on the table between them, feathered and cold. "The man who feeds you."

"He's not feeding me. He's being paid in calm beasts, and I'm failing to pay."

"Why?" the hawk asked, and it was the question underneath all its questions, the one it had been circling for six days. "Why keep us? Why keep *yourself*? You could unlatch any door. You choose the smallest one."

Because doors are how I lost everything, Coral almost said. Because the last time I opened one wide, a god answered through my throat and put a burning moth on my doorframe and a price on my head. Because small mercies are the only kind that don't leave footprints.

What she said was: "Because I'm a coward."

"No," said the griffhawk, with terrible gentleness, the way big predators are gentle when they bother to be. "Cowards don't walk to cages at midnight. Cowards pull blankets over their heads." It shifted, and one great talon curled around the bar, and the gold eye fixed on her. "You're something rarer. You're someone who thinks her kindness has to stay on a leash to be safe. Someone taught you that." A pause. "They were wrong, little listener. Kindness on a leash just strangles slower."

Coral's hand was on the latch. She didn't remember moving it.

The rusted hinge gave with a sound no louder than a knuckle cracking.

* * *

It should have been one cage. That was the plan, if a plan could be said to exist: one latch, one hawk, one plausible wolf story, one small treason to balance against the large one she owed Ryn.

But the fox smelled the open air.

*Out,* it said, urgent now, all its gray edges sharpened. *Out out out—*

And the otters took up the chorus—*out, out, the holes in the sky are closer out here*—and the marsh-cats woke and began that low yowling harmony cats make when they agree about something, which is rare and ominous, and the blind hound stood up against the bars of its crate for the first time in what the smell of it suggested was years, nose working, tail giving one uncertain thump.

"Oh," Coral said, to nobody, to the frost, to her grandmother's ghost. "Oh, this is a disaster."

Her hands were already moving.

She told herself she'd think about each one. She told herself she'd weigh it—the otters might survive the river two miles east, the foxes were born survivors, the marsh-cats would melt into the pines—but the truth was that after the third cage her hands simply knew what they were doing, quick and quiet, latch after latch, while she whispered directions: *south for the water, follow the low ground, the dogs are chained by the fire so go wide, go now, go.* The wagons emptied like lungs exhaling. Foxes poured over the tailgate in a silent ribbon. The otters went last, pausing at the edge to say, in unison, *we'll remember the shape of your voice*, which is the highest compliment an otter gives, and then they were gone, a splash-and-silence, and the camp held nothing but empty cages and frost and one very confused hound standing loose in the grass, blinking its milky eyes at the enormous open world.

The hound couldn't run. That was the problem she'd saved for last, because she'd known it was there all along, the way you know about a wound under a bandage.

It found her by smell, blundered happily into her legs, and leaned its whole weight against her knee.

"Old man," Coral whispered, crouching, scratching the ruined velvet of its ears. "Where would you even go?"

*With you,* said the hound, simple as bread. *Wherever you are is where things smell right.*

And that—that undemanding, absolute little sentence—was the thing that finally broke her, kneeling in the frost with an empty menagerie behind her and a man she'd betrayed twice waking twenty feet away. She pressed her face into the hound's neck and breathed, and the hound licked her ear, and neither of them mentioned that she was crying.

Which was when the lantern came up.

Ryn Callas stood at the edge of the firelight in his shirtsleeves, boots in hand—he'd put them on quietly, of course he had, he'd heard the latches, he must have heard every single latch—and looked at the empty wagons for a long, long moment. His face did something complicated. Coral watched it try out fury, land briefly on disbelief, and settle somewhere she couldn't name, somewhere tired and far away.

"The griffhawk alone was four hundred crowns," he said at last.

"I know."

"The whole cargo was—" He stopped. Recalculated. Decided, visibly, that arithmetic wasn't the fight he wanted. "Corvess pays me on delivery. I pay my crew on delivery. There are nine of them, Coral. They have families."

"I know." Her voice cracked on the second *know*, and she hated it, and kept talking anyway because stopping would be worse. "I'll work it off. Ten runs, twenty, whatever the road costs—I'll charm your luck, I'll sweet-talk customs officers, I'll—"

"Coral."

"—I'll do anything, I just couldn't—" Her breath hitched. "The hawk asked me to kill it or free it. *Kill it*, Ryn. And the otters sing to each other at night and the fox offered me a debt of teeth like that means anything to anyone but a fox, and I have spent my whole life starving the one thing I'm good at because it was safer for everyone, and I am so *tired*, and I couldn't listen to sixty voices begging and do nothing. I couldn't. So yes. Yes, I betrayed you. Again. Add it to the ledger. I'll never be square with you, I know that, I've known it for seven years—"

"Coral." Louder. Then, gentler, which was somehow more devastating: "Come here and look at the wagons."

She came. He held the lantern high. Empty crates, bent latches, straw, a single gray feather caught on a bar and turning slowly in the night wind.

"What do you see?" he asked.

"A ruin. Four hundred crowns of ruin."

"I see," Ryn said slowly, "the quietest jailbreak in the history of crime. Nine men sleeping thirty feet away. Me, ten feet away, and I sleep like a cat—you know I sleep like a cat, you used to complain about it." He crouched, ran his thumb along one of the latches, frowned. "You didn't drug anyone. You didn't gag the dogs. The marsh-cats, Coral. Marsh-cats scream like murdered children when they're frightened, ask anyone who's shipped them. Every animal in those wagons walked out *silent*, because you asked them to." He looked up at her. In the lantern light his eyes were the brown of river water, and very steady. "Sixty beasts, one night, and the loudest sound in this camp was you crying over a dog."

"I—"

"I've run cargo for eleven years." He straightened, slung the lantern from its handle. "Eleven years, and every run I lose three or four to fright—they batter themselves bloody in the dark, they break their own hearts against the bars, and I tell myself it's the trade, it's the cost, everyone pays it. And you walk out here one time and every animal trusts you enough to follow you *quietly* into the woods, and the difference between those two pictures is—" He exhaled through his teeth. "The difference is you can hear them, and I can't, and I've spent eleven years not knowing what I couldn't hear."

The frost ticked. The fire settled. The blind hound, sensing the moment required support, leaned heavily against Ryn's leg, nearly toppling him.

"Sorry, old man," Ryn muttered, steadying himself on its collar. Then, to Coral, in the voice of a man stepping off a cliff and finding it was a stair: "Teach me."

Coral blinked. "Teach you what?"

"To listen. Not—" He waved a hand. "Whatever it is you have, I know I don't have it, and I'm not asking for the magic. But there's a thing you do. You go still, and you tilt your head, like you're hearing a room within the room. I've watched you do it since Vaelharrow. I thought you were nervous." A short, rueful laugh. "I thought you were *nervous*. Teach me whatever's underneath that. If a smuggler could hear even half of what his cargo feels—how many fewer die of fright? How many runs go clean? Maybe—" and here he paused, choosing the words carefully, laying them down like cards, "maybe the trade changes. Maybe there's a version of this where I'm not shipping hearts in boxes at all."

"It won't work," Coral said. "Not really. Humans hear weather, not words. You'll get impressions. Feelings. Mostly you'll get hungry."

"So did you, probably, at the start."

That landed harder than he could know. Coral looked away, toward the pines where sixty sets of tracks were filling softly with frost, toward the south where a griffhawk was flying toward its mate with the wind under it, toward everything she had done in one reckless hour that no amount of luck-charms could unlatch her from.

"My grandmother tried to teach me," she said quietly. "Before the Law took her. She said the gift isn't words—it's attention. The words are just what falls out of you when your attention gets good enough." She looked back at Ryn. "She said most people spend their whole lives talking *at* the world. Listening to it is a discipline. Like sailing. Like reading current."

"Then I've got eleven years of current," Ryn said. "Start tomorrow?"

"Start now." She nodded at the hound. "Lesson one is leaning on your leg and judging your balance. He says you lean left."

"I do *not*—"

The hound shifted its weight deliberately. Ryn staggered left, caught himself, and stared down at the milky-eyed dog with the expression of a man whose entire map of the world had just acquired a coastline.

"...Huh," said Ryn Callas.

And that was when the horses began to scream—not fear-screams, Coral knew the difference now better than anyone alive, but the ragged complaint of animals pushed too hard too fast—and the firelight caught dust rising on the north road, a lot of it, coming fast. Behind it, faint but closing, rode a sound that made the hound drop to its belly and made Coral's blood go thin:

Bells. Nine of them, tolling out of the dark in the pattern every child in Vaelharrow learned before their letters.

The pattern that meant the Inquisition had picked up a scent.
