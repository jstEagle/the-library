## 9. The Inquisitor's Doubt

The summons came at dawn, which was how Dain knew it would be bad. Good news arrived by letter, delivered with the afternoon tea. Bad news arrived in person, before the sun had properly committed to the day.

High Inquisitor Sarel Vane received him in her office on the top floor of the Bastion of Law, a room so high and bare it seemed designed to remind visitors that heaven had been officially decommissioned and replaced with paperwork. She stood at the window with her back to him, watching the city wake below—smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys, all of it obediently ordinary.

"Ferrow," she said, without turning. "You've read the report on the fever-house miracle."

"I have, High Inquisitor."

"And your assessment?"

Dain had written three drafts of that assessment. The first said *the child recovered through natural means*. The second said *no evidence of divine intervention found*. The third—the one he'd submitted—said *investigation ongoing*, which was the bureaucratic equivalent of burying something alive and hoping it didn't dig.

"The evidence is inconclusive," he said.

Vane turned then. She was not a tall woman, but she had a way of occupying space that made height irrelevant, like a blade doesn't need to be long to be sharp. Her gray hair was pulled back so tightly it seemed to pull her eyebrows up with it, giving her an expression of permanent, patient disbelief.

"Inconclusive," she repeated. "A child dying of marsh-fever, given hours by three physicians, sits up at midnight and asks for porridge. A moth of light burns itself into the doorframe. And you find this inconclusive."

"Medicine surprises us sometimes."

"Medicine does not leave sigils." She crossed to her desk and laid a folder upon it—not the thin folders of routine casework but one thick as a brick. "Here is what we know, Ferrow, since your reports have become so shy lately. The moth matches no registered mark. The child's mother consulted a charm-seller in the night market two days prior. That charm-seller has since abandoned her shop and fled the district." She tapped the folder once. "Her name is Coral Nystrom."

Something cold walked down Dain's spine. He kept his face still. Twenty years of practice, keeping his face still.

"You're assigning me to the hunt?"

"Wrong." She slid the folder across the desk. "I'm assigning you to *my* hunt. Mine personally. You will report to no one but me. You will file nothing without my review. Do you understand why?"

Because I falsified a report eleven years ago and you've known ever since. Because a woman who keeps files on everyone keeps files on herself. Because this is a leash dressed up as an honor.

"Because the case is sensitive," he said.

"Because the case is *delicate*," Vane corrected. "There is a difference. Sensitive means careful hands. Delicate means some things must break quietly, where no one hears." She smiled—it was a real smile, warm even, which was always the worst part of her. "Find her, Ferrow. Bring me everything you learn about her, especially anything that seems like nothing. Especially that."

* * *

Coral Nystrom's shop sat on Tallow Lane like a thing pretending to be forgotten: shutters drawn, sign hanging crooked, dust on the doorstep undisturbed except for the tracks of pigeons who had already claimed it as theirs. Dain stood across the street for a long moment, feeling absurdly like a burglar outside his own conscience.

He'd been to the night market twice in the past days, asking quiet questions in his quiet way. The vendors spoke of her the way people speak of weather—fondly, vaguely, with no strong memory attached. *Nice enough. Kept to herself. Sold luck-charms, mostly useless ones.* Nobody remembered a miracle. Nobody wanted to remember one. The Sigil Law had taught Vaelharrow's small folk a valuable lesson: miracles were trouble, and trouble came for whoever had seen it first.

A neighbor, an old woman shelling peas on her stoop, gave him the only detail that mattered. "She talked to the birds, near enough. Not talking-talking. Whistling, calling them. They'd sit on her hand like she paid wages." A pause; peas clicked against the bowl. "Good girl, though. Whatever you're hunting her for."

"We're not hunting anyone," Dain said, and heard how hollow it sounded, and left before she could say something kinder and worse.

The lock on the shop door had been broken from the inside—she'd gone out fast, or meant someone to think so. He stepped into the dim interior and stood a while just breathing it in.

Charm-shops smelled of nothing, usually. Dried herbs and cheap wax and merchant's hope. This one smelled of living things: straw and feathers and the faint musk of animals, layered under lavender that couldn't quite disguise it. Shelves lined the walls, half-empty now. He recognized the inventory of a woman who'd packed in a hurry—or packed only what mattered. Charms remained in tidy rows: knotted red thread, river-stone amulets, little cloth mice stuffed with mint. Harmless things. *Useless* things, the market had said.

Except he'd spent twenty years reading the gap between what people displayed and what they were, and the gap here yawned like a cellar door. A woman selling useless charms for fifteen years did not flee a district over a healed child unless the charms had never been the point.

He worked the room slowly. Under the counter, a ledger—sales recorded in a round, careful hand. Luck-charm, two coppers. Hearth-blessing (decorative), four coppers. Rat-warrant, five coppers, *guaranteed*. He huffed despite himself. Guaranteed rat-warrants. Half of Vaelharrow's pest problem probably solved by a woman charging pocket change and whispering to rodents.

In the back room, a cot, a cold stove, a chipped cup holding three paintbrushes and a spoon. On the wall above the cot, someone had pinned a child's drawing—a grinning stick figure surrounded by scribbled birds—and beneath it, pressed flat between two boards of wood, a nest of dried grass where, until very recently, something small had slept.

She'd made a home here out of the world's leftovers, and the world had made her run from it.

He told himself the thought was professional observation. It lied to him comfortably, the way old lies do.

* * *

It was the stove that betrayed her—or rather, the ashes in the stove. Someone had burned papers hastily, incompletely, and Dain had spent too many years sifting other people's fires to walk past one.

He knelt and eased the fragments out with two fingers, laying them on the hearthstone like pieces of a body. Most were char beyond rescue. But near the bottom, shielded by the mass above it, a section had survived: leather cover, scorched at one corner, pages water-swollen and brown-edged. A journal. He opened it with the care of a man defusing something.

The handwriting changed over the years—he understood that quickly. Early entries were cramped and formal. Later ones slanted, hurried, the script of a mind outrunning its own caution. And the content—

*They tell us the gods are dead. Dead things do not press against the walls of the world. I have felt the pressing.*

*The binding holds because it is keyed to blood. Our blood. As long as one of our line lives unbound, the Door stays shut. They called it mercy, what they did to us. They called it the Law.*

Dain read standing up, though his legs suggested sitting. Here was the founding terror of his whole life, the catechism he'd recited at seven years old with his hand on the Sigil stone—*the gods are dead, magic is dead, and we are safe*—and here was a dead woman's handwriting saying *no, child, only locked.*

And then, near the end, the pages darkened by fire:

*The Door of Anew stands beneath the old signing hall, in the country that was. It is not a door for leaving. It never was. It is a door for letting in. If you hold this book, you are the key or you are the keeper. Pray to be neither.*

Dain became aware, distantly, that his hands were shaking.

The Door of Anew. He knew the words the way you know a word from a dream—familiar and sourceless. His grandmother had sung a nonsense rhyme about doors that open inward, about a country with its name scrubbed off. He'd assumed it was nonsense. Grandmothers' rhymes generally were.

But his grandmother had also, once, in the year before the soldiers came to Hollowmere, looked at the sky with an expression he had never been able to forget and said, softly, as if to the clouds themselves: *not yet, then.*

He closed the journal. The Bastion protocol was simple and absolute: recovered documents went to the case officer. The case officer was Sarel Vane. Everything in him trained by twenty years of service said *turn it in*—and underneath that training, quieter and older, something else said: *Vane asked for everything that seems like nothing. She knows what this book is. Why else send me? Why else demand it all?*

If he turned it in, the journal would vanish into her office and Coral Nystrom would become whatever Vane decided she needed to be—an example, a lever, a key turned by force. And if the book spoke true, if there really was a Door beneath the ruins of the capital—

Then this was not a hunt for a witch. This was a hunt for a hinge, and Vane intended to be the hand upon it.

Dain looked around the little shop—at the child's drawing, at the empty nest, at the ledger full of two-copper mercies—and performed the first deliberate act of treason in his twenty years of faithful service.

He took off his coat, folded the journal flat against his chest, wrapped his scarf around it, and buttoned the coat over all of it. Then he fed the remaining fragments back into the stove and burned them thoroughly, adding splinters of kindling to hide the smell, and swept the hearth clean, and left the shop exactly as he'd found it except for the absence of a book that had never, officially, existed.

On the doorstep, a pigeon watched him with one orange eye. It did not fly away.

"Don't look at me like that," Dain muttered. "I'm still deciding what I am."

The pigeon, having no opinion worth sharing, flew off toward the river.

* * *

That evening, Vane summoned him again. Dain had prepared himself for questions; instead he found her studying a map of the eastern roads, weighted at the corners with candlesticks, a line of ink running east out of Vaelharrow like a vein let.

"She's moving," Vane said. "Caravan traffic on the river road—a smuggler named Callas. Ryn Callas." Her finger rested on the ink line. "Do you know him?"

"No, High Inquisitor."

"Liar," she said pleasantly. "Everyone knows him. He runs the marsh route. Which means our charm-seller is leaving the country that officially isn't one." She looked up, and her eyes were bright with something Dain had learned, long ago, to fear more than anger. Certainty. "East, Ferrow. Toward the ruins. Isn't that interesting? There's nothing in the ruins. Nothing at all. So why would she go there?"

"I couldn't say."

"No," Vane agreed. "But she could. And when we ask her, you'll be there, because I want a witness I trust." The word *trust* settled over Dain like ash. "Riders go out tomorrow at first bell. We'll take her quietly, if she comes quietly. And if she doesn't—" Vane rolled up the map, gentle as a nurse tucking in a blanket. "Well. You saw what happened to the woman in Candler's Square. The Law must be seen to work, or people start wondering why we need it at all."

Dain bowed, and took his leave, and walked home through streets smelling of rain and coal smoke with a stolen heresy buttoned against his chest, warmer than any coat had a right to make him feel.

Tomorrow, riders. Tomorrow, Candler's Square again.

He climbed his stairs, barred his door, and by candlelight unwrapped the journal and read it through to the last legible page, memorizing every word as a man memorizes a prayer—except that for the first time since he was a boy in Hollowmere, kneeling on cold stone while the sky above his village went strange and golden and terrible,

he was praying to something that might actually answer.
