## 10. Where the River Remembers Its Name

The drowned chapel had been a rumor before it was a place.

Coral had heard about it from a marsh heron two days earlier — herons being the gossips of the waterways, all legs and no discretion — and the bird had described it in that flat, fishy voice of theirs: *Old stone under the shallows. Bell tower like a broken tooth. Fish go there to pray, if you believe fish pray, which I do not, but they gather anyway.*

She hadn't told Ryn where she was going. She'd told him she was scouting the ford ahead, which was true in the way that most of her lies were true lately — technically accurate, spiritually bankrupt.

"You're going to see *it*," the griffhawk said from her shoulder as she waded through the reeds. The hawk — she'd stopped calling it "the cargo" somewhere around the second night, and Ryn had pretended not to notice — had opinions about everything, delivered in a voice like gravel poured over honey. "You're going to talk to the river-god alone, without backup, without a plan, and possibly without pants, because the water looks deep."

"I have a plan."

"Your plan is 'walk toward the god.' I've heard your plans. Your plan last week was 'walk away from the god.'"

"That one worked," Coral said.

"It worked because the god let it work." The griffhawk ruffled its feathers, talons tightening gently on her shoulder — a gesture that had started as restraint and had somehow become affection, which concerned them both. "You don't walk away from something like Vess unless it wants to be walked away from."

Coral didn't answer, because the griffhawk was right, and because ahead of her the reeds opened onto a wide brown sheet of water, and rising out of it — half-sunk, half-standing, impossible — was the chapel.

It looked like a memory of a church rather than a church itself. The bell tower leaned at an angle that defied both engineering and dignity, its bell long gone, the arch where it had hung now just a stone mouth open to the sky. The nave sank into the floodwater step by step, so that only the roofline and the upper windows stood above the surface, and in those windows there was no glass — only water-light, green and gold, moving.

The birds had gone quiet. Not silent — quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters it. Coral could feel every creature within a mile holding its breath: the fish below, the frogs in the cattails, a family of voles frozen mid-commute in the bank beside her.

And beneath it all, underneath the water and the mud and the roots and the bones of the old country, something vast rolled over in its sleep and said:

*You came.*

Coral's knees nearly went out from under her. She caught herself on a fencepost that might once have been an iron gate and might, by now, legally be considered a tree.

"I came," she said aloud, because whispering felt insufficient and screaming felt unwise. "Against my better judgment, which I'd like noted for the record."

*The record,* Vess said, and the word arrived not as sound but as a pressure behind her eyes, a taste like river-stones, cold and clean and old. *There has been no record for sixty years. That is rather the point of you.*

"I'm not 'the point' of anything. I'm a charm-seller. I sold a love potion to a man who was already married last month, and I charged him double because he was annoying about it. That's my record."

The water laughed.

She felt it before she understood it — a shiver across the whole flooded plain, ripples running outward from nothing, the reeds bowing in sequence like a crowd doing the wave. It was not a cruel laugh. It was the laugh of something that had been alone a very long time and found her stubbornness charming the way one finds a mouse charming: small, doomed, delightful.

*Come inside, Coral Nystrom. The water will hold you. It holds everything, eventually. But today, kindly.*

"I would prefer," she said, stepping forward before her survival instincts could organize a proper protest, "*promptly.*"

* * *

The chapel's interior was underwater to her waist, and it should have been freezing, and it wasn't. The water around her held the warmth of a bath left too long, and light came up from below — not reflected sunlight but something older, gold-green, moving in slow coils through the flooded nave. Broken pews jutted from the depths like the ribs of some patient beast. Above the ruined altar, a mosaic clung to what remained of the wall: a figure with rivers for hair and moths for eyes, arms spread wide, and though the tiles had fallen away from its face, Coral knew it was smiling.

Her grandmother's journal had shown her sketches like this. She'd thought they were decorative.

"They prayed to you here," she said. Her voice echoed strangely, doubled — once in the air, once in the water, once somewhere deeper than either.

*They sang to me here. Praying is asking for things. Singing is giving them.* A pause, and then, with something almost like shyness: *I preferred the singing.*

"What happened to them?"

*You know what happened to them. You have known since the rats told you, since I first touched your dreams. The Law came. The Sigil burned. They took my name off every map and my voice out of every throat, and they built a country on top of me like a lid on a pot.* The light in the water darkened, coiling tighter. *Sixty years, Coral. Sixty years pressed flat under their careful, tidy, terrified little nation. Do you know what it is to be a river that is not allowed to be a river?*

"No," Coral admitted. "But I know what it is to be a speaker who isn't allowed to speak. So. Partially."

Something moved in the deep — vast and slow, a shape that might have been a body or might have been the idea of one, scales like drowned coins. The water rose gently around her, lifting her, carrying her toward the altar as though she weighed nothing, and she supposed that to Vess she didn't.

*I chose you because you understand starvation,* Vess said. *Your grandmother bound us because she thought she understood mercy. She was wrong, but she was wrong lovingly, which is more than most of them managed. And her blood runs in you. The binding knows you. The Door knows you.*

"The Door." Coral's stomach dropped. Even the name of it made the air feel thinner. "Everyone keeps saying that word like I should already understand it."

*Because you should. Because your grandmother wrote three hundred pages about it and you have been carrying the surviving ones in your coat for a month.* The amusement in Vess's voice flickered and went out. *Very well. Let me show you instead of telling you. It is kinder. Most things I do are not kind, so allow me this one.*

The water closed over her head.

She should have panicked. She had time to panic — a full, luxurious second of it — and then the light took her, and panic became irrelevant, the way weather becomes irrelevant when you are shown the sky.

* * *

Vess showed her the world as the gods remembered making it.

Rivers that walked. Mountains that grew like slow green thoughts. Forests that rearranged themselves overnight to shelter travelers, and storms that apologized afterward. Every animal speaking, every stone listening, magic not as a rare and hunted thing but as the ordinary texture of existence — as unremarkable as rain, as constant as breath. She saw the old country, the one whose name had been scraped off every map and replaced with the tidy borders of Vaelharrow-and-its-provinces, and it was beautiful the way a wild thing is beautiful: dangerous, generous, alive.

And then Vess showed her what returning would cost.

The vision turned, and beauty became fire.

Not cruelty — that was the terrible part, the part she'd turn over at 3 a.m. for the rest of her life. Not punishment, not rage, not vengeance. Simply *pressure*. Sixty years of a god compressed beneath a country, and when the seal broke, the release would not be gentle. Rivers would take back their valleys whether or not children slept in them. Forests would surge through farmland that fed cities. The Sigil itself — woven into law, into stone, into the blood-oaths of every official and the brick of every courthouse — would tear loose, and buildings raised on its geometry would come down with it. The land would be scoured. Remade. The old world pushing up through the new like spring grass through a road, except the road was made of people.

She saw Vaelharrow's night market under water. The stall where she'd sold luck-charms, the crooked awning, old Maretta's spice cart — all of it green-lit and silent, fish drifting between the stalls.

She saw the fever-child's mother standing in a doorway that no longer existed.

She saw — and this was the worst, this was the image that made her claw upward through the vision — she saw the griffhawk, free and flying, and then not flying, falling, because the sky itself was changing, remade, and wings built for the old air did not fit the new one yet.

*This is what breaking the binding means,* Vess said, and there was no apology in it, only honesty, vast and terrible and clean. *We cannot come back softly. We have been held too hard for too long. When we return, we return as floods return — not because we wish to drown, but because we are water, and the lid is coming off.*

"No." Coral's voice came out raw, echoing in the drowned nave, in the vision, in herself. "No. Find another way."

*There is no other way. There is only sooner and later, and later is worse. The prison weakens, child. When it breaks on its own, we will not arrive as gods remembering our manners. We will arrive as animals, starving, mad with confinement — and we will remake the world in our convulsions. At least if the binding is opened deliberately, by blood that understands it—*

"By *me*." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "You want me to be the key. You want me to open it. You keep saying 'deliberately,' like that makes it better — like I'm supposed to stand at your Door and pull the trigger nicely, so the apocalypse is *tidy*."

*Yes,* Vess said simply. *That is the debt. Your grandmother's blood bound us. Her blood may unbind us. I am sorry. Debts of this size do not care about fairness.*

"Well, here's the thing about debts," Coral said, and she planted her feet on the submerged altar step, waist-deep in a god, shaking, furious, absolutely certain. "I've spent my whole life watching people use power on other people *for their own good*. The Inquisition burns a neighbor for her own good. The Sigil Law erased a whole country for its own good. My grandmother imprisoned gods for their own good. And now you want to drown everything I've ever known for yours. I refuse. All of it. Every version. I'm not your weapon, Vess. I'm not anyone's weapon. I'm a woman who sells charms and talks to rats, and I would like to continue doing exactly that until I die of old age and mild scandal."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for her to count her own heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

Then the water laughed again — but differently this time. Slower. Warmer. With something in it that she was horrified to recognize as respect.

*Defiance,* Vess murmured. *Sixty years I have spoken to the creatures above me, through cracks in the prison, through dreams and rat-whispers and the fever-mad. Do you know how many humans heard me and said yes?*

"...How many?"

*All of them. Eventually. Fear is very persuasive, and hope is worse — hope makes people agreeable.* The great shape turned beneath her, and the golden light gathered close, and for a moment she could almost see a face in it: not human, not fish, something like a moth the size of a cathedral, wings of silt and starlight. *You are the first to look at the end of the world and say no. I find that I like it more than I expected. Perhaps that is why your grandmother's line was chosen — not for obedience, but for the capacity to refuse us.*

"So you'll... let me go?"

*Let you?* The amusement rippled through the water. *Child, I cannot make you open the Door. That is its design. Blood freely given or not at all — your grandmother built it that way precisely so no one like me could force it. No. I let you go because letting you go is the only move I have left.*

The light dimmed, and when Vess spoke again, the warmth had cooled into something flat and factual, which was somehow more frightening than all the fire.

*But hear me, Coral Nystrom, and carry these words carefully, because I will not repeat them. I am not the only one beneath the Law. There were others bound that day — elder things, hungry things, things your grandmother bound with cruder knots because even her mercy had limits. They feel the prison weakening too. They do not dream politely. They do not ask. They send their servants.*

The water around her went cold for the first time.

*The Inquisition has its hounds. But there are hunters coming who were never human at all — things that eat names, that wear the shapes of the missing. They will find you faster than any rider, because you shine, Coral. Since the child lived, you shine like a lamp in a cellar, and everything hungry can see it.*

"How long?" she asked. Her voice sounded very small in the great drowned room.

*Less than you need. Go to the Door. Choose. Quickly.* And then, softer, almost tender, which was its own kind of terrifying: *And child — when you get there, and you stand before the end of the world, remember that you looked at a god today and said no. Whatever you choose at the Door, choose it the same way. Freely. It is the only currency that opens it.*

The light rose. The vision folded shut like a book. And the water set her down, gently, on the muddy bank among the reeds, as though she were a passenger disembarking rather than a witness fleeing.

* * *

She sat in the mud for a while. The griffhawk landed beside her with a thump and regarded her with one ferocious golden eye.

"Well?" it demanded. "Did you die? You look like someone who died and was returned due to a clerical error."

"I talked to a god."

"And?"

"And it was polite," Coral said. "That's the worst part. It was *polite*."

The hawk considered this. "The polite ones are the worst. The rude ones just take your food. The polite ones take your *future* and thank you for the visit."

Coral laughed — a wet, hiccupping sound that was mostly exhaustion — and dragged herself upright. Her legs shook. Her coat was soaked. Somewhere in its inner pocket, the half-burned journal pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and for the first time she understood what it actually was: not a history. Instructions. Three hundred pages of instructions written by a woman who had loved the world enough to cage it, waiting for a descendant brave enough or foolish enough to undo it.

"We have to go back to the caravan," she said. "Tonight. Now. We're going to the capital."

"The ruined one? Where the Law was signed?" The griffhawk's feathers rose along its neck. "That's suicide with extra steps."

"Probably. But there's a countdown attached, and I intend to beat it."

She turned toward the road — and stopped.

Far off, at the edge of the marsh where the causeway climbed toward the hills, torchlight moved. Many torches. Too many for smugglers, too orderly for bandits. And riding at the head of them, a banner she recognized from a childhood she'd spent trying to forget: the sunburst-and-chain of the Holy Inquisition.

But that wasn't what froze her blood.

What froze her blood was that, beneath the torchlight, at the rear of the column, something *else* moved — tall and wrong and jointed in ways that made her eyes slide off it, a shape wearing the silhouette of a person the way a glove wears a hand. And as she watched, it lifted its face toward the drowned chapel, toward her, toward the light she was apparently shining into every cellar in the world.

It smiled. She couldn't see its face from here. She knew it smiled anyway.

"*Hunters,*" the griffhawk breathed, and for once the gravel-honey voice held nothing but fear. "Coral. Run."

They ran.
