## 11. The Hunt Begins

### Coral

The griffhawk had been sulking for three days.

Coral knew sulking when she heard it. Most people heard a bird making soft, discontented clicks from the top of the cargo wagon and thought nothing of it. Coral heard a litany of grievances delivered in the tone of a duchess whose soup had gone cold: *the cage is too small, the road is too bumpy, the man with the red scarf smells of fear and onions, and you, whisperer, have not visited me since dawn.*

"I've been busy," she murmured, keeping her lips barely moving as she passed the wagon. To anyone watching she was just a woman muttering to herself, which in a caravan full of smugglers made her practically furniture.

*Busy,* the griffhawk repeated, with the flat disdain only raptors could achieve. *You were sitting by the fire doing nothing.*

"Thinking counts as something."

*It does not. Thinking is what prey animals do before they die.*

"You're a delight, do you know that?"

The hawk clicked twice—its version of a laugh—and rustled its gray-and-copper feathers against the bars. Three nights ago Coral had stood in the dark with her hand on this cage's latch, and everything between her and Ryn had changed shape. Not healed. Changed shape, like a bone setting crooked but setting nonetheless. Ryn hadn't spoken to her much since, but he'd started leaving the good coffee near her bedroll instead of hoarding it, which in smuggler language was practically a love letter.

They were two days out of the marsh country now, rolling through hills that had once—according to Vess's dreams, according to the rats, according to the stones themselves—been called something other than "the Northern Tract." The old name lived in the ground like a scar under paint. Coral could feel it if she pressed her palm to the earth at night: a word the land kept trying to say.

She was thinking about that word—which was, admittedly, doing nothing—when the horses screamed.

Not whinnied. *Screamed*, all six of them at once, and every animal in the caravan answered: the caged dogs howling, the hens bursting into frantic flapping, the mules kicking their stalls. Coral's gift flared open like a door blown off its hinges and the night flooded in with voices—

*Riders. Riders riders riders iron and oil and—*

"—smell of the Law," finished a fox in a far-off den, in the tones of one describing weather.

"Vane," Coral breathed.

Ryn was already moving, shouting orders, cutting the lead horse loose. "Off the road! Into the trees—" He caught her arm, and for one heartbeat his eyes said everything his mouth wouldn't: *I told you they'd come. I told you.* "Back wagon. Now."

She ran. Behind her the first torchlight crested the hill, and it was wrong—too white, too steady, burning without flicker. Inquisitor fire didn't dance. It marched.

### Dain

Dain had ridden with Vane's riders before. He knew the choreography: the white torches fanned out in a crescent, the horn sounding three notes that meant *no survivors need be taken alive unless convenient*. He knew where he was supposed to be—at the crescent's center, riding point, because High Inquisitor Sarel Vane liked her defectors visible. *Let them see you hunt your own kind, Ferrow. It's the best sermon there is.*

He had not slept in four days. The journal sat against his ribs under his coat, Grandmother Nystrom's half-burned handwriting pressed to his heart like a second, traitorous pulse. Every hour on the road he expected Vane to look at him and simply *know*. She had a way of looking at people as though reading a ledger written on their skin.

The caravan scattered exactly as he'd predicted it would, because Ryn Callas was a good smuggler, and good smugglers always broke toward the trees. It didn't matter. The riders had cut the escape routes an hour before the raid, quiet as stitching.

"Hold the wagons!" Vane's voice carried over the chaos, calm as a market bell. "The beast-speaker is mine. Burn nothing until I've seen her face."

Dain rode into the smoke and did his job, which these days meant performing his job while quietly sabotaging it—herding two fleeing drovers toward a gully he knew was passable, "failing" to see a third. It wasn't enough. It had never once been enough. But tonight, threading between burning wagons, he heard something that stopped his horse cold.

A voice—not human, coming from a cage being dragged off a wagon by two troopers—a griffhawk's cry, and inside the cry, layered like light through a prism, *words*:

*The whisperer runs east. The whisperer runs east and the iron men follow.*

The hawk wasn't screaming in fear. It was giving directions. Screaming them, loudly, repeatedly, directly at the troopers dragging its cage, who heard only a frightened animal.

Dain felt something crack open in his chest that had been cracked for thirty years and finally came apart at the seam.

*East*, he thought. And turned his horse that way.

### Coral

She ran through the trees with branches whipping her face and the voices of the forest shrieking around her—the whole wood awake and terrified, deer crashing through brush, owls pouring out of their hollows like startled thoughts. The riders' horses thundered parallel to her through the dark, and she heard *them* too, poor beasts, torn between rider and instinct:

*Run, little runner, run, we will not catch you, we are so sorry, we are so sorry—*

"It's not your fault," she gasped, which was a mad thing to say to enemy cavalry horses while sprinting for her life, and also the truest thing she'd said all week.

Then the ground fell away into a ravine, black and root-toothed, and her foot caught a snare-root, and she went down hard enough to see stars that weren't stars. Torchlight bloomed behind her. A rider emerged from the trees above, white fire painting his armor, and raised the short cruel net-launcher the Inquisition used for gifts they wanted breathing.

And the sky screamed.

It came down out of the dark like a thrown knife with feathers—a gray-and-copper streak, wings wide, talons forward, shrieking a battle-cry that contained, distinctly, several words Coral would not repeat in polite company. It hit the rider's arm, tore the launcher away, buffeted his horse into reeling backward, and then wheeled up into the night, trailing indignation.

*Well?* the griffhawk snapped down at her. *Get up. Prey that lies down dies. This is basic.*

"You opened your own cage."

*I picked the lock with my tongue while the onion man monologued. You taught me that humans never watch hands that aren't theirs. Fly, whisperer. Run. Choose one quickly; both are better than standing.*

She chose running. The ravine swallowed her, roots grabbing, water ankle-deep and freezing, and the torches multiplied above her along the rim like a crown of small moons closing.

### Dain

He found the ravine because the griffhawk had told him where to look, and he found the chase because Vane's riders were loud even when they tried not to be. He left his horse tied and went down the slope on foot, and there she was—Coral Nystrom, cornered against a rock shelf with the water streaming past her boots, three torches descending the ravine walls toward her. One of the torchbearers was Trooper Hesk, young, eager, already nocking a binding dart.

Dain stepped out onto the rocks between them.

"Inquisitor Ferrow!" Hesk's relief was naked. "Sir, she's pinned—"

"She's mine." Dain let the words come out flat and cold, the way Vane said them. "High Inquisitor wants her taken intact and unspoiled. Your darts bruise. Your torches frighten her gift into hiding. Go back up and tell them Ferrow has the quarry."

Hesk hesitated. Dain watched the boy weigh obedience against ambition and saw, with a tired sort of grief, that ambition won—but slowly. Too slowly. Above them, more torches were coming.

"Go," Dain said again, and put his hand on his sword hilt in a way that ended the conversation.

When the boy's light had vanished up the slope, Dain turned.

Coral Nystrom stood hip-deep in the stream with a broken branch raised like a sword, soaked to the waist, mud to her eyebrows, and glaring at him with the ferocious courage of someone who has absolutely decided to die inconveniently. Up close, without the terror, she looked—ordinary. Tired. A shopkeeper with excellent posture and terrible circumstances.

"You're Ferrow," she said. "The one who falsifies the reports."

So the animals talked about him. Well. Of course they did.

"They know?" he asked.

"The mice in your garrison are very well informed." The branch didn't lower. "You're here to take me to Vane."

"Yes." He reached slowly into his coat—and felt her tense, felt the whole ravine tense, every hidden creature holding its breath—and drew out the journal. Half-burned, water-stained, his thumb worn smooth on its cover from four days of carrying it. He held it out across the stream. "Your grandmother wrote about a Door. Under the ruined capital. Where the Law was signed."

Coral stared at it. Then at him. "You're supposed to burn this."

"I'm supposed to burn a great many things." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Thirty years ago I prayed. Something answered. My village burned for it, and a woman named Sarel Vane signed the order, and I have spent my whole life since pretending the answer was my imagination." He took a breath. "I am done pretending. The Door is real. Take the journal."

She didn't move. Water rushed between them. Somewhere overhead the griffhawk circled, silent now, watching with a raptor's patience.

"Why?" she demanded. "Why should I trust an inquisitor?"

"You shouldn't." He set the journal down on a flat stone at the stream's edge and stepped back from it, palms open. "Trust is yours to spend. But there are forty riders on that ridge and one of me, and I am the only one of us who knows which paths Vane hasn't covered. The marsh oracle. The capital. Whatever your god told you—I know the roads, Nystrom, and I know which of my brothers-in-arms can be bribed, blinded, or outrun."

Above them, a horn sounded—Vane's voice calling the sweep tighter. Minutes, maybe less.

Coral waded to the stone. She picked up the journal without taking her eyes off him, the way you'd pick up a snake you hoped was friendly. "If this is a trap—"

"If it were a trap," Dain said, "you'd already be in irons, and I'd already be forgiven. That's how traps work."

Something almost like a laugh escaped her—startled, exhausted, gone in a second. She clutched the book to her chest. "The griffhawk," she said abruptly. "Did you hear it? What it was shouting?"

"That your quarry runs east and the iron men follow." He allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "First prayer I've heard answered in thirty years. Seemed rude to ignore it."

For a moment they just looked at each other across the churning water—an inquisitor and a witch, a hunter and the hunted, two people standing on opposite banks of the same long night. Dain thought: *she has no idea what she is yet.* Coral thought: *he looks like a man carrying something heavy for a very long time.* Neither said any of it aloud.

"East," she said finally. "There's a drover's track past the burned mill. Rats told me. It's ugly but it's empty."

"I know it. It's uglier than the rats think." He pulled his cloak off and held it out. "You're shaking. Vane doesn't catch corpses; she catches prisoners. Warm up."

She took the cloak. It was absurd—the fugitive wearing the hunter's cloak—but the world had been absurd for some time now, and they were both too tired to fight it.

### Coral

They moved east along the ravine floor while the horns called to each other behind them, confused now, chasing echoes. Twice Dain led them off the path on instincts Coral couldn't see, and twice she heard, minutes later, riders passing where they would have been. The griffhawk flew scout overhead, sending down terse bulletins—*two torches north, moving away, idiots*—and Coral relayed them, and Dain adjusted, and by the third mile they had become, without discussion or ceremony, something that functioned like a unit.

It was Ryn she couldn't stop hearing. Not his voice—the memory of it. The last thing he'd shouted as the riders took him: not a plea, not her name. *"Don't look back! East, you idiot, EAST!"* He'd spent his last free breath pointing her at the exit. They had him now, in a binding wagon, headed back to Vaelharrow, and Coral's chest hurt every time she thought of it, which was constantly.

"We'll get him back," Dain said quietly. He hadn't been looking at her. He'd known anyway. "Sympathizer smugglers run the river roads. If anyone can reach a prisoner convoy, it's Callas's own people—if we can find them."

"*We*," Coral repeated.

Dain's jaw tightened. "I burned a bridge tonight. Several bridges. There's a very specific tone Sarel Vane uses when she says my name, and I imagine I'll be hearing it soon." He glanced sideways at her, and in the pre-dawn gray his face looked older and lighter at once, like a house after the furniture's been removed. "Besides. Someone has to carry the journal. You'll want your hands free for talking to birds."

From above, the griffhawk descended and landed on a stump beside them, folding its wings with the smugness of a creature that had recently saved everyone.

*The onion man fought well,* it reported. *He bit a trooper. I respect this. Also, the whisperer's tall grim friend owes me a rat.*

"He does," Coral agreed solemnly.

"I do," Dain said, with the bewildered air of a man realizing he had just accepted a debt to a bird, and finding, to his private horror, that it felt correct.

They walked east into the paling sky, toward the marshes and the oracle and the ruined capital where a country's death warrant had been signed—a witch, a traitor, and a bird, none of whom belonged together, all of whom were, somehow, going the same way.

Behind them, far off, a single white torch flared on the ridge where they'd crossed. Watching. Counting.

Sarel Vane had seen the cloak go east.

She had also seen who wore it last.

* * *

By midmorning they reached the burned mill, and Coral stopped dead on the threshold of its blackened doorway, because the rats were waiting for her there—dozens of them, lining the sill like a tiny solemn honor guard, and the eldest among them spoke with a trembling urgency she had never heard from rodent-kind before:

*Whisperer. Whisperer, the marsh oracle is dead. Killed in the night. And the thing that killed it left tracks that smell of the prison under the river—and it is following you already.*
