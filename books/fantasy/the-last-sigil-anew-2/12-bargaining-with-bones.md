## 12. Bargaining with Bones

The marshes began where the map gave up.

Coral had learned that on the second day out from the burned caravan site, walking beside the griffhawk—who had, after much deliberation, named himself Brackle and refused to answer to anything else. The maps Ryn kept in his saddlebag showed the eastern lowlands as a smear of brown ink with a single word across it: *unprofitable*. The smugglers' maps were better. They marked the marsh as *the Long Quiet*, which was a lie, because the marsh was never quiet. It croaked. It dripped. It sighed in its sleep like an old man who resented being asked to do so.

But it was quiet in one way that mattered: no bells. No Inquisition riders, or none that could follow her here. Even Vane's hounds would balk at ground that swallowed hoofprints behind you like a guilty secret.

"You're sulking," Coral told Brackle.

*I am conserving dignity,* Brackle said. His voice in her head was dry and rusted, like a gate hinge that had opinions. *There is mud to my elbows. I am a creature of sky and legend. I have mud to my elbows.*

"It'll wash."

*Nothing washes in this place. I can smell it. This mud intends to stay.*

She couldn't argue. The marsh did have intentions—she'd felt them since they crossed the first black causeway. Animals spoke of places the way people spoke of people, with grudges and fondnesses and long memories, and the Long Quiet remembered being something else. A forest, maybe, before the rivers were dammed and the Law redrawn the rain. Now it sat where it had been put, festering politely, keeping its secrets under six feet of tea-colored water.

Which was exactly why the bone-oracle lived here.

The directions had come to her in fragments: a heron who'd heard it from a pike, a pike who'd heard it from a drowned man's ghost, and a ghost—well. Ghosts were new. Since the night Vess had spoken through her, since the Door of Anew had entered her life as a word and then a wound, the dead had started noticing her too. That was a problem for a future version of Coral. The current version was up to her knees in marsh water trying not to think about how many days it had been since she'd slept without dreaming of drowning chapels.

"There." She pointed to what she'd been pretending wasn't there for the last hour: a mound rising from the reeds, pale and curved, like the back of some animal too large to be believed. Bone. Not a rib—the scale was wrong—but something arched and honeycombed, riddled with openings like a cathedral built by something that had never seen a cathedral but had been told about them, dismissively, once.

Brackle went very still. *That is a skull.*

"That's a skull."

*Whose skull?*

"I don't know. Something older than names, probably." She waded closer. "The heron said knock with your knuckles on the left eyehole. Apparently the right one is rude."

*Rude,* Brackle repeated, in the tone of a creature reconsidering every choice that had led him to associate with a human. *To whom?*

"To whoever lives there now, I'd guess."

Coral found the left eye socket—a hollow big enough to park a cart in, its rim worn smooth by centuries of supplicants' knuckles—and knocked. Three times. The sound didn't echo. It was *swallowed*, the way the marsh swallowed everything, and then, from deep inside the bone, came a sound like a drawer opening in a house that had been empty for a hundred years.

"Come in," said a voice like gravel poured over velvet. "Mind the femurs. People always trip on the femurs."

***

Inside the skull, it was warm.

That was the first impossible thing. The second was the light—no torches, but a soft gold glow seeping from the bone itself, as if the whole structure remembered sunlight and was quietly homesick. Femurs lay stacked like firewood along the walls. Vertebrae formed steps. And in the center, enthroned on a pelvis the size of a rowboat, sat the oracle.

It was difficult to say what species it had been. Small, once. A woman-shaped thing wrapped in a cloak of woven sinew and river-pearls, with a face that was mostly kind wrinkles and mostly shadow. Its eyes were two chips of amber, and in each chip, Coral saw movement—tiny scenes flickering, like reflections in a shop window of a street that no longer existed.

"Oh," the oracle said, leaning forward with genuine delight. "Oh, you're a *listener*. A real one. Do you know how long it's been? The last one who came through was—" its face did something complicated— "hm. No. I sold that memory. Never mind. Sit, sit. Mind the femur."

Coral sat on a vertebra that had clearly been polished into a chair by decades of nervous visitors. Brackle wedged himself into the doorway and pretended he wasn't interested, which fooled no one, least of all the oracle.

"Brackle," Coral said, "you can come in."

*I am guarding the exit.* A pause. *Also the ceiling has teeth.*

"The ceiling does have teeth," the oracle agreed cheerfully. "They're mine. Or they were. Hard to keep track." It produced a teapot—from somewhere; Coral decided not to investigate—and poured two cups of something that steamed green and smelled like a meadow having a good day. "Drink. You're dehydrated and heartbroken and those are terrible conditions for negotiation."

"I'm not heartbroken."

The oracle looked at her with its amber-window eyes, and Coral felt seen straight down to the ledger of her, every entry itemized. She drank the tea.

"Better," the oracle said. "Now. You want the Door of Anew. Don't look surprised—everything that comes to this marsh wants either the Door or revenge, and you've got the look of someone who'd be bad at revenge." It sipped its own tea. "I can tell you where it is. Precisely. Down to which stone to stand on and which stone never, ever to touch. But the marsh doesn't give, little listener. The marsh trades."

"How much?" Coral reached for her coin purse.

The oracle made a sound like a laugh dying of natural causes. "Coins. Sweetling. What would I do with coins? Build a second skull?" It set down its cup. "Memories. I deal in memories. Good ones, bad ones—I'm not fussy, though I confess a weakness for bad ones with interesting endings. A memory freely given. One that matters." Its eyes flickered, tiny scenes swimming. "And before you try it: no, a memory of what you ate last week will not do. The trade weighs what the memory weighs *to you*. Nothing more, nothing less. The marsh is fair that way. Fairness is about all it has left."

Coral's hands went cold around the cup.

She had known, in the way you know things when you're walking toward them and refusing to look up. The heron had mentioned payment vaguely. The pike hadn't mentioned it at all. But hearing it stated—*a memory that matters*—made it solid, made it a door of its own, standing open in front of her.

"What happens to it?" she asked. "The memory. When you take it."

"I keep it," the oracle said simply. "I live a very long time, and it gets very boring, and other people's memories are how I remember what living was like. Yours would be safe. Cherished, even. You just—" it made a small gesture, like brushing away a moth— "wouldn't have it anymore. There'd be a hole. Smooth-edged, eventually. People around the hole are the hard part. They remember *for* you, and they don't understand why you flinch."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you wander the marsh until the Inquisition finds you, or Vess finds you, and between those two options, sweetling, I'd choose the Inquisition. At least Vane's people kill you quickly." The oracle refilled her cup unasked. "Take your time. The tea's free. Well—the tea costs a memory of a meal, but I'll waive it, call it a loss leader."

Coral stared into the green steam.

She inventoried herself the way Ryn inventoried cargo: what could be spared, what couldn't, what would break if removed. Her memories of Ryn—no. Those had already been half-surrendered once, the night she'd betrayed him to save him, and she would not pay the same bill twice. Her grandmother's journal pages, memorized in Dain's abandoned shop—those were load-bearing. The fever-child's mother's face, lit by candlelight, whispering *thank you*—that was the reason for all of this. The rats of Vaelharrow, the first voices she'd ever heard, the small gray congregation that had kept her sane through twenty years of starving her own gift—

No. Not the rats. She'd rather lose a hand.

That left, sitting quietly at the center of her, the thing she'd been circling since she walked in.

Her mother.

Her mother singing off-key while kneading bread. Her mother's hands, flour to the wrists. Her mother laughing at her father's terrible jokes. Coral had lost her mother twice already—once to the fever, and once to the Sigil Law, which had erased her name from the census rolls so thoroughly that Coral sometimes doubted she'd existed at all. The memory was the last proof. The last place her mother lived.

And it was also the only memory of that weight that she could survive losing. Barely. That was the horrible arithmetic of it. Everything heavier would stop her heart, and everything lighter wouldn't open the Door.

"You're doing sums," the oracle observed gently.

"I'm doing sums."

"They never come out the way you hope. That's not a flaw in the arithmetic. That's the design." The oracle leaned forward, and for a moment the amber eyes held nothing but warmth, old and bottomless. "Can I tell you something no oracle ever says? You can walk away. Everyone forgets that's an option. The Door is a door, not a debt. Whatever Vess told you about blood-debts and keys—debts can be disputed. Keys can be melted down. You could go east past the mountains and be a charm-seller in some country that still has birdsong, and no god would ever find you, because gods are terrible at borders."

For one whole breath, Coral let herself see it. A stall in a sunny market. Pigeons she could talk to openly. No bells. No moths of light over her door. No child's fever, no burning caravan, no Ryn in chains somewhere south because of her.

Then she thought of the country behind her. All of it. Every neighbor vanished for a suspected blessing, every child taught that prayer was a disease, every rat and raven and river-fish holding their tongues in a land where listening was contraband. Vess wanted to burn it clean and start over. Vane wanted to feed it, piece by piece, to a prison and call the screaming *safety*. Nobody—not the god, not the inquisitor—was going to give the country back its name.

Somebody had to open the door carefully instead of letting it be kicked down.

"Somebody has to," she said aloud, and realized she'd been arguing with the oracle's offer out loud, which explained the oracle's expression of vindicated delight.

"They always say that," the oracle said happily. "Right before paying. It's my favorite part, professionally speaking. So." It set down its cup and folded its pearl-strung hands. "Name it. Name what you'll give."

Coral set down her own cup. Her hands were shaking. She let them.

"My mother's face," she said. "All of it. Every time I ever saw her face. Singing, laughing, angry, sick, saying goodbye at the train platform. The whole... the whole collection." Her voice caught on *collection*, because that's what it was—a shelf in her, crowded and dusty and precious, and she was about to hand the entire shelf away. "You'll keep it safe?"

"With my life," the oracle said, and meant it in the literal, geological sense. "Are you certain? Once spoken, the trade is sealed. The marsh honors its bargains absolutely. It's the last honest thing about it."

"No," Coral said honestly. "But certainty's a luxury. Do it."

The oracle rose—or unfolded; the motion defied the usual categories—and came to stand before her, close enough that Coral could smell river-water and old incense and, strangely, bread baking. The oracle raised one hand, sinew-wrapped and gentle.

"This will feel like a tide going out," it said softly. "Don't fight the tide. Fighting is how people drown."

It touched her forehead.

The tide came.

It didn't hurt. That was the worst part—that it didn't hurt, that it was smooth as water leaving a shore, that her mother's face simply *drained* out of her the way color drains from the sky at dusk, gradually and then completely. She felt the shape of it going. She felt herself reach for it even as it left—*wait, wait, the song, she was singing the song about the miller's daughter, wait*—and the reaching closed on nothing. On smooth edges. On a hole with soft walls.

The oracle stepped back, and its amber eyes blazed briefly with new light, new scenes: a kitchen, flour-white hands, a woman singing badly and beautifully about a miller's daughter. It pressed both hands to its chest, and something in its ancient face cracked open with tenderness.

"Oh," the oracle whispered. "*Oh.* She had a wonderful face. Thank you. Thank you for her." And then, businesslike again, dabbing at its eyes with a sleeve: "Right. The Door. Listen closely, because I'll only say it twice, and the second time costs extra."

***

"The ruined capital," the oracle said, pacing before its wall of femurs like a lecturer who had waited centuries for an audience. "Old Caerwyn, before they renamed the country *nothing* and called it peace. Where the Law was signed—literally signed, ink and blood and a binding written in a grandmother's hand. You know whose hand."

"My grandmother's."

"Your grandmother's. The binding didn't just cage the gods, sweetling—it wrote itself into the ground. The signing hall became the lock. The city above became the tumblers. That's why they razed it and salted it and struck it from every map: not shame. *Maintenance.* You don't leave a keyhole lying about in a populated area." It stopped pacing. "The Door of Anew stands beneath the signing hall. Beneath the floor where your grandmother stood when she bound them. Blood-keyed, blood-sealed. Your blood opens it. Which means, I regret to inform you, that everyone who knows your name now knows the address of the only key in the world."

"Wonderful," Coral said faintly.

"Isn't it? Tragedy is so much better than boredom." The oracle returned to its throne and settled, pearls clicking. "Three warnings, gratis, because I liked your mother's face and I'm feeling generous. One: the dead of Caerwyn still whisper in the stones. They will show you things. Let them show you—grief delayed is grief compounded. Two: Vess will meet you there, and it will not ask this time. Gods mistake patience for permission, and yours has run out. Three—" the amber eyes dimmed, almost gentle— "there's a man coming to find you. An inquisitor. He carries a burnt journal and a boyhood prayer he's never confessed, and he is going to arrive at exactly the wrong moment, or possibly the only right one. I genuinely can't tell. The marsh shows me tides, not certainties."

Coral stood slowly. The hole inside her ached in a way that wasn't pain—more like a tongue returning to a missing tooth. Around her mother, in her mind, everything else remained: her father, her grandmother, the rats, the fever-child, Ryn's face in lamplight. Just one shelf, empty. Smooth-walled.

"What was her name?" she asked suddenly. "My mother. You have her memories now—you must know—because I'm realizing I can't..." Her voice failed. She tried again. "I can't check. I can't check anywhere. The Law took the records. Please."

The oracle was quiet a moment. Then it smiled, and in its smile was flour dust and off-key singing and a train platform in the rain.

"Maren," it said. "Maren Nystrom. She thought you hung the moon, Coral. She used to tell the neighbors you'd be a saint someday, and they'd laugh, and she'd laugh too, but she meant it."

Coral stood in the warm gold light of a dead god's skull and wept for a woman whose face she could no longer picture but whose name she could finally say aloud, and the oracle sat with her the whole time, and did not charge her for the tissues, because the tissues were imaginary anyway.

***

They left at dusk, when the marsh turned the color of bruised plums and the frogs started their evening parliament. At the edge of the causeway, Coral paused and turned back. The skull-mound glowed faintly in the gloom, and in the left eye socket, the oracle stood watching her go, one hand raised in farewell.

*Maren Nystrom,* Coral thought, deliberately, the way you press a flower in a book. *Maren Nystrom. Maren Nystrom.* Building the name a shelter of repetition, since she had nothing else to build it from.

*You've gone strange and quiet,* Brackle observed, gliding down to land on a fencepost of fused vertebrae beside her. *Stranger than usual. What did the bone-witch take?*

"My mother's face."

Brackle said nothing for a long moment, which from a griffhawk was a eulogy. Then he extended one wing—not quite touching her, but near enough that she could feel the warmth of it, and the mud, which was apparently eternal.

*Then we carry it for you,* he said. *Rats do that, you know. Crows. Ravens especially—they remember faces for years and tell each other. Somewhere in this world, every bird that ever saw her is still holding her face. You just can't ask them anymore. But it exists. It's not gone from the world, Coral. Only from you.*

Coral looked at him. Twenty years of hiding, twenty years of starving the gift—and the animals had turned out to be kinder company than nearly any human she'd known.

"Thank you," she said.

*Yes, well.* He ruffled his feathers, embarrassed. *East, then. Old Caerwyn. Try to look less like a key and more like a smuggler. You have the mud for it.*

They turned onto the east road as the first stars came out—and Coral froze, because down the southern causeway, small in the purple distance, a single lantern was bobbing toward them through the reeds. Not a patrol's rhythm. Too slow. Too careful. The walk of a man carrying something heavy that wasn't luggage.

Behind the lantern-light, faint but unmistakable on the night wind, came the sound of a hymn—the old kind, the banned kind—sung quietly, badly, and with tremendous conviction, by a voice she'd last heard reading charges in a Vaelharrow courtroom.
