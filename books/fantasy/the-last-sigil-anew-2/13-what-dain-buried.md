## 13. What Dain Ferrow Buried

The journal smelled like a house fire.

Dain sat in his quarters at the Bastion of the Ninth Vigil with it open on his knees, turning pages by candlelight, and every time he touched the paper he could smell smoke that wasn't there. It was the smell of the burned half—char at the edges of the surviving pages, ash worked into the grain—and it was also the smell of a night thirty years gone, which no candle in Vaelharrow had ever managed to burn out of him.

He should have turned it in. That was the whole architecture of his life: you found a thing, you reported a thing, you filed a thing in triplicate, and the machine of the Law ground on without needing you to feel anything about it. He had built that architecture himself, brick by brick, over thirty years of service. It had held up under worse than this.

It was not holding.

The handwriting was small and stubborn, slanting leftward as though the writer were always leaning away from something. *Mara Nystrom*, said the flyleaf. Dain knew the name now; he'd pulled the census records twice, once by daylight like an honest man and once after midnight like the other kind of man he was apparently becoming. Coral's grandmother. The binder. The woman whose signature was stitched into the Sigil Law itself, though history books called her only *the First Hand*.

And in the margins, in that same leftward-slanting hand, were drawings. Moths. Rivers drawn as if rivers had faces. A door, sketched again and again, each time slightly different—the way you'd sketch a face from memory, trying to get it right.

Dain read until the candle drowned in its own wax. Then he did what he had done every night for a week, which was to tell himself he would decide in the morning, and then lie awake listening for hoofbeats that weren't coming yet.

* * *

Here is what Dain buried, and where:

Under the floor of a granary that no longer exists, in a village called Emberlow, which also no longer exists, there was a loose board beneath which a boy of nine kept his treasures. A hawk feather. A river-stone with a white ring around its middle like an eye. And a prayer—not written down, because writing prayers down was already against the Law even in a backwater village where the Law arrived mostly as rumor—but a real one, spoken aloud into the dark of a hayloft, with his hands pressed together because that was how the old stories said you did it.

His mother was dying. That was the whole of it. Fever, the third winter in a row, the herbwoman shrugging her shoulders in that particular way that meant *no*. And Dain, who had been raised on the new catechisms—*the gods are dead, the gods are dust, the Sigil is our shield*—went out to the hayloft anyway and did the forbidden arithmetic of a nine-year-old: if the old gods were dead, praying cost nothing. And if they weren't dead—

"Please," he'd said, to nobody, to the dark, to whatever might be embarrassed enough by a child begging to break its own rules. "Please. She raises the bread. If she dies we don't have bread."

It was not a noble prayer. It has never become nobler in the retelling. But the hayloft went warm, all at once, like a hand cupped around a coal. And something spoke—not in words, exactly, but in the way his mother's voice came through the floorboards when she sang: felt first, understood second.

*Little keeper,* it said, or meant. *What will you give?*

"I don't have anything," Dain had whispered, stricken, because he'd been taught bargains and this felt like one. "I'm just a boy."

*A boy is enough. Boys grow.*

In the morning his mother's fever broke so hard she asked why her pillow was wet with river water. In the morning the well tasted sweeter. In the morning, Dain Ferrow believed in God the way other people believe in fire—totally, and with a healthy terror.

He told no one. Of course he told no one. He kept it under the loose board with the hawk feather and the eye-stone, and it stayed there for four years, warm and secret, while he grew the way boys do.

Then, when he was thirteen, the Inquisition came to Emberlow.

* * *

They came because of the Wellens girl, who was six, and who had told her grandmother that a lady made of moths tucked her blanket up at night. A child's dream. A nothing. But the grandmother mentioned it at market, and a tithing-man wrote it in a ledger, and ledgers travel.

Dain remembered standing in the square with the rest of the village, watching the gray-cloaks go door to door with their brass instruments that hummed near old blessings. He remembered the humming getting louder as they passed *his house*, and his heart going up into his throat, because the instruments would find the residue of his prayer—it had to, it had found less—and then they would ask him questions, and Dain Ferrow at thirteen was many things but a good liar was not among them.

But the instruments passed his house and only murmured, drowsy, and moved on. Thirty years later, holding Mara Nystrom's journal by candlelight, Dain finally understood why: the residue had been *given back*. Taken up, paid down, smoothed away—by whatever had answered him, quietly, before the gray-cloaks ever crested the hill. His god had cleaned up after him the way his mother cleaned flour off counters. *Little keeper. I keep you.*

The Wellens girl was not so lucky. Her lady-of-moths had been only a dream, dreamed by a child, with no god behind it and therefore no power to hide it. The brass instruments hummed over her cot anyway, because six-year-old dreams are indistinguishable from witchcraft if the reader wants them to be.

And then the gray-cloaks' captain had read out the orders, and Dain had heard a name he didn't know yet but would spend his life serving under: **Sarel Vane**, then a rising interrogator, whose written instruction was appended to the bottom of the field order in a hand like a row of nails.

*Suspected manifestation. Precedent must be set. Burn the structure; question the family; leave the record unambiguous.*

They burned the Wellens farmhouse with the family inside, because the record had to be unambiguous, and ambiguous records begin with survivors. Dain stood in the square and watched, and inside his chest the secret prayer turned over like a coal in ash, and he thought—with a clarity that felt like the hayloft's warmth, terrible and total—*they are burning houses over dreams, and my prayer was real, and I am alive because something loved me enough to lie for me.*

He joined the Inquisition two years later. Everyone assumed it was zeal. It was, in its way. He meant to climb high enough to stand between the instruments and the children. He told himself that story for thirty years, and it was even mostly true, right up until the nights he falsified reports and understood that he was not standing between anything—he was bailing the sea with a spoon, one spared farmer at a time, while the tide came in on schedule.

* * *

Now the candle was dead, the journal was open to a page he'd read nine times, and dawn was doing its gray work at the shutters.

*The Door does not lead out,* Mara Nystrom had written, in a passage that survived the burning by being folded inside another page like a letter hidden in a book. *Everyone assumes the Door leads out. Why else build a door? But a door is not an exit. A door is a hinge. It swings both ways, and what it lets out it also lets in. I bound it shut because they begged me to bind it shut, and I have hated myself in eleven languages ever since.*

Below that, smaller, almost apologetic:

*If you are reading this, you are either my granddaughter, who I hope never needs it, or someone the granddaughter trusted, which means someone brave or someone doomed, possibly both. The key is blood. The price is everything the blood can hold. Forgive me. I was tired, and they were frightened, and tired frightened people will always choose a cage over a storm.*

Dain closed the journal.

He had a report due. High Inquisitor Sarel Vane had personally assigned him the moth-sigil investigation—personally, which was itself a message; Vane did not assign things personally unless she wanted a leash visible—and the report required him to name the bearer of the sigil, confirm the miracle at the fever-child's bedside, and recommend disposition. *Disposition.* The word the Inquisition used instead of *what we will do to her*, because the Inquisition loved euphemism the way drowning men love air.

He knew the answer. He'd known it for a week. The bearer of the moth sigil was Coral Nystrom, charm-seller of the Nightmarket, a woman whose entire criminal record consisted of selling luck that worked suspiciously well and undercharging for it. He had stood in her abandoned shop three days ago, breathing the ghost-smell of dried lavender and pigeon, and he had watched her neighbors describe her with the baffled warmth people reserve for someone who has done them small kindnesses they never examined too closely. One woman had cried. A cobbler had said, "She fixed me luck with a rat, I swear it on my mother, a *rat*," and laughed until he saw Dain's face.

And Dain had pocketed the journal instead of filing it, which meant that somewhere between the shop and here, he had stopped being an inquisitor and started being something else, something with no uniform and no procedure manual, something that had to decide things *himself*, which was the one form of labor he had spent his whole life avoiding.

He dressed in his civilian coat—the gray wool one, the one that made him look like a schoolmaster, which Ryn Callas's file said was the disguise least likely to be stopped at the east gates. Yes, he had pulled Ryn Callas's file too. Smuggler, minor charges, released twice on technicalities, last seen heading east with a caravan full of caged beasts and, presumably, one fugitive charm-seller. The beasts detail had snagged in Dain's mind like a burr. Caged animals and a woman who spoke to them. Even the Inquisition's own bestiary—suppressed reading, the sort of thing that got a junior inquisitor reassigned to a swamp—said beast-speakers couldn't abide cages.

So: east. Through the Weeping Gate before the seventh bell, when the guard rotation changed and the new watch hadn't learned each other's faces yet. Then the pilgrim road, then the marsh causeway, then wherever a caravan heavy with unhappy cargo would move slowest.

Vane expected his report by noon. He composed it carefully, in his best clerical hand: *Sigil-bearer deceased of fever, body consumed per protocol, miracle attributed to residual folk-practice, investigation closed.* A clean lie. A mercy-shaped lie. The same shape as the ones he'd been filing for thirty years, except those had been pebbles dropped in a pond and this one was a keystone pulled out of an arch.

By noon, Vane would know he'd lied. By sundown, riders. By tomorrow, his description nailed to every gate in Vaelharrow: *former Inquisitor Dain Ferrow. Consider armed and apostate.*

He looked at himself in the little mirror above the washbasin. A tired man with ink on his fingers and a burned book wrapped in oilcloth against his ribs. Not the face of a traitor. The face of a man who had finally run out of spoons.

"Well," he told the mirror, in the voice he used on frightened witnesses. "Emberlow burned because everyone stood still in the square and watched. Let's not make a tradition of it."

* * *

The Weeping Gate earned its name from the mineral springs that stained the stones with long rust-colored tears, and at the seventh bell it was crowded with carters and fishwives and one enormous woman arguing about a tax on onions, which covered Dain's exit beautifully. He was three steps past the gatehouse when he heard, behind him, the bells change their pattern—one, two, three, then silence, then a rapid ninth bell repeated. Alarm cadence. Someone had opened his desk.

He did not run. Running was for the guilty, and besides, running drew eyes, and Dain had spent thirty years learning to be furniture. He walked, at the pace of a schoolmaster late for nothing in particular, down the pilgrim road with the morning sun coming up gold behind him and the alarm bells fading into the ordinary noise of a city waking up.

At the first milestone he allowed himself one look back. Vaelharrow sat on its hill in the haze, beautiful and blind, wearing its Law like a shroud tailored to fit. Somewhere up there, Sarel Vane was reading a report that didn't exist and understanding, with that nail-row handwriting of a mind, exactly what it meant. She had burned a village over a child's dream. She would burn considerably more over a defected inquisitor carrying the First Hand's journal.

Good, thought Dain Ferrow, and was startled by his own thought, and then wasn't. Some fires were worth walking toward. He'd spent his whole life on the wrong side of that sentence.

The road bent east toward the marshes, and the marshes toward the river country, and somewhere out there a caravan crawled heavy with caged grief, and a woman with a moth on her door was running from the very thing she'd been chosen to free. Dain settled the oilcloth bundle more comfortably under his arm—Mara Nystrom's voice against his ribs, saying *forgive me, I was tired*—and lengthened his stride.

Behind him, far off, a hawk turned circles over the road. Watching him, he'd have sworn. Following.

Dain tipped his hat to it, because a man choosing truth over the only structure he had might as well start practicing politeness to birds, and walked on into the country that officially did not exist.
