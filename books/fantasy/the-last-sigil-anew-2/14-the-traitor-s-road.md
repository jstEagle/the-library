## 14. The Traitor's Road

Coral had learned, in her years of careful invisibility, that the world announced danger in small ways before it announced it in large ones. The pigeons went quiet first. Then the sparrows. Then the mice in the hedgerows stopped their rustling business of seeds and secrets and simply held still, as though the grass itself had asked them to.

By the time the horses came into view around the bend of the old post road, she already knew.

"Dain." She touched his sleeve. "Three riders. Behind us. The birds say they've been following since the ford."

Dain didn't break stride. He was a man who had spent thirty years making his panic walk at a reasonable pace. "Inquisitors?"

"Grey cloaks. Vane's colors."

"Ah," said Dain, in the tone of a man discovering a leak in his roof during a rainstorm he'd been pretending wasn't happening. "Well. That's inconvenient."

"That's *inconvenient*? Dain, they're going to—"

"Coral." He glanced down at her, and there was something almost gentle under the weariness. "If I called it what it is, I'd have to stop walking, and stopping is the one thing we cannot do. So it's inconvenient. Humor me."

The road ran along a ridge above the Ashwater, through country that had once been farmland and was now, by the grace of the Sigil Law, officially nothing at all. Unmapped land. The cartographers of Vaelharrow had erased these villages from the charts a century ago, and the erasure had been so thorough that even the ruins seemed embarrassed to exist — tumbled walls, a chapel with no roof, orchards gone feral and bitter. Nobody official could be here, because here did not exist. Which meant, Coral reflected, that anything could happen here, and no one would ever have to write it down.

The griffhawk circled overhead, a smudge against the grey sky. It had refused to leave her since the marshes, riding the wind above them like a patient, sharp-eyed conscience.

"They're gaining," Coral said. "The hawk says the lead rider has a crossbow."

"The hawk has excellent eyesight and terrible news delivery. Anything else?"

"He's angry. Not at us specifically. At everything. His boots hurt and someone in Vaelharrow owes him money."

"Wonderful. A man with grievances and a crossbow." Dain's hand drifted, casually, to the sword at his hip — a weapon he wore the way an honest clerk wears a pen, mostly for show. "How far to the ruined chapel?"

"Half a mile."

"And how fast can you run?"

Coral considered the question honestly. "I once outran a tax collector."

"A noble profession. Was he armed?"

"He had a ledger. It was very heavy."

"Then yes," Dain said, "you can run faster than they expect."

* * *

They didn't make half a mile.

The riders were better mounted than the birds had let on, or perhaps the birds had simply been sparing her feelings, and the sound of hooves closed behind them like a door slamming. Dain pulled her off the road into the bracken, and for one absurd moment they crouched together among the dead nettles like children hiding from a governess, and Coral might have laughed if her heart weren't hammering hard enough to crack ribs.

"Halt!" The voice was young. Younger than Dain's. "In the name of the Law!"

There were three of them. Coral counted through the branches: two dismounted, crossbows up, and one still ahorse — the leader, broad-shouldered, weathered, with the kind of face that had started out kind and been sanded down by duty until only the outline of kindness remained.

Dain stood up out of the bracken slowly, hands visible, and Coral's stomach dropped, because she knew that posture. That was the posture of surrender. Of a man deciding, in real time, which lie would save the most people.

"Ferrow?" The leader squinted, then his whole face changed. "Saints' teeth — *Ferrow*. What in the nine hells are you doing out here?"

"Tomas." Dain's voice was steady. Warm, even. "Hello, Tomas."

"You're supposed to be on the Vane hunt. There's word out — you missed muster two days ago, they think—" Tomas stopped. Coral watched him stop. Watched the arithmetic happen behind his eyes: Dain, missing muster, in unmarked country, with a woman, moving away from the hunt rather than toward it. Watched the sandpapered kindness try to hold its shape. "Oh," Tomas said quietly. "Oh, Dain. No."

"Tomas—"

"You *found* her, didn't you? The beast-speaker. And instead of bringing her in, you—" His hand moved toward the crossbow slung at his saddle, not quickly, not with intent, just the reflexive motion of a soldier whose world had tilted.

"Don't," Dain said.

And Coral understood, with the strange clarity that comes when everything is about to go wrong, that this was the hinge. That whatever happened in the next ten seconds would be written into both their lives permanently, in ink that did not wash out. She felt the griffhawk screaming silently overhead. She felt the rats in the hedgerow holding their breath. She felt, distantly and horribly, the pull of Vess beneath the river, patient as tide.

"Tomas," Dain said again, and took a step forward, empty-handed. "Put it down. You don't have to—"

"I swore an oath," Tomas said, and his voice cracked in the middle of it, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't. "We *both* did. On the same day, Dain. I remember your oath better than mine. You said you'd protect the innocent from the dark. Well, she *is* the dark, isn't she? Isn't she?"

"No," Dain said. "She's a woman who talks to pigeons. I've heard her do it. It's not terrifying. It's mostly gossip about bread."

Tomas laughed — one broken syllable of it, involuntary — and hated himself for laughing, and raised the crossbow anyway, because the oath was heavier than the laughter, because the Law was the only wall between the world and the flood, because if the wall had cracks then everything he had done, everything he had *witnessed*, all the burnings and the vanishings, became something other than protection—

He fired.

Later, Coral could never say who moved first. Perhaps Dain simply stepped into the line, into the space between bolt and girl, the way he had stepped between innocents and reports for thirty years, except this time there was no falsified ledger to hide behind, only his own body. Perhaps Tomas, at the last instant, flinched wide, the way good men flinch when they cannot quite commit to murder. Perhaps both.

The bolt took Dain high in the shoulder, spinning him half around, and the sound he made was not a scream but something smaller — a grunt of profound annoyance, as though the universe had finally sent him an invoice he couldn't argue down.

And then everything happened at once.

Coral screamed — not words, just sound, raw animal refusal — and every rat in the hedgerow answered her, boiling up out of the grass in a brown flood that hit the second rider's horse like a wave. The horse screamed and reared; the rider went down into the nettles. The third rider leveled his crossbow at her, and the griffhawk dropped out of the sky like a thrown knife, talons-first, raking his arm, and the bolt went wild into the sky.

But Tomas — Tomas remounted, or tried to, blood running from where the hawk had opened his cheek, and he had another bolt, and his hands were shaking and steady at the same time, the terrible steadiness of a man past choosing, and he looked at Dain kneeling in the dirt and said, wetly, "You made me do this. You know that? You *made* me—"

"I'm sorry," Dain said. And meant it. And drew his sword.

It was not a duel. Duels require two willing parties and some agreement about rules. It was three seconds of ugly, graceless violence in the bracken, and Coral saw almost none of it clearly — she saw Dain lurch upward, saw steel catch grey light, saw Tomas's eyes go wide with surprise, genuine surprise, as though some part of him had never believed his old brother-in-arms capable of the thing the Law demanded of them all.

Then Dain was standing over him, and Tomas was on the ground, and there was a spreading darkness across Tomas's chest, and the birds went silent everywhere, all at once, the whole countryside holding its breath.

"No," Coral whispered. "No, no, Dain—"

"He'd have reloaded." Dain's voice was flat. Empty. The voice of a room after the furniture has been taken out. "He was a good man. Twenty years. We buried his father together. I carried the—"

He stopped. He looked at his sword as though someone had handed it to him in error. Then he cleaned it, mechanically, with the terrible competence of habit, and sheathed it, and stood there swaying slightly, and Coral realized with a cold drop through her middle that the wound in his shoulder was bleeding freely and he had not noticed at all.

* * *

They got him to the ruined chapel because there was nowhere else. Coral tore strips from her petticoat — the good petticoat, the one she'd saved from Vaelharrow, the last piece of her old careful life worth anything — and packed the shoulder wound while Dain sat on a fallen pillar and stared at the roofless sky as though it owed him an explanation.

The griffhawk perched on the broken altar and watched them with one golden eye.

"It doesn't hurt," Dain said.

"Liar."

"It hurts," he conceded. "That's not what I meant. I mean—" He searched for it. "I killed him and it hasn't landed yet. I keep waiting for it to arrive and it won't come in the door. Is this what it's like? For everyone? All those men I reported on, all those women Vane burned — did they feel this afterward, this... waiting room?"

Coral tied off the bandage. Her hands were steady. She was surprised by that. "I don't know. I've never killed anyone."

"No," Dain said softly. "You've just been paying for everyone who has."

Outside, the light was failing. One of the other riders had fled — the one the rats had thrown — and that meant word would travel, which meant Vane would know, which meant everything was accelerating, and Coral sat down heavily on the chapel floor amid the dust of a god the Law had supposedly murdered, and felt the truth of it settle over her like ash.

Tomas was dead because of her.

A good man, twenty years sworn, a man who laughed one broken syllable at a joke about pigeons — dead in the nettles because she existed, because her grandmother had bound gods, because a fever-child had lived when the Ledger said it shouldn't.

"If I give myself up," she said quietly, "it stops. The hunting. Ryn gets free eventually, maybe. Dain, you could go back. Say you found me dead. Say I fell in the river. They'd believe—"

"Stop."

"—they'd believe it, you're respected, you've been doing it for *years*, one more falsified report, and everyone lives, and I go to Vane and—"

"*Coral.*"

"Vane burns people, Dain! I watched her burn Marta Ellingsen from my shop window and the whole street smelled like—" Her voice cracked. "I'm not worth a life. I'm not worth *his* life. I'm a charm-seller. I talk to rats. The world worked fine without me knowing anything."

Dain looked at her for a long moment. Then, wincing, he leaned forward, and said with the grave precision of a man delivering a report he could no longer falsify:

"Do you know what I did, before all this? Before the journal, before the Door, before any of it? I wrote lies for thirty years. Little ones. A name misspelled so the wrong house got searched. An age changed so a boy read as a man and got prison instead of the pyre. A death recorded that never happened. I told myself each lie was a mercy, and some of them were, and I want you to understand that I am not a brave man, Coral Nystrom. I am a coward who got very good at lying in small ways." He exhaled. "Killing Tomas wasn't courage. It was the coward's arithmetic finally coming due. But saving you — walking out of Vaelharrow with the journal in my coat — that was the first true thing I've done since I was nine years old. And I will not hand the first true thing I've ever done over to Sarel Vane so she can feed it to a prison under the river. Do you hear me?"

Coral's eyes stung. "That's the least romantic speech I've ever heard."

"I'm an inquisitor. Romance was struck from our oaths in the second revision." He shifted, winced again. "Besides, if you surrender, who's going to open this Door? Your grandmother's ghost? The pigeon parliament?"

"The pigeons do have opinions," Coral admitted thickly. "Mostly about bread."

"There. You see. Irreplaceable."

She laughed despite herself, a wet, cracked sound, and wiped her face with the back of her hand, and for a moment they just sat in the ruin, two liars and a traitor apiece, listening to the wind move through a chapel whose god had been legally deceased for a hundred years.

But the feeling didn't leave her. That was the horror of it — the cost didn't wash off. Tomas's face stayed behind her eyes, surprised and ordinary and human. Every mile toward the capital would be paid for in exactly this coin, she understood now, and Vess had never lied about that part: the land would be scoured. It was being scoured already, one good man at a time, starting with whoever stood nearest her.

She looked at Dain's bandaged shoulder, at the blood soaking through her good petticoat, and thought: *I could end this tonight. Walk out onto the road. Let them find me.*

The thought was warm. That was the seductive thing about surrender — it felt like mercy. Like putting down a weight.

The griffhawk dropped from the altar and landed in front of her. It didn't speak — it had never spoken, not really, not in words — but it fixed her with that golden eye and let out a single low croak, and the meaning came through anyway, the way meaning always had, back when she still believed listening was a curse:

*Not yet. Not like this. If you give up now, he died for nothing.*

"Oh," Coral whispered. "That's not fair."

The hawk croaked again, unrepentant.

From outside, from the direction of the road, came a sound that turned her blood to river water: horns. Not one horn — many, spread wide, moving in a pattern she recognized from the rats' warnings about hunts in the deep country. A sweep line. Closing.

Dain was already on his feet, grey-faced, one hand pressed to his shoulder. "How many?"

Coral listened — really listened, opening herself to every small voice in the dark for the first time since the marshes, since she'd begun fearing what listened back.

"Dain," she said. "It's not a patrol. It's not even Vane's riders." Her mouth had gone dry. "Something's coming up the valley. Something big. And the animals are all saying the same word."

"What word?"

"*Home.*"

Far to the north, beneath the ground, beneath the river, beneath a hundred years of law and silence, something vast turned over in its sleep — and smiled.
