## 15. Vane's Sermon

The candles in High Inquisitor Sarel Vane's private study had been lit for so long that they had forgotten they were ever meant to illuminate anything. Their wax pooled and hardened into strange pale shapes on the silver trays, like small sacrifices no one had bothered to collect. Vane did not look at them. She had stopped seeing the candles years ago, the way a lighthouse keeper stops seeing the sea.

She was looking at a map instead.

Not the official map—the one with Vaelharrow fat and proud at the center of a country drawn in confident ink, its borders neat as a ledger line. That map hung in every schoolroom and garrison hall, and Vane had personally approved its latest printing. No, this was the other map. The true one. It covered the entire eastern wall of her study, floor to ceiling, and it was stitched together from forty years of reports, confessions, and things she had learned never to write down.

On it, the country was not a country. It was a cage.

The borders were drawn in red—not ink, but thread, actual thread pulled taut and pinned at each corner where the first binding-stones still stood, buried beneath customs houses and border forts and one particularly unremarkable toll bridge over the River Senn. The threads converged inward, toward a point in the ruined capital marked with a small brass pin and, beneath it, in her own careful hand: *the Door.*

Forty years. Forty years of feeding it.

Vane poured herself a measure of plum brandy—she allowed herself exactly two fingers on nights when she had burned someone—and sat down across from the young man strapped into the chair opposite her desk. He had been there since sundown. His name was Pellam Ostrey, twenty-three years old, a candlemaker's son from the Weft District who had been overheard telling his sister that the fever leaving her body "felt like something kind had carried it out."

Kindness, in Vaelharrow, was a confession waiting for a scribe.

"You understand why you're here," Vane said.

Pellam's jaw was set in the way young men's jaws always were before the procedure. Bravado first. Then bargaining. Then, if she did her work properly, understanding—which was far more valuable than fear, because understanding could be *used*.

"The Law is very clear," she continued, warming to the liturgy of it. She had delivered this sermon so many times she could perform it while thinking about her accounts, which was precisely how sermons survived. "Divine intervention is theft. Every miracle is a withdrawal from a vault we sealed at terrible cost. When you invited something outside the walls to reach inside and touch your sister, you did not heal her. You loosened a hinge."

"She just got better," Pellam whispered. "That's all. She just got better."

"Yes," Vane said softly. "And the last time something 'just got better,' I watched a village of four hundred people grow flowers through the frozen ground until the binding-stone nearest them cracked down the middle like an egg." She let the silence do its work. It always did. "You are not being punished for kindness, child. You are being weighed for it."

She stood, crossed to the cabinet, and took down the instrument case. Inside, arranged in velvet like a surgeon's promises, were the tools of her particular ministry. She chose the smallest one—the tuning fork of cold iron, plain as a dinner bell.

What she did next, she did without hatred, without pleasure, and without a single wasted motion, and afterward she wrote the report herself, in her own hand, because she trusted no clerk with the truth: *Subject processed. Subject cooperative. Subject remembers nothing of value.* Both statements were accurate. The third statement—the one she did not write, the one she never wrote—was the only one that mattered:

*The vault accepted the deposit.*

Because that was the secret at the center of Sarel Vane's forty-year career, the secret she kept the way other women kept photographs of dead mothers: the gods were alive. They had never died. The Sigil Law had not destroyed them—it had *imprisoned* them, folded them into the land itself like poison pressed into bread, and the prison required feeding. Faith, devotion, answered prayers, small acts of grace—each one was a meal, and if the meals stopped, the prisoners would begin gnawing at the bars. And the bars were the borders. And the borders were everything.

So Vane fed them. Carefully. Rationed, measured, portioned out like grain in a famine year. A miracle here, absorbed. A blessing there, swallowed. The faithful were not enemies of the state; they were, in the most obscene sense imaginable, its supply chain. She burned a few publicly—someone had to, or the whole country would remember how to pray within a generation and the prison would burst its seams in a decade—but the rest, the quiet ones, the ones whose files she personally stamped *resolved*, went into the vault.

Controlled horror. That was the phrase she used with herself, alone, on nights when the brandy was not enough. Not mercy—she had excised that word decades ago, along with most of the others. Control. Because the alternative to controlled horror was uncontrolled apocalypse, and Vane had read the old accounts of what the gods had been before the Law. The land had been a choir with no conductor. Whole valleys had changed their religion overnight because a river god sulked. Children had been taken as vessels and returned as furniture. The world before the Law was not a golden age anyone remembered honestly; it was a storm, and the Law was the roof, and roofs require someone willing to climb up in the rain and hold the shingles down.

She was the one holding the shingles. She had been holding them for forty years, and her hands were tired, and lately—

Lately there was a girl in Vaelharrow selling luck-charms who had done something no deposit in three decades had managed. Something had come through her *loud*. Vane had felt it in the binding-threads themselves—a vibration in the pins on her true map, a hum like a struck bell, and one thread, the westernmost one, had sung for six full seconds.

Six seconds. In forty years, the longest any thread had sung before was half a heartbeat.

*Beacon,* the reports called her. Vane thought of her differently. Vane thought: *crack in the dam.*

And Dain Ferrow had found her file first. Of course he had. Vane had promoted him precisely because he was honest—honest men make the best locks—and now the lock was rattling in its frame, and she needed to decide whether to oil it or replace it.

She decided, pouring a second measure of brandy against all her own rules, to try oil first.

* * *

Dain Ferrow arrived at her study at the ninth bell, summoned by a note that contained neither explanation nor threat, only the time and the words *come alone*, which in Vane's service was either a courtesy or a diagnosis.

He looked terrible, which pleased her in a distant professional way. Guilt was easiest to work with when it was fresh. He stood at parade rest in front of her desk, and behind him the true map hung in all its threaded horror, and she watched him deliberately not turn around and look at it. Good. He suspected. Suspicion was a door, and doors could be opened from either side.

"You wanted me, High Inquisitor."

"I wanted tea," Vane said, "and conversation, and you happened to be the least tedious man available. Sit."

He sat. She poured tea—actual tea, not the garrison swill—and slid the cup across the desk, and noted that he waited until she drank first. Still trained. Still hers.

"Report," she said.

"Nothing further on the moth sigil. The shop is empty. Neighbors remember a woman who kept to herself, sold charms, fed pigeons. No family listed. The trail is—" He paused, and Vane heard the pause the way a farrier hears a limp.

"The trail is what, Inquisitor?"

"Cold," he said.

It was the first lie he had ever told her. Vane knew this the way she knew the weight of her own instruments: intimately, professionally, with a certain sadness. Dain Ferrow had filed thirty-one reports under her command and every figure in every one of them had been exact to the point of pedantry. She had once seen him correct a subordinate's count of confiscated prayer-beads by two. And now he sat in her study with tea cooling in front of him and said *cold* in a voice that meant *warm, warm, running.*

She should have had him arrested on the spot. She had the guards; she had the evidence—or rather, she would have had the evidence by morning, because she had already sent riders to shadow him, and the riders reported that he had visited a certain abandoned charm-shop twice more after officially closing the investigation, and had left the second time carrying something flat and rectangular wrapped in his coat.

The journal. Her people had missed it in the initial search. He had not.

Instead of arresting him, Vane smiled, and it cost her something real, because the smile was genuine, and genuine smiles had been rationed even harder than miracles these past forty years.

"Dain," she said, "do you know why I keep candles burning in here day and night?"

His eyes flicked up. "...No, High Inquisitor."

"Because when I was a girl, in the district before this one existed, my mother told me the dark outside was hungry. She was right, though not in the way she thought." Vane rose and walked to the true map, and after a long moment—she counted his breaths; four—Dain turned in his chair and looked at it too.

She watched him read it. The threads. The pins. The word in her own hand. She watched him arrive, breath by breath, at the shape of the truth, and she gave him time, because a man permitted to discover a thing will defend it far longer than a man who has merely been told.

"The gods are alive," Dain said finally. It was not a question. His voice came out scraped flat, like metal dragged over stone.

"They are imprisoned," Vane corrected. "Alive is a consequence. The Law didn't kill them—you can't kill a flood, boy, you can only build levees. And levees need maintenance." She touched the western thread, the one that had sung. "Something has been rattling the levee. A girl named Coral Nystrom. You know this. You've known it since the night you recognized the moth sigil, because I know you, Dain, I have known you since you were a half-starved novitiate who corrected my arithmetic, and you do not recognize a sigil by accident. You recognized it because you've seen it before. Because you *prayed* once, didn't you? As a boy. And something answered."

She had never spoken the suspicion aloud in thirty years. She watched it land on him like a dropped beam.

"And your village paid for it," she went on, quieter now, almost gentle, which was the most dangerous register she owned. "Yes. I signed those orders. I have signed eleven hundred such orders, Dain, and I remember every one, because forgetting is how men like the ones you'll serve otherwise sleep at night. So before you hate me, understand what I am offering you instead."

She returned to the desk, opened the drawer, and laid out three items between them like a fortune-teller laying cards.

A document: his absolution, drafted in advance, blank in the particulars but complete in substance. Full pardon. Restoration of rank. Expungement of the incident with the journal—he thought she didn't know; she let him keep thinking it, briefly.

A key: to the archive vault, the deep one, where the true histories slept. *Learn everything,* it offered silently. *Become the keeper of the levee yourself.*

And a single sheet of paper bearing a location, written in her hand: a waystation on the smugglers' road west of the Senn ford, where her riders' intelligence placed a certain caravan, a certain disgraced inquisitor's likely destination, and—if the girl had any sense at all, and everything suggested she had—a certain beacon.

"Bring her back," Vane said. "Unharmed, if possible. She is worth more intact than broken—her blood opens things, and I need it *opening the right things*. Do this, and you take my chair when I'm gone, and you spend your life maintaining the levee with clean hands and a clear ledger, and no more villages burn. Ever. Because they'll never need to again—you'll be the one deciding."

She leaned forward, and here she spent the last coin of her sincerity, because the trick of seduction is that it must contain at least one true thing or it curdles into mere bribery.

"I am not asking you to become a monster, Dain. I became one so that you wouldn't have to. That is the entire architecture of the Law—that some few of us stand in the doorway and take the cold so the rest can sleep warm. You think I don't hear the gods at night? I have fed them for forty years. I know their hunger better than their own priests did. The choice was never between the cage and freedom. The choice is between a cage we tend and a wilderness that eats children." She tapped the waystation paper once. "Or find her and run, and in ten years the threads sing themselves apart, and everything burns, and you can spend whatever's left of your life knowing your mercy lit the match."

Dain looked at the absolution. Then at the key. Then, longest of all, at the paper with the waystation on it—and Vane saw the moment he made his decision, saw it arrive in his shoulders before his face, the way weather arrives before the wind.

"High Inquisitor," he said, "I need until dawn."

"Take until noon," Vane said magnanimously. "I'm feeling generous tonight."

He saluted. He left. The door clicked shut behind him with the soft, expensive sound of a trap not yet sprung.

Vane sat alone with her candles and her map, and finished the brandy, and permitted herself the small, dry laugh she saved for occasions exactly like this one.

He would go. Of course he would go. She had known it before he stood up—had known it when she'd chosen him thirty years ago, honest men being the best locks and the worst liars. But he would go *carrying her paper*, believing the waystation was his own discovery, believing he had outmaneuvered her, and her riders would follow him straight to the beacon like hounds following a fox that thinks it's leading the hunt.

And when they brought her in—the girl, the blood, the key—Vane would feed the prison one last great deposit, enough to buy another century of quiet, and the country would go on not existing, safe and asleep, and the candles would burn on in the dark because the dark outside really was hungry.

She rose, blew out none of the candles—for the dark outside was patient, and she was tired, but she was not finished—and pinned a new marker on the western thread.

Outside her window, far to the west, the sky over the marsh road glowed faintly, and she chose to believe it was only weather.

It was not weather. Somewhere out there, something vast turned over in its sleep beneath a drowned chapel, and dreamed of a girl who talked to rats, and the thread above Vane's head began—very softly, like a hymn remembered wrong—to sing.
