## 16. The City Under the City

The smugglers who freed them called themselves the Ferryman's Kin, which sounded grand until Coral met them and discovered it meant nine people in oilskins who smelled of eel fat and secrets. Their leader was a broad woman named Maren Ostrow who had one glass eye that she claimed could see through lies, though Ryn swore it only saw through walls, and only on Tuesdays.

"You want into Old Vaelharrow," Maren said, studying them across a table in a flooded boathouse two miles from the ruined capital. The water lapped at the pilings like a patient dog waiting to be fed. "Nobody wants into Old Vaelharrow. Pilgrims sneak in and never come back. Treasure-hunters sneak in and never come back. One fellow snuck in because he owed money to the Ash Guild, and he came back." She paused. "Mad as a wet cat. Kept saying the stones were singing to him."

"They are," Coral said quietly.

Maren's good eye narrowed; her glass eye did something unsettling and independent. "So you're one of those."

"I'm the one they've been waiting for," Coral said, and hated how it sounded — like a line from a ballad sung by someone with better cheekbones than hers.

Ryn made a sound beside her that might have been a laugh or a cough. "She's insufferable now that she talks to things. You should have known her when she just muttered."

"I still mutter."

"Differently," Dain said. He hadn't spoken much since the marshes, since the road, since the man he'd killed wore a face Coral had seen him laugh with once, briefly, around a campfire. He spoke now the way a man walks on ice: carefully, testing each word before putting weight on it. "We need passage under the river wall. The old cisterns. There's a way in beneath the signing hall if half the maps I burned are half true."

"You *burned* maps?" Ryn said.

"A responsible amount of maps."

Maren looked at the three of them for a long moment — the disgraced inquisitor with his hollowed eyes, the smuggler with his bruised ribs and his stubborn jaw, and the charm-seller whose shadow, if you squinted, didn't quite sit still. Then she laughed, short and rough as gravel poured into a bucket.

"Fine. But not for coin. Coin's no good where we're going." She leaned forward. "You get in, you find what you're finding, you come back out, and you tell me what my grandmother sang about. She used to hum songs with words nobody knew. Words from before. My mother said never ask. I've been not-asking for forty years and it's eaten me alive."

Coral reached across the table and took Maren's hand, calloused and cold from the river water. "I'll tell you everything the stones tell me," she said. "That's a promise."

Above them, faint as a memory of a memory, something vast turned over beneath the earth, and every drop of water in the boathouse trembled at once.

* * *

They went in at dusk, down through the cisterns, wading thigh-deep through water so black it seemed less like water and more like the idea of water, the thing water dreamed of being before it committed to being wet.

Ryn went first with a hooded lamp, because he was a smuggler and moving through forbidden dark was the one language he'd never needed subtitles for. Dain went last, because he insisted, because he said his eyes were trained for ambush and everyone else's were trained for looking at nice things, which was, Coral thought, the closest thing to a joke she'd ever heard him make.

Coral walked in the middle, and she listened.

The city under the city did not speak the way beasts spoke. Beasts talked in pictures and hungers and the smell-thoughts of things. The stones here talked in *names*.

They came up out of the dark like bubbles rising through deep water: names written on doorways that no longer had doors, names carved above windows that no longer held light, names pressed into the mortar between bricks by hands that had believed — stupidly, gloriously, humanly — that their children would come back someday and read them.

*Halvard Bright, wheelwright.*

*The Ossler family. We keep the lamp lit.*

*Here lived Sefa, who sang.*

"Stop," Coral whispered, and stopped, one hand flat against the slick brick.

"What is it?" Dain's hand went to his knife.

"It's — there was a woman named Sefa." Coral's voice came out strange, doubled, as if the stone were speaking through her throat without asking permission. "She sang on the corner of Tanner's Row every market morning, badly, on purpose, because her sister was deaf and liked to watch her mouth move. Her sister's name was Ilse. They had a dog named Turnip. Turnip was a coward and afraid of geese."

Silence, except the drip of water.

"That's very specific," Ryn said gently.

"She left it in the wall." Coral pulled her hand back slowly, like touching a sleeping animal. "People wrote their small things into the mortar. Their whole lives, all the tiny true parts. Before the Law, this wasn't a ruin. It was just — a city. With a tanner's row. And a woman who sang badly for love."

Dain stood very still in the lamplight. "In the seminary they taught us Old Vaelharrow was a den of chaos. God-touched anarchy. That the Law saved civilization from drowning in miracles." His voice caught on the word *saved*. "Nobody mentioned Turnip."

"History never does," Coral said. "Turnips fall out of history first. They're too small to hold onto."

They walked on, and the names kept coming, and Coral learned to let them pass through her instead of catching each one, because otherwise she would have been weeping within a hundred paces and there was a great deal of city left.

* * *

An hour in, they surfaced into the great vault beneath the signing hall — a cathedral of a cellar, ribbed with arches older than any nation, and here the whispers became a choir.

It happened the moment Coral's boot touched the ancient floor.

The dead did not rise. Nothing so rude. Instead, the room simply *remembered itself*, and Coral was inside the remembering.

— lantern-light on white marble, hundreds of people in the hall above, and the smell of ink and fear, and somewhere a child crying because the world was ending and children always know first —

— a woman with Coral's own gray eyes standing at a table, quill in hand, her face grim as winter, whispering in a language that predated the Law: *bind them gently, bind them kindly, bind them as you would bind a wound, because a bound god is a wound in the world* —

— the moment after: silence so total it rang, the whole country holding its breath, and then the terrible ordinary sounds of people sweeping up, going home, telling themselves stories —

Coral came back to herself sitting on the floor. She had no memory of sitting. Ryn was crouched in front of her, both hands on her shoulders, saying her name in the voice people use when they've been saying it a while.

"There you are," he said. "Welcome back. You were gone maybe a minute. You cried the entire minute, for the record."

"I saw her." Coral wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "My grandmother. She signed the Law — no. She *bound* the gods, and the signing was just the paperwork afterward. She did it in this room. And she was crying, Ryn. She was crying while she did it, and she did it anyway, and I don't know yet whether that makes it better or worse."

"Worse," Dain said quietly. He was staring at the center of the floor, where the marble was scored with a sigil Coral recognized with a sick lurch — the moth, wings spread wide, worn shallow by centuries of feet walking over it without knowing. "Better would be if she'd done it cold. It's worse that she loved us enough to damn us. It means the Law was never evil. It means it was just *fear*, wearing armor."

"Fear wearing armor is how most monsters are made," Coral said. She got her feet under her and rose. "Come on. The Door's below this. The dead keep pointing."

And they did — the whispers had organized themselves somehow, a current in the noise, all the little names flowing toward the far wall, toward a seam in the marble shaped like nothing at all until you looked at it sideways and then it looked like a keyhole the size of a cathedral.

But halfway across the floor, Coral stopped again. Because one whisper was louder than the rest. Closer. Not centuries old. *Fresh*, in the way that grief is fresh.

A small name, pressed into the base of a pillar, scratched with something desperate — a nail, a hairpin:

*Ilya Voss, age nine. We hid here during the burning. If anyone finds this, my mother's name was Annet and she loved lilacs and I was brave. Tell her I was brave.*

Coral put her fingers over the scratches. Beneath them, fainter, in a different, shakier hand: *He was. He was brave.*

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, no."

"What?" Ryn asked.

"The burnings started immediately. The ones Vane does now — they're not new. They're tradition. This pillar—" She swallowed. "This pillar is from the first purge. A boy hid down here while they burned his mother upstairs, and someone — his father, maybe — came down after and finished the message."

Dain closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and he let them be, which Coral understood was perhaps the bravest thing she'd seen him do.

"Sarel Vane was there," he said. "At the first purges. She was young. She's told the story a hundred times in the training halls — how she stood in the ash and swore the country would be safe forever. We all thought it was inspiring." He laughed, and there was nothing funny anywhere near it. "She was standing in the ash of a nine-year-old boy's mother, swearing to make more ash, and we clapped."

The whispers swelled around them, gentle and terrible, a tide of every small true thing the Law had tried to sand away. Sefa who sang. Halvard the wheelwright. Ilya, brave. Annet, lilacs. On and on, thousands upon thousands of names, and Coral stood in the middle of it and finally understood what the grief in her chest had been building toward all these weeks.

It wasn't grief for the dead.

It was grief for herself — for the girl who'd grown up in a country amputated from its own past, selling luck-charms to people who couldn't remember why they felt lucky, starving her gift in the dark because the alternative was ash. She had never gotten to live in the world where Sefa sang on Tanner's Row. She had inherited a silence and been told it was peace.

"We're close," she said at last, her voice rough but steady. "The Door's behind that wall. And when we open it — all of this comes back. Every name. Every song. Even the burnings, even the ugly parts, because you don't get to pick and choose with a whole country."

"Maren wants her grandmother's song," Ryn murmured. "Turns out it's all our grandmothers' song."

Dain drew a long breath and let it out. "Then let's go commit some treason," he said, and even managed, at the very end of it, the ghost of a smile.

They crossed the floor together, three sets of footsteps over ten thousand names, and the dead whispered them forward like a congregation leaning toward the altar.

Behind the far wall, faint but unmistakable, something knocked.

Three times. Patient. Almost polite.

Like someone who had waited a very long time, and was nearly done waiting.

Vess knew they were there.
