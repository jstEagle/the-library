## 17. The Door of Anew

The dead led them down.

Coral had expected the city under the city to be silent—ruins, dust, the patient rot of a place no one had tended in a hundred years. But the old capital hummed. The stones whispered as her boots found each stair, and though her gift was a candle guttering low after weeks on the road, she caught fragments: *the harvest festival, the year of the twin comets, my daughter's name was Wren, we named the river before we named ourselves.*

"Stop doing that," Ryn muttered behind her.

"Doing what?"

"Stopping in the middle of stairs like you've been stabbed. You do it every forty steps. I'm keeping count."

"You're keeping count," Dain said, "because it's easier than admitting you're frightened."

"I am not—" Ryn paused. "Fine. I'm keeping count *and* I'm frightened. It's called multitasking."

Despite everything—the ache behind her eyes, Vess's pull dragging at her spine like a fishhook, the memory of her mother's face gone soft and blank where the oracle had taken it—Coral laughed. It came out small and cracked, but real.

The stair ended at a corridor that should not have existed beneath the signing hall. The Law had been signed upstairs; Coral knew this the way every child in Vaelharrow knew it, from schoolroom primers with their cheerful woodcuts of solemn men and one solemn woman putting ink to parchment while the gods "faded gently into memory." The primers did not mention what lay below.

The corridor's walls were covered in sigils—hundreds of them, thousands, carved deep and packed tight like scales on a serpent. And every single one had been struck through with a single line.

"They crossed them out," Dain breathed. His torchlight trembled. He was a big man, Dain Ferrow, built like a door itself, but he moved down that corridor like a boy in his grandmother's cellar. "All of them. Every name."

"Not crossed out," Coral said quietly. She touched one—a curling mark that made her fingertips buzz and her teeth ache. "*Bound.* There's a difference. A crossed-out word is forgotten. This is a word being held shut by force." She pulled her hand back. "By my grandmother, if Vess told me true."

"And you trust the word of a god who drowned a chapel to make a point?" Ryn asked.

"He hasn't lied to me yet."

"No. He's just been vague, ominous, and inconveniently enormous. Entirely different thing."

* * *

They walked for what felt like hours. The whispers thickened. Once, Coral heard a woman singing a lullaby in a language that predated the Sigil alphabet, and she found herself humming along before she realized she'd never learned it. The song lived in her blood, waiting. Her grandmother's blood. Their blood.

Dain stopped first.

He didn't announce it—he simply went rigid, torch held high, and Coral followed his gaze to the end of the corridor.

The Door of Anew was not a door.

It was a wound.

It hung in the ancient stone like a tear in wet paper, tall as three men and roughly the shape of a doorway only because someone, long ago, had tried to give it edges. Through it Coral could see—not darkness, not light, but *movement*, vast and slow, like colors seen underwater. The air around it bent. Her ears popped. The whispering of the dead went quiet all at once, the way birds go silent before a storm.

And across the whole of it, woven through it like roots through soil, ran the binding: threads of pale gold light, dense as a spiderweb spun by a spider the size of a cathedral. They pulsed faintly. Slowly. Like something breathing in its sleep.

"It's beautiful," Coral said, and meant it, and hated that she meant it.

"That's the binding?" Ryn whispered. "That's holding all of—that? Thread?"

"My grandmother's work." Coral stepped closer. The gold threads stirred at her approach, the way a sleeping dog stirs at a familiar footstep. "Keyed to our blood, Vess said. Which means—"

She lifted her hand toward it, and the threads *leaned*. All of them, the entire web, bending gently toward her palm like grass toward sun.

Ryn grabbed her wrist. "Don't. Don't you dare finish that sentence with your hand."

"I wasn't going to touch it."

"You were absolutely going to touch it. I have known you eleven years, Coral Nystrom. You have the self-preservation instincts of a moth at a lantern festival."

"She freed my griffhawk too," Coral said. "Moths are underrated."

But she lowered her hand. Because now that she stood this close, she could feel what the binding actually was—and it wasn't thread. It was *voice*. Thousands of voices, braided together, speaking one long unbroken word of holding. Her grandmother had sung the Door shut, and the song had never been allowed to end.

And Coral understood, with a sick drop in her stomach, why Vess needed *her* specifically. Not any descendant. Not blood alone.

A beast-speaker. Someone whose gift was listening. Someone who could hear where the seams of the song were.

Someone who could sing back.

"Oh," she said softly.

"Oh *what*," said Ryn. "I hate 'oh.' Nothing good has ever followed an 'oh.'"

* * *

Vess came up through the floor.

There was no better way to describe it. The stones did not break—they *remembered* being water, briefly, and the god rose through them like a reflection rising in a well. He was larger than he had been in the drowned chapel. Larger than he had been in her dreams. He filled the corridor from wall to wall without touching either, a shape like a river given shoulders and a face that was mostly suggestion—until his eyes found hers, and they were not suggestion at all. They were the exact grey-green of the Vael at dawn, and they were tired beyond anything Coral had ever seen in man or beast.

*Tiny speaker,* he said, and his voice came through the stone, through her bones, through the hundred-year silence of the dead. *You came.*

"You didn't leave me much choice."

*I always leave you choice. That is what makes it yours.* His gaze moved past her—to Dain, standing very still with his hand nowhere near his weapon, and to Ryn, who had placed himself squarely between Coral and the god with a smuggler's knife held in a grip that suggested he intended to use it on theology itself. *You bring defenders. Good. You will need them shortly.*

"Why?" Coral asked. But even as she said it, she felt it—through the soles of her boots, faint as far-off hoofbeats. "How many?"

*Enough,* Vess said. *She fed us her faithful for decades, little speaker, and every meal made her stronger in the ways that matter underground. Sarel Vane knows where you are now. The dead told me, and the dead tell her too—she has spent thirty years learning to listen to the prison.* His eyes returned to hers. *So we arrive at the hour I warned you of. I no longer ask, Coral Nystrom. I tell you: the Door must open. Tonight. By your hand, or broken by mine, and if I break it, the gods come through unshaped and starving, and your country drowns in the flood.*

"And if I open it?"

*Then they come through shaped. Gently. As dawn comes, not as a dam breaks. But the price—*

"I know the price." Her voice cracked on it. She'd known since the marshes, since the oracle took her mother's face and left her a stranger in her own grief. Everything she gave, she gave in installments. "My gift. My listening. All of it."

*Yes.*

"So that's it. Either way, I lose something. Either way, everyone I—" She stopped. Breathed. "You said once the land would be scoured."

*If I break it. If you open it—scoured is what happens anyway, over years. Rivers change course. Old names return. Some things burn because they were built on lies, and lies burn quickly.* He leaned closer, and his breath smelled of rain on hot stone. *I will not pretend it costs nothing, tiny speaker. I am old enough to know that honest gods are rarer than honest men, and nearly as dangerous.*

Behind her, Ryn spoke up, knife still drawn. "For the record, I object to all of this. For the record, I'd like to file a complaint with whatever department handles gods."

*Noted,* Vess said gravely, and Coral would have sworn the great grey eyes crinkled.

Then the far end of the corridor lit with torchlight.

* * *

They came in ranks.

Dain counted them out of long habit—two dozen inquisitors in grey, crossbows levelled, and at their center a woman in white and ash whom Coral had never seen but knew instantly, the way prey knows the hawk's shadow. Sarel Vane was smaller than Dain had described her. Neater. She looked like someone's tidy aunt, if someone's tidy aunt had spent thirty years feeding people to a hole in the world.

"Nystrom," Vane called, and her voice carried down the corridor without echo, as if the stones themselves were afraid to repeat it. "Step away from the Door. You've been lied to by a drowning thing and a traitor. Whatever it promised you, it cannot deliver. I can. Walk away, live quietly, and in a year no one will remember your name—which, believe me, is the kindest fate available to anyone in this room."

"She's lying," Dain said quietly. He'd moved to stand beside Ryn, and the two of them formed a wall in front of Coral that was, objectively, ridiculous—one defrocked inquisitor and one smuggler against two dozen crossbows. "Everything she says is a lie with good posture."

"I know," Coral said. "Thank you both. Really. This is very stupid and I appreciate it."

"It's what friends are for," Ryn said. "Also I want it noted I never agreed to fight a god's war. I agreed to move cargo."

"The cargo," Coral said, "was you."

"See, and *that* was fraud."

"Coral." Vane again, patient, terrible. "Last chance. The binding is keyed to your blood. Do you truly think your grandmother bound these things out of malice? She saw what they were. She saw the floods, the famines, the villages that prayed and burned for it. I have kept her peace for thirty years. Help me keep it thirty more, and your friends walk out of here."

And Coral looked at the Door—at the vast slow colors moving behind the wound, at the golden threads leaning toward her like flowers toward morning—and she thought about her grandmother, whom she'd never met, who had sung this song and never been allowed to stop. Who had bound the gods not out of hatred. Out of fear. Out of love for a country that then erased her name along with everything else.

Out of love, done badly, in terror, in the dark.

*Just like Vane,* Coral thought. *Just like me, selling charms and whispering to rats. We're all just loving things badly in the dark.*

But there was another way to love.

She stepped forward, past Dain's shoulder, past Ryn's knife-hand, into the open space between her friends and the wound in the world.

"Coral—" Dain started.

"It's all right," she said. "Both of you. Hold the corridor. However long it takes." She looked back at them once—Dain with his jaw set like a man finally home in the wrong uniform, Ryn with eleven years of guilt and tenderness fighting openly across his face—and she smiled. "Sing something, when I start. Anything. Badly. It helps."

"It will not help," Ryn said. "I cannot sing."

"Perfect."

She turned to the Door and raised both hands, and the golden threads rose to meet her like a tide coming home.

Behind her, the crossbows cocked as one.
