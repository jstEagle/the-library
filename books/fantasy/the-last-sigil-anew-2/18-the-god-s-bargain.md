## 18. The God's Bargain

The Door of Anew did not look like a door.

It looked like a wound that had learned patience—a seam in the air beneath the ruined signing hall, tall as three men and thin as a knife's edge, glowing the pale gold of old honey held up to a lamp. It hummed, too, but not with sound. Coral felt it in her teeth, in the soft hollows behind her ears, in the place behind her sternum where her gift had lived all her life like a second heartbeat.

Her grandmother's binding ran across it in lines of silver script, dense as frost on a winter window. And threaded through every line, unmistakable even now, was the shape of blood—Coral's blood, keyed into the lock before she'd ever been born.

"You're certain it's me," she said to Dain, who stood at the head of the stair they'd defended for the last hour, breathing hard, one hand pressed over a gash along his ribs. "Because I want you to be certain. I've had a very long week of people being certain about things on my behalf."

"It's in the journal," Dain said. "Your grandmother wrote it out twice. She underlined it."

"Underlining is how my family expresses panic," Coral said. "It doesn't make it true."

Ryn laughed from his post at the broken archway—a short, ragged laugh, the kind you spend when you're nearly out of everything else. He had a smuggler's cutlass in one hand and a smuggler's grin pasted over genuine fear in the other. "She's stalling," he told Dain.

"I'm *negotiating*," Coral said. "With a god. There's a difference. Stalling has no dignity attached."

Above them, far up through the shattered vault of the signing hall, the air itself had begun to press down like a thumb on a bruise. Vess was coming. Not walking—gods didn't walk anymore, not while they were still half-prisoned—but arriving, the way weather arrives, the way grief arrives. The drowned chapel's voice had been building in the stones around them for an hour: a low tide-sound, patient and enormous, saying her name in a language older than names.

*Co-ral. Co-ral. The key comes to the lock.*

And then, from the top of the stair, boots.

* * *

Sarel Vane came down into the under-hall the way a blade comes down—no hurry, no wasted motion, flanked by six inquisitors in white-and-grey whose faces were carefully blank in the way of people who have decided, in advance, not to think about what they're about to be ordered to do.

Dain moved first, stepping between her and Coral. Ryn shifted to cover the flank. The griffhawk—her griffhawk, the one she'd freed from a cage and who had chosen, absurdly and magnificently, to stay—dropped from its perch on the broken lintel and landed on the stairs between Vane and the Door, wings spread wide, screaming in the old tongue that Coral alone could hear:

*Behind me, small-speaker. Behind me.*

High Inquisitor Sarel Vane looked at the griffhawk the way one looks at a dog that has gotten onto the good furniture.

"Inquisitor Ferrow," she said, and her voice was mild, which was the most frightening thing about it. "You are standing beside a confirmed apostate, a smuggler, a bonded beast, and the largest act of unlicensed divine working in two centuries. Would you like to reconsider your position?"

"I considered it in the marshes," Dain said. "I'm comfortable here."

"Mm." Vane stepped past him—not shoved, not fought, simply *stepped*, because Dain let her, because some part of him still couldn't raise a hand against her, and Coral saw him hate himself for it in real time. She stopped ten feet from Coral and looked at the Door with something almost like tenderness.

"You have her eyes," Vane said. "Your grandmother's, I mean. Maren Nystrom. She sat where you're sitting, forty-one years ago, and she wept, and then she signed."

"She bound them," Coral said. "That's what you call it. Signing."

"I call it what it was. It was the bravest thing anyone has ever done in this country." Vane folded her hands. Behind her, the inquisitors fanned out with terrible professional calm. Above, the pressure of Vess thickened; dust sifted down from the vault like snow. "You don't know what was happening when the Law was written. You've heard the dead whisper their pretty version. Let me give you mine. The gods were at war with each other, child. Not with us—with each other. We were the board, and the dice, and sometimes both armies' lunch. Your grandmother ended that. She didn't destroy the gods—she couldn't—but she locked the war away and gave us forty years of villages that stayed standing. Forty years of children who grew up."

"And fed them," Coral said quietly. She had heard this from the bones of the marshes, from the whispers in the walls, and she watched it land. "You've been feeding the prison. Captured faithful. All those vanishings the Ledger calls 'relocations.'"

Something flickered across Vane's face—pride, actually, pride—and Coral felt ill.

"A controlled horror is better than an uncontrolled one," Vane said. "I have made that trade every day for thirty years so that you could stand there and judge me for it. So." She unfolded her hands. "Let us speak plainly, since you're going to open that Door regardless, and we both know it. Here is my offer."

* * *

"Open it," Vane said, "and I will kill everyone you love."

The words dropped into the room and lay there.

Coral blinked. "I'm sorry—I want to make sure I have the order of operations right. You'll kill everyone I love *after* I do the thing you can't stop me from doing anyway? That seems like bad incentive design."

"Before," Vane said. "During. As it suits me. The smuggler. Ferrow. The bird, if I can manage it. Everyone who has helped you, sheltered you, sold you bread in the last month—the Inquisition keeps excellent lists, Miss Nystrom, and your little night-market neighborhood has been on one since the moth appeared on your door." Her voice never rose. That was the trick of her, Coral thought. Most cruel people needed volume. Vane needed only certainty. "But if you walk away—if you help me re-seal this Door with your blood, as your grandmother intended, and come with me willingly—we will discuss terms. Your friends live. The country stays whole. The gods stay asleep. You will lose your freedom, perhaps your life eventually, but you will die having kept the peace."

"And the other option?" Coral asked, though she already knew, because Vess had shown her, drowning-chapel-cold, days ago. "If I refuse entirely?"

"Then the binding breaks on its own within days—it's been failing since the moment you set foot in this hall, your blood calling to the lock like calls to like—and the gods come through *shaped by nothing*. No key. No gentle hand. Unbound divinity pouring through a wound into a world that forgot how to hold it. They won't be evil. That's the part your kind never understands. They'll be *hungry*. They'll be vast. They'll remake the land the way flood remakes a valley, without malice, without noticing, and everyone you love dies anyway, only slower, and screaming, and so does everyone else." Vane tilted her head. "So you see, Miss Nystrom. I am not offering you a choice between good and evil. I am offering you a choice between grief and catastrophe. Choose grief. Grief ends. I've had thirty years to practice."

Ryn's knuckles were white on his cutlass. Dain hadn't moved. The griffhawk's feathers were flat with tension, its heart hammering loud enough that Coral could feel it against her own ribs like a drum played just for her.

And underneath it all, rising like water through floorboards, Vess's voice:

*Take her offer's shape and break it. Take the Door. Take the key. Open me, Coral-of-the-Nystroms, and I will remember your kindness forever.*

*Forever,* Coral thought, *is exactly the problem.*

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being cornered: sometimes the corner is the first honest room you've ever stood in.

Coral looked at the Door. She looked at Vane. She looked at Dain, bleeding and standing anyway; at Ryn, who had followed her into the ruin of the old capital on nothing but guilt and love and a truly irresponsible sense of adventure; at the griffhawk, who had known her for weeks and had already, once, chosen death-or-freedom over the safety of a cage, and had taught her the word for it.

Everyone wanted something from her. Vess wanted release. Vane wanted control. Even her grandmother, forty years dead, wanted obedience—had built a lock that required Coral's blood and then died without leaving instructions, which Coral had decided, somewhere on the traitor's road, was the single least considerate thing anyone in her family had ever done.

And every offer on the table had the same flaw. Every one of them treated the Door as a thing that happened *to* the world—to be opened or shut, loosed or sealed, by forces beyond her. Vane's bargain, Vess's hunger, the binding's decay: all of it assumed Coral was a hinge, not a hand.

"Well," Coral said aloud, mostly to herself, "that's just shoddy thinking, isn't it."

Vane frowned. "Miss Nystrom—"

"No, hang on, let me finish, I've been slow about this and people keep dying while I'm slow." Coral turned to face the Door fully, and the honey-gold light washed over her, and she felt her blood rise in her veins like a tide answering a moon. "You want me to choose between your two futures. Kill-them-all or drown-the-world. But a choice is only impossible when you accept someone else's menu." She glanced back at Vane, and—because she was her mother's daughter and her grandmother's granddaughter and because terror and humor lived in the same room in her chest—she smiled. "I sell charms, High Inquisitor. Do you know why my luck-charms work? Not the magic. The *selling*. Because a frightened person walks into my stall and I listen to them, really listen, until they remember they're a person and not just a pile of fears wearing a coat. Listening is the whole craft. Everything else is technique."

"I fail to see—"

"The gods aren't a flood," Coral said. "They're *lonely*. Forty years in the dark with nothing to talk to but a high inquisitor's atrocities—that's not a prison, that's solitary confinement, and of course they've gone ravenous, of course they'd come through screaming. Nobody ever listened to them. Not your Law. Not my grandmother's binding. Everybody locked the door and called it mercy." She raised her hand toward the silver-scripted seam. "But I listen. That's the entire useless, dangerous, starved little gift of mine. And I am going to open this Door *myself*—not for Vess, not against Vane, not on anyone's schedule but mine—slowly, gently, with every voice I've got, so that what comes through arrives *heard*. Fed on words instead of captives. Woken instead of ruptured."

"And your price?" Vess's voice rolled through the stone, vast and sudden close. "Key-of-mine. What will you pay?"

"Oh, I know the exchange rate in this country," Coral said. "Everything costs. The oracle took my mother's face. This will take more." She swallowed, and found, to her surprise, that she wasn't guessing. She knew. She'd known since the marshes, since the drowned chapel, since the moment the Door had hummed against her sternum like a second heart recognizing its twin. "My gift. All of it. Every borrowed voice—beast, bird, sickness, god. If I open it gently, I do it *as* the bridge, and bridges don't get to cross themselves. Fine. Good. It was always too much for one person anyway. It should belong to everyone or no one."

"Coral," Ryn said hoarsely. "There has to be another—"

"There isn't, and also there is, and the 'is' is me doing it properly instead of letting any of you do it badly. Hold the stairs."

"Miss Nystrom." Vane's voice cracked—for the first time, a hairline fracture ran through all that certainty. "If you open that Door, I have orders. I will carry them out. I will kill them."

"Yes," Coral said pleasantly, rolling up her sleeves like a woman preparing to knead bread. "You'll certainly try. Dain? Ryn? Bird?"

The griffhawk screamed. Dain drew his blade and turned to face the inquisitors, and there was no hesitation left in him at all. Ryn spat on his palms and gripped his cutlass.

"Vane," Dain said quietly, "you should know something. I read her grandmother's journal. All of it. Maren Nystrom didn't sign the Law because she believed in cages." He looked at Coral, and something old and grieving and hopeful moved through his tired face. "She signed it because she hoped someone would come after her brave enough to un-sign it. She wrote that. Last page. *To whoever the blood finds: don't repeat my caution. Finish my courage.*"

For one heartbeat—one long, ringing heartbeat—Sarel Vane, High Inquisitor of Vaelharrow, keeper of the Ledger, feeder of the prison, thirty-year architect of controlled horror, looked afraid.

Coral turned to the Door, laid both palms flat against the honey-gold seam, and spoke—in rat-tongue and pigeon-song, in the language of fevers and the grammar of rivers, in every voice she had ever starved or spent—beginning the unbinding.

The Door began, very slowly, like dawn deciding, to open.
