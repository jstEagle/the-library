## 20. After the Silence

Ryn Callas had smuggled a lot of strange cargo in his life. Gunpowder in flour sacks, a fugitive bishop in a pickle barrel, one memorable season hauling live thunderwyrms for a circus that turned out to be a front for tax fraud. He had never, until now, smuggled hope.

It didn't pack well.

Three days after the Door of Anew opened and the sky over the ruined capital filled with gods like dawn pouring through a cracked window, Ryn sat on an overturned crate outside the signing hall's rubble and watched people argue about it.

A farmer two streets down was weeping into his hands because his blighted orchard had flowered overnight—every tree at once, out of season, petals drifting pink across his boots. A few doors away, a woman was packing her family's cart with the grim efficiency of someone who had decided the world was ending and intended to be elsewhere when it finished. Down by the river, children were skipping stones and screaming with laughter because the stones, apparently, sometimes skipped themselves now.

Uneven. That was the word everyone kept using. Magic had come back to the land the way water comes back to a dried riverbed—finding old channels first, pooling where it pleased, refusing entirely in some places. The farmer's orchard bloomed; the baker's oven next door went cold every time he cursed near it. A shepherd reported his flock singing hymns at dawn, badly. Nobody could explain any of it. The priests of the new-old faith were already fighting over which god was responsible, which seemed to Ryn like three dogs arguing over who had caught a wagon.

He rubbed his eyes and went back inside.

* * *

Dain Ferrow lay on a pallet of blankets in what had once been a clerk's office, back before the capital was ruined, then abandoned, then forgotten, and finally blown halfway open by the return of everything the country had spent a century pretending not to be.

The inquisitor looked terrible. That was generous. Dain looked like a man who had lost an argument with a landslide, which was more or less what had happened when Vane's riders brought the passage down around him. His left arm was splinted and bound tight against his chest. Three of his ribs were cracked, according to the field-surgeon Ryn had bullied out of a refugee column with promises of payment he absolutely could not make. His face was the color of old candle wax, and there was a bandage wrapped around his head that made him look like a very serious egg.

But he was breathing. He was alive. And every hour or so, he woke up enough to ask the same question in the same cracked voice:

"Did you find her?"

And every time, Ryn gave him the same answer: "Working on it."

Which was true, in the sense that searching a collapsed cathedral-sized ruin for one specific woman was work. It just wasn't going well.

Ryn settled onto a stool beside the pallet and poured water from a clay jug into a cup, holding it to Dain's lips while the man drank with the grim patience of someone who hated being tended and knew better than to say so.

"You should sleep," Ryn said.

"I've been sleeping." Dain's voice was gravel dragged through honey. "For days. While you dig."

"Somebody's got to dig. You're not exactly shovel-shaped right now."

"Have you—" Dain stopped. Swallowed. Tried again, quieter. "The griffhawk came back. The bird told me she was seen. Near the west colonnade, the morning after."

"The bird *told* you," Ryn said.

"I'm learning to listen." There was no self-consciousness in it. Just the flat, stubborn certainty of a man who had traded his entire life's structure for the truth and intended to get his money's worth. "She said Coral was walking. Moving under her own power. She said—" Dain closed his eyes. "She said Coral was quiet. That she wasn't speaking to anything anymore. And that the bird couldn't tell if that was grief or something worse."

Ryn set down the cup. Outside, faintly, the singing sheep started up again. He chose to ignore them.

"Coral's fine," he said. "Stubborn as a barnacle. She'll turn up complaining about my cooking any hour now."

"You're a bad liar, Callas."

"Yeah, well." He stood, joints cracking. "You're a bad patient. We're all growing."

He left Dain propped against the blankets, staring at the ceiling with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like, and went back out into the light.

* * *

The rubble of the signing hall was a country unto itself.

Ryn climbed down into it the way he'd learned to climb everything—hands first, weight low, no wasted motion. The great chamber where the Law had been signed a hundred years ago had come apart when the Door opened, stone folding like wet paper as the gods poured through and out and up. Now it was a canyon of broken marble and shattered oak, shafts of afternoon light falling between slabs, dust hanging gold in the stillness.

He worked methodically. He'd been a smuggler too long not to. Grid sections, marked with chalk. Tunnels checked and cleared. He'd pulled eleven survivors out already—a pair of clerks, a family of five, two inquisitors who had thrown down their weapons and wept when the sky opened, and an ancient archivist who had refused rescue until he finished saving a single book, and whom Ryn had therefore rescued twice: once from the rubble and once from himself.

No Coral.

He knew she'd made it. The griffhawk had seen her walking, Dain said. And there was other proof, proof Ryn carried with him everywhere now, folded in oilcloth against his heart like the contraband it technically wasn't.

A scrap of paper. Found pinned under a stone near the Door itself, weighted with a chip of marble. Her handwriting, cramped and familiar, written in the dark or near it:

*Ryn — I'm alive. I can't hear anything anymore. Not the rats, not the birds, not the wind. Just quiet. Don't dig yourself into a grave looking for me. — C.*

He'd read it maybe four hundred times. He'd read it again now, sitting on a slab of what had probably been a judge's bench, in the gold light and the silence.

*I can't hear anything anymore.*

He understood, distantly, what that cost. He'd watched her talk to beasts like they were old friends. He'd watched a dying child's fever break because she spoke to the sickness itself. Her gift had been her whole life—her hiding place, her burden, her inheritance, and in the end, her sacrifice. She'd burned it away to open the Door gently, to let the gods come home without scorching the earth, and what was left was a woman walking around in a silence so total it must have been like deafness of the soul.

He pocketed the note and kept digging.

* * *

He found her on the fifth day, and it was nothing like he'd imagined, and exactly like her.

Not trapped. Not wounded. Not lying heroically unconscious beneath a heroic slab. She was sitting cross-legged on a flat piece of fallen roof, deep in the western colonnade, sharing a strip of dried meat with the griffhawk—who perched on a broken pillar beside her like a gargoyle with opinions—and staring out at the light with an expression Ryn recognized from a decade ago, from a dock in Vaelharrow, the night she'd told him she couldn't run with him and wouldn't say why.

The expression of a woman waiting for something she isn't sure is coming.

"Coral," he said.

Her head snapped around. And then her whole face changed—not the way faces usually change, all at once, but in stages, like a lock opening tumbler by tumbler. Shock. Disbelief. And then something that broke his heart even as it lifted it, because it was so plainly, nakedly glad.

"You found me," she said. Her voice was rough from disuse. "I figured—I left the note, but I couldn't hear you digging. I couldn't hear anything. I thought maybe you'd think I was dead. I thought maybe I should let you."

"Well, that's stupid," Ryn said, because he had never once in his life led with the tender thing when an insult was available. "I don't leave good cargo behind."

Something flickered across her face—the ghost of the old reflex, the half-second pause where she used to listen to him, really listen, past the words and into whatever the animals around him were saying about his heartbeat. He saw her reach for it. He saw her find nothing there. He saw her flinch.

"It's gone," she said simply. "All of it. The rats, the birds, the—everything. It's just quiet. Like a room after everyone's gone home." She laughed, and it wasn't a funny laugh. "Do you know I haven't heard myself think this clearly in fifteen years? Do you know how loud it was? All the time? Every creature within a mile, chattering, hurting, wanting? I used to dream in a hundred voices. Now there's just—" She gestured at her own head. "Me. Only me. It turns out there's less of me than I thought."

"There's plenty of you," Ryn said. "Always has been. You're the loudest person I know, and none of it ever came from the rats."

She looked at him then, really looked, and her eyes were wet. "You would say something kind at a time like this. You have no idea how disorienting that is. I used to be able to check your heart rate."

"My heart rate is fine."

"Your heart rate is *never* fine. Your heart rate has been a scandal since the day I met you." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike, and Ryn felt his chest ache with how much he'd missed her. "The bird stayed," she said, nodding at the griffhawk. "She won't leave. She keeps making sounds at me. Little chirps. Looking at me like I'm supposed to understand." Her voice cracked, just slightly. "I don't understand. For the first time in my life, I look at an animal and see nothing but an animal."

The griffhawk made a low sound in her throat and sidled closer along the pillar, close enough that her wing brushed Coral's shoulder.

"She did the same thing with me," Ryn offered. "Wouldn't shut up. Followed me around the camp for two days. I finally asked Dain what she wanted and he said, 'To be listened to,' and I said, 'Well, that's useless, I don't speak bird,' and Dain said, 'Neither do I, properly, but you try anyway.' So I tried." He shrugged. "Turns out most of listening is just... staying. Being there. Letting them know they're not talking into a hole."

Coral was quiet a moment. Then, slowly, she reached up and rested her hand on the griffhawk's neck, feeling the warmth of her, the prickle of feathers, the pulse beneath. The bird leaned into the touch and made that sound again, softer.

"No magic," Coral murmured. "Just this."

"Just this," Ryn agreed.

* * *

They walked out of the rubble together in the long gold light of evening, Coral leaning on his arm more than she strictly needed to—her legs were fine, but he suspected she liked the anchor, and he was not going to complain about being useful. The griffhawk glided above them in slow circles, keeping pace, keeping watch.

At the edge of the ruin, the world was waiting.

Refugees and returners and the merely curious had gathered at the perimeter of the old capital, drawn by rumor the way moths are drawn to—well. To moths, in Coral's case, historically. They parted to let the three of them pass, and Ryn watched the crowd watch Coral, and saw the exact moment recognition spread.

"That's her," somebody whispered. "That's the moth-witch. She opened it."

"She freed the gods," said someone else, hushed.

"They say she talked to God himself—"

Coral's grip on Ryn's arm tightened. "They're looking at me," she said quietly. "Why are they all looking at me?"

"Because you saved the world, probably."

"I didn't save the world. I opened a door. Anyone could have—"

"Anyone could have," Ryn agreed, "but they didn't, because anyone couldn't have. You did it. Get used to it."

"I spent my whole life trying to be invisible," she said, and there was real anguish in it. "Fifteen years of charms and whispers and pretending. And now—" She stopped. Closed her eyes. Opened them. "Now I'm the only thing anybody talks about, and I can't even hide behind the pigeons anymore. They're just pigeons now. They don't know me. I don't know them."

"Pigeons are overrated," Ryn said. "Talkative. Terrible gossip. Always going on about bread."

And she laughed—really laughed, sudden and startled out of her, the sound bouncing off the broken walls of the dead capital, and heads turned toward it, and Ryn thought: there. There she is. No gift required. Never was.

An old woman detached herself from the crowd as they passed—small, bent, dressed in the faded colors of a faith that had been illegal for a century. She pressed something into Coral's hand and shuffled away before Coral could speak. Coral opened her fingers.

A charm. Simple twine and river-stone, the kind sold in ten thousand market stalls, the kind Coral herself had sold for years—harmless, worthless, ordinary.

Except now, Ryn supposed, maybe not so harmless. Maybe nothing was ordinary anymore.

"What do I do with this?" Coral asked, bewildered, holding it up.

"Dunno," Ryn said. "Sell it?"

* * *

Dain cried when he saw her.

He tried very hard not to. He was, Ryn was coming to learn, constitutionally opposed to crying, and fought it the way other men fought bandits—with gritted teeth and visible strain and eventual defeat. When Coral knelt beside his pallet and took his unbroken hand, his jaw worked, his eyes shone, and he managed roughly four seconds of dignity before turning his face to the wall and letting out a long, shaking breath that fooled nobody.

"You held the line," Coral said softly. "Vane's riders. You held it so we could finish."

"Ryn held it. I fell down mostly."

"He does that," Ryn said from the doorway. "Very dramatic faller. I've seen him trip over a chair and call it tactics."

Dain laughed, and immediately regretted it, judging by the way he clutched his ribs. But he laughed. Then he looked at Coral with those grave, tired eyes, and said, "Your grandmother's journal. The part I read. The binding was keyed to blood, but the unbinding—it needed a willing voice. Every voice she ever borrowed. That was always you, Coral. From the beginning. Before you knew it was you."

"I know," Coral said. "Vess told me. In its way."

"And your gift—"

"Gone. Spent. All of it." She smiled, small and rueful. "I'm just a person now, Dain. An ordinary woman in an extraordinary country. I can't hear the rats. I can't bless a fever-child. I can't do anything but talk and listen and stay."

Dain considered this for a long moment. Then, with the gravity of a man pronouncing doctrine: "In thirty years of service, I met perhaps six people who could actually do that last part. Stay. Listen. It was rarer than magic. It's rarer now." His voice dropped. "The land is waking up unevenly. Some places are drowning in it. Some places have nothing. And there are thousands of people like you were—people with small gifts, small voices, hiding in plain sight, terrified. The Law is gone, but the fear isn't. Fear takes longer than laws to die."

Coral was silent. Ryn watched her look down at her hands—at the charm from the old woman, still clutched there—and Ryn saw something begin to turn over behind her eyes, some slow machinery engaging, some idea taking its first breath.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight, she just sat by Dain's pallet and held his hand while he slept, and Ryn sat in the doorway and watched the stars come out over a country with its name coming back, and the griffhawk tucked herself against Coral's side like she'd been doing it all her life.

Later, when Dain was sleeping easy and the camp had gone quiet, Coral found Ryn outside. She stood next to him in the dark, shoulder to shoulder, looking up.

"I keep reaching for them," she said. "The voices. Out of habit. Every morning I wake up and reach, and there's nothing, and for a second I forget why, and then I remember." She was quiet a moment. "It doesn't hurt as much as it did. Is that terrible? That it's getting easier?"

"No," Ryn said. "That's just healing. Same as him." He nodded back toward the tent. "Same as everybody. Hurts like hell, then hurts less, then one day you realize you're still here."

She leaned her head against his shoulder. He did not move, on the theory that sudden movements might spook her, the way they'd spook everything else in his life worth keeping.

"Ryn," she said, after a while.

"Hm?"

"That day on the dock. Ten years ago. When you asked me to run with you, and I said no." She paused. "I want you to know it wasn't because I didn't love you. It was because I did. I thought if I stayed invisible, I'd keep you safe. I thought starving the gift meant starving the danger. I was wrong about almost everything, but I wasn't wrong about that one thing. I loved you then."

Ryn looked up at the stars, at the strange new constellations people were already arguing about, at the smoke rising thin and clean from cookfires all across the ruined city where people were rebuilding, or trying to, or at least boiling water hopefully.

"And now?" he asked.

"Now," Coral said, "I'm just a woman. With no powers. No gift. Nothing special about me at all except, apparently, a reputation." She tilted her head to look up at him, and in the firelight her eyes were bright and tired and more honest than he'd ever seen them. "And I find that I'm still here. Still standing next to you. Still choosing it. Every day, with my whole ordinary human heart."

Ryn Callas had smuggled gunpowder, bishops, and thunderwyrms. He had carried contraband across borders that officially did not exist, lied to inquisitors, out-bargained demons of commerce both literal and figurative, and once talked his way past a toll-keeper using nothing but a chicken and excellent timing.

But he had never, in all his life, been given anything as precious as this: a woman with no magic left, choosing him anyway. Choosing him *first*. Not because he could help her carry her secret. Because he was there, and she was there, and they were both still breathing.

"Good," he said, because he was Ryn Callas, and eloquence had never been among his talents. "Because I'm not giving you back. I've grown attached. Like a barnacle."

"Like a barnacle," she agreed solemnly.

They stood there together in the dark, in the silence that was no longer empty, and above them the griffhawk stirred in her sleep and made a small contented sound, and somewhere far off, a shepherd's flock began to sing.

Badly. But singing.

Ryn smiled into the dark, and thought: *we're going to be all right.*
