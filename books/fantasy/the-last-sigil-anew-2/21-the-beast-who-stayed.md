## 21. The Beast Who Stayed

The griffhawk came back on the first cold morning of the year, when Vaelharrow's rooftops wore frost like old lace and the city smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone.

Coral was sweeping her step — her *own* step now, in a narrow house on Tallowmere Row that she'd bought with money Ryn swore up and down he'd earned honestly, and which she had chosen not to examine too closely — when the shadow crossed the cobbles.

She knew that shadow. You did not spend three years sharing your mind with something and forget the shape of its wings.

The griffhawk came down out of the grey morning like a thrown knife, checked itself two feet above her head — showing off, always showing off — and landed on the fence rail with a clatter of talons and a rattle of feathers. It was smaller than she remembered. Or perhaps everything else had simply been so much larger for so long: gods, doors, burning skies. A bird was only a bird-sized thing again.

It fixed her with one amber eye. Its beak was chipped at the tip. There was a scar along its breast where wire had bitten through feather, a thin pale line she remembered tracing in the dark of a smuggler's wagon while it begged her, in the wordless way of desperate things, for *anything other than this*.

"Hello," Coral said.

The griffhawk said nothing.

That was the whole of it, really. That was the wound and the wonder of it both. Once, she could have reached into that fierce bright mind the way you'd dip your hand into warm water. Once, it would have poured its whole heart into hers without shame or translation — hunger, wind, the memory of thermals over mountains, the particular insult of being called a cargo.

Now there was just a bird on a fence, looking at her, and silence where the conversation used to live.

"You don't have to stay," she said, because it seemed important to say it. "I'm not — I can't offer you anything anymore. No bargains. No voices. Just me."

The griffhawk tilted its head, considered this, and then did the most griffhawk thing imaginable: it hopped down, stalked past her into the house as though it owned the deed, knocked a teacup off the shelf with one wing, and settled on the back of her chair like a gargoyle that paid rent in attitude.

Coral stood in her doorway with her broom and laughed until she cried, which she suspected was less about the teacup than she pretended.

* * *

She named him Ash, because of the scar and because naming things after their worst day felt, to Coral, like a kind of honesty.

He stayed. That was the strange part, the part the neighbors couldn't stop remarking on. Griffhawks weren't pets; everyone knew that. Griffhawks were weather with opinions. They tolerated humans the way cats tolerated rain. Yet Ash perched on her fence through the autumn and into winter, and rode her shoulder to market, and once escorted off the premises a debt collector who had been rude to Mrs. Halloway next door, in an operation involving one scream, one low pass, and no actual contact whatsoever — which Coral felt showed real restraint and growth.

"He stays because you fed him," said Tam, the baker's boy, who was eleven and thought he knew everything.

"I haven't fed him once."

"Then he stays because you're a witch."

"That word's retired," Coral said. "We're all still deciding what replaces it."

"Then he stays because you're magic."

Coral looked at the boy — flour on his nose, ambition in his eyes, the particular hunger of children who'd grown up in a country that had just remembered it had a history — and she didn't laugh at him, though it would have been easy. She knelt instead, so their eyes were level, and pointed at Ash on the fence rail.

"What do you see?" she asked.

"A griffhawk."

"What *else*?"

Tam frowned. Squinted. The griffhawk, sensing an audience, puffed up magnificently.

"He's... standing more on his left foot? His right foot looks sore."

"There," Coral said. "You saw it. Most people look at a griffhawk and see a monster or a marvel, and they stop looking. You kept going. That's the whole trick, Tam. That's all I ever had."

"But you *talked* to them," Tam said. "Everyone says so. You talked to beasts and birds and even to the god under the river, and you opened the Door, and—"

"And then it burned out of me like a candle down to the socket," Coral said, gently, because she'd learned that children could carry truth better than most adults, if you handed it to them steadily. "And do you know what I found out? The talking was never the gift. The gift was sitting very still, at night, in a market full of people who wanted charms, and paying attention. To rats. To pigeons. To what frightened them and what they loved and where they went when they were hurt." She smiled. "Anyone can learn that. Almost nobody bothers."

Tam looked at her for a long moment, in the way of the young, who are always deciding whether adults are worth the effort of believing.

"Could you teach me?" he asked.

And that — though neither of them knew it yet, standing on a frosted step with a lame griffhawk watching like a disapproving uncle — was how the school began.

* * *

It wasn't a school at first. It was four children and a widow in her back garden on Tallowmere Row, on afternoons when the light went gold and long, learning to sit still.

That was lesson one, and it was harder than it sounded. Sitting still was a dying art in Vaelharrow. The city had spent forty years teaching its people to keep moving, keep smiling, keep their eyes down and their ears shut, because noticing things got your neighbors arrested and noticing things about *yourself* got you noticed by worse. The Sigil Law was gone — torn up, officially repudiated, its signatories' names read aloud in the square every year on Remembrance Day so no one could pretend they hadn't known — but laws lived longer in muscles than in ledgers.

So Coral taught them to unclench.

"Breathe," she'd say, walking the rows of her garden benches. "Breathe out slower than you breathe in. Now listen. Not for anything. Just listen."

Mrs. Halloway, who had joined "to keep an eye on what you're filling these children's heads with" and had never once left, always heard the church bells first.

Old Piet, the cooper, who came because his wife had died in the purges and he didn't know what else to do with his hands, could hear a beetle crossing a plank from ten feet away and claimed it was nothing, anyone could do it, while secretly glowing like a lantern.

Little Sefi, who was seven and had been born the year the sky burned, could hear *everything*, and cried the first week from the sheer weight of it, until Coral sat with her under the plum tree and taught her the second lesson, the one nobody had ever taught Coral because she'd had to starve her gift alone:

"You don't have to hold it all," Coral told her, holding the girl's small hands in her own. "Listening isn't carrying. It's letting things pass through you. Like a doorway, not a jar. A doorway doesn't get heavy."

"Did you carry too much?" Sefi asked.

"For years," Coral said. "Don't be like me. Be better."

And Sefi nodded solemnly, seven years old and already wiser than half the Inquisition, and went back to listening to the worms.

The magic, when it came — and it did come, unevenly, strangely, the way water finds old channels — Coral refused to teach. Not because she feared it. Because it wasn't hers to give. Some of her students developed odd little talents, the way people do now all over the country since the Door: Tam could calm nervous horses; Mrs. Halloway's roses bloomed out of spite and season; Piet swore his barrels never leaked if he hummed to them, and Coral believed him, because why wouldn't the wood want to hold together for a man who hummed?

But she never taught anyone to *speak*. She taught them to hear, and let speech happen or not happen on its own terms.

"They're different things," she explained to Ryn, one evening, when he'd come round ostensibly to fix her leaking roof and actually to eat her stew and complain about the new harbor tariffs. "Speaking to a beast is a transaction. Hearing is a relationship. The first gets you things. The second makes you someone."

"Mm," said Ryn Callas, who had once moved a cargo of caged beasts across half a country and now, quietly, without advertisement, refused any job that involved a cage. He'd rebuilt his whole operation around it — livestock walked free alongside the wagons now, or not at all, and it cost him a fortune, and he complained about it constantly, and Coral had never seen him prouder of anything.

"You could come to a lesson sometime," she said. "Sit still. Listen."

"I'm a busy man."

"You ate half my bread."

"I'm a growing man."

"Ryn. You're thirty-four."

He grinned at her across the table — that grin, the old one, the one she'd betrayed and he'd forgiven and they'd both stopped pretending was a small thing — and stole the last of the bread anyway.

Ash, from his perch above the stove, made a sound that needed no translation at all.

* * *

Winter deepened. The school grew, which was its own kind of miracle and its own kind of grief.

Because here was the thing nobody warned her about: being useful to many people was not the same as being special to anyone, and Coral missed being special the way you miss a limb in bad weather. Not constantly. But some mornings she'd wake and reach instinctively for the great listening — that ocean she'd swum in all her life without knowing it was an ocean — and find only air, ordinary air, the same air everybody breathed. And for a moment, before she was fully awake, she'd feel the loss like a missing stair.

She had traded her gift to open the Door. She'd do it again. She'd told herself so a hundred times, and meant it, mostly. The gods were loose and gentle now, moving through the world like migrating weather; the land was remembering itself, rivers running to old beds, forests creeping back toward names they'd lost. Every day brought news of some small resurrection — a well in Eastmarch running sweet again, a child in the Weald born with eyes like her dead grandmother's, wolves sighted on the old ridgelines for the first time in forty years.

All of it had passed through her hands. None of it was hers anymore.

Some days that felt like sacrifice. Some days it felt like being robbed. Most days it felt like both, wearing each other's coats.

On the worst of those days — a grey Tuesday in deep winter, when a merchant's daughter had come to the school hoping to be taught miracles and left disappointed, saying loudly enough to be heard, *"I thought she was supposed to be powerful"* — Coral stood alone in her garden after class and let herself, for exactly sixty seconds, be bitter.

Then Ash dropped out of the bare plum tree onto her shoulder, nearly toppling her, and bit — gently, deliberately — the top of her ear.

"Ow! *Ow.* What was that for?"

No answer, obviously. Just the amber eye, close enough to count the flecks in it, and the warmth of a body that had flown across a burning country to find her, and stayed, and stayed, and stayed.

"All right," Coral said, rubbing her ear. "Point taken."

Because that was the other lesson, the one she was slowest to learn: she had spent her whole life believing her gift was what made her worth keeping company with — the hidden treasure, the secret self, the interesting thing underneath the boring charm-seller. Strip that away and surely there was nothing left but a woman who sold luck and swept her step.

Except the griffhawk hadn't come for the gift. He'd come for her. He'd known her mind when she had it and chose her after, in the silence, when all she had to offer was stew and a chair by the stove and the habit of paying attention. Mrs. Halloway hadn't guarded the garden gate out of reverence for power. Tam hadn't looked closer because she was magical. Even Ryn —

Even Ryn, who had known her longest, who had loved her plain and loved her marked and loved her hollowed-out, had never once, in all the years, cared about the gift. He'd cared whether she was *kind*, and whether she was honest, and lately whether she ate enough.

The magic had been the least true thing about her. The listening — the plain, teachable, human listening — was the truest.

She went inside and wrote that down, because she'd started keeping a journal, and because some lessons you have to write twice before they stick.

* * *

Spring came early to Vaelharrow that year, the way springs did now, arriving like a guest who no longer had to sneak in through the window.

On the first properly warm afternoon, Coral held class outdoors, twenty students on her benches and spilling onto the grass, from Tam's age to Piet's, the widow Halloway presiding from her usual spot with the best view. The lesson was birdsong — not decoding it, not translating it, just hearing its grammar, its moods, the difference between a warning and a love letter.

"Now," Coral said, "close your eyes. Someone tell me what the blackthorn hedge is saying today."

"Panic!" said Sefi immediately. "There's a cat!"

"There's *always* a cat," said Tam.

"Not that cat," Sefi said. "*That* cat."

A beat of silence. Then the hedge erupted, six sparrows going up like startled fireworks, and the entire class turned to stare at seven-year-old Sefi, who opened one eye and looked insufferably pleased with herself.

Coral laughed — and then, mid-laugh, felt it.

Not heard it. She would never hear anything like that again. But *felt* it, the way you feel thunder through the soles of your feet before the sound arrives, the way animals go quiet a moment before the storm crosses the valley. A pressure in the air. A vast attention passing overhead, unhurried, enormous, old.

Every creature in the garden went still at once — the sparrows, the hedge, Ash on the fence rail, even the cat, frozen mid-prowl in a neighbor's yard. Twenty faces turned upward.

High above Tallowmere Row, against the washed blue of the spring sky, something immense was crossing the city. Wings like weather fronts. A shape that the eye kept trying to file as *cloud* or *flock* or *storm-light* and failing, because it was none of those things, because it was a god going about its ancient business in a world that had finally made room for it again.

Vess. Passing over, the way the drowned chapel had once promised the old gods would pass over their people now — not descending, not demanding, just *moving through*, the way rivers move through countries they love.

The whole garden held its breath. Somewhere in the crowd, Piet took off his hat. Mrs. Halloway, unbelievably, was crying.

And Coral stood among her students with her empty hands and her full heart and reached up toward it with everything she had left, which was attention, which had always been everything she had —

and for one heartbeat — one impossible, unexplained, unrepeatable heartbeat —

she almost heard it.

Almost. A syllable without a sound. A warmth without words. Something vast and patient and *fond*, brushing the edge of her like a wing-tip grazing water, gone before it could become meaning.

Then the sky was just sky, and the sparrows came back down, and Sefi tugged her sleeve and asked, "What was that?" and Coral realized she was crying too and laughing at the same time, and had absolutely no intention of explaining.

"That," she said, wiping her face, "was tomorrow's lesson. Sit down, all of you. We're going to talk about what it means to pay attention — really pay attention — because I have a feeling we're all going to need to know."

And high above the rooftops of a country with its name again, the god went on its way, leaving behind it a silence that felt, for the first time in Coral Nystrom's life, less like absence than like invitation.
