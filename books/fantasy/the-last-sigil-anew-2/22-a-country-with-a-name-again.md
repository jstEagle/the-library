## 22. A Country With a Name Again

The census taker came to Coral's door on a morning so bright that the river looked like hammered silver, and he asked her a question no one in Vaelharrow had been legally permitted to ask in her lifetime.

"Full name, please. As your family wrote it before the Law."

Coral stood in her doorway with flour on her hands and a pigeon on her shoulder — not speaking to it, just enjoying its company, which was now allowed and still felt faintly scandalous — and had to think for a moment.

"Nystrom," she said. "Coral Nystrom."

"And before that? The old form?"

She smiled. "Nystromma. My grandmother dropped the last syllable when the Sigil went up. Said it made the name easier to hide." She wiped her hands on her apron. "Put both down. Let the map sort itself out."

The census taker, a young man with ink-stained fingers and the reverent air of someone who had waited his whole life for paperwork to matter, wrote *both* down. He was writing everything down. All across Vaelharrow, in fact, people were lining up at census tables to say their whole names aloud, some of them crying, some of them laughing, one old fishwife reportedly refusing to leave the table until they recorded all four of her grandmother's names and also her grievance about the harbor taxes of 40 years past, which she considered part of her family history and non-negotiable.

It was, everyone agreed, the strangest festival the city had ever held, and it consisted entirely of telling the truth to a clerk.

* * *

Coral's new shop opened three streets over from the old one, because the old one had burned, along with most of the signing quarter, along with rather a lot of everything during the Unbinding. The new shop had a wide door, deep shelves that held no charms at all, and a sign she had painted herself in letters slightly crooked:

**THE SCHOOL OF LISTENING**
*No magic required. No magic permitted, actually. Please stop asking.*

The first week, nobody came. The second week, a boy named Tam slipped in through the door, sat down hard on a stool, and announced that his father said Coral Nystrom was a witch and his mother said she was a hero and he wanted to know which.

"Neither," Coral said. "I'm a teacher. Sit up straight. What do you hear right now?"

Tam listened. His face did something complicated. "The river," he said slowly. "Under the street. You can hear the river under the street."

"That's lesson one," Coral said. "You're ahead of schedule."

By the end of the month she had eleven students, ranging from Tam (age nine) to a grandmother of seventy who insisted she had never lost the knack of talking to her hens and simply wanted "refinement." By the end of the season she had forty, a waiting list, and a standing arrangement with the fishmonger next door whereby her students' practice sessions with the harbor gulls were tolerated in exchange for Coral convincing the gulls, once a week, to steal from someone else.

She could not speak to the gulls anymore. That was the thing. Her gift was gone — poured out through the Door of Anew like water through open fingers, spent in a single word spoken with every voice she had ever borrowed. But listening, it turned out, was never the magic. Listening was just listening. And a person who had spent her whole life starving for conversation with the world had gotten very, very good at it.

"You can tell when a horse is going to bite," she lectured, pacing before her students, "not because it tells you. Because it told you an hour ago, and you weren't paying attention. Ears back isn't anger. Ears back is a letter you didn't open."

Old Marta raised her hand. "And what do you call it when my hen Gertrude gives me that look?"

"That look is a legal document, Marta. Read it carefully."

The class laughed. Outside the window, the griffhawk — who had stayed, who had never left, who slept on the roof ridge every night and followed her through the market by day like a large, judgmental, feathered shadow — roused its wings and settled again. It did not speak to her. It never had, not since the Unbinding. But it stayed, and Coral had learned that staying was also a sentence in a language, maybe the truest one.

* * *

Ryn Callas came by on census day too, ostensibly to deliver a crate of apples, actually because he had heard the census takers were recording occupations and he wanted witnesses present when he said "legitimate cargo hauler" out loud without bursting into flame or laughter.

"Legitimate," Coral repeated, taking an apple.

"Mostly legitimate."

"Define 'mostly.'"

"I have paperwork now," Ryn said, wounded. "Actual paperwork. With stamps. There's a Bureau of Roads and Reopening and everything. The country has a name again, Coral. Countries with names have bureaus. It's terrible. I love it."

The country's name was Vaelmark. It had always been Vaelmark; the Sigil Law had simply written *Vaelharrow Province, Territory of the Concord* over it, the way you'd paste a label over a portrait's face. Now the labels were coming off. Old names were surfacing everywhere — in deeds and gravestones and the mouths of grandparents who suddenly found their grandchildren asking questions at dinner. The drowned chapel where Vess had first spoken to Coral had been drained, carefully, lovingly, by volunteers, and now held a shrine with no god in it, just candles and a carved inscription: *WE WERE HERE THE WHOLE TIME.*

Some days Coral still couldn't quite believe it. Some mornings she woke expecting the silence of the Law to close back over the world like water, expecting to find her own careful smallness waiting for her like a coat she'd have to put back on. Those mornings she walked down to the river until she saw the water moving freely, unbound, remembering its own name, and then she felt better.

Dain Ferrow understood those mornings. He came to the city twice a month now, walking with a cane and a limp that the healers said would improve and Dain said was "character." He had refused every offer of rank in the reformed Inquisition — which was less an Inquisition now than an Office of Records and Restitution, a change that had made him laugh for the first time in years — and had instead taken a post teaching history to children, including the parts of history he had personally helped bury.

"They asked me last week if I was ashamed," he told Coral, sitting in her shop with tea he kept forgetting to drink. "Of having served the Law."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them shame is a debt, and debts are meant to be paid in work, not in wallowing. Then Tam threw an inkwell at another student and I had to go be an authority figure, which rather undercut the gravity."

"How is Tam?"

"Suspended from two other schools before mine. Brilliant. Frightening. Last week he told me he could hear the difference between the river rising and the river just being loud." Dain finally drank his tea. "He may be one of yours, when magic finishes deciding what it's going to do with this generation."

That was the great open question hanging over everything, gentle as weather: the magic was back, but unevenly, strangely, like frost melting off a window in patches. Some children heard rivers. A shepherd in the eastern hills claimed his dog now answered questions, though only rude ones. Nothing like the old power — nothing like gods walking — but seeds. The land remembered slowly. Everyone was learning to wait.

* * *

On the evening of the longest day of the year, Vaelharrow held a festival for the census itself, because the city had decided any excuse was a good excuse now, and Coral closed the school early and walked to the harbor with half her students trailing behind her like ducklings.

They lit lanterns on the water. Musicians played songs whose names had been illegal a year ago. An old woman danced with a statue, on the grounds that the statue had been her sweetheart's favorite meeting spot and it seemed rude to celebrate without him. Tam attempted to teach the harbor gulls to sing in harmony and was, Coral noted with pride and mild horror, making actual progress, despite having no gift whatsoever and an amount of persistence that bordered on the supernatural.

And then, near full dark, the wind changed.

Coral felt it before anyone — a pressure shift, a vast displacement of air, the particular hush that falls when every animal in a square goes quiet at once. She looked up.

High above the harbor lights, above the smoke and the lanterns and the noise of a country remembering itself, a shape crossed the moon. Enormous. Slow. Wings like weather. It was migrating — south, or perhaps following some current older than maps — and it passed over Vaelharrow the way spring passes over a field: entirely, inevitably, touching everything.

The crowd didn't panic. A year ago they might have. Now people pointed, and gasped, and someone began to cheer, and the cheering spread, ragged and delighted, up and down the waterfront. Children screamed with joy. The musicians stopped playing and let it pass overhead in respectful silence, like a ship of state.

Coral stood still in the middle of it all, her heart going like a drum, and listened with everything she had.

Nothing came. Of course nothing came. Her gift was spent; she had known that the way you know a scar doesn't itch. Whatever Vess was now — awake, free, vast, moving through a world that had room for it again — it did not speak to her, and it owed her no words, and she had never wanted anything from it except exactly this: that it should exist, somewhere, unfettered, under an honest sky.

But as the great wings beat past, directly overhead, one enormous shadow sweeping over the crowd like a benediction —

For one heartbeat. One.

Something brushed the edge of her, the way warmth brushes the edge of a window in winter. Not a word. Not a voice. Just a pressure of attention, brief and immense, the feeling of being *seen* by something older than seeing — and within it, wordless, unmistakable, something that was not thanks, because gods don't thank, but was perhaps the thing that comes before thanks, or after it, or instead of it:

*Acknowledgment. You were the door. We remember doors.*

Then it was gone, southward, into the dark beyond the harbor mouth, and the wind settled, and the crowd exhaled all together, and someone started playing again.

Coral discovered she was crying, which embarrassed her deeply, and that Tam was tugging her sleeve, which saved her.

"What was that?" he demanded. "What WAS that? Did you feel it? I felt it in my TEETH."

"That," Coral said, wiping her eyes briskly, "was lesson none. That was just the world saying hello."

"It said hello to ME?"

"The world says hello to everybody, Tam. That's the entire curriculum. Class dismissed."

* * *

Later, when the lanterns had burned low and her students had been collected by their various parents and guardians and, in Tam's case, an apologetic aunt, Coral walked home along the river with the griffhawk gliding above her, unhurried, keeping her pace the way it always did.

At her door, Ryn was waiting on the step with two cups of cider and the expression of a man who had seen the sky-shape from the rooftops and run all the way here for no reason he was willing to admit.

"You missed it," she said.

"I saw it. Whole city saw it. Couldn't miss it." He handed her a cup. "I just figured you'd want to not be alone after."

"I wasn't alone."

"You know what I mean."

She sat beside him on the step. The cider was warm and too sweet, the way he always made it, the way she had stopped correcting years ago because some things were better as signatures. Down the street, the census office still had lamplight in its windows; the clerks were working late, writing a country back into existence one honest name at a time.

"Dain says the eastern valleys are greening early," Ryn said after a while. "Places that were ash after the Unbinding. Things growing there that aren't in any book. The Bureau wants to send a survey team." He paused. "They asked me to haul the supplies. Legitimately."

"Are you going?"

"Thought about it." He turned the cup in his hands. "Roads east are bad. Weather's worse. There's talk of things waking up out there that nobody's got names for yet." A grin crept sideways onto his face, the old one, the smuggler's one, repurposed. "Figured somebody ought to go who's good at listening. And somebody ought to go who's good at getting paid. And Tam's aunt says he'll climb the walls if we leave him behind, so really it's a public service."

Coral looked at him. "You're asking me to come."

"I'm informing you that I'm going, and that there will be a seat on the wagon, and that the seat is warm and dry and faces the interesting direction." He sipped his cider. "Take it or leave it, teacher."

Above them, the griffhawk shifted on the roof ridge and made a sound — not a word, never a word, but a note, a specific note, the one it always made when Coral was about to do something it approved of.

She laughed. She finished her cider. She looked east, where the dark hills were already filling up with whatever came next — new growth, old names, things waking gently, things waking otherwise, a whole country's worth of conversations nobody had had yet.

"All right," she said. "But I'm bringing the class syllabus. Somebody has to grade Tam on the road."

The griffhawk made its note again. Somewhere far south, wings like weather were carrying a god toward warmer skies, and the world — named, unmapped, unfinished — was saying hello to everyone.
