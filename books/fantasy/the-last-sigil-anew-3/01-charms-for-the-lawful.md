## 1. Charms for the Lawful

The trouble with selling charms that don't work, Oscar Thackeray had learned over twenty years, was that eventually people stopped believing in them — and the trouble with selling charms that *did* work was considerably worse, involving as it did fire, arrest, and in one memorable case a goat.

So he sold neither. He sold trinkets.

He opened the shop at half past seven every morning, the way he had for two decades, first unhooking the little brass bell above the door and silencing its clapper with a twist of felt. A bell was honest. It announced you to the world. But an honest bell also announced the world to you, and Oscar preferred his mornings quiet: the creak of shutters, the smell of last night's lamp oil, the particular gray light of Vell Street slanting through windows he'd never gotten around to cleaning properly.

The shop itself was called Thackeray's Charms & Curiosities, which was a lie twice over — there were no charms, strictly speaking, and the curiosities were curated with great care to be entirely un-curious. Dried lavender bundles for restful sleep. Copper rings "blessed" by nobody against rheumatism. Little knotted cords in cheerful colors that were supposed to keep away bad luck and did so only insofar as any brightly colored object improves a person's morning mood, which, Oscar would argue if pressed, was not nothing.

There was a jar of river stones labeled PROTECTION (SMALL). Nobody ever asked protection from what. That was the genius of it.

"Morning, Oscar," said Mrs. Havelock, who arrived every day at quarter past eight precisely, as she had since before the purges, before the renaming, before the country on whose soil they both stood had agreed to forget its own name. She bought a lavender bundle every Tuesday without fail and had done for nineteen years, and neither of them had ever once acknowledged what they both knew perfectly well: that she remembered her grandmother's kitchen smelling exactly like this, back when kitchens could legally smell like that.

"Tuesday already?" Oscar said, ringing up nothing on the register that recorded no money changing hands, because Mrs. Havelock paid in eggs and gossip, both of which he accepted gladly.

"Widow Prentiss's boy got stopped at the checkpoint again," she said, arranging her eggs in the bowl he kept out for the purpose. "Third time this month. They say his papers smell wrong."

"Papers can't smell."

"That's what I said." She lowered her voice to something barely above the lavender. "They smelled them anyway."

Oscar nodded the way you nod at weather. Inside his chest, beneath the nod, something old and tired shifted its weight, the way a large animal does in a small room.

"I'll take the brown ones too," Mrs. Havelock said, nodding at the knot-cords. "For my knees."

"The blue ones are for knees. The brown ones are for courage."

"My knees require courage, Oscar."

"Then brown it is." He wrapped them in paper, and their fingers touched briefly over the counter, hers dry and warm, and she looked at him a moment too long with an expression he pretended not to recognize because recognizing it meant answering it, and he had been declining to answer things for twenty years.

"You ought to eat more," she said at the door. "You've gone thin as a candle."

"Burning at both ends, Mrs. H."

She laughed, and the bell — still muffled — gave its soft felt thump as she left, and the shop settled back into silence, and Oscar stood behind his counter in the gray light and let the silence settle over him like a coat that fit too well to take off.

* * *

At nine, he swept. At ten, he dusted the shelves in an order known only to himself, though anyone watching closely might have noticed the sequence was less about cleanliness than about sight lines — certain shelves checked before others, certain corners confirmed empty before others. At eleven he watered the fern, which was dead, and had been dead for six years, and which he watered anyway on the principle that some routines are prayers and prayers do not require the thing prayed at to be alive.

And at noon, every day, he went down into the floor.

Not far. Three steps' worth of floor, to be precise, hidden beneath the trapdoor under the rug behind the counter, down a ladder into a room the city's surveyors had somehow never surveyed, the tax assessors had somehow never assessed, and the Wardens had somehow never found — though "somehow" was doing twenty years of daily work in that sentence, and Oscar knew exactly how much of it was luck and how much was craft, and the ratio made him sweat on inspection days.

The room below held one thing.

It held a brazier.

To call it a brazier was like calling the sea a puddle, but it was shaped like one — a broad iron dish on three legs, older than the building, older probably than the street, and in it burned a coal. One coal. Black at the edges, gold at the heart, no bigger than a fist, and it had been burning since before Oscar's grandfather was born, and it would go on burning long after everyone who remembered why, unless Oscar failed, in which case it would go out, and then everything else would follow it into the dark.

He knelt beside it the way other men knelt at altars, and he fed it.

Feeding the coal was not a matter of wood. The coal ate stranger things. A spoonful of salt on Mondays. A thread pulled from a garment worn with real joy — rare, these days, but Mrs. Havelock's laughter at the knee-courage joke would do nicely; he'd saved a length of it in a tin. On hard weeks, a memory, spoken aloud into the flame, which consumed it the way a hearth consumes a letter, leaving him lighter and poorer both.

Today was Wednesday. Today it ate a secret.

"There's a boy on Cordwainer Lane," he told it, quietly, "who's been drawing spirals on walls. Old spirals. The kind we used to paint on doors for luck." The coal brightened a shade, drinking. "His mother thinks it's chalk mischief. I think somebody's grandmother told him stories. I haven't reported it. I won't report it. There."

The flame steadied, satisfied, and Oscar felt the peculiar double sensation he always felt after feeding it: lighter, having given something up, and heavier, carrying what remained. Twenty years of Wednesdays. Twenty years of secrets surrendered to a fist-sized fire in a hole beneath a shop that sold lies by the yard.

"You're welcome," he told it, because someone should say it.

Above him, faintly, through the floorboards, he heard the front door's felt-muffled thump and froze, one hand on the ladder.

"Shop's open!" he called up, climbing, composing his face into the mild pleasant vacancy of a man who sells dried flowers for a living.

* * *

The man in the doorway wore gray.

That was all you needed to know about him, really. The Wardens had chosen gray the way other institutions chose scarlet or gold — deliberately, thoughtfully, the color of weather, of stone, of things that endure and do not care. His badge was polished. His boots were loud on Oscar's quiet floor, and Oscar hated him immediately and thoroughly and showed none of it, because showing none of it was the whole of his life's work.

"Afternoon," said the Warden, who was not Ilva Brann, which was both a relief and a worry. Brann came Thursdays. This was Wednesday, and this one was young, with the particular eagerness of a young dog who has not yet learned which smells matter.

"Welcome to Thackeray's Charms and Curiosities," Oscar said warmly. "Can I interest you in a knot-cord? Very popular with the constabulary, actually. The green ones. Steady nerves."

"We've had a report."

"Haven't we all."

"Of unlicensed warmth." The young Warden produced a slate and consulted it with great seriousness. "A glow. Witnessed at night through your shutters. Below street level."

"A glow below street level," Oscar repeated. "Through my shutters."

"The cellar, presumably."

"Ah." Oscar spread his hands apologetically. "Well, I'll be straight with you, Warden — it's the pipes. Building's older than the charter, older than the *renaming*, if you take my meaning, and the old heating flues run everywhere. On cold nights the draft catches the banked coals in the old furnace and it glows up through the grates like — well, like a hearth. You know how it is. Old houses remember being warm."

He said it lightly. He said everything lightly; it was the armor he'd worn so long he sometimes forgot it wasn't skin. But inside, beneath the lightness, his heart had gone very quiet and very careful, the way it always did when someone stood unknowing above the most dangerous thing in the renamed world.

The Warden frowned. "I'm required to inspect the premises."

"Of course you are. Required is required." Oscar gestured graciously. "Mind the fern. It's delicate."

The fern was dead. The Warden looked at it, evidently deciding whether dead plants fell under the Act of Public Safety, and moved on. He inspected the shelves. He inspected the register. He inspected the jar of river stones — PROTECTION (SMALL) — and read the label twice, and Oscar watched him read it and thought: *there it is, there's the look, that little itch between the eyes.* Everyone felt it in this shop if they stayed long enough. Something underneath. Something patient. Most people shook it off like rain.

Young dogs sometimes didn't shake it off.

"What's below the rug?" the Warden asked.

"Damp," said Oscar promptly. "Vell Street floods in spring. Everything under there's ruined. I keep meaning to tear it up and I keep not doing it, on the principle that knowing about the damp is worse than having it. My late wife's idea, actually. Never argue with a dead woman's flooring."

The lie came out smooth and complete, with a wife attached for texture, and the Warden's frown deepened in the way frowns do when they're being handed sympathy and suspicion in equal measure and can't sort which pocket to put them in.

"Open it, please."

"Truly, it's—"

"*Please*, Master Thackeray."

And here was the moment, Oscar thought, standing there in his own shop with his own heartbeat suddenly loud in his ears. Here was the hinge the whole day swung on. He could refuse — and refusal summoned the district office, and the district office summoned instruments, and instruments were patient and thorough and did not accept stories about pipes. Or he could comply, and pray the craft held, the craft he'd laid down over three nights and two decades ago with shaking hands and Merrin's voice in his ear saying *again, again, you've missed a stitch, again.*

He lifted the rug.

He opened the trapdoor.

Below, the little room sat in darkness, smelling of iron and old smoke and nothing else. No glow. No warmth rising. The coal, bless its ancient patient heart, had banked itself down to nothing the moment the trapdoor hinges had creaked — it did that, had learned to do that, or had been taught, and Oscar had never been sure which frightened him more.

"Cellar," said the Warden, disappointed.

"Cellar," Oscar agreed. "Mostly cellar. Bit of a shrine, honestly, if I'm being frank. Old habit. Don't tell the office."

The Warden peered down into the dark, and the dark looked back with twenty years of practiced innocence, and after a long moment the young man straightened, closed his slate, and said the words Oscar would turn over in his mind all evening:

"It's odd. Standing here. Like the floor's *listening*."

"They all say that," Oscar said pleasantly. "Old houses."

* * *

After the Warden left — taking, at Oscar's insistence, a green knot-cord for his nerves, free of charge, because a calm Warden was a safe Warden — Oscar locked the door, silenced the bell, and stood in the middle of his shop with his hand pressed flat against the floorboards until the trembling in his arms worked itself out.

Weary vigilance. That was the phrase he'd come to use for it, in the privacy of his own skull. Not fear — he'd have admitted to fear, fear was honest. This was subtler. This was the state of a man who had arranged his entire existence into a shape designed not to attract attention, and then lived in that shape so long he could no longer tell where the vigilance ended and the peace began. Was he content? He genuinely no longer knew. The routines held. The shop held. The coal held. Every day the same, and every day the same was victory, and victory tasted — he reached for the word and found it — *thin*. Like soup stretched too far. Like a song hummed so many years you'd forgotten there were words.

He went down the ladder at dusk and fed the coal its supper: the memory of Merrin laughing, once, before. Just the laugh. He kept the rest.

The flame took it gently, almost kindly, and settled low.

"Another day," Oscar told it. "Nobody knows. We're fine. We're *fine*."

The coal did not answer. It never answered. Though — and Oscar leaned closer, frowning, telling himself it was the dusk light through the floorboards, telling himself it was his eyes, forty-eight years old and tired —

Though for just a moment, just a breath, the flame had bent. Not flickered. *Bent.* Leaned toward the trapdoor. Toward the shop above. Toward the street beyond it, where the rain was starting, hard and sudden, drumming on the shutters like fingers.

Like knocking.

Oscar climbed the ladder slowly, listening to the rain, and told himself the shiver down his spine was only the cellar damp.

Somewhere out in that rain, something was coming toward his door. He didn't know what yet. He only knew that for the first time in twenty years, the coal was looking forward to it.
