## 2. The Child at Threshold

The rain came in off the coast like it had a grudge to settle.

Oscar Thackeray stood behind his counter at half past nine in the evening, watching the water sheet down the window of Thackeray's Charms & Curiosities, and thought—not for the first time—that he should have closed an hour ago. The lamp above the till made a small warm island of the shop: shelves of luck-knuckles and hearth-blessing candles, jars of "genuine river-stones" (river-adjacent, at best), the rack of love charms that had never once worked but sold beautifully in spring. Beyond the lamplight, the shop went soft and grey, and beyond the window, the whole street drowned.

He liked this hour, if he was honest. He would never say so aloud, because saying so aloud felt like tempting something, but there was a particular peace to a charm shop after dark, when the day's customers had come and gone with their small hopes—safe travel for a son on the coach road, easy birthing for a daughter-in-law, a coin or two that might stick to another—and Oscar could stand among the shelves and feel like a man running a bakery that only ever sold bread-shaped stones. Harmless. Lawful. Exactly what the Wardens required of him, and exactly what he intended to give them.

Below his feet—three floors of nothing, then stone, then a door—he kept the last fire in the country burning.

But that was not a thought for evenings like this. Evenings like this were for counting the till and watering the fern that refused, out of what Oscar suspected was spite, to die.

* * *

The trouble started, as most troubles did, with a knock.

It was a strange knock even before he registered how strange. Soft. Almost apologetic. Three little raps, low on the door, as though the knocker were not very tall—or not very strong—or both.

Oscar looked at the clock. Ten minutes to ten. The rain had gone from grudge to outright war; it hammered the shutters and gurgled in the gutter and turned the cobbles outside into a black mirror. Nobody knocked on doors in weather like this unless the alternative was worse than drowning.

He crossed the shop, unlatched the door, and opened it perhaps four inches, which was as far as the chain allowed.

A child stood on his step.

Barefoot. That was the first thing. Small bare feet on wet stone, toes curled against the cold, and no shoes anywhere in sight, no bundle, no coat, nothing but a thin grey smock soaked through to the color of storm-cloud. The second thing was the eyes—dark, unblinking, fixed on Oscar with the terrible directness children have when they have stopped expecting adults to be useful. The third thing—

The third thing was that the child's lips were moving, and sound was coming out of them, and Oscar knew the language.

Not recognized. Not understood. *Knew*, the way you know a voice you've heard through a wall your whole life. The syllables were round and old and fell into a rhythm that made the hair on his arms lift, because he had heard that rhythm every single night for twenty years, whispered up through the floorboards, murmured by a door that had no mouth and no reason to speak except habit and grief.

"*Vael en dorath,*" the child said. Rain ran off their chin. "*Vael en dorath, kelem sira.*"

The brazier flared.

It was downstairs. It was under the floorboards, behind the false panel, inside its iron cage in the sealed room where no light and no heat and no word of it was ever supposed to escape—and yet the glow came up through the seams like sunrise under a door, gold flooding the cracks between the floor planks, throwing the shadow of every shelf up onto the ceiling. The jar of river-stones rattled on its shelf. The love-charms, those honest frauds, trembled on their hooks.

The child did not look surprised. The child looked at the glowing floorboards the way other children looked at windows on the first snowy morning of the year.

"No," Oscar said—which was absurd, because the child hadn't asked anything—but the word came out anyway, cracked down the middle. "No, no, no." He stepped out onto the step, dragging the door shut behind him, and knelt, and put both hands over the child's ears as though that could take the language back. Rain hammered his shoulders. "You can't—you don't understand, you cannot say that here, do you hear me? Not here. Not anywhere."

The child stared up at him from beneath his hands. Then, gently, patiently, they reached up and moved his hands away, the way you'd move a curtain aside, and said again, softly:

"*Kelem sira.*"

And Oscar—who had spent twenty years learning to be a man who sold bread-shaped stones—felt something in his chest crack open along a seam he'd thought was scarred over for good.

* * *

He got the child inside. Of course he got the child inside. He was aware, all the while he was doing it—toweling rain off a small stubborn head, setting a stool by the stove, hunting through the back room for the blanket his mother had woven before weaving was a crime—that every instinct he owned was screaming at him like a scalded cat. Take them in. Feed them. Warm them. Wonderful. And then what? A barefoot child speaking the dead tongue of a dead kingdom appears on your doorstep in a storm, and the hidden fire beneath your floor leaps up like a dog hearing its name, and your plan is... what? Porridge?

"You," Oscar told the child, kneeling to wrap the blanket around narrow shoulders, "are going to be the death of me, and I want you to know I noticed. I noticed immediately."

The child blinked at him. Then they reached into the fold of the sodden smock and produced, of all things, a button—a plain bone button—and held it out to him with both hands, formally, the way one presents credentials to an ambassador.

Oscar looked at the button. The child looked at Oscar. Somewhere below them, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, the brazier pulsed twice and settled.

"That's not payment," Oscar said weakly. "I'm not charging you. There's no charge for—" He gestured vaguely at the child, the storm, the entire situation. "For whatever this is."

The child did not withdraw the button.

"Oh, for pity's sake." Oscar took it. It was warm. Everything about this evening was going to be warm in ways that would get people hanged, he could feel it. "Thank you. Very generous. I'll treasure it."

Something flickered across the small face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning—and Oscar realized, with a lurch, that the child was trying not to smile. Trying and losing. And that when they lost, the whole face changed, went from ancient and weathered to simply, ordinarily young, and Oscar's chest did the cracking-open thing again, worse this time, right down to some load-bearing timber.

"Right," he said briskly, standing up before his knees could decide anything sentimental without him. "Food. You're a wet stick with eyes. Sit there."

There wasn't much. There was never much worth mentioning: bread, cheese going pleasantly heroic, an apple, honey he told his customers was imported (it was not; there was nowhere left to import from) and which he actually traded for, once a season, from a beekeeper upcountry who never asked questions. Oscar cut bread thick, added cheese and apple and too much honey, arranged it on his good plate—the chipped one—and set it down in front of the child, who regarded the food for a long moment with an expression Oscar couldn't read.

Then the child picked up the bread, held it briefly to their forehead—an old gesture, an old-country gesture, gratitude before eating, thanks to the hearth that gave—and ate like the starving eat, quietly and completely, with no waste and no hurry and a discipline that hurt to watch.

Oscar busied himself with the kettle. His hands were not entirely steady. Twenty years of routine, and routine is a kind of armor, and the armor was developing holes.

He fed the fire nightly. He spoke to it sometimes—just a word or two, *still here, still burning*, nonsense really, comfort really—and in twenty years the coal had answered him exactly never. It burned. It waited. Those were its whole vocabulary. And now a soaked child says three words on his doorstep and the whole house lights up like festival night, and Oscar was expected to believe in coincidences, was he? He'd sooner believe the love-charms worked.

The child finished eating, set down the plate neatly, and looked at him. Waited.

"I don't know what you want from me," Oscar said honestly. He sat down on his own stool, opposite, and found that his knees and the child's eyes between them had conspired to make lying impossible. "I don't know who sent you, or how you learned—I don't know how anyone *learned*, we made sure, we were so careful—" He stopped. Pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. When he took them away, the child had leaned forward, and their gaze had dropped to the floorboards, to the place where the glow still breathed faintly up through the seams, gold and patient and utterly unmistakable to anyone who knew what they were looking at.

The child looked back up at Oscar and pointed, once, downward.

"Don't," Oscar said. "Don't point at that. Ever. Not here, not anywhere, not to anyone, do you understand me?" His voice came out harder than he meant it to, hard enough that the child flinched, and immediately he hated himself, and softened, and heard himself say, gentler: "I'm not angry at you. I'm frightened. There's a difference, and I'm sorry it doesn't look like one from where you're sitting."

The child studied him for a long moment. Then they nodded slowly, gravely, with the solemnity of someone agreeing to a treaty, and pointed instead—at Oscar.

"Me?"

A nod.

"What about me?"

The child's hand made a shape in the air: cupped, like holding water. Like holding a coal. They pressed the shape against their own chest, then extended it toward him, and their mouth shaped a word in the old tongue—soft, careful, offering it to him the way they'd offered the button.

Oscar didn't need a translation. He had heard that word through the floorboards for twenty years, in the door's midnight murmur, and he had never once let himself wonder why the door said it in a voice like a man keeping vigil.

"*Keeper,*" the child said, in trade-tongue, in plain flat modern speech, and the word landed in the quiet shop like a stone in a well.

The kettle began, very softly, to sing.

* * *

Later—much later, past midnight, past the hour when any sensible fugitive child would have been asleep—Oscar spread blankets in the back room, in the little alcove behind the stockroom that had been a servant's bed once and had been nobody's bed for twenty years. The child lay down without being told, curled around themselves like a comma, and was asleep almost instantly, the way the young and the exhausted are permitted to sleep: totally, trustingly, as though the world had proven itself safe.

Oscar stood in the doorway a long time, watching.

Every sensible part of him drew up its list. In the morning: questions. Who sent you. Where are your people. How do you know the words. And underneath the list, quieter, the parts of him that were not sensible at all, the parts he'd boarded up and papered over with shopkeeping and small jokes and a fern: *a child, alone, barefoot, in the rain, walking out of the dark straight to my door, of all doors, knowing my word for what I am.*

The brazier glowed faintly through the floor. Steady now. Contented, almost. In twenty years it had never once sounded contented.

"You'd better not be doing what I think you're doing," Oscar murmured to it, to all of it—to the fire, to the door beneath, to whatever old patience had finally run out of waiting. "I had an arrangement. We had *years*. Quiet years. Good years."

The glow pulsed, gentle and unrepentant, like a cat that has decided whose lap it lives in.

Oscar sighed, blew out the lamp, and stood a moment longer in the dark, listening to the rain and to the small breathing in the alcove and, beneath everything, beneath the floor and the stone and the twenty years, the deep slow murmur of the door—speaking still, as it always had, but tonight, unmistakably, saying something new.

Tonight it was saying *soon*.
