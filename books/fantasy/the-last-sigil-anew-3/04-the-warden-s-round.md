## 4. The Warden's Round

The tip came in on the morning post, as tips always did, folded into a Warden-grey envelope that smelled faintly of someone's lunch. Ilva Brann read it standing at her desk with her boots still wet from the walk over, and she read it twice, because the handwriting was familiar.

*Thackeray's Charms and Curios. Unlicensed light reported after dark. Light of an unnatural color. Third floor window.*

Third floor. The shop had two floors.

She set the note down and rubbed her eyes. In eleven years of service she had learned to read informants the way other people read weather: some were honest, some were frightened, and some—the worst kind—were simply bored. A bored informant was more dangerous than any of them, because boredom invented details, and details had a way of becoming true once you wrote them down.

Unnatural light. She thought about the lamps in Oscar Thackeray's shop, the ones he sold by the dozen—little glass baubles that glowed the color of pond water and cost three coppers apiece. Licensed, every one. He kept his license framed behind the counter, slightly crooked, the way he kept everything: deliberately, so you'd correct him, and then he'd have something to talk about.

But third floor.

She took her coat off the hook.

* * *

The morning was doing its best. That was how Ilva thought of Greyharbor weather in autumn—not good, exactly, but trying. A thin sun worked at the fog over the canal, and gulls argued on the rooftops about something that had probably been true yesterday and wasn't anymore. Her rounds took her past the fish market first, where Sergeant Okonwe was already deep in a dispute with a woman selling eels, and Ilva pretended not to see him, because if she saw him he would hand her the paperwork, and there was no eel dispute in the world worth paperwork before noon.

Then down Candlemaker's Row, past the chandler who nodded to her like they were friends (they weren't; he just wanted her to believe the Wardens liked him), and left at the corner with the crooked lamppost, to Charm Lane.

Oscar Thackeray's shop sat at the end of the lane like an old dog asleep in a doorway. Painted sign: *CHARMS FOR THE LAWFUL*, with the second word repainted at some point in a slightly different shade of green, which Ilva had never asked about and suspected she didn't want to. The window display changed with the seasons and never with anything else: luck-knots for travelers, sleep-charms for teething babies, little tin houses for hearth-blessings. All of it licensed. All of it harmless. All of it, she had long suspected, exactly as dangerous as a spoon.

She stood outside a moment longer than she needed to.

Duty said go in. Guilt said go in gently. And underneath both of those, quieter, a third thing she had no name for said: *he'll know why you came, and he'll make you laugh anyway, and somehow that will be worse.*

She went in.

The bell over the door rang the same cheerful two notes it always rang, a sound so aggressively innocent it could only have been chosen on purpose. The shop smelled of cedar shavings and beeswax and, beneath that, something older and warmer that Ilva had stopped noticing years ago—or told herself she had.

"Madam Sergeant!" Oscar emerged from the back room wiping his hands on his apron, silver-haired, round-faced, wearing the expression of a man delighted beyond all reason to see her. "You're early. Or I'm late. One of us has failed at time and I refuse to let it be me."

"Ilva," she said. "How many times."

"At least forty. I keep count." He leaned on the counter. "Tea?"

"This is an inspection."

"Tea *during* an inspection, then. The law allows refreshments. Chapter nine, I believe. Or possibly I dreamed it." He was already pouring. Two cups. He'd had two cups out; she could see the second one drying upside-down on the shelf behind him, which meant either he'd expected her or he drank tea alone with two cups like a man setting a place for a ghost.

She took the cup. It was too hot and too sweet, exactly as always.

"Tip came in this morning," she said. "Unlicensed light. After dark. Third floor window."

Oscar's face did something very small and very quick—a flicker, gone before most people would have caught it. Most people. Ilva caught it, and hated herself for catching it.

"Third floor," he repeated. "Ilva. This building has two floors and an attic full of pigeons who pay no rent and answer to no council. If there's a third floor, it's news to me and I own the place."

"The informant says—"

"The informant," Oscar said comfortably, "says whatever the informant likes. You know what half this street does of an evening? They stand at their windows and invent each other. Mrs. Pell next door reported me last spring for 'suspicious humming.' Suspicious humming, Ilva. What am I going to do, hum a kingdom into existence?"

The words were out before she could stop thinking them, hanging in the sweet tea-smelling air between them. Humming. Kingdoms. Old things under old floors.

Oscar looked at her, and for a heartbeat neither of them blinked.

"More tea?" he said.

"No." She set the cup down. "Show me the upper rooms."

* * *

He showed her the upper rooms.

She knew what she would find before she found it, because she had inspected this shop eleven times in eleven years and it was always the same: a narrow stair, a landing with a coat-stand that held three coats belonging to three different men none of whom were Oscar, a bedroom made neatly enough to be suspicious and lived-in enough to be reassuring, a workroom smelling of glue and tin, a small window overlooking the lane. No third floor. No unnatural light. Nothing but dust motes turning in the thin sun and a pigeon on the roof tiles staring at her with the flat judgment only pigeons can manage.

"You can arrest the pigeon," Oscar offered from the doorway. "It's been giving me looks for weeks."

"It's your look," she said. "I've seen you make it."

"I've never made a look in my life. I'm a simple tradesman."

"A simple tradesman who repaints the word LAWFUL on his own sign."

"That's called maintenance." He followed her back down the stairs, and she felt him behind her the whole way, felt the careful weight of him, a big man being quiet on his own stairs. Why would a man be quiet on his own stairs?

Because of the floorboards.

She stopped on the bottom step.

The shop floor was old pine, wide-planked, worn silver at the threshold where twenty years of customers had shuffled their feet. And beneath it—she couldn't have said how she knew, except that she was a Warden-Sergeant and knowing was the job—beneath it there was *something*. Not a sound. Not heat. More like the feeling you get standing near a river in the dark: a sense of depth and movement and a patience far larger than yourself.

Old. Whatever it was, it was old the way bedrock is old. Older than the Wardens. Older, some traitorous part of her whispered, than the country itself—which was saying nothing, since the country had no name anymore, having erased it, having spent thirty years scrubbing itself clean of its own history until even the maps were embarrassed.

"Ilva?" Oscar said softly.

She looked at the floorboards. She looked at him. He was watching her with those mild brown eyes, hands loose at his sides, and she understood suddenly that he was afraid—not of her finding something, but of her *looking*, and that he had been afraid like this, quietly, politely, for twenty years, and that the fear had worn grooves in him the way feet wore the threshold.

There was a night she thought about more often than she admitted. Nineteen years ago. The Year of Cinders, when the purges rolled through the low quarters and the Wardens burned whatever the Council pointed at. She had been new then—young, certain, hungry to believe the law was a lamp and not a torch. And she had come to this street with a list of names, and one name on it had been *Voss, Merrin, charmwright*, and another had been *Thackeray, Oscar, associate*.

And Oscar Thackeray had opened his door to her, this same door, this same cheerful bell, and he had been holding a sleeping child—not his, a neighbor's, a girl whose parents were already ash—and he had looked at Ilva Brann, nineteen years old with a list in her hand, and he had said, "Come in out of the cold, Sergeant. You look half-frozen. Tea?"

And while she drank the tea he talked to her—about nothing, about everything, about the proper way to mend a luck-knot and the scandalous price of canal tolls—until the child woke and he introduced her as his niece visiting from upcountry, and Ilva Brann wrote *no association found* beside both names on her list, and walked out into the burning night, and never told a living soul.

The girl's name had been Sefa. Ilva had seen it again years later, in a smuggler's ledger, attached to a boat on unmarked water. She had torn that page out. She had never asked herself too hard why.

"Sergeant Brann," Oscar said again, and now there was something else in his voice, something that sounded almost like pity. "The floor?"

"The floor," she said. "Old pine. Wide-plank. Pre-kingdom cut, maybe, if the grain—" She stopped. Took a breath. Did her duty, the way she understood it, which was to say badly and honestly. "Nothing here. No unlicensed light. No third floor. I'll mark it unfounded."

"I knew you would."

"Don't." Her voice came out sharper than she meant. "Don't *knew* me, Oscar. Don't stand there being grateful. I came because someone wrote lies about you and my job is to check lies. That's all this is. That's all it ever was."

"All right," he said gently. "That's all it ever was."

She hated how warm the shop was. She hated the tea still sitting on the counter going cold, poured for her before she arrived. She hated that the bell had rung its silly innocent two notes and that somewhere below the floorboards, if below meant anything, something patient was keeping its own counsel, and she had just agreed to keep it company.

* * *

Outside, the fog had burned off and the gulls had won their argument or lost it; either way they'd moved on. She stood on the corner by the crooked lamppost and filled out the report against her knee, her handwriting neat and damning in its neatness:

*Inspection of premises, Thackeray's Charms and Curios, re: complaint of unlicensed illumination. Premises contain two floors and attic space only. No third floor exists. No unlicensed light observed. Complaint appears unfounded; possible malicious origin. Recommend no further action.*

She signed it. She hesitated over the recommendation line, pen hovering, and then added, because the truth deserved a home somewhere, even a small one:

*Informant advised against frivolous reports.*

Let whoever sent the tip chew on that.

She was three steps toward the fish market when she heard her name.

"Ilva!"

Oscar stood in his doorway, apron still on, holding a paper-wrapped parcel the size of a book. He crossed the lane without looking for carts, because he was the sort of man who crossed lanes without looking for carts, and pressed the parcel into her hands.

"For the desk drawer," he said. "The one that sticks."

She looked down at it. Through the paper she could feel tin and glass and something that gave off warmth, faintly, like a stone kept by a fire.

"Oscar, I can't take—"

"It's a drawer charm. Licensed. Three coppers retail, but for you it's free, because you once bought six of them and never picked them up and I've been storing them like a fool for a year." His eyes crinkled. "Chapter nine, Sergeant. The law allows gifts between neighbors."

"That is absolutely not chapter nine."

"Is chapter *ten*, then. One of the chapters." He was already backing away, walking backward up his own step, ridiculous, silver-haired, smiling at her with a smile that didn't reach all the way to his eyes and never quite had, not in twenty years. "Tell Okonwe the eels are fresh on Thursdays. Tell Mrs. Pell my humming has improved."

He went inside. The bell rang its two notes. The door closed.

Ilva stood in the lane a long moment, the parcel warm against her palms, duty and guilt sitting in her chest like two cats sharing a chair—neither willing to leave, neither willing to look at the other.

Then she turned toward the market, and behind her, in the shop that had no third floor, above a floor that held nothing at all, a light came on. Just the ordinary evening kind. Just a man lighting his lamps.

Except that it was barely past noon, and the light, when she glanced back despite herself, was the color of pond water and gold and something else besides—something older, flickering up between the boards like breath through a keyhole, there and gone.

She didn't write it down.

She should have written it down.

Three streets away, at her own desk, she would find another grey envelope waiting—one that smelled of someone else's lunch and bore the Council seal, requesting her presence regarding *a matter of historical records.* But that was later. For now she walked her round, and the parcel warmed her hands all the way to the fish market, and somewhere beneath Charm Lane, patiently, something waited for her to decide which side of the floor she stood on.
