## 5. Twenty Years of Small Mercies

The trick to a quiet life, Oscar had learned, was to make it so quiet that even you couldn't hear it.

He woke before dawn as he always did—no alarm but the ache in his knees and the memory of a bell that hadn't rung in twenty years—and lay still for the length of one long breath, listening to the shop settle around him. The floorboards above the cellar ticked softly as they warmed. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse was rearranging his stock of dried lavender with more editorial confidence than he'd have liked.

"Take the lavender," he told the ceiling. "Leave the cinnamon. I have plans for the cinnamon."

The mouse, being a mouse, ignored him entirely.

He went down the stairs past the landing where any honest shopkeeper would have kept a pantry, and knelt at the loose board beneath the third shelf of "protection charms"—which were, in truth, painted river stones that protected against nothing worse than a bad mood. He lifted the board. Warmth rose up to meet him like a dog greeting its master.

Below, in the dark of the cellar, the brazier burned.

It had burned for twenty years without rest, without fuel beyond what he fed it, without ever once guttering low enough to let him sleep easy. It was not a fire, properly speaking. It was *the* fire—the last coal of dragon-flame in a country that had voted, signed, and stamped itself out of existence rather than be ruled by anything that burned. Everyone else had put out their hearths. Merrin Voss had made sure of it, door by door, hearth by hearth, until only this one remained, hidden under a charm shop in a city whose name appeared on no map because there were no maps, officially, of anywhere worth going.

Oscar climbed down the ladder and sat on the cold stone beside it, the way he did every morning, and fed it a pinch of ash from yesterday's incense, which it accepted with the weary dignity of an old man being handed tea he didn't ask for.

"I know," he said. "I'm late."

The flame leaned toward him anyway.

That was the whole of the arrangement, really. Twenty years of mornings like this one. A pinch of ash, a word or two, a hand held near enough to feel the pulse of something vast pretending to be small. He talked to it because silence, in a room with a living thing, is a kind of cruelty. He'd learned that the hard way.

* * *

The kingdom fell on a Tuesday, which everyone agreed afterward was the most insulting part of it.

There had been warnings, of course. There are always warnings, and they always look, at the time, like weather. The Wardens had begun as a civic order—good people with good lanterns, sworn to keep magic out of the hands of men who would weaponize a sunrise if they could figure out the pricing. Oscar himself had cheered them in the early days. He'd been twenty-six and foolish and selling charms out of a market stall, and when the Warden criers announced the Registry of Licensed Practitioners, he'd queued up in the rain with his paperwork in an oilcloth sleeve like everyone else.

Merrin Voss hadn't queued. Merrin had been invited.

"You could be too," Merrin said to him once, across a table in a tavern that no longer existed, in a district that no longer had a name. "They're not monsters, Oscar. They're frightened people with a ledger. Frightened people with a ledger can be steered."

"And you're steering them?"

"I'm standing next to the wheel," Merrin said, "which is more than you're doing."

He'd said it with that smile of his—the one that made you feel included in some joke you hadn't been told yet. Twenty years later, lying awake in the dark above a dying god's last coal, Oscar could still see it perfectly. That was the trouble with best friends. You memorized them too well. Every expression, every cadence, filed away somewhere you couldn't burn it.

The fall, when it came, took eleven days. On day one the Registry became compulsory. On day three unlicensed practice became a crime. On day five the dragons were declared a strategic hazard, and the Wardens came for them in the mountains with cold-iron lances and licenses of their own. By day nine the last of the great fires was dying, and by day ten the Council had issued the Edict of Erasure: the kingdom's name struck from every document, every map, every gravestone, replaced by a careful blank, because a country cannot miss what it cannot name.

On day eleven, Merrin Voss came to Oscar's shop at two in the morning, soaked through, bleeding from a cut above his eye, and carrying a coal of dragon-fire wrapped in seven layers of wet wool.

"They think I'm one of them," Merrin said. "That's the only reason I got this far. Oscar—I need your floor."

"My floor?"

"The stone under your shop runs down to something old. I felt it when we built the place—you thought I was being dramatic. There's a door down there, under the foundations. Older than the kingdom. Older than the *name*. And doors like that don't care about edicts." He set the bundle on the counter, and through the wool Oscar saw a glow like a heart beating. "I can seal it. Seal the door, hide the fire behind it, key it so tight that nothing gets through—not Wardens, not Council, not time. But sealing magic needs a keeper. Someone who stays. Someone who feeds it, tends it, lives above it, and tells *no one*, forever."

"Merrin."

"It has to be someone they'd never look at twice." His voice cracked on it, just slightly, and for a moment he wasn't the man standing next to the wheel at all—he was a boy from the marsh lanes who'd once stolen honeycombs with Oscar and blamed the bees. "It has to be my best friend. Please."

And Oscar—who could have said no, who should have said no, who had a life and a laugh and a future all neatly stacked like inventory—heard himself say, "You'll come back. You'll fix it from inside. A year, maybe two, and then we take the fire somewhere safe and I get my cellar back."

Merrin smiled the smile. "A year," he agreed. "Maybe two."

* * *

In the present, the kettle whistled upstairs, and Oscar climbed the ladder to fetch it before it woke the neighbors, because a keeper's first duty was invisibility, and a whistling kettle at dawn had ended quieter careers than his.

He made tea he didn't taste and opened the shutters onto the street, where Mrs. Halloway was already sweeping her step with the aggressive thoroughness of a woman performing virtue for an audience. She bought a luck-charm from him every market day—a painted stone, three coppers—and reported to the Wardens' box every festival. He liked her. This was the particular madness of those years: you could like your informers. Sometimes they were the closest thing you had to friends.

"Morning, Oscar!"

"Mrs. Halloway. Lovely frost."

"Frost is lawful," she said approvingly, and swept on.

He laughed at the right volume, closed the shutters at the right angle, and stood a moment in the dim shop with dust turning slowly in the light, feeling the familiar weight settle over his shoulders. Not dread exactly. Dread was for things that might happen. This was older and duller and far heavier: the weight of a promise being kept so long it had grown into the bone.

*Weary vigilance mistaken for peace,* his mother would have called it, if his mother had lived to see him. She'd have said it kindly, and then she'd have made him soup, and between the kindness and the soup she'd have gotten the whole truth out of him in an afternoon. He missed her most on mornings like this.

Because here was the thing he never said aloud, not to the brazier, not to the mouse, not to the painted stones: he wasn't keeping the fire alive out of duty. Duty was the story he told the dark. The truth was smaller and sadder and sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

He was keeping it alive because putting it out would mean admitting the year was up.

Twenty years of *maybe two*. Twenty years of telling himself that any morning now, boots would come down his stairs and Merrin Voss would stand in the cellar doorway with rain in his hair and say, *Right, sorry about the wait, let's get your floor back.* Twenty years of feeding a fire that was also, though he'd never found a polite way to phrase it even to himself, feeding a friendship—one coal, tended daily, in place of the man himself.

Grief, he was beginning to suspect, didn't arrive like a storm. It arrived like a lease. Quiet terms, renewed yearly, until one day you looked up and realized you'd been paying rent on an empty room for two decades and calling it home.

* * *

The first year, he'd written letters.

Not sent ones—he wasn't suicidal—but letters, inked and sealed and stacked in a drawer, addressed to a friend who had become, overnight, the second-most-powerful man in a country with no name. *Dear Merrin. The roof leaks. The Wardens inspected Tuesday; I sold them a love potion and felt ill about it. How is the wheel? Are you still standing next to it?*

The drawer filled. Forty-one letters by the end of year one. He'd read them aloud to the brazier sometimes, doing both voices, and the fire had flared at Merrin's lines in a way that might have been recognition or might have been indigestion. Dragons' fire, he decided, was entitled to its mysteries.

Year three, the letters stopped. Year five, he stopped marking the days. Year eight, a Warden-Sergeant named Ilva Brann caught him feeding the cellar draft with his sleeve rolled up—saw the glow, saw his face, saw everything—and for one endless moment the whole fragile arrangement balanced on the point of a sword.

She'd looked at him for a long time. Then she'd looked at the ledger, and written *unlicensed candle, warning issued*, in a hand so deliberately boring it was practically a love letter to discretion.

He'd never asked why. Some mercies you don't inspect, the same way you don't count a magician's fingers while he's doing the trick. But he thought about her often—more than he thought about Merrin, if he was honest, because Merrin had become a monument in his head while Ilva Brann remained a person, tired and guilty and human, walking her rounds with a debt in her pocket she pretended wasn't there.

Everyone in that city was pretending something wasn't there. It was practically the national industry, now that the nation had erased itself. The baker pretended his bread had no blessing in it. The schoolteacher pretended the old alphabet was just decoration. Mrs. Halloway pretended she swept her step because she loved sweeping. And Oscar Thackeray pretended, twenty hours a day, to be the most boring man in the most boring shop on the most boring street, while underneath his floorboards the last true fire in the world breathed slow and patient, waiting for him to admit what it already knew:

That seals fail. That promises rot. That a year is not twenty years, and a friend who does not come back is not coming back.

* * *

That evening, after close, he carried the teapot down to the cellar out of habit—two cups, poured both, drank one, left one steaming on the stone step where it had sat, untouched, for six thousand evenings—and told the brazier about his day. The frost. Mrs. Halloway. The mouse's ongoing lavender insurgency.

The flame listened the way it always did, leaning toward him, burning low and steady, and if it understood him it gave no sign, and if it pitied him it had the decency to hide it.

"Twenty years," he said, to the fire, to the cup, to the dark. "Say something, Merrin. Anything. A letter. A rumor. One word through whatever door you're standing next to these days."

The brazier flickered.

Just once—just a lean, a shiver, a shape like a held breath—and Oscar told himself it was the draft from the stairwell, the same lie he'd been telling himself for twenty years, and reached for the poker out of habit.

Then he stopped. Because the draft from the stairwell didn't carry sound. And the sound he was hearing—faint, rising up through the floorboards above, threading down through the bones of the house like water through chalk—was a voice. Small. Young. Speaking a language he knew only from one source, from twenty years of listening to a door murmur in its sleep.

Someone outside his shop, barefoot in the frost, was talking to the door.

And the door, for the first time in twenty years, was answering.
