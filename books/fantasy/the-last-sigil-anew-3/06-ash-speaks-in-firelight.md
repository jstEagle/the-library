## 6. Ash Speaks in Firelight

For three days, Oscar Thackeray told himself the child was simply quiet.

This was a lie he maintained with the same care he applied to his window display—diligently, every morning, and with a small private knowledge that it would not survive contact with the day. Ash did not speak. Not in any tongue Oscar knew, which was two: the trade-tongue of the city, and the older, softer language that lived only in his own head and in the murmurs beneath his floorboards. But the child *communicated*. Abundantly. With hands, with eyebrows, with a whole silent theatre of expression that made Oscar feel, by comparison, like a man who had spent twenty years learning to talk and had somehow gotten worse at it.

On the first morning, Ash pointed at the kettle with an air of such wounded dignity that Oscar laughed for the first time in weeks and made tea.

On the second morning, Ash rearranged his entire shelf of luck-charms by color.

"They're not supposed to be organized," Oscar said, watching from the counter with the helplessness of a man watching someone alphabetize his thoughts. "They're supposed to look accidental. Like I just happened to make them. If a Warden comes in and sees a rainbow gradient of protective amulets, they start asking questions about *intent*."

Ash looked at him. Then, deliberately, moved one charm out of place.

"Better?"

A nod. A pause. Another charm nudged askew, with the gravity of a general adjusting a battle line.

"You're going to be trouble," Oscar told them, and found he meant it as a compliment.

By the third night, he could no longer pretend the quiet was simple. He closed the shop early—*closed early*, something he hadn't done since the year the Wardens repainted the street signs—and lit the lamp over the workbench, and lifted the floorboard, and carried the brazier's coal up into the light where he could watch it properly.

The coal sat in its iron cradle, glowing the deep orange of a hearth at the end of a good evening. It should have been steady. It was not steady. It pulsed, faintly, the way a sleeping animal breathes when the dream has gone bad.

And Ash sat cross-legged on the floor beside it, bare feet tucked under them, watching the coal with an expression Oscar recognized because he wore it himself every single night: the look of someone listening to a friend describe an illness.

"It talks to you," Oscar said. It wasn't quite a question anymore.

Ash nodded. Then they held up one hand, flat, level, and slowly let it sink toward the floor.

"The fire's low." Oscar rubbed his jaw. "I know. I feed it. Every week, same as I have for twenty years. Charcoal, ash-wood, a spoonful of the old oil when I can get it without questions. It doesn't starve. It just... rests."

Ash shook their head—not dismissively, but with the patient sorrow of a child correcting an adult who has been wrong for a very long time. They pointed at the coal. Then they pointed at themselves. Then they drew a circle in the air with one finger, closed their fist inside it, and pulled the fist away.

Oscar stared. "It's not getting what you give it."

Another nod. And then—the thing that turned his stomach over quietly, the way ice turns over in a spring river—Ash reached out and touched the edge of the coal's glow, and the flame leaned toward them. Not flickered. *Leaned*, like a plant toward a window. Like a dog hearing its name.

"Don't—" Oscar was on his knees before he'd decided to move, hand hovering, not quite pulling the child back, because the warmth coming off the coal had changed too. It wasn't the dry, banked heat he'd tended for two decades. It was *glad*. He hadn't known fire could be glad. He'd forgotten. "Ash. Careful. It's old magic, it doesn't know its own strength, it could—"

Ash looked up at him over the glow, and their eyes were lit from underneath, amber and absolutely unafraid.

"...or it could know exactly what it's doing," Oscar finished weakly. "Right. Of course. Why would anything about you be simple."

* * *

He made supper while the coal breathed on the workbench, because terror is easier to carry with your hands full. Barley and onions and the last of the smoked fish, cooked in the back room on the ordinary stove, the lawful stove, the stove that had never once leaned toward anybody.

Ash ate like the starving do—fast, then catching themselves, then slow and deliberate, as though remembering that food would come again tomorrow and there was no need to fight it for survival. Oscar pushed his own bowl across without comment. The child pushed half of it back. This negotiation repeated twice more before they both gave up and split the difference.

Afterward, Ash wiped the bowl with bread down to the glaze, set it down, folded their hands, and looked at Oscar with the unmistakable posture of Someone About To Explain Something.

"Oh good," Oscar said. "I love being lectured by an eight-year-old."

Ash's mouth twitched. Then they began.

It was slow going. They had no shared words for most of it, so they built the meaning the way children build towers—one piece at a time, testing each for wobble. Ash pointed at the coal: *fire*. At the floorboards: *below*. At their own chest, both palms pressed flat over the heart: *hunger*—no, wait, Oscar thought, watching again, *listening*, or maybe both at once, hunger that listens, listening that hungers. They were the same word down there. He was almost sure of it.

Then Ash stood, went to the trapdoor, and laid both hands flat against the stone lip of the hidden stair—the door itself, iron-banded oak older than the shop, older than the street, older probably than the idea of streets. And they spoke aloud.

Three syllables. Low and round and strange in a child's voice, like river stones turning over in deep water.

Every hair on Oscar's arms stood up.

Because he *knew* those syllables. He'd heard them for twenty years, muffled through six inches of floorboard and oak, at odd hours, in dreams, in the space between sleeping and waking where he'd always assumed his mind was making things up to keep him company. The door talked in its sleep. He'd told himself that. The door muttered nonsense, the way old houses settle, and he'd listened because listening was all he had, and he'd never once—*never once*—let himself believe the sounds were words.

They were words. Ash was saying them back. And below the floor, faint but unmistakable, the door said them again—a call and answer, a greeting, a voice recognizing a voice.

"What did you say?" Oscar asked. His voice came out rough. "What did you just say?"

Ash considered how to answer. Then they walked to the shop's front window, where the shutter stood open to the lamplit street outside, and pointed through it—at the cobbles, the signposts, the Warden-posted notice board with its neat printed edicts. *Here.* Then they walked to the far wall of the shop, past the shelves of harmless charms, to the plastered brick that hid nothing at all, and pointed at that instead. And then they made the gesture Oscar already dreaded: the circle in the air, the fist drawn through it, the wall between here and elsewhere treated like a curtain.

*Through.*

"No." Oscar heard himself say it before he'd decided anything at all. "No, that's—that's not what's down there. That's not what any of this is."

Ash tilted their head. Patient. Unmoved. The particular immovability of children and of stones.

"There's nothing through there," Oscar said, and now he was talking faster, which he knew—he *knew*—was a bad sign, because after twenty years of measured shopkeeper's calm, fast talking was the sound a man made when the floor was tilting. "There's a cellar. There's a sealed door that leads to a cellar, and the seal keeps everyone safe, and the fire is a ward and a promise, and when Merrin and I built it we—"

He stopped. Saying Merrin's name aloud felt like stepping on a stair that wasn't there.

Ash watched him stop. And then, gently, terribly, they pointed at the coal. *Fire.* They spread their hands, showing its smallness. *Little.* They mimed eating—hand to mouth, again, again—and then showed empty palms. *No food.* Then they pointed at the coal once more and let their flat hand sink, slowly, slowly toward the floor.

*Dying.*

"I feed it," Oscar whispered.

Ash shook their head. They pointed at the coal, then at their own mouth, then shook their head harder. Whatever he gave it, whatever he'd been giving it for twenty years—it wasn't what the fire ate. It was kindling for a hearth that needed something else entirely. It was soup ladled into a locked room.

The coal pulsed. Below the floor, the door murmured, long and low, a sentence this time rather than a word, and Ash listened with their whole body, eyes half-closed, lips moving faintly as though tasting each sound before swallowing it.

When they opened their eyes, they looked at Oscar with something close to apology.

They raised one hand, palm out. They walked it slowly through the air, away from the shop, away from the city, out past the walls and the checkpoints and the notices, out to some horizon only they could see. Then they turned back and pointed at him, and at themselves, and at the coal.

*Home,* said the gesture, plain as speech. *It goes home. We can take it home.*

"There is no home," Oscar said.

It came out louder than he intended. Louder than the shop, maybe; he glanced instinctively at the shutters. When he turned back, Ash hadn't flinched, hadn't stepped back, hadn't done any of the things a child does when a grown man raises his voice. They just stood there in the firelight with their terrible patient eyes, waiting for him to finish being afraid so they could continue being right.

"There is no home," Oscar said again, quieter. "That's the whole point. That's what the sealing *was*. The country's gone, Ash. Not conquered, not renamed—gone. Struck off. There are no maps because there's nothing to map. The capital's a quarry now. The king's line ended in a cellar, and I helped build the lock on the cellar door, and the entire arrangement—the entire last twenty years of my life—rests on the understanding that nobody, ever, needs to go through. Least of all the fire."

Ash frowned. Not at him—at his words, hunting through them. Then they did something that undid him completely: they crossed the room, took his hand, and pressed his palm flat against their own chest, over the heart. Under his fingers, faint as a second pulse, something answered. Warmth. Old and banked and familiar.

The same warmth as the coal.

Oscar snatched his hand back as if burned, though nothing burned. He looked at his palm. He looked at the child. He looked at the coal, which had leaned toward Ash again without shame, and at the floorboards, behind which the door hummed contentment like a cat.

"You're not a messenger," he said slowly. The words tasted like cold iron. "You're not carrying news *to* the fire."

Ash smiled—small, tired, older than eight years old—and tapped their own chest twice.

"You're carrying the fire *from* somewhere. You're..." He couldn't say it. His mouth refused the shape of it. "You're part of the door."

Outside, somewhere down the street, a Warden patrol bell rang the hour. Nine o'clock. Lawful hours, lawful city, lawful man selling lawful trinkets to lawful people, and none of it—none of it—had ever been true, except the part where he kept something alive in the dark and called it peace.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat on the stairs with a cup of tea gone cold in his hands, and Ash slept curled against the brazier's warmth like a cat in a sunbeam, and Oscar conducted the argument he'd been avoiding for three days, out loud, in a whisper, because silence had stopped working.

"She's wrong," he told the dark. "Or she's mistaken. Or she's been sent, and this is a test, and the correct answer is the one I've given for twenty years, which is *nothing comes through, nothing goes through, everyone stays safe.*" He turned the cup. "She's a child. She's a mute child who appeared out of a rainstorm speaking door-language, and I've taken her into my home, and I'm sitting here letting her convince me the world works differently than I've—than we—"

Than Merrin and I built it. There was the stair that wasn't there again. He kept stepping on it.

Below the floor, the door sighed. Long, low, patient. It sounded, he thought, exactly like a friend who has heard this argument many times and intends to win it simply by outlasting him.

"The seal is fine," Oscar said.

The door hummed a note that was almost amused.

"The seal is *fine*. Twenty years, no breaches, no incidents. Ilva Brann has inspected this shop eleven times and found nothing but a man who sells luck charms and waters his ferns too often. The Council has no reason—Merrin has no reason—to think anything is changing." He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Is anything changing?"

Silence. Then, soft as banked coals shifting, the door spoke a single syllable. Ash stirred in their sleep, frowning at it even unconscious, and murmured something back, and the door fell quiet, comforted.

Oscar put his face in his hands.

Because here was the thing he could not argue with, the fact that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: he had heard the door grow quieter these past years. He'd told himself he imagined it. He had fed the fire every week for twenty years and told himself the feeding took less time than it used to because he'd gotten efficient. He had watched the coal's glow shorten season by season and told himself it was his eyes. Twenty years of small explanations, stacked like sandbags against a flood, and one barefoot child had walked through all of them in three days without saying a single word he understood.

"Fine," he whispered to the dark, to the door, to the child asleep against the warmth. "Fine. But we don't do anything rash. We don't open anything. We don't go anywhere. If the fire is starving—if it's really starving—then I fix the feeding. Quietly. The way I've fixed everything for twenty years. Nobody finds out, nothing changes, and in the morning I figure out what a fire like you actually eats, and I find a way to get it without alerting a single soul."

From the workbench, the coal pulsed—once, twice—in a rhythm he slowly recognized, with a sinking feeling, as skepticism.

"Don't give me that. I've kept you alive for twenty years on charcoal and stubbornness. I can manage a little longer."

The coal dimmed slightly. Sulking, if a coal could sulk. Below the floor, the door made a sound that was definitely, unmistakably, a laugh.

"Oh, wonderful," Oscar muttered. "Twenty years alone with you and now you've got someone to translate, and suddenly you're *funny*."

But he was already moving, already clearing the workbench, already reaching for the good tools—the ones he hadn't touched since the sealing, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of a drawer, kept the way you keep a dead friend's coat. Because denial, he was discovering, is a fuel like any other: burn it, and what's left is work.

If the fire needed feeding, he would learn its diet. If the door was weakening, he would strengthen it. If a mute child had walked out of a rainstorm carrying the last ember of a country that no longer existed—

Well. Then he would need a charm that could hide her from the sharpest noses in the Warden order.

And he would need to make it *work*.

Oscar unwrapped the oilcloth, and the old tools caught the firelight, and for the first time in twenty years his hands did not shake.
