## 7. The Charm That Shouldn't Work

The trouble with lying for twenty years, Oscar Thackeray thought, was that you got good at it in all the wrong ways.

He could lie with his hands now. His fingers had learned to make charms that were nothing but brass and glass and wishful thinking — little suns that caught lamplight, bells that rang only when the wind wanted them to, knots of ribbon that promised luck without any intention of delivering it. The customers loved them. The Wardens inspected them, found nothing, and left. A charm that did nothing was the safest thing in the world.

And this morning, standing at his workbench with a sleeping child curled on the cot upstairs like a coal wrapped in blankets, Oscar was about to break that safety into pieces.

He'd told himself he wouldn't. He'd said it aloud, actually, the night before, to no one — "I am not doing it" — in the firm voice of a man arguing with a river. The river had not been persuaded. Neither had he.

Because Ash was *loud*.

Not out loud. Ash hadn't made a sound since arriving, except once, in sleep: a small cracked syllable that had sounded, horribly, like the door's own murmur. But underneath the hearing of things, beneath the floorboards where the brazier breathed its hidden fire, the child shone. To anything with Warden-sense — the trained sensitivity the order cultivated in its officers, the ability to feel working magic the way a fisherman feels rain coming in his knees — Ash must have been a bonfire lit in a dark field. For three days they had been in his shop, and for three days Oscar had waited for boots on his stairs.

It was only a matter of time. And time, he had learned, was the one thing the door beneath his feet never promised anyone.

* * *

He cleared the workbench properly, which he hadn't done in twenty years. Swept the false-charm scraps into their drawer. Took out the tools he kept at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, untouched so long that unwrapping them felt like disturbing a grave.

His hands remembered anyway.

That was the cruel joke of it. Twenty years of pretending to be a harmless tinkerer, and the moment he set the silver wire in the little clamp, his fingers moved like they'd only been waiting. Loop, turn, feed, close. He could hear old Master Ellery's voice from before the fall — *the shape remembers the meaning, boy, don't fight it* — and hear Merrin laughing beside him at the same bench, both of them seventeen and certain the kingdom would last forever.

He stopped. Breathed. Started again.

The charm itself was simple, in the way that avalanches are simple. A shield-knot: three interlocked rings of silver, bound with a thread pulled — he winced even thinking it — pulled from the hem of Ash's own blanket, because a shield must love what it shelters or it will not hold. Around the rings he wound a hair-fine wire of cold-iron, and around that, a whisper of the brazier's warmth. Not flame. Never flame. Just warmth, borrowed, the way you'd borrow the smell of bread to remind someone of home.

The purpose was modest. Hide the child. Muffle the shine. Make Ash read as nothing — as dull and unremarkable as every other orphan in a city full of them, invisible to any Warden who walked past.

What it was supposed to do, and what it would do instead — these turned out to be different things entirely. But he didn't know that yet. Hope is very quiet when it works. You only hear it later, breaking.

* * *

He worked through the afternoon between customers. Mrs. Hallow came in for a luck-bell for her sister's new shop and complained, fondly, about the price; Oscar gave her a discount and a ribbon for free, and she looked at him a moment too long — she'd been buying his charms for eleven years, ever since her husband died, and she had a way of looking at him like a woman reading the last page of a letter first — but she went, and the bell chimed behind her, and the shop settled back into its hush.

Twice he climbed the stairs to check on Ash. Twice the child was exactly where he'd left them, a small heap under the blanket, breathing slow. The second time, Ash's eyes were open — dark, watchful, ancient in a face that couldn't have seen more than ten winters — and Oscar nearly dropped the tray of tea.

"You," he said, recovering, setting the tray down, "are going to give me a heart condition."

Ash blinked. Reached out one finger toward the thing in Oscar's pocket — because of course the child knew, of course they could feel it, half-finished magic in the pocket of a man walking up stairs. The finger trembled slightly. Not fear, Oscar realized. Recognition. Like the child looking at a piece of a song they'd lost.

"It's not for showing," Oscar said gruffly, though there was no one to show it to and no law against showing it except all of them. "It's for keeping you dull. Dull as ditchwater. The dullest child in the city." He handed over the tea. "Drink that."

Ash drank. Then, with great solemnity, held up the empty cup as if returning an honor.

Oscar laughed despite himself, and went back down to finish the thing that would ruin him.

* * *

Dusk. Lamplight. The shop closed, the sign turned, the bolt drawn. Down below the floorboards the brazier murmured in its sleep, and Oscar lifted the loose board and let a thread of its warmth rise up to touch the charm one final time — the blessing, the oldest part of the craft, the part that wasn't technique at all but permission. *You may work,* he told it silently. *One small kindness. Nothing more.*

Then he closed the rings.

The sound was not a sound. It was the absence of one — the whole shop going suddenly, perfectly still, the lampflame standing straight as a soldier, dust hanging in the air like held breath. For three heartbeats nothing happened at all.

Then every charm in the shop woke up.

Luck-bells rang without wind. Ribbon-luck knots stood up on their shelves like little soldiers presenting arms. The glass suns in the window blazed with reflected light that had no source, and down below the floor, muffled but unmistakable, the brazier roared — a great joyful rush of flame, like a banked fire hearing an old friend's voice after twenty years.

"*No no no no—*" Oscar grabbed the charm off the bench and it was hot, singing-hot, alive in his palm, the three rings spinning slowly of their own accord and throwing off light like a coin catching the sun. It worked. Stars help him, it worked — he could feel it already reaching out and wrapping Ash in silence, folding the child's brightness down under layers of nothing, dull as ditchwater, invisible —

— and visible everywhere else.

Because a real charm doesn't just work. It *announces*. Every charm he had ever sold, every harmless trinket in every pocket and on every sill within a mile, was at that moment straining toward his shop like flowers toward light, ringing and glowing and humming, telling the whole street, the whole quarter, *here, here, here is the real thing.*

Oscar did the fastest thinking of his life. He smothered the brazier's roar with the iron lid, snuffed the lamp, and clapped both hands around the spinning rings — and the moment his flesh touched silver, the charm quieted, recognizing its maker, settling against his palm warm as a heartbeat.

Outside, the street had gone quiet too. The particular quiet of people stopping to look.

He stood in the dark, holding twenty years of careful lying in his fist, listening to doors open along the lane. Somewhere a shutter creaked. Somewhere two voices murmured. And somewhere — closest, from the house across the way — Mrs. Hallow's window scraped upward, and her voice floated out, mild and pleasant as ever:

"Lovely evening, Mr. Thackeray."

"...Evening, Mrs. Hallow," he called back, hating how normal his voice almost managed to be.

"Your shop was glowing," she said. "Just now. Like a festival lantern."

"A trick of the glass. I've been rearranging the window displays."

"Mm," said Mrs. Hallow. The pause that followed was long enough to hold a great many things. Then, pleasantly: "Well. Goodnight, then."

The window slid shut. Oscar stood in the dark and did not move for a long, long time.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat at the workbench with the shield-knot cooling in his hand, turning it over, cataloguing everything he'd done wrong, and arriving always at the same conclusion: everything. A real charm should have been grounded, veiled, released in stages over a week of nights — he'd known that once, and knowing it and being desperate were two different countries, and desperation had won the border war.

By gray morning he had a plan. Move the coal. Move Ash. Get ahead of whatever came next, because something would come next, it always did—

A knock at the door. Two soft raps. Dawn-gray light, and Mrs. Hallow on his step, holding a covered basket and wearing her church face, the one that meant charity or judgment, whichever fit.

"I couldn't sleep," she said, when he opened up. "Thinking about your window display." She held out the basket. "I brought bread. You look terrible, Mr. Thackeray."

"Kind of you," he said, taking it, because refusing would be worse.

She didn't leave. She stood on his step in the early light and looked past him, into the dim shop, with those eleven-years-of-reading-his-labels eyes. "My husband used to say you can tell a man by what he keeps under his floorboards." A small smile. "Everyone keeps something, I expect. Even honest men."

"Especially honest men," Oscar said.

"Mm." She adjusted her shawl. "There are Wardens on Cordwainer Street this morning. Asking questions. About lights in windows." She said it lightly, like reporting weather. Then, quieter, with something in her voice he couldn't name and would spend many sleepless nights afterward trying to: "Eleven years I've bought your charms, Mr. Thackeray. My Thomas carried your luck-bell in his coat when he died. Whatever you're keeping under your floorboards—" her chin came up, and her eyes were wet and fierce all at once— "I hope it's worth what it costs you."

She went down the steps before he could answer, and turned at the corner, and was gone.

He stood holding the bread until it went cold.

It was mid-morning, with Ash awake upstairs and the plan half-formed — move tonight, north, before the net finished weaving — that the boots finally came. Not up his stairs. Past his window. Four Wardens in their gray coats, unhurried, walking the lane with the terrible patience of men following a paper trail, and at their head a young officer consulting a slate, and beside the officer, pointing — pointing at his door, her face composed and apologetic and dry-eyed —

— Mrs. Hallow.

She didn't look up at his window. Perhaps she couldn't. The Wardens reached his step. The knock, when it came, was nothing like hers: three raps, official, final.

Oscar looked at the ceiling, where a small mute child sat above a dying fire and a sealed door, and at the shield-knot in his hand, warm and faithful and far too late.

"Well," he said quietly, to no one, "that's that."

He put the charm in his pocket, picked up the poker, and went to answer the door as the most harmless man in the city — knowing, as the bolts slid back, that the harmless man had died sometime last night in a shower of waking light, and whatever walked to the door now would have to be somebody new.
