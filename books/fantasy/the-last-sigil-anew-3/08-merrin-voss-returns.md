## 8. Merrin Voss Returns

Merrin Voss had not stood in front of Thackeray's Charms & Curiosities in twenty years, and he was surprised to find that his hands remembered the door.

They remembered the trick of the latch — lift and turn, never push, because Oscar had oiled it wrong on purpose to keep the bell silent for late customers. They remembered the exact height of the step, worn hollow in the middle by two decades of boots. They remembered, most treacherously of all, how it felt to walk through this doorway as a friend, back when the word *friend* had still meant something sturdier than *the man you built a prison with*.

He made himself wait outside for a full minute. That was protocol. A Chief Inspector did not scurry. He let the morning do its work around him: the smell of frying onions from the cookshop next door, the clatter of the lamplighters' cart, a pair of children chasing a hoop down the cobbles with the shrieking delight of children who had never once wondered what lived under their city.

One of them skidded to a stop at the shop's window, nose pressed to the glass, staring at a shelf of painted wooden birds.

"They're lucky," Merrin said before he could stop himself.

The child looked at him with frank suspicion, the way all children looked at Wardens, and ran off.

Lucky birds. Oscar's little joke. Every charm in that window was harmless as a hymn — luck-birds and hearth-stones and love-knots woven from horsehair, none of them holding so much as a whisper of working. Twenty years ago they had sat up nights designing them together, laughing into their cups, because if the law was going to strip magic out of the world then someone ought to make sure the world kept a little of the shape of it. *Charms for the lawful,* Oscar had called the line, and Merrin had signed off on the licensing himself, first signature of the new order, and told himself it was mercy.

It was always mercy, at the beginning. That was the trap. Nobody ever built a cage out of cruelty; cruelty was too honest to hold anything for long.

Merrin straightened his coat, settled his badge where it caught the light — silver, heavy, shaped like a closed eye — and went in.

The bell above the door rang anyway. Of course it did. Oscar would have fixed the latch years ago, or broken it worse.

"Welcome to Thackeray's," said the voice from behind the counter, warm and easy, the shopkeeper's voice, the mask. "Luck-birds are three for two this week, and I'd advise against the love-knots unless you're prepared for consequences."

Then Oscar looked up, and the warmth died in his face like a snuffed candle.

He hadn't changed much. That was the unfair thing about him. His hair had gone grey at the temples in a way that suggested distinguished rather than tired, and there were new lines around his eyes, but he still had that open, unguarded face — the face of a man who had never learned to lie with anything except words. Merrin had spent twenty years learning every other kind of lying. Looking at that face was like looking at a sun after years underground.

"Chief Inspector Voss." Oscar's voice was level. His hands, below the counter's sightline, were not. Merrin didn't need to see them. "To what does the lawful city owe the honor?"

"A routine inspection." Merrin let the door swing shut behind him. The bell settled. Somewhere beneath the floorboards — so faint that any man but him would have called it imagination — something held its breath. "There have been reports."

"There are always reports."

"Yes," Merrin said. "I write half of them myself."

That earned him nothing. Oscar just watched him across the counter, patient as a stone wall, and Merrin understood that whatever happened in this room today, it would begin with the two of them standing exactly this far apart, pretending.

"Then inspect," Oscar said.

* * *

The inspection took an hour because Merrin made it take an hour.

He examined the ledgers, which were immaculate. He examined the stockroom, which smelled of cedar shavings and glue and the particular dusty sweetness of wood shavings left too long, and he ran his thumb along a half-carved luck-bird and found the wing-angles perfect. He checked the chimney flue for illegal smoke and found only the residue of ordinary fires, banked low, economical. He checked under the counter where a second ledger might hide, and behind the plaster where a sigil might be scratched, and in the cellar stairs where contraband grimoires might sit wrapped in oilcloth.

Oscar followed him through it all without a word, hands folded, wearing the expression of a man watching a slow-witted nephew search for his own spectacles.

There were moments, in between, when the mask slipped. When Merrin asked about the new shipment from the coast and Oscar answered with a dry aside about the shipping agent's honesty, and for one sentence they were two men talking again, and the shop was warm, and it was twenty years ago. Then one of them would remember what they were, and the sentence would end, and the cold would come back in.

Merrin saved the floor for last.

He knelt in the middle of the shop's main room, in the patch of light from the window, and laid his palm flat against the boards. He did it slowly, deliberately, the way a Warden-Sergeant would, and felt Oscar go very still behind him.

Under his hand: oak, joists, packed earth, cold stone.

And under that —

Heat. Old heat. Banked and starved and patient, the way a coal holds fire inside itself through a whole night of snow, asking nothing, promising everything. It turned over in its sleep beneath the floor like a great animal dreaming, and the dream leaked up through the grain of the wood and into Merrin's palm, and for one unguarded second he was kneeling in a burning hall with ash falling like grey snow and a dying king pressing a coal into his hands and saying *keep it, keep it, keep it alive* —

He pulled his hand back. Stood. Brushed off his knee with precise, bureaucratic care.

"Floor's sound," he said.

"Glad to hear it," said Oscar. "Cost me a fortune to repair."

"You haven't repaired a board in this room in twenty years."

"No," Oscar agreed pleasantly. "But I tell the joiner I have, and it keeps the price of telling him so high that he never checks."

Despite himself — despite everything — Merrin laughed. It came out of him rough and rusty, a hinge long unoiled, and he saw Oscar's mouth twitch in answer before it flattened again, and God, it would be so easy, it would be so unbearably easy to stay here in this shop trading old jokes until the Council's patience ran out and men with less history than the two of them came to do what needed doing.

Which was, of course, why Merrin had come.

* * *

"Tea?" Oscar offered, when the inspection was done and the ledger signed. "I've got proper leaf. Not the Warden ration."

"I know what your tea is, Oscar. I helped you smuggle the first tin past the customs house."

"So you did." Something flickered in his eyes — not quite softness. Memory wearing softness's clothes. "Well?"

"One cup."

They sat at the little table by the back window, the same table where they had once drafted the seal's design on the backs of licensing forms, and Oscar poured, and for a while neither spoke, and the steam rose between them like a small domestic ghost.

"The child," Merrin said finally. "The one who came to you in the rain. Barefoot. Mute, they say, except not."

Oscar set down the pot with exaggerated calm. "You've been reading reports about my customers."

"I've been reading reports about *you*. There's a difference, and the difference is the reason I'm sitting here drinking tea instead of arresting you." Merrin wrapped both hands around his cup, because they wanted to shake, and he would die before letting them. "The Council has a name for what's under your floor, Oscar. Do you want to hear it? They call it *the asset*. Not the flame. Not the last dragon-fire in the world. An asset. Item nine on the agenda of the quarterly session, filed between grain tariffs and bridge repairs."

Oscar said nothing. The silence had a texture Merrin remembered well — it was the silence of a man deciding how much of the truth to spend.

"And the Council has decided," Merrin went on, "that a weakening seal around a living fire beneath a populated street is an unacceptable risk. There was a vote. Eleven to four." He smiled, and hated the smile. "I voted with the eleven."

"You would." Flat. Final.

"I voted with the eleven," Merrin repeated, quieter, "so that I would be the one sent to carry out the decision. Because the alternative was sending Sergeant Brann. Or worse, sending no one, and having the Council send *engineers*." He looked up. "Do you understand me? They're not going to raid you, Oscar. Raids are loud. They're going to bring the quiet men with the iron tools and the null-salts, and they're going to put out the fire the way you'd put out a kitchen blaze, and they'll file it under hazard abatement, and in fifty years nobody will even remember there was ever a door."

The tea went cold between them.

"How long?" Oscar asked.

"Less than a month. Maybe less than that. There's a survey team mapping the foundations now." Merrin drank, though the tea had gone bitter on his tongue. "I came to tell you because — "

"Because we're friends?"

"*Because I built it.*" The words cracked out of him louder than he meant, and he lowered his voice with effort, down to a rasp. "I keyed that seal, Oscar. Every ward, every binding, every lock — my hand, my blood, my fear. And a seal keyed to a man weakens when the man weakens, and I am sixty-one years old and tired in ways sleep doesn't touch, and some night soon I am going to fail, and when I fail, everything under this floor comes up. So yes. You may call it friendship if it helps you sleep. I call it maintenance."

For a long moment Oscar just looked at him. And Merrin saw — with the peculiar clarity that only comes in the presence of the people who knew you before you became yourself — that Oscar was not angry. That was the worst part. He looked sad, and tired, and older than his years, and underneath all of it there was something that might have been pity, and Merrin could have borne hatred much more easily.

"Merrin," Oscar said gently. "What did you think the seal was *for*?"

"To contain—"

"It was never a cage." Oscar leaned forward, and his voice dropped to the register he used for telling frightened children that the thunder was only clouds talking. "We sealed it so it could *rest*. We sealed it so it wouldn't have to burn anymore. You built a door, my friend, and you've spent twenty years calling it a wall, and now you've convinced an entire Council to help you brick it up because you can't stand the idea that something might still be alive in there and *waiting*."

"Waiting for what?"

"For someone to knock," Oscar said simply.

Merrin set down his cup. His hands were steady now, which frightened him more than shaking would have. "Then I'm sorry," he said. "Truly. Because nobody is going to knock. In a few weeks, the quiet men come with their iron tools, and the fire goes out, and the door goes to rubble, and the world stays lawful and safe and dead. And I will sign the order myself, and I will sleep afterward, because I have learned how. It's the one thing the last twenty years taught me."

He stood. Straightened his coat. Settled the badge.

At the door, he paused with his hand on the frame, not looking back.

"There's a boy in the marsh camps," he said, "a smuggler's lad, who carries messages for anyone who pays in secrets. If a certain shopkeeper needed to move something small and hot and precious out of this city before the survey team finishes its maps — the ferryman at Unmarked Water takes strange cargo and asks fewer questions than I do." He opened the door onto the noise of the morning street. "Not that I said so. Inspection's logged as clean. Lucky birds and all."

"Merrin."

He stopped. He couldn't help it. Twenty years, and the sound of his own name in that voice still stopped him like a hand on the chest.

"You could knock," Oscar said quietly. "While you still remember how."

Merrin stepped out into the sunlight and let the bell ring shut behind him, and did not answer, and walked back toward the lawful world with the ghost of heat still sleeping in his right palm — and the terrible, growing knowledge that he had just handed his oldest friend a map to the one crime the Council would never forgive.

Behind him, in the shop, faintly, almost hopefully, something under the floorboards stirred in its sleep.

And began, very softly, to glow.
