## 9. Running on Empty Streets

Oscar had always imagined that if this night ever came—and he had imagined it more times than he cared to admit—he would be calm about it. He would move through the shop with the steady hands of a man who had rehearsed for twenty years. He would pack the lantern, wake the child, and walk out into the dark like a character in one of his own better chapters.

What actually happened was that he dropped the iron lantern twice, swore in three languages (only one of them lawful), and got his coat stuck on the coat hook like a man fighting an octopus.

"Oscar," said Ash, from the doorway of the back room, watching him with the flat, patient expression children reserve for adults making fools of themselves.

"I'm fine," Oscar lied, wrenching free. "This is all part of it."

The brazier beneath the floorboards had been fed its last meal an hour ago: a spoonful of ash from the hearth above, a drop of lamp oil, and—because Ash had insisted, pressing their small hand over his until he understood—a single hair from each of their heads. The coal at its center had brightened to the color of a struck match held close to the eye, and when Oscar lifted it out with the long brass tongs he'd kept for exactly this purpose, it did not cool. It sat in the bottom of the iron lantern like a sleeping animal, breathing light.

It weighed almost nothing. That frightened him most of all. Twenty years of feeding it, guarding it, lying awake listening to it hum under his feet, and now it fit in one hand, small as an apple, old as a kingdom.

Ash carried nothing. Children on the run, Oscar was learning, needed less than you'd think: no bundle, no spare cloak, just the word *yes*, which Ash could still not say aloud but could write in frost, in spilled flour, in the fog of the shop window. They had written it there tonight, big as a dinner plate, before he'd wiped it away with his sleeve.

"One rule," Oscar said, crouching to their height at the back door. "If I say *run*, you run. Not toward me. Away from me. Toward the river. There's a cooper's yard with a broken gate two streets down—if we're separated, you go there and you wait until dawn. Can you remember that?"

Ash nodded. Then, after a moment's thought, they reached up and took hold of two of Oscar's fingers. Just that. Held on.

Oscar's throat did something inconvenient. "Right," he managed. "Well. Yes. Fingers are also permitted."

He opened the door onto the rain-washed dark, and the city of Vellmark—the city with no proper name, because names were what the Wardens had outlawed first—swallowed them whole.

* * *

Ash knew the streets by their colors.

The lamplighters' oil burned gold, ordinary and dull, the color of porridge. But underneath, everywhere, ran other lights that only they could see: the green ghost of the river sliding beneath the cobblestones, the blue threads of the water pipes, the faint orange worms of banked hearths sleeping behind shuttered windows. The whole city glowed softly, like a bed of coals raked over with ash. Like something waiting.

And ahead—faint, flickering, wrong—red.

Red meant Wardens. Red meant Warden-sense, the cold net they cast from their lanterns of licensed flame, sweeping the streets for anything warm that shouldn't be. Ash had felt it once already, through the walls of the shop, and had learned what it was the way you learn what a dog is: instantly, completely, in your bones.

They tugged Oscar's fingers twice. Left.

"Hm?" Oscar whispered, bending down. His breath made a small ghost in the cold.

Ash pointed left, then made the shape with their hands that they both knew now: fingers spread wide, closing slowly. *Net.*

Oscar looked left, then right, then left again, and Ash watched him do the arithmetic of a hunted man—the quick darting eyes, the jaw working. Then he nodded and turned them down a narrow alley that smelled of cabbage water and cats.

"Good catch," he breathed. "You're better at this than me, and I've been dreading this night for two decades. There's something deeply unfair about that."

Ash didn't smile, exactly. But something in their chest loosened, the way it had begun to loosen these past few days, around meals and mended socks and Oscar's terrible humming. Belonging, they had learned, was a thing that could be taken away. They kept theirs small and hidden, folded up tight like the coal in its lantern.

But they kept it.

* * *

The first checkpoint was where Oscar had known it would be: Tanner's Bridge, the only crossing over the millrace within a mile, its arch hung with two Warden lanterns whose red sense-net spread across the water like oil.

He stopped in the shadow of a dye-works wall and pressed himself flat, pulling Ash against the cold brick beside him. Across the water, four Wardens stood stamping their boots against the chill, checking papers by lamplight. A fishwife argued pleasantly with one of them; her cart of herring was being poked at with a licensed prod.

"Right," Oscar murmured. "Options. We can wait for the shift change—they rotate at the fourth bell, always have—or we can try the weir, which is suicide, or..." He trailed off, because Ash was looking at him with an expression he was beginning to dread, the one that preceded trouble. "...or you have an idea, don't you."

Ash held up one finger. Waited.

Then they pointed at themselves, at the bridge, and opened their mouth in a perfect, silent imitation of a scream.

"No," Oscar said immediately. "Absolutely not. No child of mine is going to walk up to four armed Wardens and—" He stopped. "You're not my child. Legally. Which is somehow worse. No."

Ash tilted their head. And then they did the thing they had only done twice since arriving: they spoke—not in words, but in warmth. They took Oscar's hand and pressed it flat against their own chest, and let him feel what they felt: the coal in the lantern, dimming. Starving. The door's voice, which only Ash could hear clearly, saying what it had been saying for days now in its slow stone language: *less, less, less.* Every hour they stood here was an hour off the fire's life. An hour off whatever time remained before the seal failed and the Council came to snuff everything out.

Oscar felt all of it pass through the child's small hand into his palm, and he sat down heavily against the dye-works wall, right there in the wet street, like a puppet with cut strings.

"That's cheating," he whispered.

Ash shrugged, eloquently.

"The plan," Oscar said at last, rubbing his face with both hands, "is that you walk out onto the bridge alone, looking lost—which requires no acting whatsoever—you get their attention, and when they come to check your papers, which you do not have, you point back this way, toward the fish market, as though someone abandoned you there. They will send two of them to look. Two is fewer than four. Meanwhile I cross with the lantern while they're busy being confused by a mute child who speaks Old Tongue at them, which will frighten them more than any sword, frankly, half of them can't read." He exhaled. "And then we meet at the cooper's yard. If you're not there by dawn, I'm coming back for you, rules be hanged, and we'll both be caught, and it'll serve us right."

Ash nodded solemnly. Then, quickly, before Oscar could change his mind, they kissed his cheek—once, briskly, the way you'd stamp a document—and stepped out of the shadow.

Oscar watched the smallest person he had ever loved walk away from him across an open bridge, straight toward the red light, and discovered that terror and purpose, properly mixed, taste exactly the same: copper and smoke, and your own heart counting bells.

* * *

It worked. Of course it worked.

Later, crouched in the reeking dark of the cooper's yard among stacked barrels that smelled of vinegar and old wine, Oscar replayed it: Ash walking out into the lantern-light, small and barefoot and deliberately, heartbreakingly lost. The Wardens converging, their red nets folding inward. One of them kneeling, lifting his visor, speaking gently—and Ash pointing, urgently, back toward the fish market, and the Wardens' heads snapping around like weathervanes in wind. Two of them jogging off into the dark after a ghost. And in the confusion, in the beautiful human mess of it, Oscar crossing the bridge behind a hay cart, the lantern wrapped in sacking against his chest, feeling the coal pulse against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

Now he waited. The fourth bell came and went. The fifth.

*Come on,* he thought, in the direction of the bridge. *Come on, little star. You taught me the word for ember in Old Tongue, you can manage one bridge.*

At the fifth bell plus a hundred heartbeats—Oscar counted; he had always counted—a barrel shifted at the yard's broken gate, and a small shape slipped through the gap, moving with a new confidence that made his chest ache. Ash crossed the yard, climbed into his arms without ceremony, and held up one grubby finger: wait. Then they held up two more.

*Two Wardens,* they mimed, still out there, searching the fish market for a child who had never been there.

"They'll find nothing but herring," Oscar whispered into their hair. "Best lie you ever told, and you didn't say a word of it."

Then Ash went rigid in his arms.

Oscar felt it too, a half-second later: the coal, through the sacking, through his coat, flaring hot against his chest. Not the slow hungry pulse of the past days. A flare—bright and urgent, like a shout.

Ash twisted around to face the east, where the rooftops fell away toward the old quarter, toward the hills beyond, toward the capital that appeared on no map. Their eyes were wide and shining, reflecting a light that wasn't there.

"What?" Oscar breathed. "What is it? What's it saying?"

Ash took his hand and pressed it to their throat, and for the first time since the shop, since the rain, since everything, a sound came out of them—not a word, not yet, but a hum, low and rising, shaped unmistakably like the note the sealed door used to make on quiet nights twenty years ago.

Oscar's blood went cold and then, strangely, warm.

"It knows we're coming," he whispered.

Ash nodded. And somewhere far off across the sleeping city, a bell began to ring—not the fourth bell, not the fifth, but the alarm bell, ragged and urgent, from the direction of Charm Street. From the shop. From the hole in his floorboards that was, by now, being found empty.

Oscar stood, settled Ash on his hip with the ease of long practice he hadn't known he had, and turned west, toward the river and the unmarked water beyond it.

"Well then," he said to the child, to the coal, to the country with no name. "We'd best not keep a door waiting."

* * *

Behind them, the alarm bell rang on, and the red lanterns of Vellmark woke one by one along every street between the cooper's yard and the water—except the one street Oscar chose, because Ash pulled his sleeve twice, firmly, left, and pointed at a gap between buildings no wider than a chimney, glowing faintly green with the ghost of the river below.

The city that had outlawed magic was showing them, its last two criminals, the secret paths it had been keeping lit all along.

Oscar laughed—actually laughed, breathless and disbelieving—as they slipped into the dark.

"Lead on," he said. "I'll follow."
