## 10. The Ferryman of Unmarked Water

Sefa Marlow had three rules, and she was proud of every one of them.

One: never ask a passenger's name. Names were debts waiting to be collected by the wrong people. Two: never cross before moonset, because the Warden patrols changed shift at moonset and dead smugglers ferried no one anywhere. Three: never, under any circumstances, accept coin.

Coin could be traced. Coin could be counted. Coin could be planted on you at a checkpoint by a sergeant with a grudge and a quota. Secrets couldn't. A secret lived in your head, weighed nothing, and if the Wardens searched you from boots to collar they'd never find it — though Sefa privately suspected some of her secrets had gotten so heavy over the years that they slowed her rowing on the upriver pull.

She kept them all in a ledger of memory, filed under the names she didn't ask for. *The woman with the red scarf paid me the location of her brother's grave.* *The tax clerk paid me his superior's gambling debts and wept about it.* *The boy who wasn't a boy paid me the fact that he could turn into a heron, which I still don't entirely believe, but he did it right there in the boat, so.*

Twenty years of secrets, and not one of them spent. That was the fourth rule, the one nobody knew because it was also the first: a secret given freely is worth ten times one pried loose. Sefa had built a whole livelihood on the difference between those two things, and she'd have defended the distinction with her oar if it came to that.

The river itself had no name anymore. It used to be called something lovely — the Silverthread, according to the oldest songs, or maybe the Merrow, depending on which grandmother you asked — but when the country across the water erased its own name from every map, the maps got confused about what to call everything near it. The Wardens called it Border Water Seven. The locals called it the River. Sefa called it hers.

On the night the child came, the fog was lying on the water like something asleep, and Sefa was mending a net she had no intention of using, mostly for the look of it. Fishermen fished. Smugglers mended nets. Nobody questioned either.

She heard them before she saw them — footsteps on the shingle, two sets, one heavy and careful, one light as a dropped leaf. Then a lantern's glow pushing through the fog like a thumb through wet paper.

"Evening," said the man, and then, because people always did this, "I'm looking for—"

"Don't," Sefa said pleasantly. "Rule one."

He stopped. Good. Most people argued, which told her everything she needed to know about most people. This one just stood there getting rained on, holding a lantern in one hand and an iron thing in the other — a lantern inside a lantern, which was a new one — while the child beside him stared at her with eyes that made her think of coals settling in a stove.

"You're the ferryman," the man said.

"I'm a fisherman having a quiet night. If someone were, hypothetically, a ferryman, they'd want to know where you're going. But since I'm not, I can't help you."

"We need to cross." He glanced back down the bank, the way hunted people do, checking a road that was empty and wouldn't stay empty. "Tonight. Before dawn."

"Mm. And what would you pay a hypothetical ferryman? Because I'll tell you now, if you say *coin*, our conversation is over and I go back to my net."

The man's mouth did something complicated. "I have—" He patted his pockets with the optimism of a man who already knew. "I have a charm against damp socks. Genuinely excellent craftsmanship, if I say so myself. Made it myself."

"A charm against damp socks."

"The finest in the city."

"And how has that been going for you, standing in the rain in wet boots?"

He looked down at his own feet. "...I've been running," he said, with dignity. "There hasn't been time for magic."

Despite herself, Sefa liked him. That was dangerous. She'd liked passengers before and it always ended with her doing something stupid like caring whether they survived.

* * *

The child hadn't said anything. Not a word, not a sound, through all of this, and Sefa had learned to read silence the way other people read ledgers. There were silences that meant fear, silences that meant scheming, silences that meant someone had taught a person to be quiet so long ago they'd forgotten how not to be.

This was none of those. This was a listening silence. The child stood there in the fog, barefoot on cold shingle — barefoot, in this weather, and not shivering, which Sefa noted and filed away — and listened to her the way you'd listen to a door behind which something important was happening.

Then the child stepped forward and spoke.

Not in Border-tongue. Not in any tongue Sefa knew, and Sefa knew all the tongues worth knowing along this river, plus three that weren't. The words came out low and round and old, old the way river-stones are old, and they did something to the air. The fog pulled back a handspan, politely, like a cat making room on a chair.

Sefa's hands went still on the net.

She didn't understand the words. That was the strange part. She understood *around* them — felt their shape the way you feel the shape of a key without seeing the lock. And underneath the strangeness was something else, something that reached past her ears and into the place where she kept twenty years of other people's secrets, and it said, in effect: *we know what you carry, and it is not a burden here.*

"What did it say?" the man whispered. "Ash, what did you—"

The child looked at him. Looked at Sefa. Spoke again, slower this time, and this time Sefa caught the cadence of it, the rise and fall, and thought: *that's a payment. That's someone naming a price.*

"She says—" Sefa started, then stopped, because her throat had gone tight. She cleared it. Tried again. "That's not a language. That's *the* language. That's what the river used to be called in." She pointed at the black water. "There's a word in there for this river. I've been carrying it around for years without knowing it was a word. My grandmother sang it. I thought it was nonsense syllables. It's *the river*."

The man had gone very pale. Or possibly very grey; it was hard to tell by lantern-light. "You understand her?"

"No." Sefa laughed, and it came out wrong, wobbly. "No, I don't understand her, I just—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. She was crying. When had that started? She was forty-six years old and she did not cry in front of passengers, rule five, unwritten, absolute, and here she was leaking like a bad hull because a barefoot child had said a sentence in a dead language and one of the words had landed somewhere twenty years of smuggled secrets had never managed to reach.

Because — and here was the thing, the thing she'd never told anyone, the secret she didn't even keep in the ledger because it wasn't a debt anyone owed, it was just hers — Sefa's grandmother hadn't been born on this side of the river. Nobody talked about that. The country across the water had erased itself, and everyone on this bank had very carefully agreed to pretend there had never been anything to erase, including grandmothers, including lullabies, including the word for the river.

And the child had just said the river's true name out loud, gently, like setting down something breakable, and then added four more words after it, and whatever those four words meant, they sounded like *your grandmother's song was real*.

"That's the payment," Sefa managed. "Isn't it. That's what she's paying with."

The man looked between them, lost. "I don't— Ash, is that—"

The child nodded once. Then, deliberately, held out both empty hands, palms up. *Take it. It's yours.*

"Oh, don't do that," Sefa said, wiping her face furiously with her sleeve. "Don't stand there being gracious, I have a reputation. I'm known as a hard-bitten woman of commerce. Men have wept in my boat and I mocked them for it." She sniffed. Took a breath. Took another. "Fine. Fine! Yes. Payment accepted. Get in the boat, both of you, before I change my mind and start charging by the tear."

* * *

The crossing took forty minutes and should have taken thirty, but the fog was thick and Sefa was steering by memory and by the particular hush the water made over the drowned ford, and also she kept glancing at the child sitting in the bow with the iron lantern, watching the little coal inside it breathe.

It was a strange cargo even by her standards. The man — Oscar, he'd finally offered, breaking rule one himself, which she chose to take as a compliment — sat rigid in the middle of the boat, flinching at every distant sound from the far bank, and talking, because nervous men talked and Sefa had long ago stopped minding.

"—twenty years I've sold charms against warts and heartbreak and weather, perfectly lawful charms, harmless as bread, and I never once—" He caught himself. "Sorry. You didn't ask."

"Didn't I?"

"You said no names."

"I said I don't *take* names. You can spend them all you like." She shipped the oar and let the boat drift through a slow bend. "Oscar." She tried it out. "Shopkeeper."

"Charm-monger. Legally distinct."

"And the child?"

"Ash."

"Not short for anything?"

"If it is, nobody remembers what."

The coal in the lantern pulsed, dimmer than it should have been. Sefa had seen dying fires and living ones, and this one was living the way an old dog lives — stubbornly, and with visible effort. Ash had both hands cupped near the iron cage, not touching, and the fog around the boat bent toward the child like grass toward sun.

"It's starving," Sefa said.

Oscar's head came up sharply. "What?"

"The fire. Whatever's in the box. I ferry a lot of things, shopkeeper, and half of them are alive in ways the Wardens haven't invented laws for yet. That thing is hungry." She nodded at Ash. "And the little one's feeding it, aren't you? Out of yourself."

Ash didn't deny it. Just looked at her with those ember-eyes and gave a small shrug that somehow contained an entire philosophy: *what else would I do?*

"That's why you crossed tonight," Sefa said slowly, working it through. "Not just running from the Wardens. The fire doesn't have far to go." She watched Oscar's face do the thing faces did when a stranger said aloud the thing they'd only let themselves think at three in the morning. "Where does a fire go, Oscar? What's across this water that a fire would want?"

"You've heard the stories."

"I've heard *songs*. There's a difference. Stories are what people tell to fill silence. Songs are what people couldn't stop themselves keeping." She dipped the oar again. "My grandmother's had a line in them about a door. I always thought it was about the mountain pass. Maybe it isn't."

They ran aground soft on the far shore, on a shelf of mud and reeds that appeared on no map, and Sefa held the boat steady while they climbed out. Oscar hesitated on the bank, lantern swinging, and turned back.

"I can't pay you more," he said. "We gave you what we had."

"You gave me plenty." Sefa looked past him at Ash, small and barefoot in the reeds, waiting with the patience of somebody much older than their body suggested. "But I'll tell you where you're heading, since you clearly don't know. Two miles inland there's marsh, and in the marsh there's people. The law forgot them, which means they remember everything else." She dug in her coat and tossed him a twist of oilcloth. "Dry socks. On the house — the charm's clearly not pulling its weight."

Oscar caught it, blinked at it, and smiled — a real smile, crooked and tired, the first one she'd seen on him. "Thank you, Sefa."

"How do you know my—"

"You talk in your sleep," Ash said.

In the Border-tongue. Perfectly accented. Grinning.

Sefa sat down abruptly in her own boat.

* * *

She didn't go straight back. She drifted along the far shore instead, oar shipped, letting the current hold her, and watched the little light of Oscar's lantern dwindle into the reeds until the marsh took it.

Twenty years of secrets in her head, and not one of them hers. That had always been fine. That had been the trade — she carried other people's truths so she never had to look at her own, namely that she'd spent her whole life on this river ferrying people *away* from the place her grandmother came from, and had never once gone the other direction to see what all the forgetting was hiding.

The child had paid her a secret, all right. But it hadn't been the river's name.

It had been the way Ash said the four words after it — the ones that sounded like *it's still there* — and the certainty in them, the plain unarguable certainty, like someone reporting the weather. The old country remembered them. All of them. Every grandmother, every song, every name scrubbed off every map.

And now, apparently, so did Sefa.

She picked up the oar. Turned the boat downstream, toward home, toward the mended net and the rules and the ledger in her head.

Then she swore, thoroughly and with feeling, turned the boat around, and started rowing after the lantern.

Rules were rules. But somebody had to show two fools where the marsh people camped, and somebody had to make sure the child with the ember eyes ate something hot, and somebody had to be there when — because she was suddenly, absolutely certain of it, the way you're certain about weather — when this all went sideways.

Besides. She told herself, hauling hard against the current. She'd been paid in advance. That made it business.

The lantern-light flickered ahead in the reeds, and Sefa rowed toward it, and if she was smiling, well — the fog kept that secret too.
