## 11. Outlaws' Hearth

The marsh did not want them, and that was precisely why they loved it.

Sefa's boat slid through the reeds in the last grey light of evening, and Oscar Thackeray — twenty years a shopkeeper of harmless trinkets, three days a fugitive — watched the water close behind them like a curtain drawn by a careful hand. No road reached this place. No map admitted it existed. The Council's surveyors had declared the Vessel Marshes "unproductive wetland" forty years ago and had not troubled themselves since, which meant the marsh belonged to nobody, which meant it belonged to everybody the law had decided was nobody.

"There." Sefa pointed with her chin. "Don't wave. Don't call out. And whatever the big one says to you, don't take it personal. He takes everything personal. It's how he shows affection."

The camp announced itself the way a hedgehog announces itself: reluctantly, defensively, and all in points. A palisade of driven alder stakes half-swallowed by reeds. A watch-post built from an overturned ferry hull. Smoke rising thin and disciplined from three separate fires, because one fire made a beacon and three made only weather.

A figure stepped onto the walkway above the stakes — a broad man with a deserter's posture, shoulders braced for an order that never stopped coming. He carried a spear with more conviction than point.

"State your business," he called down.

"Refuge," Sefa said.

"State your *specific* business."

"Ferrying two souls and one lantern, Doss. You've met my rates."

Doss squinted down into the boat. His gaze found the iron lantern between Oscar's feet, where a coal of undying dragon-fire breathed its slow gold light through the shutter-slats, and something in his face went carefully blank, the way men go blank when they've seen a thing they'll be thinking about for weeks.

"That's not a lamp," Doss said.

"No," said Ash, from the bow, in a voice like a small bell rung once. It was the first word the child had spoken aloud in two days, and it went through the camp like a spark through dry grass. "It's *not* a lamp."

* * *

The healer's name was Odile Fenwick, though she answered to nothing for the first hour, being busy.

She was a spare woman with ink-stained fingers and the particular economy of motion that comes from having worked, for eleven years, without proper supplies. The law had struck her name from the rolls after she treated a Warden's prisoner as thoroughly as she'd have treated a magistrate's son — same hands, same care, different paperwork. She'd come to the marshes the way water comes to low ground: inevitably, and without any intention of apologizing for it.

She looked at Ash first, because healers always look at children first, and her frown deepened.

"When did this one last eat?"

"Yesterday," Oscar said.

"This morning," Ash said, at the same time.

"They're lying," Odile said, to no one in particular, already moving. "Both of them. Sit down before you fall down, both of you. Not you, smuggler, you know where the tea is."

What followed was less a meal than a small siege. Barley soup with marsh-onion and a fish Doss had bullied out of the shallows that morning. Hard bread softened in the broth. Odile standing over them with crossed arms until every bowl was empty, then refilling them, because in Odile Fenwick's taxonomy of the world there were two kinds of people: those who ate when food was offered, and those who were about to.

Oscar hadn't been fussed over in twenty years. He found it disorientating. He also found, somewhere around the second bowl, that his eyes had gone hot, and he blamed the smoke, and everyone at the table politely pretended to believe him, which was the kindest lie anyone had told him in two decades.

"You're the charm-seller," Odile said quietly, while Ash dozed against his shoulder. "From Copperlane. My sister bought a luck-knot from you the year before the purges. She swore it was nonsense." A pause. "She also swore it worked. She could never reconcile the two, so she settled on being annoyed about it."

"I sold very good nonsense," Oscar said. "The best in the city. Lawful, licensed, and completely hollow."

"And now?"

Oscar looked down at Ash, asleep, breathing in the rhythm of someone who had walked a very long way on very small feet. He looked at the lantern.

"Now," he said, "I appear to be selling something real, and I haven't the faintest idea what it costs."

Odile nodded slowly, the way one nods at a diagnosis.

"Everything real costs," she said. "That's how you know it isn't nonsense."

* * *

Doss took the second watch, which was to say: Doss stood at the north walkway holding his unpointed spear and narrating the marsh to himself, because Doss could not bear silence and had long ago concluded that if no one else would talk, he would carry the conversation alone, both parts, with feeling.

He noticed Ash climb up beside him around midnight. Small feet on the planks. Bare, still bare — the child refused shoes with a stubbornness Doss privately rated higher than courage, having seen what courage did to men.

"Shouldn't be up," Doss said. "Cold. Dark. Reeds full of things."

"The reeds are asleep," Ash said.

"They are *not*—"

"They breathe slow when they sleep. In. Out. Like this." Ash demonstrated, chest rising, falling. "You should try it. You breathe like you're still running."

Doss opened his mouth to say that he most certainly did not breathe like he was still running, and discovered, upon inspection, that he absolutely did. Eleven years since he'd walked away from the garrison at Fennwick Bridge — eleven years! — and his body had apparently not received the news.

"I was a soldier," he said, because Ash was looking at him with the terrible patience of children who wait for truths the way fishermen wait for fish. "Eleven years ago. I left my post. There was a village burning, and my orders were to hold the bridge, and I—" He worked his jaw. "I held the bridge."

"You mean you didn't help."

"I mean I *held the bridge*, which is what soldiers do, which is what I was *for*, and there is not a day since that I don't hear the orders in my head telling me it was correct. Correct." He spat over the rail. "The marsh is full of people who were correct."

Ash considered this with great seriousness. Then the child reached up and put one small hand flat on Doss's forearm, right over the old scar from a training accident he'd never told anyone the story of, and said, in the old tongue, three soft syllables that sounded like water going over stones.

"What's that mean?" Doss whispered.

"It means your bridge is behind you now," Ash said. "You can stop holding it."

Doss did not cry. Soldiers don't. But he breathed in, and out, slow, like the reeds, and he did not say anything else for the rest of the watch, and when Ash finally fell asleep sitting up, Doss arranged his own cloak around the small shoulders with the exact care he had once reserved for polishing a sergeant's insignia, back when he had believed insignia meant something.

* * *

Maren sang the old country awake every morning, and had done for nine years, alone, to an audience of herons.

She had been a schoolteacher, once, in a town whose name was removed from the maps so thoroughly that even Maren sometimes lost the shape of it — woke some mornings reaching for it the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue and find only smooth nothing. The songs were all she had left. Her grandmother's songs. Songs for planting, songs for mending nets, one rude song about a miller that Maren would deny under torture but taught to every child in camp within a fortnight regardless.

So when she heard, on the third morning, a small voice picking its way through *The Mill-Race Song* — stumbling on the vowels, mangling the miller, but *getting it*, actually getting it, the rise and fall of it, the way the language leaned forward like a person walking into wind — Maren came out of her hut faster than dignity allowed.

Ash sat on a root, wrapped in Doss's cloak, singing to a kettle.

"Where did you learn that?" Maren demanded.

Ash blinked up at her. "I didn't learn it. I just — know it? The way you know rain is wet. Everyone knew it, where I'm from."

"*Where* you're from?" Maren knelt, and her voice cracked straight down the middle. "Child. Where are you from?"

And Ash, puzzled by the question, gave the name of a place that has not appeared on any map in living memory, and Maren sat down hard on the cold ground and wept, openly, hopelessly, joyfully, while the kettle whistled and the herons scattered and the whole camp came running to see why the song-lady was crying, and when they heard the child say it again — the town's name, the true name, spoken plainly in morning air for the first time in twenty years — several of them sat down too.

By noon, Ash was holding court on an overturned hull, teaching fragments of the old tongue to anyone who wanted them, which turned out to be everyone, including Sefa, who insisted she was only learning it "for professional purposes, secrets sound better in dead languages," and including Odile, who took notes in her precise healer's script, and even Oscar, who hung back at the edge of the circle with his arms folded until Ash caught his eye and patted the hull beside them with an authority no eight-year-old should possess.

"Sit," Ash said. "You need it most."

"I beg your pardon, I ran a shop for twenty years, I spoke to the public daily—"

"In *their* language. Not yours." Ash's voice gentled. "Your door talks all the time. You told me. You just stopped listening, because listening hurt too much."

The circle went quiet. Outlaws, it turns out, can smell grief the way horses smell storms.

"First lesson," Ash announced briskly, mercifully, turning back to the others. "The word for *home*. It sounds like *halla*. But it doesn't mean a house. It means the place where your fire knows you."

They practiced. *Halla*. Doss got it wrong four times and right on the fifth and looked absurdly pleased. Odile wrote it down twice. Sefa said it in a low smoky murmur that made it sound like a password into somewhere expensive.

"Halla," Oscar said at last, quietly, and the word fit in his mouth like a key he'd kept in a drawer for twenty years without knowing what it opened.

* * *

That night, they gathered around the lantern.

Not all of them — the fire was secret, precious, starving; they kept it shuttered except for a few minutes at dusk, a ritual Sefa had named "the lighting" with the gravity of a woman who charged for secrets and gave rituals away free. But each evening the camp's core drew close, and Oscar lifted the lantern's shutter a finger's width, and the coal's gold light spilled across their faces, and for those few minutes nobody was a fugitive or a deserter or a disgraced anything. They were just people around a fire, which is the oldest family there is.

"Tell us a story," Doss said. He said it every night. Every night, Oscar pretended reluctance. This was their liturgy.

"Very well." Oscar settled back. "Once, there was a kingdom that erased itself rather than let itself be conquered. It locked its own name away behind a door, and set a keeper to watch, and told the keeper: no one will ever need this door again."

"Was the keeper handsome?" Sefa asked.

"Weary," Oscar said. "Mostly weary."

"And did anyone ever need the door again?" Ash asked softly, and the gold light flickered, and for a moment — just a moment — Oscar could have sworn the flame leaned toward the child like a flower toward sun.

He didn't answer. Around the lantern, the others waited, patient as the marsh.

But before he could speak, Doss stiffened on the walkway, spear snapping up.

"Light," he said. "North bank. Torches. A lot of them."

Far off across the black water, a line of fires moved along the ridge — orderly, spaced, deliberate. A search line, sweeping the marshes' edge. And riding down toward the nearest crossing, unmistakable even at this distance in the torch-glow, the tall silhouette of a Warden officer on a grey horse, pausing at the ford to study the far shore —

a silhouette Oscar knew, had known since boyhood, had loved, had buried alive inside himself twenty years ago.

"Merrin," he breathed, and the coal in the lantern guttered, as if it too remembered the name.
