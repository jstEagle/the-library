## 12. Ilva Closes the Net

The order came down on a Tuesday, because of course it did. Catastrophe in Ilva Brann's experience preferred Tuesdays. It liked the way they sat in the middle of the week with nowhere to hide.

She stood in the garrison briefing room while Captain Odo Ferren—round-faced, ambitious, and about as subtle as a dropped anvil—read the Council's directive aloud from a sheet of heavy cream paper that cost more per page than Ilva earned in a day.

"Unauthorized settlement identified in the Vellmarsh," Odo said. "Estimated occupants: eleven. Unlicensed fire, unlicensed gathering, suspected unlicensed language." He paused there, savoring it. "You are authorized to clear and burn."

"Clear and burn," Ilva repeated. "Eleven people."

"Eleven lawbreakers." Odo smiled at her over the paper. "Congratulations on the promotion, by the way. Everyone noticed how you handled the Thackeray inspection. The Council likes thoroughness."

Ilva thought about the shop beneath which something old had breathed against her senses like a sleeping animal, and about the debt she'd owed Oscar Thackeray for nine years, and about how thoroughness was a word people used when they wanted you to do something terrible without flinching.

"When do we move?"

"A week from Friday. Full complement. Torches, oil, the works." Odo rolled up the paper with a flourish. "I'll want your route plan on my desk by tomorrow."

"You'll have it," she said, and walked out before he could see anything on her face that wasn't duty.

* * *

Her route plan took four hours to write, mostly because she kept rewriting the same sentence: *the sergeant will proceed via the eastern levee road.* It was true. She would proceed via the eastern levee road. Slowly. With many stops to inspect culverts. A culvert could take half a morning if you were determined, and Ilva Brann was very determined where culverts were concerned.

But stalling was only buying time, and time bought with nothing behind it spends fast. So on Wednesday night, after the garrison lamps went low, she put on her plainest coat—the one without the badge—and walked to the Hall of Records.

The Hall was a squat stone building that smelled of dust, mouse, and the particular despair of clerks. The night clerk was a young man named Pellin who had once been assigned to her squad for two weeks before the Wardens realized he could faint at the sound of raised voices, including his own. He brightened when he saw her.

"Sergeant Brann! I mean—" he glanced around the empty hall, "—ma'am? Is there trouble?"

"I need archive access, Pellin. Deep stacks. Historical."

"At this hour?" His voice did its little tremolo. "That requires a writ, or a captain's seal, or—"

"Pellin." She set two silver marks on his desk, gently, the way you'd set down a nervous bird. "It's about the purges. I'm checking old case files. Nobody's going to know, and if anybody asks, I threatened you terribly and you had no choice."

"Oh, I'm very brave under coercion," he said earnestly. "Everyone says so." He handed her a lamp and a brass key stamped with the number nine. "Deep stack nine. Mind the third shelf on the left; it bites."

* * *

Deep stack nine held the purge records, and Ilva had expected them to be thick with justification. That was how these things worked. When you did something awful to a lot of people, you wrote a great deal of paper explaining why it was actually kindness. She'd read enough of it during her training to know the genre: *threat assessment*, *containment necessity*, *regrettable but proportionate response.*

What she found instead was arithmetic.

The first file was dated three years before the purges began. It was a survey report, sealed Council-eyes-only, and it mapped the old kingdom—not the erased country, but what lay beneath it. Rivers of heat under the bedrock, drawn in careful ink, all of them converging on a single point marked with a symbol Ilva didn't recognize but felt in her teeth anyway. A door. Someone had written *DOOR* next to it in the margin, underlined twice, which struck her as the least bureaucratic annotation she had ever seen in an official record and therefore the most honest.

The second file was older still, and thinner. A single letter, unsigned:

*The gate cannot be moved. It can only be owned. Whoever holds the key-holdings holds the crossing, and whoever holds the crossing holds the country beyond. Recommend acquisition of all sites. The safety question is secondary and may be used to secure public consent.*

*May be used to secure public consent.*

Ilva sat down on the biting shelf—it bit her, and she didn't notice—and read the sentence again, and then again, the way you press a bruise to confirm it's really yours.

She had joined the Wardens at nineteen because the purges had saved her village. That was the story she told herself, and it was even true, mostly. There had been a hedge-witch whose working had gone wrong and burned three cottages and a child, and the Wardens had come, and afterward there had been order. Rules. No more accidents. She owed them for that. More than that: she owed them for the night nine years ago when she'd been a green corporal sent to arrest a charm-seller who'd sheltered her during a blizzard, fed her soup, and asked no questions, and she'd stood in that warm little shop with her hand on her sword and chosen, quietly, not to see. She'd spent nine years paying that choice forward in small mercies, telling herself the balance was good.

And the whole time, above her head, the Council had been playing property law with a door between worlds.

The third file was the worst one. It was recent—this year's ink—and it listed holdings. Sites acquired during the purges, cross-referenced against the underground rivers. Every seizure matched a convergence point. Every burned village, every scattered family, every name on every warrant she had ever served: they'd all been sitting on top of the map's red lines. The purge hadn't been about safety. Safety was the *consent*, and the consent had been harvested, and now the Council owned nearly everything except—

Except one site. One line on the map left blank, marked *contested*, with a note in a familiar tidy hand: *Sealed independently. Keeper unknown. Do not approach until Council assets are sufficient.*

She knew that handwriting. She'd seen it on a hundred directives, rolled up and flourished by a round-faced man who smiled too easily. But underneath Odo Ferren's note, in different ink, someone else had written a single word: *Voss.*

Merrin Voss. Chief architect of the Warden order. The man whose name appeared in the old files as co-designer of the original containment—the man who had built the thing under the charm-shop, or helped build it, or betrayed it, depending on which rumor you believed, and Ilva had never believed any of them until tonight.

She copied everything. Her hand shook on the first page and steadied by the fourth, because hands are loyal creatures and eventually they decide to help.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat in her quarters with the copies spread across her cot like a spread of losing cards, and she did what she always did when the world stopped making sense: she cleaned her sword. Not because it needed cleaning. Because the motion gave her somewhere to put her anger.

The trouble with discovering you've been lied to for twenty years is that it doesn't just indict the liars. It indicts everyone who looked away. And Ilva had looked away so professionally, for so long, that she'd gotten commendations for it.

There was a knock. She swept the papers under her blanket and said, "Enter," in her best *nothing-is-happening* voice, and Odo Ferren's head appeared around the door, wearing his anvil-subtle smile.

"Burning the midnight oil, Sergeant?"

"Route planning, sir."

"Mm." He came in without being asked, which was a thing Odo did. He picked up her whetstone, turned it over, set it down. "Thought I'd check on you. Big operation. Some sergeants get sentimental about clearance work." He said it lightly, watching her the way a fisherman watches a float. "Especially out here, where everyone's got a cousin in the marshes or an old friend with a charm shop."

"Ilva Brann doesn't burn friends," she said. "She burns illegal fires. You'll get your route plan on time."

"That's what I like about you." He patted the doorframe twice, some old habit of his, and left.

She waited until his footsteps faded, then pulled the copies back out and looked at the last line of the last file, the one she'd copied without quite letting herself read it. It was an addendum, dated this month:

*Contested site shows renewed activity. Flame detected. Council resolution pending: extinguishment recommended over acquisition, given instability of keeper arrangements. Asset Ferren authorized to prepare final action following marsh settlement clearance.*

They weren't going to take the door anymore. They were going to kill it. Kill the fire, kill the keeper's work, kill whatever chance remained of—of what? She turned back to the first file, to the careful inked rivers converging on that symbol she felt in her teeth. To the country beyond. The country that remembered them, if the songs were true, and Ilva's grandmother had sung those songs, and Ilva had let the Wardens teach her they were nonsense.

Her grandmother had also taught her a saying: *when the map lies, follow the river.*

Ilva put the copies into an oilcloth pouch, and her badge into a drawer, and stood for a long moment looking at the badge lying there on the folded linen, small and brass and suddenly very far away from her. Defection wasn't a lightning bolt, she discovered. It was a drawer closing.

She wrote the route plan first. Odo would have it on his desk by dawn, exactly as ordered: eastern levee road, full stops at every culvert, arrival at the marsh camp projected for late afternoon—a full six hours after the company's stated departure time, with every delay documented, justified, and unimpeachable. Let him court-martial her for thoroughness.

Then she packed the rest: sword, bread, the oilcloth pouch, and the silver marks she'd meant for Pellin's silence, which he'd refused anyway with the wounded dignity of a man who fainted honorably. She blew out her lamp.

Six hours. If she rode hard through the dark, she could reach the marshes hours ahead of her own company. She could warn them. She could show them what she'd found—the maps, the rivers, the word *Voss* sitting in the margin like a splinter—and let them decide what a disgraced Warden-Sergeant was worth.

She paused at the door, hand on the latch, and allowed herself one moment of honest accounting: everything she'd believed about herself had just been confiscated, and she was walking out into the night carrying stolen Council records and no idea whether the outlaws in the marshes would shoot her or feed her soup.

Oscar would feed her soup, she thought. That was practically his whole personality.

Ilva Brann closed the drawer on her badge, shouldered her pack, and went to find the people she'd been sent to burn.
