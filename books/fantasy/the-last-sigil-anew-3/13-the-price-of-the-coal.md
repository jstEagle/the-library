## 13. The Price of the Coal

The marshes taught Oscar Thackeray a new kind of arithmetic.

He had always been good with numbers—twenty years of running a charm shop meant knowing exactly how many copper bits bought flour, how many flour sacks kept a lie looking like a life. But this was different. This was subtraction with stakes.

The lantern hung from his hand by an iron handle gone cold as a dead man's ring. Inside it, the coal—the heart of the brazier he had fed every night for twenty years beneath his floorboards—burned small and stubborn and *dimmer than yesterday*. That was the part that made his stomach hurt. He had carried fire before. He had carried candles up stairs in drafts, lanterns through rain, all the ordinary errands of flame. Fire did not dim because you moved it. Fire was indifferent to distance.

This fire was counting miles.

"Stop frowning at it," said Sefa from ahead, where she picked their path through the reeds with a smuggler's certainty. "It can feel you staring. Everything old can feel you staring."

"It's smaller," Oscar said. "It was the size of my thumbnail when we left the shop. Now look at it."

Sefa looked. Her face did something complicated and then settled into the flat expression of a woman deciding not to have opinions until paid for them. "Smaller," she agreed. "Still burning, though."

"That's not comforting. That's a countdown wearing a cheerful hat."

Behind them, Ash made a sound. It was not quite a laugh—the child had not laughed yet, not properly, not with the whole chest of it—but it had the shape of one trying on shoes. Three days in the marsh camp had done that. Three days of the healer Marta pressing willow-bark tea into their hands, of the deserter Tam teaching them to skip stones off the black water, of the song-woman Odile humming fragments of melodies that made Ash go still and wide-eyed, as if hearing a language they'd forgotten they knew.

Oscar glanced back and caught the tail end of it: Ash's mouth curved, quickly hidden, quickly returned to the solemn mask children wear when they are not sure laughing is allowed.

"You're allowed, you know," he said quietly.

Ash looked at him. In the grey light of the marsh morning their eyes were the color of the water where it deepened—dark, and holding more than you could see down into.

"When I was your age—" Oscar began, then stopped, because when he was their age there had been a kingdom, and festivals, and Merrin Voss setting fire to a hayrick by accident and both of them running from a farmer's dog until their lungs gave out. "When I was your age, someone told me that keeping a thing safe meant never enjoying it. That joy was a door left open for trouble to walk through." He shifted the lantern to his other hand. "Twenty years later I can tell you what that buys you. It buys you a shop full of trinkets nobody needs and a floorboard you're afraid of. So. Laugh. It's not a door. Trouble has legs; it comes in whether you're laughing or not. You might as well be laughing."

Ash considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then they laughed—small, rusty, real—and the sound went through Oscar like warm water through cold hands.

*Well,* he thought. *That's dangerous. I'm getting attached.* As if attachment were something that happened suddenly rather than something that had been happening since a barefoot child stood in his doorway speaking the tongue of a sealed door.

* *

They walked all morning. The marshes, Odile had told them, went on for three days in any direction unless you knew the ridges, and even then the ridges moved—peat does that, shrugging its slow shoulders over decades, swallowing paths and occasionally pilgrims. The Wardens didn't patrol here. Nobody did. The law forgot places like this the way a busy man forgets rooms he doesn't use, and the forgotten gathered in them like dust: Marta, struck from the healer's rolls for treating a Warden's enemy; Tam, who had walked away from a garrison and could not walk back; Odile, whose crime was memory itself, songs about a country no map admitted to.

By midday the light thickened, gold coming through the reeds, and they stopped on a ridge of dry ground where Tam had left snares. There were two hares in them, which Tam regarded with professional satisfaction and Ash regarded with the complicated expression of someone who was hungry and sorry about it in equal measure.

"The fire eats either way," Sefa said, building a cook-smokeless fire with a smuggler's economy. "You may as well."

Oscar set the lantern down between them and crouched over it while the others worked, and felt the cold of the iron seep through his trouser knees into his bones, which were forty-eight years old and increasingly vocal about it.

The coal had been the size of his thumbnail at the shop. He was nearly sure of it. Now it sat in its little bed of ash like the last currant in a bun, and when he tilted the lantern the light swung wrong—not the steady amber of dragon-fire but something with a stutter in it, a heartbeat missing every fourth beat.

"Marta," he called. "Come look at this. Not as a healer. Just—as someone who's seen things die."

Marta wiped her hands and came. She was a broad woman built like a door herself, with kind eyes and forearms that had held down many a thrashing patient, and she studied the coal with her head tipped.

"It's hungry," she said.

"I know it's hungry. It's been hungry for twenty years. I fed it."

"Not hungry like a hearth is hungry." She crouched beside him. "I've seen this. In men, mostly. Old soldiers. There's a wasting that isn't illness—it's spending. The body paying out strength faster than it earns it back." She looked up at him. "You said this fire slept under your floor. Did it sleep, or did you feed it?"

"Both. Every night. Charms to keep it banked. A spoonful of—" He stopped. How did you explain twenty years of ritual to people who'd only known him three days? "There's a way of tending it. My friend and I worked it out together, back when. It costs almost nothing when it's still. But we've been moving it for six days now."

"And each mile spends years," Marta said. It wasn't a question. She had the healer's habit of arriving at conclusions slightly ahead of everyone else, so that following her felt like being late to your own funeral. "How many years does it have left, Oscar?"

"I don't know. It's the last dragon-fire in existence. It's supposed to outlast mountains."

"Everything outlasts mountains right up until it doesn't." She put a hand briefly on his shoulder and went back to the hares.

Oscar stayed crouched by the lantern. The coal pulsed. Missed. Pulsed again.

*Six days,* he thought. *We've spent six days of its life walking.* And ahead lay the capital-that-was-no-longer-named, the ruins, the mountain, the door—weeks away, maybe more, across country that would not get easier or emptier. The arithmetic finished itself without his permission: carry the fire to the gate and there would be nothing left to carry. They would arrive at the door of the old country holding a coal as dead as any other coal, and the whole journey, the flight, the marsh, the deaths waiting somewhere behind them on the road—

No. Not behind. Ahead. He was learning that too.

* *

It was Ash who came to him that evening.

The others had turned in—Tam snoring with the untroubled depth of a man whose conscience had finally been issued a permit, Odile murmuring half a lullaby to herself the way she did most nights, half a lullaby because the other halves had been confiscated along with everything else. Sefa sat watch at the edge of the ridge, silhouette sharp against the star-hung reeds. And Ash crept close to the lantern, close to Oscar, and sat down with their knees drawn up, and looked into the fire with an expression Oscar had learned to dread, because it was the expression of someone rehearsing words.

"Ash?" he said gently. "What is it?"

The child's hands moved first. They always spoke with their hands a little, even now that sounds came—hands shaping things that words hadn't grown around yet. *Small*, the hands said. *Less.*

"I know," Oscar said. "I've seen. We'll rest tomorrow. Full day of rest, no walking. Let it catch its breath."

Ash shook their head. Their fingers found the old tongue then—the true one, the door-tongue, the one that made the hairs on Oscar's arms stand up because it sounded less like speech and more like stone remembering being spoken to. One word. He knew it. He had heard it whispered from under the floorboards on quiet nights for two decades and had told himself it meant *patience*.

It did not mean patience.

"*Spending*," Oscar translated, slowly. "The fire is spending. Yes. Marta saw it too. We'll be careful. We'll—"

Ash took his hand.

Their palm was small and warm and dry, and it pressed flat against the back of his hand the way you'd press a hand against a door, and Oscar understood, with a lurch like a floorboard giving way, that this was how Ash spoke the important things. Not with the borrowed words of the new language. With touch. With the grammar of doors.

Images came. Not visions exactly—impressions, the way heat reaches you before flame does. He saw—felt—the coal from inside, or near enough: a vastness gone narrow, a river run to a thread. He felt the twenty years of his own careful feeding like a hand cupped around a candle, precious and insufficient. And underneath all of it, patient as sediment, he felt the truth that Ash had been carrying since the night of the hard rain, the truth they had not known how to say because saying it in any ordinary tongue would make it ordinary:

*The child is not a messenger.*

The knowledge arrived whole, and it arrived with horror riding on its back.

"You're the key," Oscar said. His voice came out strange, flattened, as though heard from underwater. "Not carrying the key. Not sent to find the key. You *are* the key. Made flesh. Made—" He stopped. Started again. "The door doesn't need a key brought to it. It needs a key *become*. And opening it will—"

Ash's hand tightened on his. Their face was calm. That was the worst part, the terrible adult calm of it, the calm of someone who has known their own price for longer than they've known their own name.

"No," Oscar said.

*Unmaking*, Ash's hand said against his. *Door opens. Key goes into the lock. Lock keeps the shape of the key.* The images came gentler now, softened, the way you soften bad news for the dying: light, dissolution, a child-shaped brightness pouring itself into a seam of stone and staying there, glad, glad, *glad to be used at last*—

"No." He pulled his hand free and immediately regretted it, because Ash flinched, small and quick, and Oscar Thackeray had spent twenty years being careful with fragile things and could not bear to be careless with this one. "Ash. Listen to me. Listen. There is always another way. Twenty years ago people told me there was only one way and I believed them, and it cost—" His voice cracked on the name he wouldn't say. Merrin's name. The seal's name. "There is *always* another way. We find it. That's the whole job now. Not the fire, not the door—you. We find another way or we don't open anything, do you hear me? We sit in these marshes until the reeds grow over us and the Wardens forget our names twice."

Ash looked at him for a long moment. The coal-light moved on their face.

Then, slowly, deliberately, the child reached out and turned his hand back over, palm up, and laid something in it.

A pebble. Marsh-grey, ordinary, damp. And pressed into the soft stone—not carved, *grown*, the way frost grows on glass—was a tiny spiral sigil. Oscar knew it. Every keeper knew it. It was the mark of the locking, the sign cut into the underside of the sealed door, the sign that meant *one holds the hinge so the rest may pass*.

Ash tapped the sigil. Tapped their own chest. Tapped the lantern.

Then they pointed—at the dark beyond the firelight, at the direction of the mountain, of the door—and drew in the air with one finger a second figure. Small. Child-shaped.

*Unless someone else takes their place.*

The words didn't come in any language at all. They came as weight, settling onto Oscar's shoulders like a coat made of wet sand.

Someone else. Someone else becomes the key-flesh. Someone else pours themselves into the seam and stays, holding the hinge, so the child can walk through into green hills and a living hearth. Someone else pays.

And Oscar sat very still in the marsh dark with a pebble in his palm and the last dragon-fire guttering between them, and understood that the arithmetic had been running longer than he knew—that it had started the night a barefoot child appeared in his doorway, that every kindness since had been a coin sliding toward a scale, and that the universe, patient as peat, was now presenting him the bill.

"Ash," he said hoarsely. "Who told you this? Who sent you? Who *made* you the—"

The child opened their mouth to answer—

And out on the black water, far off, a light blinked. Once. Twice. A third time. Deliberate. Spaced. Not lightning, not swamp-gas, not anything the marsh made on its own.

Sefa was on her feet at the ridge-edge before Oscar had finished standing, her knife already in her hand, her voice dropped to a whisper that somehow reached every sleeping soul in the camp at once:

"Wake up. We've got company on the water—and there's only one kind of fool that signals in threes."
