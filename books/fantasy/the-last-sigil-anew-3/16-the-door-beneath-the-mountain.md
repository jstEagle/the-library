## 16. The Door Beneath the Mountain

The capital-that-was-no-longer-named announced itself the way grief announces itself: not all at once, but in pieces you kept tripping over.

First it was a road that grew too straight for any cart, too wide for any market—built for processions, Oscar thought, for banners and trumpets and kings whose names had been scraped off every milestone like candle wax flicked from a table. Then it was the smell: wet stone and old iron and, beneath both, faintly, impossibly, woodsmoke. Hearth-smoke. As if the city still remembered being lived in.

Then it was the mountain itself, rising out of the morning fog like a shoulder under a blanket, with the ruins of the palace spilled down its flanks—the shattered crown of a country that had voted, in its last desperate act of self-preservation, to forget itself entirely.

Oscar stood at the edge of the old processional road and felt twenty years settle onto his back like a coat he'd never truly taken off.

"Well," said Sefa, squinting up at it. "That's a lot of rock."

"It's more than rock," said Ilva quietly. She had her badge in her hand—not wearing it, just holding it, turning it over the way a person turns over a coin they no longer believe is lucky. "Every Warden learns the shape of this place. We're taught it's a ruin. A warning." Her jaw tightened. "We're not taught what's inside."

"Nobody is," said Oscar. "That was rather the point."

Ash stood beside him, small and barefoot as the night they'd arrived at his shop, though Sefa had long since wrestled a pair of boots onto them and Ash had long since abandoned the boots at every opportunity. The child was staring at the mountain with an expression Oscar had learned to read as clearly as weather: the look of someone coming home to a house they'd been told didn't exist.

Ash raised one hand and signed—quick, fluid, the language they'd built between them out of the old tongue and gesture and sheer stubbornness—and Oscar translated aloud because the others needed it, but mostly because saying it made it realer.

"They say the mountain remembers us. All of us. Even you two." Ash's hands paused, then finished, almost shyly. "Even Sefa, who they say smells of river and secrets."

"Flattering," said Sefa. "Accurate, but flattering."

* * *

They climbed through the ruins in single file, because the paths were narrow and because, Oscar suspected, none of them wanted to walk abreast with their fear visible from the side.

The city had been beautiful once. You could see the bones of it under the ruin—the colonnades, the fountains, the wide steps where children had sold roasted chestnuts and Oscar had bought them for a copper and Merrin had haggled the seller down to three-quarters of one, laughing, always laughing, even then. Moss had taken everything green. Ivy poured through windows like something escaping. In one shattered square, a statue of a king stood headless, and someone—recently, by the freshness of the chisel-marks—had carved into the plinth a single word in the old tongue.

Ash stopped, touched it, and signed: *It says "remembered."*

"Who carves things out here?" Ilva asked, uneasy. "There's no one left."

"There's someone," said Oscar. "There was always someone. That was the whole tragedy of it. We sealed a door on a country full of people who refused to stop existing."

The coal in its iron lantern swung at his hip, dimmer than it had been in the marshes, dimmer than it had been at the ford, where Tam the healer had died holding the crossing so the light could pass. Every mile cost years. Oscar had stopped calculating exactly how many, the way you stop counting a fever. But he could feel it now, a faint coldness through the lantern's iron, like holding a bird whose heart was slowing.

Ash kept glancing at it. Each glance lasted a little longer than the last.

"Don't," Oscar said gently, when the fourth glance went on too long. "I know that look. It's my look. I've been giving that look to a brazier for twenty years."

Ash signed something short and fierce: *It's dying faster.*

"I know."

*You could let me carry it.*

"You can't."

*I'm already half door,* Ash signed, and there was something terrible in how matter-of-fact the small hands were about it. *What happens to the lantern won't happen to me.*

"And that," said Sefa from ahead, without turning around, "is exactly the kind of sentence we're going to unpack properly once we're indoors and everyone's had tea. Marching order. Less philosophy."

But her voice was rough, and she walked closer to Ash after that, close enough that her elbow bumped the child's shoulder at every turn—a smuggler's way of holding someone's hand.

* * *

The gate was where Oscar remembered it, which surprised him. He had expected the mountain to have changed, grown, shifted its weight in twenty years of keeping a secret. But the path wound down between two walls of black stone into an amphitheater of rock, and there it was: a door set directly into the mountainside, tall as three men, seamless, without handle or hinge or keyhole, carved—or grown; Oscar had never decided which—with lines of the old tongue flowing across it like water frozen mid-pour.

Twenty years ago it had hummed. He remembered standing here with Merrin, both of them young and bleeding and victorious in the way that only feels like victory for about a week, laying their palms against the stone while the seal took hold. It had hummed like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing.

Now it was silent.

No—that wasn't right either. Oscar pressed his palm flat against the stone, and the silence had texture to it. It was the silence of a held breath. Of a room full of people waiting for someone to speak first.

"Ash," he said. His voice came out strange, thin in the vast amphitheater. "Whenever you're ready. And if you're never ready, that's also—we can also do that. We can stand here indefinitely. I've gotten very good at indefinite."

Ash looked up at him. Then, with the gravity of a child performing the most important act of their life—which, Oscar thought, they were—they stepped forward, pulled off the boots Sefa had re-wrestled onto them twice that morning, and laid both bare hands flat against the door.

Nothing happened.

For three heartbeats nothing happened, and Oscar felt something in his chest begin to quietly come apart—

And then the lines of script lit.

Not all at once. It began where Ash's fingers touched and spread outward like warmth returning to frozen limbs, letter by letter, line by line, gold rising through the black stone, until the whole door was a sheet of slow fire and the amphitheater was full of light and a sound—the sound, oh, the sound. The bell-note he'd carried in his memory for twenty years, unfinished until this moment, finally completing its ring.

The lantern at Oscar's hip flared so bright it threw shadows behind him.

Ash laughed. It was a small, cracked, rusty thing—Ash had never spoken and might never speak—but it was unmistakably a laugh, and they turned to Oscar with tears running down their face and signed with both hands shaking: *It knows me. It says—"welcome home, little key."*

"Hello," Oscar told the door, absurdly, uselessly, his own eyes burning. "Sorry about the delay. There were complications."

The door began to open.

Not swinging—there were no hinges—but dissolving along its center seam, the two halves going soft as wax and drawing apart, revealing not the dark passage Oscar remembered but a corridor of that same golden light, receding inward, down, toward the heart of the mountain.

And standing in it, silhouetted, alone, with his hands folded behind his back like a man waiting to be scolded—

"Merrin," said Oscar.

* * *

He had imagined this meeting perhaps nine hundred times over twenty years, and in none of those versions did Merrin Voss look like this.

In some versions Merrin was in Warden white, armored in rank. In some he was sneering. In one particularly satisfying recurring version he apologized immediately and meant it. What Merrin actually looked like was tired. Tired in a way that had gone past exhaustion into geology—tired the way the mountain was tired. His chief-architect's coat was gone. He wore plain wool. His hair, once the envy of every academy hall, was shot through with grey and cut badly, as if by his own hand, impatiently.

His Wardens were nowhere. The corridor behind him was empty of everything but light.

"You dismissed them," Ilva said sharply, hand on her sword. Behind her, Sefa had produced a knife from somewhere with the ease of long habit.

"I sent them home," Merrin said. His voice was the same. That was the worst part. Twenty years, betrayal, sabotage, a raid at a ford where a good man died—and his voice was still warm, still dry-edged, still the voice that had talked Oscar into a hundred bad ideas and out of ninety-five of them. "This isn't their fight anymore. It never should have been anyone's fight. It was mine and Oscar's, and I made it everyone's, and I'm—" He stopped. Looked at his own hands. Started again, smaller. "Come inside. Please. The seal will want you all, and I would very much like to stop being alone down here. I've had twenty years of practice and I remain terrible at it."

"You planned to kill the fire," Oscar said flatly. "Ilva found your records. Extinguish it, end the risk, end the door forever. That was the plan, yes?"

"That was the plan I wrote down," said Merrin, "for people I assumed would read it. Yes."

"And the real one?"

Merrin's eyes went past him—to Ash, standing barefoot in the doorway-light with the waking script glowing faintly off their skin like sunlit dust—and something in his face broke open, quietly, the way ice breaks on a river in spring: slowly, and then all at once.

"The real one," Merrin said, "is a great deal harder to explain, and I'd rather do it sitting down, because I suspect at least one of you is going to need to sit."

"We're all going to need to sit," said Sefa. "Some of us are going to need a drink."

"I have water," said Merrin. "It's very clean. The mountain filters it. It's honestly the one thing I've maintained properly."

Despite everything—despite the ford, despite the records, despite twenty years of a silent shop and a hidden brazier—Oscar felt a laugh trying to climb out of him and hated it and let it. It came out cracked, halfway between laughter and sobbing, the way all his feelings had gone these last weeks.

"You're unbelievable," he told Merrin. "You're genuinely the most unbelievable man alive. I sealed a door with you."

"You did," said Merrin softly. And then, with a small, crooked, terribly familiar tilt of his head, gesturing into the golden light of the country that no longer existed:

"Welcome home, Oscar. Both of you. All of you. Mind the third step—it's been twenty years, and I never was much of a carpenter."
