## 17. Two Keepers Argue with the Dark

The door did not open so much as admit them.

Oscar had expected stone corridors, Warden steel, a gauntlet of traps. Instead the passage beyond Ash's waking touch was a single stair, worn smooth in the middle by feet that hadn't climbed it in twenty years, and it let them out into a round chamber lit by nothing at all—and yet lit thoroughly, the way a memory of noon is lit. The sealed door stood at the far side, whole and vast and patient, its sigils dim as banked coals.

And Merrin Voss stood before it with his back turned, his Warden-grey coat hanging open, his sword lying on the floor between him and them like something he'd set down rather than dropped.

"Lock the door behind you," Merrin said, without turning. "Habit."

"Merrin." Oscar's voice came out steadier than he felt, which he counted as the last useful trick his shopkeeper's manners had left him. "You dismissed your Wardens."

"I dismissed my Wardens." Merrin turned, and Oscar's chest did something complicated and painful, because twenty years had silvered Merrin's temples and carved lines around his mouth, but his eyes were exactly the eyes that had once argued with Oscar over the proper way to ward a jam jar—fierce, tired, and fond underneath it all, like a knife wrapped in wool. "Hello, Oscar. You look terrible."

"You look worse."

"I've been chief architect of an order built on a lie for two decades. I should hope so." His gaze slid past Oscar to Ash, who had pressed close to Oscar's side and was watching Merrin with the stillness of a small animal deciding whether the large one was predator or weather. "And this is the key. Hello, little one. I'm sorry about everything I'm about to do to your evening."

Ash said nothing. Ash never said anything, not aloud; but their hand found Oscar's sleeve and gripped it, and Oscar understood every word of that.

* * *

There were chairs in the chamber—two of them, old ones, dragged down here twenty years ago on the night of the sealing, when two young fools had sat up until dawn arguing over wards and drinking terrible plum brandy and pretending neither of them was crying. One chair had rotted. The other still stood. Merrin gestured at it, and Oscar refused it on principle, and they ended up both standing, which was somehow more honest.

"You knew we were coming," Oscar said.

"I built this door, Oscar. I feel it the way you feel a tooth you're going to lose." Merrin walked to the great seal and laid his palm against the stone, and for a moment—the length of one held breath—the sigils brightened under his hand, warm gold, *recognizing* him, and Oscar felt the floor tilt beneath everything he'd believed for twenty years.

"You," Oscar said quietly. "It answers *you*."

"It's keyed to me." Merrin took his hand away and looked at it as if it belonged to someone he disliked. "That was the flaw. That was always the flaw. I told you, the night we made it, that I'd bound it to the pact itself—to our promise, to the fire, to something larger than either of us. It made the sealing cleaner. More elegant." He laughed, and there was no humor in it at all, only the dry sound a man makes when he finally stops carrying a joke he's carried too long. "I lied. I keyed it to myself because I was afraid. Because if the seal answered to *me*, then no one could ever undo it without coming through me first. Not the Council. Not a future keeper. Not even you."

"Why?" Oscar's voice cracked on it. "Merrin, why would you—"

"Because I didn't trust anyone else to hold it!" And there it was, the thing under twenty years of grey coats and cold orders: Merrin Voss, shouting in the dark like a frightened apprentice, his voice bouncing off stone older than kingdoms. "You weren't there, Oscar. You saw the purges from the outside. I was *in* them. I watched good people—brilliant people—break under interrogation and give up names they swore they'd die keeping. I watched what fear does. So I made sure the most important working in the world depended on exactly one man's fear staying intact." He spread his hands. "Mine. The one fear I could audit daily."

The silence after was long enough that Oscar could hear the brazier-heart in Sefa's iron lantern, ticking faintly as it cooled, a sound like a very small clock running down.

"And now your fear is failing," Oscar said.

"My fear is *tired*." Merrin sank onto the surviving chair after all, abruptly, like a puppet with cut strings. Up close, without the coat, without the rank, he looked less like the chief architect of the Wardens and more like a man who hadn't slept properly since the kingdom fell. Which, Oscar realized with a lurch, was precisely true. They had that much in common. They had always had that much in common. "Do you know what it costs, holding a seal keyed to yourself? Every ward I build anywhere passes through it. Every charm the Wardens confiscate and destroy—I feel each one go out, like candles pinched in another room. Twenty years, Oscar. Fourteen thousand small extinguishings. And now the fire is starving, the door is waking, and every night I can hear it *asking* me to let go." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "So yes. The Council wants to put the flame out. But understand this, and understand it clearly: the order to extinguish it was *mine*. I wrote it myself. I have been writing it for years. Extinguishing the fire ends the seal's purpose, and the seal lets go of me, and I can finally—" His voice failed. He tried again. "I can finally stop."

* * *

Oscar stood very still and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the marshes.

Twenty years of believing Merrin had betrayed the pact for ambition—for the Council, for power, for the grey coat. Twenty years of rehearsing the argument in the bathwater, in the dark above the floorboards, feeding the brazier crumbs of grievance along with crumbs of coal. *He sold us out. He chose the law. He forgot her.* All of it, and the truth was smaller and crueler: Merrin had never chosen anything but the weight. He had simply decided to put it down by breaking it.

"You absolute idiot," Oscar said, and his own eyes were stinging now, and he didn't fight it. "You utter, magnificent, catastrophic idiot. You built yourself into a lock and then planned the burglary."

"There's no need to be poetic about it."

"There is every need. Poetry is the only honest language left in this country—you erased all the others." Oscar began to pace, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, the circuit of a shop aisle worn into his bones. "Kill the fire. Do you know what the fire *is*, Merrin? It isn't a weapon. It isn't a risk. It's the last living ember of a hearth that warmed a whole country—a country your Council unmade on paper and couldn't unmake anywhere else. Ash came through the rain barefoot to find it. A child. Sent by *something* to keep it alive. And your grand solution, your elegant end to your own suffering, is to snuff it out so you can sleep."

"I know," Merrin said simply. "I've known for months. That's why I came to warn you at the shop. Why do you think I let Ilva Brann stall? Why do you think I dismissed my Wardens tonight instead of bringing forty swords?" He rose from the chair. "I'm not here to fight you, old friend. I'm here because there are only two ways this ends, and I wanted to offer you the choice before the third way chooses itself."

"Say it, then."

"Help me kill the flame." Merrin's hands trembled as he said it, and he looked at them with something like contempt. "Together, cleanly, tonight. You carry the lantern; I open my half of the seal; the fire goes out gentle, like a candle after prayers, instead of starving ugly in some marsh or gutter where it takes the child's light with it. The key stays safe—Ash lives. You go home. I go—" he gestured vaguely at the darkness, "—wherever a man goes when his tooth finally comes out. Or—"

"Or?"

"Or you fight me. Right here. Beat me, break my hold on the seal, and carry the fire through the door to whatever's on the other side. And I will oppose you with everything I have, because that is what the flaw demands of me, and I am too old and too afraid to be better than my own working." He smiled, wretchedly. "I did tell you I wasn't brave. I told you that night, over the brandy. You said I was. You were wrong, Oscar. You were wrong about a lot of things, but mostly you were wrong about me."

* * *

From the corner, small and clear, came a sound.

Ash was laughing.

Not aloud—they never made a sound—but their shoulders shook, and their eyes were bright, and their hands moved in quick shapes that Oscar, after these weeks, read almost as easily as speech. Oscar translated anyway, partly for Merrin, mostly because saying it aloud gave it weight.

"They say—" Oscar huffed, despite everything, despite the tears standing in his eyes, "they say you're both idiots. They say two men built a door together, and each one has spent twenty years trying to sacrifice himself alone, and neither one thought to just—ask the other to help carry it."

"I don't need—"

"You *do*." Ash's hands flashed again, fierce, imperious, unmistakably someone who had grown up around strong opinions. "They say the seal is keyed to you, fine. But the fire was never keyed to anything. The fire belongs to whoever feeds it. And they say—" Oscar stopped. His throat closed. He knelt, slowly, so he was eye-level with the child, and Ash finished the sentence themselves with one final gesture, patient and precise, touching their own chest, then Merrin's, then Oscar's, then closing their fist around all three.

"*Family*," Oscar said hoarsely. "They say family. They say the door doesn't need one keeper. It needs a household."

For a long moment Merrin Voss, terror of the purges, architect of the grey law, stood in the dark and cried like a boy caught thieving plums. He didn't wipe his face. Perhaps he'd forgotten how.

"The flaw can be mended," he said at last, roughly. "Re-keyed. Not to me—not to any single person. To a pact again. To a promise. It would take all three of us. And when the door opens—if it opens—it will ask for a keeper on the far side, and it won't take no for an answer, and I don't know yet who it will ask for, and I am *not* going to pretend I haven't already guessed." He straightened, and drew a breath like a man surfacing from deep water, and looked at Oscar with the old fierceness, the wool-wrapped-knife fondness, undimmed by twenty years of grey. "So here is my real choice, offered properly this time: help me mend it. Both of us. Together. Like we should have done from the start."

Above them—or below them, or possibly everywhere at once—the great door shivered. The sigils flared from ember-gold to white, and a sound rolled through the stone that was not quite a groan and not quite a word, and Ash spun toward it with their hands already raised, and Oscar felt the lantern's little heart leap against the iron like a startled bird.

"What is it?" Merrin snapped. "What's happening?"

Ash signed fast, urgent, and Oscar's stomach dropped as he understood.

"That's not the door waking," he said. "That's the door answering. Someone's already opening it—from the other side."
