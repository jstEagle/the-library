## 18. The Guttering

Ilva Brann had held a pass once before, in another life, when she still believed the Wardens were something other than a long arm for frightened men. That pass had been stone and snow and honest soldiers on both sides. This one was a goat-track of broken slate above the drowned valley, and the soldiers coming up it were hers—had been hers until an hour ago—and they carried orders she had read twice by lantern-light before folding them into her boot.

*Burn the camp. Take the child. Extinguish the coal.*

She counted eleven of them in the grey pre-dawn light, climbing in loose file, torches unlit because their sergeant had sense enough not to advertise themselves to smugglers. Ilva stood where the track narrowed between two leaning slabs of rock, a place she'd noted yesterday with a professional eye and now regarded with something closer to affection. It was a good spot for holding. It was a very good spot for holding.

"Sergeant," said young Pell, bringing up the rear, breath steaming. "Orders say—"

"Orders say I take point," Ilva said. "I'm taking point."

Pell looked at her, then at the narrow place in the track, then back at her, and did the arithmetic that all good soldiers do eventually: that their sergeant had stopped marching toward the objective and started standing in front of it.

"Ilva." He used her name, which he wasn't supposed to. "You owe these people nothing."

"No," she agreed. "That's rather the point, isn't it? Everything I've done for twenty years, I've done because someone told me it was owed." She rolled her shoulders and settled her weight. "I'd like to try giving something away instead. See how it sits."

Behind Pell, the file had stopped. Eleven faces, mostly young, all tired, all cold, all watching her the way people watch a fire decide what it's going to burn.

"Anyone who wants to walk past me," Ilva said pleasantly, "is welcome to try."

Nobody moved. Down in the dark behind them, far below, something flickered—the first orange bloom of torchlight from the second company, the one the Council hadn't bothered telling her about, the one that proved this had never been an inspection or even an arrest but a slaughter with paperwork. Ilva felt the last warm thing inside her go out like a snuffed wick, and found, oddly, that what remained was lighter.

"Pell," she said. "There are two companies coming. One will be here in half an hour. The other is about to have a very wet morning."

He blinked. "...What did you do?"

"Nothing yet. But I know a woman who owes me a ferry ride." Ilva drew her sword—not at her men, but turned flat-side out across the track, a bar of dull iron between them and the mountain. "And I intend to collect."

Sefa had always said the river kept its own accounts, and that she merely acted as its clerk.

The approach to the mountain ran along a shelf of land between the old flood-channel and the cliffs—a road built, if the songs were right, by masons of the country-that-had-no-name, wide enough for four wagons and proud enough to assume no one would ever need to defend it. It assumed wrong. Sefa stood at the head of the flood-channel with her hands on the timber gate she'd spent two days pretending to repair, and grinned into the wind.

"You're sure?" called Toma, the deserter, from beside the spill-lever. His voice cracked on the second word. He'd been a Warden once too, briefly, badly; he understood exactly what they were about to do to his former colleagues.

"Toma." Sefa spat over the edge, ceremonially, into the black water. "I have ferried tax collectors, informers, three bigamists, and one man who tried to pay me with a forged letter of credit. This is the finest thing I will ever do with my river. Pull the lever."

The gate went. The channel, dammed for two days by mud, brushwood, and a truly criminal quantity of stolen canvas, remembered what it was for.

It came down the valley not as a wave—waves are honest—but as a *decision*, a broad brown sheet of water shouldering out of the dark with a sound like a giant clearing its throat. Sefa watched it take the wagon-road, watch the torches of the flanking company scatter and gutter and go out, watched men who had come to burn children out of a marsh suddenly, urgently, become swimmers.

"Not deep enough to drown them," she said, more to herself than anyone. "Not quite."

"You wanted it deeper?" Toma asked.

"I wanted it *honest*," Sefa said. "But we work with what we have." She turned, wiping her hands, and her grin faded into something quieter. Somewhere up-slope, past the pass Ilva was holding and the ruins of the capital-that-wasn't, there was a lantern with a dying coal in it, and a mute child made of key-light, and a charm-shop keeper who had once paid her a secret so heavy she'd wept for an hour and never told a soul what it was.

"Go," she told Toma. "Take the others up to the pass. Tell Brann she can stop being a wall in about ten minutes, because nobody's getting through the wet way either."

"And you?"

"I'm going to sit here," Sefa said, settling herself comfortably on the spilled timbers, "and count the ones who try to climb out. If any of them make it up-slope, I'll sing them a song about it." She patted the short blade at her hip. "A very discouraging song."

Oscar Thackeray had spent twenty years learning the language of small sounds—the tick of cooling brass, the whisper of a floorboard over a hidden brazier, the particular silence of a shop just before a Warden walked in. So he knew, before he saw anything at all, that Merrin Voss had been busy.

The door beneath the mountain had begun to *click*.

Small sounds. Precise sounds. Like a clock counting down somewhere inside the stone. Ash heard it too—the child stood pressed against the great sealed face of the gate, both palms flat against the rock, and every click made them flinch, made the faint golden glow under their skin gutter like a lamp in wind.

"What did you do?" Oscar asked.

Merrin stood ten paces off, near the ruined archway, lantern in hand. Twenty years had silvered him and stiffened him, and the Warden-grey uniform sat on him like a costume he'd forgotten to take off after a play. When he spoke, his voice was almost gentle, which was the worst thing about it.

"The same thing I've been doing since the night we sealed it, Osc. Holding it shut. Only now—" he turned the lantern slightly, and Oscar saw the mechanism within: a lattice of fine iron wire, wound tight around a core of pale crystal, humming with a magic so controlled and so joyless it hurt to look at. "Now I'm doing it faster."

"That's why the seal's failing." It landed in Oscar's chest like a dropped stone. "It was never the fire starving. You've been *bleeding* it. All this time—"

"Someone has to hold the lock, and the lock eats whatever holds it." Merrin's composure slipped, just a hairline crack, and underneath it Oscar glimpsed the boy he'd known, the friend who'd wept in the ashes of the capital and sworn *never again* with such force it had broken them both. "Do you know what it costs, Oscar? Do you know what I gave it? Every year, a little more. My memory of my mother's face. My ability to sleep past dawn. The color blue—I haven't seen blue properly in nine years. And the Council thinks I built the flaw by accident. They think I want to *keep* the door." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "I built the flaw so that someday, someone would have to open it or end it. Either way, it ends. Either way, I'm free."

"So you'd kill the flame."

"I'd kill the flame," Merrin agreed. "Or you'd open the door. And then—" his eyes went to Ash, and something in his face twisted, "—and then the key does what keys do. Dissolves. Becomes the turning. You've worked that out, I think. You have that look."

Ash turned from the door.

Later—much later, in the years of rebuilding—those who were there would each remember this moment differently, but all would agree on one thing: the child did not hesitate. Ash crossed the space between the gate and the lantern in three steps, took the iron handle in both small hands, and looked up at the two old men with an expression of such perfect, furious tenderness that Oscar's heart simply stopped arguing.

Then they lifted the lantern high and tipped it, and the coal of undying flame slid free into their cupped palms like an egg returning to its nest.

"Ash—*" Oscar reached for them.

The light took him at the wrist. Not heat—*recognition*. The fire poured itself up the child's arms and into their chest, and Ash glowed from within, gold bleeding through skin like dawn through shutters, and when they opened their mouth the voice that came out was the door's voice, the old tongue, layered and low and *glad*:

***"Keth-annu. Vael. Keth-annu."***

*Home. Open. Home.*

The clicks inside the stone stopped. In their place came a slow, vast exhalation, the sound of a mountain remembering it had lungs. Seams of light spread outward from beneath Ash's palms, tracing patterns older than the kingdom that had erased itself—doorways within doorways, sigils waking after twenty years of sleep.

And Ash began, gently, at the fingertips, to come apart into light.

"No," Oscar breathed. "No, no—child, listen to me—" He grabbed for their hand and his fingers passed through gold motes and closed on air. Panic rose in him, huge and stupid, the panic of a man who has guarded a door for two decades and only now understands what doors are *for*. "There has to be another way. There's always—Merrin! Your flaw! You keyed the seal to yourself—can't you—"

"I can hold it shut," Merrin said quietly. "I can't hold it *open*. Nothing can, except the key. Except them." And then, softer, almost pleading: "Let it be quick, Osc. Don't make them carry us both."

"I'm not making anyone carry anything!" Oscar rounded on him, and twenty years of weary vigilance finally, gloriously caught fire. "You want absolution, Merrin? You want your freedom bought cheap? Look at them! They walked out of nowhere in the rain, barefoot, mute, sent by a country that doesn't exist anymore to find a shopkeeper who sells charms that don't work—and do you know why? Because somebody *believed* in us. Somebody believed in the two idiots who swore never again. And you want to stand here and let them pay our bill!"

Above them, faintly, came the clash of steel on the pass. Below, the roar of released water. The world converging, as worlds do, on the smallest and most stubborn of human hearts.

Ash smiled at him. Through the dissolving light, through the door-voice, through the terror, they smiled—and raised one fading hand, and shaped, slowly and deliberately, two words in the gesture-language they'd taught him by lamplight in the marsh camp, the words Oscar had learned third, after *eat* and *sleep*:

*Your turn. Then rest.*

The seams of light in the gate widened into a doorway. Beyond it: green. Impossible, impossible green, and the smell of rain on living earth, and something that might have been wind or might have been singing.

Oscar Thackeray looked at the open door. He looked at the child coming apart like sparks off a bonfire. He looked, last, at Merrin—who had fallen to his knees in the dust without seeming to notice, staring at the green country with an expression Oscar had waited twenty years to see on that face: hope, naked and undefended.

And Oscar began, in a voice cracked with disuse, to speak the old tongue.

---

On the pass, Ilva Brann heard the mountain breathe.

She didn't know what it was—none of them did—but every soldier on both sides of her sword stopped fighting at the same instant, the way birds stop singing, and Pell whispered, "What was *that*," and Ilva, bleeding from a cut on her brow, her arms shaking, eleven men and forty yards of broken slate behind her and somehow still unbowed, answered honestly:

"That, Private, is the sound of orders becoming obsolete."

Far below, the flood-water glittered. Far above, the peak of the ruined capital lit from within, gold seeping out of the stone like yolk from a cracked egg. And in the marsh camp behind them, the woman who remembered the old country's songs began, unbidden, to sing—one clear thread of melody rising over the water and the rock, a song about a door, and a hearth, and everyone coming home.

Ilva sheathed her sword, picked up her lantern, and started running toward the light.

* * *

Inside the doorway, the green country waited. And Oscar, with the last of the old tongue rising in his throat and Ash's fading hand pressing him forward, understood at last what the final word of the binding would cost—and stepped into the light anyway.
