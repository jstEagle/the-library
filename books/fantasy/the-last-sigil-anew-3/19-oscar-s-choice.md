## 19. Oscar's Choice

The door was singing.

Oscar had spent twenty years listening to that door—its low mutter through the floorboards of his shop, its grumble in cold weather, its occasional sulky silence when he'd forgotten to feed the brazier properly—and in all those years it had never once sung. Now the whole mountainside rang with it, a note too deep to hear and too large to ignore, felt in the teeth, in the breastbone, in the soles of his boots.

He stood at the threshold of the capital-that-was-no-longer-named, in a hall of black stone that the maps had agreed to forget, and he watched Ash dissolve.

That was the only word for it. The child stood with both palms flat against the door's ancient seam, and where their fingers touched the stone, they were coming apart—not bloodily, not painfully, but like smoke deciding to be light. Edges first. Then hands. The key-light that poured off them turned everything gold: Sefa's river-damp hair, Ilva's cracked knuckles white around her sword hilt, the iron lantern at Oscar's feet where the last coal of the dragon-fire guttered like a candle in a draft.

"Ash," Oscar said, and his voice cracked in the middle like an old plate. "Ash, stop. Stop."

*It doesn't stop,* Ash signed. They had learned signs from the healer, before the ford, before the healer died buying them ninety seconds of running room, and now their fingers moved quick as birds even as the wrists went translucent. *I told you what I am. I told you in the marsh.*

"You told me you were sent," Oscar said. "You didn't tell me you were *spent*."

*There wasn't time.* And there it was again, that terrible practicality, the child who had crossed a country barefoot without ever once complaining about their feet. *The door needs a key. I'm the key. Keys open things. That's all keys are for.*

"No," said Oscar. "That is absolutely not all keys are for. Keys get kept. Keys live in drawers with the good scissors. Keys—" His voice went to pieces again, and he hated it, hated how twenty years of practiced calm could be undone by one small dissolving person.

Behind him, boots on stone. He didn't turn. He knew the tread. Twenty years of friendship teaches you a man's walk better than your own heartbeat.

"Oscar." Merrin Voss stood at the edge of the gold light, and he looked terrible—hollowed out, his Warden-grey coat scorched at the shoulder from the fighting in the pass above, his hands hanging empty at his sides. No sword drawn. That was something. "The seal was keyed to me. I built it that way because I was afraid. If anyone can take Ash's place—"

"Don't." Oscar still hadn't turned. "Don't you dare offer me a martyrdom contest, Merrin Voss, not after everything."

"It's not a contest. It's arithmetic. You're the keeper of the fire. The fire goes through; the fire needs you on the other side to plant it. I'm the lock. Locks belong in doors. It's practically tidy."

"It's practically suicide, and it's *mine* to do, and we will argue about whose guilt is larger when nobody is evaporating." Oscar finally looked at him. Twenty years fell away and then piled right back up. "You keyed it to yourself out of fear. Fine. Then hear this: I'm going to unkey it out of love, and love holds better. Ask any charm I ever sold."

Merrin laughed—a broken, wet sound—and pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "You always did think you were funnier than me."

"I am funnier than you. It's documented."

Above them, faint through the mountain's throat, came the sounds of the battle that was buying them this moment: steel ringing on steel in the pass, the groan and rush of water where Sefa had opened the old flood-channels and turned the eastern approach into a river again, the deep boom-boom-boom of Wardens' siege-drums. Ilva Brann was up there somewhere, holding the line she'd chosen over her badge, buying seconds with her sword the way the dead healer had bought minutes with her life. Every second cost somebody. Oscar felt each one land on him like a stone dropped in his pocket.

And the coal in the lantern guttered lower.

That was the worst of it—the arithmetic under everything. The fire was starving. Each mile they'd carried it, each hour, each fight, it had burned years of itself just to keep burning. What remained would not survive another journey, another winter, another decade sealed beneath a charm shop floor while a country pretended not to exist. It was now or never. Through the door or out forever.

*Oscar.* Ash's signing had slowed. Their forearms were gone now, dissolved into threads of gold that ran into the door's seam like roots into soil. Their face was still theirs, though—the stubborn chin, the wide dark eyes that had stared at him through a rainy shop window on the worst night of his life. *Listen. When I go, the door will ask. It asks everyone. It asked my mother. She answered wrong, or she answered late, and so it made me instead—a key with legs, so it could send itself to find someone who'd answer right.*

"Answer right how?" Oscar whispered.

*In the old tongue. The words I taught you.* Ash had spent weeks teaching him, patiently, word by word by word, in firelight and marsh-mist and the backs of carts: *keeper*, *hearth*, *stay*, *return*. He had thought he was learning phrases. He understood, now, that he had been learning a vow. *It wants someone to say: I will hold the door from within. Not shut it. Hold it. There's a difference. My mother tried to shut it, I think. Shutting is fear. Holding is—*

Their voice ran out. Not their courage—their voice, the last of it, spent into the stone. But their hands finished it, slow and certain:

*Holding is staying because you choose to. Not because you're trapped.*

The hall went very quiet except for the door's song and the far-off war.

Oscar crouched by the lantern. The coal sat in its iron cradle, no bigger than a walnut, red-black, breathing. Twenty years he had fed it—scraps of charm-ash, drops of honey on solstices, his own warmth cupped around it on the coldest nights when the shop was dark and he was lonely enough to talk to it. *Hello, old girl,* he'd say. *Quiet day. Sold three luck-knots to a woman getting married, none of which worked, which is honest work for a luck-knot.* And the coal would brighten, just slightly, the way a cat blinks at you slowly when it has decided you are acceptable.

"I promised you'd see green hills again," he told it now. "I keep my promises. That's the whole job."

Then he stood, and stepped toward the door, and Merrin grabbed his arm.

"Oscar. Oscar, wait—" Merrin's face was wrecked, open, twenty years younger and twenty years more frightened than any Warden-architect should have been allowed to look. "There has to be another way. There's always another way. You taught me that. Back when we built it—you said, *we'll find another way, we always do, we're clever men—*"

"We were clever boys," Oscar said gently, "and we found a way, and it worked, and it cost us everything, and now the way forward costs one thing more. That's not failure, Merrin. That's just the bill arriving." He put his free hand over his friend's grip. "You want to help? Then do the flaw. Mend it. From your side. Because I'm going to need this door solid behind me, and I know—I *know*—you left a weakness in it somewhere, because you've never in your life built anything without a back door for yourself."

Merrin flinched as if struck. Then, slowly, he nodded. "The third stone. Left of the hinge, at knee height. I can mend it from here. It'll hold for—" His voice broke. "It'll hold long enough for them to come back for you. They'll come back for you. That girl won't rest until—"

"I know," Oscar said. "That's rather the point of her."

He turned to Ash one last time.

The child was nearly all light now. Only the face remained, and one hand, hovering above the stone, waiting. Waiting for permission—that was the unbearable part. A key, made flesh, sent across a country barefoot, hunted, mute among strangers, and still polite enough to wait for permission.

Oscar knelt. He took the small hand—it was warm, warm as the brazier, warm as every kindness he had ever failed to say aloud—and pressed it gently to the door himself.

"There's my brave one," he said, and his voice held, mostly. "Now listen carefully, because I'm only doing the accent once."

He spoke the old tongue. His pronunciation was atrocious—he knew it, Ash knew it, possibly the door knew it—but he put his whole heart into it, twenty years of quiet mornings and hidden braziers and small mercies, every unsaid thing, every held breath, every night he'd stayed awake so the flame wouldn't have to gutter alone:

*I will hold the door from within. Not shut it. Hold it. I stay because I choose. Let the fire go home. Let the child be a child. Let the country remember its name.*

The door stopped singing.

For one heartbeat the whole mountain held its breath—and then the seam split open with a sound like dawn breaking, and light poured out, but not gold anymore. Green. Green-gold, the color of hills after rain, the color of a living hearth seen through a window from a long way off, the color of a place that existed, that had always existed, that remembered.

And through it Oscar saw—just a glimpse, just a breath's worth—grass. Actual grass. Rolling away under a sky with clouds in it, real clouds, moving.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, it's *lovely.*"

*Oscar.* Ash's face was streaming tears, and the tears turned to light before they landed. Their hands moved frantically: *Don't. Don't don't don't—*

"Hush." He gathered them in—one armful of almost-nothing, a child made mostly of morning now—and pressed his forehead to theirs the way he'd done every night since the marsh, the little ritual he'd invented without noticing, goodnight, brave one, sleep well, dream of grass. "Listen to me. This isn't goodbye. Goodbye is a closing word, and I have just become professionally opposed to closing things. This is *see you later.* Say it with me. Well—sign it. You know my sign-language is shameful."

Trembling, half-laughing, half-broken, Ash lifted two fingers and tapped twice against their palm. *See you later.*

"That's the one." He set them down facing the open door, and put the lantern in their arms. The coal flared—actually flared, bright as the first day, smelling suddenly of woodsmoke and summer—as if it knew. "Straight through, no dawdling, mind the step. Plant her deep in good hearth-stone. Feed her honey on solstices and complaints on ordinary days; she likes both. And Ash—" he crouched to their height, and the green light made them both look young, "—when you build the new door, build it with a peephole. I want to see the garden."

Ash threw themselves at him, all the light they had left, arms around his neck, and for one moment he held a child, whole and warm and weeping, and then he held light, and then he held the memory of a child, and then the doorway was carrying them away down a hill of grass toward a hearth he could not follow yet—

—yet. He was choosing to believe *yet.* It was the only word worth building a door out of.

He stepped backward over the threshold as the seam began to draw shut behind them, and he felt it take him—not cruelly, nothing about it cruel, more like a coat being fitted, like a name being learned. Stone rose around him gentle as rising water. Through the narrowing gap he could see the others: Sefa sprinting up from the flooded approach, soaked to the ribs and swearing joyfully; Ilva limping down from the pass with her sword over her shoulder and her badge abandoned somewhere in the rocks; Merrin on his knees at the third stone, mending, mending, tears cutting tracks through the dust on his face.

They were shouting his name. All of them. Even Merrin. Especially Merrin.

Oscar smiled, and raised one hand, and called out the last thing he had breath to say in either language, old tongue and new tangled together, a joke and a promise and a prayer all in one:

*"See you later! Mind the shop!"*

The seam closed. The song resumed—deeper now, steadier, a note with a keeper inside it. And somewhere on a green hillside, in a country with its name remembered, a coal of undying dragon-fire touched good hearth-stone and caught.

Far below the mountain, in a city of erased maps, in a charm shop with dusty windows, a bell chimed over an empty doorway, though no one had entered.

On the counter, a luck-knot that had never worked for anyone quietly began to glow.
