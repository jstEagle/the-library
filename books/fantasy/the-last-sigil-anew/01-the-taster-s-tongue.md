## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The poison came in the seventh course, riding on a river of honeyed eel, and Silas Oyelaran knew it the way a man knows rain before the first drop strikes his skin—not by sight or smell, but by some older arithmetic of the body.

He stood at the king's left shoulder in his dove-grey livery, silver spoon at his lips, and tasted it: almond and bitter orange, the court's favorite mask, and beneath them a cold green thread like sap from a cut stem. *Hemlock water-dropwort*, said the ledger in his blood. *Fourth encounter. You will survive this one too.*

He swallowed.

Around him the banquet blazed on—two hundred candles drowning in their own wax, lords and ladies glittering like fish scales, the king at the high table laughing at something Lord Vessic had murmured against his ear. King Aldous was not an old man yet, though the crown had aged him strangely, sitting low and heavy on his brow as if it were less an ornament than a hand pressed to his skull. He laughed, but his eyes did not. They never did, anymore. Silas had noticed that years ago, the way he noticed everything, because noticing was the whole of his trade.

The second spoonful he took slower, letting the poison pool beneath his tongue where the old marks lived—thirty-one scars no physician would ever find, each one a debt the poisons had paid into him and could never collect again. His heart stuttered once, politely, like a servant knocking. Then it went on.

"Hm." He set down the spoon, dabbed his mouth with linen, and turned to the under-steward. "The eel is excellent. Send up my compliments."

The steward beamed. The poisoner, whoever they were—and Silas had three candidates already, by seating arrangement and access to the kitchens—would go on believing their work had merely been slow tonight.

That was the other half of his trade, the half the crown never spoke aloud: not only to die for the king if need be, but to *not* die, quietly, so that the kitchen could be searched tomorrow and the guilty party found with the evidence still warm. A taster who survived loudly saved no one. A taster who survived silently was worth more than a garrison.

Silas folded his hands behind his back and watched the candles gutter, and thought about bread.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its ledgers in a cellar beneath a chandler's shop in Tallow Row, where the smell of tallow and beeswax covered every other scent in the district—a mercy, because the guild dealt in things that had smells no candle could hide.

Silas came home past midnight through the door behind the drying racks, and the family was waiting, the way they always waited, which is to say they pretended not to be.

Grandmother Ash sat nearest the banked hearth, blind eyes fixed on some middle distance where perhaps the future kept shop, her hands moving over a knotted cord she read the way scholars read print. Fenwick the lantern-keeper—or rather, keeper of the bottled light of a summer afternoon in Year of the Ox, which he doled out by the thimbleful to grieving households—was asleep in his chair with his boots off, which meant he'd spent himself empty again on someone else's sorrow. And Wren—

Wren was not there. Of course she wasn't. Hunt-captains did not haunt cellars in Tallow Row, no matter what promises got whispered over the chandler's back fence on nights when the moon was generous.

"You're late," said Ash, without turning her head. "And you've been drinking hemlock."

"Water-dropwort," Silas corrected, shrugging off his coat. "There's a difference, and I'll thank you to respect my profession."

"There's a difference between a knife and a sword, and both of them mean someone wanted you dead." Her hands went still on the knot-cord. "Who?"

"That's tomorrow's problem. Tonight I have accounts to settle." He knelt by the ledger shelf—the real shelf, behind the false one of cookbooks nobody cooked from—and drew out the great book bound in grey leather, its spine stamped with nothing at all. The Guild of Impossible Things did not put its name on anything. Names were how the Hunt found people.

He opened to the current page and began writing by candlelight:

*Bought: one memory, bottled, of a mother's voice singing (the daughter in Cooper's Lane, who has forgotten her own name-day). Price: two jars of firefly-light and a favor owed.*

*Sold: one small miracle, minor category — the mending of a broken promise. The cooper swore he'd come home; the sea disagreed for eleven years. We disagreed with the sea. Sea conceded.*

*Received: bread. Twelve loaves, from the widow Ammer, in payment for the dream we sold her son so he could say goodbye to his father properly. She insists bread is all she has. Bread is never all anyone has, but we take the bread, because refusing a gift kindly given is its own kind of theft.*

The rituals of the guild were small ones, and Silas loved them the way misers love coins: entirely, and without shame. The way Fenwick always left one lamp burning for whoever came home last. The way Ash touched the doorframe twice on entering, once for the living and once for the dead. The way they ate together at midnight whatever hour the day had ended, twelve loaves of widow's bread torn and passed hand to hand, because food shared standing up was charity but food shared sitting down was family.

So they sat. All except Wren, whose chair stayed empty and whose absence sat in it like a guest.

"She'll come when she can," Ash said, reading his silence the way she read everything. "Oaths are heavy things, child. You don't set them down all at once. You set them down the way you set down anything too heavy—you bend first."

"I know how oaths work." He tore a loaf, passed it. "I carry thirty-one of them myself. To a king who doesn't know my face and wouldn't care if he did."

"He knows your face." Ash's blind eyes found him with unsettling accuracy. "Kings know everyone who kneels close enough to die for them. That's the trouble with kings. It's the only knowing they're permitted."

* * *

It was nearly dawn when he finished the ledgers and climbed the cellar stairs to fetch his coat from the hook, and saw the poster.

It had gone up overnight on the chandler's wall outside, paste still wet at the corners, and the chandler himself stood squinting at it in the grey light, his face doing something complicated.

Silas stepped out and read it beside him.

BY ROYAL DECREE, it began, in letters tall as knives.

*Let it be known throughout the realm that a Purging Hunt is proclaimed. All persons bearing unlicensed gifts—miracles, workings, blessings, charms, or any art beyond nature's allowance—are hereby declared abominations before crown and creed. Let them be sought. Let them be found. Let them be brought to the pyre or the pit, and let no house shelter them, for shelter shall share their sentence.*

Below, the royal seal. Below that, the signature of Lord Vessic, Keeper of the Privy Seal, whose ink always looked wet even when it was decades dry.

The chandler spat, carefully, away from the paper. "Third one this morning," he said. "They're on every wall from here to the river. My brother's girl has a gift—small thing, makes bread rise double, never hurt a soul." He shook his head. "What's *double*-rising bread to a king?"

Nothing, thought Silas. And everything. Because gifts were power, and power was fear, and fear was the only crop the crown had ever learned to farm well.

Except—that wasn't quite true either, was it? He had watched the king for nine years from one pace away. Had seen him flinch at nothing. Had seen him laugh without his eyes. Had seen, just last month, the crown seem to *lean* on him as he signed some order or other, the way a hand leans on a cane, and had told himself he imagined it.

Kings were cruel. Everyone knew that. But Silas's tongue knew better than everyone; it had been lied to by experts, and it had learned to taste the truth underneath. And what he had tasted, watching Aldous across nine years of banquets, was not cruelty.

It was something worse. It was *absence*. A man being eaten slowly, from the top down.

"The bread rises double, does it?" Silas said aloud to the chandler. "Send her round to the widow Ammer. Tell her the Guild owes the neighborhood some kindnesses, and we pay in kind."

The chandler looked at him sharply—at the grey livery he hadn't yet changed out of, at the face that belonged to the palace six days of seven—but he only nodded, because Tallow Row kept its accounts the way the guild kept theirs: in trust, and closed mouths.

Silas went back down the stairs. Ash heard his footsteps change weight and set aside her knot-cord.

"Well?" she said. "Say it."

"They've proclaimed the Hunt." He sat down heavily across from her. "Every wall in the city. Ash—it's not a purge. Purges end. This reads like a beginning. And the seal—" He hesitated. "The seal was Vessic's, but the decree itself, the language of it... it doesn't sound like a man giving orders. It sounds like a man *reciting* them. Like he's reading from something older than he is."

Ash was quiet a long moment. The banked fire ticked. Somewhere above, the chandler's shutters opened on the new day, and the city beyond it woke into a world where twelve loaves of bread were now contraband kindness.

"Fetch me the old fragment," she said at last. "The rite-page. The one we keep wrapped in oilcloth because paper remembers what was done to it."

His stomach dropped, slow and cold, like a stone lowered into a well. "Why?"

"Because," Grandmother Ash said, "if the king is reciting, then something is making him. And I have wondered for thirty years why our founder's books speak of a sigil-crown fed on dragon-fire—and why the dragons are all gone, and why the king stopped having a will of his own the very year the last one died."

She turned her blind face toward him, and for the first time in all his years in the guild, Grandmother Ash looked afraid.

"Wake Fenwick, child. And send word to the captain. Whatever comes down that road to us—we will want all our impossible things home before it arrives."
