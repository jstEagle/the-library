## 2. A Hunt Proclaimed

The decree arrived at dawn, carried by a rider whose horse had frothed blood at the bit, and Wren knew before she broke the wax that the world she had sworn to was about to ask her for something terrible.

She read it first alone, in the captain's alcove of Gullsgate Barracks, where the window glass was old and wavy and turned the morning into something underwater. The seal was the crowned stag of King Aldric IV, pressed deep and clean, and beneath it the herald's hand marched in black lines like a column of soldiers:

*By order of the Crown, in this the thirtieth year of the King's reign, let it be proclaimed throughout the realm: that all who bear the tainted gifts — the hedge-witcheries, the hearth-spells, the unlicensed workings of blood and breath — are declared abominations. Let them be named, let them be found, let them be purged. The Hunt is proclaimed. There will be no quarter for the gifted, and no mercy for those who shelter them.*

*Purge the taint. Preserve the throne.*

Wren read it three times. The words did not soften with repetition. If anything they grew heavier, the way a corpse grows heavier the longer you carry it.

Outside, her squad was assembling in the yard for morning drill — she could hear Ferren's voice calling the count, hear boots on wet cobbles, hear someone laughing about breakfast. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a world that still believed itself decent.

She thought of Sera.

Her sister lived two days' ride south, in a village called Mirefen where the houses leaned together like conspirators and everyone grew onions and kept their opinions salted away like winter stores. Sera had married a cooper there. Sera had two children now, twins, gap-toothed and loud. And Sera could make bread rise with a touch and a whispered word, could coax a fever down through a child's scalp, could find water under dry ground the way other women found coins between couch cushions.

A small gift. A hearth-gift. The kind the decree's fine print devoured along with all the rest.

*No mercy for those who shelter them.* Which meant, if the lists ever reached Mirefen, no mercy for the cooper either. No mercy for the twins.

Wren set the decree down on the scarred oak table and pressed both palms flat against its surface, as if she could hold the world steady by leaning on furniture. Her oath was stitched into her left forearm — not literally, though sometimes it felt literal, an itch beneath the skin: *I serve the crown, I guard the realm, I keep the king's peace.* She had spoken those words at seventeen, kneeling on cold stone, so full of purpose that she'd trembled. Her father had been a soldier. Her grandfather too. Duty ran in the family like a vein of ore.

Duty, she had always believed, was a compass. It pointed one direction. You walked.

But compasses assumed the north held still.

She took up the decree again, rolled it, and went down to the yard to do the thing captains do with unbearable news, which is to deliver it in a voice that gives nothing away.

* * *

The squad stood in formation under the grey sky, breath steaming, twenty-two faces turned toward her. Ferren at the front rank, broad and reliable, his jaw already set the way it set when he smelled trouble. Beside him little Osma, who still wrote letters home to her mother every week. Behind them the rest, a mix of veterans and green recruits, spears shouldered, helmets dull with drizzle.

"Stand easy," Wren said, and they shifted, and the yard settled into that attentive silence soldiers make when they sense the shape of what's coming.

She unrolled the parchment. "This came from the capital before first light. I'm required to read it to you verbatim." Her voice sounded strange to her own ears — level, official, a voice belonging to someone else wearing her throat. "So I will."

And she read it. All of it. The proclamation, the naming of gifts as abominations, the licensing of informants, the bounty schedule — she made herself read the bounty schedule, ten crowns silver per confirmed bearer reported, fifty per bearer taken — and while she read she watched the faces in front of her change.

It happened the way frost happens. Not all at once but everywhere at once. A tightening here, a flicker there. Osma's mouth going small and hard. Ferren's eyes moving, just once, toward the barracks gate — and Wren knew why. Everyone in Gullsgate knew why. Old Tam the lamplighter, who could light his wicks with a snap of his fingers and had been doing it, gently and illegally, for thirty years. Half the city loved him. He'd lit the lamps at Ferren's mother's funeral without being asked.

"There will be questions," Wren finished. "There will be orders. Some of them will be hard." She rolled the parchment closed. "Dismissed to drill."

They didn't move immediately. That half-second of collective stillness said more than any speech could have. Then Ferren barked the count and the ranks broke apart into practice pairs, and the yard filled with the clatter of wooden staves, and everything looked normal again, the way a house looks normal after the argument has only been postponed.

Wren spent the morning watching her squad pretend. She watched Osma throw herself into drills with a violence that had grief in it somewhere — Wren would learn later that Osma's grandmother had been a charm-mender in the river towns. She watched the recruits look to the veterans, and the veterans look at the ground. She watched the oath curdle in all of them, or begin to, and she understood that she was not alone in what she felt; she was merely the one who had to feel it first, and loudest, and in charge.

At midday a courier found her in the map room. Second dispatch of the day. This one bore no royal seal — it bore the seal of Lord Vessic, Keeper of the Household, which was stranger and worse, because Vessic's hand rarely reached this far from the palace unless something mattered very much.

The orders were brief. *Captain Wren of Gullsgate is commanded to conduct a raid upon premises in the Tanner's Row district, suspected of harboring unlicensed bearers. Raid to be executed this night, before moonrise. Discretion paramount. Report directly to the Keeper's office.*

Attached was a list. Three names. One of them was a chandler's widow known to give blessings over cradles. Another was a boy of perhaps fourteen seen making gutter-water run clear. The third name was simply *the establishment known as the sign of the Broken Compass,* and beneath it, in smaller ink, a note: *believed to be a gathering-house of the trade in impossible things.*

Wren stood over the map of Tanner's Row for a long time. The candle burned. Somewhere below, the mess hall clattered with supper.

She had raided places before. Smugglers' dens, a counterfeiter's loft, once a pit-fighting ring that had made even Ferren sick. She knew the mechanics: surround, breach, secure, sort the guilty from the incidental. She was good at it. That was the whole horror of competence — it kept working whether or not your heart agreed.

But tonight's mechanics ended differently. Tonight's mechanics ended with people who lit lamps and blessed cradles being dragged through the streets in chains, or worse — because *purged* was a word with teeth in it, and everyone in the yard this morning had heard the teeth.

And underneath the professional arithmetic, quieter, constant as tinnitus: *Sera.* Because if this was the law now, then the law did not stop at Mirefen. The law would come south eventually, with its lists and its bounty schedule, and it would knock on her sister's door, and some captain exactly like her — young, dutiful, good at mechanics — would be the one holding the paper.

Unless the law changed. Unless someone bent it first.

Or unless captains stopped delivering.

"No," Wren said aloud, to the empty map room, testing the word in her mouth the way you test ice before trusting it with your weight.

Then she went down to the yard, where the evening shift was forming, and gave the orders in her captain's voice, and hated herself cleanly and completely while she did it.

* * *

Tanner's Row at night smelled of lime pits and old blood, the honest stink of a trade that never pretended to be pretty. Wren took the raiding party in quiet — twelve soldiers, torches unlit, moving through streets gone soft with fog off the river. Ferren walked at her shoulder. He hadn't asked a single question when she briefed them, which was itself a question.

The safehouse — if that's what it was — occupied a narrow house behind a rope-maker's yard, three stories, shuttered, dark. A sign hung above its door: a painted compass rose with the needle snapped off. The Broken Compass. Someone had repainted it recently; the colors were bright against the rot of the street.

Wren posted her pairs at the corners and the back. Standard. Textbook. Then she stood before the door with her fist raised to knock the signal — three blows, pause, two — and did not knock.

One breath. Two.

Inside, faintly, she heard voices. Not panicked voices. The low murmur of people sharing an evening — a woman laughing softly at something, the clink of crockery. The sound of a family that didn't know yet, or had chosen to believe the night was ordinary.

Ferren shifted behind her. "Captain?"

"I'm thinking," she said.

"About what?" And he asked it gently, which was the worst part, because Ferren had served under her for six years and had earned the right to be blunt, and instead he offered her gentleness like a hand extended over a drop.

She thought about the oath. *I serve the crown, I guard the realm, I keep the king's peace.* She tried to fit tonight inside those words and found they had become a coat cut for a smaller woman. She thought about Sera's hands rising from bread dough dusted white, about old Tam snapping his fingers at the lamplight, about the boy who made gutter-water run clear — fourteen years old, on a list, priced at ten crowns silver.

She thought: *a compass assumes the north holds still.*

She knocked. Three blows. Pause. Two.

Doors opened on both sides of her intention — the safehouse door swinging inward on lamplight and startled faces, and behind her, at the street's mouth, the sound she had been dreading since dusk: boots. Many boots. A second party, moving fast, torchlight blooming orange through the fog.

Not her men. She knew her men's cadence. These came with the particular rhythm of the palace companies — Vessic's own, the ones that didn't drill in public, the ones people spoke of only in lowered voices.

"Wren." The man leading them stepped into the torchlight. Captain Dree of the Household Guard, a neat, grey man with a voice like filing paper. "Lord Vessic anticipated the possibility of hesitation. You're relieved of command of this action. Stand aside."

Behind her, inside the doorway, someone whispered a word, and every lamp in the house went out at once, and the darkness that followed was the truest thing Wren had ever stood inside.

She looked at Dree. She looked back at Ferren, at her twelve, at their faces turning to her out of the fog, waiting — all of them waiting — to see which way her needle pointed.

And Wren reached into her coat, drew out the folded orders with Vessic's seal, and held them up to the nearest torch until they caught.

The flame took the parchment edge-first, curling it brown, then black, the Keeper's words glowing briefly red like embers in a grate. She let it burn down to her gloves. Then she dropped the ashes in the gutter, where the fog dampened them to nothing.

"That," she said to Dree, in a voice that shook only a little, "is my report."

Somewhere across the rooftops, a bell began to ring — alarm, alarm, the city waking — and Wren stepped backward through the safehouse door, into the dark, into the company of strangers and impossible things, and heard the door close behind her like the sound of an oath breaking, which is not so different, she would remember afterward, from the sound of one beginning.
