## 3. Ledgers of Impossible Things

The safehouse smelled of tallow, ink, and cardamom—the way home always did when the family gathered.

Silas climbed the last stair from the cellar with the ledgers strapped to his back like a second spine. Nine books, bound in mismatched leather, each one a life the crown would burn if it could read them. He had carried them through three raids now, sleeping with their corners pressed into his ribs until he dreamed in columns of figures.

Below, the kitchen was full.

Not crowded—full. There was a difference, and Silas had learned it here. Crowds pressed; families held. Marisol sat on the table itself because she had never once in her life used a chair that wasn't someone else's, her gift humming faintly off her skin so that the candle flames leaned toward her like sunflowers. Tobbin was asleep in the bread basket, which was where Tobbin slept whenever he wasn't needed, being nine and made mostly of appetite. Old Ferren had wedged himself by the hearth with his hands open in his lap, palms up, as if waiting to catch something—which, given that his gift was catching things falling out of the sky, was not far wrong. And at the head of the table, in the chair that had been hers since before any of them were born, sat Grandmother Ash.

Her eyes were white as boiled milk. She turned her face toward the door before Silas touched it.

"You're late," she said. "And you're bleeding."

"A graze." He set the ledgers down one by one, and the room quieted the way it always did, the way a chapel quiets. "A poster-man saw me reading one of the proclamations too long. He wanted to know why a food-taster cared about the Purging."

Marisol's flame-colored eyes narrowed. "What did you tell him?"

"That I care about the price of mutton. That the Hunt will make meat scarce and my tongue is too expensive to waste on gristle." He pulled out the stool beside Ash. "He laughed. I laughed. We are both very good at laughing at things that frighten us."

Tobbin woke up inside the bread basket just enough to say, "I'm frightened," and went back to sleep, and no one laughed at all after that.

* * *

They argued the way they always argued: in circles, around the fire, with the proclamations spread across the table like tarot cards.

FLEE, said the first one, in effect. The king's seal dripped red wax onto the word *gifted*, and beneath it, in the beautiful looping hand of some clerk who had never seen a miracle die: *Let all who bear or harbor unnatural gifts present themselves for clemency. Let all who shelter them be counted among them.*

"Clemency," Ferren said, spitting into the hearth. "My brother took clemency in the last purge. They gave him a cell and a name tag and thirty years of silence."

"Then we fight," said Marisol, and the candles flared. She caught herself, breathed, and the flames sank. Even her temper was a thing she rationed. "There are forty of us in this house alone if we count the ones in the walls. Two hundred across the city. We could—"

"We could what?" Ferren's voice was gentle, which was how he said terrible things. "Light fires? I've watched you light fires, girl. They're beautiful. They're also visible from the palace."

"Then we hide," Tobbin said, awake again, small voice enormous in the quiet. "We've hidden before. We're good at hiding."

"The safehouse in Copperlane hid," Silas said quietly. "It burned anyway. Last night. Wren Thackeray's squad stood outside it and did nothing while the fire-scribes did their work—and then she let two children walk out through her line without a word."

Ash's blind face lifted. "You know this how?"

"I keep accounts, Grandmother. People pay me in truths." He looked down at the wax-sealed decree. "She hesitated. One breath too long. The other captains noticed. By tomorrow she'll be branded, or dead, or both."

"And why does that matter to you?" Ash asked, mild as milk.

Silas didn't answer, because the honest answer was that it mattered in a way he hadn't yet entered in any ledger, and unentered debts frightened him more than proclamations did.

* * *

Later, when the others had gone up to their pallets and Tobbin was dreaming in the flour bin, Ash drew Silas to the window seat with two fingers hooked in his sleeve.

"Show me your hands," she said.

He gave them to her. Her fingers were cool and dry and terribly precise, reading the lines of his palms the way he read the columns of his books.

"Thirty-one poisons," she murmured. "That's what your blood says tonight. Thirty-one deaths you swallowed and digested and kept."

"How can you possibly—"

"I was the Guild's founder, child, not its grandmother by accident. Before my eyes went out I could see workings—the threads under the world. Now I see them differently. Through skin." Her thumb found the old scar on his wrist, the one shaped like a coiled serpent, left there by a viper's venom when he was nineteen. "Every poison is a sigil, did you know that? A little working written in harm. When you survive one, you don't destroy it. You *keep* it. Your body is a ledger of deaths, Silas Oyelaran. A book of sigils, bound in flesh instead of leather."

His mouth had gone dry. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because tonight I need to tell you about the crown." She turned her ruined eyes toward the dark window, toward the distant smudge of the palace against the stars. "Everyone believes King Aldous is cruel. Cruel kings are convenient. You can hate them cleanly." Her voice dropped. "But thirty years ago, I read the working on that throne myself, before they blinded me for reading it. It is a crown of iron and dragon-fire, forged in secret from the last living ember in the world. It is a sigil, Silas—a binding. It feeds on the king's will the way a leech feeds on blood. Every decree, every purge, every hunt—it is not Aldous who wants these things. It is the crown, wearing him."

The words landed in Silas's chest like swallowed glass. He thought of the banquet, the king's face above the goblets—that strange flicker, that half-second where the royal eyes had seemed to *plead* before smoothing over into indifference. He had logged it as indigestion. He had logged it as nothing.

"He's a prisoner," Silas said. "On the most comfortable chair in the realm."

"He is a prisoner, and his jailer is dying. Dragon-fire is not endless, whatever the ballads say. It gutters, child. And when it goes out—" Ash's grip tightened on his hands, hard as a shackle, "—every gift in this realm goes out with it. Because the fire was never only fuel for the crown. It was the wellspring. All our impossible things, all our little miracles—they are tributaries of that one burning river. Marisol's warmth. Ferren's catching. My sight, such as it was. When the ember dies, the gifts die, and the gifted become merely people, and merely-people the crown can kill without ceremony."

Silas sat very still. Below them, faintly, he could hear Tobbin talking in his sleep to someone who was not there—children with gifts often did that, conversing with the quiet.

"The rite," Ash said. "There is an unbinding. Fragmentary—I have pieces of it, recovered over thirty years, hidden in places even I've forgotten. But the working requires a blood that has already refused death. A ledger of survived poisons. A body that knows every way the crown's own sigil-language can kill, and has answered *no* to each of them." Her blind eyes found his face with terrible accuracy. "It requires you, Silas."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard sighed under someone turning in sleep.

"And the price?" he asked, because he was a taster and an accountant and he knew that nothing in this world came without a figure in the margin.

Ash was silent a long moment. Then she said, gently, the gentlest cruelty he had ever heard: "Ask me again when you've decided whether to run."

* * *

He did not sleep. He went down to the cellar instead, where the ledgers lived in their locked chest, and he opened the ninth book—the newest one, the one with his own handwriting still sharp on the page—and he read the entry he'd made at the banquet, hours before the world changed:

*Received: 1 memory (bottled, first snowfall of childhood), from the widow Anselm.*
*Paid: 3 loaves, 1 jar of honey, 1 hour of listening.*
*Balance: fair.*

Such small accounts. Such enormous ones. He thought of Marisol rationing her own temper like coin. Of Ferren's open hands. Of Tobbin, asleep in the bread because bread meant safety, and safety meant family, and family was the only treasury the guild had ever truly kept.

If the ember died, all of it guttered. Not just the gifts—the *keeping*. The whole impossible architecture of people who traded miracles for bread because the world wouldn't house them otherwise.

He turned to a fresh page. His pen hovered.

*Fear,* he wrote at last, *is a debt like any other. It accrues interest while you ignore it.*

Then, beneath, in the column where he recorded assets:

*1 body, marked with thirty-one refused deaths.*
*1 rite, incomplete.*
*1 ember, dying.*
*1 question I am afraid to ask Grandmother Ash, because I already suspect the answer.*

He blew out the lamp and sat in the dark with his blood humming its thirty-one quiet refusals, and he understood, finally, what the fear had been trying to become all night. It was not fear anymore. It was purpose, fragile as a match-flame, cupped in both hands.

Above him, through the floorboards, came the sound of the front bell—one sharp ring, past midnight, the ring they'd agreed meant *running* or *hunting* but never anything kind.

Ferren's voice drifted down the stairs, tight and low:

"There's a woman at the door in a hunt-captain's coat. She says her name is Wren. She says the guild sent her. And Silas—" a pause, "—she's carrying her orders, and they're on fire in her hand."
