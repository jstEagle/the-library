## 4. The Captain Who Burned Her Orders

The ash of her orders still tasted in Wren's mouth like a promise.

She had burned them at the boundary stone on the Vell Road, kneeling in the wet grass with her squad's lanterns bobbing away behind her like a string of dying stars, and she had watched Captain Ondrey's seal curl and blacken—the king's hawk, the words PURGING HUNT, the list of names, her sister's name among them—and when the last corner flared white and was gone, she had pressed her palm flat against the cold stone as if swearing a new oath to it. No god had witnessed it. Only the rain, and the road, and whatever slept beneath the city listening.

Three days later they were calling her traitor in every garrison from the Salt Gate to the Tanners' Quarter. Three days, and her face was already inked onto the same kind of poster that had gone up for the gifted—WREN VALDERA, CAPTAIN, SECOND COMPANY, WANTED ALIVE FOR QUESTIONING—and she had learned what it was to walk through the world wearing your own name like a wound.

*Alive for questioning.* She knew what questioning meant in the cells under the Bastion. She had escorted enough people down those stairs.

So she did the only thing left that felt like duty: she went looking for the people she'd been ordered to hunt.

The guild's safehouse was supposed to be ash. She had seen the smoke herself on the night of the decree—a black pillar standing over Candlemakers' Row like an accusation—and she had stood in the street with her hand on her sword hilt, doing nothing, while everything inside that building burned or fled. But Grandmother Ash had not been blind for sixty years without learning how to disappear, and if anyone in this city knew where the impossible things had gone to ground, it would be the woman who kept ledgers of them.

Wren found the trail by accident, or by the kind of instinct that masquerades as accident. A chalk mark on a cistern grate in the Ropewalks—a spiral with a line struck through it, small enough that a tired captain's eye would pass over it a hundred times. She had seen the mark once before, years ago, on the wrist of a fruit-seller who had made her a bruised peach taste like summer, and had said nothing, because even then some part of her had known that reporting kindness would be the first crack in her armor.

She memorized the mark. She came back after midnight, out of uniform, in a stolen fishwife's cloak that smelled of brine and old oil, and she knelt at the grate and waited like a beggar outside a temple.

The grate opened from below with a scrape like a knife being drawn.

"Well," said a voice, dry as paper and twice as sharp. "The Hunt has learned to kneel."

* * *

They did not take her sword away. That was the first thing that surprised her—that Grandmother Ash, seated in a chair of salvaged oak in a cellar that smelled of tallow and dried lavender, simply looked at her with those milk-pale eyes and said, "Keep it. If we cannot trust a woman with her own blade, we cannot trust her at all."

The second surprise was the ledger room.

It was not the candlelit den of conspirators she had imagined. It was a kitchen. Someone was frying onions. A boy of perhaps fifteen sat cross-legged on a table mending a net with hands that moved too fast, his fingers blurring like hummingbird wings, and a woman with iron-grey braids was arguing gently with him about whether he was mending the net or unraveling time itself. Jars lined the walls—jars of honey, jars of salt, jars of things Wren could not name, one of which glowed faintly, like a firefly that had decided to be a jar instead. On the long table between the onion pan and the door lay open books. Ledgers. Columns of entries in a dozen hands:

*Bottled memory of first snow — traded to the cooper's daughter, three loaves, one blanket.*

*A song that cures fevers — given freely to the Ropewalks, no charge. Ash insists.*

*One impossible thing, unnamed — owed to S.O., against a day I hope never comes.*

"S.O.," Wren read aloud before she could stop herself.

"Silas," said the boy with the quick hands, not looking up. "He's not here. He's—" The woman with the braids cleared her throat, and the boy's mouth snapped shut with the particular guilt of someone who has almost said too much.

"Where," Wren said slowly, "is Silas."

Grandmother Ash folded her hands. "At present, Captain Valdera, I would wager my remaining teeth that he is crawling through the palace archives with a candle stub and a very bad plan, because tonight is the one night in the season when the archive scribes drink past their sense." Her blind eyes found Wren with unnerving accuracy. "And since you were, until three days ago, the crown's own hound, I imagine you know the archives better than any of us. And since you are now the crown's most hunted traitor, I imagine you have nowhere else you'd rather be than underground with us."

"That's not—"

"You burned your orders." Ash's voice softened, which was somehow worse. "Ondrey's whole company knows. Half the city knows. Whatever you think you're protecting by pretending you haven't chosen a side, girl—you chose it at the boundary stone. You just haven't told yourself yet."

The onions hissed. The boy's needle paused. Wren stood in a stranger's kitchen with her hand nowhere near her sword, and felt the last wall inside her go down as quietly as a tide going out.

"When does he come back?" she asked.

"He doesn't come back," said the woman with the braids grimly. "That's rather the problem. He goes out. Coming back requires luck, and our Silas spends luck like a drunkard spends wages."

* * *

The palace cisterns smelled of drowned centuries.

Wren went down into them alone, because Ash had put a hand on her arm at the grate and said, "Bring him home, and I'll consider your debt paid," though what debt Wren owed or to whom, she couldn't say. The water was shin-deep and cold enough to make her teeth ache; the walls wept mineral tears that caught her shuttered lantern in veins of green and white; somewhere ahead, water dripped in a rhythm so patient it seemed to be counting something—her heartbeats, maybe, or the seconds left to her old life.

She heard him before she saw him. A soft curse, very refined, echoing off the vaulting.

Then a light bloomed around a buttress ahead—not a lantern but a candle stub stuck to a flat stone, and crouched beside it, soaked to the thighs, a lean man in servant grey was prying at an iron hatch with a knife and muttering to it in the coaxing tone other men used on horses.

"—I have been nothing but respectful," he was telling the lock. "I bypassed your cousin, the grate. I complimented your architecture. Open."

"Don't," Wren said.

He didn't jump. That was the first thing she noticed about Silas Oyelaran, though she wouldn't learn his name until later: he went very still instead, the way prey animals do, and turned his head just enough to show one dark eye and a smile that was trying hard to be charming and mostly achieving exhausted.

"A fishwife," he said, taking in the cloak. "With a soldier's stance and a sword under all that brine. Either the fishwives of this city have changed considerably since breakfast, or the Hunt sends its prettiest down the sewers now."

"I'm not Hunt. Not anymore." She stepped into the candlelight. He looked at her face properly then, and she watched recognition move through him like cold water—she saw him place her, wanted-poster by wanted-poster, and she waited for him to run or shout or draw.

Instead he sighed and leaned back against the hatch. "Of course," he said. "Of course the night I finally find the sigil-registry, the universe sends me a hunt-captain. Ash will laugh at me for a year. She'll do it kindly, which is worse."

"I'm not here for you."

"No one ever is. That's what makes it hurt." He held up the knife. "This lock and I are having a private disagreement about a registry of bindings—crown-work, thirty years old, sealed records. You may know it. There's a wing of the archives the servants aren't allowed to dust."

Wren's breath stopped in her throat. Bindings. Crown-work. Thirty years—the exact span of the king's strange, gentle, hollow reign, the span during which His Majesty had signed every decree put in front of him and never once, in living memory, been seen to choose anything.

"The sealed east annex," she said. "Third door past the cartography vault. The lock isn't the problem—it's warded. It tastes the blood of whoever touches it and screams the guard rotation awake."

Silas stared at her. "You know that how?"

"Because I arrested a thief there two winters ago who tried it. I read the incident report. I remember things." She was already moving past him, boots splashing, pulling the fishwife cloak off over her head and dropping it into the water. Underneath she was still in her blacks—old habit, and thank every indifferent god for it. "Move aside."

"Wren Valdera," he said softly, reading her the way she'd read the posters. "You're more trouble alive than dead, you know. Every guard in that palace has your face in his pocket."

"Then I'd best not meet any guards." She drew her knife across the pad of her thumb and pressed blood to the ward-seal, and felt the little hum of the working taste her, consider her, and—because the Hunt's wards knew her blood as friend, as officer, as theirs—go quiet as a sleeping dog. The hatch clicked open.

Behind her, Silas let out a low whistle. "You just opened the crown's own archive with the crown's own trust. That's either the bravest or the saddest thing I've ever seen done with loyalty."

"It's both," Wren said. "Come on."

* * *

They nearly died twice in the archives, and both times she noticed the same thing about him: Silas Oyelaran did not freeze, and he did not flee. When the patrol's lanterns swept the map-room, he poured himself into the shadow behind a globe stand with a fluid economy that spoke of practice, and when a scribe stumbled drunk into the corridor, Silas stepped out and became—instantly, seamlessly—a lost scullery man asking directions to the privy, bowing and apologizing so sincerely that the scribe apologized back and gave him wine.

An actor. A survivor. A man whose body was a ledger of close calls.

But it was in the cisterns again, on the way home with the registry pages sewn into Silas's coat lining, that the night tilted sideways and became something else.

The alarm bells had started—of course they had; her blood on the ward would show in the morning log—but the underground swallowed sound strangely, and for a few minutes there was only water and dripping and the two of them wading side by side through the dark. Then a section grating groaned overhead and a shaft of lamplight fell through, and Silas pulled her back against the wall into a pocket of shadow barely wide enough for one.

He was warm. That was the indecent thing, the thing she would turn over at 3 a.m. for weeks afterward: after the cold water and the fear, his shoulder against hers was *warm*, and his heartbeat, where her arm pressed his chest, was quick but steady, and he smelled of candle smoke and onions and underneath it something faintly bitter, like the ghost of every poison he'd ever swallowed for another man's safety.

"Lanterns," he breathed, unnecessarily, his mouth an inch from her ear.

"I can see them."

"They're coming from the north gallery. We should—"

"I know."

Neither of them moved. The lantern-light crawled along the ceiling far above, touched the far grating, and slid away, and still neither of them moved, and in the darkness Wren became aware that she had turned her head and he had turned his, and their faces were close enough that she could feel his breath, and that some reckless animal part of her—the part that had burned her orders, the part that had always known duty was a poor substitute for wanting—was leaning closer.

"We shouldn't," Silas whispered.

"No," Wren agreed.

They stayed exactly where they were for eleven more heartbeats, which she counted, because counting was the only discipline she had left. Then the moment broke like ice underfoot, and they waded on in a silence that was not uncomfortable so much as *loaded*, a silence full of everything they'd agreed not to say.

At the guild grate, he paused with one hand on the iron ring.

"For what it's worth," he said, not quite looking at her, "the report said you hesitated one breath too long before raiding the safehouse on Candlemakers' Row. One breath. I've been thinking about that breath all week." He pulled the ring; the grate swung wide on warmth and onion-smell and lamplight below. "Most people never spend theirs."

Inside, Grandmother Ash took the registry pages, ran her fingers over the wax seals, and went white as salt.

"Silas," she said quietly. "These aren't records of the crown's bindings." Her blind eyes lifted, and Wren saw fear move through the old woman like a current through deep water. "These are maintenance schedules. The crown is fed, child. Regularly. By a keeper. And according to this—" her finger stopped on a line near the bottom, "—the next feeding is in six days. At the coronation masque. And if it misses its feeding—"

She didn't finish. Nobody needed her to. Somewhere above them, far off, the dragon-fire that had burned beneath the throne for thirty years guttered, and every gift in the city flickered with it—and in the sudden hush, Wren reached the grate and barred it behind them, and found her hand was shaking, and found that Silas had stepped back to stand beside her without seeming to notice he'd done it at all.
