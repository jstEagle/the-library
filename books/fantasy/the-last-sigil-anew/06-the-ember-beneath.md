## 6. The Ember Beneath

The way down began behind a wall of rain-barrels in the tanner's row, three streets from the palace's western face, and Grandmother Ash found it the way she found everything: by listening.

"Stone remembers," she said, one hand flat against the damp brick while Silas pried at the seam with a knife. "Every weight that's ever pressed on it. Every footfall. This wall has been listening to footsteps going down for two hundred years, and it is very tired of keeping the secret." She smiled her terrible blind smile. "So am I."

The door, when it came loose, exhaled air that smelled of deep water and old copper and something else beneath—something Wren had no name for until she was three turns of the stair below the street, and then she did: it smelled like the moment before lightning. Like the sky holding its breath.

She went last, as captains do. Behind her, the guild descended in their odd procession: Ash with her white stick tapping a rhythm Wren slowly understood was not for finding the path but for comforting it; Marisol with her bottled light uncorked a finger's width, spilling a glow the color of honey; Tam and his sister Odile walking hand in hand because neither could bear the dark alone; and Silas ahead of them all, silent, carrying a satchel of vials that clinked softly at every step like a purse of small coins.

He had not looked at her since the cisterns. Not properly. She told herself it was the climb, the caution, the weight of what waited below. She told herself a great many things, the way she had once told herself the Purging was justice, the way she had told herself her sister's gift was an aberration rather than a grace. Wren Thackeray was learning that she had spent her whole life telling herself things, and that the truth had been waiting patiently underneath all of it, like this stair, like this dark, like the smell of held breath growing stronger with every step.

"Ash," she said finally, because someone had to ask. "What exactly are we walking toward?"

The old woman did not break stride. "The beginning," she said. "Or very nearly. Everything in this realm that cannot be explained—the gifts, the graces, the small impossible mercies—it all comes from fire. From *her* fire. And she has been pouring it out through a crown for thirty years to keep one frightened man's will from drowning him." Her stick tapped. Tapped. Tapped. "We are walking toward the bottom of the world's account book. And accounts, as our Silas knows, always come due."

* * *

They passed through the old cistern first, and Wren's heart clenched at it—the black water, the arches receding into darkness like the ribs of some drowned cathedral, the place where she and Silas had run side by side with the archive seals still warm in their fists. He had laughed there. Once. A short, startled sound, as if laughter were something he'd swallowed long ago and wasn't sure he could keep down. She had thought about that laugh for six days without admitting she was thinking about it.

Now they crossed a plank over the same black water, and Marisol's honey-light made gold coins of every drop that fell from the vaulted ceiling, and no one spoke, because the dark here was not empty. It was *attentive*.

Beyond the cistern, the passage changed. The masonry gave way to raw stone, then to stone that was not stone at all but something fused, glassy, veined with threads that glimmered faintly when Tam's breath touched them—threads the color of embers banked too long in a dying grate.

"The veins were rivers once," Ash said softly. "I walked this place when I was younger than you, girl, before the binding, before the crown. The whole mountain under the palace glowed like a forge at dawn. The king's grandfather built his throne room directly above it, you know. They called it wisdom, sitting your power above your own hearth." Her mouth twisted. "It was theft. It was always theft. They just dressed it in ceremony."

"How do you know all this?" Odile whispered.

"Because I was there when they decided to do it." Ash said it the way one says *it rained today*. "And I have been waiting thirty years for someone foolish enough to come back down with me."

Wren wanted to ask. The question rose in her like water up a well-shaft—but the passage opened, and every question she owned fell out of her at once.

* * *

The chamber beneath the palace was vast beyond sense, a hollow in the world's bones tall enough to hold the palace itself, and it was lit—not by Marisol's bottle, not by any flame Wren knew, but by the dragon.

She lay coiled in the center of the hollow like a hill that had decided to breathe, and Wren understood in one lurching instant that everything she had ever believed about scale was a child's misunderstanding. The dragon was vast and she was *dying*, and both truths were written in the same script: scales gone dull as old pewter, wings folded tight as a moth's closed case, and along the length of her body the ember-veins flickering in a rhythm that was unmistakably, horribly, a heartbeat missing every third beat.

Her eye was open. It was the size of a cart wheel and the color of molten amber going gray at the rim, and it turned—slowly, with effort that made Wren's chest ache—to find them at the edge of her shadow.

"*Small ones,*" the dragon said, and her voice was not sound so much as warmth, a hearth-voice that moved through Wren's bones and lit every candle of memory she possessed—her mother's kitchen, her sister laughing, the first time she'd held a sword and felt, wrongly, that she'd finally become real. "*You came down the old stair. No one has used the old stair since they sealed my fire away in metal.*"

"We've come to unbind the king," Silas said. His voice was steady. Wren marveled at how steady, knowing now what she knew about what steadiness cost him.

The dragon's great head lowered, slow as a season changing. "*The king,*" she repeated, and there was something in it that might have been pity. "*Yes. Vessic forged the crown from my fire when I would not give it freely. Thirty years I have poured myself through that sigil, holding one man's soul together because the alternative was a tyrant wearing his face. Do you know what it is, small ones, to be a lamp? To burn only as another allows?*" The ember-veins guttered. For one horrible moment the whole chamber went dim, and Wren heard Odile whimper, heard Tam's hand tighten on hers, felt her own heart stutter in sympathy. Then the light breathed back. Weaker.

"You're guttering out," Marisol said, and her voice broke on it. She was the guild's trader in bottled memories, and Wren realized she was looking at the oldest memory in the world and watching it die.

"*I am,*" the dragon agreed, without self-pity, which was somehow worse. "*Thirty years of siphon. But listen, and listen carefully, because I have strength for perhaps one more telling. The sigil can be cut. The crown can be broken without killing the man beneath it. But sigils are not cut with steel or with words. They are cut with payment. And the price is specific.*"

Ash stepped forward, white stick raised, and her blind face was turned up toward the dragon like a sunflower to a sun it could not see. "Tell them," she said. "You owe me that much, old friend. Tell them true."

"*The sigil can only be cut,*" the dragon said, and each word came slower than the last, like a bell tolling further away, "*by blood that has already swallowed death and lived. Poison after poison, death after death, taken into the body willingly, borne, survived. A ledger of endings written in flesh. Only such blood knows the grammar of the sigil. Only such blood can read itself aloud to unmake what was written.*"

Silence. Wren turned, very slowly, and looked at Silas Oyelaran, royal food-taster, keeper of poisons, whose body she had just learned was a map of every death the court had ever tried to feed its king.

His face told her nothing. That was the thing about him—she was learning it, learning him, the way one learns a language—and his face told her nothing at all, and that nothing was the loudest confession she had ever seen.

"No," she said.

"Wren—" he began.

"*No.*" It came out captain-voiced, parade-ground hard, and she hated herself for using it on him. "There has to be another way. Ask it again. Ask if blood freely given means—means something smaller. A wound. A—"

"*Small captain,*" the dragon said, gently, terribly, "*the price is a life willingly given. That is what 'swallowed death' means. The blood must be poured out entire. I am sorry. I have watched this realm's cleverness for a thousand years, and I have never once known cleverness to find a discount.*"

* * *

They made camp at the chamber's edge because no one could face the stair again that night, and the dragon dimmed herself to a banked glow, courteous as a candle, so they could rest.

They did not rest.

Wren sat with her back against warm glass-stone and watched Silas across the low fire Marisol permitted herself, a fire the size of two cupped hands. He was inventorying his satchel. Of course he was. Vial by vial, checking seals, and she knew—he had to know she knew—that he was counting the doses that had made him into the answer to the dragon's riddle.

Tam sat down beside her. "In the guild," he said quietly, "when we can't pay for something, we don't steal it. We share the debt." He nodded toward Silas. "That's what family means. It's the only thing it's ever meant."

"It isn't his debt alone."

"No," Tam agreed. "But it's his blood."

Odile curled against her brother's shoulder. Across the fire, Ash sat nearest the dragon, closer than any of them dared, her hands folded in her lap, her face full of some private grief thirty years old.

Wren stood. Crossed the firelight. Sat beside Silas before either of them could decide otherwise.

"If you think," she said, low, "that I'm going to stand in that throne room and watch you pour yourself out while I hold a door—that's not love, Silas. That's just duty wearing its other coat. I burned my orders once already."

"And I'm asking you to burn nothing now." He looked at her then, finally, and his eyes in the ember-light were steady and wrecked and certain. "Wren. My whole life I've tasted death for other people's safety. Every mark in me, every poison—I took them so the king wouldn't. So someone, anyone, would wake up tomorrow." He almost smiled. "Let this be the one taste that buys everyone. Let it mean what all the others only pretended to mean."

"That's not—"

"Family shares the debt," he said, echoing Tam. "But some debts are written in a single name. Mine. Ash read the rite. The dragon confirmed it. There's no clause, Wren. There's no fine print. There's just me, and a crown, and a masque in nine days where every noble in the realm will be wearing masks and the guards will be drunk on anniversary wine."

Above them, far above, the palace slept with a monster on its head.

Behind them, the dragon's ember-veins pulsed, missed, pulsed—a heart running out of patience with the world. And as Wren reached for Silas's hand and found it, and held it, and could not say anything that would change the arithmetic of what was coming, Grandmother Ash began, very quietly, to sing—an old song, in an older tongue, and the dragon listened with her one vast dying eye, and joined in.

The harmony of it went up through the stone like smoke through a chimney, toward the crown, toward the king, toward the masque where Lord Vessic was even now rehearsing his welcome.

Nine days.

Eight, by the time they climbed back into the cold morning air, and found the city plastered overnight with new decrees: *THE MASQUE SHALL BE GUARDED BY THE HUNT ENTIRE. ALL GIFT-BEARERS DISCOVERED WITHIN THE PALACE WALLS SHALL BE BURNED ON SIGHT.*

And beneath the decree, fresh ink, fresh cruelty: *CAPTAIN WREN THACKERAY IS HEREBY SENTENCED. BRING THE TRAITOR'S HEAD TO THE ANNIVERSARY FEAST AND BE REWARDED.*

Wren read her own death warrant twice. Then she folded it into her pocket, next to her sister's ribbon, and followed Silas home to plan a miracle.
