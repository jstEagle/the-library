## 7. Crownfall

The masque smelled of honey and fear.

Silas had learned, over eleven years of banquets, that the two scents always traveled together. Honey because the wealthy sweetened everything—wine, meat, the lies they told one another across gilt tables. Fear because a palace full of masked nobles was a palace full of people who knew what happened to those who fell from favor. Tonight the fear ran thicker than usual, and Silas tasted it in the very air of the Rose Colonnade, in the torch-smoke and the crushed petals scattered underfoot, in the sweat beneath a hundred lacquered masks.

He moved among them as a serving-taster, which was the oldest mask he owned: plain gray livery, a taster's silver chain at his throat, a face no one bothered to remember. Thirty years of being nobody had made him very good at it.

*The throne room doors at the third bell,* Ash had said, her blind eyes turned toward him as if she could see straight through to the ledger written in his blood. *The crown will be displayed on its cushion during the toast. Vessic never lets it leave his hand—but even a keeper must lift the cup.*

Beside him, moving with the same gray anonymity, went the guild. Marisol wore a pastry-cook's whites and carried a tray of candied figs that were, if one counted rightly, seven small miracles disguised as dessert. Tobin had been folded into the ranks of lantern-bearers, his gift for stillness so complete that three guards had already looked directly at him and seen nothing but furniture. And Grandmother Ash came as herself—a blind crone in mourning grays, hired by some absent noblewoman to hold her train, because no one suspects the blind, and everyone pities them, and pity opens more doors than gold.

Wren was elsewhere. Wren was where Silas could not think about her too long or his hands would shake.

*The east service door,* she'd told him that morning, her fingers laced through his in the dark of the cistern, her voice steady in the way he knew meant she was holding something terrible very still. *I'll hold it until the third bell. After that—*

*After that you run,* he'd said.

She'd smiled at him then, and it had been the worst smile he'd ever seen, because it had been a goodbye wearing festival colors. *After that,* she'd said, *I do what I should have done the day I burned my orders.*

Now the third bell rang.

It came down through the colonnade like a stone dropped into deep water, and every masked head turned toward the great doors of the throne room, where the heralds lifted their trumpets. The anniversary of the coronation. Thirty years since the crown had settled on a king's brow, thirty years since Lord Aldous Vessic had become the most beloved man in Vharen—not despite the Purging Hunt but because of it, the gentle-faced keeper who only wanted to protect the realm from the corruption of gifts.

Silas drifted with the flow of servants toward the open doors.

The throne room swallowed them whole. It had been built to make human beings feel like insects: vaulted to a height where the banners of old wars hung like the wings of drowned things, floored in marble so polished that the chandeliers doubled themselves in it, lit by a thousand candles that gave light without warmth. At the far end, beneath the sigil of the realm worked in silver and lapis, stood the empty throne—and beside it, on a cushion of cloth-of-gold, the crown.

Silas's blood knew it before his eyes did. Every poison he had ever survived stirred at once, a cold congregation rising in his veins, and he understood suddenly what Ash had meant when she said his body was a map. The marks in him were all roads, and every road led here—to the small, coiled thing of black metal and buried fire sitting on its golden pillow like a sleeping adder.

And beside it, one hand resting on the cushion's edge, stood Lord Aldous Vessic.

He was not what the posters promised. No cruelty in the face, no hunger. He looked like a tired scholar at the end of a long lecture, silver-haired, mild-eyed, his keeper's chain of office catching the candlelight. He was smiling at the crowd. His hand did not leave the crown.

*Third bell.* Somewhere behind him, far away down corridors of marble, Silas knew Wren was taking her place at the east door. He could not look toward it. He put his eyes on the crown and let the crowd carry him forward, one anonymous gray body among dozens, close enough now to see the sigil-work etched along the crown's inner band—close enough to see that it was *moving*, faintly, like script written in water.

The toast was called. Wine went round. Vessic lifted his cup—

—and the lights died.

Not all of them. Just the chandeliers, all at once, as though a vast hand had cupped itself over the room. Gasps. In the sudden half-dark, Tobin's stolen shadows bloomed, and Marisol's figs did whatever figs did, and somewhere a woman screamed with what sounded suspiciously like delight, because the Guild of Impossible Things had never once managed a subtle entrance and had stopped apologizing for it years ago.

Silas moved.

He crossed the marble like a man walking through a dream of it, past frozen courtiers, past guards whose lanterns guttered blue, up the seven steps to the dais where the crown sat on its cushion. His hands were steady. Eleven years of tasting had taught his hands everything his heart did not know: how to be calm inside catastrophe.

He reached for the crown.

"Take care," said Lord Vessic, without turning around. "It bites."

Silas froze. The keeper turned then, slowly, mildly, and looked at him with an expression of genuine, weary curiosity—the look of a man examining a stain on his sleeve.

"The food-taster," Vessic said. "Yes. I wondered when you'd come. You've been in my ledgers for some time, Master Oyelaran. A remarkable document, your blood. Every poison the realm has ever brewed, catalogued in living flesh. Do you know what that makes you?"

"A dead man," Silas said, "if you call the guard."

"I don't need the guard." Vessic's smile deepened by a fraction. "Look at your hands."

Silas looked. The sigil-marks that lived in his skin, the quiet constellation Ash's rite needed—they were *glowing*. Faintly, coldly, answering the crown the way iron answers a lodestone. And the crown on its cushion had begun, very softly, to sing—a sound below hearing, felt in the teeth, in the marrow.

"It calls its own," Vessic said gently. "You were never infiltrating, Master Oyelaran. You were *answering*."

"That's not—" But even as he spoke, Silas felt it: the pull, patient and immense, like a current beneath still water. His feet had carried him exactly here. Had they ever carried him anywhere else?

"No," said another voice, ragged and small.

The king stepped out from behind the throne.

Silas had seen Edmund IV twice in his life, always at distance, always flanked by keepers: a hollow man with a hollow voice, reading decrees written by other hands. But this—this man stumbling into the candle-dark with his hands bound before him in silk cord, this man with his eyes wide and wet and *awake*—was not the king Silas knew. This was someone locked inside his own face for thirty years, watching everything, permitted nothing.

"You have to listen," the king whispered, lurching toward Silas, dragging his bound hands like a man hauling chains. "Whatever it told you—whatever it *showed* you—it lies, it has always lied, it wears my voice, it wears my *hands*—thirty years I have screamed inside myself and no one heard, no one *heard*—" His voice cracked and he pressed the bound hands to his mouth, and Silas saw the silk cord was soaked through, had been soaked for years, with blood where the king bit through it night after night trying to free himself.

"Your Majesty," Silas breathed.

"Don't kneel." The king laughed, horrible and breathless. "Don't kneel to me, taster-man, I've watched you kneel to me for decades and none of it was mine, do you understand? None of it was *mine*. The Hunt—the burnings—I screamed every time, I was *there*, I felt my hands sign the orders—" He seized Silas's sleeve with bound hands, and his eyes, so close now, were the most terrible thing in the room: sane, and drowning. "You've come to unbind me. I can feel it. I can feel *him* planning to use you instead."

"Use me?" Silas looked at Vessic.

Vessic sighed, and set down his untouched wine.

"There it is," the keeper said sadly. "The moment every plan arrives at. Yes, Master Oyelaran. The unbinding requires marked blood freely given. Yours is the finest ever bred. And yes—" his mild eyes flicked to the king, and something ancient and cold surfaced in them, something that did not belong on a scholar's face, "—the binding is *old*, and hungry, and I have kept it fed for thirty years so that it need not feed on *you*." He spread his hands to the darkened hall, the frozen nobles, the realm entire. "Every gift in Vharen runs on that fire. Extinguish the crown, and you extinguish *everything*—every blessing, every wonder, every miracle your little guild trades in. Is that what your found family wants? A world made ordinary? I am not the villain of this story, taster. I am its *steward*. Someone must hold the leash."

"You could have asked the dragon to rest," Silas said. "Ash offered. Thirty years ago she offered."

For just a moment—one flicker, quickly buried—something moved behind Vessic's eyes that looked almost like grief.

"The fire refused," he said quietly. "So I chose the realm."

"And the king?"

"The king is the price." Vessic drew his sword.

It was a keeper's blade, long and plain, and it came out of its scabbard without ceremony, the way a key turns a lock. The singing in Silas's blood rose to a shriek. The king cried out and threw himself between them, bound hands raised, shouting words that dissolved halfway to his lips—the crown eating even his voice.

And that was when the east doors burst open.

Silas turned, and the world narrowed to a single point of fire: Wren, in her captain's blacks, backlit by torchlight, standing alone in the doorway with her saber drawn and her former squad fanned out behind her in the colonnade—eight of them, armored, crossbows leveled. She had not run. She had not fled to the cistern. She had come *here*, to stand in the gap, because of course she had; she had burned her orders once and had apparently decided that one fire was not enough.

"Wren, *no*—"

"Hold the door," she said, and her voice carried through the whole vast room, steady as the third bell. "That was the plan. I'm holding the door."

"Sergeant Thackeray." Vessic's voice was almost kind. "There is still a pardon in this for you. There has always been a pardon in this for you. Step aside."

Wren smiled at him—the same terrible festival-colored smile—and planted herself in the threshold like a nail driven home.

"My sister bore a gift," she said. "Her name was Lark. You burned her name off the rolls and called her *nothing*. So no, my lord. I'll stand here."

"Loose on my mark," said her sergeant, raising his hand.

Crossbows racked. Wren's saber came up. And Silas, with the crown singing in his blood and the king begging at his side and Vessic's blade already falling toward him, understood that everything had arrived at this single held breath—that there was no plan left, no ledger, no clever entry anywhere that covered what happened next.

Only the rite. Only his blood. Only the choice he had hidden from her, burning in his palm like a coal.

He reached for the crown.

* * *

The crown was colder than any poison he had ever tasted.

That was his first thought, absurdly, as his fingers closed over the black metal and the world went white at the edges: *colder than bellshade, colder than widow's-tears, colder than the draught that killed the Duke of Serel in '09.* His tongue—the taster's tongue, the ledger-tongue—read it instantly, helplessly, the way it read everything: *dragon-fire, bound; thirty years of stolen will; grief compressed to a cutting edge.*

Then Vessic's blade took him through the shoulder, and the taste became simply pain.

Silas went down on one knee on the dais, the crown clutched against his chest, blood sheeting warm down his arm. The hall was screaming. Crossbow bolts hissed past overhead—one, two—and each was met with a ring of steel, because Wren had left the doorway after all and was somehow *everywhere*, saber blurring, turning bolts from the air with a skill no drill-yard had ever taught her, with a skill that could only be the guild's doing, Marisol's blessing or Tobin's impossible grace spent on her like coin.

"*The rite!*" Ash's voice rose above the chaos, blind and blazing, from somewhere near the foot of the dais. "*Silas, boy—the words are in your blood, they've always been in your blood—open the ledger!*"

Vessic wrenched his blade free and raised it again. The king threw himself onto the keeper's arm. Bolts flew. Wren shouted his name—his true name, the one no one at court knew—and the sound of it went through Silas cleaner than any sword.

He pressed the crown to his bleeding chest, opened his mouth, and began to speak the first word of the unbinding.

The crown began, very softly, to laugh.
