## 8. The Unbinding

The masque above us was all gold leaf and laughter, a hundred candles burning in shapes of swans and suns, and none of it mattered. What mattered was the cold of the under-stone rising through my boots, the ember-light bleeding up through the cracks in the throne dais like dawn seen from the wrong side of the world.

Wren's hand found mine in the dark beneath the gallery stairs. Her glove was off. I could feel the calluses, the sword-rough skin, the tremor she would never have allowed anyone else to witness.

"The door holds as long as I hold it," she said.

"Then don't let go."

"I wasn't planning to." She almost smiled. It was the kind of smile that has already said its goodbyes and decided to stay anyway. "Silas. Whatever happens down there—whatever the rite asks of you—you come back up. That's an order. I outrank you."

"You burned your commission."

"I outrank everyone now. That's how treason works."

Above us, faintly, the music changed—a slower measure, the anniversary waltz, the one they'd danced thirty years ago when a boy-king first felt the crown settle on his head and thought it merely heavy. Somewhere in that glittering hall, her former squad was checking blades and looking for a traitor in borrowed finery. Somewhere Lord Vessic was smiling his oiled smile and pouring will into a sigil older than the kingdom it had hollowed.

I kissed her once, brief as a struck match. Then I went down.

* * *

The dragon's chamber opened around me like a cathedral built by something that had never heard of gods. The walls were not stone but scale, vast overlapping plates gone grey and dull as old coins, and in their center—the reason for everything—lay the last fire in the realm.

It was smaller than a hearth-flame. That was the cruelty of it. Thirty years of siphoning, thirty years of a crown drinking deep while the source starved, and what remained of the dragon that had burned mountains into being was a trembling ember no larger than my two cupped hands, guttering in a bed of its own fallen scales.

I knelt at the edge of the ash-bed. The heat touched my face like a fevered palm.

"You came," the dragon said.

Its voice was not sound so much as warmth—a resonance in the floor, in my ribs, in the fillings of my teeth. Its eye, half-lidded, was a slit of dim amber watching me with the terrible patience of things that measure time in centuries.

"I brought the price," I said. I held up my hands, palms out, the way Grandmother Ash had taught me. "Every poison I've ever swallowed is written in me. You said the sigil can only be cut by one who has swallowed death and lived. I've swallowed it eleven times."

"Eleven." Something moved in the ember—amusement, or grief; with dragons the two were close kin. "And your blood remembers each one. Show me."

I drew the ritual knife across my left forearm—not cutting to wound but cutting to *open*, the way Ash's fragmentary rite demanded—and held the arm over the ash. My blood fell black in the ember-light, and where it struck, it did not soak in. It *wrote*. Thin lines of dark script crawled across the dead scales, mapping themselves: wolfsbane in a looping hand, arsenic in sharp angular strokes, the king's own venom-of-state coiling like a signature. My body, unbound onto the floor. A ledger made flesh, and the flesh finally read aloud.

The ember flared. For one heartbeat the whole chamber remembered what fire used to be.

"Yes," the dragon breathed. "Yes. Now listen quickly, taster, because we have very little time and Vessic has just felt you begin."

* * *

He had. I knew it before the dragon spoke, because the temperature of the room changed—not hotter but *hungrier*, the air pulling toward the ceiling where the crown sat invisible and vast, thirty feet of stone above, feeding.

The sigil-crown was not on the king's head anymore in any way that mattered. It was everywhere the king's will had been eaten. And its keeper stood somewhere above us, feeling his life's work start to unravel, and doing the only thing he knew: pouring more in.

I felt it happen to the rite. My blood-script began to smudge, the lines blurring as though a thumb had been dragged across wet ink. The words Ash had given me—the counter-sigil, the unbinding, the great *undoing*—started sliding out of true.

"Hold the pattern," the dragon said, and its voice cracked like green wood. "Hold it, or everything you are becomes kindling."

I pressed both palms flat against the written floor and *held*. It was like holding a door shut against a flood. The sigil above pushed down and down, and every poison in my ledger rose to meet it—each one a death I had already refused, each mark a small immovable *no*. Wolfsbane said no. Arsenic said no. The eleventh, the worst, the one whose name I still couldn't say without tasting copper—all of them stood in me like a garrison that had never surrendered.

But a garrison can be starved too. I felt the crown begin to pull at the marks themselves, trying to drink my immunities the way it drank the dragon, trying to make my ledger its next meal.

That was when the ceiling of the world opened.

Not literally. But light poured down the stairwell—warm light, guild-light, impossible light—and I heard footsteps I knew better than my own heartbeat. Wren came first, sword drawn, her captain's coat scorched along one sleeve, and behind her came all of them. Merrin with her bottled memories uncorked and glowing like fireflies. Old Tobbin, who could mend anything except his own loneliness, hands already weaving. Sister Quill, whose gift was truth, standing straighter than I had ever seen her. And at the rear, leaning on Wren's arm because blindness makes its own courage, Grandmother Ash.

"We couldn't hold the masque," Wren said, breathless, blood on her cheekbone that wasn't hers. "Vessic ordered the ball cleared. He knows. Silas—he's coming here himself."

"Then we finish before he arrives." I looked at my family, my impossible, improbable family, gathered in a dragon's tomb because a food-taster had once taught them that miracles could be kept rather than sold. "Ash. The rite needs more than my blood. It needs gifts freely given. All of them. Can you—"

"Child," Ash said gently, "I have been waiting thirty years for someone to ask."

* * *

What happened next, I can only describe the way a drowning man describes the surface: imperfectly, and grateful for every word.

Merrin poured her bottled memories into the circle—not her own this time but ones the guild had traded and kept, a hundred stolen joys, and they became the rite's *foundation*, the ground it stood on. Tobbin mended the fraying edges of my blood-script faster than the crown could blur them, his gift stitching line to line like a seamstress drunk and desperate and magnificent. Sister Quill spoke the true names of the poisons in my ledger, each one named aloud and thereby *disarmed*, transformed from death into testimony.

And Wren—

Wren broke her final oath.

I saw her do it. She stood between me and the stairwell, blade drawn, facing the sound of Vessic's approaching guards, and she spoke the words of renunciation clearly, formally, in the old tongue of the Hunt, the oath that could never be taken back: *"I was sworn to the crown. I am sworn now to the man. Let the first oath burn."*

Her oath caught fire. Actually burned—a ribbon of pale flame rising off her shoulders like smoke given form—and the rite took it, drank it, grew fat on the magnitude of a thing freely surrendered. The blood-script blazed white. The dragon's ember rose from its ash-bed for the first time in thirty years, no longer a dying coal but a *tongue*, reaching upward through stone and story toward the crown it had fed so long.

"It's asking," the dragon said, and its voice was thunder now, was youth remembered, was everything it had ever been. "Taster. Your blood is the key. Your *life* must be the turning."

I had known. From the moment Ash first traced the rite's shape in spilled wine, I had known, and I had hidden it from Wren the way you hide a wound from someone who would bleed for you.

I looked at her. She was holding the stairwell alone, back to me, guarding my working with her body, and she turned—just enough to see my face—and whatever she saw there, she understood.

"No," she said.

"Wren—"

"*No.*" She started toward me. "Find another way. Find—there's always another way, you taught me that, you and your ledgers and your—"

"There isn't." I smiled at her, and it didn't even hurt, which was the strangest part. "Eleven times I swallowed death and lived. This is the twelfth. But this time—" I pressed my bleeding arm harder against the blazing script, "—this time I get to choose it."

The crown felt my intent and *screamed*. The whole palace screamed with it, stone groaning, chandeliers shattering floors above. Vessic's will came down like a landslide, trying to crush the rite, trying to drown five gifts and a broken oath and a dragon's last breath in sheer brute hunger.

And my blood answered.

All of it. Every mark, every poison, every death I'd carried so a king could eat safely—it rose in me at once, not as agony but as *accounting*, the great final reconciliation of a ledger kept too long. I felt myself becoming the ink, the page, the hand that writes *paid in full*.

I gave it.

The world went white, then warm, then quiet.

* * *

When I came back to myself—if it was myself—I was lying on cool ash and someone was shaking me and shouting my name, and the shouting voice was Wren's, and she was crying, which I had never seen, and which undid me more than the dying had.

Above us, the crown came apart.

Not shattered—*unbound*, thread by golden thread, thirty years of stolen will streaming back down through the palace like rain finding its river. I felt the moment the king woke. Everyone in the kingdom felt it, perhaps: a great collective inhale, a weight lifting off minds that hadn't known they were bent.

Where the sigil's shadow had pooled, Lord Vessic stood fused to it, his own ambition finally worn as a garment. He did not scream. He simply crumbled, quietly, thoroughly, a man reduced at last to the sum of what he'd served, until there was nothing left of him but dust settling on a dragon's scales.

The ember burned steady. Bright. Fed at last instead of fed *upon*—fed by freedom, which turns out to be the only fuel it ever wanted.

The dragon looked at me with its young-old amber eye.

"Clever taster," it said. "You gave your immunity, not your life. Every poison you survived gave up its claim on you when you spent it. You are ordinary now, Silas Oyelaran. Utterly, gloriously mortal."

Ordinary. The word landed in me like a second sunrise.

Wren's arms closed around me, and I buried my face in her shoulder, and above us the palace rang with a king's first free breath, and somewhere far away a hunt was dissolving into rumor, and somewhere closer a blind old woman was laughing through her tears.

"Your ledger," Wren whispered into my hair. "It's blank now. What will you write in it?"

I thought about it. I was tired, and poisoned no longer, and loved, and alive in a way I had never once been while surviving.

"A new account," I said. "First entry: *everything owed, repaid. Everything kept, begins.*"

Far above, through the broken ceiling of the world, dawn was coming—and with it, the long reckoning of a kingdom waking to learn what had been done in its name.
