## 9. Ashes and Accounts

The throne room still smelled of burnt sugar.

That was what Wren noticed first, standing guard three days after the world broke open—the cloying sweetness of masque-cakes scorched on their silver trays, mingling now with ash and the mineral tang of dragon-fire that had passed through stone like water through cloth. The great windows had been shattered by the sigil's shattering, and morning light came through them unfiltered, laying long pale bars across the floor where courtiers had knelt and wept and, in some cases, simply stared at the king as though seeing him for the first time.

Perhaps they were. Wren knew she was.

Aldric Vane sat upon the throne he had occupied for thirty years, but he did not sit like a king. He sat like a man waking from a fever that had lasted half his life, his hands loose in his lap, his crown—no crown at all now, only a circlet of ordinary gold that someone had set aside—resting on the armrest beside him like a shed skin. He was thinner than his portraits. His eyes, which Wren had always known as the flat gray of a coin, were gray the way rain is gray: full of weather, full of weight.

He was looking at the scorch mark on the dais where Lord Vessic had crumbled into shadow and salt.

"Captain Thackeray." His voice was rough, unused to its own freedom. "You are not required to stand so far away."

"I am not required to stand anywhere near you at all, Your Majesty," Wren said. "I'm a traitor. Officially."

"Not anymore." He lifted the proclamation from his knee—a single sheet, sealed in wax that was red as an old wound—and held it out. "Read it. I would rather it be read aloud than whispered about. I have had thirty years of whispers doing my speaking for me."

She came forward slowly, boots loud on marble that had heard her boots before, when she was eleven and being sworn into the Hunt with her hand on her father's sword. She took the paper. The seal was warm; the king had been holding it a long time.

*"By order of Aldric Vane, third of his name"*—and here Wren felt the strange vertigo of realizing the man had a name beyond *the crown*, beyond *the throne*—*"all decrees of the Purging Hunt are dissolved. All sentences passed against persons bearing gifts under the Edict of Purity are voided. All who were hunted are pardoned. All who did the hunting—"*

She stopped.

"Finish it," the king said quietly. "I wrote it at dawn. I have read it forty times and I still cannot make it mean what I want it to mean."

"All who did the hunting," she read, "will answer for it—not to the crown, but to those they wronged. The crown will not stand between them."

Silence, except for the wind moving through broken glass somewhere high above, making a sound like a distant choir tuning itself.

"You burned your orders," Aldric said. "In the courtyard at Harrowgate. I know. I saw it, from inside—" His hand drifted toward his bare brow and fell back. "I saw everything, Captain. Every raid. Every fire. Every name on every list. I screamed inside my own skull for thirty years, and no one heard me, because the thing wearing my face never screamed. It smiled. It signed. It—" He closed his eyes. "I will not ask forgiveness. Forgiveness would require that I did something, and I did nothing. Nothing is not forgivable. But I can do this. This much, at least, with my own hands."

Wren folded the proclamation with the care she'd once used folding burial flags.

"The Hunt won't dissolve because you say so," she said. "Half my former squad believes the gifts are plague. The other half believes you're still bound and this is a trick. And Vessic's people—the ones who fed off the crown's fear—they're scattered, but scattering isn't dead."

"No," the king agreed. "It isn't. Which is why I need captains who understand what obedience costs when it's given blindly." He looked at her then, fully, and there was something almost unbearable in it—not pity, not gratitude, but recognition, soldier to soldier, of a wound they both carried. "I am offering you reinstatement. Command of the reformed Hunt—or whatever name we give the thing that replaces it. Guardianship instead of purging. Your rank restored. Your family name cleared."

*Your sister safe,* hung unsaid beneath all of it, bright as a lamp.

"My sister doesn't need my name cleared," Wren said. "She needs bread that isn't bought with someone else's blood. We've all needed that for thirty years."

"And Silas Oyelaran?"

The question landed soft as a moth and heavy as a stone. Wren kept her face still the way she'd been trained to, oath and drill and iron discipline—and then, deliberately, let it soften, because she was done with training.

"He's alive," she said. "Barely. Grandmother Ash says his heart is learning to beat like an ordinary heart again. He spent thirty years' worth of poison-ledger in one night, and what's left is just—" Her voice caught, traitor to her composure, and she let it. "Just a man. An ordinary man who can get sick now. Who can be poisoned now. Who flinched yesterday when I handed him a cup of wine, because for the first time in his life, he couldn't taste the difference between safe and deadly."

The king bowed his head. "Then tell him the realm owes him a debt no treasury can pay. And tell yourself, Captain, that my offer stands. Take whatever time you need."

"Time," Wren said, turning toward the broken light, "is the first impossible thing anyone ever traded. We're keeping it, these days. Not spending it."

* * *

He was in the guildhouse kitchen, of all places, elbow-deep in flour.

Wren stood in the doorway a long moment, drinking the sight in. The old safehouse had burned to its bones during the Purging, but the guild had found a new home in a decommissioned granary near the river, all honey-colored brick and high windows, and Grandmother Ash had declared the kitchen its heart before the first bed was even made up. Now the room smelled of yeast and woodsmoke and the particular green smell of spring through open shutters.

Silas had flour on his cheekbone, in his dark hair, on the tip of his nose. His sleeves were rolled. His hands—those careful, ledger-keeping, poison-reading hands—were kneading dough with the concentration of a scribe copying scripture.

"You're staring," he said without turning around.

"I'm memorizing," Wren said. "There's a difference."

He turned then, and smiled, and the smile did what it always did—knocked something loose in her chest that she'd spent a lifetime nailing down. He looked thinner. There were new lines at his eyes, and a pallor that Ash kept clucking over. When he wiped his hands and crossed to her, he moved carefully, like a man relearning the weight of his own body.

"Well?" he asked. "What did the king say?"

"He dissolved the Hunt." She pulled the folded proclamation from her coat. "Pardons for everyone. Trials for the hunters—including me, if the wronged call for it. And he offered me reinstatement. Command of whatever comes next."

Silas went very still. It was the stillness she'd learned to read across banquet tables, the taster's stillness, the body cataloguing danger. Except now there was no poison to catalogue. Just her words, sitting between them.

"And what did you say?"

"I said my sister needs bread that wasn't bought with blood." She took his floury hand. "And then I came home to find you making some."

"It's terrible bread," Silas admitted. "I've made six loaves and Ash has eaten one and fed four to the gulls. She says my palate was wasted on poison detection anyway." He laughed, and the laugh wobbled at the edges. "I can't tell if the starter's gone sour, Wren. Do you understand? Thirty years I could taste a drop of hemlock in a barrel of honey, and now I stood over this bowl for an hour genuinely unable to say whether it smelled right."

"That sounds horrifying."

"It's the most terrifying thing I've ever done," he said solemnly, "including the night I walked into the masque with death in a vial against my ribs."

They stood together in the warm light while the dough rose under its cloth, and outside, the river went on doing what rivers do, indifferent and eternal. Somewhere above them, Wren could hear the guild's afternoon settling into its rhythms: Ash's cane tapping the floorboards as she taught two gifted children to listen to the shape of a room; the twins arguing about whether a bottled memory of snow counted as weather or as memory; the low murmur of petitioners downstairs—people who had heard the pardon and come, cautious as deer, to ask if the stories were true.

"They're calling us keepers now," Silas said. "Instead of traders. Did you hear? There's talk of opening a school."

"I heard."

"A school, Wren. For impossible things." He shook his head, wonder and grief braided together in his face. "All those years trading miracles in secret, hoarding them like contraband, and the whole time what people needed was to be *taught*. To be told their gift wasn't a sickness."

His voice frayed on the last word, and Wren understood why. She pulled him close, flour and all, and felt him lean into her with the whole exhausted weight of a man who had paid the unbinding price and lived—which was its own kind of miracle, the kind Ash still wouldn't explain, only smile about, blind eyes turned toward some private horizon.

"You're shaking," he murmured into her hair.

"I stood in front of the throne today and didn't salute." Her throat was tight. "Thirty years of drilling and oaths and I just—stood there, and told a king I had somewhere better to be."

"Did you?"

"This is where I was going." She drew back enough to look at him. "I told him no, Silas. Not yet, not maybe—*no*. Whatever the Hunt becomes, it'll be built by someone else. I burned my orders once. I'm not going to spend the rest of my life saluting things." Her thumb traced the flour on his jaw. "I'm going to keep accounts instead. Yours and mine."

Silas looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached behind him, took up a knife, cut a slice from the sixth loaf—the ugly one, the lopsided one—and held it out to her on his palm like an offering, like a sacrament, like a trade.

"Taste it first," he whispered. "Tell me it's not poison. I need to hear someone say it out loud."

Wren took the bread, tore it, and ate. It was dense and slightly burnt and utterly, magnificently ordinary.

"It's not poison," she said. "It's just bread."

He exhaled like a man surfacing after drowning, and laughed, and kissed her with flour on his lips, and outside the gulls wheeled over the river crying like things newly freed.

It was later—much later, dusk pooling violet in the high windows—that Ash's cane came tapping down the stairs, faster than usual, and her voice preceded her into the kitchen, sharp with an urgency none of them had heard since the burning of the old safehouse:

"Someone's at the gate. A child. Gifted, and running from something." A pause, and then, quieter, in the tone of a woman reading a ledger whose next line she does not like: "She says men in gray cloaks burned her village last night. She says they're calling themselves the Crown's Loyal Hunt."

Silas's hand found Wren's in the darkening kitchen.

"So," he said softly. "The account isn't settled after all."
