## 10. Keepers of Impossible Things

The ledgers smell different now.

Silas noticed it the first morning he opened them in the new house—no longer the sour tang of candle-grease and desperation that had clung to every page back when they were written by lamplight behind a locked door, but something cleaner. Beeswax. Woodsmoke from a hearth no one had to hide. And underneath it all, faint as a watermark, the warm mineral breath of the ember two floors below, rising through the bones of the building like sap through a tree.

He sat at the long table by the eastern window and dipped his pen.

*A season,* he wrote, *since the crown fell.*

Outside, autumn was doing its slow honest work on the garden Wren had insisted on planting against everyone's advice. The beds she'd sown with such furious optimism—kneeling in ash-grey soil, her captain's hands suddenly farmer's hands—had come up ragged and glorious: late beans, windfall apples going soft and sweet beneath the tree, marigolds burning orange like small loyal fires that refused to be put out. A cart rattled past on the lane below, and Silas heard a child laughing at something, just laughing, the way children laughed now, without checking first whether anyone would hear.

*It is strange,* he wrote, *to keep accounts in a world where nothing needs hiding.*

* * *

The house had been a granary once, before the war nobody remembered, all stone and deep cellars and one enormous room where grain had been poured from the loft in golden avalanches. Now the loft held beds. The great room held tables—and students.

That was the word Ash had chosen, over Silas's objection that *guild* had history and weight and grief braided into its very letters. But Ash had only smiled her blind smile, turning her face toward him as though his protest were a sound she could touch.

"Traders take," she said. "Keepers hold. But neither of those is what we are anymore, child. We were never meant to hoard the impossible things. We were meant to pass them on. You don't call a school a warehouse."

So: the Keepership of Impossible Things. Painted over the door in letters Wren had cut herself, because she didn't trust anyone else's hand with it.

They came slowly at first—the gifted, the frightened ones who had spent thirty years learning to fold their gifts up small and swallow them like contraband. A boy whose shadow moved half a heartbeat ahead of him. An old ferryman who could taste tomorrow's weather in river water. A woman named Ilsa whose gift was so modest she'd nearly died believing she had none: bread she baked simply did not go stale, and people who ate it slept without nightmares for a week.

Ilsa stood in the doorway on her first day with flour still on her sleeves and said, "I don't even know if this counts."

Silas had taken both her hands. "Everything counts," he said. "That was always the lie we were told—that some miracles are too small to matter. Small miracles are the only kind most people ever get."

Now there were eleven of them at the long tables, and the lessons were not lessons so much as conversations that happened to change lives. Ash taught listening—how to hear what a gift wanted, because gifts, she insisted, had wants the way rivers had currents. The boy with the running shadow learned to let it walk beside him instead of ahead, and stopped being afraid of his own dusk. The ferryman learned that weather-tasting could warn a village of frost in time to save an orchard.

And Silas taught tasting.

Not poison—not anymore. He taught them attention. He set out cups of water drawn from six different wells and made his students find the differences until their tongues ached. He taught them that a gift begins as noticing, that every miracle in the world started with someone paying closer attention than anyone thought reasonable.

"You were a taster," Ilsa said to him once, wonderingly. "You spent your whole life noticing."

"I spent my whole life surviving," Silas said. "Noticing was how. It took me thirty years to learn the difference." He turned his cup in his hands—ordinary cup, ordinary tea, and he tasted it now the way an ordinary man tastes, simple and grateful and unmarked. "Don't waste your years learning that lesson. Learn it here, in one afternoon, and then go home."

* * *

Two floors down, past the cellar doors and through the passage Wren had widened with her own pickaxe, the dragon's ember breathed.

They called the chamber the Hearth, because calling it a lair felt wrong and calling it a shrine felt worse. It was simply a room of black stone at the heart of the hill the palace had been built upon, and in its center, in a basin carved—so the dragon said—from its own shed scale, the last fire of the last dragon kept its small red vigil.

When they had first found it, guttering beneath the throne-room floor, it had been the size of a dying man's pulse. A coal. A rumor of heat. The crown above had drunk from it for thirty years, and what remained had barely strength enough to be afraid.

But fear, it turned out, was not the same as death. And tending—free tending, given tending—was not the same as feeding.

Every day someone went down. That was the rule, and it was the easiest rule in the world to keep because everyone wanted to. They brought no fuel; the ember needed none. What it needed was witness. Someone to sit beside it in the warm dark and speak to it, tell it the news of the surface—what the king's new council debated, what songs the children sang, how the orchards bore. The ember listened. The ember grew.

It was the size of a brazier flame now, and its light had changed color: deeper gold threading through the red, like dawn deciding to happen underground. When Silas knelt beside it, he could feel it considering him—a vast, slow attention folded into something small enough to cradle, the way the ocean is present in a single wave.

"You are growing," he told it, on the evening he came down after the day's accounts were closed.

*I am being believed in,* the ember answered. Its voice was not sound; it arrived as warmth against the skin, as a shifting of pressure behind the eyes. *There is a difference. Your king drank me for power. Your guild gives me company. Power spends. Company accumulates.*

"And when you're grown?" Silas asked, the question he asked every time, and every time the ember gave the same answer, patient as geology.

*Then there will be eggs.*

He still wasn't sure whether the ember was joking. He suspected dragons did not joke. He suspected, also, that hope wore whatever face it had to.

Before he left, he did what he always did: pressed his palm briefly to the rim of the scale-basin, where the stone stayed blood-warm. His palm, unmarked now. Unpoisoned. Unprotected. When he'd cut it accidentally on a broken jar three weeks ago, he had bled like any fool and sworn like any fool, and Wren had bandaged it while laughing at him, and he had loved her so fiercely in that ordinary moment that it had frightened him more than Vessic ever had.

Ordinary. He was ordinary now. Every poison that had ever written itself into his blood had burned away in the unbinding—the price paid, the ledger wiped clean. Sometimes, in the quiet before sleep, he missed it: the certainty of being proof against the world's worst intentions. Then he remembered what that certainty had cost, and he kissed Wren's sleeping shoulder, and he chose ordinary again. Chose it daily, the way you choose bread, the way you choose morning.

* * *

The final entry took him three attempts.

The first he wrote too formally, in the old ledger-hand, all columns and balances: *Debts owed, debts forgiven, debts repaid.* It looked like an obituary for everything they'd been. He struck it through.

The second attempt he abandoned halfway, because Wren leaned over his shoulder reading it upside down and said, "You've spelled 'love' wrong twice," and then read the rest aloud in a voice of scandalized tenderness until he surrendered the page entirely.

The third attempt he wrote the way Ash taught listening: honestly, without deciding first what honesty should look like.

*To whoever keeps these books after me—*

*Know that this ledger began as a record of debts. A bottle of memory traded for bread. A forged passport owed against a favor. We counted ourselves in risks and repayments because we believed the world was arithmetic: that everything taken must be balanced, that kindness was a loan, that love—*

He stopped. Dipped the pen again.

*—that love was a debt we couldn't afford.*

*We were wrong. Here is the true accounting, and I give it freely, which is the whole point:*

*A blind woman saw us first and best, and asked for nothing but our courage, and received our whole lives, and called that a fair trade. A hunt-captain broke every oath she swore and kept the only one that mattered, and she plants marigolds now over graves she dug herself, and the marigolds come back every year, and she says that's the answer to every hard question about justice, if you have eyes. A king walks unarmed among the people he failed, and listens, and does not ask forgiveness because he knows better than to ask for what can only be given, and slowly, unevenly, it is being given. A fire smaller than my two fists has decided, against all evidence, to become a sunrise someday.*

*And I—*

His pen hovered. Through the window, the last light lay along the garden rows, and Wren was out there with her sleeves rolled and dirt to her elbows, showing the boy with the quick shadow how to know when beans were ready by their sound in the wind. She looked up, caught him watching, and did not pretend she hadn't. She never pretended anything. That was her gift, the one she'd been born with and the Hunt had tried to burn out of her: she looked straight at what was true.

*And I am rich,* he wrote. *Richer than any taster counting his survivals, richer than any guild counting its trades. I was paid in full the night the fire answered, and the coin was this: that I am known. Every scar explained, every cowardice witnessed, every ordinary morning chosen and seen and chosen again. If you want to know what we keep here—impossible things, yes, gifts and wonders and a fire that dreams of eggs—but understand that none of it is the treasure. The treasure is the keeping. The treasure is each other, attended to, day after unremarkable day, until the attending becomes the magic.*

*The accounts are open. The debts are done. Come in, and bring what you are. We will teach you what it's worth.*

*—Silas Oyelaran, Keeper*

He set down the pen. Below the window, Wren was walking toward the door with beans in her apron and the boy trailing after her, and the ember's warmth rose through the floorboards like a second heartbeat under the house's first, and somewhere in the dark of the hill a fire the size of a promise dreamed, patiently, in gold and red, of wings.

Silas blew out his candle early, because the light in the house no longer needed his help—and went down to meet them.
