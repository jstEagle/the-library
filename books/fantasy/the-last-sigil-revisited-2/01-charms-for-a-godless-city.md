## 1. Charms for a Godless City

The rain came to Vellharrow the way it always came — politely, and from the sea, as if it had been invited by someone with better manners than the city deserved. Silas Dravenmoor felt it arriving an hour before the first drops fell, a pressure behind the eyes like a held breath, a taste of iron and lightning at the back of the throat. They had learned, over nine long years, to swallow that pressure. To smile around it. To let the storm pass through them unclaimed, the way water passes through a closed fist.

They were arranging their stall in Copperlace Row when the first drops struck the awning. The night market was already waking: lanterns kindling along the canal like a procession of patient fireflies, the smell of fried dough and tallow smoke and wet stone rising all together, the babel of a hundred vendors calling their wares to a city that had officially decided, three generations ago, that there was no such thing as magic and had never looked closely enough at its own shadows to be proven wrong.

Silas's wares were laid out on a square of faded indigo cloth. Love-charms carved of riverbone. Wards-against-sorrow shaped like sleeping birds. Little brass bells that rang a half-second before bad news arrived, which was close enough to prophecy for most customers, and cheaper. Each one harmless-looking. Each one, if you knew where to press and how to listen, something else entirely.

They touched the bell first, always the bell. Beneath its clapper, coiled so fine it might have been a maker's mark, was a thread of working no thicker than spider silk — a whisper of storm caught mid-breath and taught to sleep. It would do nothing unless the world ended near it, and then it would do very little indeed. That was the art of it. Nine years of practice had taught Silas that the safest magic was the kind that barely worked.

*Charm-seller,* they thought, straightening the cloth so the folds fell just so. *Purveyor of trinkets and nonsense.* The word sat in the chest like a stone in a shoe. Not painful, exactly. Just always there, reminding you of the shape you'd been forced into.

"Master Charmwright." A woman in a fishmonger's apron leaned over the stall, her breath sweet with plum wine. "My daughter's wedding is at month's end. I want something for luck."

"Luck I have," Silas said, and meant it, mostly. They lifted a small disc of grey slate, veined with silver, and turned it in the lantern light so the veins seemed to move. "Carved from the old seawall at Greyhaven. Storms break against luck like this; ask any sailor." Which was true, though not in the way she'd hear it. The seawall slate did carry a blessing — Silas had put it there themselves, three nights ago, whispering into the stone the way their grandmother had taught them before the world decided such teaching was a crime. *May her house stand when the weather turns. May the roof hold.*

It was such a small thing. Such a small, forbidden thing. Enough to hang from a rope if the crown's wardens ever pried the charm open with their null-tongs and read what lived inside it. Enough to earn a black hood, a wagon ride, and a cell with no windows in the Bastion of Records, where the gifted went and from which nothing gifted ever returned.

The fishwife paid four coppers and went away blessed, knowing nothing.

Silas watched her go and pressed their thumb against the underside of the stall table, where a knot in the wood hummed faintly with stored thunder. Just to feel it. Just to remember what they were.

That was the loneliness of it, they thought — not the hiding, exactly, but the way the hiding got inside you until you couldn't tell where the disguise ended. A gift buried deep enough, long enough, stopped feeling like a treasure hidden from thieves. It started feeling like a shame hidden from yourself. Some mornings Silas woke and reached for the storm out of habit, found it leashed, and felt — for one unguarded heartbeat — *relief*. And then the shame of the relief, which was worse. A godless city didn't only make you hide your magic. It made you grateful for the chance.

Above the market, the rain thickened, drumming on canvas, running bright down the canal steps. Somewhere a fiddle started up under the arches. Vellharrow glowed and chattered and sold its fried dough, and above it all the sky rolled with weather that no one in the crowd thought to thank or fear, because weather was only weather here. Weather had never been anyone's inheritance.

Silas pulled their collar up and went on selling small, safe lies.

* * *

The thief came an hour past midnight, when the market had gone soft and slow and the vendors' voices had dropped to the low warm register of people counting coin and thinking of beds.

Silas marked her before she ever touched the stall — a habit older than the exile, honed in years when being noticed meant dying. She moved through the crowd the way nervous things move: too quickly, shoulders drawn in, eyes flicking everywhere except where they ought to rest. Young. Sixteen, perhaps seventeen. Thin as a winter branch, in a coat two sizes too large with pockets that hung wrong, weighted. Hair dark and rain-frizzed, escaping a knit cap. She drifted along the row of stalls with the studied aimlessness of someone who had rehearsed not looking suspicious and hadn't quite mastered the part.

She stopped at the neighboring spice seller's table. Moved on. Stopped at the candle-dipper's. Moved on. And then, inevitably, she was at Silas's indigo cloth, hunching over the charms with her hands clasped behind her back like a good customer, and her eyes — quick, grey, terrified and hungry at once — were cataloguing everything.

Silas kept polishing a brass bell and let her look.

"Those are real?" the girl asked. Her voice cracked in the middle, and she covered it with a cough. "The charms. They actually work?"

"The bells warn of trouble. The birds take sorrow. The slate brings luck." Silas smiled the shopkeeper's smile, easy and empty. "Whether any of that is *magic*, miss, I couldn't say. Magic's been illegal in Vellharrow since before my grandmother's grandmother. I sell well-made objects and reasonable hope."

Something flickered across the girl's face at the word *grandmother* — a wince, quickly buried. She laughed, too loud. "Right. Hope. How much for the slate one?"

"For you? Three coppers."

"I've got—" She dug in her pocket and produced, with the flourish of a merchant queen, a single copper button and a lint-covered sugarplum wrapped in wax paper. "Best offer."

"Then keep hoping," Silas said pleasantly, "and come back Thursday."

The girl laughed again, and this time it almost sounded real, and Silas felt the strange pull they sometimes felt toward the young ones of this city — the ones who grew up believing in nothing, who made jokes about nothing, who carried their emptiness lightly like it was a fashion. She turned away, still grinning, still shaking her head at the absurdity of paying for luck —

— and her hand, passing over the display with a smoothness that contradicted every nervous line of her body, closed around the stone at the corner of the cloth.

Silas saw it happen the way one sees weather turn: not the act itself but the pressure before it. The girl's sleeve brushed the corner of the indigo cloth. Her little finger hooked. And the sigil-stone — the one piece on the whole table that was not for sale, that Silas kept displayed precisely because a stall with nothing worth stealing looked wrong — vanished up her oversized sleeve with a thief's economy of motion.

The sigil-stone. Grandmother Maren's oath-stone. The last object in the world that proved Silas Dravenmoor had ever belonged to anything.

The girl made it four steps.

"Miss." Silas's voice carried gently across the row, gentle as falling water, and the girl froze as if the word had been a hand on her shoulder. "You've forgotten to pay for your hope."

She turned slowly. Up close, in the lantern light, she was even younger than she'd seemed — hollow-cheeked, chapped lips, a fading bruise along her jaw gone yellow at the edges. Her eyes did the arithmetic of escape routes with terrible speed. The canal at her left. the alley mouth behind. The fat spice merchant watching with interest, the candle-dipper pretending not to.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"Your sleeve." Silas held out one palm, open, unhurried. Around them the market flowed on, oblivious; a pickpocket's drama was the least interesting thing in Vellharrow on a rainy midnight. "The grey stone, about the size of a sparrow's egg. It has a carving on it that looks like weather seen through a window."

For a moment he thought she'd bolt. Her weight shifted, her heel turned — and then her eyes found his face properly for the first time, and whatever she saw there made her stop. Later, Silas would wonder what it was. Pity, perhaps. Or recognition: the look of someone else who had spent their whole life being hunted by ordinary days.

Her jaw set. "It's mine now," she said. "Finders keepers. It's the law."

"It's really not."

"You weren't even watching it! You were *polishing bells* like some — some granddad. Anything left out is fair game, everyone knows that, and anyway—" Her voice climbed, cracked, steadied with visible effort. "Anyway I need it more than you do."

That, at last, was interesting. Silas leaned an elbow on the stall and studied her. "Why? What does a stone mean to a girl who doesn't have three coppers?"

"Nothing. It's just a rock. It's — it's pretty." But her hand had drifted, without her seeming to notice, to the place on her sleeve where the stone now hid. Rested there. Pressed, the way one presses a hand to an aching tooth. Or a heartbeat.

And the stone answered.

Silas felt it before anyone could see it — a shiver through the air pressure, the particular wrongness of the night bending around a point just off-center. The lantern flames along Copperlace Row leaned, all together, as if a door had opened somewhere. Rain hissed once, loudly, on every awning at once, and then resumed its ordinary rhythm.

The girl gasped and snatched her hand away from her sleeve as if it had burned her.

Light was bleeding through the wool. Not reflected light, not lantern-glow — living light, pale gold shot through with threads of blue-white, pulsing in slow waves like something breathing. Like something *waking*. The sigil-stone had slept in Silas's keeping for nine years, dormant as a seed in frozen ground, obedient to every discipline of suppression they knew. Now it flared against a stranger's skin like dawn breaking inside her coat, throwing her shadow huge and wavering across the indigo cloth, across the love-charms and the wards-against-sorrow, across Silas's stunned face.

Around the market, heads began to turn. Conversations stumbled. Down the row, the spice merchant said, "What in the—" and the candle-dipper dropped a wick into the rain.

"*Stop it,*" the girl whispered to the stone, frantic, patting her own arm as though she could smother it. "Stop it, stop it, please—"

"It won't stop for that," Silas heard themself say. Their voice came out strangely calm, the calm they kept for storms. "It's answering you. Hold still."

"I didn't *do* anything—"

"No," Silas said quietly. "I know. That's rather the point."

The light swelled. And beneath the market's noise — beneath the fiddle, the rain, the rising murmur of the curious — Silas felt, unmistakably, a pressure behind the eyes. Iron and lightning at the back of the throat. The old taste. The stone was calling, and not only to the girl; it was calling to every buried thing in a mile's radius, and Silas's swallowed storm stirred in its nine-year cage and pressed against the bars.

Somewhere across the city — distant, faint, but growing — a bell began to ring. Not a vendor's bell. Not a church bell. A hard, fast, official clangor, the sound Vellharrow made perhaps once a generation, the sound mothers used to frighten children into behaving.

The ward-bells.

The girl heard it too. Her face went white as the light pouring from her sleeve. "What is that? What's happening?"

Silas was already sweeping the charms into their satchel, hands moving with a speed that surprised them, nine years of caution collapsing in four seconds into a single clear knowledge: *the city has changed tonight, and we are standing in the middle of the proof.*

"That," they said, pulling the awning down, "is the sound of a godless city remembering it was wrong."

The stone blazed in her sleeve like a captive star. And Silas Dravenmoor, charm-seller, purveyor of nonsense, took the thief by the wrist and ran.
