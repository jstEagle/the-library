## 2. The Proclamation

The bells began before dawn.

Silas heard them from the loft above the charm-shop — three strokes, a pause, three more — the pattern the city used for royal decree, and lay still beneath the rough wool blanket while the sound rolled down the crooked streets of Vellharrow like cold water finding every low place. Three and three and three. He had not heard it in eleven years. He had hoped never to hear it again.

Below him, the shop was silent, its shelves heavy with his harmless wares: copper charms against toothache, knotted threads for safe travel, painted stones that did nothing at all except give the buyer the comforting illusion that the world could be bribed. That was the trade he had built here, in the godless city, where the temples had been pulled down a century ago and magic was a story told to frighten children. In Vellharrow, a charm-seller needed no license because no one believed charms worked. It was the safest lie Silas had ever lived inside.

He dressed in the dark, fingers finding each buckle without thought, and paused at the narrow window. The sky over the rooftops was the color of pewter. No rain coming — he would have felt it gathering in his teeth, in the old ache along his forearms where lightning had once written itself into him. Just grey, flat, patient.

Three and three and three.

"Damn them," he said quietly, to no one, to the bells, to whatever clerk in some marble hall had decided that this morning, of all mornings, the crown wished to speak.

* * *

By the time he opened the shutters of his stall in Gullmarket Row, the criers were already abroad. They moved through the crowd in their scarlet tabards, flanked by soldiers with halberds, and their voices — trained, resonant, terrible in their calmness — carried over the market's morning murmur like a blade laid flat across a table.

"Hear ye! Hear ye! By decree of His Majesty, the Crown proclaims a Purge of the Gifted! All persons harboring unlicensed magics are declared enemies of the realm! Wards of detection shall be raised throughout the city at noon! Concealment is treason! Surrender is mercy! Hear ye!"

The words went through the market the way frost goes through a windowpane — silently at first, then all at once, cracking outward. A fishwife stopped mid-haggle. A boy dropped his basket of eels. Somewhere near the fountain a woman began to weep, quickly, as though she had practiced it, and her husband's hand closed on her arm and steered her away before anyone could mark her face.

Silas kept his eyes on his own hands, arranging copper charms on velvet that had never once been touched by anything but craft. *Do not look up. Do not listen twice.* Around him the market performed its own concealment: too much laughter, too many jokes about witches and bogles, the nervous theater of people who wanted very badly to seem unafraid. He knew that performance. He had been giving it for eleven years.

But under it, in the corners, other things happened. An old woman by the spice stalls turned her wedding ring twice around her finger and slipped it off, into her pocket — and Silas saw the faint shimmer die as it left her skin. A tinker wrapped something small and humming in oilcloth and buried it in the straw of his cart. Little surrenders, all down the row. The city beginning to amputate itself in advance.

*Noon*, the criers said. *Wards of detection.*

Silas had seen such wards before, long ago, in another life with another name. They did not hunt spells. Spells were quiet things, easy to smother. What the wards hunted was older than spellcraft: they sought the *gift* itself, the inherited spark, the thing you could no more cut out of yourself than your own shadow. When the wards rose, everyone who carried the blood would flare like a lamp behind paper. There was nowhere to hide from it except outside the city walls, and the walls would be watched.

He calculated, the way he always calculated, coldly and with loathing for himself afterward. One exit open before noon. The river gate, maybe. Or nothing at all.

Then he made the mistake of looking up.

* * *

She was at the far end of his stall, and she had been there longer than she should have managed without him noticing — which meant she was either very good or very desperate, and the two often looked identical. A girl of perhaps fourteen, thin as a whip handle, hair cropped raggedly short in the way of someone who had done it herself with a knife. Her eyes were fixed on the tray of sigil-stones at the stall's corner.

Not the copper charms. Not the knotted thread. The stones.

Silas sold those stones as curiosities — "river-smoothed, said to be fae-touched," he told buyers, which was even true, though the fae-touch had faded centuries ago into mere prettiness. All except one. One stone at the back of the tray, dull grey to every eye but his, still held a thread of living light. He had never found the heart to bury it in the river, and he had never found the courage to sell it honestly, so it sat there year after year, a splinter of his old life displayed among trinkets, hiding in plain sight exactly as he did.

The girl's hand drifted toward it with the unhurried grace of long practice. Their eyes met.

She ran.

Silas lunged; his elbow caught the tray; half his stock clattered into the mud; and by the time he had righted himself the girl was thirty feet gone, threading the crowd like water through reeds, the stone already swallowed in her fist. He stood there with a broken display and a heart hammering against his ribs, and understood, with terrible clarity, what he was holding now: a stolen sigil-stone, alive with covenant-light, loose in Vellharrow on the one day in eleven years when the city's wards would rise and drink in every flicker of true magic they could find.

The stone would flare. The girl would be marked. And whoever wore the scarlet of the new Inquisition would come for her with nets and irons and questions that ended lives.

Silas looked at the mud-spattered trinkets, at the criers' scarlet dwindling down the row, at the grey sky that promised nothing.

"You fool," he told himself, and went after her.

* * *

He caught her trail in Tanner's Close, where the gutters ran fat and brown and the smell could peel paint. She had wedged herself into the gap between a rendering shed and the wall, knees to chest, trying to make herself smaller than hunger had already made her. When his shadow fell across her she bared her teeth — an animal sound, no words in it.

"I'm not going to take it back," Silas said.

That stopped her. Not trust — nothing stopped fear that fast — but confusion, the brief paralysis of a creature whose script has been torn up. He crouched, slowly, keeping his hands visible, palms out, the way you'd approach a snared fox.

"The stone you took. Keep it. I don't want it." This was a lie and they both probably knew it was a lie, but it was a lie shaped like an offering. "What I want to know is why that one. Out of everything on that table."

Her eyes flicked down to her fist, white-knuckled around the stone. "It was warm," she said finally, defiant, as if daring him to laugh. "The others were just rocks. That one was warm. Like—" she struggled, hating herself for saying it— "like it knew me."

Silas felt the words go through him like a fishhook. *Like it knew me.* Eleven years he had told himself the stone was dead weight, sentiment, a relic. And a gutter-thief had walked past forty trinkets and known it in a heartbeat, the way you know kin at a funeral.

"It's dangerous to keep," he said quietly. "Especially today."

"Because of the criers?" Her chin lifted. "I've heard worse decrees. Nobody comes down into the Warrens for a thief. They'll purge the fine houses first, all the secret hedge-witches with their little garden charms—"

"No." He said it flat enough to cut. "You don't understand what's coming at noon. The wards won't care if you're rich or poor. They'll find the light in you and they'll hang a lantern over your head until the Inquisitors arrive, and the Inquisitors don't ask whether you stole or earned or were born with it. They harvest. That's the word I've heard used. Harvest."

Somewhere above them, distantly, a bell tolled the quarter hour. Two hours to noon.

The girl looked at the stone in her hand, and Silas watched terror arrive in her properly — not the sharp flash of a near-miss theft but the deep, cold flood of realizing the ground itself was about to turn against you. He knew that flood. He had drowned in it once, in another city, under another name, and crawled out the far side alone, and had spent eleven years telling himself that loneliness was the price of survival and survival was enough.

Her name, when she finally gave it, was Tessa. She spat it like a challenge.

"Tessa," Silas repeated, and hated how much it sounded like the beginning of something.

* * *

They were three streets from the river gate when the wards rose early.

Later, Silas would learn the Inquisitors had sprung them ahead of schedule precisely because the element of surprise harvested more. At the time, there was only the sound — a single vast toneless hum, felt in the fillings of teeth and the marrow of bones rather than heard — and then light bloomed everywhere at once, soft and awful, like dawn breaking underground.

Every hidden thing in Vellharrow lit up.

Down the whole length of Cooper's Lane, glimmers kindled in doorways and windows and pockets: a shimmer behind a shuttered window where a grandmother's protective knot hung over a cradle; a glow in a beggar's cup where a luck-piece worked harder than luck should; a halo around a young man's throat where he wore, God help him, a love-charm with one real drop of power in it. The city revealed itself in a hundred small fires, and the crowd on the lane recoiled from its neighbors as though they had all been walking among lepers.

And in Tessa's clenched fist, the sigil-stone blazed.

It was not a glimmer. It was not a candle. The stone burned through her fingers like a captive star, throwing her face into stark relief, painting the alley walls with pale covenant-light, and Silas saw the exact moment she understood that she could hide the bread she stole and the coins she stole but she could not hide *this*, could not hide what lived in her, that the light was coming out of her as much as the stone—

"There!" A voice like a slammed door. Scarlet at the mouth of the lane.

The Inquisitors came four abreast, and they did not run so much as advance, unhurried, certain, the way men walk when the law walks with them. The one in front carried the net — Silas knew that net, had seen its like in the last purge, woven of cold-iron thread and binding-runes, a thing that drank strength and gave back numbness. Behind him, two soldiers with pikes, and a fourth figure in a hooded cloak who held a ledger open against his forearm and wrote as he walked, unhurried, recording.

Tessa froze. It was the worst thing she could have done and the most human. The light poured from her fist and she stared at it as if it were a wound she couldn't staunch, and the net-man raised his arm with the smooth economy of long practice, and the runes along the net's rim woke green—

Silas moved.

He did not think about it; that was the truth he would carry afterward, the uncomfortable seed of everything that followed. Eleven years of careful, cowardly, meticulous concealment, and when it mattered he did not calculate at all. He stepped between the net and the girl, raised his right hand — the hand he kept gloved in public, the hand with the storm-scar running wrist to elbow like a vein of ore — and tore the glove off, and *called*.

Not lightning. He hadn't the reserves for lightning, hadn't called true weather in eleven years, and Vellharrow's dead air fought him like a drowning man fights his rescuer. What he summoned was smaller and older: the static charge of an approaching storm front, the prickle before the first drop falls. It leapt from his palm to the net's iron weave and the runes shrieked and died, and the net — worth more than the street it hung over — sagged in its bearer's grip like a landed fish.

The lane went silent. Even the wards' hum seemed to lean closer.

Four scarlet faces stared at him. The hooded one stopped writing. Slowly, deliberately, he turned to a fresh page.

"Storm-blood," the hooded man said, and his voice was almost gentle, almost reverent, which was somehow worse than cruelty. "In Vellharrow. Oh, they will want you *alive*."

"Run," Silas said — to Tessa, without turning, without taking his eyes off the net-man's arm. "Now."

"They'll chase—"

"*Run!*"

She ran. He heard her boots slap the cobbles, fading toward the river, and blessed her for having the sense he'd lacked at her age. Then the soldiers leveled their pikes, and the net-man reset his grip, and Silas Dravenmoor — charm-seller, coward, exile, storm-caller — stood alone in the middle of Cooper's Lane with his scarred hand bare and burning, and understood that the life he had built here was over, had been over since the bells, had perhaps been over since the day he was born.

The fight was short and ugly and he lost it, mostly, on purpose. He let them think the static-burst had emptied him. He let the pike butt catch him across the shoulder. He went down in the mud beside his scattered, worthless charms and let them bind his wrists with cold iron, and all the while he counted — the number of them, the weight of them, the exact moment their attention pooled on their prisoner and slackened everywhere else.

The hooded man crouched, tilted Silas's chin up with a pen of all things, and studied his face the way a merchant studies goods.

"What's your name?"

"Marlow," Silas said. "Charm-seller. I sell toothache coppers. Whatever you think you saw—"

"We saw a storm in a bottle," the hooded man murmured. "We saw eleven wasted years." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man reading an excellent ledger. "Oh yes, we know the records. We know every name the old purges missed. You've been *found*, Marlow."

They hauled him upright. And that was when the second light bloomed.

It came from the direction of the river — a pillar of pale radiance standing up out of the slums like a second dawn, unmistakable, enormous, the covenant-light of the sigil-stone answering the wards with all the voice it had. The Inquisitors turned as one. The hooded man's pen stopped moving.

"That's—" one of the soldiers began.

"That's the thief," Silas said, and his heart was slamming and his voice came out steady as bedrock, and he marveled at himself even as he spoke. "The one who stole from my stall. That light's my stolen goods. You want the gifted? She's your prize. I'm just a seller of junk."

It was madness. It was suicide. It was the first honest thing he'd done in eleven years, and he heard it land in the hooded man's mind like a coin in a dry well: greed, ambition, the hunger for a bigger harvest. For three heartbeats the Inquisitor weighed a storm-blooded charmer against a source of raw covenant-light, and Silas watched the scales tip.

"Take him anyway," the hooded man snapped. "But send riders to the river. Now."

They dragged him toward the lane's mouth — toward the wagons, the irons, the harvest — and Silas let his head loll and his body go heavy, playing the drained prisoner, while inside him something he had thought dead turned over in its sleep and opened one eye.

*Tessa,* he thought, watching the pillar of light tremble above the rooftops as she ran. *You utter fool. You magnificent, doomed little thief. Why did you stop to steal the one thing in this city that could get you killed?*

And beneath the question, quieter, an answer rising like groundwater: *Because it was warm. Because it knew you. Because somewhere in you, the same inheritance is waking up, and neither of us asked for it, and now neither of us can put it down.*

The wagon bed was filthy with straw and old blood. They threw him in it. The lock clicked. Above the rooftops, the light by the river flickered once — twice — and began to move, swift and desperate, north along the water.

North. Away from the riders, if she was clever. Toward the marsh roads, if she wasn't. Toward anything at all.

Silas tested the cold iron on his wrists, once, gently, feeling it drink at him, and settled back against the wagon's rail in the attitude of a broken man, and began, methodically, patiently, with eleven years of practice in the art of being overlooked, to work the first knot loose.

The wagon lurched forward. The bells rang on. Somewhere behind him, the hooded man's pen scratched steadily across a page that already held too many names.

There were, Silas reckoned, perhaps three hours of daylight left — three hours before the riders closed on a runaway girl who had never been taught anything except how to run, three hours in which he could be a coward or he could be the last of a line whose whole purpose, once upon a forgotten time, had been to stand between the hunted and the hunters.

He worked the knot.

The rope gave a whisper of movement. Outside, the wheels turned toward the Inquisitors' compound — and Silas Dravenmoor, exiled storm-caller, began to plan how to rob the Crown of its harvest.
