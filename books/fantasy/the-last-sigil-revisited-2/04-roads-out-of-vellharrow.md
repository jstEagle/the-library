## 4. Roads Out of Vellharrow

Tessa had never seen so much water in her life, and all of it was falling on her.

The rain came down in ropes, thick and silver, drumming the flooded lowlands south of the city into a single shivering mirror. They waded knee-deep along a drowned cart-road, past fences with only their shoulders showing, past an orchard whose trees stood to their waists like drowned men still holding their apples. The sky had been grey since dawn. Not storm-grey — Silas kept saying *not yet, not yet* — but heavy as wet wool, sagging under its own weight.

"Keep your hood up," Silas said, for the fourth time.

"My hood has a hole in it."

"Then keep the hole pointed away from the wind."

Tessa pulled the ruined wool tighter anyway and trudged on. Her boots were full. Everything was full. The whole world felt full — of water, of noise, of the strange electric hush that pressed behind her teeth like she'd bitten foil. She had decided, somewhere around the third mile, that this was what freedom tasted like: cold, soaked, and slightly terrified.

Behind them, Vellharrow's walls were a smudge of smoke and bell-sound, already dissolving. Somewhere back there, criers were reading names. Somewhere back there, wards flared blue at every window where a gifted soul hid their light, and Inquisitors moved through the streets like scissors through thread. Somewhere back there was everything Tessa had ever known, which was a rented room, a stolen supper most nights, and nobody's name spoken warmly in her direction except by accident.

She looked at the two people walking ahead of her through the flood.

Silas Dravenmoor moved like someone carrying a barrel of gunpowder through a candle-factory. Careful. Measured. Every step placed as if the ground might report on them. The charm-seller's shabby coat hung off a frame that seemed too still for how fast they could move when it mattered — Tessa had seen it matter, in that alley, when the Inquisitor's net came hissing out of the dark and Silas's hand had snapped up and the rain itself had caught the net mid-air like a loyal dog catching a stick. Nobody had taught her that magic could look like that. Nobody had told her anything about magic at all, really, except *don't*, which turned out to be the same lesson everyone taught her about everything.

And behind Silas, limping and glowering and refusing help, came Bram Corvale. Former Inquisitor. Current disaster. He wore his grey coat open because he said wearing it closed made him feel like a liar, and he'd spent the first mile of their flight explaining, in excruciating detail, why he was not fleeing with them so much as conducting a parallel investigation in the same direction.

"You're limping," Tessa told him now.

"I'm favoring a strategic ankle."

"It's swelling."

"It's *considering* it."

She grinned despite herself. He scowled harder, which she was learning was just his face's way of saying good morning. Three days ago he'd cornered her and Silas in a drainage tunnel with a drawn ward-blade, and instead of killing them he'd opened the gate. He hadn't explained it well. He'd mentioned a sister named Wren, once, in a voice like a man describing weather in a country that no longer existed, and then he'd stopped talking about it entirely, which told Tessa more than the telling would have.

The stone rode against her chest, under her shirt, warm as a sleeping bird.

That was the thing she couldn't stop thinking about. She'd stolen it fair and square — or unfairly and squarely, depending on whom you asked; theft had been her trade since before her voice finished changing — and she'd stolen plenty of stones before. This one had flared. It had *lit up* in her hand like a held breath finally let go, and ever since, it hadn't gone fully dark again. It sat against her sternum and hummed, faintly, patiently, the way a kettle hums before anyone admits the fire is lit.

And lately — she hadn't told them this yet, mostly because she couldn't find words that wouldn't make her sound mad, and partly because some small greedy part of her wanted to keep one secret that was only hers — lately, it whispered.

Not words, exactly. Not at first. More like the feeling of standing outside a house at night and knowing people were home. But last night, sheltering in the lee of a fallen granary while the rain stitched the world shut, she had heard something almost like a name. A woman's voice, worn smooth as river glass, saying a name that wasn't Tessa's and somehow felt like it should be.

She touched the stone through her wet shirt. It warmed.

Ahead, Silas stopped so abruptly that Bram walked into them.

"Sorry," Silas said, though they weren't looking at Bram. They were looking at the sky.

"What?" Bram's hand went to his blade. "Hunters?"

"No." Silas's jaw worked. "Worse. I think—" They exhaled slowly through their nose. "I think we need to get off this road. Now. There's a treeline east of here—"

"Everything east of here is underwater," Tessa said.

"Then we swim a little." Silas was already turning, and there was something in their face that frightened her more than any Inquisitor: fear without a target. "Please. Both of you. Quickly, and don't argue."

"Why? What's wrong?"

"I'm wrong," Silas said quietly. "I can feel it coming up my arms like—like water finding cracks. I haven't called anything. I haven't *asked*. And that means it's asking me."

The pressure behind Tessa's teeth sharpened. She realized the hush she'd been feeling all morning wasn't the weather at all. It was attention. The whole sky leaning closer to listen.

They left the road, plunging into thigh-deep water toward the distant smudge of trees. The rain thickened around them as they went, and Tessa noticed — with the particular horror of watching something happen in slow motion that you are powerless to stop — that it thickened *around Silas*. Fell harder where they walked. Swirled in little eddies at their shoulders like the drops couldn't decide whether to fall on them or worship them.

"Silas," Bram called over the rising wind. "Your hands."

Tessa looked. Light crawled beneath Silas's skin, pale and branching, like lightning remembering old roads. When Silas spoke, their voice came doubled, layered with a low rolling undertone that lived somewhere below hearing.

"I know," Silas said. "I'm sorry. I've buried it so long I forgot burial isn't the same as death. The city's wards going down — the Purge tearing holes everywhere — it's like every cage door in the world opened at once, including mine."

"Can you close it again?" Tessa asked.

"I closed it for fifteen years." Silas laughed, and the laugh cracked like ice on a pond. "You don't close a storm, girl. You just agree with it to hold still. And right now I am very, very tired of agreeing."

Then the sky agreed with *them*.

Lightning hit the flooded plain half a mile north — not striking down so much as *arriving*, a white column of it, and thunder followed so hard and fast it felt less like sound than like being slapped by the air itself. The water around their legs shivered into rings. And far behind them, back along the drowned road, Tessa heard the thing Silas must have been dreading: voices. Horns. The high, thin whine of a ward-net charging.

"Hunters," Bram said flatly. "Congratulations, storm-caller. You just rang the dinner bell for half the province."

"They'll have seen the strike," Silas said. "They'll have felt it. Run. *Run.*"

They ran, or did the drowning-land version of running, crashing through water and mud and reeds, Bram swearing steadily in a rhythm that almost helped, Tessa clutching the strap of her satchel with one hand and her shirt-front with the other as if the stone might leap free and betray them. The trees grew nearer with agonizing slowness. Behind them the horns multiplied. Ahead, the rain fell so hard it became a kind of blindness.

And through it all, under the thunder, under her own hammering heart, Tessa heard it clearly for the first time:

*A name.*

"*Maren,*" the stone whispered, in that worn-smooth voice, urgent now, awake now, as if the lightning had reached all the way inside it too. "*Child of the roof-rain, run east, run east, the oak that holds the lightning remembers the way—*"

Tessa nearly fell. She grabbed Silas's sleeve.

"The oak!" she shouted, over the storm, over everything. "There's an oak! East! The stone says — the stone says it remembers!"

Silas stared at her. Rain sheeted off their chin. For one suspended moment they looked at her the way she imagined people looked at doors they thought had been bricked up years ago — stunned that anything could still come through.

"You hear it," Silas said. Not a question. Something older than a question. "You hear the *voice*."

"Is that bad?"

"I don't know!" And Silas laughed again, wilder this time, even as they seized her wrist and hauled her forward. "But it's the first thing in fifteen years I haven't known alone, so let's find out together!"

*

The oak, when they found it, was worth the legend.

It stood on the last dry knoll before the true marsh began, vast beyond sense, its trunk broad as a chapel, black bark seamed with ancient lightning-scars. Its canopy thrashed in the wind but the great limbs held, and beneath it, improbably, a hollow between two roots formed a dry dark pocket just large enough for three exhausted fugitives. As they crowded into it, the storm raged on every side — but not here. Rain bent around the knoll like current around a stone in a stream. Lightning struck twice within sight, both times sliding off into the flood, deflected, *politely declined*.

"This tree is warded," Bram breathed. "Old warding. Pre-Purge. I've read about this in confiscated grimoires — oath-trees. Living anchor-points for the old Covenant." He looked at Silas with an expression caught between accusation and awe. "You knew about this."

"I knew nothing," Silas said. "I've never seen it before in my life. But I've *dreamed* about it." Their hand rose, unbidden, to touch the scarred bark. "Every dream since I was a child. This tree. This exact angle of its roots. I thought it was just a dream."

"It's a memory," Tessa said.

They both turned to her.

Her ears were ringing. Not from the thunder — from the voice, which had grown stronger the moment she'd crossed under the branches, as though the tree and the stone recognized each other across centuries. She sank down between the roots, pulled the sigil-stone from her shirt, and held it out on her palm. It glowed softly, gold shot through with green, pulsing gently in time with — she realized with a lurch — her own heartbeat.

"She talks to me," Tessa said. "The stone. Or — someone in the stone. A woman. Old voice. Kind, but like — like kind the way winter is kind, you know? Honest-kind. She started whispering yesterday. Just feelings first. Then today, when the storm woke up—" Tessa swallowed. "She said a name. Maren. And she said *child of the roof-rain*, which is weird, because that sounds like it should be about me but it can't be, because I grew up in a room above a chandlery and the roof leaked and nobody ever called me anything but *girl* and *thief* and worse."

Silas had gone very still.

"Say the name again," they said.

"Maren."

"Say what she calls you."

"Child of the... roof-rain." Tessa frowned. "Why?"

"Because," Silas said slowly, "that is what my grandmother called me. Before they took her. Before they erased her — not killed her, *erased* her, scraped her out of every record and every memory they could reach, so thoroughly that until three days ago I couldn't even have told you I'd had a grandmother." Their eyes were shining, and their voice shook with something between grief and wonder. "Maren oathspeaker. First keeper of the Covenant of Sigils. And she is speaking through a stolen stone to a runaway pickpocket, and calling her by my childhood name."

The silence between the roots was very full.

Tessa looked down at the stone glowing in her palm, and for the first time she didn't feel like a thief holding stolen goods. She felt like someone holding a letter that had taken years to arrive, addressed in handwriting she somehow recognized.

"She says things," Tessa offered, quieter now. "When I listen. Mostly names, so far. Names and — directions. Like she's leaving me a trail." She hesitated, then made herself say the rest, the part she'd been hoarding. "And sometimes, when I hold it at night, it doesn't feel like a stranger's voice at all. It feels like..." She struggled. "Like sitting outside a kitchen door listening to family talk. Warm. You know. Belonging-shaped."

Bram made a small sound, quickly suppressed.

Silas reached out — slowly, asking permission with the pause — and covered Tessa's hand with their own, over the stone. The glow brightened at the contact. Silas's breath caught.

"Oh," they said softly. "Oh, hello, Grandmother."

Outside, the storm raged on. Inside the root-hollow, rain-wet and shaking, three strangers who were not strangers sat in the dark while a dead woman's voice warmed a stolen stone, and Tessa felt something crack open in her chest that she suspected would never fully close again.

She had belonged to nothing her whole life. Gangs didn't count — gangs were just hunger wearing a group costume. And here, in a hole in a lightning-tree, hunted by an empire, soaked to the bone and more afraid than she'd ever been, she was *known*. Called by name. Given a grandmother she'd never earned and couldn't return.

It was terrifying. It was the best thing that had ever happened to her.

"So," Bram said at last, practical as a boot. "The hunters are ten minutes behind us and closing. Do we stay in the magic tree, or do we run?"

"The stone will tell us," Tessa said, and found she meant it.

The stone pulsed. The voice rose, clear now, urgent, threaded through with something that sounded almost like laughter — grim, fierce, ancient laughter:

"*East, little thief. East into the deep fen, where the drowned abbey keeps its candles lit. Your grandfather's roof waits under the ivy. Come and see what they tried to burn.*"

Tessa looked up. Silas was already staring east, into the rain, their face lit gold and green by the stone's glow — and lit also, she saw, by tears that the storm could not explain away.

"An abbey," Silas whispered. "I dreamed that too. I dreamed *everything*."

"Then we go east," Tessa said, standing, tucking the stone back against her heart, feeling it beat there like a second pulse, like an answer, like a hand reaching down through the years to take hers. "All of us. Together."

Somewhere behind them, horns split the rain. Somewhere ahead, a drowned abbey waited with its candles burning for no one.

Tessa ran into the storm smiling.
