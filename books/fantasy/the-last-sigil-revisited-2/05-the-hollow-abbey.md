## 5. The Hollow Abbey

The rain found them an hour past dusk, and Silas let it.

That was the trouble with a gift buried too long, they had learned — it did not die of neglect. It grew patient. It waited in the marrow like a seed under frost, and when the clouds finally broke open over the flooded lowlands, something in Silas's chest turned toward the falling water the way a caged bird turns toward an open window. They could have called it off. A word, a gesture, a breath shaped just so, and the storm would have parted around them like a crowd making way for a king.

Instead Silas walked in it, hood up, hands in pockets, and let the rain say what they could not.

*You are still mine,* the water said against their shoulders. *You are still mine, and you are hiding.*

"Silas." Tessa's voice, pitched low beneath the drumming. She had stopped three paces back, her stolen sigil-stone cradled against her chest where she'd carried it since the tunnel, since Bram, since the whole improbable chain of events that had taken a charm-seller and a pickpocket and turned them into fugitives. "There's a light. Ahead. Or — not a light. More like a *lack* of dark."

Bram grunted behind her. The Inquisitor moved like a man carrying his own gallows on his back; he had not spoken more than ten words at a time since he'd opened that gate and stepped through it himself, damning his order, his uniform, his whole careful life. His hand rested now on the ledger-stub of his badge, which he hadn't thrown away. Silas had noticed. Silas said nothing about it. There were things a man needed to carry until he was ready to set down.

"That'll be the abbey," Silas said.

"You know it?" Tessa asked.

"I know *of* it." And even that was strange, wasn't it? Silas frowned, turning the thought over like a coin of uncertain mint. "Everyone from the lowlands knows of Hollow Abbey. It's a ruin. Children dare each other to sleep there. It's supposed to be — "

*Hollow,* said a voice that was not theirs, rising up through the wet earth and the old stones and the roots of things. *It is called hollow because we emptied it.*

Silas stopped walking.

The rain went on falling. Tessa and Bram came up beside them, and for a moment the three of them stood together in the dark and the water, listening to a silence that was somehow fuller than the storm.

"Did you feel that?" Tessa whispered.

"Yes," said Silas, who had felt it, who had felt it *in their teeth*, in the same place the storm lived, in the same place their grandmother's name should have been and was not. "Come on. We need walls tonight, whatever's in them."

* * *

The abbey announced itself by absence. One moment the night was night, unbroken; the next there was a shape in it, a vast pale suggestion of arches and buttresses, as if someone had drawn an abbey in negative space and forgotten to fill it in. The west wall had fallen outward centuries ago, its stones tumbled into a field gone wild with wet grass and night-blooming something — Silas couldn't name the flowers, and the failure to name them itched. The roof survived only over what must have been the central nave, half-collapsed, sagging like a tired animal.

But it was dry under the surviving vaulting. That was enough. Dry ground, stone at their backs, a single entrance wide enough that no one could creep up on them unseen. Bram made a soldier's circuit without being asked, tested the acoustics with a boot-heel, and settled near the doorway with his sword across his knees — a sentry who no longer knew whose side he was guarding.

Tessa, meanwhile, had discovered the murals.

"Silas." Her voice came out strange. Flat, and then not flat at all. "Silas, you need to come see this."

He crossed the nave slowly, boots crunching on fallen plaster, and looked up at where she was looking, and the world quietly came apart at the seams.

The mural ran the length of the eastern wall, thirty feet of it maybe, painted in pigments that had somehow refused the centuries — deep lapis and storm-grey and a gold that still glimmered faintly when Tessa's sigil-stone swung near it, as if the paint remembered being alive. It showed a procession of figures ascending a spiral path toward an altar, and every figure stood in rain.

Every figure stood in rain, and none of them were drowned by it. The rain bent around them. It rose off their shoulders in silver threads and braided itself above their heads into crowns of weather. Lightning answered their lifted hands like hounds answering a whistle.

And their faces —

Their faces were Silas's face.

Not alike. Not reminiscent. *Identical.* The same narrow jaw. The same high cheekbones with that slight asymmetry, left higher than right. The same eyes rendered in pigment so old it should have faded to ghost-grey but held instead a color Silas had spent forty years avoiding in mirrors: grey-green, the exact shade of a sky deciding whether to storm.

An old woman at the bottom of the spiral. A middle-aged figure halfway up. A young person near the top, arm raised, mouth open in what might have been song or command or grief. Different clothes, different centuries written in the cut of collars and the drape of cloaks. The same face, repeated like a refrain.

"There are nine of them," Tessa said softly. She had counted. Of course she had counted; she counted everything, the little thief, hoarding numbers the way other people hoarded coins. "Nine figures. Same face. Same face all the way up." She turned, and the sigil-stone in her cupped hands threw moving light across her expression. "Silas. What is this?"

"I don't know," Silas said, which was true, which was also the most comprehensive lie they had ever told, because some part of them knew exactly what it was and had always known and had been *forbidden* to know, and the knowing was pressing up against the inside of their skull like floodwater against a door.

Bram had come to stand beneath the mural too. He was quiet a long time. When he spoke, his voice had the careful neutrality of a man reading charges aloud.

"This is oathkeeper iconography. Pre-Purge. I've seen fragments in confiscated texts — the Order burns most of them, but some survive in the archives. The spiral path. The rain-crowns. The figure ascending to swear the Covenant." His finger traced the air below the highest painted face. "This is a lineage painting. Storm-callers. An unbroken line of them, sworn to the Covenant of Sigils, generation to generation." He paused. "The archives say the line ended. Extinct. Purged to the last heir two generations ago."

"Two generations," Silas repeated.

The words came out very small.

Two generations. A grandmother who had died — how? When? Silas reached for the memory and found a smooth place, a river stone in the mind where a story should have been. They had always assumed grief had worn it away. Grief wore everything away eventually; that was what they'd told themselves. But grief left rough edges. Grief left *scar tissue*. This was different. This was a hole cut clean, the edges polished, deliberate, the work of someone with good tools and bad intentions.

"My mother never spoke of her," Silas heard themselves say. "My mother never spoke of *her own mother*. I thought — I was a child, I thought they'd quarreled. Families quarrel."

"And you?" Tessa asked gently. "Do you remember your grandmother?"

"I remember — " Silas closed their eyes. Reached. The rain outside redoubled against the broken roof, and somewhere far below the floor, in the abbey's drowned crypts, water was moving through channels that had once been built to carry sound as well as water, and the whole ruin was a throat, and it was beginning, very softly, to speak.

"I remember a roof. Rain on a roof. I remember being very small and standing under the eaves and watching it come down, and someone was laughing. Someone was laughing because the rain was *listening* to me. I remember that much." Their eyes opened. "I don't remember her face. I don't remember her voice. I don't remember her name."

The silence that followed had weight.

"Someone took it," Tessa said. Not a question. Her knuckles had gone white around the sigil-stone, and the stone had begun, very faintly, to pulse — slow, steady, patient as a heartbeat. "Someone reached into your head and took your grandmother out of it. Took her *name* out of it. Who does that? How do you even — "

"The Order has rites," Bram said. His voice was rough. "Memory-work. I trained in the theory. I never — " He stopped. Started again. "There's a rite called the Flaying of Threads. It removes a person, root and branch, from every mind that holds them. It's forbidden even within the Order except by crown decree. It requires a dozen Inquisitors working in concert. It leaves scars like the one you're describing." He looked at Silas, and there was something terrible in his face — not pity, something rawer. "It's used on oathkeepers. On anyone the Covenant might reach through. My sister's records ended with a notation I never understood until tonight. *Threads flayed.* I thought it meant executed."

"It means they're not dead," Silas said. The vertigo was worse now, a slow spiraling, the mural's painted faces wheeling overhead. "It means they're *unmade*. You can't mourn someone if you can't hold the shape of them. That's why the city doesn't remember the Covenant. That's why Vellharrow is magic-free — not because magic left, but because everyone who remembered it belonging to ordinary people was — " They pressed the heel of their hand against their sternum, hard, as if they could hold their ribs shut around the shaking. "How many? Bram. How many times has the crown done this?"

"How many purges?" Bram laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as old bone. "Officially, three in the last century. Unofficially?" He shook his head. "The archives I accessed before I defected suggested the harvests have been continuous. Small ones. Quiet ones. Villages here and there. Gifted children reported missing. Whole family lines struck from parish rolls." He looked up at the mural, at the nine identical faces climbing toward the altar. "A lineage like this wouldn't be destroyed in a single purge. It would be *thinned*. Generation after generation. Kept small. Kept frightened. Kept forgetting."

"Kept *breeding*," Silas whispered, and hated the word even as they said it. "Like livestock. Like a well you don't drain dry because you might want water next year."

They turned away from the mural. Walked to the broken western wall. Stood looking out at the rain-hammered dark, at the wildflowers they couldn't name, and felt the full vertiginous horror of it settle onto them at last: not that they had lost a heritage, but that the loss itself had been *manufactured*. Curated. Maintained, year over year, by people whose job it was to make sure Silas Dravenmoor never once wondered why their storms answered them like family.

"They didn't exile me," Silas said to the rain. "Exile implies you did something. Exile implies there was somewhere you belonged and were cast out of. They just — pruned me. Cut the branch and burned the cutting and left the tree standing because the tree was useful. Because someday they'd want another storm-caller, and better to leave one growing wild than start from seed."

Behind them, Tessa said nothing. But Silas heard her sit down on a fallen block of masonry, heard her open the satchel where she kept the journal she'd started keeping two nights ago without explanation — writing, always writing, in her cramped thief's shorthand — and heard the scratch of her pencil begin.

"What are you doing?" Silas asked without turning.

"Writing down what you just said," Tessa said. "All of it. Word for word as best I can. If they can take memories out of heads, then heads aren't safe. Paper might not be either, but paper can be copied. Paper can be hidden in six places. Paper can be *buried* and dug up." The scratching paused. "Nobody can flay a thread out of a hundred copies in a hundred cellars."

Silas turned. The girl sat in the pooled light of the sigil-stone, seventeen years old at most, knees drawn up, hair plastered dark with rain, writing down a stranger's grief so it couldn't be stolen twice. Something in Silas's chest cracked — not painfully. The way ice cracks on a river in spring. The way a lock cracks when the right key finally turns.

"Tessa."

She looked up.

"When we get wherever we're going — if we get anywhere — remind me to teach you properly. Not charm-tricks. The real work." A breath. "Storms listen to you whether you ask or not. Least I can do is return the courtesy."

The pencil scratched faster.

* * *

Sleep came in shifts, as it always did on the road. Bram took first watch at the doorway, silhouette black against the lesser darkness, sword bare across his thighs. Tessa slept curled around the sigil-stone like it was a hearth-cat. And Silas lay on bedroll under the surviving vaulting and stared up at the painted faces wheeling overhead whenever lightning flashed beyond the broken roof, and did not sleep at all.

Sometime past midnight, the abbey began to sing.

Not with voices. With resonance. The rain had found the old acoustic channels beneath the floor — Silas could feel it now, could feel the water threading through the crypts the way blood threads through a hand — and the builders, whoever they had been, had carved those channels into pipes and grooves that turned falling water into sustained tone. A chord. Deep and minor and patient. It rose up through the flagstones and into Silas's spine and it was *familiar*, gods help them, it was familiar the way a language you dreamed in as a child is familiar, half-forgotten and wholly known.

Silas got up. Crossed the nave barefoot. Stood before the mural while the chord swelled, and put their palm flat against the lowest figure — the old woman at the bottom of the spiral, the beginning of the ascent.

Under their hand, the pigment warmed.

Not metaphorically. Warmth bloomed against Silas's skin like a hand returning pressure, like a grip closing around a grip, and the sigil-stone across the nave flared bright enough to throw Tessa's shadow huge against the far wall, and a voice spoke — not aloud, not in the air, but in the water in Silas's blood:

*Little storm. You came home the long way.*

Silas's knees nearly went. They braced against the wall, against the painted woman, against the impossible warmth of her, and managed, aloud, in a whisper: "Who are you?"

*You know my roof. You stood under my eaves. You cannot hold my name — they cut it out of you — but the stone remembers, and the wall remembers, and the rain remembers, and when you stand in the rain you are standing in me, child, you have been standing in me your whole life.* The warmth pulsed, steady, grief-stricken, fierce. *Listen. There is little time. The hunters circle wider each night. Hear what was stolen and begin to take it back.*

And the wall opened.

Not physically. But knowledge began to move into Silas the way water moves into cracked ground — slowly, painfully, finding every fissure. The Covenant of Sigils: five great seals binding mortal realm to Otherworld, sworn on this very altar by the first storm-caller and the first fae queen, maintained ever after by an unbroken line of oathkeepers whose faces climbed this wall. The king's grandfather had broken the first seal and called it reform. The harvests had fed the breaking. Each purge had not been persecution but *mining* — gifted lives torn open for the memory-stuff that powered the severance. And still the Covenant held, barely, because the line endured, because somewhere in the world a child was born with rain crowning them, and the crown could prune but never quite kill the tree.

Until now. Until the Great Severing. Until the last five sigils were gathered and the last oathkeeper's line was ended forever.

*Five sigils,* sang the wall, and the stone in Tessa's sleeping arms pulsed in time. *Scattered when the second seal broke. Hidden by the last full covenant of oathkeepers, in places only our blood could find. Tide in the drowning chapel. Root beneath the old capital. Ember in the ash court's keeping. Two more besides, names worn from even this stone. Gather them, little storm. Reforge what was broken. And know this — the final forging demands everything the oathkeeper remembers. The Covenant does not accept partial payment.*

Silas pulled their hand back. The warmth lingered on their palm like a brand.

Behind them, Tessa was awake — had been awake, Silas realized, for some unknowable span — sitting up with the stone blazing in her lap, watching Silas with wide eyes. Even Bram had turned from the doorway, though his hand had gone to his sword out of pure institutional reflex.

"It talked to you," Tessa breathed. "The wall talked to you. What did it say?"

Silas looked up at the nine faces. Their face. Their inheritance, cut out of them and painted here in secret, waiting a lifetime and more for them to walk in out of the rain.

"It said," Silas said slowly, "that I'm the last of a line of kings the world forgot on purpose. It said there are five keys to putting the world back together, and I'm the only lock they fit. And it said—" their voice caught, steadied, "—it said the price of the fixing is everything I am."

The chord beneath the floor faded, note by note, like a tide going out. In the ringing quiet, Bram spoke from the doorway, his voice flat and cold and certain:

"Then we'd better move fast. Because there are riders on the road — I saw their lanterns twenty minutes ago. Three parties, fanned out, sweeping toward us." He drew his sword an inch and let it slide home. "The Order taught me how they hunt. They'll have this valley sealed by dawn."

Outside, the rain was stopping. And in the sudden hush, from far away across the flooded fields, came a sound that made the sigil-stone dim and Tessa go white: a horn, long and low, blown three times.

The hunting call of the Purge.
