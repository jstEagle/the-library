## 6. What the King Severed

The abbey had no roof left to speak of — just ribs of stone reaching up into a sky gone the color of wet slate — and so the rain came in freely, pooling in the hollows of the nave where the faithful had once knelt. Bram Corvale stood at the broken doorway with his back to the storm, watching the road they had come by, and told himself he was keeping watch. That was the honest word for it. Keeping watch. Not hiding from two people he had sworn to hunt.

Behind him, Silas Dravenmoor crouched over a small fire built of rotted pew-ends, coaxing it alive with hands that moved too surely for a mere charm-seller. The flames bent toward those fingers like dogs toward a whistle. Tessa sat wrapped in Bram's own travel cloak — he'd given it without deciding to, which troubled him more than anything else about this day — turning the sigil-stone over and over in her cupped palms as if it were an egg she was trying not to crack.

It flared every few breaths. A soft pulse of gold under her skin, lighting her face from below so that she looked, for a moment, like one of the painted saints still clinging to the abbey walls above them, haloed and half-flaked away.

"You should eat," Bram said, because his voice needed something ordinary to do. He set down the ration tins he'd carried out of the garrison — three of them, unopened, stolen from his own quartermaster with a forged chit he was almost proud of. "There'll be riders behind us by morning. Corvale's hounds don't lose scent."

"They won't find us," Silas said quietly. "Not tonight." And Bram understood that the storm hammering the lowlands outside was not weather. It was a fence made of thunder.

He sat down on a fallen slab of altar-stone. It was strange, sitting where priests had stood. Stranger still, what he was about to do.

* * *

Bram Corvale had been nine years old when the first Purge came through Marrowfen, the village where he was born, and he had learned early that the Inquisition did not take people all at once. It took them in ledger-lines. Names entered, names struck through. His sister Wren had been fourteen when the grey-coated assessors arrived, and she had been taken not because anyone saw her do magic but because the assessor's brass needle trembled when it passed over her — a resonance reading, they called it, a gift detected like fever in the blood.

His mother had signed the papers herself. That was the part Bram never said aloud, even now, alone, in the rain. She signed because refusing meant the whole family went on the list, and the list, everyone knew by then, was not a list you came off of.

Wren had written letters for two years. Then the letters stopped, and the reply the family received was a single printed card: *Subject reassigned to Crown service. Correspondence discontinued per statute.* Reassigned. As if she were a clerk moved between offices.

When Bram was old enough, he applied to the Inquisition. People assumed it was loyalty. His mother wept with pride at his oath-taking; the neighbors toasted him. But the truth was smaller and uglier: he joined because inside the Inquisition, there were records. Files. And somewhere in the deep archive beneath the Hall of Questions, there was a file with Wren's name on it, and Bram had spent eleven years climbing the ranks for the privilege of reading it.

He had finally read it eight months ago.

That was when he learned what *reassigned* meant.

* * *

"The king isn't hunting you," Bram said, staring into the fire. "I need you to understand that first. You're not the point. None of the gifted are the point. You're the fuel."

Silas looked up sharply. Tessa went very still, the way animals go still when they hear something too low for human ears.

"Fuel," Silas repeated.

"The Purge has three arms," Bram said. "You've seen the first — the assessors, the nets, the public burnings of charms and books. That's theater. It keeps the cities calm, gives them someone to fear and someone to cheer. The second arm is the harvest convoys. When we take a gifted person, we don't hang them. We don't imprison them. We send them north, to a facility called Vespergate. There hasn't been a single execution in four years. Did you never wonder why? A crown that hates magic would make examples of you. Instead it makes you vanish."

Tessa's voice came out thin. "What happens at Vespergate?"

Bram made himself say it plainly. He owed them that much, after the drainage tunnel, after the gate he'd opened instead of closing. Plainness was the only coin he had left.

"There's a machine," he said. "Older than the Inquisition, older than the current dynasty — the engineers who maintain it found its bones already in the rock when they dug. It's called the Loom. It takes memory. Not metaphorically — I mean memory as substance, as thread, as weight. The gifted carry more of it than ordinary folk, because inherited magic is nothing but memory — your grandmother's storms living in your blood, her grandmother's before hers. The Loom draws it out. All of it. Every name, every face, every year."

"And the person?" Silas asked. Their voice was flat, controlled, the voice of someone holding a door shut against a flood.

"What's left is called Hollowed." Bram's mouth tasted of ash saying it. "They breathe. They walk. They follow orders with perfect obedience because there's nothing left in them to disobey *with*. We're told it's mercy — that the alternative is execution. We're told their memories are stored safely, archived for the realm's protection." He laughed once, a short ugly sound with no humor in it anywhere. "Stored. Like grain."

The fire popped. Rain drummed on the flagstones beyond the nave, filling the silence.

"I read Wren's file," Bram said. "Eight months ago. Her resonance reading, her intake date, her processing date. There was a column at the end labeled *yield*. Do you know how they measure a human being at Vespergate? In threads. Wren yielded four hundred and twelve threads of memory. Four hundred and twelve. Somewhere in the Loom's storehouse is everything my sister ever loved, wound onto a spool, and walking around wearing her body is something that answers to her name and knows nothing else."

Tessa made a small sound and pressed the sigil-stone against her sternum, hard, as if it could beat for her.

* * *

"Why?" Silas asked. It wasn't horror in their voice anymore — or rather it was horror, but horror with a spine in it, horror standing upright. "Why would a king do this? Memory is power, yes, but harvested from a few hundred gifted—"

"A few thousand," Bram corrected. "And rising. Every city, every province. The quotas tripled last spring." He drew a breath, and here it was, the thing he had carried out of the Hall of Questions like a coal in his chest, burning him from the inside ever since. "Because the memories aren't the prize. They're the payment. You can't cut a world loose for free."

Silas rose slowly to their feet. The firelight made their shadow climb the ruined mural wall behind them, up across the faded figures Bram hadn't yet let himself look at closely — a procession of robed figures, each with lightning drawn around their heads like a crown.

"Say it," Silas said.

"The Great Severing." Bram stood too. Some things you couldn't confess sitting down. "The Otherworld. The fae realm, the twilight country, whatever name you like — it's been thinning for a century, everyone agrees on that, the crossings close a little more each winter. What nobody outside the inner council knows is that the thinning isn't natural decay. It's *pressure*. Two worlds bound together by the old Covenant of Sigils, and one of them pulling at the knot. The king means to untie it. Permanently. To sever the mortal realm from the Otherworld entirely, forever, so that no crossing will ever open again, no fae bargain will ever bind a crown again, no inherited magic—" he gestured at Silas, at Tessa, at the storm muttering overhead "—will have anywhere to come from. Magic is a river, Dravenmoor. Cut off the source, and every stream runs dry within a generation. No gifted children born. No needles trembling over cradles. No lists. The king calls it the Clean Realm."

"But the Severing needs power," Tessa whispered. "To cut something that big."

"To cut a bond woven by gods and oathkeepers," Bram said, "you need thread of equal strength. Human memory is the only thread there is. Every soul fed into the Loom strengthens the blade. They've been harvesting for four years, building the edge, stroke by stroke, name by name." He looked at Silas directly, and did not look away, though it cost him. "Your grandmother was right, whatever she was. Whatever you are. The Covenant of Sigils is the knot itself — the binding that holds the two worlds together. Break the Covenant, and the Severing holds. That's why they erased the lineage. That's why they burned the abbeys. They weren't destroying magic, they were cutting the rope at both ends."

* * *

Silas had turned to the mural wall. Bram watched them raise one hand, slow as a man approaching a shrine, and touch the painted hem of the foremost robed figure.

In the better light of the fire, thrown sideways, Bram could see the faces now properly for the first time. Generations of them, processioned along the stone like beads on a wire — and the same face recurring, the same narrow jaw, the same storm-grey eyes rendered in pigments that had somehow kept their color while everything holy around them flaked to dust.

"That's you," he said. It wasn't a question. Nothing about this night deserved the comfort of questions.

"It's me," Silas said. Their voice had gone strange — distant, underwater. "Or it's my mother. Or her mother. I don't know which. I don't remember enough of any of them to know which face is mine and which face I've been given." They laughed, softly, terribly. "Do you understand what he's done, Corvale? He hasn't just been harvesting strangers. He's been eating a family. My family. Thread by thread, until there was one left who didn't even know she had a name worth stealing."

Tessa stood and went to them — not touching, just standing near, the way you stand near a struck bell. The sigil-stone pulsed against her chest, once, twice, in a rhythm that Bram realized, with a cold sliding feeling, matched Silas's breathing.

"My sister is in that machine," Bram said. "Maybe not her body anymore. Maybe not even that. But her four hundred and twelve threads are wound into the edge of the king's blade, and when the Severing falls, everything Wren ever was becomes the instrument of ending everything she believed in." His fists were shaking at his sides and he let them shake. "I wore the grey coat for eleven years. I held nets over frightened children. I told myself it was order. I told myself the alternative was chaos, that gifts unchecked burn villages, that the lists protect people. And underneath all of it, the whole time, the machine was using my hands to feed itself."

"Then why are you here?" Silas asked, turning from the wall. Firelight and storm-light fought across their face. "You opened a gate. Fine. One gate. You could have walked back tomorrow, reported a tunnel collapse, been clean by supper. Why give us your rations, your cloak, your truth?"

Bram looked at the fire for a long time.

"Because yesterday morning," he said, "I processed an intake manifest from a village called Thistledown Hollow, and I recognized the assessor's handwriting, and I checked the resonance returns, and there was a girl listed on it. Age thirteen. Gift class: unclassified, anomalous. Village record: emptied." He nodded at Tessa, whose face had gone white as the salt-rimed stones outside. "They think you died in the crossing at Fenmarsh, child. Your name is in the ledger with a line through it. But names don't come off that list. They only come *off* when they come off the living roll of the realm entirely — and the only way that happens is Vespergate." He swallowed. "Someone falsified your death to hide you. Someone with access to the master rolls. I intend to find out who, because whoever they are, they're either your salvation or the king's trap, and I can no longer tell the difference between the things I serve and the things that serve it."

* * *

The storm broke harder then, as if Silas's control had slipped — and perhaps it had; the fire guttered flat, shadows lunging up the mural wall, and for one instant the painted storm-callers seemed to move, a procession stepping out of their own erasure.

"We need the ledger," Silas said.

Bram blinked. "What?"

"The master ledger. The harvest manifests. You said names — names are threads, and if threads can be *taken*, they can be—" Silas stopped themselves, pressed two fingers to their temple as if pressing a wound. "I don't know what I'm saying. I know it the way the stone knows things. Half-heard. My grandmother's voice in my blood telling me the Covenant can be reforged, and that reforging needs back what was stolen." They looked at Bram, and their eyes were the exact grey of the eyes on the wall, and Bram felt eleven years of grey coat settle over his shoulders like a shroud. "You served the machine. You know where its heart keeps its records. Get me the ledger, Corvale, and I will show you whether a stolen memory can be given back — and whether my sister can be pulled out of the king's blade before he swings it."

Outside, far off across the flooded lowlands, a horn sounded. Long, low, patient. Then another, answering it, closer than it should have been.

The hounds of Corvale's own command, running the scent he'd tried to drown in storm-water.

Bram turned toward the sound, and the taste of ash in his mouth turned suddenly to iron — the iron taste of a decision already made, arriving late to be spoken.

"We move tonight," he said. "And I know a place we can steal the ledger from. But you're not going to like where."

* * *

Silas raised an eyebrow — a small, exhausted, almost human gesture amid all the storm and scripture.

"The Hall of Questions," Bram said. "Under the Inquisition's own roof. Where they keep the files." He looked at Wren's imagined name, struck through in his mind's eye, and then at the girl clutching a singing stone and the exile wearing five generations of a stolen face.

"After all," he added, "it's time somebody robbed the machine that's been robbing us."
