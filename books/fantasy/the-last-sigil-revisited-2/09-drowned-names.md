## 9. Drowned Names

The sea at Greyhaven did not roar; it muttered. It spoke in the low, patient voice of something that had swallowed a great many things and intended to keep them.

Silas stood at the edge of the drowned quarter as the tide went out, watching the water peel back from rooftops like a bandage drawn from an old wound. Below the harbor's green surface lay the chapel of Saint Maren-of-the-Deeps, sunk three generations ago when the king's engineers broke the seawall to flood a smugglers' district — one small severing, Bram had called it, practice for the larger one. The chapel's spire still rose from the shallows at low tide, black with weed, its bell tower tilted like a man leaning to hear a secret.

"You're certain it's down there," Tessa said. She had her arms wrapped around herself against the wind, and the sigil-stone hung at her throat on its cord of braided hair — Silas's own hair, offered freely, because the stone answered to oathkeeper's blood and would not stay quiet otherwise. "It's been whispering since dawn. Not words. More like... singing underwater."

"Everything down there sings," Silas said. "The dead mostly. The sea keeps them tuned." They pulled off their boots and bound their trouser legs at the knee, and the wind coming off the water tasted of iron and old grief, and beneath it — faint, familiar, unbearable — salt and lavender. Their mother's scent. Which was impossible, which was memory lying to them again, and still their hands shook.

Bram crouched at the tideline, testing the water with two fingers like a farmer reading soil. "Two hours until it turns. After that the currents in the flooded streets run like rivers in a hurry. You'll drown or be dashed against a lintel, and I'll have let two gifted people die inside a week." He said it flatly, but his eyes stayed on Silas a moment too long. He was still learning how to look at them — not as a quarry, not as a case, but as a person whose magic smelled like storms and whose silences smelled like fear.

"Then I'd best be quick," Silas said.

Tessa caught their sleeve. "Take me instead. The stone answers me now. Maybe—"

"The stone answers *blood*," Silas said, gentler than they meant to. "It's been answering mine since before you were born. I just spent thirty years refusing to listen." They looked out at the dark water sliding over the rooftops, over the chapel door that stood open below like a mouth. "Time I stopped."

* * *

The descent was a falling through color. Green first, then a deeper green like bottle glass held to firelight, then a gray-blue where the light came down in slanted columns and moved with a patience no living thing possessed. Silas swam through a street where shopfronts gaped and eels poured from doorways like smoke given flesh. A child's shoe rocked gently on a sill. Barnacles had colonized a shrine to some minor god of luck, and the god's smiling face wore them like a pox.

The chapel doors were oak banded with bronze, and they had been forced open long ago — by looters, by priests fleeing with their relics, by the sea itself pressing its thumbs against the wood. Silas slipped through into the nave.

Inside, the water was clearer, lit by shafts from the collapsed roof, and the chapel hung suspended in those columns of light like something between a ruin and a cathedral of glass. Fish drifted through the transept in slow congregations. The altar had toppled, and before it, half-buried in silt and shells, lay a font of white marble carved in the shape of a wave frozen mid-curl.

The Tide Sigil rested in the wave's hollow, exactly where an oathkeeper would have left it: a disc of blue-gray stone no larger than a coin, veined with silver, turning slowly in the current as though breathing. Even at this distance Silas felt it the way one feels a struck bell through the soles of the feet. Around it the water was warmer by degrees. Small fish hovered near it without touching, the way people once gathered near their grandmother when she told stories.

Silas reached for it.

The moment their fingers closed around the stone, the chapel vanished.

* * *

They were standing on a hill above the sea, under a sky bruised purple with coming storm, and the grass was the impossible green of places that exist only in memory — too vivid, too still, every blade distinct. Silas knew at once that this was not a memory of theirs. The body they wore was wrong: shorter, broader in the hip, hands roughened by rope-work and net-mending. They looked down at those hands and felt the vertigo of wearing someone else's life like a coat cut for another shoulder.

*Mother*, they thought. *This is my mother's memory.*

And then the scene assembled itself around them, and understanding arrived like cold water closing over the head.

Below the hill, in a cove sheltered by black rocks, a ship waited at anchor — not a smuggler's lugger but a crown vessel, white sails trimmed with gold thread, and on its deck stood men in the gray-and-silver livery of the Inquisition, though their badges bore an older design, a design Bram had once described from the archives: the circle broken by a single vertical stroke. The mark of the First Accord. Before the Covenant. Before everything.

And walking up the hill toward the farmhouse — walking with the unhurried confidence of a man who has never once been told no — was a young man in traveling clothes, handsome in the way of portraits, with his brother's face.

*Aldric*, Silas thought, and then corrected themselves, because this was decades past. This was the father. King Osric, before the crown sat heavy enough to bend him, come himself to a storm-caller's farmstead at the edge of the map.

Their mother — young, sun-brown, a stranger's face that was somehow also the face Silas saw in every reflective surface and hated — met him in the dooryard. Silas could not hear the first exchange; memory, even implanted memory, is stingy with its sound. But they heard the rest, because the wind chose that moment to carry it, and because some sentences are spoken at a volume the world refuses to forget.

"—a severing of the shallow crossings only," the prince was saying. "The dangerous ones. The ones through which the Unseelie pour. No more changeling thefts, no more bargains that eat children. Your gift would make the working possible. The realm would owe your line a debt written in gold."

"And the deep crossings?" his mother asked. Her voice was low and even, the voice of a woman who had argued with tides and won. "The ones my grandmother swore to keep? The ones the fae courts keep on their side, as we keep ours?"

The prince smiled, and it was a fine smile, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "There need be no deep crossings if there are no courts to cross to. Think of it, mistress. An end to the whole weary arrangement. Mortal lands, mortal laws, mortal kings. Clean."

For a long moment their mother said nothing. The wind moved through the grass in long silver strokes. Somewhere below, gulls cried over the anchored ship, and Silas understood suddenly that the gulls were not gulls — that the air along the treeline shimmered very slightly, that watchers stood among the trees in shapes borrowed from birds, and that everyone in this scene knew it and pretended otherwise.

"I think," his mother said at last, "that you have never made a bargain in your life. Only takings dressed as bargains." She folded her hands, and Silas saw that they were trembling, and saw her will them still. "The Covenant is not a chain on us, Highness. It is a knot that holds two boats together in a storm. Cut it, and you do not free either boat. You only learn what the sea does to things that float alone."

"Your grandmother swore that oath to a court that has grown fat and strange," the prince said, and now the silk was gone from his voice, and underneath it was something flat and cold as a ledger. "Courts decay. Oaths outlive their purpose. I am offering you the chance to stand on the right side of history, storm-caller. Refuse, and you will find history moves regardless, and it does not look back for those it passes."

"Then it will pass me standing," she said.

Silas wanted to scream at her — *run, take the child, take the stone, run to the Otherworld while the crossings are still open* — but the memory was already moving, the way nightmares move, faster than pleading can follow. The prince turned and walked down the hill. The ship's boat came for him across the bright water. And that night — the memory skipped, the way damaged memories skip, a smear of dark — that night the soldiers came without livery, without banners, without anything but torches.

What followed, the memory recorded with terrible fidelity. Silas's mother running through the burning barley with an infant bundled against her chest — an infant with a shock of dark hair, an infant who did not cry, because storm-callers' children learn early that crying calls the weather. The crossing stones. The shallow ford where the veil between worlds lay thin as breath on glass, and beyond it the first pale towers of the Otherworld, and voices calling welcome in a language Silas's bones remembered though their mind did not.

She stopped at the waterline.

Stopped, and looked back at her burning farm, and at the child in her arms, and did the arithmetic that mothers are forced to do in the worst hours of the world. If she crossed, the child grew up fae-touched, marked, hunted by every crown agent from here to the capital — for the prince had made clear what refusal earned. If she stayed, she could hide them both in the human realms, bury the bloodline so deep beneath charms and small magics and silence that perhaps, perhaps, it would be forgotten.

She chose the hiding. She turned from the crossing. And as she turned, the memory showed Silas the thing that had been withheld until now, the thing the sigils kept like a splinter under the skin: the figure waiting on the far bank, tall and crowned in twilight, with a face like frost worked into silver — a face Silas had seen last week in the Ash Court, older now, wearier now, but unmistakable.

The fae sovereign who had loved their grandmother. Who had sworn, watching the storm-caller turn away, a sentence the memory gave whole and ringing:

*"One of her line will return to finish what she refused. I will wait as long as stone waits. But I will wait."*

* * *

Silas came back to themselves in the flooded nave with the Tide Sigil clenched in their fist hard enough to bruise, and their lungs burning, and their cheeks — absurdly, unforgivably — wet in a way that had nothing to do with the sea.

They kicked for the surface. Light widened above them, gray then silver then the ordinary dirty pearl of Greyhaven's morning, and they broke through gasping into air that tasted of tar and fish-offal and freedom, and Tessa's hands were under their arms hauling them onto the tilted roof of a customs house, and Bram was saying their name, saying it again, louder.

"I'm here," Silas managed. "I'm—" They laughed, and it came out cracked. "I'm furious. Entirely, magnificently furious."

Tessa knelt beside them, dripping, the sigil-stone flaring at her throat in sympathy with the one in Silas's hand. "What happened down there? You went under and the whole bay went still. Every wave. Like the sea was holding its breath."

"It was showing me something." Silas sat up. The Tide Sigil lay warm in their palm, and beside its silver veins they could see, now that they knew to look, faint darker traceries — script in the oathkeeper's hand, the same hand that had written the names Tessa heard whispered. "My mother's memory. Planted in the sigil, the way all of them are planted — the Covenant's insurance against forgetting. Except this one wasn't meant for any oathkeeper. It was meant for *me*. My grandmother hid it in the Tide Sigil because she knew someday one of us would dive for it."

Bram had gone very still in the way he did when an account was about to refuse to balance. "Showed you what?"

"The first Severing." Silas looked out at the harbor, at the masts swaying against the gray sky, at the city that had exiled them and the crown that now hunted them, and felt the anger arrive not as a wildfire but as something worse and better: a settled thing, a load-bearing thing, a beam laid across the soul. "The king's father tried it forty years ago. A partial severing — close the shallow crossings, starve the fae courts of traffic, see if the whole arrangement wouldn't wither on the vine. My mother refused to power the working. So they burned our farm and drove us into exile and called it justice for unlicensed magic." They turned the sigil over, and over, watching the light move in its veins. "Everything I have ever believed about why we were cast out — that we were too strong, too proud, too dangerous — it was all a story written by the men who burned the house. We weren't cast out for having magic. We were cast out for *refusing to spend it on their machine*."

Tessa made a small sound. When Silas glanced at her, her eyes were wet. "So your whole life —"

"My whole life was the cover story," Silas said. "Mine, and hers, and hers before her. Three generations paying interest on a debt we never owed." They stood, unsteady on the slick tiles, and the wind came off the water and pushed at them like a hand urging them forward. "But here is what the bastards didn't plan for. Here is what they never understand, because they think inheritance is property." They closed their fist around the Tide Sigil and felt its pulse answer the pulse of the stone at Tessa's throat, two hearts finding the same rhythm. "You cannot erase a lineage by scattering it. Every sigil they scattered, they scattered into *our keeping*. Every memory they buried, they buried in soil we were born from. They've spent forty years handing us the pieces of the weapon we'll use against them."

Bram rose slowly, and there was something new in his face — the guilt of the tunnel transmuted now into something harder, something with edges. "The ledger," he said quietly. "The master ledger in the Inquisitorium records the harvest sites. If the first Severing began this — if the king's house has been feeding on gifted blood and memory for two generations —"

"Then it's written down somewhere," Silas finished. "Ledgers always are. Kings trust paper more than they trust gods." They looked east, toward the capital, toward the spire whose silhouette they could not yet see but could feel the way a storm feels the coast it is walking toward. "Three sigils remain. Ember. Root. And the Last, whatever and wherever it is. And now I know precisely what we are carrying and precisely whom we carry it against."

Tessa scrambled up beside them, journal clutched to her chest, salt-stiff hair whipping across her face. "Then we go now? Today?"

"We go now." Silas took one last look at the drowned chapel, its spire breaking the surface behind them like a raised finger, like a promise kept by the sea on behalf of the dead. Somewhere below, in the dark and the green, their mother's ghost finished her long vigil at the waterline. *I'm going back to the crossing,* Silas told her silently. *Not to flee through it. To hold it open.* "Today. And Bram — when we reach the capital, I want that ledger. Whatever it costs."

Bram met their eyes, and nodded once, slow and grave, a man signing something in blood. "Whatever it costs," he agreed. "Though you should know — the last time I walked into the Inquisitorium, I left as one of them. There's no telling what I leave as this time."

"That," said Silas, feeling the storm rise gladly in their chest at last, with nowhere left to bury it, "is exactly what I'm counting on."
