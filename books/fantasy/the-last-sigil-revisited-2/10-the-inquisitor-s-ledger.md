## 10. The Inquisitor's Ledger

The archive beneath the Inquisitors' Hall smelled of tallow and cold iron, a smell that had once meant order to Bram Corvale. It still did, in the way that the smell of a slaughterhouse might mean supper to a man who had stopped asking where the meat came from.

He had been back in Vellharrow for six days. Six days of walking corridors he had once commanded, wearing the grey coat they had stripped from him in disgrace and which he now wore like a borrowed skin, too loose at the shoulders because grief had carved weight off him that no one had thought to measure. Six days of nodding to men who would have arrested him a season ago, and who now nodded back because the Purge needed every hand it could find, and hands did not ask questions.

Tonight his hand was going to ask questions.

The master ledger was kept in the Vault of Records, three floors down behind a door of blackened steel that answered only to an Inquisitor's seal-ring. Bram's ring had been confiscated when he was cashiered. But seals were wax and wax was patient, and he had spent two evenings in the Hall's own chapel — where no one prayed anymore except out of habit — pressing a cast of Deputy Inquisitor Hale's ring into a stub of grey candle-wax while the man himself slept off the evening's wine two doors away.

The forgery was ugly. It would hold for exactly as long as it took a bored archivist to glance at it and look away, which was all the time in the world that Bram required.

He descended the stair at the changing of the night-watch, when the lamps along the walls burned lowest and the archivist on duty — a narrow, watery-eyed man named Pell — sat hunched over a game of solitaire laid out with tarot cards he was not permitted to own. Bram set the wax-sealed letter of request on the desk, addressed in a fair imitation of Hale's cramped hand.

*Retrieval: Master Ledger, current volume. Reference for the Deputy's morning report.*

Pell looked at the seal. Looked at Bram. Bram watched the arithmetic move behind the man's eyes — the disgraced Corvale, back in favour or not, carrying a deputy's seal, and what business was it of a third-rate archivist anyway if the deputies wanted their own paperwork fetched.

"Sign the register," Pell said, and went back to his cards.

Bram signed *H. Vane*, a name belonging to a clerk who had died of fever two winters past and whose ink-fingered ghost could bear the blame. Then the black door swung inward under the false seal, and the cold breath of the vault came out to meet him, and he understood, in some animal way he could not have put into words, that he was walking into the belly of the thing that had eaten his sister.

* * *

The Master Ledger was not a book. He had imagined a book — a great tome chained to a lectern, its pages soft with use. What sat in the reading alcove, lit by a single shielded lamp, was a ledger bound in boards of pale wood and iron, as thick as a paving-stone, its spine stamped with the sunburst of the Crown and beneath it a word in the old formal script: *Harvests*.

Bram sat down before it and did not open it for a long moment. His hands were steady. That surprised him. He had expected them to shake; instead they hung at the ends of his arms like tools belonging to someone else, waiting to be picked up.

He opened the cover.

The first page was an index, ruled in columns so fine they seemed stitched rather than written. *District. Parish. Names Taken. Yield.* The word *yield* appeared without shame, without even a euphemism's blush. Beside each parish name stood a number, and beside each number a smaller notation in red: *processed, stored, allocated*. As though these were sacks of grain. As though these were winter stores against famine.

He turned pages. The lamp hummed. Somewhere above him, boots crossed the floor of the watch-room in slow, unhurried strides.

*Vellharrow, Low Ward: forty-one taken. Yield: strong. Two resistances, terminated.*

Forty-one. He had walked the Low Ward market not four days ago, buying bread from a baker whose daughter used to make little charms of knotted string — nothing, harmless things, knots tied for luck — and he had thought, standing there in the crowd, how quiet the ward seemed lately. How few children played in the alleys.

*Terminated.* He made himself keep reading. That was the discipline they had beaten into him at the Academy: read to the end of the entry, however foul. A record half-read is a lie you tell yourself.

He found the parish index for the western provinces and ran his finger down the column of names. *Marrowfen. Coldharbour Row. Ashmill. Thornbeck.* And then —

*Greywater Vale.*

His finger stopped.

There it was, in the same unlovely clerk's hand as everything else, the village where Maren had been born, where she had learned to swim in the mill-pond and to whistle through cupped hands and to braid crowns of meadowsweet that wilted by evening but never seemed to mind. The village she had fled to, after the last purge, after their father's arrest, after the family name became something whispered rather than spoken. She had gone there because it was small. Because it was nothing. Because the Crown's long arm had always reached for cities first, and she had believed — she had told him this, laughing, the last time he ever saw her, her hair wet from the pond, fourteen years old and certain of the world's large careless mercy — *they'll never bother with Greywater Vale, Bram. There's nothing here worth burning.*

The entry read:

*Greywater Vale: eleven taken. Yield: moderate. One resistance, terminated on site. Village emptied; structures repurposed.*

Eleven names followed, listed in a column. He read them the way a man reads a wound. An old woman. A ferryman. Twin boys, aged nine — aged nine, and the clerks had entered them without a tremor in the pen, *taken, yield noted, processed*.

And fourth from the top:

*Corvale, Maren. Age 19. Gift: hearth-binding (minor). Resistance: none recorded. Yield: high. Stored: Root Vault, Tier Three.*

Bram read the line seven times. The words did not change. That was the terrible thing about ink; it held still while everything else fell.

She had been nineteen. He had been twenty-two and already wearing the grey coat, already believing — God help him, he could still feel the shape of the belief, smooth and warm as a river stone — that the Purge was a mercy. That the gifted were sick with power they couldn't govern, and the Crown's harvest was a kind of quarantine, and those taken were *stored*, the word itself gentle as a blanket drawn up over a sleeping child. Stored. Kept safe. Until such time as —

Until such time as nothing. There had never been an until. They had fed her into a vault like coal into a furnace, and her hearth-binding — the small warm gift that let her make any room feel like home, that had made their bare barracks cell feel, once, for one impossible evening, like a kitchen — her gift had yielded *high*.

High. He put his hand flat on the page as though he could press the word flat, kill it, smother it under his palm. The paper was cool and indifferent and it went on holding the letters in their neat rows.

He had signed the intake rosters that autumn. He remembered now — the memory arrived with the specific cruelty of things remembered too late — he remembered signing a bundle of transfer orders, a hundred names, a hundred and six, and feeling nothing, because the names were only names, because the Academy taught you to see columns and not faces, and somewhere in that bundle had been *Corvale, Maren*, and his own pen had passed over her name or near it, close enough that his signature and her sentence had shared a page.

He did not weep. Some part of him observed this from a distance, clinically: the man does not weep; the man sits very still in a lamplit vault with his hand on the page like a man taking an oath he hasn't yet found words for.

What came instead was colder and more durable than tears.

* * *

It was Tessa's village that broke him open the rest of the way.

He had told himself he would look for one thing only — Maren — and then leave, take the ledger knowledge and run, get back across the city before the dawn watch changed. But the book was open, and his eyes were trained by ten years of service to sweep a page whole, and so he saw it, in the district listings for the eastern lowlands:

*Dunlow Fen: taken in full. Yield: exceptional for so small a parish. Note: source of sigil-stone recovered from local child, escaped. Pursuit ongoing.*

Tessa had called it Harrow's Edge. A fen-edge village of maybe sixty souls, eel-catchers and reed-cutters, a place with one chapel and two pubs and a maypole that leaned. She had talked about it the way children talk about small places — with fierce boredom and fiercer love tangled together. The woman who sold honey cakes. Her cousin Wynn who could skip stones eleven times. The smell of the reeds cut in autumn, sweet as hay.

*Taken in full.*

Sixty people. Not harvested from — emptied. The word *exceptional* sat next to it, and Bram understood with a lurch what the clerks had found exceptional: a whole community, root and branch, every grandmother's story and every child's knack, scooped up in a single net because a village that small keeps its gifts quietly, generation upon generation, until the whole place is steeped in inherited magic like tea gone strong in the pot.

And the note about the stone. *Recovered from local child, escaped.* They knew. They knew the stone had come from Dunlow Fen, that it had belonged to someone there — to Tessa, or to whoever gave it to her — and they had written her escape down as a clerical matter, *pursuit ongoing*, as though she were a misfiled document.

He thought of her that afternoon, sitting in the flooded culvert outside the city walls, turning the sigil-stone over in her fingers while Silas slept. She had said, in the offhand voice children use to carry enormous weights: "Everyone I know is either dead or gone quiet. I checked. I wrote to my aunt in Millbrook and the letter came back unopened." She had said it the way you'd report weather. And then she had asked whether storm-callers could make it rain hard enough to wash a road clean, because she liked clean roads, and Bram had thought her merely brave.

She was not merely brave. She was a survivor walking around inside the wreckage of her entire world, carrying the last piece of it in her pocket, and the Crown had entered her in a ledger as *escaped property*.

Something in Bram Corvale finished breaking and began, in the same motion, to set itself in a new shape. It did not feel like courage. It felt like a bone knitting — painful, necessary, the body insisting on a form it could actually stand in.

* * *

He copied what mattered. He could not take the ledger itself — it was chained, and its absence would be discovered within the hour, and the hunt would swallow all three of them before noon. So he worked. He tore leaves from the memorandum pad in his coat pocket and transcribed, in the smallest hand he could manage, the entries that counted: Greywater Vale, complete. Dunlow Fen, complete. The routing codes that showed where yields were *allocated* — and there was the word again, allocation, and following it, a distribution table that turned his stomach even colder than the rest: allocations to the Spire, to the ritual engines of the Great Severing, quantities measured in *units of memory-yield*, with projected totals that implied thousands upon thousands of the taken, stacked like cordwood in the dark beneath the old capital.

At the bottom of the final page he copied the one entry he had not been looking for and could not now unknow:

*Projected completion of Severing matrix: forty days. All field harvests to conclude by Embermoon. Remaining at-large assets: one (1) storm-caller lineage bearer, designation DRAVENMOOR. Priority: absolute.*

They had a designation for Silas. *Lineage bearer. Priority: absolute.* The king's machine had looked across the whole realm and seen, in all its ledgers, one item marked indispensable.

Bram folded the stolen pages into a packet the size of a prayer-book and slid it inside his coat, against his chest, where it lay like a second heart. Then he sat for a moment longer with the ledger open, looking at Maren's name.

"I'm sorry," he said aloud, very quietly, to the vault, to the ink, to whatever remained of a girl who could make any room feel like home. "I wore the coat. I signed the pages. I thought it was mercy." The lamp flame bent in some small movement of air, attentive or indifferent, impossible to say. "It stops with me. Whatever this costs — it stops being a machine, starting tonight."

He closed the ledger. The sound of the cover settling was soft and final, like earth on a coffin.

* * *

Getting out was easier than getting in; fear makes men punctual, and the watch changed on the hour to the minute. He returned the false-sealed requisition slip to Pell's desk with a nod, climbed the three flights, and walked out through the Hall's front doors into the Vellharrow night, where rain had begun to fall — thin, cold, ordinary rain, the honest kind, with no storm in it, because the storm-caller was miles away in the smugglers' quarter keeping his gift leashed like a dog that dreamed of wolves.

Bram walked the long way, through streets slick and lamplit, letting the rain wash his face so that anyone watching would see only a tired man coming off shift. At the mouth of Tanner's Row he paused beneath the dripping sign of the cooper's shop and checked his shadow, his trail, the windows overhead. Nothing moved but a cat and the rain.

The safehouse — a disused rope-walk above the tannery, rented under a dead man's name — showed one light at the shuttered window. He knocked the pattern they had agreed: twice, pause, once. Silas opened the door, took one look at him, and stepped aside without a word. The storm-caller missed nothing; Bram had learned that much on the road. Those grey eyes went straight to the shape of the packet beneath his coat the way a hawk goes to a vole in grass.

Tessa sat cross-legged on the floor amid their scant gear, mending a strap of her satchel by candlelight, the sigil-stone resting in her lap like a sleeping bird. She looked up. "You've got it," she said. Not a question. Children of emptied villages, Bram thought, learn to read faces early; hope is too expensive to spend unwarned.

"I've got it." His voice came out lower than he intended, scraped raw by the vault's cold. He knelt, unfolded the packet, spread the pages on the floorboards between the candle and the stone. "Names. Numbers. Where they're taking everyone, and where they're sending what they take. And—" His throat closed. He made himself finish, because they deserved the truth delivered whole, not in fragments they'd have to pry out of him later. "I found my sister. Maren. Greywater Vale, nineteen years old. She's been in their vault for three years. And Tessa—"

"Don't," Tessa said. Very quietly. Her hands had gone still on the strap.

"You should hear it from me and not from the page."

She looked at the transcription. He watched her eyes travel the ruled lines, watch her lips move faintly over the familiar names — the honey-cake woman, the ferryman, Wynn — watch her arrive at the note at the bottom, *taken in full, village emptied,* and watch her understand, in one long terrible heartbeat, that the letter to her aunt had come back unopened because there was no aunt anymore to open it.

She didn't cry either. That was the worst thing, Bram thought — that neither of them cried, that the age had taught even its children to grieve in silence, efficiently, like clerks. She just reached out and touched the list of names with one fingertip, the way he had touched Maren's line, and then she drew a slow breath and said, in a voice with iron poured into its foundation: "Tell us where the vault is."

"Under the old capital. Tier Three." Silas had picked up the page bearing the *priority: absolute* notation and was reading it with an expression Bram had seen once before, on the quay at Greyhaven, in the half-second before the storm came down. "And they've marked me *absolute*," the storm-caller said softly. "Forty days to the Severing. They mean to finish it by Embermoon."

"Then we have forty days," Bram said. He heard his own voice and barely recognized it — stripped of the Academy's measured cadences, stripped of the grey coat's institutional calm, down to something older and harder underneath, something with his sister's hearth-warmth burned out of it and left only the ember. "I served this machine for ten years. I know its roads, its garrisons, its rot. Every weakness in it, I helped build." He gathered the pages, folded them, and pressed them back against his chest. "We're going to the capital. We're going to empty their vault the way they emptied our villages — completely. And then we are going to burn the ledger."

Outside, the rain thickened, drumming on the tannery roof, and far to the east — though none of them could have known it yet, and none of them would forget it after — the first thunder of the season rolled up from the fens, like something buried answering its name.
