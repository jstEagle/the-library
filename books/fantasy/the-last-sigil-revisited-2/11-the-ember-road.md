## 11. The Ember Road

The road inland was called the Ember Road because of what had happened to the trees.

Tessa saw them first from the crest of a long hill, where the lowlands fell away behind them like a wet gray cloak and the country ahead rose in slow folds toward the mountains. The trees lining the road were not burned — that was the wrong word, she decided, though it took her half a mile of walking to find the right one. They were *emptied*. Charcoal without flame. Bark gone matte and lightless, branches held up in attitudes of reaching, as if every one of them had been mid-gesture when something passed through and drank whatever gesture was made of. Their shadows lay strange on the road too — thin, reluctant, more suggestion than darkness.

"Do they bother you?" Silas asked quietly.

They had all learned to watch for what bothered Tessa now. Since Greyhaven, since the stone had begun whispering names into the cup of her hands, she had become the party's instrument — the one who heard things before they arrived, felt things before they could be seen. It should have made her proud. Mostly it made her feel like a door left unlatched in a house full of weather.

"They're not dead," she said. "That's what bothers me."

Bram walked with his hand near his sword, which he'd done since the trees started. He did that a great deal lately. "Dead is simpler," he said. "I preferred dead."

Silas said nothing at all, but Tessa noticed they kept glancing back the way they'd come, toward Vellharrow, toward everything burning behind them that wasn't smoke. The sigil-stone rode against Tessa's chest on its cord, warm as a sleeping bird, and when she pressed her palm over it she could feel it murmuring — not words yet, just the shape of words, the way you feel someone talking through a wall.

Three days out from Greyhaven. Three days since Silas's storm had flattened a quay and a bounty-hunter's face along with it. Three days since Tessa had stood in the wreckage of a smugglers' market and understood, with a giddy horror she couldn't put down, that she belonged to these people now — to this frightened, furious, half-broken family assembled by accident — and that belonging meant the hunters would come for her too.

She hadn't known how literally that would prove true.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of an emptied orchard, because there was no better shelter and no worse, and because Bram said the Hollowed avoided open ground. He knew about the Hollowed. That was the worst part. He told them around the small fire Silas permitted, cupping his hands as if warming them even though the fire gave little heat, and his voice was the flat voice he used when the truth was too heavy to carry any other way.

"The Purge doesn't kill the gifted," he said. "You know that part. It harvests them. Memory, gift, name — drawn off like marrow. But nothing is ever simply destroyed." He looked into the fire. "What's left walks."

Tessa's skin crawled. "Walks where?"

"Wherever they're sent. The Inquisition calls them retrievers. The soldiers call them the quiet ones. I called them—" He stopped. Started again. "I escorted six of them once, early in my service. I thought they were prisoners under escort. They don't speak. They don't eat, or if they eat, no one sees them do it. They move like people remembering how bodies work, one motion at a time." His jaw worked. "There was a woman among them who hummed. All night, every night we traveled. One tune. I asked her about it and she looked at me—" His hands tightened around each other. "She looked at me like a window looks at you. And three weeks later, reading a confiscated ledger, I found a description of a larkspur-song that mothers in the Fenmarch sing their daughters. My mother sang it to my sister."

The fire cracked. Somewhere off in the emptied orchard, a branch settled with a sound like a sigh.

"So they hunt us," Silas said, "with ourselves."

"With what's left after they take everything worth keeping." Bram finally looked up, and Tessa saw that he was looking at her — or past her, at some girl-shaped absence. "If the Hollowed come, don't try to fight them. Run. Don't let them touch you. Touch is how they take."

"And if they catch us?" Tessa asked, though she already suspected the answer, the way you suspect the fall before your foot finds the loose stair.

"Then somewhere down the Ember Road," Bram said gently, terribly, "there will be one more thing walking."

Tessa didn't sleep much after that. She sat with the stone against her sternum and listened to it whisper, and for the first time she tried to make out the words instead of letting them wash over her. *Grandmother*, Silas had said the voice might be — the erased oathkeeper, the first thread of a lineage unraveled on purpose. Tessa closed her eyes and leaned into the murmur until individual syllables surfaced like fish rising: *ember*. Always ember. The stone had been saying *ember* for days.

"Silas," she whispered across the dead campfire. "The stone keeps saying a word. Ember."

Silas lifted their head, eyes catching the last coals' glow. "Say it again."

"Ember."

"Road," Silas breathed. "The Ember Road. There's a sigil here, Tessa. Under our feet the whole time. We've been walking toward it since Greyhaven." A pause, and then, softer, with something almost like wonder: "It's been leading *us*."

Tessa looked out at the charcoal trees standing sentinel in the dark, all those empty arms raised toward a sky with no stars willing to show, and felt the warmth of the stone pulse once against her chest — patient, certain, utterly indifferent to her fear.

Something out in the orchard answered it.

Not a sound exactly. More a subtraction of sound — a patch of night where the cricket-noise simply stopped, and then another patch, closer, and another, a spreading silence moving through the trees the way wind moves through wheat.

"Bram," Tessa whispered. "Bram, wake up—"

He was already awake. He was always already awake now. His sword came out of its sheath without a whisper, and his other hand closed on Silas's shoulder, and the three of them stood together in the dark listening to the silence walk.

* * *

They came out of the orchard at moonrise.

There were five of them. Tessa counted because counting was something her mind did when terror needed a job: five shapes detaching from the black trunks, moving with that terrible one-motion-at-a-time deliberation Bram had described, like puppets whose puppeteer had died but whose strings still remembered him. The moon came out from behind cloud as if to apologize and showed her their faces.

She had expected emptiness to look like nothing. It didn't. It looked like *almost* — faces that were almost human, features arranged correctly but lit from nowhere, eyes that reflected the moonlight without holding any of it. They wore the gray travel-cloaks of pilgrims, of peddlers, of ordinary people on ordinary roads, and that was the cruelest thing, that whoever dressed them had dressed them as travelers, as if the realm itself were trying to pretend nothing had been taken from anyone.

"Hollowed," Bram said, stepping in front of them both. "Five. Spread wide. They'll try to flank." His voice was steady; his sword-hand was not. "Run for the road. Don't stop. Don't look back."

"I'm not leaving you," Silas said.

"You're not *staying*," Bram corrected, and then the first Hollowed reached him and there was no more time for talk.

Tessa ran. She hated herself for running and ran anyway, boots hammering the dead road, the stone bouncing against her chest, and behind her she heard the awful sound of Bram's blade meeting something that didn't cry out — steel ringing on flesh that accepted the cut without comment, the way a fencepost accepts an axe. She heard Silas shout, heard thunder gather where no cloud was, smelled ozone bloom sharp and sudden in air that had tasted only of ash for days.

And then a hand closed on her arm.

It didn't hurt. That was the horror of it — it didn't hurt at all. The grip was cool and gentle, almost courteous, and Tessa spun and found herself face to face with the humming woman, or what had been the humming woman, gray-faced and window-eyed, lips parting around no breath.

The world went white and silent.

No — not white. *Drained*. Color left things first. Then warmth. Then—

Tessa felt it begin to pull, and she understood in one blazing instant what harvest meant. It wasn't theft the way thieves stole, grabbing and running. It was *unraveling* — the Hollowed's touch finding the loose thread of her and drawing it outward, and with the thread came everything strung upon it: her mother's kitchen, the smell of tallow and rain-soaked wool, the name her mother used for her when she was sleepy, the shape of the door of the house she'd run away from, the reason she'd run, the face of the person she'd run from, the—

*No.*

She didn't say it aloud. She didn't have to. Something in her rose up that had never risen before, untrained and enormous and terrified, the way floodwater rises through floorboards — not skill, nothing so graceful as skill, just pressure, pure and screaming. Her gift flared.

The Hollowed flew backward.

Not pushed — *rejected*. Thrown twenty feet into a charcoal tree that powdered to ash on impact, and Tessa stood alone on the Ember Road with her hands outstretched and light coming off her skin in ragged sheets, wild silver-white light that guttered and spat like a signal fire, and every remaining Hollowed on the road stopped.

All four turned toward her at once. Window-eyes fixed. And Tessa felt her own power meet theirs across the distance and recognized, with a sickness that went all the way down into her bones, what she was looking at.

They were gifted. They had been gifted, once — mages, hedge-witches, storm-callers maybe, hearth-speakers, people whose gifts had been pulled out through their skin and poured into the king's machines. What remained of them still *reached* toward magic the way a severed hand remembers grasping. And her light, blazing wild and free, hurt them. She could see it hurting them — see the almost-faces flinching, the almost-hands rising as if against a sun. Her gift burned them because they had none, and they had none because men like Bram had taken it, and if she kept burning she could drive them off, she could destroy them, she could—

One of them opened its mouth. No sound came out. But its mouth shaped something, shaped it slowly, deliberately, one motion at a time, and Tessa read the shape of it the way you read lips across a crowded room:

*Help.*

Or maybe it was only her own guilt shaping the dark. She couldn't know. She would never be able to know, and that was the wound, that was the scar being written right then into the soft tissue of who she was going to be: that she would spend the rest of her life unable to learn whether the Hollowed suffered, whether anything of them survived beneath the emptiness, whether she had just driven off people or only the shells people leave behind.

Her light surged. The Hollowed broke — scattered back into the orchard like crows off a carcass, folding themselves between the dead trunks, and the silence of them receded patch by patch until the crickets dared resume.

Tessa's knees hit the road. The light went out all at once, like a snuffed candle, and the night rushed back in cold and ordinary, and she knelt there shaking, pressing the sigil-stone against her mouth like a child with a comfort blanket, tasting ash and lightning on her own lips.

"Tessa." Silas dropped beside her, hands hovering, afraid to touch. Behind them Bram limped into view, coat torn, a dark line of blood down one sleeve, alive. "Tessa, look at me. Are you taken? Did they touch you long? Look at me."

"I'm here." Her voice came out sanded flat. "I'm here. I think I'm here." She looked up at Silas, and her eyes were streaming without her permission. "Silas, I *felt* it. The pulling. I felt what they do, I felt my whole life start to come loose like—" She swallowed. "And then I threw her. I threw that poor woman into a tree and she *shattered*, and I don't know if I killed someone or saved myself or both, and I don't know which is worse, and Silas, when it flared — my gift — for one second, one second, I felt what they feel. The reaching. The *hunger*. Like the magic wanted to pour out of me forever and never stop and never care what it burned."

Silas took her face in both hands then, gently, storm-gray eyes steady in a way Tessa had never seen them — steadier than the night market, steadier than Greyhaven, steadier than the flooded roads. This was the face of someone who had lived decades with exactly this fear and survived it, mostly.

"Listen to me," Silas said. "The hunger isn't yours. It's what they built the hunger into — the Crown, the Purge, the whole machine. They want the gifted to be afraid of our own gifts. Fear makes us easy to herd." Their thumb brushed Tessa's cheek, wiping ash. "Your gift flared to save your life and it stopped the moment your life was safe. Do you understand? You *stopped*. The Hollowed can't stop. That's the difference. That's the whole difference, and it's everything."

"But I liked it." The confession tore out of her, small and vile. "For one second. While it was pouring out of me. I felt like I could do *anything*, and I loved it, and loving it is how they start, isn't it? Isn't that how it starts?"

Silas was quiet a long moment. Firelight — real firelight, Bram had gotten the coals breathing again — moved over their face.

"Yes," Silas said at last. "That is how it starts. For me it was a storm when I was nine years younger than you are now, and I leveled my master's barn and laughed until I cried." They sat down in the ash of the road beside her, shoulder to shoulder. "The love doesn't go away, Tessa. Anyone who tells you otherwise is lying or selling something. You carry it. You carry the love and the fear in the same hand, and you choose, every day, what to do with the hand. That's all mastery is. That's all the Covenant ever was, before they broke it — a promise that no one carries it alone."

Tessa looked down at the stone in her lap. Its warmth pulsed, slow, patient. And inside her, where the flare had torn through, she could feel a new tenderness — raw tissue, a place that would heal crooked no matter how carefully it healed. She would never again reach for her gift without hearing that mouth shape its silent word. She would never again see an empty doorway without checking it twice.

But she was also still here. Still herself. Still carrying her whole life, every thread of it, and knowing now — viscerally, in her marrow — exactly how much a life weighed.

Bram lowered himself heavily onto her other side, wincing, and for a while none of them spoke. The orchard of emptied trees stood around them like a congregation, and far off toward the mountains the sky had begun to bruise toward dawn.

It was Bram who finally broke the quiet, and his voice had changed — stripped of the flat institutional calm, down to something older and harder.

"That woman," he said. "The one who hummed. She's the sixth escort I lost track of, eleven years ago. I reported her as escaped." He stared into the coals. "She wasn't escaped. She was stationed. On this road. Which means the Inquisition has been placing retrievers along the Ember Road for over a decade — guarding it, watching it." He turned to look at Silas, and Tessa watched the old guilt in his face transmute, grain by grain, into defiance. "Guarding it *against something*. Or waiting for someone. If the Hollowed hold this road, then the Ember Sigil they're sitting on isn't forgotten. It's *kept*. And whatever vault holds it, the Crown knows about it — and will be racing us there the moment those five report back. If they report."

Silas rose, pulling Tessa gently up with them, and looked east toward the mountains where the sky was bleeding its first iron light.

"Then we don't sleep again," Silas said. "We walk. Tonight, if we must." A pause, and the ghost of the old market-charmer's smile, gone almost as soon as it came. "The stone says *ember*, Tessa. Let's go find the fire."

They shouldered their packs and took the road east, three small figures on a dead bright line running toward the roots of the mountains — and behind them, deep in the orchard, in a place the dawn had not yet reached, something that had once been a woman knelt in the ash where Tessa's light had thrown it, and slowly, one motion at a time, began to follow.
