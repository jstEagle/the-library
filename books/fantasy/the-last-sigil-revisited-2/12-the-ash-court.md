## 12. The Ash Court

The crossing was not a door so much as a wound.

Silas felt it before they saw it — a thinness in the air above the moor, like the skin of water stretched over something deeper. The heather there had died in a perfect circle forty paces wide, and within that circle the grass grew silver, and frost clung to it though no breath of winter had touched the lowlands in a season.

"It's bleeding out," Tessa whispered. She held the sigil-stone cupped in both palms like a candle she was afraid would gutter. Since Greyhaven she had not let it far from her skin, and Silas had stopped pretending not to notice how her thumb moved over its facets — the same gesture, they realized with a jolt, that their grandmother must have used, generations before either of them existed to remember it. "The stone says the veil here is thinner than anywhere left. It says—" Tessa paused, and her voice went small. "It says it hurts."

Bram stood at the circle's edge with his hand resting on his sword hilt, not gripping it, just touching it, the way a man touches a thing he has decided not to trust himself with. He had been quiet since the ledger. Quiet in the way of someone carrying a name — *Maren Corvale* — like a stone swallowed whole, sitting somewhere behind his sternum where it could never be coughed up or set down.

"Once we cross," Bram said, "there's no pretending anymore. No charm-seller's stall, no drainage tunnel, no roads back. The fae will see what we are. All three of us." He looked at Silas. "And they have every reason to hate what we are."

"I know," Silas said.

"They'll want a price."

"They always do."

"And if they ask for something you can't give?"

Silas looked at the silver grass, at the frost that was not cold but only a memory of cold, worn thin by centuries of being remembered from the wrong side. Somewhere beyond it was the Otherworld — the realm their grandmother had sworn an oath to keep bound and whole, the realm the king now meant to amputate from mortal memory entirely.

"Then I'll pay what I can afford," Silas said, "and lie about the rest."

Tessa laughed — a startled, cracked sound — and even Bram's mouth twitched, and for one moment the three of them stood together on the dying edge of two worlds almost easy in each other's company. Then Silas stepped into the circle, and the moment ended.

Crossing felt like passing through a wall of standing water that did not wet them. Sound came apart first: Bram's boots on the heather, Tessa's breath, the distant complaint of crows — all of it stretched and thinned into threads and then woven into something else, something older, a sound like wind through a hall too large for any fire to warm. Light changed next. The grey morning became a light without a source, amber and dim, falling as though through stained glass in a cathedral that had burned down long ago and whose glass still remembered hanging.

Then they were through, and Silas fell to one knee on ash.

* * *

It had not always been ash. That much the ground itself confessed, the way a corpse confesses a life. Beneath the grey film, the soil still held the shapes of roots — vast ones, tree-roots, avenues of them running away toward a horizon where towers rose like the ribs of some enormous drowned thing. The trees themselves were gone. Not felled: gone, taken, as though someone had unmade the idea of them and left only the holes they had occupied.

"Gods below," Bram breathed.

"Don't invoke them here," Silas said quietly. "They may be listening, and they may be in a mood to agree with you."

The road to the court was paved once in white shell and river-stone; now the shells were grey powder and the stones showed through like bones through wasted flesh. They walked it because there was nothing else to walk toward. The sigil-stone pulsed faintly against Tessa's chest — slow, labored, like a heart climbing a hill — and when Silas asked her what it said, she shook her head.

"It's not saying anything. It's listening." Her eyes were wet. "Everything here is listening. Can't you feel it? Like the whole country turned its ear toward us the second we crossed."

Silas could feel it. It was the opposite of Vellharrow, where magic had to be buried so deep it curdled into shame. Here magic lay everywhere, open and starving — in the dead orchards where fruit-trees still blossomed out of season and bore ash instead of apples; in the streams that ran clear but tasted of nothing, water without weather in it; in the air itself, which should have smelled of rain and green growth and honey and instead smelled of nothing at all, a clean blank nothing, like a page scraped for rewriting.

They made camp that night — if night it was; the amber light simply dimmed, sourceless, like a lamp being carried further down a corridor — in the shell of a waystation built for travelers between realms. Its hearth was large enough to roast an ox, and cold enough that Tessa's small fire-magic, coaxed nervously from her cupped hands, seemed less like warmth than like an apology.

"The murals in the abbey," Bram said, after a long silence. "The Covenant. What exactly did your people promise them?"

"That the realms would stay bound. That magic would flow both ways — mortal need and fae wonder, traded fairly, oath-bound, generation to generation." Silas fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "And the fae promised the same in return. Memory for memory. The mortals kept the Otherworld alive by believing in it. The Otherworld kept the mortals' magic alive by feeding it."

"And now the king is cutting the artery."

"Now the king is cutting the artery," Silas agreed. "And everything downstream is dying."

Tessa looked up sharply. "Then we're not coming here to ask for help. We're coming to ask them to help save themselves."

"We're coming to ask them to trust us," Silas corrected gently. "Which is harder. Help can be compelled. Trust can't."

The fire crackled. In the silence after, from far off across the ash plains, came a sound none of them could name and all of them flinched from: a long, high note like a bell struck underwater, mournful and vast, rising and fading and rising again.

"The court is calling assembly," Silas said. "We've been noticed."

* * *

The Ash Court sat in what had clearly once been a garden.

That was the horror of it, Silas thought as they were led — escorted, though the word felt too kind — up a processional avenue of petrified hedges. This had been a place of deliberate beauty. You could read it in the geometry beneath the ruin: terraces cut for roses, pools dug to hold lilies, arbors bent to frame views of the towers. Now every pool held grey dust, every hedge was mineral and dead, and the views framed nothing but more ash. Beauty starved in place. It was worse than ugliness. Ugliness could be endured. This was grief given architecture.

The courtiers came out to meet them, and Silas made the mistake of looking directly at the first of them.

The fae were fading. Everyone knew the stories said so, but knowing and seeing were different things. These were beings who had once been too vivid for mortal eyes to hold — colors that didn't exist east of the veil, faces that rearranged themselves slightly with every blink. Now they were washed-out, translucent at the edges, like paintings left in a window for a century. Their beauty remained, but it remained the way a melody remains in a room after the singer has gone. One of them, tall and crowned in antlers that had gone grey as driftwood, turned eyes upon Silas that had once perhaps been spring-green and were now the color of old ice.

"You bring storm," it said. Its voice was beautiful the way ruins are beautiful. "We smell it on you. Rain that hasn't fallen yet. How dare you carry weather into a land that has none."

Silas bowed. Kept the bow deep and slow, though every instinct screamed to straighten, to call lightning, to meet power with power. *Diplomacy,* they told themselves, *is just fighting with the violence taken out and the humiliation left in.*

"High ones of the Ash Court. I am Silas Dravenmoor, of the line of the oathkeepers. I come under the Covenant—"

"There is no Covenant." A second voice, lower, from a figure whose face was a smooth oval with features that surfaced and submerged like things under water. Murmurs ran along the ranks of the court like wind through wheat. "There is a memory of a Covenant. There is a grave-marker of a Covenant. Your grandmother swore it, storm-caller, and your grandmother is ash, and her sigils are scattered, and her heirs forgot her name until they needed her. Do not speak oaths to us. Oaths are what mortals break."

"We haven't broken it," Tessa burst out. "The king is breaking it. We've come to stop him."

Every eye — and some things that were not quite eyes — turned to her. She stood her ground, chin up, the sigil-stone glowing at her throat, and Silas felt something twist with pride and terror in equal measure.

"A child," the antlered one said softly. "A mortal child comes into our dying court and tells the fae what our own hearts already scream." It descended the terrace steps, each footfall leaving a print of silver frost in the ash. It stopped close enough that Silas could see the fine cracking of its skin, like glaze on ancient porcelain. "Yes. The king severs. And who gave the king his crown? Who taught him to fear us, to tax wonder, to burn the gifted? Mortals did. Century after century, mortals pulled away first. The severing began long before your king took up his knife. He is only finishing what your whole kind started."

Silas held its gaze. "That isn't fair."

"No," the fae agreed. "It isn't. Fairness died here some years ago. We buried it under the third terrace." A ripple passed through the assembled court that might have been laughter, or weeping; with the faded ones, Silas had learned, they sounded the same. "You crossed the veil, oathkeeper's heir. Crossing is petitioning. Petitioning has a price. You knew this before you came — your kind always knows, and always comes anyway, and always calls the price extortion afterward."

"What price?" Bram asked. His voice was flat and careful, a man defusing something.

"One true memory," the antlered one said. "Freely given. Not stolen, not sold, not traded under duress. Given. A memory is a seed, storm-caller. Plant one true thing of yours in our soil and perhaps something grows here again. Perhaps not. Perhaps we are past growing, and your gift will only feed us another season of dying. But the price stands regardless. That is the cruelty and the justice of the old law: payment does not depend on outcome."

The court went silent. Even the wind, which had been keening faintly through the petrified hedges, seemed to hold its breath.

Silas looked at Tessa. Tessa looked back, and her face said everything her voice didn't: *don't, don't, we'll find another way, please.* Beside her, Bram had gone very still, the stillness of a man watching a debt come due that he'd hoped to die before owing.

One true memory. Freely given.

Silas turned inward and walked the corridors of their own life, and found how few rooms there were, and how many doors already bricked shut — grandmother's name, mother's exile, half a childhood sealed behind the king's harvesters. What remained was small. A handful of kindnesses. A handful of cruelties. Rain, always rain.

There was one memory that had never hurt them. One that had stayed warm through every exile, every hiding, every year of selling lies in a godless city.

Their seventh summer. A roof of tin over a grandmother's kitchen — no; that memory was gone, harvested, they couldn't even picture the kitchen anymore, only the sound the rain had made on whatever roof had sheltered it. Tin, or slate, or straw. The sound itself was all that survived, and it survived perfectly: rain arriving in waves, drumming, softening, drumming again, and underneath it a voice humming, wordless, unhurried, keeping time with the weather. Whoever had hummed. Whatever kitchen. The sound alone, whole and untouched, the last unbroken thing.

If I give that away, Silas thought, I will never again know what rain sounds like when it means *home.*

"If I give this," Silas said aloud, and their voice came out steadier than they deserved, "I want something named in return. Not vague favor. A name. Tell me what you will do, High Ones, if we reforge the Covenant. Swear it before your court."

The antlered one regarded them for a long moment. Around it, the faded courtiers leaned in, and for an instant — one flickering instant — Silas saw what they had been: colors blazing, faces shifting like weather, a beauty that made mortal eyes ache. Then it was gone, and they were porcelain and driftwood again.

"You drive a bargain at the door of the dying," the fae said. "Good. Your grandmother would have done the same." It raised one grey-antlered head, and its voice rang out across the ash gardens, and every courtier answered it in a single voice like a bell struck underwater:

"When the Covenant stands reforged, the Ash Court swears: the veil opens both ways. Magic flows under accord, not purge. No mortal child shall be harvested for the crime of inheritance. And the Otherworld sends its strength to the storm-caller at the reforging — root and ember and tide, all that remains of us, gathered into their hands."

The echo faded. The antlered one extended a hand — long-fingered, translucent, trembling faintly, whether with age or eagerness Silas could not tell.

"The Ember Sigil sleeps in our deepest vault, storm-caller. It opened once for your grandmother's memories. It will open for yours. But the vault takes its toll separately from ours." The fae smiled, and the smile was kind, which was somehow worse. "The price is one true memory. Choose well. Or choose quickly. Those, in the end, are the only choices anyone gets."

Silas closed their eyes.

Rain on a roof. A voice humming. Home, reduced to sound.

"I give it," they said, and reached out, and took the fading hand.

* * *

The taking was gentle, which was the cruelest part.

No pain. Just a coolness behind the eyes, like a window opening in a warm room, and then the memory lifting free — Silas felt it go the way one feels a tooth leave the gum: relief and loss in the same instant, an absence shaped exactly like what had filled it.

They staggered. Bram caught their elbow; Tessa caught their sleeve, and the sigil-stone flared bright against her chest, drinking the released memory as it passed — Silas could see it, actually see it, a shimmer of sound and warmth spiraling into the stone's facets, joining the whisper of their grandmother's voice already living there.

And the court — the court *bloomed.*

Not everywhere. Not a miracle. But where Silas knelt on the ash, color spread outward in a slow ring: the ash greening at its edges, a single shoot pushing up through the crust, unfurling one leaf, two, five, trembling in air that suddenly — faintly, briefly — smelled of rain.

The courtiers made a sound. All of them, at once. Not the bell-underwater mourning-note from the road. Something higher, broken, astonished. Grief and hope sound the same, Silas thought dizzily, when a thing has forgotten both.

"Enough." The antlered one's voice was rough. "Enough, before we spend it all on one leaf." It gestured, and a door appeared where there had been a hedge — not appeared, *was revealed*, a doorway of black wood banded in silver, older than everything around it. "The vault, storm-caller. Your memory is planted at its threshold. Go and wake the Ember Sigil — and know that what wakes it will demand more than you have already paid. Vaults keep accounts too."

Tessa helped Silas stand. Her face was streaked and furious and fierce. "What did you give them?" she demanded. "Tell me. Right now, before you forget you ever had it. Tell me everything."

And because Silas could no longer answer — because the room where that memory lived was swept bare, and when they reached for *rain on the roof* they found only a shape of absence, a hole in the shape of a sound — Tessa grabbed Silas's hands and said, quieter, shaking:

"Then tell me what's left. Everything that's left. Every story, every name, every stupid little thing. Starting tonight. Because if the courts and kings and vaults are going to take pieces of you piece by piece, then somebody in this world is going to keep the whole of you written down. Do you understand me? Somebody is going to remember you *on purpose.*"

Silas looked at her — this runaway thief, this child of an emptied village, this stranger of three weeks' acquaintance — and felt something shift in their chest that had nothing to do with magic.

"Yes," Silas said. "I understand you."

Behind them, the black door stood open on darkness, and from far down inside the vault, something ancient heard the planting of a memory in its threshold soil and turned, slow as a season, toward the light.

The Ember Sigil was waking.

And it was calling Silas by name.
