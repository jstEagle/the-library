## 13. A Memory of Rain

The Ember Sigil's vault did not look like a vault. It looked like a door in the side of a mountain that had decided, long ago, to stop being a door.

Tessa stood at the edge of the fae road where it frayed into ordinary stone, watching Bram hold a torch against a cliff face that offered no crack, no seam, no handle — only a faint warmth radiating off the rock, like standing near an oven. The Otherworld behind them had thinned to almost nothing: grey meadows where the grass grew in slow motion, a sky the color of old pewter, and everywhere that terrible quiet, as if the whole realm were holding its breath and forgetting, breath by breath, why.

"It's warm," Bram said. He pressed his gloved palm flat against the stone and pulled it back quickly, flexing his fingers. "Warm enough to burn."

"That's how you know it's listening," Silas said.

They had crossed into the Ember Road three days ago, after leaving the Ash Court with one fewer memory than they'd arrived with. Tessa still thought about it at night — the way Silas had stood before the fading throne and said, *I give you rain on my grandmother's roof,* and how the court had taken it, drinking the words out of the air, and how Silas had come away from that throne looking exactly the same and somehow smaller, like a house with one window bricked over.

Except — no. That wasn't right. Tessa had caught herself doing this for days now: reaching for the details of Silas's childhood the way her tongue reached for a missing tooth. But she hadn't lost anything. It was Silas who couldn't remember anymore. It was Silas who had stood outside the Ash Court afterward, staring at their own hands, saying, *There was a roof. I know there was a roof. I can feel the shape of knowing it and there's nothing inside the shape.*

So Tessa had bought a journal in the last mortal village before the border — three coppers from a woman who also sold candles blessed against fae theft — and had begun, quietly, without asking anyone's permission, to write everything down.

She wrote now by torchlight, hunched against the warm rock while Bram paced and Silas knelt before the blank stone face of the vault, palms open, waiting.

*Silas says the vault listens. Silas says the sigils don't want power, they want payment.* Her pen scratched. *Payment is always memory here. Everything in the Otherworld runs on remembering.*

"What does it want?" Bram asked, echoing her thought, because he'd been doing that too lately — finishing Silas's sentences, or Tessa's, as if the three of them were slowly braiding into one rope.

"An oathkeeper's proof," Silas said. Their voice was steady. Tessa had learned to distrust that steadiness; it was the same voice Silas used when selling charms in Vellharrow, pleasant and closed, a lid on a pot that was boiling underneath. "The Tide Sigil took salt water and a drowned name. The Root will take earth and bloodline. This one—" they touched the stone, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, "—this one burns things clean. It wants to be given something worth keeping. Something the keeper would rather die than lose."

"And you know what that is," Bram said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know what it asks." Silas sat back on their heels. In the torchlight their face was all planes and shadow, older than it had been in the night market, older than exile had made it, as if every mile toward the Covenant aged them another year. "It asks for the oldest thing I have left."

Tessa's pen stopped.

"The roof," she said. "You already gave—"

"The rain," Silas said. "I gave the court the sound of it. The feeling. What it's like to be small and safe under a storm you love." They laughed once, short and awful. "Do you understand what a theft that was? The fae didn't even have to trick me. I sold them my own comfort because I wanted the Ember Sigil more. And what's left—" they pressed two fingers to their temple, hard, like trying to pinch out a splinter, "—what's left is the fact of it. Rain. Roof. Grandmother. Three words. A list. I know the list. I can't feel any of it."

The torch hissed. Somewhere far above, wind moved through a sky that belonged to no one anymore.

"You could lie to it," Bram said quietly. "Vaults can be lied to. I've read the interrogation manuals. Every lock made by hands can be fooled by hands."

"This wasn't made by hands." Silas rose. Dust fell from the knees of their coat. "And I'm done lying to my own inheritance. It's how we got here. Everyone in my line lied a little less until the king lied completely." They turned, and found Tessa's eyes, and held them. "Whatever happens, don't let me stand up too fast. The last time I paid a memory, I tried to walk and the ground came up to meet me."

"I'll catch you," Tessa said. She meant it as a joke and heard, halfway out, that it wasn't one.

Silas smiled anyway — the first real smile since the Ash Court — and turned back to the stone.

*He smiled,* Tessa wrote later, by the fire. *It looked strange on him, like a charm hung on the wrong door. I think he's been practicing smiling so we won't count what it costs. I am going to count. Somebody has to keep the ledger honest. Bram keeps the other kind.*

* * *

The giving took less time than any of them expected.

Silas laid both palms against the warm rock and spoke the opening words of the Covenant — the ones the whispering stone had taught Tessa, the ones that tasted like iron and honey — and the cliff face remembered being a door. Light seamed outward from beneath Silas's fingers, thin gold lines spreading like cracks in an egg, and heat rolled off the stone in waves that made the torch gutter and made Tessa's eyes sting and made her think, absurdly, of standing too close to her mother's bread oven on baking mornings, back before her village emptied, back before—

She shut that door firmly. There would be time to grieve villages later. Right now there was only this: Silas swaying on their feet, the light climbing their arms like ivy, and the vault beginning, very softly, to hum.

*State your payment,* said a voice that came from everywhere — from the rock, from the heat, from inside Tessa's own teeth.

"My earliest memory," Silas said. Their voice cracked down the middle on the second word and they went on through the crack, which Tessa thought was probably the bravest thing she'd ever watch anyone do, and she had watched a man hold a river ford against the Hollowed. Wait. No. That hadn't happened yet. Would happen. The journal was getting confusing. Time near sigils ran like spilled milk — everywhere at once and pooling wrong.

"The rain," Silas said. "On my grandmother's roof. Clay tiles. I was four years old, maybe five. She'd set me on the sill and tell me each drop had a name and if I listened closely I'd hear mine." A breath. "I never heard it. But she kept telling me. Every storm. For years. And I kept listening. And that listening is the first true thing I ever did, and it is the reason I can call weather instead of merely suffering it, and I give it freely, whole, without reserve, to the Covenant of Sigils, for the reforging of the accord between the realms."

The humming deepened. The gold lines blazed.

And then — Tessa saw it happen and would write it down that same night so she'd never doubt herself — the memory left him. It left visibly. It lifted off Silas like steam off morning grass, a shimmer in the shape of a small child sitting on a windowsill, silver-grey and translucent, rain falling through it in slow bright threads. For one heartbeat the little ghost turned its head, and Tessa would swear forever after that it looked at her. Not at Silas. At her. As if it knew who would be keeping what it couldn't.

Then the vault drank it, the way the Ash Court had drunk the sound of rain, and the door opened, and Silas folded sideways like laundry slipping off a line, and Tessa caught them — mostly. Enough. She went down under the weight with her arms full of storm-caller and her cheek against cold flagstone and Bram shouting her name somewhere above, and none of it mattered, because she'd promised, and promises were the only currency she owned outright.

* * *

The vault itself was small. A round chamber, walls of dark iron-veined stone, and floating in its center, turning slowly, a sigil of hammered copper the size of a dinner plate — a spiral of flame frozen mid-curl, glowing faintly orange, throwing heat like a hearth. When Silas, gray-faced and leaning hard on Tessa's shoulder, reached out and closed their hand around it, the copper cooled instantly to the temperature of skin, and the spiral sank into their palm and vanished, and the fourth mark bloomed along the line of sigils already written into their forearm like a row of brands healing backward.

Four, Tessa counted. Four of five.

"One more," Bram said. His voice did something complicated on the word *one*. "The Last Sigil."

"Don't," Tessa said.

Bram glanced at her. "Don't what?"

"Don't say it like that." She adjusted Silas's arm over her shoulders. Silas weighed more than they should have, the way exhausted people do, as if grief had mass. "Like it's a coin we've already spent. We haven't spent anything yet."

"Tessa." Bram lowered his voice, and she hated him a little for the gentleness in it, because gentle was what people got right before they told you the truth. "You've read the murals. You heard the stone. The final sigil doesn't want a memory. It wants the whole remembered life. Every story Silas gives me on the road, every name, every kindness — that's the price list. We're not collecting stories. We're spending them."

"Then we'd better make some new ones," Tessa said, "so there's something left over," and she steered Silas out of the vault and did not look back to see what Bram's face did.

But that night, at the fire, with Bram asleep sitting up against his pack — the man slept like a soldier even now, especially now, one hand always near his stolen ledger — and with Silas drifting in and out of a dreamless payment-sleep, Tessa opened her journal to a fresh page and began to ask questions.

Not big ones. She'd learned that much. Big questions made Silas grope at the bricked-over windows of themselves and come away bleeding. Small questions worked. Small questions were doors left ajar.

"Tell me about the first charm you ever sold," she said.

Silas surfaced slowly, blinking at the fire. "That's — hm. That's not a payment question. That's just a question."

"That's the point."

A pause. Then, wonderingly, like a man finding a dry room in a flooded house: "A luck-knot. Red thread, seven knots. I was fourteen. I charged a fishwife two coppers and it actually worked — not because of the magic, I'd barely any then, but because she believed it so hard she stopped second-guessing her nets, and belief catches more fish than luck ever did." Silas's mouth curved. "First lesson of commerce: half of magic is someone wanting it to be true."

Tessa wrote it down. All of it. The red thread, the seven knots, the fishwife, the wanting-it-true. Her pen moved fast and her handwriting got ugly when she hurried and she didn't care.

"Why are you doing that?" Silas asked. Softly. Not accusingly. Which was worse, somehow.

"Because you keep giving yourself away," Tessa said, not looking up. "Piece by piece. To courts and vaults and whatever comes next. And nobody's writing any of it down except me, and I am not losing the whole of you to a rock, Silas Dravenmoor, I don't care how holy the rocks are."

Silas was silent a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the thinning dark, something fae sighed and did not come closer.

"My grandmother used to say," Silas said at last, "that a story isn't yours until someone else can tell it back to you." They looked into the fire. "I can't tell you hers anymore. But you could tell me mine. If I forget. When I forget."

"When," Tessa repeated. "Not if."

"When." Silas met her eyes across the flames, and there was no flinching in it, and Tessa understood suddenly that this — this exact moment, this calm — was the most frightening thing she'd seen since the Hollowed. "Promise me the journal survives even if I don't. Promise me it goes to the gifted children. Whatever children. After."

"I promise," Tessa said, because refusing would have broken him, and lying to the dying is a mercy the living are allowed.

Then she wrote: *Silas made me promise the journal goes to the children. I promised. I intend to break that promise by making sure Silas is alive to hand it over personally. Some mercies are meant to be cheated.*

* * *

Three more nights of stories. Tessa filled eleven pages.

She learned about a tutor named Wren who taught Silas to read cloud-shapes like scripture and was erased in the first purge, name and all, so that Silas alone remembered him and had begun to doubt even that. She learned that Silas was afraid of moths, secretly, disproportionately, and would deny it under torture. She learned about a dog called Barley, a one-eared farm dog who followed Silas for a summer and died old, which Silas called "the only good ending I witnessed before I was twenty." She learned the words to a lullaby in a language that predated the Covenant, four lines of it, and when Silas sang them softly to the fire, the flames leaned in like listeners, and Bram woke up and pretended he hadn't.

And on the third night, Silas stopped her mid-sentence — she'd been writing about the fishwife again, expanding, embroidering, adding color the way you patch a coat — and said:

"Read it back to me."

"What?"

"What you've written. Read it back. I want—" and here the steadiness finally failed, just for a breath, "—I want to hear myself in someone else's voice. Before there's less of me to hear."

So Tessa read. By firelight, haltingly, her ugly handwriting slowing her down: the luck-knot, the fishwife, the moths, Barley, Wren, the lullaby, the grandmother's proverb about stories belonging to whoever tells them back. And Silas lay with their head on their pack and their scarred forearm facing the fire — four sigils, four payments, four holes where a life used to show through — and listened to their own existence read aloud like a bedtime tale, and wept without noise, the way people do who learned young that noise invites hunters.

When she finished, Silas said, "Thank you," and then, after a while, "That's how you fight them, you know. The king. The Severing. Everyone who thinks a person is just what the crown hasn't harvested yet." Their eyes were closed. "You write it down. Memory hoarded dies with its keeper. Memory shared—" a yawn broke the sentence; they finished it half-asleep, "—memory shared is a fire. You pass it hand to hand and it doesn't go out."

Tessa sat a long time after Silas slept, pen hovering, unwilling to disturb the page with anything so small as her own handwriting over those words.

Then she wrote, at the bottom, beneath everything:

*Memory shared is a fire. — S.D., night 3, Ember Road. If you are reading this and Silas cannot remember saying it: they said it. It counts. You may tell them so.*

* * *

They came down out of the Ember Road on the fifth morning, into the first true mortal air in a week, and the world had changed while they were underground.

Not subtly. Not at the edges.

The sky over the eastern plains — the direction of the old capital, the direction of the Root Vault — was the wrong color. Not storm-colored; Tessa knew storms the way she knew her own heartbeat now, having traveled inside one. This was different. A bruise-colored band stretched across the horizon like a seam, and along it, faint but unmistakable, hung threads of light that did not flicker, did not move, and did not belong to anything natural in either realm.

The Otherworld was bleeding through. Or being pulled through. Or being cut.

Silas stood at the ridgeline with the wind pulling at their coat and all four sigils humming under their skin, and Tessa watched the color drain from their face in real time.

"That's not weather," Bram said. He had the ledger out, though there was nothing in it about skies. "That's the ritual starting early. Weeks early. Why would he start early—"

"Because we have four sigils," Silas said quietly. "Because he knows we're coming, and he'd rather fight us while his machine is already running than wait for us to arrive complete." They turned, and the wind turned with them, obedient, eager, and Tessa felt the air pressure shift the way it did before hail. "We have maybe two days. Less."

Below them, on the road winding north toward the capital, Tessa saw them at the same moment Silas and Bram did: riders. A dozen. Black-and-silver livery. And at their head, unhurried, riding like a man out for pleasure, a figure in dove-grey whose posture was — horribly, unmistakably — a mirror of Silas's own.

Bram swore and grabbed for his sword. Silas's hand filled with static. But Tessa, squinting against the wind, felt the recognition land first, cold as well-water, because she had spent three nights learning Silas's face and its echoes, and the rider ahead wore the family of it like a uniform.

"Aldric," she said.

Silas didn't answer. The storm gathered around their shoulders like a mantle, and far below, the rider in dove-grey raised one hand — not in threat.

In greeting.
