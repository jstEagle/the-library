## 18. The Severing Begins

The first sign was the birds.

Silas noticed it on the road below the old capital — a silence where there should have been evening song. The hedgerows were full of wings, every starling and finch in three parishes flying inland in one vast murmuring ribbon, as though the land itself had tilted and poured them toward the king's city.

"They know," Tessa said. She walked with the sigil-stone cradled against her chest, wrapped in flannel, though it glowed through the cloth now like a coal that would not die. Four sigils rode with them: Tide at Bram's belt, Ember sewn into Tessa's collar, Root bound in cord across Silas's back, and the stone itself, which held a dead woman's voice and had not stopped whispering since dawn. "The birds know before we do."

"Animals always feel it first," Silas said. "When the Veil thins. When anything tears."

They crested the hill, and the capital opened beneath them like a wound.

The city sat in a bowl of dark hills, ten thousand rooftops silvered by a rising moon, and above its highest tower — the king's spire, a needle of white stone older than any crown — the sky was wrong. Not cloudy. Not stormy. Wrong. There was a line up there, faint as a scratch on glass, running from horizon to horizon, and along that line the stars seemed to tremble, to smear, to bleed color they should not have had: green, then violet, then a bruised gold that made Silas's teeth ache.

Bram stopped beside them. He had dressed his wounds from the ford badly, binding his own ribs with strips of shirt because neither of the others could bear to pull the knots tight enough. His face was gray. But when he saw the tear in the sky, he went grayer still.

"That's not possible," he said. "The rite needs seven nights of preparation. The choir of harvested voices. The — I read the specifications myself, when I still believed the reports were about containment. It cannot begin early."

"It began when Aldric failed to take the sigils from us," Silas said quietly. "He isn't waiting for perfection anymore. He's waiting for nothing." They looked down at the city, at the needle of the spire, at the thin gold light already leaking around its base like fire under a door. "He's spending everything he has, because he knows what we carry. Because he knows we're coming."

Tessa's hand found Silas's sleeve. Her fingers were cold. "Then we run," she said. "We run now. Tonight. Before—"

Before she finished the sentence, the sky tore.

* * *

There was no thunder. That was the worst of it. A rip across the whole vault of heaven, wide enough to see the curvature of the world through it, and it happened without sound, the way a wound opens in flesh — silently, terribly, with only the body's knowledge afterward that something is missing.

Through the tear, Silas saw the Otherworld.

Not the glimpses they had known all their life: not the shimmer at dusk, not the fae lanterns seen sideways out of the corner of an eye, not the thin places where the two worlds breathed through each other like lovers sharing air. This was the Otherworld *naked*. Whole. Vast beyond sense — forests that grew upward into their own skies, rivers that ran with light, courts and towers and wild green distances, all of it suddenly visible, all of it suddenly *separate*, hanging in the torn sky like a painting ripped off a wall and held at arm's length.

And it was bleeding.

Light ran from the tear like water from a cut — slow at first, then faster, falling in long luminous threads over the hills, over the rooftops, soaking into fields and streets. Where it fell, things changed. Grass silvered. Old stones remembered being altars. In a farmyard far below, Silas watched a scarecrow sit up, look at its own straw hands, and begin — very softly — to weep.

"No," Bram said. He had gone to his knees in the road, and his voice came out stripped of everything but horror. "No, no — the reports said the Severing would be clean. A closing. A door shut. They said—" He stopped. He pressed both hands over his mouth.

"The reports were written by people who thought of the Veil as a wall," Silas said. Their own voice sounded distant to them, calm in the way that comes before great violence, the way the sea goes flat before a hurricane. "It was never a wall. It was skin. It was the boundary between two living things, and he is flaying the world alive."

Then it reached them.

Silas felt it first as warmth — a strange, treacherous warmth blooming behind the eyes, like standing too close to a hearth after years of cold. For one heartbeat it was almost kind. Almost like homecoming.

Then the memories began to smoke.

* * *

Later, Silas would struggle to describe it, because there was nothing in any language quite like the feeling of your own past leaving you. It did not hurt, exactly. It *thinned*. It was as if someone had taken the book of your life and begun holding pages to a candle — each page going translucent, going brown, going to ash at the edges, the words still legible but fading even as you tried to read them.

Silas gripped the roadside fence. Held on. Fought.

*Rain on grandmother's roof* — no. Gone; given already to the Ash Court, surrendered freely at the Ember Sigil's vault, and the absence of it was an old wound that now tore wider. What else — *the smell of the charm stall, cedar and beeswax* — fading, fading, the cedar note going first, then the beeswax, until it was just the idea of a smell, a memory of having once known a smell—

Silas snarled and called the storm.

It answered instantly, hungrily, glad to be used. Lightning walked down the hillside in branching steps and struck the fence-post beside their hand; the thunder cracked the night open; and for a moment — only a moment — the smoke of losing memories met the smoke of a real storm, and the two were kin, and the kinship anchored them. Storm-callers remembered through weather. It was in the murals at the Hollow Abbey, in the lineage of faces that wore Silas's face: *we hold ourselves together by holding the sky.*

Around them, the realm was screaming.

Every gifted person within a hundred miles felt the Severing reach for them at once. Silas felt them the way a spider feels its web: a thousand small vibrations of terror along threads of magic that the Crown had spent centuries pretending did not exist. The hedge-witch in her cottage clutching her cat and forgetting its name mid-sentence. The ferryman whose luck-charms guttered out as he forgot his mother's lullaby. Children — gifted children, untrained, undefended — waking in the dark with their short histories already smoking off them like mist off a pond, so that some of them, Silas knew with sick certainty, would forget their own names before morning.

And the harvested ones — the Hollowed, the emptied, the poor devoured souls stored like grain in the Root Vault — Silas felt them too. All across the countryside they stopped where they stood. Turned their blank faces toward the spire. And began, in perfect silence, to walk.

"Tessa." Silas turned, grabbed the girl by both shoulders. Her eyes were enormous, reflecting the torn sky, and tears ran freely down her face, though her jaw was set like someone much older. "Listen to me. It will come for you next — you're gifted, the stone marked you, it will find you. When it does, you do what we practiced. You hold a story. One story. You tell it to yourself over and over, loud, inside your head, until this passes. Do you understand?"

"My story is the night market," Tessa said. Her voice shook. "The night you caught me stealing. You didn't shout. You bought me a meat pie and told me the stone chose thieves because thieves have quick hands and quicker hearts. I've told it to myself every night since the abbey." She wiped her face with her sleeve, furious at the tears. "It's mine. He can't have it."

"He won't," Silas said. "Now — Bram. Bram!"

But Bram was not listening. He was standing rigid in the middle of the road, facing the tear in the sky, and Silas saw the moment it took him — saw his sister's name try to leave him.

"Maerin," he said aloud, desperate, gripping the word like a rope thrown to a drowning man. "Maerin Corvale. Harvested in the third year of the Purge. She liked — she liked —" His breath hitched. "She liked plums. She was afraid of deep water. She let a sparrow nest in her hair once and wouldn't let anyone shoo it out for three days. Maerin. *Maerin.*"

He turned to Silas, and his face was the face of a man who has just learned the exact weight of his own soul and found it lighter than it was yesterday.

"Some of it's already gone," he whispered. "I can feel the holes. Things I know I knew. Silas — I can't remember what her voice sounded like. I know I knew it. It's *gone.*"

* * *

They moved through the night like survivors of a flood, wading through a drowning world.

The roads toward the capital were full of the displaced. Families stood in their doorways looking up at the bleeding sky, holding children who cried without knowing why. An old man knelt in the middle of the high road, turning his pockets inside out again and again, searching for something that had been taken not from his pockets but from him — and when Silas paused to help, he looked up with mild, terrible confusion and asked them if they had seen his wife, describing her in loving detail, unable to recall that she had been harvested eleven months ago, unable even to grieve, because grief requires remembering who was lost.

That was the true cruelty of the Severing, Silas understood then, walking on with their heart like a stone in their chest. It did not merely kill. Killing left mourners. The Severing left *holes* — absences so smooth that the people around them would smooth themselves to fit, would forget there had ever been anything to miss. A realm severed from the Otherworld would not remember it had ever been whole. Children born after would inherit a world with one fewer color in it and never know to wonder.

And it was happening everywhere. All at once. To everyone.

The despair of it nearly broke Silas on that road. Four sigils against a ritual already tearing heaven. Three exhausted people against a crown, an army, a brother who had chosen the machine. What was the Covenant, really? A story old women told. A mural in a ruined abbey. And against it, a wound in the sky the width of the world.

Silas stumbled. Caught themselves on a milestone. And the sigil-stone spoke.

It did not speak in Tessa's presence this time — or perhaps it did, and none of them had words left for it. It simply pulsed against Silas's back, warm as a sleeping child, and the whisper that lived in it — grandmother-voiced, patient, iron underneath — said:

*You are counting yourself against the sky, child. Count instead what the sky is losing.*

Silas closed their eyes. Reached out along the web of the world, past the terror, past the smoke.

And found it still there. Under everything. The Veil itself — wounded, flayed, *bleeding*, yes, but not yet cut. The Severing was not done. It was beginning. And a thing that has begun can be undone. A ritual is a machine, and machines need their workings intact, and the workings of this one stood in a white spire in a city full of stolen grain-memory, tended by men who believed the reports.

The realms were not severed yet.

They were only bleeding. And blood meant the body was still alive.

"Keep moving," Silas said, and heard their own voice come back to them steadied, tempered, forged into something new. "All of you. Keep moving. We go tonight, straight through, while the Hollowed march and the guards watch the sky. While he thinks the hard part is finished." They lifted their chin toward the spire, toward the gold light, toward the brother waiting at the top of it. "He started the Severing early because he feared us. Good. Fear means he knows exactly how much damage we can do."

Tessa fell into step at their side, the stone glowing against her chest, her lips moving silently around the story of a meat pie and a thief's quick heart. Bram limped behind them, whispering his sister's name like a prayer he refused to let die.

Behind them, the tear in the sky widened another finger's breadth, and light fell like rain.

Ahead, the spire waited — white, ancient, crowned in gold, humming with the voices of everyone the crown had ever eaten.

Silas felt the storm gathering in their bones, answering the storm in the sky, and understood with sudden clarity that there was only one way now: up.

They would climb into the hurricane itself.

They would make it theirs.
