## 20. The Last Sigil

The sky above the old capital was coming apart.

Silas felt it before they saw it — a wrongness in the pressure of the air, like the world holding its breath through a torn lung. The Severing had begun. Far to the north, above the king's spire, a wound of white light split the clouds from horizon to horizon, and through that wound the Otherworld bled: colors that had no names in mortal tongues, spilling downward like dye through water, thinning as they fell. Every gifted soul in the realm would be feeling it now — the slow smoke rising off their memories, names going gray at the edges, faces softening like wet ink.

Silas ran anyway.

The Root Vault lay beneath the ruined cathedral district, down stairways that predated the city that had buried them. The sigil-stone burned against Silas's chest where it hung on its cord, whispering in a voice that was half Tessa's grandmother and half something older — *down, oathkeeper, down, the roots remember even when the crown forgets* — and behind them came the sound they both dreaded and needed: Bram's boots, uneven now, one hand pressed to the bandaged ruin of his side, and Tessa's quick breath, and the journal clutched against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

*Hold the line,* Bram had said at the ford, and he had held it, and the Hollowed had broken against him like waves against a sea-wall until there was nothing left standing between Aldric's hunters and the vault but three people who should have died a dozen times over.

The stairs opened into darkness that was not empty.

The Root Vault breathed. Silas felt it in their teeth — the vast slow pulse of the first Covenant, sworn here when the world was young enough that mortals and fae still shared a language. The chamber rose around them like the inside of a heart, walls of living root woven through pale stone, and along every surface the sigils crawled and gleamed: thousands of them, ten thousand, each one a promise someone had once kept. The four reclaimed sigils — Tide, Ember, Root, and the storm-caller's own — hung in the air ahead, orbiting a point of darkness where the fifth should have been.

And all around, stacked in luminous drifts like grain in a tithe-barn, the harvested memories of the purged waited to be fed into the king's machine.

*Oh,* Silas thought, and could not finish the thought, because the sight of them was too large for any single feeling. Names. Faces. A fisherman's memory of his daughter learning to walk. A hedge-witch's memory of her mother's hands teaching hers to knot charms. Whole lives, bundled and shelved, waiting to be burned as fuel for a wall between worlds.

"The altar," Tessa said. Her voice cracked on the second word. She was staring up at the drifting sigils, and the light from them turned her face silver. "It wants — Silas, it's already asking."

It was. Silas felt the question settle over them like snowfall, gentle and total. The Covenant did not speak in words, not quite, but meaning moved through it the way weather moves through a valley, and what it meant was this:

*The last sigil is an oathkeeper's whole remembered life. All of it. Freely given.*

* * *

"There has to be another way." Tessa's hands were shaking. She grabbed Silas's sleeve — the same sleeve she'd stolen from, a lifetime ago in Vellharrow's night market, when she was a thief with quick fingers and no idea what she'd taken. "There's always another way. We found four sigils without — we can find—"

"Tessa." Silas said her name and watched her stop, because they both knew. They'd known since Greyhaven, maybe since the abbey, maybe since the first moment the stone flared alive in a thief's hand and chose her. Some prices were not negotiable. Some doors only opened outward.

Bram leaned against the root-wall, gray-faced, blood seeping slow and dark through his coat. He didn't argue. Inquisitors were trained to weigh costs; Bram Corvale had spent twenty years learning exactly what the crown's arithmetic cost, and he knew a fair price when he finally saw one.

"Then we do it right," he said hoarsely. "Not the way the king does it. Not taking. Giving. Say it aloud, Silas. Make it an oath. Make them witness."

Silas looked at the two of them — the disgraced Inquisitor bleeding out against the walls of the institution that had made him, the runaway apprentice who had never once let go — and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that this was what belonging actually was. Not a lineage written in murals. Not a name in a ledger or a sigil in a stone. It was this: two people who refused to let you do the terrible necessary thing alone.

"I'm frightened," Silas admitted. It seemed important to say it plainly. "I don't want to forget you."

"Then don't," Tessa said fiercely. "Fight for every piece. And whatever's left — I'll carry the rest. I've been writing it down since the Ash Court, you great stubborn storm, every story you ever told me. You think I lugged that journal across half the realm for my health?"

A laugh broke out of Silas, wet and startled, the last laugh of the person they had been. Then they stepped forward, out of reach of her hands, onto the dais where the first oathkeeper had knelt a thousand years before the first king drew his first border.

The four sigils drifted close, circling, waiting.

Silas knelt.

* * *

"I am Silas Dravenmoor," they said, and their voice carried strangely in the vault, doubled and tripled, echoing back from walls of root and bone and memory. "Storm-caller. Exile. Charm-seller of Vellharrow. Oathbreaker's daughter, oathmaker's child. I come freely, and I hold nothing back."

The darkness at the center of the sigils' orbit opened like an eye.

"And I give—" Silas closed their own eyes, and reached inward, and took hold of everything they were.

It came away whole, like a plant lifted from rich soil, roots trailing.

Every name. Their grandmother Maren, singing rain-songs on a tin roof while storms gathered obediently beyond the hills. Their mother, proud and afraid, pressing a forged travel-pass into Silas's hand the night the king's riders came. Their father, whom Silas had barely known — a face, a laugh, a door closing. Aldric, small and fierce and golden, before the crown got its hooks into him; Aldric at seven, swearing they'd always look out for each other; Aldric turning away in a corridor of the spire, saying *the suffering ends, Silas, one way or another.*

Every exile. The road south in winter. The flooded lowlands. The taste of river water and fear. Vellharrow's night market, lamplit and godless, where Silas had sold harmless-looking charms and hidden storms inside them like smuggled letters, because a gift buried deep enough starts to feel like shame, and shame starts to feel like home.

Every kindness. Tessa's face the first time the sigil-stone flared in her stolen hands — terror becoming wonder becoming trust. Bram opening the drainage-tunnel gate instead of closing it, choosing his dead sister over his living masters. The innkeeper at Greyhaven who lied to bounty-hunters for strangers. The fae of the Ash Court accepting a memory of rain as if it were treasure, because to the fading ones, being remembered at all was treasure.

Every failure, too — the Covenant asked for those as well. Every lie told in Vellharrow. Every time Silas had chosen hiding over helping. The quay at Greyhaven flattened by rage, innocents swept screaming into the water. All of it, given. Held nothing back.

The last sigil drank it in.

Silas felt themselves thinning — not dying, nothing so simple as dying, but *unwriting*, the self coming loose from its moorings like a boat cut free. The vault filled with light. The five sigils aligned, and the Covenant of Sigils woke beneath the world like a sleeper remembering why she'd lain down, and every harvested memory in the tithe-drifts stirred and shone, recognizing kinship, recognizing *oath*.

Far above, the Great Severing faltered.

Silas felt it happen even as they dissolved: the ritual at the spire meeting something older than crowns, older than borders, a law woven into the roots of both realms before either had names. Aldric's machine shrieked against it. The white wound in the sky shuddered, bled color, began — slowly, like ice giving way in spring — to close. The severed realms strained toward each other across the narrowing dark, and the new accord caught them, bound them, swore them together in words Silas no longer had the language to read but could still feel, the way you feel sunlight through a window: warmth, without understanding.

The last thing the person called Silas Dravenmoor thought was not a grand thought at all. It was Tessa's voice, from somewhere very far away and very close, reading aloud:

*"Entry one. The night I stole a stone that turned out to be stealing me right back—"*

And then the dark, and then the quiet,

and then—

* * *

Light.

Rain-sound, though there was no rain. Someone breathing nearby, fast and scared. Hands on Silas's face, small hands, chapped at the knuckles, patting cheeks as if checking whether a lamp still held flame.

"Silas. Silas, look at me. Look at—"

Silas looked.

A girl crouched over them, young, mud-streaked, eyes red-rimmed and blazing, holding a battered journal open against the crook of her arm. Behind her, slumped against the glowing root-wall, a big man with gray in his beard watched with an expression Silas couldn't read — grief and hope fighting openly on his ruined face. Above them all, five sigils turned slowly in the vault's warm light, and somewhere impossibly far overhead, the sky was knitting itself whole.

"Who," Silas began, and stopped, because the word had no shape behind it. No name answered when they searched for their own. There was nothing — no grandmother's roof, no exile road, no brother, no storm. Just this room, these faces, this astonishing ache of being alive with no reason given.

The girl's breath caught. For one horrible moment her face crumpled — and then she set her jaw the way soldiers do, the way oathkeepers do, wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, and held the journal out into the light.

"That's all right," she said, and her voice only shook a little. "That's all right. I brought everything. We'll start from the beginning."

She cleared her throat, found the page, and read:

"*Entry one.*"

Silas listened.

Somewhere above, the last light of the Severing guttered and died, and the rain that had been holding off all night finally, gently, began to fall — on the ruined capital, on the king's broken spire, on the roof of the world, which belonged, once again and by sworn accord, to everyone under it.
