## 1. Ink That Cannot Leave

There were three things every novice of the Silent Temple of Vael Hollow learned before they learned anything else, and Paloma Hartwell had learned them all in her first week, aged nine, mostly by breaking them.

First: magic does not work here. Do not waste your prayers. Second: silence is a discipline, not a punishment—though it is also a punishment. Third: your debt is not yours to question.

She had spent fourteen years since then getting very good at all three.

The copying room sat at the bottom of the temple's eastern stair, below even the wine cellar, which the brothers insisted was for *medicinal purposes* and which Brother Aldous guarded with the ferocity of a mother bear. It was a long stone room lit by forty candles that the Temple bought cheap because they guttered, and it smelled of tallow, cold stone, and the particular sourness of old ink. Forty desks. Forty stools. And on this particular evening in late autumn, thirty-nine of them stood empty, because the other scribes had finished their quotas and gone to bed like sensible people.

Paloma's desk was the one nearest the brazier, not as an honor but as a punishment—the brothers had decided years ago that she worked too well to be allowed to freeze, and too much to be allowed to be comfortable. The candle at her elbow had burned down to a stub shaped rather like a kneeling monk, which she supposed was appropriate.

The ledger open before her was the tithe-book of the village of Marrow's Edge, and it was, without exaggeration, the worst piece of record-keeping she had ever encountered. The previous clerk—a man named Tollo, who she suspected had been paid in turnips and enthusiasm—had recorded the year's grain levy in three different hands, two different calendars, and one instance of what appeared to be a poem about his donkey.

"*And Bramble bore the sacks up hill,*" she read under her breath, though her lips barely moved; old habits. "*Though Bramble is a mule of ill—*"

"Brother Tollo," said a voice behind her, "was a poet and a fraud."

Paloma did not jump. Fourteen years of practice had trained the jump out of her, or at least buried it deep enough that only her shoulders knew about it. She set down her pen, aligned it precisely with the edge of the desk, and turned.

Master Auditor Crevan Quill stood in the doorway holding a lantern he did not need, because the room already had forty candles, but auditors were like that. They believed light should come from them personally.

He was a narrow man made narrower by his coat, which was grey, and his collar, which was stiffer grey, and his expression, which was the grey of a sky deciding whether to ruin a harvest festival. He carried a ledger of his own chained to his belt—not metaphorically. Actually chained. Paloma had once asked him if the chain was ceremonial. He had said no, and had seemed offended by the suggestion that anything about him might be merely ceremonial.

"Master Quill," she said. "It's late."

"It is." He came down the aisle between desks with the soft, deliberate steps of a man who wanted you to know he could sneak up on you whenever he pleased. "You are still working."

"You are still auditing."

"I am always auditing. It is what I am *for*." He stopped beside her desk and looked down at her page without asking, because auditors did not ask. His eyes moved across her neat columns, her careful marginalia, the little correction notes she'd left where Tollo's arithmetic wandered off into fantasy. Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. If Master Quill had ever smiled, no witness survived to describe it, but that flicker was the closest anyone had come.

"Marrow's Edge owes the Temple eleven barrels of barley more than they have recorded," he said.

"Yes. Tollo counted the same field twice in spring and forgot the miller's tithe entirely. I've noted both errors and drafted a corrected figure. Eleven barrels, plus the interest accrued since midsummer." She turned the ledger toward him. "I've written to the village elder suggesting they send the barley before the frost ruins the stores, with a reminder that charity may be extended to households who show good faith early. The letter's there, third sheet. You'll want to check my sums."

"I will always check your sums."

"I'd be worried if you didn't."

Something almost happened to his face again. He picked up the letter and read it through, and Paloma waited with the particular patience of a person who has done this dance four hundred times and knows every step.

Here was the thing about Master Crevan Quill: he was not cruel. Paloma had thought about this a great deal over the years, in the way one thinks about weather or the price of candles—as a fixed feature of the world requiring accommodation. Cruelty would have been simpler. A cruel auditor you could hate. But Quill was scrupulous, precise, and utterly without mercy in the way that a set of scales is without mercy: he weighed exactly, no more and no less, and if the weights came out against you, that was not his cruelty, that was arithmetic.

He was also, Paloma suspected privately, the only person in the Temple who actually read everything she wrote. It was a strange kind of companionship. It was the only kind she could afford.

"The figures are correct," he said at last, setting down the letter.

"Thank you."

"That was not praise. It is a statement of fact, and facts are my trade." He folded his hands. "Your bond stands at six hundred and forty marks, Hartwell. As of tonight."

Paloma kept her face pleasant and her hands still. Inside, something small and tired lay down again, the way a dog does when it knows the knock at the door isn't for it. Six hundred and forty marks. She earned, after room, board, candles, ink, and the Temple's various fees for various things—there was a fee for the laundering of scribe's robes, and one, inexplicably, for the *spiritual maintenance of the copying room floor*—perhaps four marks a month against her debt.

"Do you know," she said lightly, "at this rate I'll be free just in time to die of old age at this desk. There's a certain tidiness to it. They could bury me under the floorboards and save on funeral fees."

"Fees are assessed by the Bursar's office, not mine."

"I know. I was joking."

"I know." He looked at her a moment longer than the joke required. Then: "There is a matter we must discuss. Sit comfortably."

Nobody in the history of debts had ever been comforted by the phrase *sit comfortably*. Paloma sat anyway, folding her hands on the desk beside the kneeling-monk candle.

"Your indenture contract," Quill said, "was drawn fourteen years ago. Under its terms, the Temple advanced your family's debt—your father's debts, principally—and you serve until such time as the principal and interest are discharged. The standard term allows for accelerated discharge through exemplary service."

"Yes."

"You have provided exemplary service."

"Thank you."

"For fourteen years."

"...Yes."

He let that sit. Paloma watched him do it, watched him arrange the silence the way a mason arranges stones, and felt the first cold thread of understanding go through her.

"The contract also contains a clause," Quill continued, "which I confess I have never before invoked, because I found it distasteful. Clause nineteen. It states that where a bonded scribe's productivity exceeds the median by a sustained margin, the Temple may deem her services *structurally essential*, and extend the bond by"—he paused, and Paloma realized he genuinely did not enjoy saying it—"ten years."

The candle flame leaned in the draft from the door. Somewhere above them, a floorboard creaked; Brother Aldous making his medicinal rounds, probably.

"Ten years," Paloma said.

"A decade."

"I heard you. I'm checking my sums." Her voice came out level. She was rather proud of it. Inside, the small tired dog was no longer lying down; it was standing very still, hackles up, in a room that smelled of smoke. "Clause nineteen. Let me see if I follow the logic. Because I copy ledgers well—better than well, better than any scribe you have—you are going to keep me here ten more years. My reward for competence is more debt."

"It is not a reward," Quill said. "It is an assessment."

"It's a trap with excellent handwriting."

"Hartwell."

"No, tell me if I'm wrong." She kept her tone mild, almost conversational, because that was the trick of it—she had learned long ago that the only way to shout in a silent temple was to say terrible things quietly. "If I copied badly, I'd owe less time?"

"If you copied badly, you'd be disciplined and your bond lengthened regardless."

"So there is no version of my behavior that ends my debt sooner."

"There is discharge by payment."

"Which requires marks I cannot earn, because every mark I might earn goes to the Temple that keeps me from earning them. Which is—" she gestured elegantly at the forty candles, the stone, the whole patient weight of the valley above them, "—the design."

Quill was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost some of its ledger-flatness. "I did not write clause nineteen. I inherited it, as you inherited your father's debts. Neither of us chose the terms."

"But you're invoking it."

"I am required to report structural essentials to the Council of Brothers. The reporting is mandatory. Whether they invoke—" He stopped. Paloma looked at him, really looked, and saw what she had seen perhaps twice in fourteen years: the auditor doing arithmetic he did not like.

"They've already decided," she said.

"The Council votes tomorrow night." He straightened, and the grey closed back over him like water over a stone. "I am informing you of the hearing as courtesy requires. You may attend. You may speak, within silence rules. That is the extent of what is in my power to offer."

"And if I asked you—man to ledger—what you would advise?"

For one unguarded instant, Crevan Quill looked tired. Deeply, humanly tired, the tiredness of a man who had spent thirty years weighing things exactly and being blamed for their weight.

"I would advise," he said, "that a clever woman in a valley with one road ought to think carefully about how many roads she believes exist."

Then he lifted his lantern and went up the stairs, and his footsteps faded, and Paloma sat alone among the forty candles with the ghost of clause nineteen hanging over her like a second ceiling.

* * *

She did not sleep. She told herself she was finishing the Marrow's Edge corrections, and she was, but her hands moved while her mind ran the numbers the way a frightened animal runs a fence line—again, again, looking for the gap.

Six hundred and forty marks. Ten more years. She was twenty-three. She would be thirty-three before the ledger called her free, and free would mean what? A worn scribe with magnificent handwriting and no history outside these walls, stepping blinking into a world that had gotten on perfectly well without her.

Her father had owed two hundred marks to a wool merchant in Lowmarsh. That was the beginning of it, the whole absurd beginning—two hundred marks, a bad harvest, a proud man, a contract signed with an X because her father had never learned his letters. The merchant had sold the debt to the Temple within the year, because the Temple bought debts the way other institutions bought relics: patiently, in bulk, forever. And debts, once inside Vael Hollow, behaved like everything else in Vael Hollow.

They stayed.

That was the valley's whole nature, its single law. Magic did not enter here. Pilgrims came from three kingdoms to stand at the border stones and feel it—the sudden flatness, the silence under the silence, the sense of a held breath that never released. The brothers said the Temple had been founded to guard the hollow's emptiness, to keep it pure, unsullied, a sanctuary from sorcery. Paloma had long ago formed her own theory, scribbled once in a margin and scrubbed out before the ink dried: that the valley didn't repel magic so much as it repelled *leaving*. Nothing changed here. Debts didn't dissolve. Prayers didn't rise. People arrived with burdens and the valley simply agreed to keep them, indefinitely, at a modest monthly rate.

Even ink obeyed. That was the joke she'd never dared make aloud: the Temple's famous copies were prized in the outside world precisely because nothing written in Vael Hollow could be altered by enchantment, glamoured, forged, or hexed. Ink made here was honest because it was trapped. Ink that cannot leave, ink that cannot change. Fourteen years of her life pressed between pages that would outlive her, accurate forever, while she stayed exactly where she was.

She blew out the kneeling-monk candle and walked the corridors up toward the novices' dormitory, past the chapel with its bare altar—no god was worshipped in Vael Hollow; gods were, the brothers explained, technically a form of magic, and the valley took a firm line on the subject—and out, on impulse, through the side door to the outer wall.

The night was sharp and enormous. Autumn stars in their cold multitudes, the black shoulder of the mountain, and below, spread along the valley floor, the town of Vael Hollow itself: three hundred rooftops, one road in, one road out, and the border stones a half-day's walk south, where the pilgrims stood and shivered and felt nothing happen, which was the entire miracle.

Paloma pulled her cloak around her and breathed the cold. Tomorrow night, the Council voted. Ten years. Unless—

Unless what? She was one woman with a pen. Her weapons were grammar and arithmetic. Against clause nineteen, what could she possibly—

A sound.

Faint, at first, so faint she took it for wind working at the gate hinges. But wind did not moan in rhythm, and wind did not stop and start the way breathing does when breathing is failing. Paloma leaned over the wall.

The outer gate of the Silent Temple stood at the end of the pilgrims' path, and it was shut—it was always shut after compline—but there was a shape crumpled against it. A man, by the size of him, dark clothes gone darker in places she could guess at even in this light, one arm stretched toward the gate as though he'd been crawling and had finally, simply, run out of will.

And on the air, thin as thread, came his voice. Not words yet. Just sound, rising and falling, rising and falling—and then, as the wind shifted and carried it whole to her, three words that meant nothing and everything:

"*...the thirteenth turn...*"

Paloma stood very still. Behind her lay the dormitory, the rules, the hearing tomorrow night, clause nineteen, the whole patient machinery of the valley designed to keep her exactly where she stood. Ahead, below, a dying stranger at a locked gate, in a valley where no magic could reach—which meant that whatever was bleeding out of him, whatever had chased him here, had been made somewhere else.

She was already moving toward the stairs.
