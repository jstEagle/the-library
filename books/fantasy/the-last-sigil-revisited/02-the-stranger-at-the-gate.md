## 2. The Stranger at the Gate

The bells of the Silent Temple did not ring. That was rather the point of them.

Paloma had lived in Vael Hollow for six years now, and she still found the silence of the place less like quiet and more like a held breath—a valley so deep in magic's absence that even sound seemed to move cautiously, as though it might wake something. The temple's bronze bells hung in their tower, green with age and never touched by rope or hand. The brothers said they had not rung in four hundred years, since the last time anything holy had happened here, which by all accounts was also the last time anything at all had happened here.

She copied ledgers.

That was her life, distilled to its essence: she copied ledgers. Tithes owed, candles purchased, roof slates replaced, the endless arithmetic of a temple that had sworn silence and apparently interpreted this as a vow against excitement as well. Her candle stub—temple property, logged, rationed, and docked from her wages if burned past the quarter-hour—guttered on its brass dish while she worked through the autumn accounts of Brother Aldous's herb garden, in which he had recorded, with scholarly precision, the theft of eleven carrots by persons unknown.

*Persons unknown,* Paloma wrote in her neat copy-hand, and then, because no one could hear her thoughts and the valley could not either, added silently: *It was the goat, Brother Aldous. It is always the goat.*

Her wrist ached. She flexed it without looking up. Six years of copying had given her a scribe's callus, a scribe's squint, and a scribe's particular talent for measuring time in pages rather than hours. Three more pages and the candle died. Fourteen more days and the auditor came.

The thought arrived the way cold water finds a crack in a wall—slowly, then all at once—and Paloma bent lower over her desk as if proximity to the ink could hold it off.

Debt-auditor Magister Corven Hale came twice a year, in spring and in the dying of autumn, and he carried with him a set of scales so fine they could weigh a hair's difference in a ledger line. He would sit in the abbot's parlor, drink one measured cup of tea, and review her bond: two hundred forty crowns remaining, of the three hundred her father had borrowed against her future when the fever took her mother and the winter took the farm. Six years served. At her current rate—room, board, and two copper pennies a month applied against principal—she had calculated, on a scrap of paper hidden beneath a loose floorboard, that she would be free at forty-one.

Except.

Except that last spring, Magister Hale had smiled—not a cruel smile, he was far too professional for cruelty—and observed that her copy-work had grown *exceptionally* fine, and that exceptional work commanded an exceptional rate, and that perhaps her indenture should be renegotiated upward to reflect the value she now produced. A decade's extension. He had left the papers with Abbot Serene, who had neither signed nor refused them, because Abbot Serene believed all decisions improved with sufficient delay, and had been proven right only twice.

The papers sat in the abbot's cabinet like a knife in a drawer. Everyone knew where it was. No one wanted to be the one to open it.

Paloma blew out her candle at precisely the permitted moment, closed her ledger, and sat for a moment in the dark listening to the valley hold its breath.

*One more year,* she told herself, as she told herself every night. *One more year, and then the next, and then the next.* It was not hope, exactly. It was more like a rope thrown to a drowning woman that turned out, on inspection, to be made of the same water she was drowning in.

She went down to sleep in the sisters' cell, and the valley kept its silence around her like a cupped hand, and nothing holy happened, and nothing at all happened, until three nights later, when everything began.

* * *

Frost had come early that year. On the morning it happened, Paloma was carrying the wash-water up from the cistern—the task rotated among the indentured, and this week it was hers—when the dog began to bark.

This required explanation, and got none. There was no dog. There had been no dog for six years. The temple kept cats for the mice and goats for Brother Aldous's despair, but dogs were loud, and loud things were not welcome in Vael Hollow, and so there was no dog, and yet a dog was barking somewhere down the switchback road that climbed out of the valley, a thin frantic sound bouncing off the slate cliffs like a dropped bell.

Then a man's voice, cracked and distant, shouting words she couldn't make out. Then the barking again, closer, wilder.

And then, impossibly, the dog itself came over the ridge—a lean brown thing with frost on its whiskers and terror in every line of it—and bolted past Paloma so close she felt the wind of it, straight through the outer gate and into the courtyard, where it stopped, looked back the way it had come, and began to howl.

Brother Fenwick appeared from the gatehouse with the speed of a man who has been waiting his whole life for something to go wrong. "Close the gate," he said. "Close the gate."

"The gate's already—" Paloma began.

"Then close it *again.*"

She set down her wash-water and helped him haul the great oak doors inward, and it was through the narrowing gap between them that she saw him: a figure coming down the switchback road at a stumbling half-run, dark against the pale frost, leaving a trail behind him that was not snowmelt.

He fell twice on the last stretch. The second time he did not get up so much as continue arriving, hands first, dragging himself the final twenty feet to the threshold of the gate, where he collapsed against the oak with a sound like a sack of wet grain.

Up close, in the grey morning light, he was worse than he had looked from a distance. His coat had been good once—travelling wool, capital cut, the kind of garment that cost what Paloma earned in a decade—but it was ruined now, torn along one sleeve and soaked through the left side with blood that had frozen to a dull black crust. His face was grey. His lips were the blue-grey of old pewter. One hand clutched a leather tube to his chest the way a drowning man clutches driftwood, knuckles bloodless, fingers locked.

"Water," Paloma said, before anyone asked her opinion. "He needs—"

"He needs nothing from us," said Brother Fenwick, who had already positioned himself between the man and the gap in the doors with the righteous firmness of a man defending not a principle but a schedule. "You know the rule, sister. Nothing crosses the threshold that carries the taint. Nothing comes *in*."

"He's bleeding, Brother Fenwick, not bewitched."

"Bleeding men carry their own kinds of trouble." Fenwick's jaw was set. Behind him, other brothers had begun to gather—drawn by the dog, the shouting, the smell of blood, all the things the valley was supposed to keep out—and they formed a silent crescent of disapproval. Abbot Serene arrived last, robed against the cold, and stood regarding the dying man with the expression of one reading a mildly inconvenient letter.

"Thirteenth," the man said.

Everyone stopped. Even the dog, pressed flat under the courtyard wall, seemed to stop breathing.

His eyes had found Paloma—or perhaps they had simply found the nearest face, though something in the way they fixed on her suggested otherwise, something urgent and specific that raised the small hairs on her arms. His voice was a ruin, gravel dragged over gravel.

"Thirteenth turn," he rasped. "It comes. You don't—nobody listens. Nobody ever *listens*." A bubble of red formed at the corner of his mouth. "Count the moons. Count them and you'll see. Twelve and then—one missing. One *taken*."

"Delirium," said Brother Fenwick comfortably.

"Sir," Abbot Serene said, in the tone he used for beggars, tax collectors, and weather, "this is a house of silence. We cannot shelter you. There is a waystation at Merrow's Cross, two days east. If you can walk—"

"I can't walk." The man laughed, and it was a terrible sound, wet and short. "No. No, I've come too far to walk anywhere else." His hand shot out—faster than it should have been able to move—and caught the hem of Abbot Serene's robe. "Father. Father, I am asking. I have walked out of the capital. Out of *Aurelholm*. Past the checkpoints. Past the—do you know what watches the roads now? Do you know what wears the king?" He coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it ended he was laughing again, or crying; at this distance the two sounded alike. "Three years. Three years I've carried it. And the moons, father, count the *moons*—"

"Let go of my robe, please."

Abbot Serene extracted his hem with two courteous fingers, the way one might retrieve it from a puddle. The man's hand fell open on the frost. He did not seem surprised. That was the worst part, Paloma thought later. He did not seem surprised at all. He lay back against the gatepost as though he had known exactly how this scene would end before he'd ever started climbing the road, as though he had simply needed to say his piece aloud to someone, anyone, before the end—and the Silent Temple of Vael Hollow, holiest refuge in the valley, had declined the role.

"We will pray for him," Abbot Serene announced to the brothers generally. "From a respectful distance. Sister Hartwell, your water is freezing. See to it."

It was a dismissal. She knew a dismissal; she had been collecting them for six years. She picked up her wash-water buckets and turned toward the cistern, and the brothers began to disperse, and the man in the gateway closed his eyes, and the whole machinery of the temple moved smoothly, quietly, mercifully on—

Paloma stopped.

She stood very still between the buckets, water slopping gently against her ankles, and looked at the man in the gate. At the frozen blood. At the leather tube crushed against his chest. At the way his breath now came in long, rattling pauses, each one a little longer than the last.

*Nothing crosses the threshold that carries the taint.* Fine. Rules were rules; she had broken exactly none of them in six years, and the ledger of her obedience was the one asset she owned outright.

But the gate was a threshold. The gate was not the world.

She turned, walked out through the gap in the doors, and knelt in the frost beside him.

Behind her she heard Brother Fenwick's indrawn breath and Abbot Serene's soft, appalled, "*Sister*," but the valley could not stop her and neither, it turned out, could she. She unstoppered the small water-flask at her belt—it was meant for the climb to the cistern, and she would pay for it, she always paid for everything—and held it to the stranger's cracked lips.

"Slowly," she said. "Small sips."

His eyes opened. Up close they were brown going yellow at the rims, fever-bright, and they studied her face with an intensity that made her want to look away and made her refuse to.

"You're the one," he whispered.

"I'm nobody," Paloma said. "Drink."

"You're the—" A cough took him, doubled him nearly in half; she steadied his shoulder and felt the heat of the fever burning through the ruined wool. When it passed he sagged against the gatepost, smiling, which frightened her more than the blood had. "They told me you'd be young. They told me—" Another cough. "Doesn't matter. Doesn't matter what they told me. What matters is the thirteenth turn, girl. The count is wrong. It's been wrong for three years and everyone pretends—" His grip found her wrist, shocking hot, and squeezed with the last of his strength. "Everyone pretends the sky is honest."

"Who's *they*? Who told you about me?"

But his eyes had drifted, unfocusing, tracking something above the gatehouse or beyond it, and whatever he saw there made his smile falter into something rawer. "Not enough time," he murmured. "Never enough. I was meant to reach the archive. Vesha would have—I was meant to reach *Vesha*—" He blinked, refocused, and his gaze sharpened on her again with terrible purpose. "Listen. Listen to me. When it burns—when the map wakes—you'll feel it in your blood, and you'll think you're dying, and you must not stop walking. Whatever you see. Whatever you become afraid of. You walk *toward* it. Do you understand me?"

"I don't understand a single word you're saying."

"No." He laughed softly. Blood threaded from the corner of his mouth. "No, you won't. Not yet." His hand slid from her wrist to the leather tube clutched against his chest, and with fingers that shook and slipped, he began working it loose from his own death-grip. "I carried it thirty years. My mother carried it before me. Before that—" He winced. "Long chain. Long, stupid, brave chain of nobodies, and now it's yours, and I am so sorry, and I am so *relieved*, and I don't have the strength to apologize properly, so you'll have to be angry on both our accounts."

"Sir, please, I'm just a scribe, I have debts, I can't—"

"Nobody can," he agreed warmly. "That's rather the qualification."

The tube came free. It was lighter than it looked, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with handling, and when he pressed it into her hands he held on a moment after she'd taken it, so that for one strange heartbeat they held it together, a relay passing mid-stride.

"Hide it," he breathed. "Tonight, before they sense it moving. They'll have felt it cross the border by tomorrow. Hide it, and don't read it, and don't—"

The gates groaned.

Paloma looked up. Down the switchback road, small against the frost, riders had appeared—four, maybe five, dark cloaks, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been refused entry anywhere. The dog under the wall began to tremble.

The stranger saw them too. Something eased in his face, some last tension letting go. "Ah," he said. "There you are." And then, to Paloma, urgently, his hand closing over hers around the tube: "The thirteenth moon. Count them yourself. Trust the sky over any man wearing a crown. And when you meet the knight—"

"Meet the *what*?"

"—tell him Caelmoor wasn't his fault. Tell him he was lied to. Tell him—" A shudder ran through him, head to heel, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "Tell him I forgive him. He'll need to hear it said aloud. Stubborn man. Wonderful, stubborn, guilty man."

The riders were close enough now that Paloma could see no insignia on their cloaks, which somehow was worse than any insignia could have been.

"Go," the stranger said. "Now. Inside. Be nobody. It's the safest thing you'll ever be, and the hardest."

She should have run. Every instinct she owned, every rule she'd ever kept, screamed *go inside, be nobody, let the temple's silence swallow this whole day.* Instead she heard herself say, "What do I do with it?"

His smile was the last thing she ever saw of him clearly.

"Carry it," he said.

Then the brothers were surging forward—Abbot Serene's voice rising in protest at last, too late, about thresholds and taints and the sanctity of the valley—and hands were pulling Paloma back through the gates, and the last glimpse she had of the stranger was of him settling against the gatepost with the profound contentment of a man finally allowed to rest, watching the riders come down the road as calmly as if they were family arriving for supper.

The gates boomed shut. Bolts slammed home. And in the sudden hush, Paloma Hartwell stood in the courtyard with a dead man's map crushed against her chest, her heart hammering out a rhythm the valley's silence could not cover, while outside, unhurried footsteps crossed the frost toward the door, and stopped.

A knock. Polite. Almost gentle.

"Sisters and brothers of the Silent Temple," said a voice through the oak, smooth as poured wax, "we've come about a book."
