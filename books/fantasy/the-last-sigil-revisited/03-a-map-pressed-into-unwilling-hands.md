## 3. A Map Pressed Into Unwilling Hands

The water helped for exactly as long as it took him to swallow it.

Paloma watched the color come back into his lips—a thin, dishonest pink that lasted perhaps a minute—and then watched it drain away again like ink rinsing off a stylus. The man lay against the outer gate's stone post where she had dragged him, half in shadow, half in the last grey light of evening, and his breathing had gone from ragged to shallow to something with long pauses between each pull.

"No," he said, when she reached for the water skin again. "Save it." His hand closed around her wrist—not gripping, just resting there, the way a man rests his hand on a table he intends to stand up from and never does. "Listen. There isn't time. There was never time; I only thought there'd be more."

"You need a healer," Paloma said, because it was the sort of thing one said. "I can fetch Brother Aldous—"

"Brother Aldous will call the guards, and the guards will burn whatever I give you, and then three years of running will have been for a very tidy funeral." His mouth twitched, which she supposed was his version of a smile. It was not a good version. "You're the scribe. The one who copies ledgers past curfew because the numbers won't leave her alone until they're *right*."

Paloma went very still.

She had never seen this man before in her life. She was certain of it the way she was certain of her own handwriting—certain down to the small flourishes. He was forty-some years old, road-worn, grey at the temples, with a scar through his left eyebrow and boots that had been resoled at least twice by someone who didn't much care how they looked afterward. Nobody she knew. Nobody from anywhere she had ever been.

And yet he knew about the ledgers.

"How—"

"There's no time," he said again, and this time it came out as almost a laugh. "You're going to keep saying that to yourself for weeks, so let me save you the trouble: no, there wasn't another way. Yes, it's unfair. No, I don't know your whole story. I know enough." His grip on her wrist tightened by a fraction. "Paloma Hartwell."

The valley seemed to lean in around them. Vael Hollow did that sometimes—the mountains cupped sound the way a bowl cups water, so that even the wind felt eavesdropped upon. Somewhere behind her, inside the temple walls, the evening bell rang its flat, toneless note. In Vael Hollow, bells could not ring true. Nothing here could. Magic died at the ridgeline like a moth against glass, and everything within the valley was muted, flattened, honest in the way that only things incapable of pretense are honest.

Her name, though. Her name sounded the same here as anywhere.

"That's my name," she said stupidly.

"It is. And in about four minutes it's going to be the most dangerous word in the kingdom, so I'd ask you to hold onto it carefully." He fumbled at his belt with his free hand. His fingers weren't working properly; she could see him willing them to move and watching them fail, the way she used to watch her father try to rise from his sickbed in those last weeks. "Tube. My right side. Take it—no, don't open it. Don't open it here. Don't open it under any roof of theirs."

"The temple's roof?"

"Any roof they've blessed. Which is all of them." He managed, at last, to drag the object loose: a leather map-tube, capped in brass gone green with age, scuffed along one side as though it had been dropped down a good many staircases in its life. It was warm. That was the first thing she noticed and immediately tried to unnotice. The evening air in Vael Hollow carried a chill that got into everything—stone, cloth, bone—but the tube sat in her palms like something freshly baked.

Nothing in the valley should have been warm. Nothing in the valley could hold warmth overnight, or hold a charge of light, or hold anything at all beyond what ordinary physics deigned to permit. The Silent Temple had stood for nine hundred years precisely because magic could not enter Vael Hollow, which made it the safest place in the world to hide things that needed hiding—from kings, from sorcerers, from history itself.

And this dying stranger had carried a warm tube across the ridgeline anyway.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"A map," he said. "Of course it's a map. Everything important is either a map or a ledger, and you strike me as someone who understands both." His eyes were glazing now; she could see him hauling his attention back from somewhere far away, again and again, like a man dragging buckets up a well. "Listen to me. Listen. When you're beyond the valley—when you cross the salt-flats, past the third cairn—"

"I'm not crossing anything," Paloma said. "I'm indentured. I have eleven years left on my bond. If I set one foot outside the boundary stones without writ, the debt-collectors—"

"—will find you whether you stay or go, once they learn what you're carrying. That's rather the point." He coughed then, wetly, and she flinched. "I'm sorry. I truly am. I spent two years looking for a way to deliver this without involving anyone, and the answer turned out to be *die next to a scribe*, because apparently the world has a sense of humor after all."

"This is a joke to you?"

"No." The laugh went out of him entirely. What remained was worse: a plain, tired sincerity. "It's an apology. I'm sorry, Paloma Hartwell. You were quiet and careful and you gave water to a stranger when your rules said not to, and that kindness is going to cost you everything you've built. I would undo it if I could. But if I could undo it, I'd be undoing the only chance anyone has."

Somewhere up the hillside, gravel shifted. Or perhaps it didn't. Paloma's heart had begun doing arithmetic on its own—*guards make their rounds at dusk-bell, dusk-bell was four minutes ago, the gate is thirty paces from the refectory door*—and every sum came out badly.

"Tell me what it is," she said. "If I'm to be ruined, tell me what ruins me."

He smiled properly this time, briefly. "There she is. The ledger-keeper wants the entry written before the audit." He drew a breath that whistled. "It's a key. To the capital. To the palace—to what sits beneath the throne room. Your king has been dead behind his eyes for three years, girl, and something else has been wearing him to court. This—" his chin dipped toward the tube, "—is how you unmake it. The sigil-key. The binding was cast wrong on purpose, see, so it could be unwound, but it needs... it needs..."

His voice failed. Not from weakness—from something that looked horribly like fear.

"It needs what?" she pressed.

"A bearer," he whispered. "A living bearer. Written in blood. That's why I—why they hunted me, why I ran, why I—" His eyes fixed on hers with sudden, terrible clarity, the way a lamp flares before the oil runs out. "Do you understand why I said your name? Do you understand why I knew it? Check your left wrist, scribe. Check it now."

Paloma did not want to check her left wrist.

Her left wrist had been perfectly ordinary her entire life: pale, ink-stained at the edges from years of copying, prone to aching in cold weather. She checked it anyway, because he asked, and because some part of her had already known since the moment he spoke her name—some part of her had been holding that knowledge at arm's length the way one holds a letter that is obviously bad news.

Beneath the smudge of iron-gall ink, in fine lines she had never seen before and yet somehow recognized the hand of, dark red traced a pattern. A circle. Within it, smaller marks, deliberate as script, precise as anything she had ever copied from a master's fair hand. It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and it was growing out of her own skin, and it was *warm*.

"Oh," she said. Very quietly. It was not a word so much as a structural failure.

"Don't faint," the stranger said urgently. "Faint later. Faint in a week. Right now I need you to hear the rest, because they're coming—I can feel the horses, or I could, back when I could feel things—and you must hide it and say nothing and *live*, do you hear me? Living is the mission now. You're the map's final page. Keep yourself safe and the key stays safe. Lose yourself and—" He coughed again, longer this time, and when he finished there was blood on his lip and less of him left than there had been. "Thirteenth turn," he breathed. "Before the thirteenth moon turns. Remember. Five weeks. Find someone who knows the old astronomy. Trust no one who offers to carry it for you. And when they ask—whoever asks, however kindly—if you ever doubt, ask them what they lost at Caelmoor. Anyone who flinches at that name is either an enemy or..."

He trailed off. His mouth kept moving for another few seconds, shaping words with no breath behind them. Then it stopped.

The valley was very silent. The Silent Temple kept its silence the way it kept everything: absolutely, and on purpose. Even the wind seemed to have signed a vow. Paloma knelt beside a dead man at her temple's gate with a warm tube in her lap and a burning sigil on her wrist, and the only sound in all the world was her own breathing, which she realized, distantly, had gone quick and high and not entirely voluntary.

*Faint later,* she told herself. *Faint in a week.*

Then the dogs started baying in the kennels, and she stopped having time for internal monologue of any kind.

---

Here is what nobody tells you about panic: it makes you meticulous.

Paloma had spent six years in the temple archives learning to work while terrified—terrified of the Auditor's quarterly reviews, terrified of miscounting a column, terrified of the particular silence Brother Aldous deployed when she had disappointed him. Fear, she had discovered, could be refined like tallow into a steady, usable flame. So while the larger part of her mind screamed in small circles, some older, calmer Paloma took inventory:

*Dead man at the gate. Cannot move him alone; cannot leave him visible. Guards incoming—two minutes, perhaps three, if the dogs woke them late. Tube must not be found on me. Sigil must not be seen. Water skin is evidence. Kneeling position is evidence. Everything is evidence.*

She wiped the water skin clean of fingerprints—an absurd gesture, she knew; the temple did not teach fingerprint lore, but it felt right—and tucked it under her shawl. She dragged the stranger's cloak straight, arranged his limbs with the grim efficiency of someone who had laid out her father, and folded his hands over his chest as though in prayer. It was a lie done well, and she hated herself for how well she did it.

The tube would not fit up her sleeve. She tried. It fit instead down the front of her robe, against her stomach, where its warmth sat like a second heartbeat and where any searcher would have to touch her to find it, which bought her perhaps ten seconds of hesitation she might desperately need.

Then she did the bravest and stupidest thing available to her: she stayed.

Running would look like guilt. Guilt would invite questions. Questions were death. So Paloma Hartwell, indentured scribe of the Silent Temple of Vael Hollow, knelt beside the corpse at the gate, bowed her head, and began—in a clear, carrying voice that would reach the guards before they rounded the wall—to recite the Rite for the Unshriven Dead.

She did not know it perfectly. She had copied it forty times and memorized perhaps half, and she filled the gaps with psalm-verses from the Ledger of Hours, and she was quite sure Brother Aldous would have corrected her phrasing seven times in the first minute. It did not matter. What mattered was what it *looked like*: a dutiful sister of the temple, found weeping over a poor wanderer, praying for his soul.

What it actually was, was a woman buying time with borrowed holiness, while against her stomach a dead man's secret pulsed warm as a coal, and beneath her cuff a circle of red script burned faintly, patiently, like a word waiting to be read aloud.

The lantern-light came around the wall. Two temple guards, spears sloped, faces set in the expressions of men whose temple forbade speech and therefore made every encounter an exercise in elaborate mime. Behind them, unhappily, trotted Brother Fenwick, the night steward, whose vows of silence had never quite extended to disapproval—he could convey an entire homily with one raised eyebrow.

Paloma rose, hands open and empty, eyes suitably red.

Brother Fenwick looked at the body. Looked at her. Looked at the body again. His eyebrow performed a slow ascent that communicated, roughly, *explain yourself, and also I am deeply disappointed, and also explain yourself.*

"He came to the gate dying," Paloma said. Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "I was returning from the archive—I heard him fall. I gave him water. I—" she faltered, artfully, "I couldn't save him. I was praying."

More eyebrow. *Why were you outside after dusk-bell?*

"I lost track of the hour," she said, which was true, and therefore unimpeachable, and also a lie, because the truth was that she had been hiding in the gatehouse alcove waiting for the corridor to clear so she could speak to a dying stranger whom no rule permitted her to speak to. Truth was such a flexible material. She was beginning to suspect she'd been trained in its misuse all along; it was called bookkeeping.

One of the guards knelt by the body, prodded it with professional irreverence, then looked up sharply and gestured—at the gatepost, at the ground, at a scatter of something dark near the stranger's side. Blood. Of course, blood. The guard mimed a blade-draw, then pointed down the road, toward the ridgeline, toward the world outside.

*Wounded by violence. Fled from outside.* Fenwick's face performed a slow calculation, and Paloma watched the conclusion arrive in real time: *an outlaw, pursued, dead at our gate, and trouble walking up the mountain behind him on horseback.*

Trouble meant the Abbot. Trouble meant the Auditor, who arrived whenever trouble did, drawn to disorder the way she was drawn to unpaid debts. Trouble meant questions, searches, lanterns held close to frightened faces.

*Don't open it under any roof of theirs,* the stranger had said.

Paloma clasped her hands, bowed her head, and let the brothers do what silent brothers do best. They carried the body inside on a litter, and Fenwick waved her along perfunctorily—*back to your cell, sister, this is above your station*—and she obeyed with downcast eyes and measured steps, and if her gait was slightly stiff, well, grief stiffens anyone.

In the corridor between the gatehouse and the sisters' cells, she passed the little alcove where the novices stored candle-stubs. Without breaking stride—an act of larceny so smooth it startled her—she lifted a stub and a twist of wick and let them vanish up her sleeve.

Because she had thought of something, there in the gate-lantern light, something that changed the shape of the night entirely.

The stranger had said *check your wrist*. Had said *you are the map's final page*. Which meant the tube was not merely a container. It was a text. And Paloma Hartwell had spent six years learning that a text could be studied, transcribed, understood—*mastered*. Whatever was hunting her across the mountains, whatever wore the king like a garment, whatever price this secret demanded—it would demand it in writing. And writing was the one territory in all the world where she had never once been helpless.

She reached her cell, barred the door, and sat on the narrow cot in the dark, not lighting the candle stub. Not yet. Under any roof of theirs, he'd said. Well. Her cell was eight feet of stone with a straw tick and a shelf, and it belonged to the temple as thoroughly as she did. But it was *hers* tonight, tonight only, and she was owed one night of cowardice before the courage started.

Against her ribs, the tube was still warm. On her wrist, the sigil had faded to a ghost of red, patient as marginalia.

*Five weeks,* the stranger had said. *Before the thirteenth moon.*

Paloma stared into the darkness and did the thing she always did when the world became unbearable: she began keeping a ledger. *Assets: one map (unexamined), one sigil (unexplained), one dead man's trust (unearned). Liabilities: eleven years' bond, one Auditor's suspicion, one body at the gate with my footprints all around it.* She almost laughed, alone in the dark, and the almost-laugh cracked something loose in her chest, and for a moment she was simply a twenty-four-year-old woman sitting in a cold cell, more frightened than she had ever been, and more alive than she had ever been, both at once, in the same small body.

Below the window, torches moved across the courtyard. Voices—real voices, spoken aloud, which meant officials—which meant the Auditor had been woken.

And through the wall, muffled but unmistakable, she heard Brother Fenwick's rare, aloud voice saying the words that turned her blood to ice water:

"...and the sister Hartwell was found with the body. Send for the Auditor. She'll want to question the girl herself."

* * *

Paloma sat motionless until the footsteps faded, counting her heartbeats the way she counted ledger lines—one hundred, one hundred and one—as if accuracy could hold the night together.

Then she slid the tube from her robe, and in the dark, with fingers that shook only a little, she unscrewed the cap.

Inside, coiled tight, was a strip of leather a finger's width wide and impossibly long. Even unlit, even in a cell with no moon, the markings on it were faintly visible—lines and characters that seemed to sit *slightly deeper* than the surface of the leather, as though written from underneath the world. She tilted it. The lines tilted with her, always facing her eye, like a portrait's gaze.

And at the bottom edge of the strip, in the same buried hand, in letters she recognized because she had seen them an hour ago on her own wrist, was a single line of script that she could not read and yet, somehow, *understood*, the way one understands a mother's tone before the words resolve:

*Bearer: P. Hartwell.*

Someone had sold her ahead of herself. Someone had written her into this story before she was born into it, or at least before tonight—and had spelled her name correctly, which was more than the Auditor ever bothered to do.

From the courtyard below rose the clip of hooves: fresh horses, arriving fast, carrying someone important and displeased up the dark mountain.

Paloma re-coiled the strip, capped the tube, and pushed it deep beneath the loose flagstone by her cot, where the mice had long ago excavated a cavity she'd always intended to report and never had. She smoothed the dust flat over it, retrieved her ledger and stylus from the shelf, and opened it to a blank page as the knock came at her door—three slow raps, official, inevitable.

"Sister Hartwell," said a voice like dry paper, "the Auditor will see you now."

Paloma rose, smoothed her robes, and picked up her ledger—for the first time in her life not as a weapon of compliance, but as a shield. Let the Auditor search a scribe. Scribes, everyone knew, were furniture: quiet, useful, harmless.

Everyone, it turned out, was about to be wrong.
