## 4. What the Ledger Cannot Hold

The rain found Sef Corvane two miles short of the valley and seemed to take it personally.

It came down in fine, needling sheets that soaked through his cloak, through the shirt beneath the cloak, and — he was fairly certain — through his regrets as well, which had always been porous things. By the time he crested the last ridge above Vael Hollow, he was wet to the bone and in no mood for beauty, which was unfortunate, because Vael Hollow was beautiful the way a held breath is beautiful: green and deep and utterly silent, ringed by hills so steep they looked like cupped hands, as though the world itself had decided this valley was something worth keeping safe.

Or keeping *in*, said a voice in his head that sounded unpleasantly like his old commander.

Sef knelt at the treeline, out of habit more than sense, and looked down at the temple.

The Silent Temple of Vael Hollow sat in the valley's floor like a stone boat anchored in grass — low-walled, slate-roofed, its bell tower conspicuously empty of bells. No smoke rose from it that wasn't cookfire smoke. No light showed that wasn't candlelight. He could see figures moving in the cloister garden, gray-robed and slow, tending herbs or graves or whatever it was silent monks tended when they weren't busy not talking. The whole place radiated the particular smugness of an institution that had solved all its problems by refusing to have conversations.

Three years ago, Sef Corvane would have ridden straight up to the gate, announced himself, and been given bread and a bed by sundown, because that was what happened to knights-errant of the realm. Three years ago, he'd had a name worth announcing.

Now he had a horse named Turnip — named by a stable girl in Lowmarsh, and renamed by nobody because Sef lacked the energy to fight about it — and a face he kept under the hood, and one purpose left, walking on legs that had been walking for eleven days.

*Find the map. Burn the map. Then you can stop.*

That had been the oath. His own oath, spoken over a grave in the rain very like this rain, with his hand pressed flat against cold earth: *I will find it before they do, and I will end it.* He had repeated it every morning since like a prayer, and like most prayers it had done absolutely nothing except keep him moving.

He'd tracked the stranger from Caelmoor.

The thought of Caelmoor still landed in his chest like a dropped stone. He pushed past it, the way he did every day, the way a man pushes past a door he has nailed shut and then checks the nails on anyway.

The stranger — Aldous Merrin, once cartographer to the royal court, once the only other living soul who knew what lay beneath the throne room — had run well for a man of fifty with a bad knee. Sef had followed him through three towns and one genuinely impressive act of bribery at a river toll, always two days behind, then one, then half. And then, four days ago, on the salt road within sight of the valley's rim, Sef had found the place where Merrin stopped running.

There had been blood on the stones. A great deal of it, and not all of it Merrin's — whoever had caught him had paid for the privilege. But there had been something else, too, something that had turned Sef's stomach colder than the rain ever could: the marks on the stones around the blood were *sigils*. Erasure-sigils, half-finished, the kind the Crowned's agents used to scrub a man's name out of every record, every memory, every ledger in the land. They hadn't just killed Aldous Merrin. They had begun the work of making sure he had never existed.

Except someone had dragged him. There was a smear trail, faint, leading toward the valley — toward a gate where magic could not follow, where erasure-sigils went dull and dead, where a dying man might yet remain himself for another hour.

Sef had stood over those stones in the wind and understood, with the clarity that only arrives after everything else has gone wrong, that he was too late. Again. That word again. It had a whole vocabulary built around it by now. Too late at Caelmoor. Too late at the king's bedside. Too late here, on a wet road, kneeling beside the unfinished death of the last honest mapmaker in Aurelholm.

But dead men who die carrying secrets usually try very hard, in their final hours, to make those secrets somebody else's problem. And a temple full of silent monks was the worst possible place to hide anything, which meant it was also the best possible place, because nobody ever searched the one spot that made them uncomfortable.

So Sef had spent four days being uncomfortable on a ridge.

* * *

He learned the valley's rhythms the way he'd once learned the rhythms of the palace guard rotations — by watching until boredom became knowledge. The monks rose before dawn. They ate in silence. They worked in silence. They slept, presumably in silence, though Sef had privately wondered whether they snored and simply couldn't apologize to each other for it.

And among them moved one figure who was not a monk.

She wore gray like the others but the wrong gray — newer, harder-worn, the robe of someone whose clothes were issued rather than chosen. She carried things. She was always carrying things: ledgers, ink pots, bundles of fresh vellum, baskets of onions (once; Sef had watched her haul an entire basket of onions across the courtyard with the expression of a woman renegotiating her relationship with God). She walked fast everywhere, even places she clearly went ten times a day, as if she might outrun the buildings themselves if she tried hard enough.

The first time Sef saw her face properly, through his spyglass, across a quarter mile of rain, he felt something turn over in his gut.

Not recognition. He'd never seen her before; he was certain of it. It was something worse. It was the look on her face — the particular set of a jaw clenched around words it wasn't allowed to say, eyes that catalogued everything and owned nothing. He knew that face. He'd worn it for three years in every reflective surface he passed.

*That,* he'd told himself, *is a person in a cage who hasn't admitted the door is right there.*

Then he'd told himself it was none of his business, which was true, and had never once stopped anything from being his business anyway.

Her name he learned on the second day, from a drover watering his mules at the ridge spring, a talkative man who talked mostly to his mules and considered Sef close enough.

"Temple scribes, they're indentured," the drover said. "Debt-bonded. Whole valley runs on it. That one there—" he nodded toward the fast-walking figure, "—that's the Hartwell girl. Paloma. Best hand in the scriptorium, they say. Copies ledgers faster than the abbot can find sins to enter in 'em." He chuckled at his own wit; the mules did not. "Ten years on her bond, I heard. Maybe twelve now."

"Twelve," Sef repeated.

"Debt-auditors come round twice a year. Add interest, add penalties. Man could copy his whole life and owe more at the end than the start." The drover shrugged with the vast indifference of a free man discussing bondage. "Temple calls it holy work. Auditor calls it arithmetic. Girl probably calls it nothing. Can't, can she? Sworn to silence." He laughed again, alone, and led his mules off downhill, still narrating.

Sef stayed on the ridge a long time after that, watching the small gray figure cross the small gray courtyard, and felt the old guilt shift inside him the way a wound shifts when the weather changes.

Here was the thing he had learned about guilt, these three years: it was not a passive thing. Everyone imagined it as a weight you carried. It wasn't. It was a *hunger*. It wanted feeding. It would eat your sleep, your appetite, your name, and when it had finished with all that it would start looking around for a purpose, and if you didn't give it one it would make one up, and it always made up the same one.

*You failed. Find something to fail at correctly, and maybe the ledger balances.*

A ledger. He almost laughed. Everything came back to ledgers. Debts entered, debts owed, sins recorded in neat columns by girls with quick hands who couldn't speak. Somewhere beneath the palace in Aurelholm there was a binding written in a dead language on a living king, and it too was a ledger — an entry, a transaction, a cost agreed upon by men in a warm room while the rain fell somewhere else. He knew, because he had been one of the men in the warm room. He had held the door. He had believed — God help him, he had *believed* — that they were saving the king's life.

They had saved something. It just wasn't the king.

*Burn the map,* he reminded himself. *Find it, burn it, and then the ledger closes.* Not balanced. Ledgers like that never balanced. But closed. Closed was all anyone got.

* * *

On the third night, the moon came out from behind the rain clouds, thin and white and one night shy of full, and Sef saw the glow.

He'd gone down closer than usual — reckless, and he knew it, but four days of onion-hauling surveillance had frayed his patience to threads, and he wanted a look at the gate where Merrin had died. The outer gate of the temple stood open to the night as temples' gates did, because a valley without magic needed no walls against sorcery and the monks feared only the ordinary sins of ordinary men. Someone had left a lamp burning in the gatehouse niche. And kneeling in that lamplight, sleeves rolled, washing something in a basin of water —

— was the Hartwell girl.

And on her left wrist, where the sleeve had fallen back, her skin was *lit*.

Not brightly. Anyone else would have missed it entirely; Sef nearly missed it, and he'd spent three years learning to see exactly this. It was a glow like a coal remembered rather than seen — gold threaded with a color he had no good name for, a color that lived slightly to the left of visible, the color the sigils had burned in the binding chamber at Caelmoor. It moved as she moved. When her hand dipped into the basin, the light swirled along lines on her skin, fine lines, deliberate lines, in patterns that were not veins and not scars and not anything a body ought to grow on its own.

Sef forgot how to breathe.

Because he knew those patterns. He had watched a royal magus draw them — smaller, cruder, incomplete — onto parchment by candlelight while a knight he trusted stood guard at the door and asked no questions, because asking questions felt like disloyalty and disloyalty felt like the assassination they were all so afraid of.

The map. Merrin's map — the sigil-key, the master diagram of the binding, the thing Sef had sworn over a grave to destroy — was not in the temple archives. It was not sewn into a saddle. It was not buried under the third flagstone of the gatehouse, which Sef had checked, at considerable risk to his dignity, on the first night.

It was on her.

Written into her. *Growing* in her, the way ink grows into blotting paper, the way roots grow into the foundations of a house until the house and the tree are the same argument. Merrin hadn't hidden the map from the Crowned's hunters.

Merrin had *passed it on.*

"Oh, you absolute fool," Sef whispered to the dead man, and meant both of them, and possibly everyone alive.

The girl finished washing — he couldn't even tell what; her hands looked clean to begin with, the hands of someone who washed them because it was the one part of the day no auditor could tax — and she rose, and snuffed the lamp, and walked back across the courtyard fast, always fast, carrying nothing now but the basin and, unknowing, the fate of a kingdom on the inside of her wrist.

Sef sat back against a pine trunk in the dark and let the rain have him for a while.

He should go down tonight. That was the disciplined play. Wake the abbot, invent a pilgrimage, get inside, get the girl aside, tell her — carefully, gently, in the manner of a man delivering news that ruins a life — that she was carrying a weapon pointed at the heart of the realm, and that the people who killed Aldous Merrin would come for her next, and that the kindest thing he could offer was to cut the sigil out of her history before it finished writing itself.

He imagined telling her. He imagined the look on that clenched-jaw face. He imagined saying *you are not a person anymore, you are a page, and pages exist to be destroyed,* and hearing exactly how it sounded, and understanding that she would hear it too.

And underneath the discipline, quieter, the other voice — the hunger, the guilt, wearing its purpose like armor: *she is the key. She is the only key. And keys do not merely open doors. Keys unmake locks.*

If the map could bind a king, the map — rewritten, reversed — could *free* him. Merrin must have known that. Merrin hadn't run to hide the key. He had run to *deliver* it. To the one place in the world where the Crowned's agents could not touch it, could not erase it, could not finish what they started on the salt road — because in Vael Hollow, magic starved, and a growing sigil grew slowly, and a hunted girl stayed hidden.

The old man hadn't been fleeing. He'd been *planting.*

Sef put his head back against the bark and laughed once, quietly, at the sky. Eleven days of pursuit, an oath of destruction, a grave-side vow — and the whole time, the truth had been simpler and far more terrible. You could not burn this map. Burning it would only kill the messenger. The map was a seed now, and seeds did not answer to oaths.

Which left him with a choice he had already made and was simply waiting for his courage to catch up to.

*Get her out. Get her beyond the valley's edge, where the magic wakes and the sigil completes itself, and then decide — decide like a man, for once, instead of like a guilty one — whether the kingdom needs a key turned or a key broken.*

Turnip, tied nearby, blew a wet breath through his lips. Sef chose to interpret this as agreement, or possibly contempt; with Turnip the two were indistinguishable.

* * *

Dawn came gray and unhurried. From the ridge, Sef watched the valley wake: smoke from cookfires, monks filing to prayers, and the Hartwell girl crossing the courtyard with a stack of ledgers clutched to her chest, head down, fast, always fast — and then stopping. Stopping dead, in the middle of the yard, looking up at the eastern rim of the valley. At the ridge. At, he realized with a lurch, approximately *his* ridge.

For a long moment neither of them moved. Quarter mile of rain-thinned air between them. She couldn't possibly see him; he was certain of it, wrapped in gray-green wool against wet pine shadow. Certain, mostly. Her eyes were very good. He'd noticed that through the spyglass — the way they catalogued. The way nothing got past them, least of all, perhaps, a shape that had been sitting on the same hillside for four days like a gargoyle with self-esteem issues.

Then a bell — no, not a bell, a *knock*, wood on wood, the temple's mute substitute — sounded from the scriptorium, and she shook herself and hurried on, swallowed by a doorway.

Sef exhaled slowly.

Down in the valley, behind that slate roof, somewhere among the ledgers and the silence, was a woman with a kingdom written on her wrist and a debt-collector due within the week — because Sef had heard the drover mention that too, had filed it away, the way you file away the location of dry powder. Twice a year the auditors came. Interest accrued. Penalties mounted. Twelve years on her bond and climbing.

And Sef Corvane, disgraced knight, breaker of kings, keeper of one useless oath, sat on a hillside in the rain and understood with terrible certainty that he was about to become the most complicated problem in that girl's meticulously copied ledger — and that the auditors were coming sooner than either of them would like, and that whatever happened next would begin the moment someone in that temple asked her a question she couldn't answer.

He shouldered his pack and started down toward the valley, telling himself firmly that he had a plan.

He was still telling himself that when the first riders appeared on the southern road.
