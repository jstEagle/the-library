## 5. Debt Collectors Do Not Knock

The trouble began, as most troubles in Vael Hollow did, with a ledger.

Paloma had copied eleven pages of the grain-tithes by candlelight when Sister Marenal appeared at her elbow without a sound. The Silent Temple trained its sisters well; Paloma had lived among them six years and still could not hear a door open if Marenal stood in it.

"You were seen at the outer gate," Marenal said, "twice after compline."

"The man was dying."

"He was an outsider. Outsiders bring dust on their boots and debts in their pockets, and we want neither." Marenal's gaze dropped to Paloma's hands—ink-stained, steady, always steady. "You gave him water."

"I gave him water," Paloma agreed, because there was no point lying to a woman who could read a lie the way other people read weather. "And he died anyway, so I expect the ledger will balance itself out."

Something that might have been amusement flickered behind Marenal's eyes and was extinguished at once, like a candle pinched between fingers. "Auditor Vessick arrives tomorrow. Whatever you carry from that man—the tube, the leather, whatever he pressed into your hands—you will surrender it before he arrives. Not because I care what it is. Because if he finds it first, your bond extends another ten years, and I have watched you count every day of these six like coins you couldn't spend."

Paloma kept her face very still. Inside, something small and furious turned over, the way a sleeper turns when the blanket is pulled away.

She had hidden the map-tube inside the hollow spine of the *Codex of Tithes, Volume IX* — a book so dull that no one had opened it voluntarily since the temple's founding. She had told herself she was keeping it safe for the priests to examine. She had told herself many things that week.

"I don't know what you mean, Sister," she said.

Marenal sighed, the sigh of a woman who had buried too many clever novices. "Then may whatever gods are left have mercy on your arithmetic."

* * *

Auditor Vessick arrived precisely at noon, which was rude of him, because noon was when the temple took its meal and everyone knew debt-auditors fed on the discomfort of others the way moss fed on north-facing stone.

He was a small man made smaller by his own importance, wearing a coat of dove-grey wool cut in the capital fashion, which in Vael Hollow looked less like elegance and more like an apology. He had two assistants: one tall and silent, who carried the scales, and one short and silent, who carried the ledgers, and between them they carried the unmistakable air of men who enjoyed their work far too much to be trusted near anything breakable.

"Paloma Hartwell," Vessick said, settling into the visitor's chair in the scriptorium as though it were a throne. He set a small brass instrument on the table—a lens of smoked glass in a ring of silver, humming faintly, though nothing hummed in Vael Hollow. Nothing was supposed to. Paloma felt the hairs on her arms rise. "Scribe of the third order. Indentured, six years served, fourteen owed. Your account shows—" he consulted a paper with theatrical slowness— "an irregularity."

"Does it?" Paloma said. Her voice came out level. She was rather proud of that.

"A resonance. Faint. Detected at the valley's eastern edge at moonset last night." He tapped the brass lens. It hummed again, a sound like a trapped wasp. "Something crossed the border of the Hollow in the night. Something that carries a signature. And signatures, scribe, do not cross borders on their own."

"Perhaps it was a bird."

"Do birds sign themselves?"

"In my experience they mostly just leave droppings."

The tall assistant made a noise that might have been a swallowed laugh. Vessick did not laugh. Vessick had probably never laughed; Paloma suspected that if you cut him open you would find not a heart but a second, smaller auditor.

"Your humor is noted," he said. "It will be entered into the record under *obstruction of audit*. Each entry adds days to your term. Shall we continue?"

Paloma folded her hands. Underneath the table, her wrist—the one where, two nights ago, she'd scratched herself on the map's broken clasp—itched maddeningly beneath her sleeve.

"Ask your questions," she said.

"Where is the object the dead man carried?"

"I never saw him carrying anything."

"Liar," said Vessick pleasantly, making another note. "Obstruction, second entry. Where were you at moonset?"

"Asleep."

"Liar. Third entry. Scribe Hartwell, let me save us both some tedium." He leaned forward, and up close his eyes were the color of dishwater with something patient swimming in them. "You are a debtor. Debtors are not people to me; they are sums. And this sum has just become interesting, which is the worst thing a sum can be. Interesting debts attract buyers. Do you know what happens when a debt attracts buyers?"

"I imagine someone buys it."

"And then you belong to them. Not the temple—them. The temple at least pretends to mercy. The syndicates out of Thornbridge do not pretend anything." He smiled, and it was like watching a door open onto an empty room. "So give me the object. Give me the resonance. Be dull again. Dullness is safety, scribe. Dullness is freedom, eventually."

For one mad moment, Paloma considered it. Hand over the tube, take the extra years, go back to copying grain-tithes until her eyes gave out, die in a cell of quiet competence with ink under her nails and nothing else ever. It would be safe. It would be *dull*.

Then she thought of the stranger's hand closing over hers, cold as riverbed stone, pressing the tube into her grip. She thought of him whispering her name—her name, which he should never have known—and the way the word had sounded less like an introduction and more like a key turning.

"No," she said.

Vessick sat back. For a moment he simply looked at her, and there was something almost like respect in the dishwater eyes—almost, and then gone, drowned under the arithmetic.

"So be it," he said. He produced a document from his coat, already sealed, already signed. Of course it was. He had known her answer before he asked the question; the questions were only ceremony. "Effective immediately: the indenture of Paloma Hartwell, scribe of the third order, is transferred from the Silent Temple of Vael Hollow to the Meridian Syndicate of Thornbridge. Terms: perpetual. Transferable. Collateral recoverable on sight." He rose, smoothing his coat. "They collect within the hour."

"The temple wouldn't sell me to the Meridians," Paloma said, standing too. Her heart had begun to hammer, slow and heavy, like a fist against a locked door. "Even Marenal wouldn't—"

"The temple didn't sell you." Vessick gathered his assistants with a tilt of his head. "You sold yourself, the moment you became interesting. I merely found the buyer." At the scriptorium door he paused. "A word of advice, freely given, worth more than anything in your ledgers: when they come, run east. The salt-flats are wide, and the Meridian collectors hate getting salt in their boot-hinges."

And then he was gone, and the silence of the temple rushed back in, and Paloma discovered that silence could feel exactly like being buried alive.

* * *

She went to the Codex of Tithes, Volume IX, and her hands shook so badly she dropped it twice.

The spine was empty.

She turned the codex over, shook it, ran her fingers into the hollow where the map-tube should have been wedged, and found nothing but dust and a dried beetle. She must have made a sound, because a shadow detached itself from the stacks, and there was Marenal, holding the tube.

Paloma's whole body went cold. "Give it back."

"Sit down, child."

"*Give it back.*"

Marenal looked at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she held the tube out—not withdrawing it when Paloma snatched it, but letting her take it, all the way, watching her face while she did.

"They will search the dormitories first," Marenal said quietly. "Then the scriptorium. You have perhaps forty minutes. The east postern gate is unbarred between the hours of none and none-and-a-half; Brother Aldous naps, and God rest him, he has napped through three audits and a fire." She paused. "I am telling you this because I never liked Vessick, and because in thirty years of auditing I have watched him turn four hundred souls into sums, and you are the first one who said no."

Paloma stared at her. The tube was warm in her hands, warmer than leather had any right to be. "You're helping me escape."

"I'm helping a debtor avoid a collector. The distinction matters enormously to the temple's conscience." Marenal turned away, then stopped. "There was a time I wanted to see the world outside the valley. I was your age. I chose the silence instead, and I have regretted it in small ways every single day since, the way water wears stone." Her voice did not waver at all, which somehow made it worse. "Do not choose the silence, Paloma Hartwell. Choose the noise. Even the terrible noise. Especially the terrible noise."

Then she was gone between the shelves, silent as she had come, and Paloma stood alone in the scriptorium holding everything she owned in one hand.

Everything she owned fit in a map-tube. That was, she thought wildly, either a tragedy or the punchline to a joke she hadn't heard yet.

She packed in fifteen minutes, which took almost no time at all: a spare habit, a needle case, a stub of good graphite, the small knife she used for scraping vellum, and—after only a moment's hesitation—three blank quires of paper, because a scribe without paper was just a woman walking. She left her debt-ledger open on her cot, and beneath the final entry she wrote, in her best copperplate, the only sentence she had ever wanted to write in it:

**Account disputed.**

Then she ran.

* * *

Brother Aldous was indeed asleep at the east postern, chin on chest, keys draped over his knee like a sleeping cat. Paloma lifted the ring off with the tenderness of a surgeon, eased the gate open a hand's width, and slipped through into a world she had not stood inside for six years.

The salt-flats began a half-mile beyond the wall and did not end anywhere in particular. They were white to every horizon, blinding under the afternoon sun, cracked into hexagonal plates like the skin of something enormous sleeping just below. The air smelled of mineral and distance. There was no shade, no water, no road—only a track of darker earth winding east toward the mountains, worn by traders who came twice a year and complained about it the whole time.

Paloma walked. Then she jogged. Then, hearing behind her—faint but unmistakable—the temple bell begin its alarm pattern (three strikes, pause, three strikes: *debtor fled, debtor fled*), she ran.

She ran until her lungs burned and the temple was a smudge and the smudge was gone. She ran until her legs shook, and then she walked fast, and then she ran again, because somewhere behind her were men whose job it was to find her, and whose job description, according to Vessick, included the phrase *collateral recoverable on sight*, and she had a fairly good idea what collateral recovery meant for a person.

The sun leaned west. The flats turned gold, then pink, then a violet so deep it looked like spilled ink, and Paloma finally stopped in the lee of a leaning salt-crusted boulder and allowed herself to slide down its side and shake.

Not crying. Shaking. Crying would come later, presumably, once scheduled and approved.

She got the map-tube open with numb fingers and unrolled the leather across her knees. In the fading light the sigils along its edge seemed almost to move—not glowing, exactly, not here, not in the dead valley—but *waiting*, the way a held breath waits.

"Right," she said aloud, to the flats, to the stranger, to nobody. "You got me killed or free. Possibly both. I hope you're pleased."

Her wrist throbbed where the clasp had scratched her. Absently, she pushed back her sleeve to look at it, expecting a scab.

The mark was not a scab. It was a fine dark line, curling like a tendril of ink dropped in water, and even here, even in the magic-dead valley where nothing was supposed to answer—it seemed, very faintly, to pulse.

Somewhere behind her, out on the darkening flats, she heard hoofbeats. More than one horse. Moving fast. Coming her direction—or maybe just passing; there was no way to tell; there was nowhere to hide on a landscape flat as a page.

Paloma rolled the map, jammed it in the tube, slung the tube across her back, and started running east again, into the violet dark, toward the mountains, toward whatever the noise was going to be.

Behind her, the hoofbeats grew louder.
