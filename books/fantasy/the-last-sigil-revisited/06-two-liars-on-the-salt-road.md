## 6. Two Liars on the Salt Road

### Sef

Sef Corvane had been lying for three years, and he liked to think he'd gotten good at it. So it was a matter of some professional irritation that the first lie of his day collapsed before he'd finished telling it.

He'd planned to approach her as a fellow traveler. Simple. A man walking the salt road alone was either a pilgrim, a smuggler, or a fool, and she wouldn't ask which. He would fall into step, make pleasant conversation about the weather—there was no weather on the salt flats, which made it pleasantly easy—and by evening she'd have told him everything he needed to know about where she'd hidden the map.

Instead she saw him coming from half a mile off, sat down on a salt crust like a woman settling in to watch a bad play, and said, "You've been following me since the temple gate."

"I haven't," Sef said.

"You have. You walk like a soldier and you hide behind rocks like a child. I saw your shadow on Tuesday." She squinted up at him. "Also you have very loud boots."

Sef looked down at his boots. They *were* loud. He'd worn them across two kingdoms and through one war, and in all that time no one had ever commented on them. He found this disproportionately upsetting.

"They're standard issue," he said.

"From whose army?"

And there was the trap, laid neat as a snare-wire across the road. He could hear the question underneath the question: *whose army, knight?* She didn't know what he was yet. He intended to keep it that way until he understood what she intended to do with the thing strapped under her cloak—the thing that glowed faintly at her wrist when she thought no one was watching, the way an ember glows when the wind finds it.

"A militia," he said. "Out east. Disbanded now."

"Disbanded militias don't chase scribes across salt flats."

"Disbanded militias do what they like. That's rather the problem with them."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, more the ghost of one deciding whether to haunt the premises. Up close, she looked worse than she had from the ridge: hollow-eyed, salt-crusted to the elbows, with the particular grayness of someone who had not slept properly in four days and had recently run from something. But her eyes were steady. Debt-collectors hadn't broken her. Whatever the syndicate had done to her bond, they hadn't gotten the part of her that mattered.

"My name's Tam," Sef said, because it wasn't.

"Is it."

"It is."

"Tam," she repeated, tasting it. "Tam the disbanded militiaman with loud boots who follows young women across the Vaelish salt flats for no reason at all."

"For reasons of employment," Sef said, and immediately wished he hadn't, because it was true in a way that was worse than any lie.

"Whose employment?"

"A private concern."

"What sort of private concern?"

"The sort," Sef said, "that pays."

She stood up then, brushing salt from her skirt with great deliberation, and looked him over the way a farrier looks over a horse with a limp—assessing, unimpressed, already calculating whether he was worth the trouble. He had the uncomfortable sense of being priced.

"Well, Tam," she said. "I'm traveling to Aurelholm. Alone. If your employers want me to go somewhere else, they can come tell me themselves, and I'll give them the same answer I'm giving you, which involves a word I'm not supposed to know, being temple-raised."

"I don't want you to go anywhere else."

"Then why are you talking to me?"

*Because I need to take the map from you before the Crowned's agents find you,* he did not say. *Because the last bearer died pressing it into your hands and the one before him died too and I have been running from this thing for three years and it always, always ends with someone dead on a road.* 

"Because the road's dangerous," he said, "and I'm going the same way, and it's stupid for both of us to die separately."

It was, he reflected later, the most honest sentence he'd spoken in years, which was probably why it worked.

She studied him a long moment. The wind came off the flats carrying nothing—no smell, no sound, just the flat white silence of the valley's edge, though here near the border he could feel it thinning, the way a deaf man feels a door open onto noise. Her wrist, he noticed, had stopped glowing. Clever girl. Or frightened girl. With sigil-bearers, in his experience, those were frequently the same thing.

"You can walk behind me," she said finally. "Twenty paces. You don't speak unless I speak first, and if you reach for anything metal, I will scream, and there are caravan guards at Merrow's Cross who owe the temple favors and are bored enough to enjoy breaking a militiaman's arms."

"That seems fair," Sef said.

"And you're carrying my pack from now on. It's heavy and you look like you can afford to be tired."

He picked up the pack. It weighed as much as a small child full of books, which, given that she was a scribe, was presumably exactly what it was.

"Twenty paces," he agreed, and fell in behind her, and began, with the patience of a man who had once besieged a city for eleven months, to wait.

### Paloma

The man calling himself Tam lied the way other people breathed—continuously, automatically, without apparent effort or awareness. Paloma had spent nine years copying ledgers in a temple where every entry was checked twice against every other entry, and she knew a falsified column when she read one.

He claimed to be a militiaman; he sat like a kingsguard. He claimed to be heading to Aurelholm for work; he flinched, very slightly, every time the capital's name came up. He claimed to know nothing about magic; but when they crossed the border stone at dusk and the world outside Vael Hollow came rushing back in—that pressure change, like surfacing from deep water—his hand went to his hip before his face even registered surprise, reaching for a sword that wasn't there.

She catalogued all of it. She had nowhere to write it down, so she wrote it in her head, in her best ledger hand: *Subject T. Lies re: occupation (military bearing), destination (fear response), knowledge (reaches for absent blade). Motive unknown. Threat level: high, but has carried pack for two days without complaint.*

That last entry bothered her. Villains didn't carry packs.

They walked. The salt road ran white and straight toward mountains that were still, after two days, no closer—a trick of the light, or of the land, or possibly a punishment. Above them the sky did things sky was not permitted to do inside the valley. Clouds moved. Colors happened. On the second night out from the border stone, Paloma lay awake watching two moons rise, and realized she was crying, and couldn't have said why except that the world outside was so much larger than the ledgers had ever admitted, and she had spent twenty-three years being told that largeness was dangerous, and it turned out largeness was also beautiful, and no one had warned her about that part.

"You're crying," said Tam, from twenty paces away, where he was absolutely supposed to be silent.

"No, I'm not."

"All right."

"It's the wind."

"There isn't any wind."

"It's the *absence* of wind," she snapped, "which is worse," and he had the decency to pretend to sleep after that, though she heard him turning over for another hour, keeping watch, which villains also didn't do, and which annoyed her almost as much as the kindness.

On the third morning, the map tried to kill her.

She'd felt it waking since the border—warmth along her forearm like sunlight through a window, a faint itch under the skin of her wrist. She had told herself it was sunburn, then insect bites, then a nervous condition, in ascending order of implausibility. But on the third morning she unwrapped her wrist to check, and there it was: a line drawn on her skin in no ink she owned, fine as a hair, curving from the heel of her palm up toward her elbow. And as she watched, appalled, a second line grew out of the first, branching, precise, patient, like frost forming on glass. Like handwriting. Like a route, drawing itself northward, toward the mountains, toward—

"TAM."

He was beside her before she finished the word, faster than any disbanded militiaman had a right to move. He looked at her wrist. She watched his face do something complicated—recognition, horror, and underneath it, briefly and terribly, *relief*, as if he'd been waiting three years for confirmation of something and hated himself for being glad.

"That needs treating," he said carefully.

"With what? Prayer? Because I've met the gods of Vael Hollow and they mostly handle grain disputes."

"With—" He stopped. Started again. "I've seen markings like that before. In the east. Militia business." His eyes weren't on her face; they were doing arithmetic with the lines, tracing the route she could see growing there, the way it bent around the Thornbridge checkpoint, the way it avoided something at Caelmoor with a wide deliberate sweep, the way a man avoids a house where something happened to him once. "Where did you get it?"

"Where did *you* get the ability to lie with a straight face while kneeling in the dirt?"

"I told you—"

"You told me nothing that survived contact with evidence." She pulled her sleeve down over the sigil, heart hammering. It was still writing itself. She could feel it now, faintly, like a finger tracing letters on skin from the inside. "You reached for a sword at the border stone. Soldiers reach for swords. Scribes reach for pens, and we're useless, and everyone knows it, and nobody follows us for three days across a salt flat unless they think the pen is worth something."

For a long moment he said nothing. The silence stretched between them, white and flat as the salt.

"If I tell you something true," he said at last, slowly, "will you believe it?"

"No," said Paloma. "But I'll write it down."

And something in his face cracked—not much, just a seam—but she saw it, and she thought: *good*. Let him know she was watching. Let him know she kept accounts. She was a Hartwell of Vael Hollow, indentured, indebted, and hunted, and she had precisely one skill in this world, and it was noticing what people wrote down even when they thought no one was reading.

### Sef

She didn't believe a word he said, and Sef respected her enormously for it.

He gave her the partial truth, the way he'd rehearsed it on the ridge outside Vael Hollow: that he had served the king once, years ago; that something had changed in Aurelholm three winters past; that men who asked questions had started dying, and he had run rather than become one of them. All of it true. None of it the truth. The truth involved a binding circle in a cellar beneath the royal wing, and a magus named Ilvane with steady hands, and Sef Corvane standing guard at the door with his sword drawn—not against enemies, but against anyone who might try to stop what was happening. Against anyone who might save the king.

He omitted that. Obviously.

What surprised him was how much harder the omission had gotten. Three days on a salt road with a sharp-tongued scribe who caught every lie and filed it away had done something to his discipline. She watched him the way auditors watch ledgers. He kept expecting her to demand the missing entries.

Instead, on the fourth evening, she shared her bread with him. Half a loaf, broken without ceremony, handed over the small fire they allowed themselves. He stared at it.

"You're feeding me," he said. "You think I'm a liar and possibly an assassin, and you're feeding me."

"Hungry liars make sloppier lies," she said. "It's practical."

"Ah."

"Also you gave me the larger share of water yesterday when you thought I wasn't looking, which was extremely foolish, and suggests either incompetence or conscience, and you're not incompetent."

"I'm flattered."

"Don't be. Conscience is a liability on this road." She chewed, frowning at the fire. Then, quieter, in a different voice entirely: "Do you know what a debt-bond is worth, Tam? Do you know what they sell a person for? Nine years of my life bought my mother's medicine and my father's funeral, and the auditor says I owe another decade, and yesterday—I found this out yesterday, actually, which has been a wonderful week—the syndicate sold my bond to someone else without asking me, because they never ask, because I'm not a person, I'm a *column*." She looked up. Firelight made her eyes fierce and wet at once. "So whatever your conscience costs you, keep it. It's the only thing they can't put in a ledger."

Sef held his bread and said nothing, because there was a confession sitting in his chest the size of a kingdom, and it was not the one she needed, and he could not give her either.

"I'm sorry," he said instead, which was true, and insufficient, and the story of his entire adult life.

"Yes, well." She wiped her eyes briskly, businesslike, embarrassed. "Eat your bread, Tam the militiaman. We cross the hills tomorrow, and if your eastern militia taught you anything useful, I suggest you start teaching it to me, because whatever is written on my arm knows where it's going, and I intend to arrive before it does."

* * *

They crested the pass at midday on the sixth day, and the world opened below them like a ledger page: the river road winding silver toward the plains, the smoke of towns, the far smudge that might have been the capital's towers or might have been cloud. Paloma stood at the top with her sleeves pushed back, and Sef saw the sigil gleaming on her wrist in full daylight for the first time—complete now, or nearly, its final strokes curling toward the north.

And he saw, on the road below, half a mile distant, three travelers in dusty pilgrim gray moving with the unhurried precision of wolves deciding not to hurry.

Paloma hadn't seen them. She was laughing—actually laughing, breathless, pointing at the river, saying something about how maps in books got the colors wrong. The sigil on her wrist pulsed once, softly, like a heartbeat answering another heartbeat far away.

Sef put his hand on his absent sword-hilt and made his decision, and hated how easy it was.

"Pilgrims," he said. "Below us. Don't point. Don't stop smiling—they watch for fear. We leave the road at the next bend and travel the riverbank, and Paloma?" He waited until she looked at him. "Everything I told you about the east was true. Everything I *didn't* tell you is about to matter. Walk fast."

Her smile faded. She looked at the pilgrims, then at him, and he watched her weigh him—one more entry in the ledger, threat level revised, motive still unknown—and then, terrifyingly, she simply nodded and started downhill, trusting him, or pretending to, and Sef understood with sudden clarity that between the two of them, hers was by far the more dangerous lie.

Because hers, he suspected, was starting to become true.
