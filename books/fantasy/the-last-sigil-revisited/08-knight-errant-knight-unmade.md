## 8. Knight Errant, Knight Unmade

The fire had burned down to that stage where it was less a fire and more a rumor of one — orange embers breathing in and out like something asleep. Sef Corvane sat on the far side of it, which was deliberate. He had spent three years perfecting the art of sitting on the far side of things: fires, conversations, truths.

Across the flames, Paloma Hartwell slept sitting up, her back against a leaning stone, her wrist cradled against her chest like something injured. Which, he supposed, it was. The sigil had stopped glowing since they'd made camp, but he'd seen it. In the flood of magic at the valley border, when the map had burned itself into her skin and she'd cried out — he'd seen it plain as a brand.

He knew that sigil. He'd helped carry it, once, in an iron box lined with wool, up seven flights of stairs, telling himself all the way that what he was doing was mercy.

*You didn't know,* said the voice he kept for such occasions. It was a reasonable voice. A courtly voice. It sounded, uncomfortably, like his old self.

*I carried it anyway,* said the other voice, the one that came at night.

Sef fed a twig to the embers and watched it catch. The salt road behind them was empty. The road ahead curved toward Merrow's Cross, and after that, if the maps in his head still meant anything, the long climb into the hill country, then Aurelholm itself, glittering on its rock like a lie told beautifully.

She stirred. He tensed. But she only murmured something — a word that might have been *column* or *covenant*, scribe's dreams, ledger-ghosts — and settled again.

Three days ago he had put a blade to this woman's throat on the salt flats, and she had looked at him not with fear but with the weary contempt of someone who had dealt with worse men over smaller sums. Three days ago she had lied to him about why she was traveling east. He had known she was lying; she had known he knew; they had traveled together anyway, two liars sharing a fire, because the alternative to each other was the road alone, and neither of them could afford that.

It was time, he decided, to give her a piece of the truth. Not the whole truth — the whole truth would send her running, or worse, keep her walking beside him with those sharp gray eyes recalculating everything — but a piece. Enough to buy trust. Enough, maybe, to buy forgiveness later, if there was any to be had, which he doubted.

A knight's first duty, his father had told him, is honesty. His second is courage. His third, his father had added with the dry smile that Sef had inherited along with his sword, is knowing how much of the first the moment can bear.

* * *

She woke before dawn, the way temple people did, all at once and without complaint, as though sleep were a debt she resented owing.

"Water's in the skin," Sef said. "There's bread. What's left of it."

Paloma took both without thanks, which he was learning was simply how she was built. She ate watching the eastern sky go from slate to pearl, her fingers stained to the second knuckle with ink that no amount of scrubbing seemed to fully lift. A scribe's hands. Honest hands, he'd thought once. He was beginning to suspect that honest hands were just hands whose owner hadn't yet been caught.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"I slept."

"You sat by the fire moving twigs around like a man negotiating with them."

"They were difficult twigs."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth that on another face might have been called the beginning of a smile. It retreated quickly, suspicious of its own impulses. "You've been circling something for two days," she said. "Since the border. Since you saw my arm." She turned the wrist toward him, almost casually, the way a gambler shows a card. The sigil lay under the skin like a vein of dark gold. "So. Circle."

Sef looked at the fire instead of at her. This was easier with a fire. Most hard things were.

"My name isn't just Sef," he said. "It's Ser Seforin Corvane. Knight-errant of the Order of the Pale Star. Sworn to King Aldric the Third." He paused. "Formerly sworn. The Order doesn't take attendance anymore. There wasn't anyone left to call the roll."

Paloma lowered the bread. "You were kingsguard?"

"Not guard. Errant. I rode out ahead of trouble. Messages, escorts, investigations." He shrugged, and the shrug cost more than he let it show. "When things began to change at court — three years ago — I was one of the last who still moved freely between the palace and the provinces. That made me useful. And then it made me dangerous. And then it made me nothing, because I ran."

"The change." She said it carefully, tasting the phrase. "You mean the king himself."

"I mean the king himself." Sef pulled a stick through the ash, drawing lines that meant nothing, which was the point of them. "You've seen the murals by now. Or you will. The face that never quite matches itself. People blame the painters. Painters are convenient scapegoats; they can't defend themselves, they're mostly drunk." He glanced up. "The face doesn't match because it isn't one face. Something wears him. Something has worn him for three years, and everyone close enough to see it clearly is either dead, exiled, or — " he gestured at himself with the stick — "running."

Paloma was quiet for a while. In the growing light, the sigil on her wrist seemed faintly luminous, though he suspected that now was imagination and now was memory.

"And you failed to stop it," she said. Not cruelly. Just precisely, the way she copied ledgers.

"Yes."

"What happened?"

And here was the fork in the road, the one he'd been staring at all night while the twigs refused to cooperate. Here was where he could say it — *I stood in the binding chamber, I held the door, I believed the magus when he wept and told me it was the only way to save the king's life* — or here was where he could say the shape of it without the weight of it.

"The night it was done," Sef said slowly, "I was told the king was dying. Poisoned, some said. Cursed, others. There was a plot — a real one, I believed it, I'd tracked pieces of it myself. The royal magus, Orenvale, came to me and said there was a rite. An old rite. That it would save Aldric's life." The fire popped. He made himself continue. "I believed him. I helped him. And when it was finished, the king lived — but the man who woke up in the king's bed wasn't entirely the king. And I understood, too late, what I had held the door open for. So I left. No explanation. No letter. I took my horse and my disgrace and I went east, and I've been going east ever since, looking for a way to undo it."

Every word of it true. Every word of it a lie, in the way that a wall built entirely of real stones can still be built to hide something.

Paloma studied him for a long moment. She had a way of studying that felt less like looking and more like proofreading.

"That's not the whole of it," she said.

"No," Sef admitted, because denying it outright would be worse than useless with her. "It isn't. But it's the whole of what I can give you tonight."

"Why not the rest?"

"Because the rest would make you leave. And I need you not to leave." He heard how that sounded and winced. "Not for me. For the map. Whatever you're carrying, whatever wrote itself into your blood at the border — it's the first thing in three years that points toward undoing instead of enduring. I've been walking east on faith and guilt. You're walking east on evidence. I'd rather walk with the evidence."

Paloma looked down at her wrist. When she spoke again, her voice was quieter. "In the temple, we had a saying. Debts don't disappear. They only change hands." Her jaw tightened. "Everyone owes something. The question is whether they pay."

"I intend to pay," Sef said. "However long the ledger runs."

"Mm." She tore the last of the bread in half and handed him the smaller piece, which he noted, and filed away, and did not comment on. "Then here's what I know, so we're even. The stranger who died at our gate gave me a map. The priests wanted to burn it; I stole it. Then the auditor accused me of theft anyway — apparently honesty is only theft with better paperwork — sold my debt to the Vane Syndicate, and I ran." She chewed. Swallowed. "I'm not a thief. I want that written somewhere permanent. I'm a scribe who committed one theft, under provocation, and I'll answer for it someday. But not before I find out what this thing on my arm opens."

"A door in the palace," Sef said. "Or a grave. With sigil-work, those are frequently the same room."

"How comforting. Do you say things like that often?"

"I was told once I had a gift for reassurance."

"They lied."

"They usually do." He found, to his mild alarm, that he was smiling. It felt strange on his face, like a coat that fit a former owner. "Rest more. We reach Merrow's Cross by midday if the road behaves."

"Roads never behave," Paloma said, and closed her eyes again with the discipline of a woman who had learned to sleep whenever sleep was offered, because tomorrow it might be repossessed.

* * *

They walked. The salt flats fell behind them and the land began to remember it had once been green; stunted pines appeared, then actual grass, aggressive and cheerful, the sort of vegetation that had never met a tax collector. Sef walked slightly ahead, the way he'd walked for twenty years, reading the road the way Paloma read ledgers — for discrepancies. A broken twig. A hoofprint too fresh. A bird gone quiet.

"So," Paloma said, after a mile of companionable silence, which for her seemed to be the maximum allotment. "The Order of the Pale Star. What was it like? Before."

Sef considered the question. Nobody had asked him about *before* in years. People asked about the disgrace, or the price on rumor, or whether the stories about Caelmoor were true (he always said no; this was technically accurate, since the stories understated it). But *before* — before was a country he'd closed the border to.

"Cold mornings," he said at last. "Bad wine in good halls and good wine in bad ones. A great deal of riding, and the particular loneliness of being trusted completely by people who barely know you." He ducked a low pine bough and held it for her. "And the king, before. He was — hm. How do I explain Aldric?"

"He was my king too, technically," Paloma said. "From very far away. Through a veil of taxes."

"Right. Well. He was the sort of king who read the petitions himself. All of them. Thousands. He'd get ink on his fingers worse than yours, and when the ministers complained he was spending too much time on farmers' drainage grievances, he said that a kingdom was just a very large farm with better lawyers." Sef laughed — actually laughed, briefly, before catching it and putting it away. "He used to sneak out of the palace dressed as a merchant to hear what people said when they thought no one important was listening. Once he came back with a black eye, because a fishwife caught him cheating at dice. He promoted her husband."

"To the guard?"

"To head of the fisheries. He said anyone married to a woman like that deserved institutional support."

Paloma made a sound that was almost a laugh, quickly converted into a cough. "That's not how kings behave."

"No," Sef agreed. "Which is why what's wearing him now is such a — " he searched for the word and found the honest one — "such a desecration. It's not just a body it took. It's a habit of kindness. It walks around in his generosity and uses it to open doors."

They crested a low ridge, and below them the road bent toward a cluster of rooftops around a river crossing — Merrow's Cross, smoke rising thin and ordinary, a town like ten thousand towns.

Except that as they drew closer over the next hour, Sef began to notice the wrongness accumulating in small ways. A shrine by the roadside with the dedication plaque chiseled blank. A milestone where the town's name had been scraped off. A woman selling apples who, when Sef asked the price, answered fluently enough but flinched when he asked *whose apples these were*.

"Notice it too?" he murmured.

"The milestones," Paloma whispered. "Someone's been erasing names."

Ahead, at the town gate, a painted board leaned against the wall where a welcome sign should have been. Someone had tried to write *Merrow's Cross* on it in careful letters, and someone else — or perhaps the same hand, trembling — had crossed it out. Beneath the crossed-out name, in different paint, fresher, someone had written:

**THE TOWN FORMERLY KNOWN. PLEASE DO NOT ASK. WE ARE FINE.**

Paloma stared at it. On her wrist, unbidden, the sigil flickered — a pulse of dark gold, like a heartbeat answering a knock.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, no. I know this work. I've *seen* this work." She looked up at Sef, and for the first time since the valley, she looked afraid — not of the road, not of the syndicate, not of him, but of the thing on her own arm recognizing its family.

"Sef," she said quietly. "Whatever erased their names — it's written in the same language as me."
