## 9. The Town That Forgot Its Name

The road to Merrow's Cross smelled of rain and old woodsmoke, which Paloma would later think of as the last ordinary thing about it.

They had walked two days out of the salt-flats, following the map's route now seared faintly into the skin of her left forearm—a branching line like a river delta, or a vein, that warmed when they strayed wrong and cooled when they turned true. Sef had stopped asking her how it felt. She suspected he'd never actually asked in the first place; he just watched her wrist with an expression she was learning to read as *guilt wearing its traveling clothes*.

"Town ahead," he said, shading his eyes. "Merrow's Cross. We can resupply. Horses, if we're lucky."

"We've been lucky twice this week," Paloma said. "I don't trust the arithmetic."

He almost smiled. He'd been doing that more—almost smiling—and she'd been pretending not to notice, because noticing felt dangerous, the way standing too near a cliff edge feels dangerous even when you have no intention of jumping.

Merrow's Cross announced itself first by sound: a millwheel turning somewhere out of sight, groaning its slow wooden complaint, and beneath it a hum of market voices. Then by smell—baking bread, wet straw, the green bite of a tannery. Then, at last, by sight: a huddle of slate roofs around a stone crossroads where a weathered statue of some local saint held a fish aloft like a trophy nobody wanted.

It looked, Paloma thought, like a hundred towns she had copied entries about in temple ledgers. Tolls paid, tithe-records kept, births and deaths in tidy columns.

It took her until they were actually inside it to understand what was missing.

* * *

The trouble started small, the way rot does.

An old woman sold them bread from a cart and refused, flatly, to give them change. Not because she was cheating them—Paloma checked the coins twice—but because when Paloma asked her name to thank her properly, the woman flinched.

"No need for that," she said. "No call for names here."

"Forgive me," Paloma said, temple-polite. "I only wished to—"

"Don't." The word came out sharp enough to cut. Then softer, with a glance over her shoulder toward the square: "You'll want to be careful, asking after names. Some folk here get upset."

Sef's hand had drifted, casually, to his sword hilt—the way he did when he was worried but didn't want anyone to know he was worried. "Upset how?"

The woman looked at him for a long moment. Her eyes were red-rimmed, Paloma noticed. The eyes of someone who had been crying recently and often, in private, because there was no one left whose shoulder she could cry on without remembering why she shouldn't.

"Just careful," she said, and pushed the bread into Sef's hands, and would not take payment beyond exact coin, and shuffled off down the street calling no one by name—not the butcher, not the children, not the dog.

Paloma stood holding warm bread in a town full of people who wouldn't say each other's names aloud.

"Sef," she said quietly. "Look at the doors."

He looked. So did she, and once she saw it, she couldn't stop seeing it.

Every doorpost in Merrow's Cross bore a mark. A sigil, chalked or painted or scratched, refreshed often enough that some were still wet. They varied in their details, but the grammar was identical—a central stem, three branches, a closed loop at the base like a knot pulled tight. She knew that grammar. She had been staring at versions of it for days, inked into leather and then into her own skin.

"That's—" Sef began.

"I know what it is." Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was one of the few temple skills she still valued. "It's the binding-mark. Or near kin to it. Someone has marked every door in this town."

"Not every door." He nodded across the square. "That one."

The house at the corner of the square was different. Larger, well-built, with a merchant's prosperity in its leaded windows. Its doorpost was bare. And its windows were shuttered though it was midday, and no smoke rose from its chimney, and no one in the market so much as glanced at it—which, Paloma thought, was stranger than if everyone had stared.

"Let's find an inn," Sef said. "And then let's not ask any questions."

"When have you ever known me not to ask questions?"

"I'm counting on you being sensible for once in your life."

"I'm a scribe. Sensibility is my entire personality."

"It truly is," he said gravely, "the saddest thing I've ever heard."

* * *

The inn was called the Drowned Bell, which its keeper told them cheerfully was because the bell of the old chapel had fallen down a well a century ago, though Paloma privately suspected the truth was less picturesque and more geological. The keeper was a broad, cheerful man who talked the way water runs—constantly, in all directions, pooling nowhere.

"You'll be wanting supper, beds, and gossip, in reverse order of importance," he said, dealing out stew like cards. "I can manage all three, though the gossip's gone thin lately. Town's gone quiet. Not unfriendly, mind—just quiet. Like everyone's waiting for something and hoping it isn't them."

"Waiting for what?" Paloma asked.

The keeper shrugged hugely. "Pilgrims came through, oh, six months back? Nice folk. Very devout. Set up a little shrine by the crossroads, said the old saint statue needed company. Said the town ought to show devotion." His cheer dimmed a degree, like sun behind cloud. "Devotion's a funny thing. Starts with singing. Ends up costing."

"Costing what?" Sef asked.

But the keeper only spread his hands, as if the answer were too large to hold, and went to fetch ale.

Paloma ate slowly and watched the common room. And the common room, she realized, was watching nothing at all. That was the horror of it—it took her a quarter hour to see it, because absence is a difficult thing to look at directly.

No one spoke to anyone by name.

A woman called across the room to her sister—"Oi! You! Pass the salt!"—and the sister passed it, and neither seemed troubled, as if this were normal, as if *you* were a name. A father called his son in from the street not by any name but with a whistle and a clap, and the boy came running, happy, obedient, answering to a sound the way a shepherd's dog answers.

And when the innkeeper finally sat down beside them for his own supper, Paloma tried it gently.

"This is very good stew. Whom should I thank?"

The big man smiled, and for a moment the smile was the loneliest thing she had ever seen on a living face.

"Nobody," he said. "Thank nobody. It's easier that way."

Then he got up, still smiling, and Paloma saw that above the bar, where taverns keep a painted board listing the landlord's family and generations of the same, the board was blank. Not unmarked—*blank*. Scrubbed. Painted over so many times the wood beneath had begun to warp.

She set down her spoon.

"Sef."

"I see it."

"Do you *see* it?" Her voice shook, and she hated that, and let it shake anyway. "They've given up their names. All of them. Voluntarily—or they think it's voluntary. Six months of pilgrims and a shrine and now nobody calls anybody anything, and they smile about it, and the ones who flinch are the ones who are still fighting it inside—"

"Paloma." Low. Careful. "Breathe."

"You breathe." She pressed her palm flat against her forearm, against the map-line burning there, cold now, patient, like a snake sleeping under snow. "This is what the Crowned *does*. This is the shape of it. Names are tribute. That's what this is—someone's collecting."

Sef was quiet a long moment, turning his cup. When he spoke, it was barely above the fire's crackle.

"My order had a saying," he said. "A kingdom is a story everyone agrees to tell together. Take away enough words, and the story stops being true." He looked up. "We should leave tonight."

"The map says we pass through. There's a bridge east of here, and the next crossing is three days' detour through hill country with no food and your charming company."

"My company is impeccable."

"Your company snores like a landslide."

"That," said Sef Corvane, disgraced knight of a broken order, "is slander, and I will treasure it."

But he wasn't smiling when he said it, and neither was she.

* * *

She woke past midnight to the sound of singing.

It came from the direction of the crossroads—low, many voices, wordless. A hymn without lyrics, which struck her as exactly the kind of prayer a town without names would sing. She dressed in the dark, took up the little knife she'd bought at the last market town (she was a scribe; her weapons ran to blades for cutting quills, but a blade was a blade), and slipped downstairs.

Sef was awake before she reached the bottom step, of course. He slept like a cat that had once been a soldier: lightly, and armed.

"No," he said.

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You were going to go look. Your face does a thing."

"My face does many things. It's a very expressive face."

"Paloma." He caught her arm at the door, gently, and she felt him feel the map-line under his fingers—warm suddenly, warming further, the closer she stood to the crossroads. His grip tightened. "It's pulling you. Do you understand that? Whatever's out there, it knows you're carrying it, and it's *pulling*."

"Then all the more reason to look," she whispered. "If it knows I exist, I'd rather know what it looks like while I still have the advantage of surprise."

For a moment she thought he would refuse. Then something in his face gave way—that tired, guilty surrender she was coming to hate and rely upon in equal measure—and he picked up his sword.

"Together," he said. "Ten paces behind me, and if I say run, you run."

"And if you say run and I don't run?"

"Then I'll carry you. I've carried drunker people than you."

* * *

The square was full.

Not crowded—full, in the way a church is full. Every soul in Merrow's Cross stood in the moonlight, facing the little pilgrim-shrine at the foot of the fish-bearing saint, and sang. Their faces were slack and peaceful. Children sang. The old bread-seller sang. The innkeeper, vast and gentle, sang.

At the center of them, before the shrine, stood three figures in gray robes with their hoods back—pilgrims, surely, except that pilgrims do not stand so still, so precisely, like chess pieces placed rather than people standing. Their lips moved with the hymn but Paloma, straining, could see that no sound came from them at all. They were conducting. Feeding. Whatever they were doing, the song flowed *through* them.

And above the shrine, in the air itself, hung writing.

Paloma's breath stopped. It was sigil-work, done in light instead of ink—vast, slow-turning lines of it, coiling up out of the shrine like smoke given grammar. The central stem, the three branches, the knot at the base. But where her map-mark was a single elegant phrase, this was a ledger. Columns and columns of entries, each one small and tight and knotted shut.

Each one, she understood with a sick lurch, was a name.

They weren't erased. Erasure was clean, merciful, final. These were *kept*—stored, filed, hoarded—drawn up out of the mouths of a town and wound into the sky like thread onto a spool. Somewhere in that luminous archive were the bread-seller's name and the innkeeper's family-board and every child whistled home at dusk, and the people below sang because some part of them, deep under the peace, was screaming.

Her own arm blazed.

The map-line burned white-hot, and one branch of the great sigil above—just one—turned, slow as a key turning, and pointed at her.

One of the hooded figures stopped moving its lips.

Paloma did not wait for the rest. She grabbed Sef's sleeve—"Walk, don't run, walk, *walk*"—and they withdrew down the dark street with the hymn swelling behind them, and only when they'd rounded three corners did she let herself breathe.

"They saw me," she said. "Or it did. The mark answered them, Sef. It *knows its own kind*."

He was looking at her arm, where the sigil-glow was fading back to its sullen ember. When he raised his eyes, there was something in them she hadn't seen before. Not fear of the town. Fear *for* her—and beneath it, older and worse, fear of something he clearly wasn't telling her.

"Tomorrow we cross the bridge," he said. "First light. No supplies, no questions, no stopping."

"What did you recognize back there?" she asked. "In the square. You looked at that sigil like you'd seen it before—really seen it, not read about it. Where?"

The pause lasted three heartbeats. She counted; it was a scribe's habit.

"Caelmoor," he said at last. "Three years ago. I saw work like that at Caelmoor."

"And?"

"And that's a story for daylight," he said, "and a longer road than this one."

Above the rooftops, faint and terrible, the nameless hymn went on singing—and high over the crossroads, Paloma could have sworn one small bright entry in the sky's great ledger trembled loose of its knot and drifted, like ash, toward her.
