## 10. Hunters Wearing Friendly Faces

Sef Corvane had learned, over three years of running, to count the ways a town could be wrong.

Merrow's Cross had been wrong since the moment they entered it. Not wrong in any way a magistrate would notice—the streets were swept, the market stalls were stocked, the innkeeper smiled—but wrong the way a portrait is wrong when someone has painted over the eyes. The people here were too polite. Too smooth. They moved through their days like actors who had memorized their lines but never once been told what the play was about.

He watched it at breakfast.

The common room of the Drowned Bell was full—market day, or what passed for one—and every soul in it was smiling. A woman sold turnips and smiled. A boy chased a dog and smiled. An old man wept quietly into his porridge, and he was smiling too, and that was the one that put ice down Sef's spine.

"They don't remember," Paloma said across the table, keeping her voice low. She'd been up half the night copying sigil-fragments from the map into a little notebook she thought he didn't know about. Ink stained her fingers all the way to the second knuckle. "I asked the innkeeper's daughter her name this morning. She looked at me like I'd asked her the weight of the moon. Then she smiled and said, *'We're all friends here.'*"

"Friends," Sef repeated.

"That's what they say instead of names. Everyone. I've counted eleven of them."

Twelve, actually—he'd heard the baker say it—but he let her have her victory. There were few enough of those lately.

* * *

The pilgrims arrived at midmorning, and Sef knew them within a minute of seeing them, which annoyed him, because there were six of them and only two eyes on the back of his head.

They came up the south road in matching gray cloaks, walking in that particular formation soldiers use when they want to look like civilians: staggered, casual, hands free. Pilgrims carried staffs. These six carried walking sticks held wrong—gripped for striking rather than leaning. Pilgrims sweated. These six moved like men who had been told to sweat and found the instruction undignified. And every one of them glanced—at the inn, at the well, at the temple ruin on the hill—with the flat, inventorying attention of people counting exits.

"Paloma." He said it quietly, without looking up from his ale.

"I see them."

"Do you?"

"The gray cloaks? Yes, Sef, I'm nearsighted, not blind." She was crumbling bread into very small pieces, which she did when she was thinking. "Pilgrims to what? There's no shrine here. The temple burned down before I was born."

"Pilgrims to us."

Her crumbing stopped. "You think they're following the map."

"I think the Crowned doesn't leave loose threads lying around a valley full of salt flats. Someone felt the map cross the border. Someone sent word ahead." He took another slow drink. His mouth had gone dry anyway. "We've been in this town a day. We're the most interesting thing that's happened to Merrow's Cross in years, and everyone here has had their interest surgically removed."

"So we leave."

"We leave tonight. Quietly, out the north gate, before—" He stopped.

Because one of the pilgrims had seen them.

It wasn't dramatic. The man simply turned his head as he passed the window, and for one unguarded second the pleasant nothing fell off his face, and underneath it was something old and hungry and patient, like a fish looking up through ice. Then the pleasantness came back, and he raised one hand—a small, friendly wave—and walked on toward the inn door.

"Back room," Sef said. "Now."

* * *

The back room of the Drowned Bell smelled of tallow and old fish, which Sef supposed was fair, given the name. He wedged a chair under the door handle while Paloma stood in the middle of the floor with her arms crossed, radiating the particular indignation of a competent person being managed.

"You're going to say 'stay here,'" she said.

"I am going to say stay here."

"And then you're going to go out there alone and do something noble and stupid, and I'll have to rescue you, because that's how these things go."

"That is not how these things go."

"Chapter and verse, knight-errant. When has anything gone according to your plan since the salt road?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. She had, infuriatingly, a point. Every plan he'd made since Vael Hollow had survived approximately four minutes of contact with reality, and most of the deviations had involved Paloma doing exactly the thing he'd forbidden, competently, at the exact moment his own plan failed.

"Fine," he said. "Not *alone*. But you stay behind me, and if I say run, you run, and if I say hide, you hide, and if I say—"

The knock at the front door of the back room was soft. Polite. Three little raps, like a neighbor come to borrow sugar.

Sef blew out the candle.

"—if I say nothing," he finished in a whisper, "you get out the window."

Another knock. Then a voice, warm as fresh bread: "Master traveler? Forgive the intrusion. We're pilgrims, passing through. We've lost our way, and we hoped a man of your bearing might point us back to the road south."

Through the gap under the door, Sef could see candlelight flickering in the hall, and shadows—two of them, standing patient. The other four would be at the windows by now. He'd have done it himself, three years ago, before everything. Surround the room. No hurry. Prey that thinks it's hidden stays calm, and calm prey doesn't make noise.

"South road's a half-day back the way you came," he called pleasantly.

"Ah." A pause. The warmth in the voice cooled by exactly one degree, the way milk cools when you stop stirring it. "But we haven't *come* from the south, master traveler. We've come from where you've been."

Paloma was already at the window, easing the shutter open with the silence of someone who'd spent years sneaking books past temple wardens. She looked back at him and mouthed, *Run means run.*

"Then you've had a long walk," Sef said, drawing his sword an inch from its scabbard so it would come freely when it mattered. "I'd hate to send you back empty-handed."

"Oh, we won't be empty-handed." The voice was still warm. It was the warmth that frightened him—not cruelty, but the total absence of any feeling at all wearing cruelty's clothing. "We only need the girl. You can keep your sword, your pride, whatever else you've got left in that shabby scabbard. Hand her over, and you can walk away. That's more mercy than most men in your position ever get. Most men in your position don't even know what position they're in. Do you, Ser Corvane?"

The name landed like a stone in still water.

They knew who he was. Which meant the Crowned hadn't just sent hunters—it had sent hunters with a dossier. Which meant somewhere in Aurelholm, in whatever cold place the thing wore its king like a coat, someone had written *Sef Corvane* on a list and drawn a line under it.

Beside the window, Paloma had gone very still. He saw the question form in her face—*how do they know your name?*—and saw her swallow it, file it away, add it to the ledger of things she wasn't asking yet. She was getting good at that. It worried him more than the hunters did.

"Now," the voice said, and the door exploded inward.

* * *

Later—in the years after, when he sometimes let himself remember this night at all—what Sef recalled most clearly was not the fight. It was the smell of tallow smoke, and the sound Paloma made when the window frame gave way, and the terrible slow-motion clarity of watching her drop into the yard just as a fourth hunter stepped out from behind the rain barrel.

She landed hard, rolled, scrambled up—and froze, because the hunter had a knife at her back before she'd finished standing, and Sef was fifteen feet away on the wrong side of a broken door and a dead man's body, and the arithmetic of the distance was already done, and the answer was *no.*

Everything after that happened in the space between two heartbeats.

He went through the doorway rather than around it—wood and hinges surrendering together—and crossed the yard in four strides that his body remembered from a training ground he'd spent three years pretending never existed. The hunter holding Paloma began to turn. Sef took the man's wrist in his left hand, broke the grip on the knife, and drove his blade through the gap beneath the jaw, up into the skull, the way Brother-Armorer Hesk had drilled into him when he was nineteen and laughed at everything.

Clean. Instant. Painless, probably. None of that mattered.

What mattered was the sound the man made, which was almost nothing—a small wet exhale, a syllable of surprise—and the way his weight came off the knife differently than a training dummy's, alive and then suddenly, entirely not, and the way Paloma looked up at Sef with her cheek pressed to the dirt and her eyes huge, and the way he understood, in that single endless instant, that he had just become again the thing he'd sworn three years ago never to be.

The other hunters came over the fence.

* * *

There were five left, and they fought like instruments rather than men—no fear, no fury, no self-preservation, which made them worse than any knight he'd ever faced. A knight wanted to live. These wanted only to finish. Sef gave ground across the yard, giving Paloma time to scramble behind the woodpile, parrying two blades at once and hating every second of it, because his body knew this work the way a riverbed knows water and he could feel the old channels opening inside himself, the old cold competence rising like groundwater.

*You are good at this,* whispered a voice in him that sounded distressingly like his younger self. *This is what you're for.*

*No,* he told it, and meant it, and kept fighting anyway, because Paloma was behind him and the mathematics of the distance had changed and now the answer was yes.

He killed the second one with the pommel. The third he tripped into the well. The fourth and fifth exchanged a glance—did constructs exchange glances? did they *talk* to each other?—and broke off together, flowing backward over the fence with that same unhurried patience, and one of them paused at the top and looked at Sef with mild professional interest, the way a carpenter looks at a joint that didn't hold.

"He'll want to know about the sword-work," the hunter said conversationally. Blood ran from Sef's forearm; he hadn't noticed taking the cut. "The style, specifically. Caelmoor style. He'll find that *very* interesting."

Then they were gone, over the fence and into the dark, and the yard was quiet except for Sef's breathing and the small sounds of a town that had heard violence and decided, collectively, not to have heard it. Somewhere a shutter closed. Somewhere a dog whined and was hushed. In the morning, Sef suspected, the Drowned Bell would have no record of them ever staying, and the innkeeper would smile, and the whole town would fold the night away like a letter nobody wanted to reread.

"Sef." Paloma's voice, from behind the woodpile. Steady. Too steady—the steadiness of someone holding a door shut against a flood. "Are they gone?"

"Gone." He knelt. His legs were less certain about kneeling than his mind was. "Are you hurt?"

"No. Are *you*?" She was looking at his arm, at the blood, at the two bodies in her line of sight, and he watched her do what she always did with unbearable information: organize it. File it. Write it down somewhere internal in a neat hand. "That's twice you've killed tonight."

"Three times. The first one through the door."

"Three times." Her voice didn't change, but her hands, he noticed, had begun to shake, very slightly, and she pressed them flat against her knees to stop it. "You said—you told me on the salt road you hadn't drawn a blade on a living man since you left the capital."

"I hadn't."

"And now?"

He looked down at his hands. They were steady. That was the worst part—they were perfectly steady, trained steady, steady as a scribe's hand on a ledger line, and he hated them for it.

"And now I remember why I stopped," he said.

* * *

They sat in the ruins of the back room afterward, because leaving immediately in bloodied clothes down a dark road felt like volunteering for ambush, and because Paloma insisted on binding his arm, and because—though neither of them said it—they needed somewhere small to sit while the night finished happening to them.

She worked on his forearm by candlelight, cleaning the cut with the methodical fussiness of someone who had mended a hundred copyists' paper-cuts and never once a knife wound. Her hands had stopped shaking. Mostly.

"You hesitated," she said. "At the window. Before you came through the door. Half a heartbeat."

"I weighed whether I could reach you in time."

"You reached me."

"I reached you *this* time." He watched her tie off the bandage. Her knots were excellent. Everything about her was excellent, precise, ledger-straight, and it occurred to him—not for the first time—that he was going to get this woman killed with his secrets, and that she deserved better than secrets, and that he was going to keep telling them anyway because the truth was heavier than any debt she owed the temple. "Paloma. What those men said. About knowing me."

"I'm not asking."

"Why not?"

"Because you'll either lie to me or tell me something I'm not ready to hear, and I can't afford either one until we're out of this town." She snipped the thread with her belt knife. "Also because I already know you're lying about something big. You have the face of a man carrying a boulder uphill and calling it a knapsack. I've known since the river crossing. I just don't know *what* yet."

"And that doesn't frighten you?"

"Of course it frightens me." She looked up, and in the candlelight her eyes were tired and fierce and honest, and he felt the boulder shift on his back. "But you put yourself between me and four knives tonight, and you didn't hesitate after the window, and whatever you're hiding, you hid it *in front* of the knives too. So." She packed away her needle. "So."

*So.* It was, he thought, the most generous verdict anyone had handed him in three years, and it weighed more than any sentence.

They slipped out the north gate an hour before dawn, past the sleeping town with its borrowed smiles, and Sef did not look back at the bodies in the yard because looking back was a luxury for men with clean ledgers. Ahead, the road climbed east toward the hills, and somewhere beyond those hills, an astronomer in exile supposedly knew things about moons and bindings that would start the clock ticking on all their lives.

His arm throbbed. His hands were still steady. Old violence lay in him like embers banked under ash, warm now, awake, and he understood that it would not go back to sleep easily, and that some part of him had missed it, and that this was the true cost of the night—not the blood, but the relief.

Paloma walked beside him in the gray light, ink-stained and unbowed.

"Thirteenth moon's waning," she said, apropos of nothing. "Wren used to say the stars keep better ledgers than priests."

"Wren?"

"Astronomer. Exiled. Vesha mentioned—" She caught his expression and frowned. "You've heard of her."

He had. And the fact that she was asking, now, with dawn coming up and hunters' words still ringing in his ears—*he'll find that very interesting*—meant the questions he'd been dreading were finally, inevitably, on the road behind them and gaining.

"Caelmoor," Paloma said quietly, not stopping, not looking at him, watching the hills instead. "That's where you learned that sword-style. Isn't it, Sef?"

The road stretched east. The thirteenth moon hung pale above the ridgeline, one sliver short of full, and Sef Corvane walked toward an astronomer who demanded confessions, carrying the one confession he had sworn to bury.

"Yes," he said.

And that, at least, was not a lie.
