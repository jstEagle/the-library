## 11. Thirteen Moons, One Missing

The road out of Merrow's Cross smelled of woodsmoke and burnt feathers, and neither of us talked about why.

We walked through the morning with soot on our sleeves and Sef's sword still sticky, though he'd cleaned it three times with a rag that had given up being a rag somewhere around the second pass. I kept my hands busy instead of thinking — checking the map-tube, checking the sigil-glow beneath my wrist-wrap, checking that my fingers still had ten of each. They did. It seemed ungrateful to ask for more.

"Your shoulder's bleeding again," Sef said.

"It's a scratch."

"It's a gouge."

"You say tomato," I said, which made no sense in any language spoken in this kingdom, and he gave me the look he reserved for when I said things like that — the look of a man doing arithmetic about how much trouble one scribe could possibly be worth.

The answer, apparently, was: quite a lot, because half of Merrow's Cross had tried to kill us before breakfast.

* * *

We found the astronomer by accident, or she let us believe it was an accident, which in my experience is the same thing with better manners.

Her tower stood a mile past the town's eastern edge, alone on a bald hill, built of grey stone and stubbornness. There was no door on the ground floor. There was a rope ladder hanging from a window forty feet up, and painted above the window, in letters visible from the road, were the words:

**I HAVE NO NAME AND THEREFORE NOTHING YOU WANT. GO AWAY.**

"She sounds charming," Sef said.

"She sounds like someone who has been robbed before." I took hold of the ladder. "Also she's expecting us. Look — the rungs are freshly oiled."

Sef squinted upward. "You can tell that from here?"

"I'm a scribe. I notice maintenance." I began to climb. From below, he muttered something about scribes and death wishes, and then the ladder creaked under his considerably larger weight, and we both pretended his arms weren't shaking by the thirtieth rung.

The woman waiting in the window was small and round and approximately as old as the hill, with white hair cropped close as a winter field and ink stains on her fingers that put mine to shame. She looked me over once, top to bottom, and her eyebrows climbed toward her hairline like two caterpillars fleeing a fire.

"Well," she said. "You're glowing."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Under the wrap. Left wrist. It leaks." She stepped back and waved us in with an air of a woman admitting two cats into her house because the alternative was listening to them cry all night. "Come in, come in. Mind the orrery. If you break the orrery I will feed you to it."

The room inside was a cathedral of brass. Rings upon rings upon rings hung from the ceiling, nested spheres turning in slow, silent orbits around a central lamp, and every surface — floor, walls, the underside of the roof — was papered with charts. Not star charts, or not only. Moon charts. Hundreds of them. Thirteen moons drawn again and again in silver ink, their paths braided across the pages like the plaited hair of some enormous sleeping girl.

One moon had been crossed out. Over and over and over, in what looked increasingly like fury, the thirteenth circle bore a single line through it.

"The thirteenth turn," I said.

The old woman went very still. "Say that again."

"A man said it. Dying, at our temple gate. 'The thirteenth turn.' And then—" I hesitated. Then: *he pressed a map into my hands and whispered my name, and now there is a sigil written in my blood that lights up when I cross borders.* "And then other things."

She studied me the way I studied a manuscript of doubtful provenance: thoroughly, skeptically, and with mounting interest. Then she thrust out her hand.

"Maren Oduya. Formerly Astronomer Royal of Aurelholm. Currently a madwoman on a hill, according to the Crown's official position, which I encourage you to adopt, because madwomen on hills live longer than Astronomers Royal." Her grip was surprisingly strong. "Tea?"

"Yes, please."

"No," said Sef, at exactly the same moment. We looked at each other.

"He doesn't drink tea," I translated. "He drinks suspicion."

"One cup won't hurt you," Maren told him, already pouring from a dented kettle. "It's chamomile. If I wanted to poison you I'd have done it on the ladder. Cheaper."

Sef took the cup. He did not drink it. He held it, steaming, like a small hostage.

* * *

Maren sat us down beneath the great orrery and asked to see my wrist, and I unwound the wrap because — I don't know. Because she'd earned it somehow. Because anyone who paints GO AWAY in letters that big is telling the truth about at least one thing.

The sigil lay along the inside of my forearm where the map had burned itself weeks ago: fine lines of pale gold beneath the skin, branching like frost on glass, like rivers seen from very high up. When Maren leaned close, the lines brightened faintly, as if they knew they were being read.

"Fascinating," she breathed. "Horrible. Fascinatingly horrible." She fetched a magnifying lens the size of an egg cup and peered until her nose nearly touched my arm. "Do you know what you're looking at, girl?"

"A map."

"A map is a drawing of a road." She straightened, wincing, one hand pressed to her lower back. "This is a *contract*. A contract with a road in it, and a tollbooth, and a signature, and — if my reading is right, and it is, I spent thirty years reading the sky while lesser men read ledgers—" she shot me an apologetic glance, "—no offense—"

"Some taken."

"—a clause of termination. This isn't ink on leather anymore. The stranger didn't give you a map. He gave you *himself*. Or what was left of himself. The bearer carries the key in their blood, and the lock—" she pointed upward, vaguely northward, toward a capital none of us could see, "—the lock is sitting on a throne wearing a dead man's crown."

Sef set down his tea. He hadn't touched it, but setting it down was its own kind of statement. "You know about the king."

"I know about the moons." Maren hauled herself up, joints crackling like green firewood, and began turning the rings of the orrery. Brass whispered against brass. Twelve small silver moons slid into motion along their tracks. "Everyone looks at the sun and thinks the sky is simple. One light, steady, dependable. But the moons — ah, the moons are gossip. They argue. They fall in and out of step like dancers after too much wine." She flicked a ring, and a thirteenth track appeared — empty, a groove with nothing riding in it. "Except once every long while, they agree. All twelve, aligned, and the thirteenth moon — the dark one, the one you can't see, the one your grandmothers called the Debt Collector's Eye—" I flinched, and she noticed, and filed it away with the efficiency of a lifelong scholar, "—comes home to roost in the middle of them. Conjunction. Every power in the world holds its breath, and doors that are usually shut stand open."

"And that's when it happened," Sef said quietly. "Three years ago."

"Three years ago, yes. I watched it from the observatory in Aurelholm. The most beautiful alignment in four hundred years, and I remember thinking — *someone will do something unforgivable tonight.* People always do, when the sky makes a doorway." Her voice flattened. "Two days later, the King's astronomers were dismissed. Then arrested. Then, in several cases, drowned. I was faster than the others. Slower than wisdom, faster than the guards." She smiled without any humor in it whatsoever. "I've had three years on this hill to work out what was done, using nothing but arithmetic and spite. Both of which I have in abundance."

She turned another ring. The twelve moons crept toward alignment on the brass tracks, and the empty thirteenth groove waited at the center like a missing tooth.

"Here's what I know," she said. "Whatever sits in Aldric's skin anchored itself during the conjunction. Such things cannot simply *stay*, you understand. They aren't native to this world. Holding on requires purchase — a fixed point, a nail driven through the fabric of things. The binding was cast that night, keyed to the conjunction, and the conjunction is also the only time the nail can be pulled." She tapped the empty groove. "The moons come round again soon. Very soon. Five weeks, two days, and — call it nine hours, accounting for drift I haven't fully corrected. On that night, the thirteenth turn completes, and if the thing is still seated, it will have everything it needs to make the binding permanent. No more lucid hours. No more cracks. The king will be gone — not dead, worse than dead, *furnished over* — and whatever wears him will wear this kingdom until the mountains wear down to sand."

The orrery turned. The little moons crawled. In the silence I could hear my own heart, and it sounded embarrassingly loud.

"Five weeks," I said.

"Five weeks, two days, nine hours, plus or minus the drift."

"You said that."

"Because you didn't write it down." She nodded sharply at my sleeve. "You've got ink on your fingers, girl, and a scribe's habit of looking at everything like it's a page. Write it down. Things written down survive. Things only heard get argued with."

I wrote it down. My hand shook slightly, which annoyed me, so I wrote it again, steadier, and Maren approved with a grunt.

* * *

Sef stood at the window while Maren fed the fire, watching the road we'd come by. Watching, I suspected, for pilgrims. For friendly faces. For men whose smiles sat wrong.

"Unbinding it," he said, without turning. "You said the conjunction opens the door. Does it also mean the thing will be strongest that night? At its most defended?"

Maren snorted. "Of course. Doors swing both ways. That's the cruelty of it — the only night the lock can be picked is the night the whole house is awake and armed." She glanced between us. "You're going anyway. I can see it. You've both got the look. I used to see it on young knights reporting for coronation guard — that particular mixture of terror and duty that smells, honestly, rather like vomit."

"We don't have a plan," I admitted. "We have a direction and an argument."

"That's how all the great plans start. The good ones just lie about it afterward." She settled into a chair across from me, and for the first time since the window, she looked tired. Old. Frightened, maybe, in the way of people who have been frightened alone for so long they've forgotten the posture for it. "Then listen to me, scribe, because this is the part I worked out third year, alone, with nothing but arithmetic and spite, and it kept me alive when knowing it should have gotten me killed."

I leaned forward.

"The binding is a *sentence*," she said. "Not a punishment — a sentence in the grammatical sense. Subject, verb, anchor. Something must be named to bind something else in place. Whoever cast it three years ago needed a living word to hang the magic on — a bearer, a vessel, a page. Your dying friend at the temple gate was that page. Now you are." Her eyes dropped to my wrist, where the golden lines pulsed gently, patiently, like something breathing in its sleep. "Which means the same rule runs backward. To pull the nail, you don't need an army. You need to *unwrite the sentence*. And the only pen that works is the bearer's own blood."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the bare trees on the hill, and the whole tower creaked like a ship considering whether to sail.

"So I'm not carrying the weapon," I said slowly. "I am the weapon."

"You're carrying the weapon the way a quill carries the poem," Maren said. "Whether that comforts you or terrifies you depends entirely on how much you trust the hand that holds the quill." She looked at me over her folded hands. "And that, girl, is the question nobody can answer for you. Not me. Not him." A nod toward the window, toward Sef's rigid shoulders. "Not even the sky. Though the sky has opinions, and lately its opinion is: *hurry*."

* * *

We stayed the night, because Maren threatened us with the orrery, and because the road was dark and my shoulder had graduated from gouge to grievance.

She gave me her cot and took a chair, refusing all argument with the terrifying serenity of the elderly. Before I slept, she caught my wrist — the marked one — and turned it toward the lamplight, studying the sigil one last time.

"When I was Astronomer Royal," she said, quieter than she'd spoken all day, "I kept records the Crown never saw. Private ones. Names of everyone the purges took. Charts of the conjunction, annotated. Three years of observations of the palace, timed to the minute." She released my wrist. "There's a copy of everything in the false bottom of that chest, under the wool socks. If you're going to the capital, you'll need a reason to be let inside the walls, and 'glowing scribe' is not currently on the list of acceptable pilgrim types. Take the records. Forge yourself a better story. You strike me as a woman who lies beautifully on paper."

"That," I said, "is the kindest accusation anyone's ever made about me."

"Don't thank me yet." She banked the fire, and the light shrank to a red coal, and her voice came out of the darkness dry as old parchment. "Thank me in five weeks and two days, if the moons come round and you're still holding a pen."

I lay in the dark and did not sleep for a long time. Through the window, past the brass rings hanging silent overhead, I could see the sky — vast and cold and crowded, twelve moons wheeling in their patient courses toward an appointment five weeks away, and the space where the thirteenth would sit, empty now, waiting to be filled.

Five weeks, two days, nine hours.

Somewhere north of us, a king was drowning slowly inside his own body, mouthing words no one could hear. Somewhere behind us, agents of the Crowned were counting the bodies in Merrow's Cross and asking questions about a scribe. And here, on a bald hill full of paper, an old woman who had lost everything except arithmetic and spite had just handed me the only thing I feared more than debt collectors:

A deadline.

In the morning, we would take the records and the road, and point ourselves at the capital like an arrow loosed at a moving target. But lying there in the dark, watching the moons crawl, I understood at last what the dying man had meant at the gate — not *thirteenth turn* as a warning, but as a promise. The sky keeps its accounts too.

And it was coming to collect.
