## 12. The Astronomer's Price

The astronomer lived at the top of a tower that had no business still standing.

Sef said as much while they climbed the spiral stair, which had eaten most of the morning and was now eating his knees. "The whole thing leans."

"It leans *charmingly*," Paloma said from above him, her voice slightly breathless. She'd taken the stairs two at a time out of what he suspected was stubbornness rather than enthusiasm. "Like an old man listening to gossip. Vesha said to look for the tower that listens."

"Vesha," Sef said, "describes everything as if it were a person. I once heard her call a locked door 'shy.'"

"And you called a horse 'that traitorous bag of bones,' so perhaps we're all allowed our little poetry."

The tower did lean. It leaned west, toward the hills outside Merrow's Cross, and its uppermost window — the one glowing with lamplight against the late afternoon — jutted out over empty air like a chin thrust in defiance of every law of masonry Sef had ever pretended to respect. He put his hand on the wall and felt it hum faintly, the way a plucked string hums after the music stops.

Magic. Faint, but real, this far from the valley's silence. Three weeks on the road and he still hadn't gotten used to the way the world outside Vael Hollow prickled against his skin like wool worn too long.

The door at the top was painted with stars. Most of them had flaked away; the ones remaining clustered near the handle as though even paint got lonely. Paloma knocked. From within came a crash, a curse in a language Sef didn't recognize, and then footsteps approaching with the unhurried confidence of someone who owned nothing worth stealing except knowledge, and knew it.

The door opened onto a woman roughly the shape and temperament of a storm cloud: short, gray, round-shouldered, with iron spectacles perched on her nose and a smudge of ink — actual ink, not chalk — across one cheek. She looked them up and down twice, then sighed as if they were the fourth disappointing delivery of the day.

"The scribe," she said, pointing at Paloma. "And the knight who isn't a knight anymore." Her eyes, behind the smudged glass, were sharp as awls. "Vesha sent word ahead by crow. The crow was rude about you, knight. It called you 'the sad sword.' I'm inclined to agree with the crow."

"That crow has never met me," Sef said.

"No. Crows are excellent judges of character at any distance." She stepped back and held the door wide. "I am Ondile. Come in before my roof finishes deciding whether it wants to be a roof."

* * *

The room at the top of the leaning tower was, against all odds, the most orderly space Sef had ever entered. Every instrument had its place. Brass orreries hung from the rafters at graduated heights like a mobile of tiny worlds. Charts papered the walls floor to ceiling — lunar charts, mostly, thirteen moons' worth of phases drawn in silver ink that caught the lamplight and seemed, when Sef wasn't looking directly at it, to move.

He stopped looking at it directly.

Ondile put water on to boil over a brazier, set out three cups, and then sat down across from them with her hands folded and said, "Show me your wrist, scribe."

Paloma hesitated. Then, with the careful deliberation Sef had come to know — she treated her own body like a ledger that might be audited at any moment — she pushed back her sleeve.

The sigil had spread since the border. What had been a single mark was now a lattice, fine lines of dark gold threading up her forearm like frost growing in reverse. In the lamplight it moved, very slowly, the way clouds move: imperceptibly, but undeniably.

Ondile studied it for a long moment. Then she took off her spectacles, polished them on her sleeve, put them back on, and studied it again.

"Well," she said. "That's unfortunate."

"That's not reassuring," Paloma said.

"I wasn't hired for reassurance. I was hired because I charted the conjunction of the thirteenth moon eleven years ago, predicted it would matter, and was laughed out of the Royal Observatory for saying so." Ondile's mouth twisted. "They gave me a pension instead of a platform. I took the pension. Pensions buy telescopes."

"You knew about the binding," Sef said quietly. "Before it happened."

"I knew something would be *done*, in the window the conjunction opened. I didn't know what, or by whom, or to whom. Astronomy tells you when doors open. Not who walks through them." She stood, went to the wall of charts, and pulled down one near the ceiling with the ease of long practice. "But I can tell you this much now, with the benefit of hindsight and a great deal of spite: the binding that holds your king is anchored to the lunar cycle. Thirteen moons. Twelve turns of the year. And once every — " she tapped the chart — "thirteenth turn, the anchor loosens. For exactly one night, the thing wearing Aldric must spend its strength holding on instead of ruling. That night is five weeks from tomorrow."

"So that's when we act," Paloma said.

"That is when you *may* act. Whether you *can* is another question entirely." Ondile came back to her chair and sat, folding her hands again. "Unbinding requires a bearer. A living sigil. Someone whose blood carries the counter-language. Your friend there is the first such person I've seen in thirty years of looking, and I have seen perhaps four candidates die of old age waiting for a fifth."

Sef kept his face still. He was good at that. He'd had three years of practice keeping his face still while everything under it screamed.

"What do you need from us?" Paloma asked.

"From you? Nothing yet. Preparation comes later — calculations, timing, a route into the palace that doesn't exist on any map I'll draw for you until I trust you." Ondile turned her head, slow as a siege engine, toward Sef. "From *him*, I need one thing. And I need it tonight."

The kettle began to whistle. None of them moved to take it off the fire.

"Name it," Sef said, because the alternatives were worse.

"Tell me what happened at Caelmoor." 

The name landed in the room like a dropped anvil. Sef felt it go through the floorboards, through the tower, through the hillside beneath. Beside him, Paloma went very carefully still — he saw it happen, saw her scribe's stillness settle over her like a garment, and hated that he recognized it, and hated more that he'd taught himself to read it.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"Mm." Ondile poured the tea without asking anyone. "Eleven years ago I published a monograph on resonance anchors. Six people bought it. One of them wrote to me afterward — a royal magus, very flattering, very curious about how a binding might be timed to a conjunction. We corresponded for two years. Then, three years ago, on the night of the last thirteenth turn, the correspondence stopped mid-sentence. And the king changed. And a knight of the kingsguard — a decorated one, Corvane, the pride of some provincial academy or other — vanished from the rolls the same week." She handed Paloma her cup. "I don't need you to confess to a crime, knight. I need to know the mechanics. What was used. What words. What anchor-point. Because the man who bound your king is dead — I've confirmed that — and whatever he wrote down burned with him, unless someone carried it out. And you walked out of Caelmoor carrying something. Everyone who flees carries something. I've never met an exception."

The tea steamed. The charts rustled faintly in a draft that shouldn't have reached the top of a sealed tower.

Sef looked at his cup and found he had no memory of taking it.

* * *

Here is what Sef Corvane thought, in the silence that followed:

He could lie. He was good at lying; he'd been lying so long it had grown comfortable, like an old boot reshaped to his foot. He could give Ondile a version — partial, plausible, shaped around the truth the way a glove is shaped around a hand. *I served the magus. I believed I was protecting the king. I fled when I understood.* All of that was even true. It was simply not all of the truth, and the part he'd leave out was the load-bearing wall.

*I held the king's arm while the sigil was cut.*

*I watched his eyes and told him it would be over soon, the way you comfort a horse.*

*I signed the witness line.*

He could lie. Ondile would likely accept it. Paloma might accept it. The mission would continue, clean and unencumbered, and he would carry the rest of it alone, as he had carried it alone for three years, as he deserved to carry it, because —

Because.

"Corvane." Ondile's voice was not unkind. That was somehow worse. "I've watched men decide things for fifty years. You're deciding right now, and I can tell you're deciding wrong."

"Is that so."

"You're weighing the truth against the mission and calling the truth a luxury." She sipped her tea. "Truth is never a luxury. It's ballast. Throw it overboard and the ship rides lighter right up until the storm flips it."

"With respect," Sef said, "you don't know what you're asking me to say."

"I know exactly what I'm asking you to say. That's why I'm asking." She set down her cup. "Here is my price, plainly, because I'm old and tired and have no patience left for games: I will give you the timing, the mechanics, and the only viable approach to the throne room — knowledge that will keep your scribe alive, incidentally, which I notice matters to you — and in exchange you tell me, honestly and completely, what happened the night of the binding. Not for my curiosity. For accuracy. If I don't know what was done, I can't know precisely how to undo it, and approximations kill people. Usually the wrong ones."

The fire popped. Somewhere below them, in the town, a dog barked at nothing in particular, and Sef envied it profoundly.

He became aware, distantly, that Paloma was watching him.

Not staring. Watching — the way she watched ledgers, the way she watched strangers at market, gathering data with that patient, terrible thoroughness of hers. She had gone quiet in a new way. Not her road-quiet, not her tired-quiet. A listening quiet. And Sef understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that whatever he said next would be read twice: once by the astronomer, and once, far more carefully, by the woman beside him who had trusted him for three hundred miles on nothing but his word.

His word. Which was, at present, the word of a man about to refuse.

"I can't," he said.

It came out quieter than he intended. He made himself say it again, properly. "I can't tell you that. Not here. Not — " *not in front of her* — "not now."

Ondile nodded slowly, without surprise. "Then I'll tell you what happens next. You'll find another way into the palace. You'll guess at the timing. You'll be wrong in some small, fatal particular, because bindings of this kind are exact, and exactness is precisely the thing I'm selling." She rose, joints crackling, and walked them toward the door with the courtesy of a hostess ending an execution. "My price stands for as long as the moon allows. Five weeks. My door is open. So is my offer."

At the threshold, Paloma paused. Sef felt her pause the way you feel a change in wind.

"Thank you," she said to Ondile, gracious as ever, courteous as ever, and Sef thought: *she learned that stillness in a temple, and temples teach you to be polite to people you're measuring for coffins,* and the thought was unfair and true in equal measure.

The stairs down were darker than the stairs up. Nobody spoke.

* * *

They made it four streets before she said anything.

Merrow's Cross at dusk was a town holding its breath. Lamps lit in windows where no one had names to light them for. A woman swept her step with the mechanical patience of someone who had forgotten why sweeping mattered but remembered that forgetting was the enemy. Sef walked with his hood up and his hand near his sword, because towns like this taught you habits, and Paloma walked beside him with her arms crossed against the cold and her mind, evidently, working.

"You could have lied to her," she said at last.

"Yes."

"It would have worked. She wanted specifics, not sincerity. You could have fed her half of it dressed up as the whole."

"Yes."

They crossed the empty square. The fountain at its center was dry, and someone had arranged stones in its basin in a rough circle, the way people arrange stones when they want to remember a shape they can no longer name. Paloma glanced at it and looked away quickly.

"So why didn't you?" she asked.

Sef considered several answers. *Because lies compound. Because she'd catch it. Because the mission.* All serviceable. All, tonight, unbearably hollow.

"Because you were in the room," he said.

That landed badly. He watched it land badly. He watched her stop walking — actually stop, boots scraping on cobblestone — and turn, and look up at him with those gray eyes that missed nothing, and he realized he had just said the single most revealing sentence available to him, and that it revealed everything except the thing itself.

"Because I was in the room," she repeated.

"Yes."

"Sef." Her voice was gentle, and gentleness from Paloma Hartwell was more frightening than anger, because she deployed it precisely, like a scalpel, and she was deploying it now. "You refused a confession that would save lives. To protect *me* from hearing it?"

"To protect — " He stopped. Started again. "There are things a man should be permitted to bury. That's all. Some graves shouldn't be opened just because someone asks nicely and pours tea."

"And some graves," Paloma said, "have people standing on top of them, pretending they're solid ground."

The words sat between them in the lamplit dark. Down the street, the dry fountain waited. Above, the leaning tower glowed with its single stubborn window, and somewhere in it, an old woman was presumably making notes about the sad sword and the fool who loved — no. Who *trusted*. Who traveled with him. Whom he had, through three years of disciplined cowardice, managed to deceive about nearly everything except the fact that he was deceiving her.

Which was, he understood suddenly, the cruelest trick of all: she had known. Not the content. The *shape*. She had known there was a locked room in him, and she had knocked politely for three hundred miles, and tonight she had finally heard him refuse to open it, and she had filed it away in that meticulous, unforgiving memory of hers.

"I'm going back to the inn," she said.

"Paloma."

"Not tonight." She held up a hand — not angry, worse than angry, orderly. "You don't owe me the story. You've made that clear. But I'm done pretending I haven't noticed that you're keeping it. Sleep well, Ser Corvane."

She walked away down the street, small and straight-backed and utterly alone in a town full of people who had lost their names, and Sef stood in the square and understood that he had paid for Ondile's refusal with something more expensive than knowledge.

He had paid with the first crack. And cracks, he knew from towers, from walls, from men — cracks do not stay the size they start.

Behind him, in the dark above the dry fountain, something shifted — a shadow detaching from a doorway where no shadow should have been, moving with the unhurried purpose of someone following orders — and Sef's hand found his sword hilt as three weeks of instinct sang a single note of warning through his spine.
