## 13. River of Small Treasons

The barge was called the *Merry Widow*, which Paloma thought said everything anyone needed to know about the barge master's sense of humor and possibly his marital history. She did not ask. On a vessel forty feet long and crewed by six taciturn river-folk, curiosity was a currency best hoarded.

Three days out of Merrow's Cross, she had learned the rhythm of the river: the groan of the hull at dawn when the mist lifted, the soft slap of water against tarred planks, the endless creak of the sweep-oar where old Doss stood steering with one hand and chewing on a pipe he never lit. The *Merry Widow* carried wool downstream and salt upstream, and now, illegally, three passengers.

Paloma sat in the lee of the wool-bales near the stern, a ledger-board balanced on her knees, pretending to practice her penmanship. This was her first and finest deception: nobody on a river barge questioned a scribe practicing letters. It was like questioning a fish for swimming. Sef had even nodded approvingly the first morning, murmured something about keeping her skills sharp, and gone forward to help haul rope, because a disgraced knight apparently could not bear to be idle even when idleness was the entire point of paying for passage.

Which meant that while the whole boat believed she was writing the alphabet over and over like a diligent schoolgirl, Paloma Hartwell was committing small treasons against an empire.

She glanced up. Doss had his back to her. The two deckhands were arguing about eel-traps in the low, bored way of people who had been having the same argument for years. Sef was forward, coiling line, his shoulders doing that thing they did when he thought no one was watching — drawing inward, bracing against some weight only he could feel.

She looked back down at her board.

The map-tube lay hidden beneath the false bottom of her traveling case, but the map's true hiding place had never been leather. Since the border of Vael Hollow, since the night the sigils had burned their route into her skin and faded, Paloma had begun to suspect what Vesha would later confirm with such terrible clarity: the map lived in her blood now. Which meant she could not simply unroll it and copy it. She had to remember it.

And remembering, it turned out, was a skill like any other. You could get better at it. You could practice.

Her method was simple and, she suspected, deeply forbidden. Each evening, in the dark of the sleeping tent she shared with nothing but her own breath, she would close her eyes and let the sigils rise behind them — not forcing, just watching, the way you watched shapes in smoke. Then, before sleep took them, she would fix one in her mind like a specimen pinned under glass. In the morning, she wrote it down from memory. And then came the treason proper: she studied it.

Not copied. Studied. The distinction mattered enormously to her, though she could not have explained why without admitting how much of the temple scribe still lived in her spine. Copying was obedience. Study was appetite. The Silent Temple had spent nine years teaching her that ink was a tool of devotion, that letters existed to serve the liturgy and the ledger, and that a scribe who asked *why* a text was written was a scribe on the road to trouble. Well. Here she was, on an actual road, asking why with every stroke of the pen.

Because there was grammar in the sigils. She was almost certain of it.

* * *

The discovery had come on the second morning, quite by accident, and it had made her drop her stylus into the bilge, which she still mourned.

She had written out three sigils from memory — the ones she privately called *the gate*, *the lock*, and *the listening ear* — and arranged them in a row as they appeared along the map's route. And she had noticed that where *the lock* appeared alone, its lowest stroke curved left. But in the one place on the route where *the lock* appeared beside *the gate*, that same stroke curved right.

Coincidence, probably. Except that Paloma had spent nearly a decade copying temple ledgers, and temple scribes were drilled mercilessly in the science of coincidences — which is to say, in the art of noticing when a number refused to add up. A misaligned column was never an accident. Neither, she'd have wagered her remaining stylus, was a curving stroke.

So she had tried it. She drew *the lock* twice on her practice board, once with the stroke left and once with the stroke right, and pressed her thumb briefly to each — carefully, carefully, ready to snatch her hand away.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. They were ink marks on waxboard, drawn by a debt-bound scribe on a wool barge, not the burning script of the original. But when she looked at them — truly looked, the way the stranger's map had looked *through* her that first night — she felt something. Not power. More like recognition. The faint sensation of standing outside a house where people were speaking a language you almost knew, hearing laughter through the shutters.

Left-curved, the sigil felt closed. Turned inward. A thing locked *against* the world.

Right-curved, beside the gate-sign, it felt... addressed. Aimed. A lock that answered a specific key.

"Grammar," she whispered to the bilge-water, delighted and horrified in equal measure. "It has grammar."

And if it had grammar, it had rules. And if it had rules, it could be *learned*. And if it could be learned —

She had stopped herself there, heart hammering, and done what any sensible person would do: hidden the board under a wool bale and gone to eat porridge with shaking hands.

* * *

"You're smiling at your alphabet," Sef said, settling onto the bale across from her that afternoon. He said it mildly, but his eyes were not mild. They rarely were. "In my experience, people who smile at their alphabet are either saints or forgers."

"Scribes," Paloma said. "You've forgotten the third option."

"I haven't forgotten anything about scribes." Something flickered across his face — there and gone, like a fish turning under brown water. "I've known one or two."

"Oh?"

"The royal chancery kept a dozen of them. Clever people." He picked at a splinter on the bale's edge, not looking at her. "Quiet. Careful. You'd never guess what they could do until they did it."

Paloma kept her face pleasant and her pen moving. *Careful*, she thought. *He's circling something.* Sef Corvane circled things the way cats circled: patiently, pretending indifference, waiting for the moment to pounce or to flee. Lately he had been circling her practice sessions in particular.

"You watch me write," she said pleasantly.

"I watch the river."

"You're facing the wrong way for the river."

He had the grace to look slightly caught. "Habit," he said. "Watching people. It kept me alive once."

"And what do you see when you watch me?"

He considered her honestly, which was somehow worse than suspicion. "A woman who writes the same twenty letters every day and never gets faster at them," he said, "because speed isn't the point. A woman who arranges them in patterns instead of lines. And a woman whose wrist," — here his voice dropped, gentle as a hand on a frightened horse — "glows faintly when she thinks no one can see it."

The river slapped the hull. Somewhere ahead, a kingfisher shrieked its blue opinion of the world.

"It's a birthmark," Paloma said.

"It moves."

"It's a *lively* birthmark."

Sef almost smiled. That was the trouble with him — the almost-smiles were worse than real ones, because real ones you could dismiss, but the almost ones made you wonder what the whole thing would look like, and then you lost track of the conversation entirely. "Paloma." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "I'm not going to ask you what it is. I want you to know that. Whatever it is, it's yours, and you owe me nothing."

"That's remarkably decent."

"But," he went on, "if whatever-it-is ever turns dangerous, I'd take it kindly if you told me *before* it turned dangerous, rather than after. I've seen after." His jaw tightened. "After is expensive."

She wanted to tell him. That surprised her — the wanting. Nine years at the Silent Temple had taught her that secrets were load-bearing walls; remove one and the whole structure of yourself came down. But Sef had carried her across the salt-flats when her feet gave out. Sef had killed a man in Merrow's Cross and gone grey and silent afterward, and when she'd touched his shoulder he hadn't flinched away, which she suspected was more than most people got from him these days.

And yet. There was the other thing, the shape of it always at the edge of her mind: Sef had known the dead stranger's name before she'd spoken it. Sef had been looking for the map before the map had ever reached her. Whatever he carried, he wasn't carrying all of it openly either.

"Noted," she said. "Before, not after. I'll try to schedule my catastrophes accordingly."

"That's all I ask." He rose, paused. "For what it's worth — your alphabet is very convincing."

"Thank you. I had excellent teachers who would be horrified to hear it."

He walked forward, and Paloma exhaled, and understood that she had just lied by omission to the only person on this river who might help her, and that he had very likely just done exactly the same thing to her. Two liars in a wool hold, each politely pretending the other was honest. It was almost companionable.

* * *

That night, anchored in a bend where willows trailed their fingers in the current, Paloma worked by feel under her blanket, tracing sigils on the inside of her own forearm with a fingertip — not writing them, just walking through their strokes, learning them the way you learned a prayer. *Gate. Lock. Listening ear. The open hand. The closed eye.*

The grammar was revealing itself stroke by stroke, and it was stranger and more beautiful than she had imagined. The sigils weren't symbols *of* things, the way temple glyphs stood for virtues or saints. They were instructions. Verbs. *Open. Hold. Hear. Give. Refuse.* And their relationships — the curves, the junctions, the order — were conjunctions and cases, the connective tissue that turned a pile of verbs into a sentence.

*A lock that answers a gate.* A binding.

She lay staring at the black underside of the tent cloth, and the word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Binding. Sef used it sometimes, half-asleep, in the mutters he didn't know he made. *Shouldn't have bound it. Shouldn't have — * and then he'd wake, and apologize to no one, and go stand in the cold until his hands stopped shaking.

The king, the dying stranger had said. Something wears the king.

If the Crowned had bound itself to Aldric, it had done so with language. With sentences written in this grammar, in flesh or blood or will. And every sentence, however terrible, had a structure — and every structure could be read, and every reading was the beginning of an answer.

Paloma pressed her fingertips to the faint warmth at her wrist, where the sigil slept under her skin like a banked coal.

"They wrote a sentence," she whispered to the dark. "Then I'll learn to read it."

It was the most ambitious thing she had ever said aloud, and she said it very quietly indeed, because somewhere beyond the tent wall, Sef was probably awake too, staring at his own ceiling, carrying his own stone. Small treasons, she thought. Mine against the empire's silence. His against whatever memory rode him. We are both smugglers, he and I. We simply run different cargo.

She slept, and dreamed in curves and junctions, and woke with the answer to a problem she hadn't yet managed to pose: the route-sigil at the map's center — the one she privately called *the crown* — was not a single sign at all. It was four signs interlocked, a sentence folded into a knot.

Unfold it, and you could read it.

She was still sitting with that knowledge, warm and terrifying as fresh bread, when Doss's voice came down the length of the barge, mild as ever and absolutely final:

"We've company. Toll-station ahead. And they're waving us down with something more than a flag."

Paloma closed her practice board over her stolen grammar and looked upriver, where a chain stretched across the gray water and men in pale livery waited on a stone platform — and at the sight of them, Sef's hand went to a sword he no longer wore.
