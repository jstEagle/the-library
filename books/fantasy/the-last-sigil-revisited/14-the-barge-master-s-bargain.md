## 14. The Barge Master's Bargain

The barge was called *Duchess Maren*, which Sef considered an act of optimism on someone's part, given that she sat low in the water like a woman who had eaten every one of her own banquets and intended to finish the table besides. Her hull was green-black with river moss. Her single sail hung slack. Her figurehead—a carved lady whose face had been weathered into a permanent expression of mild reproach—stared down at Sef as though he had already done something disappointing.

"You're the sad-looking one," said the barge master.

Sef turned. The man who owned those words stood on the dock with his thumbs hooked into his belt, and he was built along the same generous lines as his vessel: broad, deep-keeled, unhurried. He wore a coat that might once have been red and a hat that had clearly lost several arguments with the weather. A pipe jutted from the corner of his mouth at an angle suggesting it had been there so long it was now structural.

"I'm sorry?" Sef said.

"Don't be sorry. Be certain." The man sucked his pipe. "You want passage to Aurelholm. You've been asking at every dock from here to the weir, and you've been asking wrong. Too polite. Polite folk get robbed or ignored, and mostly ignored." He looked Sef up and down with the frank appraisal of a man pricing a horse he suspected of being lame. "You've got soldier's shoulders and a beggar's purse and a face like a funeral nobody came to. And you've got her."

He nodded past Sef's shoulder. Paloma stood at the end of the dock, haggling with a fishwife over dried eels with the focused intensity of a general negotiating a treaty. She caught Sef looking, held up two fingers—the price—and then one finger, and turned back to the argument wearing the small, stubborn smile she thought he hadn't learned to read yet.

"The scribe," the barge master said. "She writes things. I've seen her writing things since you moored. Little book, little pencil, always writing."

"She keeps accounts," Sef said carefully.

"Everyone keeps accounts." The barge master removed the pipe and pointed it at him. "Question is what kind. Mine are written in beer and bad decisions, and I can account for every drop. Come aboard, funeral-face. We'll talk terms where the fishwives can't listen. They hear everything. It's how they got so rich."

* * *

The cabin of the *Duchess Maren* smelled of tar, tea, and forty years of the same pipe smoked in the same room, until the smoke itself had become a sort of furniture. The barge master poured tea from a pot that had seen honest service, into cups that had not, and introduced himself as Oskar Brant, Master of the Duchess Maren, Licensed Carrier of Goods Both Legal and Otherwise, and Sworn Enemy of the Tollhouse at Grey Ford, "which is a long story involving a goat, and you don't want it before breakfast."

"We haven't eaten," Paloma said.

"Then you do want it before breakfast. Marvelous." He launched into it anyway, and it was indeed about a goat, and by the end of it Sef still did not understand what the goat had done, but Paloma laughed twice—real laughs, startled out of her—and Oskar looked so pleased with himself that Sef understood the whole story had been constructed for exactly that purpose.

"Now," Oskar said, setting down his cup. "Passage to Aurelholm. Nine days on the water, if the wind cooperates, six days if it doesn't and we row, and I'll warn you now: my crew consists of my nephew Tam, who is seventeen and strong as a bull but thinks with his elbows, and myself, and I rowed my last good stroke during the reign of the old queen, whom I miss terribly, mostly because she taxed fairly and told excellent jokes." He laced his fingers over his belly. "So. What have you got?"

Paloma produced coins. Not many of them; Sef watched her count them out with a scribe's precision, and watched Oskar watch her too, with the expression of a man reading a ledger and finding it interesting rather than sufficient.

"That gets you to Grey Ford," Oskar said, "where the tollhouse sits, and my sworn enemy will charge you double for looking suspicious, triple for the sword, and quadruple because he knows me and holds grudges the way other men hold breath. After that you're walking, and between here and Aurelholm there are three checkpoints and a stretch of road where they say the Crowned's people take names right off travelers' tongues." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all. "You're going to Aurelholm for something to do with the Crowned. Don't tell me what. I don't want to know what. But you've got that look."

"What look?" Sef asked.

"The look of people carrying something heavier than their luggage." Oskar refilled his cup. "I'll take your coin. All of it. It's not enough and we both know it. In exchange, I'll give you passage, meals, a berth behind the cargo netting where the tollmen won't look, and my personal guarantee that no one on this boat will ask you a single question you don't want answered." He paused. "In exchange, you carry one passenger."

Sef felt it before he understood it—that drop in the stomach, the old kingsguard instinct waking up and sniffing the air. "A passenger."

"A child," Oskar said. "From Merrow's Cross."

The cabin went very quiet. Outside, water slapped against the hull in its patient way, and somewhere above them Tam was arguing with a rope and losing.

"Merrow's Cross," Paloma repeated. Sef glanced at her. She had gone very still, in the particular way she went still when something touched the wound the town had left in her—the streets where people wandered smiling and nameless, calling one another *friend* and *you there* because their own names had been taken as tribute. He remembered how she'd gripped his arm in that town square. He remembered thinking she looked like someone watching her own grave being dug.

"There were children there," she said quietly. "We saw them. They didn't—" She stopped. Started again, more carefully, the scribe choosing words like tools. "They didn't cry. That was the worst part. Children who can't remember their names should cry."

"This one remembers hers," Oskar said. "That's why she ran."

He told it plainly, the way men tell stories they've decided not to embellish. A girl, eight years old, maybe nine. Hiding in the reeds along the bank when the Crowned's collectors came through Merrow's Cross with their ledgers and their vials. She'd seen what they took. She'd run upstream, half-starved, and been pulled out of the river by Tam, who had been fishing instead of working, as usual, and who had then hidden her under nets for two days while deciding whether to tell his uncle.

"He told me this morning," Oskar said, "because he is a coward about important things and brave about goats, and not the reverse, which would be more useful." He tapped ash from his pipe into a dish shaped like a fish. "The collectors come through again in three days. They check boats. When they find her—and they will find her, she snores like a stevedore—they'll take whatever name she has left, and then they'll take the rest, because a child with no name can't be missed by anyone. Can't even be reported missing. There's no word for her absence. Do you see? They've built a machine where stolen children leave no hole in the world."

Paloma had both hands flat on the table. Sef realized she wasn't breathing properly.

"And you can't take her yourself," Sef said slowly, "because a barge master known to the collectors, carrying a stowaway—"

"Is a dead barge master and a captured child, yes. They know my face. They know my route. They let me work because I pay my fees and ask nothing, and the moment I become interesting, I become useful to them, and then it's over." Oskar's voice stayed level, but Sef heard the thing underneath it—the sound of a man who had lain awake doing this arithmetic. "You two, though. You're going to Aurelholm anyway. You're strangers. You're already running from something, which means you're good at it. And you're going to the Undermarket."

It wasn't a question. Sef kept his face still and felt Paloma do the same beside him, and Oskar smiled without much humor.

"Everyone going to Aurelholm quietly goes to the Undermarket eventually. It's where the city keeps everything it pretends not to have." He leaned forward. "There's a woman there. Runs a safehouse for the nameless. Old smuggler, meaner than a wet cat, heart like a locked drawer. They call her Mother Quill. Get the girl to her, and she'll give her paper, protection, a place to grow up angry and free. That's all I'm asking. Passage for delivery. One job."

"And if we refuse?" Sef asked.

Oskar spread his hands. "Then I take your coin, carry you to Grey Ford, and spend the rest of my life knowing there's a girl under my nets waiting for the collectors, and that I chose a stranger's comfort over her. Which is a weight, friend. I've carried weights all my life. This one I'd rather not."

* * *

They stood at the rail afterward, watching the river move. It was late afternoon, and the water had gone the color of hammered copper, and the far bank was just trees and shadow and the occasional egret standing in the shallows with the smug patience of a creature that has never owed anyone anything.

"You want to say no," Paloma said.

"I want to say a great many things." Sef rested his forearms on the rail. "I want to say that taking a child toward the capital—toward *that* city—is madness. I want to say we can't protect ourselves, let alone her. I want to say that every extra person is a liability, a witness, a weakness the Crowned's agents can use." He turned his head. "And I want to say yes. Both things at once. It's very inconvenient."

Paloma almost smiled. "Welcome to my last month."

"Paloma." He made himself look at the water instead of at her, because it was easier to say the next part to copper-colored river. "If they take us—if it comes to that—a child can't run the way we can. She can't hide the way we can. And if they learn what you are—" He stopped. They had agreed, somewhere on the salt road without ever saying it aloud, not to speak of the sigil near strangers. Even now he lowered his voice to almost nothing. "If they learn what you carry, everyone near you becomes leverage. That's not cowardice. It's arithmetic."

"It's arithmetic," she agreed. "And arithmetic is what got me indentured at fourteen. Somebody added up what I was worth against what I cost, and I lost." She looked out at the river. "Oskar's doing arithmetic too. His says the girl costs more than the coin. Most people's say otherwise. Most people's always say otherwise."

Sef said nothing, because there was a truth sitting in his chest that had been sitting there since Caelmoor, three years and a lifetime ago: that he himself had once done that arithmetic, weighed one man's life against a kingdom's peace, and chosen the cold column, and told himself it was nobility ever since. He looked at Paloma adding up a child's worth against their danger and arriving at the answer he couldn't quite bring himself to arrive at first, and he felt the old shame turn over in its sleep.

"Yes," she said. "Obviously yes. Before you argue further."

"I wasn't going to argue further."

"You were going to argue further. You have a whole speech. I've seen you load speeches." She finally looked at him, and there it was—not forgiveness, they weren't there yet, but the working alliance they'd built, the thing that was becoming harder to name. "Sef. In Merrow's Cross, when we figured out what they'd done to those people—I couldn't sleep. I lay there thinking about a town full of people who couldn't grieve their own mothers because they didn't know who their mothers were. And now there's one girl who *escaped*, who kept her name, who is hiding under a fishing net waiting for us to be the ones who show up." Her voice didn't rise. It never rose. It just got quieter and sharper, like wire. "I spent eleven years copying other people's debts into ledgers. I am not going to write this one off."

"All right," Sef said.

"All right?"

"I said all right. I agree with you. I was always going to agree with you. I merely wanted it noted, for the record, that I identified the risks." He straightened. "Also I wanted to delay saying yes long enough to preserve some dignity."

"Noted," she said gravely. "For the record. Dignity preserved."

* * *

The child was brought aboard after dark, wrapped in a tarpaulin among the eel baskets, which Sef felt was undignified until he saw her emerge from it—small, sharp-elbowed, with river-mud dried in her hair and eyes that took inventory of everything and trusted none of it. She looked at Sef the way you look at a dog of uncertain temperament.

"Are you the sad sword?" she asked.

Sef blinked. "...What?"

"Tam said. He said there's two of them coming, an ink lady and a sad sword." She looked past him to Paloma and her whole face changed—cautious, hungry, the way a stray looks at a lit window. "You're the ink lady. Are you really a scribe? Can you write anything? Can you write *my* name?"

Paloma crouched down to her height, which Sef had noticed she did naturally, without the condescension most adults carried like luggage. "What's your name?"

The girl hesitated. Then she leaned in close, cupping her hand around her mouth as though the river itself might be listening, and whispered it. Sef didn't catch it. He saw Paloma's expression, though—saw her go careful and bright at once, like someone holding something breakable.

"That's a good one," Paloma said. "Hold onto it tight."

"I do. I hold it at night." The girl patted her own chest, over her heart, matter-of-fact. "Under the ribs. That's where I keep it. Where do you keep yours?"

Paloma opened her mouth. Closed it. For one strange moment she looked at Sef over the child's head with something unguarded in her face—something that had to do with a map burned into skin, and blood that spoke a language, and the growing suspicion, which Sef shared and had said nothing about, that her name might be worth more than either of them knew.

"Somewhere safe," she said at last.

The girl—Wren, Tam called her, because she'd been found in the reeds and it stuck, and she allowed it the way royalty allows a nickname—was shown to the berth behind the cargo netting. She inspected it, pronounced it acceptable, claimed the corner nearest the wall ("in case of running"), and was asleep within minutes, mid-sentence, still talking about whether eels had feelings.

Sef stood watch on deck that night, as he had every night since the salt road. The river breathed beneath the hull. Somewhere downstream, three days away, the tollhouse waited with its grudge and its scales. Somewhere beyond that, the capital glittered like a mouth full of gold teeth, and inside it, something crowned and ancient wore a king like a stolen coat.

Behind him, soft as breath, a child snored in the hold, holding her name under her ribs where no collector could reach it.

Oskar found him at midnight, offered him the pipe, was refused, and smoked in companionable silence for a while.

"They'll keep her safe," Oskar said finally. Not a question. A request.

"If it's in my power," Sef said, and heard how thin that sounded, and hated it.

Oskar nodded anyway, as if thin promises were the only kind sailors ever got, and maybe the only kind worth making. "Then that's the bargain," he said. "Sleep if you can, sad sword. Grey Ford at dawn the day after tomorrow, and my sworn enemy doesn't believe in mercy before noon."

But sleep did not come easily to Sef Corvane, and it did not come at all that night—for as the moon rose fat and white over the water, he counted the nights remaining on his scarred fingers, the way he did every night now, and arrived at the same answer that had been chasing him down the river:

Five weeks until the thirteenth moon turned.

And somewhere ahead, in the gilded dark of Aurelholm, the thing that wore King Aldric had begun, perhaps, to dream of them coming.
