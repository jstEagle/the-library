## 15. Wren

Wren had three important rules for surviving on the barge, and she had invented all of them herself, which made them the best rules in the world.

Rule one: never let anyone see you counting your bread.

Rule two: if a grown-up asks where you're from, say "from," and then look sad until they stop asking.

Rule three: the ink lady is scary but safe. The sad sword is safe but scary. Learn the difference before you need to.

She was getting very good at rule three. She practiced every day from her hiding spot behind the barrels of dried fish, which smelled like the bottom of everything and had become, over four days on the river, something like home.

The ink lady's real name was Paloma, but Wren had heard her say it only once, quietly, to herself, the way you'd check that a coin was still in your pocket. Mostly she was the ink lady because of what her fingers did. They were always stained dark to the second knuckle, and she was always writing—on scraps of paper, on the wooden rail, once, memorably, on the back of Wren's own hand when Wren had drifted too close and been caught.

*Hold still,* the ink lady had said, drawing a small looping shape between Wren's knuckles. *There. Now if anything with too many teeth comes near you tonight, it will taste this first and find it bitter.*

"Is it magic?" Wren had whispered.

"It's chemistry," the ink lady said. "Magic can't get you here anyway."

"That's not true. The map lady has magic."

The ink lady had gone very still, the way a cat goes still when it hears a sound in the wall. "What map lady?"

Wren had decided, in that moment, that some information was worth trading. "You show me yours first."

That was how Wren learned that the ink lady could make a tiny light sit in the air like a sleepy firefly just by pressing her wrist, and that when she did it, her face looked frightened and happy at exactly the same time, which was a very confusing face to see on a grown-up. Grown-ups were supposed to pick one feeling and commit to it.

In exchange, Wren told her about the map lady: how sometimes, when Wren was almost asleep, she saw a woman made of folded paper standing at the end of the barge, and the woman unfolded and unfolded and inside every fold there were roads, and the roads all led somewhere Wren couldn't see but could feel, like a toothache in her chest.

The ink lady hadn't laughed. That was the thing about her. Other grown-ups would have laughed and patted her head and told her dreams weren't real, which was a lie so lazy it wasn't even worth being scared of. The ink lady had written down every word.

* * *

The sad sword's name was Sef, which was fun to say, like spitting a seed. Wren knew his name because he'd told her directly, crouching down to her level on the second morning, which no grown-up ever did unless they wanted something or were sorry about something.

"Wren," she'd said. "Like the bird."

"Like the bird," he agreed gravely, as though this were an exchange between two generals.

He was enormous. Not the tallest man Wren had ever seen—the tax collector in Merrow's Cross had been taller—but the biggest in the way a door is big, in the way a locked thing is big. He had a sword wrapped in cloth slung across his back, and he touched the wrapping more often than he touched anything else, the way other men touched lucky charms or old scars.

Here was what Wren had figured out about the sad sword: he thought nobody could see him being sad, because he kept his face very still, like a pond pretending to be a floor. But ponds have edges, and things fall into them.

For instance: every evening, after the ink lady went below deck to write, the sad sword would stand at the stern and look back the way they'd come, toward Merrow's Cross. And his hand would go up—not all the way, just partway, like he was waving to someone who wasn't there—and then drop again, and then he'd touch the wrapped sword, and go inside, and be normal-shaped for the rest of the night.

Wren had waved back at him once, from behind the fish barrels, when his hand came up. He hadn't seen. She was glad. Some things you shouldn't catch people doing, even good things. Especially good things. Good things embarrassed people worse than bad ones did.

* * *

On the fifth morning, the rain came sideways, and Wren's hiding spot stopped being a secret, because the ink lady found her shivering behind the barrels and didn't yell at her for stowing away, even though technically Wren was cargo, not crew, and technically nobody had asked the barge master's permission to keep a child aboard.

"You'll freeze back here," the ink lady said. It wasn't a question or a scold. It was just a fact, delivered the way she delivered facts, like coins counted out onto a table.

"I'm not cold."

"You're blue."

"Blue is my color."

Something happened to the ink lady's mouth then. It pulled sideways, against her whole face's opinion. "Come inside, Blue."

And that was how Wren got a new name, which was better than her old one, because her old one had been taken away by men in gray coats who wrote it in a ledger, and this one had been given by someone who drew protective chemistry on children's hands, which was a much safer kind of person to take a name from.

Inside the little cabin, the sad sword was already sitting with his knees nearly to his chin, because the cabin was built for smaller men or less careful ones. He moved over without being asked and put his cloak around Wren's shoulders, and the cloak smelled like woodsmoke and iron and something underneath that might have been fear, worn smooth from long carrying.

"There's tea," he said. "It's terrible."

"Why?"

"The barge master makes it from river water and spite."

Wren drank it. It *was* terrible. It was also hot, and nobody took it away from her, and she held the cup with both hands and felt the heat go all the way through her like she was the cup.

They sat in the small cabin while the rain worked at the roof, and Wren watched them the way she watched everything, which was closely and without apology.

The ink lady wrote. Her pen scratched and scratched, and occasionally she'd stop and stare at her own wrist, where there was a mark that glowed faintly when the lamp guttered—a thin line like a road drawn on skin. When she stared at it, the sad sword watched her staring, and when the sad sword watched her, his face forgot to stay a floor, and all these sad shapes fell into it, one after another, like leaves into water.

"You should sleep," the sad sword said finally. "You've been writing since Thornbridge."

"So have you been watching me since Thornbridge."

"Somebody has to count the river miles."

"You don't know any river miles."

"I'm learning them." He paused. "Slowly. From you."

The ink lady made the sideways-mouth thing again, but bigger this time, and Wren realized with a jolt that it was a smile, or the skeleton of one, waiting for flesh it hadn't worn in years. Then the ink lady caught Wren watching and the skeleton went back underground.

"What?" she said, sharper than the words needed.

"Nothing," said Wren, who had survived Merrow's Cross by knowing exactly what *nothing* meant and when to say it.

But later, when the lamp was low and the sad sword pretended to sleep with his hand resting on the wrapped sword the way other men rested hands on their own chests, Wren crawled over to the ink lady and whispered:

"He does that wave thing at night. To the south. You should know, in case it's a signal."

The ink lady went still. Then, softly: "To the south."

"Toward the town with no names." Wren picked at a thread on the borrowed cloak. "He looks sorry about something. Grown-ups are always sorry about things they won't say. My mother was sorry about my father for six years and never said his name once, and then one day she said it, and that was the day we left, and I never found out which saying it was that made us leave."

The ink lady looked at her for a long time. In the low light, her eyes were the color of the ink on her fingers, and Wren thought: *she writes everything down except herself.*

"You're not like other children," the ink lady said.

"Yes, I am. I'm exactly like other children. Nobody ever checks."

That got the full smile, the one with flesh on it, brief as a fish jumping. Then it sank again, and the ink lady said, "Sleep now, Blue," and Wren slept, wedged between two dangerous, damaged adults who were both lying about something, and it was the safest she had felt since the gray-coated men came to Merrow's Cross with their ledgers and their long faces and their terrible pens.

Because here was the thing Wren knew that the grown-ups didn't know she knew:

People who lie about big things still tell the truth with their hands.

And the ink lady's hands drew wards around sleeping children. And the sad sword's hand rose every night toward a town he'd failed, halfway, like a man practicing an apology he'd never finished.

Neither of those hands would hurt her. Wren was very sure. Being sure was its own kind of magic, and unlike valley-chemistry, it worked anywhere.

* * *

On the seventh day, the river narrowed and the banks grew steep and dark, and the barge master called down that they'd reach the capital's outer docks by tomorrow's dusk if the wind behaved, and the wind, being a thing that had never behaved in its life, did not answer.

That evening Wren climbed up beside the sad sword at the stern, uninvited, because uninvited was the only way she arrived anywhere.

He glanced down at her. "It's cold out here."

"I'm wearing your cloak. Technically it's you who's cold."

He huffed, which from him was practically music. For a while they watched the water slide past, brown and patient, carrying everything toward Aurelholm whether it wanted to go or not.

"The ink lady says you were a knight," Wren said.

"She says too much."

"Were you?"

A long pause. The wrapped sword rose and fell on his back with his breathing.

"I wore the coat once," he said.

"Do you still have the coat?"

"No."

"Good," said Wren. "Coats are how they know you. If you don't have the coat, you can be somebody else."

The sad sword looked down at her then, really looked, the way you look at a lock when you realize the key has been in your pocket the whole time. His eyes were wet at the edges, or maybe that was river spray; on a barge you could blame the river for almost anything.

"When did you learn that?" he asked quietly.

"The same place everyone learns it," Wren said, and leaned her head against his arm, which was like leaning on a dock post, if dock posts could be sad. "The place where they take things."

Downriver, past the last bend, far off but coming closer, lights were gathering in the dark—hundreds of them, thousands, gold and white and merciless, strung along a skyline like a crown someone had set down on the earth and forgotten to pick up again.

Wren didn't know yet what waited under those lights. But she felt the folded-paper woman stir in the folds of her almost-sleep, unfolding one page further than ever before, and on the newest page there was a door, and beneath the door, a name being spoken over and over by something that had none of its own.

She squeezed the sad sword's arm.

"They're waiting for us," she said.

"I know," said Sef Corvane. "So am I. That's the problem."
