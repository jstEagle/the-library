## 16. Checkpoint at Thornbridge

The trouble with forgery, Paloma had discovered on the second night of practice, was that most forgers thought too much about ink and not enough about boredom.

Anyone could fake a seal. Seals were vanity; they announced themselves in gold and wax and wanted to be admired. But a temple travel writ—*that* was designed to be ignored. It was meant to be glanced at by a bored customs clerk who had already seen forty that morning and would see sixty more before supper. A convincing forgery didn't look perfect. It looked *tired*. It had a smudge where a thumb had dragged through drying ink. It had a correction made in the margin because some overworked temple clerk had miscounted the escort allowance. It had been folded wrong—not once, but twice, by two different hands, because nobody refolded a document along its original creases unless they cared about it too much.

Paloma had spent eleven years caring about documents exactly as much as she was supposed to, which is to say not at all beyond what her debt required. She knew how clerks thought because she had *been* the clerk. She had been the bored one at the desk, waving people through, her eyes sliding off papers without reading them.

So when the road bent north out of the hill country and the walls of Thornbridge rose against the afternoon sky—grey stone, black banners, and the unmistakable geometry of an imperial checkpoint—she did not panic.

She stopped at a milestone, sat down on it, and asked Sef for his knife.

"Why?" he said.

"Because I need to trim this quill, and because if you hand it to me without arguing we'll both feel like this is normal, and feeling like things are normal is ninety percent of not being arrested."

He handed it over. He was getting better at that.

Wren watched from atop a fencepost with the serious attention of a small bird observing weather. "Are you going to make us real now?" the child asked.

"I'm going to make us *boring*," Paloma said. "Boring is better than real. Real people get remembered."

---

She worked by the light of the lowering sun, using the flat of the milestone as a desk. The materials were humble: a stub of good goose-quill she'd bought with their last coins in Merrow's Cross, a stick of sealing wax she'd warmed over the barge master's stove before they'd parted ways at the river landing, and three sheets of decent rag paper liberated from a chapel donation ledger whose contents had been, she felt, adequately honored by memory.

The writ itself took shape under her hand in the cramped, upright hand of the Silent Temple of Vael Hollow—the hand she'd been beaten into fluency in, the one script in all the world she could write half-asleep, which was fortunate, because her hands were shaking slightly and pretending very hard not to be.

*SILENT TEMPLE OF VAEL HOLLOW, DEPT OF EXTERNAL SCRIPORIAL AFFAIRS.*

There was no such department. That was the beauty of it. The Temple's whole doctrine held that nothing of Vael Hollow should leave the valley—no goods, no correspondence, no souls. In consequence, the outside world had only the vaguest notion of how the Temple organized itself, and any official confronted with a temple envoy would assume the paperwork must exist somewhere, because paperwork always assumed other paperwork existed. Bureaucracy was a hall of mirrors, and Paloma had lived long enough inside one mirror to know how the reflections worked.

The envoy: *Sister Maren Duskmantle,* returning from a survey of heretical record-keeping in the river towns. Escort: one hired guard, *Corvane, S.* — she'd kept his actual surname because the best lies were load-bearing truths — noted as *muted by vow, communicates by slate.* This served two purposes: it explained why Sef wouldn't speak at the checkpoint, and it flattered him, because Sef had the particular pride of men who believed silence was dignified even when it was merely terrified.

And the child. She'd turned the quill over twice before deciding.

*Accompanying ward: foundling, unnamed, in transit to Temple care per Doctrine of the Unmarked.* Every scribe in the empire knew the Doctrine existed, mostly as a rumor, mostly as a thing temples claimed to do and never did. It was the perfect fiction precisely because everyone half-believed it and no one had ever seen it exercised. Wren, who genuinely had no name to forge, would pass inspection as the most authentic person in the entire party.

"You're making me paperwork," Wren said, delighted. "I'm a *document* now."

"You're a *doctrine*," Paloma corrected. "Try to act obscure."

"I can act very obscure." Wren screwed up her face into an expression of such profound blankness that Paloma briefly worried the child had actually died standing up.

"Good. Keep that. If anyone asks you questions, answer them slowly and strangely."

"That's just how I talk."

"Wonderful. You're a natural."

* * *

They slept badly in a ditch and walked the last mile in the grey of morning, arranged in what Paloma hoped read as procession and probably read as three exhausted people walking in a suspiciously straight line. Sef wore the plain hooded cloak of a hired sword, sword wrapped in sackcloth on his back like farm equipment, which was a lie so brazen Paloma almost admired it—no farmer ever carried a blade that long, and no guard would look closely enough to notice.

Thornbridge's checkpoint occupied the town's single stone bridge over the Thorn, because empires are lazy and checkpoints go where the geography does the work. Two barriers, a customs house squatting beside them like a toad, four soldiers in imperial grey, and above the far barrier, freshly painted, a proclamation board bearing the Crowned's seal: a crown with no head beneath it.

Paloma looked at the empty crown for exactly as long as everyone else did—which is to say she looked away quickly—and stepped up to the customs desk.

The clerk was perhaps fifty, with the complexion of a man who had spent thirty years indoors judging travelers, and the eyes of a man who had judged every one of them and found the exercise wanting. He held out his hand without looking up.

Paloma placed the writ in it.

Here is what she had learned in eleven years of indentured scribery, and what she deployed now with the calm of a woman laying out cards she had marked herself: *do not watch him read it.* Watching invited scrutiny. Instead she busied herself adjusting Wren's cloak, frowning at it with mild professional irritation, the way a temple sister genuinely would fuss over a foundling's collar. Her peripheral vision did the work. Peripheral vision, she had long ago decided, was the honest part of the eye.

The clerk read. His lips moved slightly. He reached the escort notation—*muted by vow*—and glanced up at Sef, who stood with his hands folded and his face arranged into the expression of a man who has taken vows of silence chiefly to spare the world his opinions. It was, Paloma admitted, a very good expression. Sef had clearly practiced it on someone.

"Vael Hollow," the clerk said. "That's the silent lot. Down in the—where the magic doesn't—" He waved a hand vaguely, as if the concept itself were damp.

"The valley," Paloma said, with the gentle patience of the clergy.

"Right." He looked back down at the writ. And here Paloma felt the moment tilt, the way she'd felt a thousand ledgers tilt—one line, one figure, one thing that didn't balance. His finger came down on the page. "This department. External Scriporial Affairs. I haven't heard of—"

"Few have," Paloma said, and let one degree of weariness enter her voice. Not much. A degree. "The Temple prefers it that way. The Doctrine of Separation is rather undercut by a visible foreign office." She leaned in slightly, conspiratorially, and lowered her voice as though sharing a professional grievance. "I've filed the same quarterly report for six years. As far as I can determine, no one reads them. Possibly no one knows the office exists. I've begun to suspect *I* may not exist."

Something flickered across the clerk's face—the twitch of a man recognizing a fellow inhabitant of bureaucratic purgatory, the universal brotherhood of the unseen. "Six years," he said. "I've stamped this bridge for nineteen."

"Nineteen," Paloma echoed, with reverence. "You must know every kind of liar there is."

"I do," said the clerk, and stamped the writ.

* * *

It should have ended there. Paloma would think about that later—how close the edge always was, how the difference between passage and catastrophe was often one sentence, one soldier's mood, one breath of wind.

What happened was this: as the clerk waved them through the first barrier and they crossed the bridge's crown, a sergeant at the far barrier—a younger man, ambitious, the dangerous kind of bored rather than the safe kind—stepped into their path and said, "Writ."

"We've been cleared," Paloma said pleasantly.

"Bridge clearance. Now I need party clearance." He held out his hand with the confidence of a man who had recently invented a procedure and fallen in love with it. "Names of all persons. Written. In your own hand. For the register."

The register. Of course there was a register. Empires loved registers the way magpies loved silverware: indiscriminately, and with eventual ruinous consequences. Names written in an imperial book were names that could be checked, cross-checked, summoned, sold. Names written here would follow Sister Maren Duskmantle into whatever archive swallowed such things, and archives talked to each other in ways that forged departments did not survive.

"Of course," Paloma heard herself say. "Though I should note"—she was already unshouldering her scribe's case, already producing paper, buying seconds the way she once bought candle-hours from her own sleep—"the foundling cannot be registered under a name. Doctrine of the Unmarked. You'll want to note her as a doctrinal entry, not a nominal one. Your registrar will understand. If you write a name for her, I'm afraid the entry becomes void and we'll all have to start again at the first barrier, and Sergeant—" she glanced at his insignia and performed a small, apologetic wince, "—I don't believe Corporal Hask would enjoy learning his bridge lost its afternoon to a paperwork error."

She did not know whether the man at the first barrier was named Hask. She did not know whether he was a corporal. She knew only that men who invent procedures hate being corrected by their own inventions' paperwork, and that the fastest way to make a soldier obedient was to make him afraid of a form.

The sergeant's jaw worked. "Fine. The child goes in as doctrinal. The rest of you—names."

"Sister Maren Duskmantle," Paloma said, writing it in her neat temple hand as she spoke. "And my escort—"

"Corvane," Sef said.

The word dropped into the morning like a stone into still water.

Paloma's quill did not stop—it was the one advantage of a life spent writing while terrified—but every muscle below her neck clenched in sequence, like doors slamming down a corridor. Because the writ said *muted by vow*, and muted men did not announce themselves in clear, carrying, faintly noble voices, and the sergeant's head was turning toward Sef with the slow delight of a dog that has finally been thrown something.

"Didn't know the mute ones could talk," the sergeant said.

Sef's hand had moved. Paloma saw it happen—the small, catastrophic shift of weight onto the back foot, the right arm hanging loose the way it did when the sword-wrapped bundle on his back stopped being luggage and started being an option. Three steps away, four imperial soldiers. A bridge with one exit behind and one ahead. Wren's small hand in hers going very still.

"*Brother Corvane*," Paloma said loudly, stepping neatly between them, "is permitted speech *once daily*, at noon, as a discipline of humility. He spends it on his name." She turned and looked up at Sef with an expression of radiant, saintly disappointment—the look of a woman who has explained this rule to him nine hundred times. "Brother. We discussed this. It isn't yet noon."

Sef stared at her. Something warred in his face—pride, alarm, the reflex of a swordsman who had spent three years solving problems with the speed of his arm meeting, for the first time, a problem shaped like a woman's raised eyebrow.

"It isn't," he said at last, grudgingly, "noon."

"Noon," Paloma agreed, "is at noon," and turned back to the sergeant with a smile of long-suffering serenity. "You see what I endure. Write him down as Corvane, S., escort, mute-by-vow-with-exception. The exception is new. The Temple is experimenting." She wrote it. She signed the register with a flourish that was almost, but not quite, the flourish of a woman with something to hide. Then she added, mildly, "You may note in your log that the escort's speech occurred at mid-morning, unauthorized, and was formally rebuked. It will save questions later."

The sergeant, who had prepared himself for violence and been handed instead a lesson in ecclesiastical administration, visibly deflated. He stamped the register. He waved them through with the air of a man glad to see the back of all of them.

They walked off the bridge into Thornbridge proper, past the proclamation board with its headless crown, and did not speak until the checkpoint was out of sight behind the first row of houses.

Then Sef said, quietly, "Once daily at noon."

"Mm."

"I don't spend it on my name."

"You do now. It's in the register. Congratulations—you're a man of rigid discipline." She kept walking, but her heart was doing something undignified in her chest, and she suspected he could hear it. "Also, your hand was moving toward your sword. On an imperial bridge. In front of four soldiers. While carrying a child's trust and my entire plan in a satchel."

"He insulted—" Sef began, and stopped, because there was no way to finish that sentence that didn't end in *me*, and Sef Corvane, whatever else he was, apparently couldn't say *me* out loud to save his life.

"He called you a mute," Paloma said. "I called you disciplined. Only one of those is true, and only one of those got us across the bridge." She softened, because his face had gone stiff and wounded in a way she recognized—the look of a proud animal deciding whether a kindness was a trap. "Your sword would have gotten us killed. My pen got us through. That's not a criticism, Sef. It's an inventory. We're going to need both before this is done, and it would help enormously if you learned which tool the moment is asking for."

They walked on through streets waking up—shutters banging open, a baker's boy hauling baskets, ordinary life resuming around three liars and a doctrine. Wren trotted between them, holding a crust someone had given her at the bridge, and said, with her mouth full:

"You lied beautifully. Even your hands lie beautifully."

"Thank you, Wren."

"The sad sword nearly ruined it, though."

"I heard," Sef said.

"The sad sword knows," Wren agreed solemnly, and offered him a piece of crust, which he took, which was, Paloma thought, the closest thing to absolution Thornbridge was going to issue that day.

* * *

They took a room above a dye-shop that smelled of mordant and secrets, because Paloma reasoned that anyone searching for fugitives would avoid premises where every surface stained. Sef went out to find bread and rumor; Paloma sat by the window with the last of the map-work burning faintly along her forearm whenever she let her mind drift near it, copying sigil-forms into her private notebook, teaching her blood's grammar to her hand.

It was there, mid-stroke, that Sef came back up the stairs faster than a man carrying bread climbs stairs, and stood in the doorway with color gone from his face.

"There's a rider asking questions at the stable yard," he said. "Imperial courier. Grey livery. He's showing a sketch around." He paused, and Paloma watched him choose, deliberately, to finish the sentence. "It's a drawing of a knight. Old kingsguard insignia on the tabard. They're calling him a traitor of Caelmoor."

Outside, the dye-shop's vats breathed their chemical quiet, and Paloma looked at the man who had spent a week lying to her gently, and wondered—not for the first time, but now with a name attached—what exactly had been buried at Caelmoor, and whether it would reach them before the thirteenth moon did.
