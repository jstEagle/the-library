## 17. What Sef Buried at Caelmoor

Three years and one season before the thirteenth moon, in the autumn the leaves at Caelmoor turned the color of old blood, Sef Corvane still believed he was a good man.

He thought about this often now, on the road, in the dark before sleep took him. Not the belief itself—that had died somewhere between Caelmoor and everywhere else—but the *mechanics* of it, the way a lie fits itself into a man so comfortably that he stops feeling its edges. He had been a kingsguard then. Sworn steel of Aurelholm, second chair from the king's left hand, which was a position of no particular glory but considerable proximity, and proximity to power, he had learned, was its own kind of intoxicant.

He remembered the day Magus Orenn came to him because it had rained, and because Orenn hated rain, and because a man who hates rain will say almost anything to get out of it.

* * *

"Ser Corvane." Orenn stood in the doorway of the guard barracks, water streaming off his grey cloak, his hood pulled low. He was not an old man—fifty, perhaps—but magic had a way of aging its practitioners unevenly, like a house that settles badly, and his face seemed to have been assembled from three different decades. "A word. In private."

Sef had been mending a strap. He set it down. "If this is about the wine cellar incident—"

"It is about the king's life," Orenn said, and something in his voice made the barracks feel suddenly smaller.

They walked through the rain to the east tower, where the magus kept his workrooms. Sef remembered thinking: *this is what trust looks like*. The royal magus did not share his stairwell with just anyone. The royal magus did not look over his shoulder twice at a landing unless the matter was grave.

The workroom smelled of hot metal and dried lavender, which Sef would later learn was the scent of binding-ink, though that night he assumed it was some scholar's affectation. Candles everywhere. A table cleared to bare wood except for a single sheet of vellum covered in writing so fine it looked like frost.

"The king is going to die," Orenn said, without preamble. "Within the month. Poison, most likely—I've traced the plot as far as I can, and there are hands in the kitchens I don't recognize. Hands with money behind them."

Sef felt his spine straighten. This was, after all, the thing he existed for. "Who? Give me names and I'll have their heads on—"

"No." Orenn pressed his palm flat against the vellum, as if holding down a living thing. "That's precisely what we cannot do. Kill the assassins and more come. There is a patron behind them, ser, and patrons do not exhaust themselves. We cut off one hand, they grow two." He looked up, and his eyes were very tired and very certain, which is a dangerous combination in any face. "But there is another way. An old way. The king can be made... unreachable. Wrapped so deep inside himself that no poison, no blade, no whisper can touch him. Until the danger passes."

"How long?"

"A year. Perhaps two."

"And afterward?"

"Afterward, we unmake it. Simple as untying a knot." Orenn smiled, and Sef would spend three years remembering that smile and hating himself for having believed it. "I need a guard I trust in the room. Someone whose presence the king accepts. The working requires a witness bound by oath—a steady hand, a loyal heart. I could think of no one else."

There it was. The flattery, folded into the duty, warm as a coal slipped into his pocket. Sef Corvane, steady hand, loyal heart. Chosen above all others.

He said yes. Of course he said yes. He would say yes again tomorrow, knowing everything he knew now, and that was the part that kept him walking instead of lying down in some ditch to be done with it: he could not honestly claim he'd been tricked. He'd been *shown* a story he wanted desperately to live in—the faithful soldier, the savior behind the throne—and he had stepped into it and buttoned it up to the chin.

* * *

King Aldric met him in the garden two days before the working, and Sef remembered this scene too, remembered it with the awful clarity of things that cannot be revised.

"You look grim, Corvane." The king was pruning roses, of all things, sleeves rolled to the elbow, thorn-scratches along his forearms. He'd never quite outgrown the country lord he had been before the crown; he liked dirt under his nails and complained that palace bread tasted of nothing. "Has Orenn been filling your ears with doom? He does that. It's how he flatters people—by letting them in on catastrophes."

"He told me about the plot against you, majesty."

"Mm." Aldric snipped a dead bloom and let it fall. "There's always a plot. My grandfather survived four. My father, six. You learn to sleep through it eventually." He glanced sideways, and his eyes were quick and kind. "You believe him, though. I can see it. You've got the face of a man who's decided to carry something heavy."

"I want to protect you, majesty. That's all."

"I know it is." The king set down the shears and gripped Sef's shoulder, callused thumb pressing hard. "And that's why I'll tell you this once: whatever Orenn asks of you, if anything about it makes your stomach turn—if it smells wrong, if it *feels* wrong—you come to me first. Not him. Me. Promise me that, Ser Corvane."

And Sef had promised.

Three days later, standing in the sealed vault beneath the throne with binding-ink drying on his own wrist—a small anchor-mark, Orenn called it, purely ceremonial, merely to hold the circuit steady—he heard the king screaming from the antechamber where two of Orenn's other men held him, and every hair on his body told him this was wrong, and he did not go.

That was the moment. Not when the sigil closed. Not when the light went strange and gold and the air filled with the smell of lavender and burnt sugar. The moment was earlier, smaller, quieter: the moment he heard the screams and stayed where he stood, because Orenn's hand was on his arm and Orenn was saying, urgently, reasonably, "*It hurts him now only so it cannot hurt him later, you swore to protect him, this IS protection*—" and Sef's legs, traitorous, sensible legs, remained exactly where they were.

He had told himself a story in that moment too. He could hear himself telling it, even then, some detached part of his mind narrating: *This is necessary. This is medicine. Medicine tastes bitter.* And the narrator's voice was calm, and the calm was the worst thing about it, because calm meant he had already chosen, and the rest was just ceremony.

When it was done, the king lay quiet on the stone floor, breathing slowly, his face smooth as a coin. Orenn knelt beside him and wept, or produced something that looked remarkably like weeping, and said, "He is safe now. No one can reach him. In a year, perhaps less, we wake him, and he will never know how close death came."

"And the men who helped hold him?" Sef asked. His voice sounded far away.

"Will be rewarded and dismissed. Loose ends, ser." Orenn smiled again. "You understand."

He understood. That was the horror of it—he understood immediately, completely, the way you understand a trap only after your leg is in it. He was a loose end. The steady hand, the loyal heart, the witness bound by oath: all of it useful right up until the hour it became evidence.

So he ran. Not that night—he wasn't brave enough for that, or perhaps he was cowardly enough to wait until morning, history would never settle the question—but within the week, using an excuse so thin he couldn't now recall it. Resignation from the kingsguard, citing family matters in the provinces. Nobody stopped him. That frightened him most of all, looking back: how easily the door opened. As if someone had greased the hinges. As if leaving were also part of the design.

* * *

The first year of flight, he drank. The second year, he stopped drinking and started counting, which was worse. Counting the moons. Thirteen in the cycle, everyone knew that, thirteen full turns before the great conjunction when the sky's doors all opened at once—and Orenn's knot was tied to the conjunction, Sef had learned that much in the tower before the working, reading upside-down papers while pretending to study a tapestry. A year, Orenn had said. Perhaps two.

Two years came and went, and the king did not wake.

Sef wrote letters he never sent. To the council: *Your king is imprisoned; check the vaults; ask the royal magus why the court physician hasn't bled His Majesty in three years.* To old comrades in the guard: *Look at the king's eyes at the solstice feast. Look closely. Whose eyes are those?* He burned each letter within a day of writing it. What was the use? Who believed a disgraced kingsguard with a drinking history and no proof? And worse—who *believed*, and acted, and brought Orenn's attention down on the handful of innocents Sef might still protect by remaining invisible?

Invisibility became his discipline. He got good at it. He learned which roads to walk at night and which faces to wear, and he learned, above all, to stop looking in mirrors, because the man in the mirror kept asking questions with no comfortable answers.

*Did you save the king, or bury him?*

*If Orenn lied about the year—about the plot, about the poison, about all of it—then what exactly did you swear your steady hand to?*

*And why, ser knight, why oh why, did the screaming not stop you?*

The third question was the one he never managed to answer honestly, and so of course it was the one that visited nightly, punctual as a debt-collector. Because the truth was that some part of him—the part that had been flattered, the part that had finally mattered to someone important—had *wanted* the working to be real. Had wanted to stand in a candlelit vault doing secret, terrible, necessary things for the crown. Heroes in the stories never get to save anyone in daylight. He had wanted the story so badly that when the king screamed, he filed the screaming under *the price of the tale*, and stood still.

He was not sure, even now, which disgusted him more: Orenn's lie, or his own hunger for it.

* * *

Then came the stranger.

Sef had tracked the map the way he tracked everything now—patiently, invisibly—from the capital's underworld to a courier who didn't know what he carried, to a border town, to a dying man riding hard for the one place in the world where no magic could follow him. Vael Hollow. The silent valley. Where the Crowned's agents could not scry, could not sense, could not reach.

The previous bearer, running for sanctuary. Running to die somewhere the sigil couldn't be harvested.

And Sef had watched from the ridge as the temple refused the man at the gate—as they refused a bleeding man, these pious keepers of their immaculate silence—and he had watched a young woman in temple-grey slip out with a cup of water, breaking rules written into her bones, because someone was hurting and rules could go hang.

He had seen the glow on her wrist from thirty yards. Faint. Gold. The same color as the light in the vault beneath the throne.

*Oh no,* he had thought, with a clarity that felt almost like relief. *Oh, no. Oh, you poor girl.*

Because he recognized the pattern. He had spent three years studying it from the outside, the way you study a scar. The stranger hadn't simply carried a map. The map had been moving toward her—or she toward it—and now it lived in her blood, and she didn't know yet, and the world's cruelest joke was that the only person alive who fully understood what was waking inside Paloma Hartwell was the man who had helped tie the knot in the first place.

She needed him. That was the unbearable arithmetic of it. She needed his knowledge—the vault's layout, the working's grammar, the shape of Orenn's lies—and she needed him specifically, irreplaceably, the way a lock needs the key that was cut for it.

And he needed her. Needed her fury and her competence and the terrifying steadiness of her, needed to stand next to someone who broke rules for strangers carrying cups of water, because maybe—maybe—some of it would rub off. Maybe if he walked beside her all the way to the throne room, if he put his guilty knowledge to work at last, the ledger would balance. Or if it didn't balance, if nothing balanced, at least he would be facing the right direction when it came due.

He told himself this on the salt road, walking beside her, trading careful half-lies. *I'm helping her. This is reparation. This is the debt I'm paying.*

And some nights, honest in the dark, he heard the older voice underneath, the voice that had stood still in the vault:

*You're helping yourself. You're building a story again—the repentant knight, the redemption arc. Careful, ser. You've worn this coat before.*

He was getting better at hearing that voice. Hearing it and walking anyway. Perhaps that was the whole of what a man could manage: not purity, not certainty, just forward motion with both eyes open, dragging his self-deceptions along behind him like a sled he couldn't unhitch.

Ahead, Merrow's Cross waited with its stolen names. Ahead, the moons counted down. Ahead, sooner than he wanted, Paloma would start asking questions about Caelmoor—she was sharp, she missed nothing, she had already noticed how he flinched at the word *binding*—and when she asked, he would have to decide, again, whether his legs would stay where they stood or carry him toward the truth.

He prayed they'd move this time. He prayed, and he practiced the confession silently as they walked, shaping the words like a man rehearsing a speech he hoped never to give:

*I helped bind your king. I heard him scream. I stayed.*

*I stayed.*
