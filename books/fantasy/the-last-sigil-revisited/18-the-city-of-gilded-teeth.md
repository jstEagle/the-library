## 18. The City of Gilded Teeth

The first thing Paloma saw of Aurelholm was its teeth.

They rose out of the morning haze like something the earth had grown rather than built—a hundred spires capped in pale gold leaf, catching the sun and throwing it back in shards. From the crest of the last hill on the pilgrim road, the capital looked less like a city than a jaw, open and gleaming, waiting for the road to feed it.

"Stop gawping," Sef said, without any heat at all, because he was gawping too.

"I am *not* gawping," Paloma said. "I am conducting a professional assessment of architectural ambition versus structural sanity."

"Which is?"

"Nobody sane builds a tooth that big."

Wren, riding pillion behind Paloma with her thin arms wrapped around the scribe's waist, stood up on the stirrups to see better. "It looks like it's smiling," she said.

"It's always smiling," said the barge master's cousin, who had lent them three mules and a great deal of unsolicited opinion since dawn. "That's the trouble with it. My gran used to say, never trust a door that smiles before you knock." He spat thoughtfully over his mule's shoulder. "Anyway, you'll want the Pilgrim's Gate. The Salt Gate's closed for repairs, which is what they say when they mean they're searching everyone twice."

Paloma thanked him, and he rode off toward the river gate still talking to no one, which she had learned was simply how people traveled near the capital.

* * *

The road down was lined with murals.

Paloma noticed them one at a time, the way you notice teeth in a smile—one, then another, then all at once. They were painted on every wall facing the road: enormous, luminous, freshly maintained. King Aldric breaking bread with farmers. King Aldric healing a child with a touch. King Aldric standing atop the palace steps, arms wide, while doves rose around him like snow falling upward.

The workmanship was extraordinary. The colors were rich enough to eat. And every single face was wrong.

Not badly wrong—that was what unsettled her. The first mural's king had a long, grave face with deep-set eyes. The second had broader cheeks, a softer mouth. The third was younger, or older, or both, the jaw narrower, the hairline creeping back an inch as though time couldn't decide where to put him. Each painting was unmistakably meant to be Aldric. Each one was unmistakably a different man.

"They can't get the face right," Wren said quietly. She had gone very still against Paloma's back.

"The court painters are rivals," Sef said. His voice was flat in the way Paloma had learned meant he was lying by omission rather than invention. "Each one paints his own idea of majesty."

"That's not how commissions work," Paloma said. "I've illuminated charter documents for temple lords. When there's a patron, there's a likeness. You paint what sits for you, or you paint from a portrait everyone agrees on. You don't paint five different kings." She turned in the saddle to look at him. "Unless nobody will say what he looks like anymore. Unless looking too closely has become dangerous."

Sef said nothing. He nudged his mule forward, which was his second-favorite way of ending a conversation, after pretending to check the hooves.

Paloma let it go—for now—and looked back at the murals. In the nearest one, the king's painted eyes seemed to follow them down the road, and she found herself doing something she despised in others: she stopped looking directly at him.

* * *

The Pilgrim's Gate was a cathedral disguised as a doorway, if a doorway could be forty feet high and staffed by forty bored soldiers. The queue stretched back half a mile, a slow river of carters, peddlers, pilgrims, and one extremely dignified flock of geese whose owner charged a copper apiece for blessings.

"Three coppers each for entry tax," said the gate sergeant, a woman with a voice like gravel being poured politely. "Pilgrim's papers, if you've got them. Temple envoy papers get you through the side arch and no tax."

Paloma's heart performed a small, disciplined somersault. "We're temple envoys," she said, before her brain could veto her mouth.

"Are you," said the sergeant, without interest.

"I'm a scribe of the Silent Temple of Vael Hollow, traveling under seal. These are my escort and my ward." She was already unrolling the satchel as she spoke, already producing ink-stained fingers, already reaching for the blank parchment she carried because a scribe without blank parchment was a fish without water. "You'll want the seal witnessed. It takes me only a moment."

What took her only a moment was, in fact, the most delicate forgery she had ever attempted: a Vael Hollow travel writ, written left-handed, upside down relative to the reader, in the dry formal hand of Auditor Cray himself—the man whose signature she had copied eleven thousand times across eleven thousand debt ledgers, until she could reproduce the way his pen hesitated before the final flourish of his name, that small bureaucratic vanity, the little pause that said *I have signed so many things and I am tired but important.*

She even got the wax right. She'd been carrying a stub of the temple's grey sealing wax in her satchel for years, stolen in a moment of rebellion she'd never had the courage to act on until now.

Sef watched her work with an expression Paloma chose to interpret as admiration and refused to examine further.

"Hm," said the sergeant, reading the writ. She read it twice. She held it up to the light, which made Paloma's palms sweat and her face do nothing whatsoever, because eight years of hiding terror from an auditor had trained her well. "Vael Hollow. That's the valley where nothing works? No magic, no wonders?"

"No magic," Paloma agreed. "Also no taxes on temple envoys, per the Concord of the Nine Shrines."

"Per the what?"

"The Concord of the Nine Shrines," Paloma repeated serenely, inventing nothing, because there was no such Concord, but the sergeant's eyes had already glazed at the word *per,* and somewhere behind those eyes a memory of paperwork far more tedious than this one was asserting itself.

The sergeant waved them through the side arch. "Welcome to Aurelholm. Keep your ward off the fountain squares after dark. Next!"

"You are terrifying," Sef murmured as they passed under the gate's shadow.

"I'm thorough," Paloma said. "There's a difference, and it lives in the margins."

* * *

Above the bridge, the city was everything the murals promised.

Aurelholm climbed its hill in terraces, each one richer than the last, and the light came off the gold-capped roofs in warm sheets. There were fountains that sang—actually sang, tuned pipes buried in the stonework, magic humming through them so gently that Paloma felt it in her teeth like a struck bell heard from far away. After the dead silence of Vael Hollow, where magic pooled nowhere and even prayer fell flat, the sheer *presence* of working enchantment nearly brought tears to her eyes. A street sweeper's broom moved itself in patient arcs. A lamplighter touched each lamp once and walked on while flames kindled themselves. A fruit-seller's display rearranged its own pyramids whenever a customer lingered, presenting the ripest plum with the shameless confidence of a courtier.

"Oh," Wren breathed, hanging off Paloma's arm. "Oh, look at the *plums.*"

"The plums cost more than my yearly wage," Paloma said, "but yes. Look at them."

They crossed the High Bridge over the Aurel River, and Paloma leaned on the balustrade and looked down into the water, and the awe curdled exactly as she should have known it would.

Below the bridge, built into the cut banks and the bridge's own underbelly, was the other city. It had no gold. Its roofs were scrap timber and boat canvas, its streets were mud and stone dust, its light came from cook fires burning whatever would burn. People moved down there in the shadows of the magnificent arch above them, thousands of them, quiet, quick, heads down. And here was the thing that stopped Paloma's breath—not the poverty; she had seen poor, had *been* poor, had audited poor line by line—but the way the people below did not look at each other. Not once. Eyes down, shoulders in, faces carefully empty, like a crowd of people trying very hard to be no one in particular.

In Vael Hollow, the poor were loud. They complained, they sang badly, they argued about bread prices. Down there beneath the singing bridges, nobody was complaining about anything, and Paloma found that far worse than hunger.

"Why aren't they shouting?" Wren asked, in the direct way children ask things that adults have agreed not to notice.

"Maybe they've got nothing worth shouting about," Sef said.

"Everyone's got something worth shouting about," Wren said. "Even if it's just *the plums are too expensive.*"

Paloma laughed despite herself, and then stopped laughing, because a woman on the underbank below them had glanced up at the sound—just one glance, quick and startled, as though laughter were a crime reported by neighbors—and then ducked away into a doorway.

"She looked scared of us," Wren said.

"Not of us," Paloma said slowly. She watched the doorway a moment longer. "Of being seen. There's a difference, and it—" she caught herself before repeating her own aphorism a third time and sounding like a ledger, "—it matters."

They came down off the bridge into the lower streets, because Sef said—and Paloma believed him—that a temple envoy arriving at a palace gate with no appointment would be detained within the hour, and that the first rule of entering a city where someone wanted you dead was to become boring as fast as possible.

Boring, in Aurelholm, meant the tanners' quarter, which smelled like a crime against several gods and cost two coppers a night for a room where the ceiling wept. Paloma had slept in worse. She had slept in worse *this month.*

* * *

They went out at dusk for food, and that was when Paloma began counting names.

It started as a game, or something shaped like a game, to keep Wren cheerful: count the shop signs, read them aloud, rate them. *Hobb's Fine Boots* — four out of five, deducting one point because Hobb's boots were visibly not fine. *The Gilded Tooth* tavern — three points, overpromising. *Marisol's Mending* — five points, honest work honestly named.

Then she realized what she was counting.

Shop signs had names. Shopkeepers had names. But the customers coming and going—the porters, the water-carriers, the women selling chestnuts, the men hauling stone up the terraced streets—they called to each other in shouts and whistles, in *oi* and *you there* and *hey, red cap.* Not one person in an hour called another person by name. Not once. In Vael Hollow, a town of four hundred souls, Paloma knew every name in the parish registers by heart because she had copied them annually, and she could not remember a single day when someone hadn't hollered "Marta!" or "Young Tam!" across the square for some grievance or kindness.

Here, in a city that must hold fifty thousand people, names had gone silent among the poor like birds going quiet before a storm.

"There," she said quietly to Sef, nodding across the street. A child of perhaps ten was chasing a rolling hoop, and an old man caught the hoop and handed it back, and the child said "thank you, sir," and ran on. "*Sir.* To a chestnut-seller. Children don't call strangers 'sir.' They call them by name, wrongly, loudly, because children haven't learned manners. Unless—"

"Unless they've been taught that names are dangerous," Sef finished.

"Taught how?" Wren asked. "Who teaches a whole city?"

"Someone with a very large classroom," Paloma said, and the dread she had been carrying since the murals settled fully into place at last, cold and specific. She thought of the sigil-work glowing on her own wrist, the grammar of binding-magic she had been teaching herself from a dead man's map. She thought of Merrow's Cross, where residents' names had been erased as tribute, and how the sigil-shapes there had looked horribly, intimately familiar. Names could be taken. She had known that in theory. Here was the theory wearing fifty thousand faces and walking home in the dark.

And above it all, lit by the last of the sunset, the golden spires threw their light down the terraces like coins scattered on a table—payment, Paloma thought, for something. Everything gleaming in this city had been bought from somebody.

* * *

They ate chestnuts on the steps of a shuttered shrine—itself a strange thing; in Vael Hollow the shrines were poor but never shut—and Wren fell asleep against Sef's shoulder halfway through her third chestnut, mid-sentence, in the manner of children everywhere, which was to say completely and without negotiation.

Sef sat rigidly for a moment, clearly unsure whether sleeping children were permitted to lean on disgraced knights, then arranged his cloak over her with the extreme care of a man defusing something explosive.

"She trusts you," Paloma said.

"She's asleep. Sleep is bad evidence."

"She trusted you awake, too. She walked between us through the tanners' quarter holding your hand."

"Because you were carrying the satchel."

"Sef."

He looked at the dark street instead of her. Above the rooftops, one of the singing fountains played a slow melody to nobody, sweet and hollow, and somewhere below it the nameless city breathed.

"Those murals," he said at last. "You were right. It's not rival painters."

"I know."

"You don't know. You suspect. There's a difference, and it lives in the margins." His ghost of a smile came and went. "When we go deeper in—when we start asking questions—you'll see things that make the murals make sense. And when you ask me about them, I'll tell you what I can. I need you to believe that part. I'll tell you what I can."

"And the rest?"

"The rest is mine to carry until I can't." He shifted Wren gently. "Get some sleep, scribe. Tomorrow we find the underworld, and underworlds are best entered rested."

Paloma lay back on the cold steps and looked up past the eaves at the sky, where the thirteenth moon hung thin and late among its sisters, waning, five weeks short of its turn. The moon looked like a nail-paring. It looked like something clipped off a larger body and forgotten.

She thought of the vial she'd glimpsed in a peddler's tray that evening—glass stoppered with wax, holding a curl of silver smoke, labeled in neat imperial script with a name she hadn't been able to read before the peddler covered it. She thought of the woman who'd flinched from laughter. Of *thank you, sir.*

Names were currency here. Which meant somewhere in this city, names were being sold.

And Paloma Hartwell had come to the market with the rarest stock of all: a true name, whole and unspent, written into her own blood—carried openly through the gates, past the soldiers, under the smiling golden teeth of a city that ate names for tribute.

She pulled her cloak tighter, told herself firmly that the prickling along her wrist was only the cold, and slept badly among the gilded echoes, dreaming of murals that kept repainting their king's face every time she blinked.
