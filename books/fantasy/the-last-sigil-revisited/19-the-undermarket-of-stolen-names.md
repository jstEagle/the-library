## 19. The Undermarket of Stolen Names

The way down into Aurelholm's underbelly began, as all respectable descents do, behind a fishmonger's stall.

"Mind the eels," the barge master had said, pressing a tarnished brass token into Wren's small fist. "And mind yourselves. The token gets you past the first door only. After that, you're on your own reputation." He'd looked at me when he said it, which was either a compliment or a threat, and with him it was honestly difficult to tell.

So there we were: me, Paloma, and Wren, squeezing through a corridor that smelled of brine and old secrets, while the fishmonger's wife pretended very hard not to see us and gutted her mackerel with the focus of a woman performing surgery.

"I don't like eels," Wren announced.

"Then walk quickly," I said.

"They're *looking* at me."

"Eels don't look at people."

"These ones do."

I glanced down. The barrel by the wall did, in fact, seem to contain an unusual number of eels, and they did seem to be oriented toward us in a way that suggested either remarkable coincidence or remarkable magic. In Aurelholm, I'd learned to bet on neither and simply keep moving.

Paloma had gone quiet since the barge. Not her usual quiet—the working quiet of a scribe counting words, or the wary quiet of a liar measuring her lies—but something flatter. She'd been that way for two days, and I told myself it was the city, the noise, the walls of gilded stone closing in. I told myself a lot of things those days. It's a knight's oldest skill: telling himself stories until the truth stops knocking.

Wren took my hand without asking. She'd been doing that since Thornbridge—since I'd nearly broken a checkpoint officer's jaw over forged papers, and she'd seen whatever it was children see in grown men when the violence wakes up. Since then she held my hand like she was holding a door shut. I let her. I'm not made of stone, whatever the stories say, and most of the stories are lies anyway.

The first door was exactly where the barge master said it would be: an unremarkable door in an unremarkable wall, guarded by an unremarkable man whose arms were anything but. He took the token, bit it, frowned at it as if the token had personally disappointed him, then jerked his thumb sideways.

"Stairs," he said. "Don't talk to anyone wearing white. Don't buy anything that breathes. And if a woman offers to tell your fortune, run."

"Why?" Wren asked.

"She's always right," he said grimly, "and nobody wants that."

* * *

The stairs went down farther than stairs should. I counted ninety-one steps and stopped counting when the count started feeling like a confession. Torches gave way to lanterns gave way to something pale and cold and sourceless that seemed to grow out of the stone itself—a light with no flame, which in my experience meant one thing only: someone down here was buying or selling magic, and doing it quietly enough that the Crowned's agents hadn't burned them out yet.

Or couldn't afford to. That was the other possibility with Aurelholm. Everything above was gilded; everything below was greased. You could buy silence in this city the way you bought bread, and the Crowned's coin, I suspected, didn't reach into pockets already full.

The stairs opened onto the Undermarket.

I have seen battlefields. I have seen the inside of a siege city in its third winter. I have seen what men do to each other when they've decided the other men are no longer men. None of it prepared me for the sight of a market where the merchandise was *people*—not their bodies, not their labor, but the thing underneath all that. Names.

Stalls stretched away in crooked rows under the cold light, hung with lanterns of colored glass, and every stall sold some piece of a self. There were memory-brokers with shelves of stoppered bottles, each swirling faintly with colors that hurt to look at directly. There were face-sellers, displaying masks woven from hair and intention that would settle onto a buyer's skull and become, for a little while, someone else entirely. There were booths selling habits—one promised "the handwriting of a saint, gently used"—and booths selling fears, and one cheerful establishment near the back advertising SECOND CHANCES, SLIGHTLY STAINED.

"This is horrible," Paloma whispered.

"Yes," I agreed.

"It's also—" she hesitated, and I watched her wrestle with the thought, watched her lose, watched her say it anyway. "It's the best-organized archive I've ever seen. Look at the labeling system. Whoever runs this has a *taxonomy*."

Only Paloma Hartwell could stand in the mouth of hell and critique its filing practices. I felt something in my chest loosen slightly, which was dangerous, because I'd been clenching it for three years and wasn't sure I remembered how to go without.

"Wren stays between us," I said. "We find the child's contact, make the delivery, and leave. Nobody browses. Nobody asks questions. Especially you."

"I would never ask questions," Paloma said, in the tone of a woman who had asked four questions before breakfast and been caught at three of them.

"You asked the ferryman his mother's maiden name."

"For *verification purposes*."

"He told you, by the way."

"I know," she said, with deep satisfaction. "It's Ondrelia. Isn't that lovely? It suits her."

* * *

The contact was named Mudge, because apparently in the underworld of the capital even criminals had given up on dignity. We found him—or he found us—in a tea stall wedged between the memory-brokers and a fortune-teller whom everyone gave a wide berth, per the door-guard's advice. Mudge was a round, soft-spoken man with ink-stained fingers and the eyes of someone who counted everything twice.

"The nameless one," he said, crouching to Wren's level. He didn't reach for her, didn't coo, just waited. I approved. Children are cats; chase them and they vanish, wait and they arrive on their own schedule.

"I'm not nameless," Wren said. "I'm Wren. They took my other name but they can't take this one. I picked it myself off a bird."

Mudge smiled like a man watching a candle refuse to go out. "A bird's a fine thing to be. Birds get everywhere." He straightened, produced a ledger from somewhere within his coat—it seemed to be mostly coat—and made a mark. "She'll be safe below. Fed, schooled, hidden. There are others like her. We keep them together. A flock, if you like."

"A flock," Wren repeated, testing the word. She looked up at Paloma. "You could be in the flock too, ink lady. You're almost nameless. Your name's got holes in it."

I saw Paloma go still.

"Holes," I said carefully. "What do you mean, holes?"

But Mudge was already looking at Paloma too, and his eyes had changed—from warm to appraising to, briefly, genuinely sorry. That last expression frightened me more than anything else in the Undermarket so far.

"You're Hartwell," he said. "P. Hartwell."

"How do you know that?" My hand found the hilt of a sword I technically wasn't carrying anymore. Old reflexes die hard; mine had died several times and kept getting back up.

"Because," Mudge said gently, "you've been for sale for eleven days."

* * *

He led us deeper than I wanted to go, past the memory-bottles and the secondhand saints' handwriting, to a stall that sat apart from the others—not larger, but quieter, the way a grave is quieter. The merchant there was a thin woman with silver rings on every finger and no expression whatsoever, which in her line of work was probably a professional courtesy.

Her wares were vials.

Rows of them, ranked on black velvet like teeth pulled from a hundred different mouths. Each vial glowed faintly from within—not the cold stone-light of the tunnels but something warmer and worse, like a hearth seen through a window from a road you can never return down. And each vial bore a small tag in meticulous script.

*Margret of the Ash Rows.* *Tomas Brightwater.* *Ilse, called the Laughing One.*

Names. Bottled names. The tribute-taken identities of Merrow's Cross and places like it, harvested by the Crowned's agents and—this was the part that made my stomach turn with a slow, grinding dread—*sold*. Not hoarded. Sold. The Crowned didn't just erase people; it liquidated them. Someone above was profiting from the unmaking of a town, and someone below was retailing it.

"Eleven days ago," Mudge murmured beside me, "a consignment came through from the north road. Salt-road provenance. Most of it moved fast—there are buyers waiting on anything fresh out of Vael Hollow, the valley's names are considered... pure. Untouched by market wear." His voice dropped. "One lot didn't sell. It's still here. I paid the keeper to hold it when I heard you'd come down the river. Call it a favor to the barge master. Call it decency. Down here we pretend we haven't got any, but we keep a little in reserve, like brandy."

The keeper unlocked a case beneath her table. From it she drew a single vial and set it on the velvet with the tenderness of a priest handling relics.

The tag read: **P. HARTWELL — SCRIBE — VAEL HOLLOW.**

The glow inside it was pale gold shot through with threads of green, and it flickered. That was the worst part. It didn't burn steady like the others. It *flickered*, quick and anxious, like a pulse, like a candle in a draft, like—

Like her.

"That's impossible," Paloma said.

Her voice came out very small. I turned. She was standing with both hands pressed flat against her own sternum, as though holding a door shut from the inside, and her face had gone the color of the vial's glass.

"It knows me," she whispered. "Sef. It's *flickering at me*."

"Who sold it?" I asked the keeper. My voice came out flat and cold, a voice I hadn't used since Caelmoor, and I hated hearing it again the way you hate meeting an old friend and realizing he hasn't changed.

The keeper shrugged, rings clicking. "Broker's privilege."

"Someone walked into a temple valley," Paloma said, and now the smallness was burning off, and something else was coming up underneath it, something I recognized from the salt-flats and the checkpoint and the night she'd copied forged papers by moonlight with steady hands, "into a temple that magic cannot enter, and took my *name* out of me without my knowledge, carried it across the border where it could be bottled, and *priced it*." Her voice cracked on the last word, then steadied into something harder. "What did it sell for?"

"Unsold lots start at forty crowns," the keeper said.

"*Forty*—" Paloma made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "Forty crowns. I copied ledgers for ten years at three shillings a week to pay a debt of two hundred marks, and my entire *self* wholesales at forty crowns. That's not even a good price. That's an insult at auction."

"Ink lady," Wren said, tugging her sleeve, "is that yours? Is that *you*?"

"It was," Paloma said. "I think it still is. I think—" She pressed her palm against the case glass, and inside, impossibly, the gold-and-green light leaned toward her hand the way a cat leans toward a familiar lap. "Oh," she said softly. "Oh, that's *deeply unpleasant*."

I knew the feeling. I was having it myself, in duplicate.

Because here was the arithmetic, and it wouldn't balance any other way: the map had come to her. The stranger had died pressing it into her hands, whispering her name—a name he should never have known. I'd told myself it was chance, or fate, or the particular cruelty of gods who deal in paperwork. But if her name had already been taken, already carried north, already bottled and tagged eleven days before we ever reached this market—then someone had marked Paloma Hartwell long before the dying stranger ever staggered to the temple gate.

She hadn't been chosen by accident. She'd been *harvested in advance*. The map hadn't fled to her. It had been *delivered*, like goods to a buyer, like tribute to a throne.

And that meant whoever had sold her name knew what she was before she did. Knew it, and priced it, and waited.

"Sef." Paloma's voice cut through my spiraling. She was staring at the vial, at the tag, at her own reflection warped in the curved glass. "If my name is here—if it left me before I ever left the valley—then what have I been signing documents with? What did I write on the forged papers at Thornbridge?"

"Your name," I said slowly.

"And it *worked*. The seal accepted it." She laughed again, and this time there was nothing in it but horror dressed up as humor, which is the only kind of laugh anyone in this story ever manages. "Do you understand? The paper took a signature from someone who doesn't fully own hers anymore. Either the empire's seals are broken—and no one's noticed—or..."

"Or your name isn't gone yet," I finished. "It's *in escrow*."

Mudge made a small noise. When we looked at him, he had the expression of a man watching two people walk calmly toward a cliff he'd been politely trying to mention.

"Escrow," he repeated. "That's a word we use down here. Things in escrow can be reclaimed. By the rightful owner. For a price." His eyes flicked to the keeper, to the vial, to Paloma's hand still pressed against the glass. "The reclaiming fee is usually ruinous."

"How ruinous?" I asked.

"Well," said Mudge, apologetically, "the last person who tried to buy back their own name paid for it with a secret they'd been keeping for twenty years. The market took the secret in trade. His wife found out everything by morning." He sighed. "The Undermarket doesn't take coin for names, Sir Knight. Coin's too common. It takes *truths*. And it always, always takes the one you least want to give."

The vial flickered against Paloma's palm, gold and green, patient as a held breath.

And behind us, from the direction of the stairs, came the sound I had been dreading since we entered: footsteps. Many of them. Moving in step.

White robes, the door-guard had said. Don't talk to anyone wearing white.

I turned, put my body between Paloma and the lamplight, and reached for the sword I'd sworn I'd never draw again.
