## 22. Palace Gardens, Open to No One

The seal of a kingsguard opened doors that gold could not.

Sef had learned that the hard way, three years and one lifetime ago, back when he'd worn the white-and-silver and believed—gods, he'd actually believed—that the badge meant something. The palace of Aurelholm had changed its murals since then, its banners, its voice. But it had not changed its locks, because locks were honest in a way kings were not, and the old wards still answered to the sigil stamped on his credentials: *Corvane, Sef. Order of the King's Shield. Status: active.*

*Active.* He almost laughed every time he read it. Somewhere in a clerk's ledger, in ink no one had bothered to update, Sef Corvane was still a knight in good standing. It was the single most useful lie anyone had ever told about him, and no one had even told it on purpose.

"Credentials," said the gate warden at the Servants' Post, a boy who couldn't have been more than nineteen and had the bored eyes of someone who checked forty seals a day.

Sef handed over the leather folio without letting his hand shake. That was the trick of it—he'd discovered early on that guilt didn't show on paper, but trembling did. "Corvane. Kingsguard. I'm here to inspect the garden rotations."

The warden looked up. Looked down at the seal. Looked up again, and this time there was something like reverence in his face, which was worse than suspicion. "You served the king? Before?"

"I serve the king," Sef said evenly. "That's rather the point of the badge."

"Yes, ser. Of course, ser." The boy waved him through with a haste that suggested he wanted to tell this story later, to someone, anyone. *A real kingsguard came through my post.* Sef walked past him feeling like a thief who'd been thanked for robbing the house.

The gardens were open to no one—which was to say they were open only to the king, and whatever wore the king these days.

Sef had spent four days learning the rhythms of the place. Four days of walking corridors with a purposeful stride and an empty head, counting guards, timing shifts, mapping the routes between Vesha's safehouse and the royal wing until he could have walked them blindfolded and probably, given his luck lately, eventually would. Paloma had wanted to come. Paloma had argued for two hours, actually, in the low fierce whisper she used when she was losing an argument and knew it.

"You're the one with the face they remember," she'd said. "If they recognize you, everything ends."

"They won't recognize me," he'd said. "I'm not worth remembering. That's what disgrace is—it's being erased politely."

She hadn't liked that. She'd looked at him the way she looked at a badly copied page, like something valuable had been transcribed wrong, and said, "That's not what you are."

But she'd stayed behind, and Wren had stayed with her, and so here he was alone in a garden where the roses grew in rows too perfect to be natural, each bush pruned to exactly the same height, each path swept to exactly the same cleanliness. There was not a fallen petal anywhere. It was like walking through a painting of a garden rather than a garden itself, and Sef thought, not for the first time, that the Crowned's taste ran toward the uncanny—the world arranged just slightly wrong, beauty with the breath held out of it.

He found his position by the third bell: a service alcove near the western wall, half-hidden by a trellis heavy with white flowers that had no scent. From there he could see the full sweep of the king's walking circuit—a gravel loop maybe two hundred paces long, bordered by hedges, watched from four directions by guards who never shifted their weight and never, ever looked at the king.

That last detail was what told him the most. He'd been a guard himself; he knew how guards behaved around royalty when royalty was merely human. They stole glances. They traded whispers about the king's mood. They were curious, because curiosity was the one luxury of the lowborn watching the high. These men looked everywhere except at Aldric. They watched the hedges. The sky. Their own boots. As if looking directly at their sovereign might cost them something they couldn't afford.

*Or as if they've been ordered not to see,* Sef thought. *Or as if some part of them knows there's nothing there worth seeing—and something else entirely worth not-seeing.*

At the fourth bell, the king came out to walk.

Sef had seen Aldric perhaps a hundred times before the binding—on parade grounds, at council distances, once close enough to smell the horse on him after a long ride. The man had been built like the soldiers who'd raised him: broad, sun-darkened, with a laugh that carried across a courtyard and a habit of talking with his hands. A king who gestured. Who interrupted his own speeches to ask a servant's name. Who had personally pulled Sef out of a river during the Caelmoor floods, laughing, hauling him up by the collar like a drowned cat, and said, *Well, Corvane, now we're even for the boar hunt.*

That man was gone. What walked the gravel loop moved like a puppet rehearsing humanity: measured steps, hands at its sides, head tilted at precisely the angle a thoughtful king would tilt it. It paused at the rose bushes. It touched a bloom without plucking it. Every gesture was correct and none of them were alive, and watching it made Sef's skin crawl in a way he couldn't have described to Paloma without sounding mad.

Except—

It stopped.

Mid-stride, mid-loop, in the open center of the garden where any of the four guards could see, the king stopped walking. And Sef saw the shoulders drop—not the puppet-drop, not a performed weariness, but the real collapse of a man setting down a weight he'd carried too long. The hands unclenched. The head bowed.

And then Aldric raised his face to the western wall, to the trellis, to the shadow where Sef stood, and his eyes—

His eyes were *his*. Brown and bloodshot and terrified and awake, and Sef felt three years fall away like a cloak cut from his shoulders, because he knew those eyes. He'd sworn an oath to those eyes. He'd betrayed them in a vault beneath this very palace while telling himself it was mercy.

The king's lips moved.

No sound came out—or almost none, a thread of voice strangled somewhere in the throat, as if the words had to fight through whatever else lived in there. But the garden was silent as a held breath, and Sef read lips well enough; he'd learned it as a young soldier eavesdropping on officers, and the skill had never left him.

*Help me.*

Two words. The smallest possible prayer.

Then the king's gaze dropped, and something flickered across his face—shame, Sef realized, shame, as though being seen like this were worse than the captivity itself—and his mouth shaped more words, faster now, urgent:

*Ninety seconds. Maybe less. Listen.*

Sef pressed himself against the trellis, heart hammering against his ribs like a fist on a door.

*The binding anchors at my heartbeat,* the king mouthed. *Not my mind. My heart. Whatever you're planning—* his eyes squeezed shut, and when they opened they were wet— *it has to reach the heart. And Corvane—*

Sef went cold. *Corvane.* He knew. Some fragment of Aldric, buried under three years of occupation, remembered the night at Caelmoor. Remembered the face above him as the magus worked and the knights held him down.

*I know what you did,* the king mouthed, and there was no accusation in it, which was somehow infinitely worse. *I asked you to. Don't carry it alone. It was mine before it was yours.*

Sef's legs nearly gave out. He gripped the trellis hard enough to snap a stem, and the scentless flower trembled against his cheek, and the king—the real king, the drowning man inside the garment of himself—looked at him with something that was not forgiveness, not yet, but the ground forgiveness might be planted in.

*There's a girl,* Aldric mouthed. *Ink on her wrists. I've seen her in the Crowned's dreams. It fears her. Understand? Three years it has slept soundly, and now it dreams of a scribe. Go home, Corvane. Tell her to hurry.*

Somewhere beyond the hedge, a bell rang. The fifth bell, or perhaps only a minute's worth of the fifth bell—the king's whole body jerked like a man yanked back by the collar, and Sef watched the light go out of those brown eyes in real time. Shoulders squared. Hands clasped. Head lifted to the precise angle of a thoughtful king surveying his roses.

The puppet resumed its walk.

Sef stayed in the alcove long after the circuit ended, long after the guards escorted the king back inside, long after his own knees had stopped shaking enough to trust. He looked down at his hands and found a crushed white flower in his fist; he didn't remember picking it.

*Ninety seconds.* Ninety seconds in three years. Ninety seconds in which a prisoner had used his single window of freedom not to scream for rescue but to absolve the man who'd nailed the window shut.

Sef walked out through the Servants' Post with his credentials untouched and his composure held together with wire, and the young warden beamed at him like he'd met history, and Sef thought: *You have no idea, boy. History was standing in a rose garden mouthing two words, and I'm the only witness.*

* * *

Vesha's safehouse smelled of lamp oil and old paper, which Sef had come to think of as the smell of hope with dust on it. He found Paloma at the worktable, bent over the counter-sigil drafts, her sleeves pushed up and the blood-mark on her wrist faintly luminous in the lamplight—the way it always glowed now, softly, patiently, like a candle waiting to be needed.

Wren sat cross-legged on the floor beside her, sorting Vesha's scrap parchment into piles by color with the absolute seriousness of a general deploying troops.

"We're making a flag," Wren informed him, without looking up. "For after. When we win."

"After we win," Sef repeated.

"Everyone needs a flag." She held up a scrap of blue. "This one's yours. It's sad-colored."

"That seems right," Sef said gravely, and took it, because you took the flag a child handed you.

Paloma had looked up at his entrance, and whatever she saw in his face made her set down her pen. "Sef. What happened?"

He should tell it plainly. He'd rehearsed it on the walk back—*the king is in there, ninety seconds a day, maybe less, he says aim for the heart, he says hurry*—a clean report, soldier-shaped, deliverable.

Instead he heard himself say: "I saw him. The real him."

Paloma rose slowly. Wren stopped sorting scraps.

"He walks the gardens every day at the fourth bell. Same loop. Same gestures. It's—it's a performance, Paloma, a perfect one, down to the way he touches the roses. And today he stopped. In the open. Where anything wearing his face shouldn't dare stop." Sef's voice was doing something unsteady; he let it. "Ninety seconds. He gave me ninety seconds, and do you know what he spent them on?"

"What?"

"Not screaming for help. Well—not only that." Sef laughed, and it came out wrong. "First thing he did was tell me not to carry it alone. Caelmoor. He *knows*, Paloma. He knew all along, some buried piece of him knew, and his first free breath in three years went to telling me the sin was his before it was mine."

Paloma crossed the room and put her hand flat on his chest, over the heart, the way you'd steady someone—or check whether they were still standing. "And the rest?"

"The binding anchors at his heartbeat. Not his mind—his heart. Whatever we write, wherever we write it, it has to reach him there." He looked down at her wrist, at the sigil glowing its patient glow. "And there's more. He's dreamed of you. Or—the Crowned has dreamed of you, and he's seen the dream from the inside. Ink on your wrists, he said. It *fears* you, Paloma. Three years of sleeping like a lord, and now it lies awake worrying about a temple scribe with a debt problem."

Something moved through her expression—fear and ferocity tangled together, inseparable, the way they'd been since Merrow's Cross. "Then it should worry," she said quietly.

"It also said hurry." Sef swallowed. "Paloma, when it stopped him, it wasn't anger I felt coming off that garden. It was *preparation*. Like a man pacing before a speech. The thirteenth turn is weeks out and it is getting ready for something, and I don't think it's planning to sit on a throne and smile forever."

Wren tugged at his sleeve. He looked down. She was holding out the blue flag.

"You look like you need it more than me now," she said.

Sef took it. Sad-colored. He folded it carefully and put it in his breast pocket, over the heart, where a man keeps the things he intends to answer for.

"So we hurry," Paloma said. She turned back to the drafts, and her pen hovered, and did not touch the paper. "Sef. If the anchor is his heart—if the counter-sigil has to be written *onto a living king*—" She stopped herself, but the sentence finished itself in the silence.

"Then we need to be in that room," Sef said. "Before the thirteenth turn. However we get there."

Outside, the bells of Aurelholm rang the hour, bright and indifferent, counting down toward a moon no one else was watching.

And in Sef's breast pocket, folded small and sad-colored, a flag waited for a victory no one had promised them.
