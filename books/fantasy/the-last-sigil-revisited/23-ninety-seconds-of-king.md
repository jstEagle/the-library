## 23. Ninety Seconds of King

There is a game Aldric plays with himself, in the dark behind his own eyes.

He calls it *counting the silverware*.

It works like this: somewhere in the vast dimness of his stolen mind, there are objects that remain his — small things, domestic things, useless to a thing like the Crowned. The weight of his grandmother's soup spoon. The exact blue of the tiles in the palace laundry, where he once hid as a boy of nine after setting fire to a tutor's beard. The way his late father's boots squeaked on the left foot only, never the right, so that you always knew he was coming before you saw him.

The Crowned wants none of it. It wears his memories the way a man wears a coat too fine for him — appreciating the cut, caring nothing for the pockets. And so the pockets remain Aldric's. He keeps his treasures there.

*A spoon,* he thinks, turning the image over. *Silver, worn thin at the bowl from sixty years of stirring. She stirred her tea far longer than any tea requires.*

Somewhere far away, something vast and patient shifts its attention, and Aldric holds very still, the way one does when a wolf crosses the road ahead of the cart. It passes. It is always passing. It has been passing for three years.

Three years. He has counted them the only way he can — by moons, glimpsed through windows his body walks past without him. Thirteen moons to a turn, they say, and this year there will be a thirteenth turn of some terrible kind. He does not understand the arithmetic of it. He understands only that the thing wearing him grows *hungrier* as it approaches, and that hunger leaks through the seams of their shared skin like heat through an oven door.

*Grandmother's spoon,* he thinks again, harder. *The left boot squeaks. The laundry tiles are the blue of a heron's egg.*

Hold the line, soldier. That's what old Marshal Vane used to bark at green recruits holding a ford. *You don't have to win the battle, boy. You have to hold the ford.* Aldric had been twenty-two and terrified at the river Verre, knee-deep in cold water, holding a shield he barely knew how to use, and he had held it, because the man next to him was watching, and you cannot let down a man who is watching.

No one is watching now. That is the cruelty of it. A man alone in a siege can tell himself all manner of lies.

And yet — and this is the thought he returns to, the coin he worries smooth — someone once told him that no man is alone who remembers being loved. His mother said that. Or perhaps he read it in a book and assigned it to her afterward, which is what memory does when it is starving: it dresses up borrowed goods and calls them heirlooms.

Fine. Let it be a lie. He would hold the ford anyway.

* * *

The mornings are worst. Not because of anything the Crowned does in them — its cruelties are administrative, mostly, conducted with the brisk cheer of a tax assessor — but because morning is when Aldric is most aware of his hands.

His body wakes, washes, dresses, eats. Aldric watches all of it from the inside, a prisoner pressed to the window of his own house. And every morning, without fail, there comes a moment — usually while the barber scrapes his jaw, or while some minister drones about grain tariffs — when the Crowned's attention thins. It is never gone. But it *thins*, the way ice thins at the edge of a pond, and in that moment Aldric can feel his own fingers.

He practices moving them.

Not visibly. Visible rebellion earns punishment, and the punishments are not pain, exactly — the Crowned considers pain vulgar — but *subtraction*. It takes a memory and makes it flat. After the first such punishment, Aldric could recall his coronation day only as one recalls a description of rain: accurate, complete, and utterly without wetness. He had wept for a week inside his skull, silently, while his mouth smiled at ambassadors.

So: invisible rebellion. A twitch of the little finger, timed to a yawn. A curl of the toes beneath the robe. Small enough to be mistaken for nothing. He has been doing it for three years, and in three years he has accomplished precisely nothing with it except the knowledge that he is still, in some irreducible sense, *the one doing the twitching*.

Which, he has decided, is not nothing.

*They think I am furniture,* he thinks, as the barber's blade whispers along his jaw and the Crowned idly reviews a petition about bridge tolls. *Furniture does not practice.*

This morning the thin place comes early. The barber — Tomas, gray-bearded, hands steady as a mason's, a man Aldric has known since the man was an apprentice who once fainted at the sight of royal blood from a shaving nick — this morning Tomas hums. An old song. A harvest song from the eastern valleys, the kind with nonsense words that exist purely to carry the tune.

Aldric's throat almost moves. Almost sings along. He strangles the impulse — visible, visible, fool — but he lets himself *listen*, and listening is a form of holding, and for four bars of a silly song about turnips he is entirely, gloriously King Aldric of Aurelholm, a boy who burned a tutor's beard and a man who loved his kingdom and a prisoner who refuses to be furniture.

Then the Crowned notices the song.

*Tomas,* it says aloud, in Aldric's voice, pleasant as a warm room. *"That tune. Where is it from?"*

"Ah — my village, Majesty. East of Merrow's Cross. Nothing much—"

*"Sing more of it."*

Tomas sings. His voice is poor and his hands do not stop working, because a man learns quickly under this reign to keep his hands busy and his face agreeable. And Aldric listens, and memorizes, and adds the song to the pockets: the spoon, the tiles, the squeaking boot, the turnip song.

The Crowned lets it happen. That is the horror Aldric cannot make anyone understand, if anyone were left to understand it: the Crowned does not mind these small hoards. It finds them *amusing*. It has said so, once, in the long dark, in a voice like silk dragged over stone:

*Keep your trinkets, little king. A bird may keep its seed-hoard in a cage. It changes nothing about the cage.*

Aldric had answered — he answers, always, though his voice reaches nothing but the inside of his own prison — *A cage with a bird in it is not empty.*

It had seemed, in the moment, like a victory. He clings to it still, on the bad nights, the way a drowning man clings to a plank that is technically correct about buoyancy.

* * *

Some days, the Crowned talks to him.

Not often. It is a busy entity — he has gleaned fragments: it is old beyond old, it came from somewhere outside the world's counting, it regards names and selves as a kind of property dispute it intends to settle permanently — but on certain evenings, walking the walls or reviewing troops or sitting through yet another banquet where the wine tastes of nothing because taste requires a self to taste *with*, it converses. The way a cat plays with something. The way a scholar quizzes a particularly dense student.

*Do you know what your nobles fear most?* it asked last month, wearing his smile across a dinner table.

*A war,* Aldric had answered, inside. *Famine. Each other.*

*Being forgotten.* The Crowned had sipped his wine — Aldric felt the swallow, felt nothing of the pleasure. *Every one of them lies awake rehearsing how history will say their name. I could grant them peace from that. I will, eventually. They should thank me.*

*You call it peace,* Aldric had said, *being deleted.*

*I call it rest. You cling to your name the way a drowning man clings to a stone. I offer you water instead. Buoyant. Weightless. No one to disappoint. No debts—*

*I owe my people,* Aldric had said. *Debts are how love is kept honest.*

It had laughed then. He hates the laugh. It uses his lungs for it.

But here is the thing Aldric has learned in three years of listening to it monologue, here is the intelligence he hoards alongside spoons and songs: **it argues**. A god does not argue. A god states. The Crowned explains itself, defends itself, seeks converts — which means that on some level, in some way it would deny to the end of time, it is *not sure*. Things that are certain do not need a drowning man to agree that the water is fine.

He holds onto that, too. His armory is full of planks, each one technically correct about buoyancy.

* * *

Today is different. Today there is a garden walk scheduled, and Aldric knows it before he knows anything else, because the Crowned's body — *his* body, damn it, he will not surrender the pronoun even now — today his legs take him toward the south terrace at the hour when the light goes long and gold, and the Crowned is quiet in the way it gets quiet when it is *performing*. The ministers believe the king takes contemplative walks. The poets have written odes about the king's solitary communion with the roses.

The truth is that the Crowned likes the garden for the same reason Aldric does: it is the one place in the palace where no one speaks unless spoken to.

What the Crowned does not know — what it cannot know, because it pays attention to power and protocol and not to servants — is that the garden walk follows a route past the west wall, and the west wall has a gap in the hedge where a boy named Aldric used to eavesdrop on his father's council meetings, and that in three years of walking this route, the thin places in the ice have come more often here than anywhere else. Something about the route itself. Muscle memory, perhaps. The body remembering being his even when the mind is occupied.

Today the light is gold and the roses are heavy and the Crowned strolls, and Aldric drifts in the back rooms of his own skull doing inventory — spoon, tiles, boot, turnips, the smell of his wife's hair, which he keeps wrapped in oilcloth in the deepest pocket because grief is a treasure too, when it is all you're permitted — and then it happens.

The ice thins.

Not gradually. All at once, like a window shattering outward — and suddenly Aldric is *at the front of his eyes*, gasping inwardly, hands alive, the world shockingly specific, the roses almost painfully red, and he has perhaps a minute, perhaps less, and his heart slams against ribs that finally answer to him—

*Move the finger.*

The little finger twitches. Real. His.

*Legs. Walk.*

His legs slow. Not stopping — stopping draws attention, and there are guards at the corners of the hedges — but slowing, a king pausing to admire his roses, and Aldric drinks in the garden like a man drinking after a desert crossing. Three years since he chose his own pace. Three years since he chose anything.

*Ninety seconds,* he thinks. *Make them count. Don't waste them on despair. Despair keeps.*

He turns his head — slowly, naturally — toward the west wall. Toward the hedge-gap. Old habit, old hope: for three years, at moments like this, he has looked there, half-expecting rescue, fully expecting nothing.

The hedge-gap is empty. Of course it is.

But — wait.

There is someone at the far end of the terrace. A big man, standing badly on purpose, dressed in the brown of a minor functionary, hat brim low. He is pretending to inspect a topiary with the intensity of a scholar deciphering a dead language, and he is *terrible* at it, because his stance is wrong for a functionary and right for a soldier, weight balanced, sword-side clear, scanning sightlines between glances at shrubbery.

Aldric knows that stance. He knows those shoulders. He has knighted those shoulders. He has stood beside them at the river Verre, both of them knee-deep and terrified, holding the ford.

*Sef Corvane.*

The name rises in him like a fish breaking water, and with it everything attached: the binding night, three years ago, the magus's tower smelling of ozone and burnt sugar, and Sef — young Sef, straight-backed Sef, Sef whose faith in the crown was the purest thing in the whole palace — standing at the circle's edge with tears running openly down his face, saying *I'm sorry, Majesty, I'm sorry, it's the only way to save you,* and the light coming up white, and—

And the Crowned arriving. And Aldric going out like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

*He ran,* Aldric realizes, watching the man pretend to study topiary. *He survived, and he ran, and he has spent three years carrying it — look at him, look how he stands like a man made of unfinished business — and now he's back. Now he's here, in my garden, badly disguised.*

Hope is a dangerous cargo in a sinking ship; it shifts, it capsizes. Aldric makes himself breathe. Makes himself be slow. Ninety seconds, maybe eighty now, and he must spend them well.

The Crowned stirs at the edge of the thin place, sensing the temperature change, the way a sleeper senses a draft. Aldric feels it gathering, patient, inevitable, the tide coming back in.

Not yet. *Not yet.* One more thing.

He angles his body — a king admiring roses, nothing more — until his face is briefly, accidentally, unmistakably turned toward the man by the hedge. And he looks at Sef Corvane with everything he has, with three years of held ford burning in his eyes, and he shapes words with his lips. Silent. Small. The mouth of a man murmuring to flowers.

*Help me.*

Two syllables. The smallest possible prayer. He has no idea if the distance is too far, the light too low, the man too broken to see. It doesn't matter. Dignity, his father taught him, is not whether the message arrives. Dignity is sending it.

The tide comes in. The ice closes over. Aldric recedes into the back rooms, into the pockets, into the dark where the spoon and the song and the squeaking boot are kept safe — but he goes *laughing*, inwardly, silently, because for the first time in three years the ledger has changed.

Someone came looking. Someone stood in his garden with guilt in his spine and purpose in his fists. Whatever brought Sef Corvane home — remorse, duty, desperation — it means the story is moving again, and a story moving means an ending is possible, and an ending is the one treasure the Crowned cannot confiscate, because the Crowned, for all its ancient hunger, has never once understood endings. It only knows how to continue.

In the deep pocket, next to his wife's hair and his grandmother's spoon, Aldric files away the newest item in his hoard: a big man in a bad disguise, pretending to admire topiary.

And far above, in the gold light, the king's borrowed mouth smiles at the roses — and somewhere across the city, in an archive that owes no allegiance to the crown, a scribe's wrist begins, faintly, to glow.

Aldric cannot see it. But he would have bet the whole kingdom on it anyway.
