## 24. The Confession Over Cold Tea

The tea had gone cold an hour ago. Sef knew this because he had been staring at it the entire time, watching the skin form on its surface like something trying to become solid and failing—which was more than he could say for himself.

Paloma sat across the little table in Vesha's archive, in the lamplight, with her hands folded and her wrist uncovered where the sigil glowed faintly under her skin like a coal refusing to die. She had not said anything in a long while. That was the worst part. Paloma Hartwell was never quiet unless she was about to be devastating.

"You're doing the thing," Sef said finally.

"What thing."

"The thing where you wait until I confess to something I haven't done yet so you can be right twice."

"I'm drinking my tea." She lifted the cup, drank, and grimaced at the cold of it without breaking eye contact. "It's terrible tea. Vesha buys it from a man who stores it next to his fish. But I'm drinking it. That is all that is happening here."

"Right."

"Caelmoor," she said.

And there it was. No wind-up, no circling. She had learned that from him, he supposed—or maybe she'd always had it, and the valley had only taught her to hide it behind ledger-columns and polite silence. He set down his cup. It clicked against the table too loudly in the dark of the archive, between the shelves of condemned books and stolen names.

"You've been asking about Caelmoor since Merrow's Cross," he said.

"You've been *not answering* about Caelmoor since Merrow's Cross. One of us has been consistent."

He almost laughed. That was the cruelty of her—he'd have followed this woman into a fire partly because she was brave and partly because she'd make him laugh on the way down. He looked at her wrist instead. The sigil pulsed once, slow, like something breathing in its sleep.

"If I tell you," he said, "you'll look at me differently. And I need you to understand—I'm not telling you because I want absolution. I'm telling you because you deserve to make your choice with your eyes open. Whatever you decide after. Even if what you decide is that you'd rather walk into Aurelholm alone."

"Then say it."

*Say it.* As if it were one word and not three years of running compressed into a single night.

Sef Corvane had been many kinds of coward in his life—polite cowardice mostly, the kind that wore armor and called itself duty—but he had promised himself, somewhere on the salt flats with her blood burning a map into her arm, that he would not be a coward about *this*. Not again.

"All right," he said. "Caelmoor. Three years ago. I was kingsguard. Fourth chair, left side—the chair nobody watches because the king's shadow falls across it. Which is exactly why Magus Ondrell wanted me there."

"The royal magus."

"The king's oldest friend, actually. That's important. Remember that part." He turned his cup slowly on the table, a full rotation, like a lock turning. "Ondrell came to me in the spring of the last year. He looked—gods, he looked *awful*. Ten years older than he'd been at midwinter. He told me there was a plot against Aldric. A poisoner in the court, or a curse, he wasn't sure which, and he wasn't sure it mattered, because either way the king would be dead before the harvest moon. He showed me evidence. Real evidence—I checked it. Letters. A vial. A servant who wept and confessed and then hanged herself before I could ask her who paid her."

Paloma's brow furrowed. "You investigated."

"I investigated. That's the thing people don't understand about how it happened—it didn't happen because I was careless or greedy or ambitious. I did everything right. I verified everything I could verify. And everything pointed one direction: Aldric was going to die, soon, and no one else believed it, and the only man offering a solution was the magus whose loyalty was beyond question."

"What solution?"

Sef's mouth had gone dry. He reached for the cold tea and drank it anyway, fish-taste and all, because he deserved the fish-taste.

"A binding," he said. "A stasis. Ondrell claimed there was an old rite—pre-imperial, from before the Crowned wars—that could seal a soul against harm. Put the king somewhere nothing could reach him. Buy time until the plot was found and cut out. He said it would feel like sleep. He said—" Sef stopped. Started again, quieter. "He said it would be temporary. Weeks. A moon at most."

"And you believed him."

"I *wanted* to believe him. There's a difference, and the difference matters, and I've spent three years telling myself the difference makes me less guilty when it doesn't make me guilty at all." He heard his own voice go flat and careful, the way it went when he recited this in his head at two in the morning in whatever barn or ditch he'd slept in. "The rite needed witnesses. Anchors, he called them. Four of them, sworn and true, to hold the circle while the binding was spoken. Kingsguard were ideal—we'd already given our lives to the king's protection. It was just protection by other means. That's how he framed it. Protection by other means."

"How many anchors?"

"Four." His hand had stopped moving the cup. He made himself keep talking. "Two of them are dead now. Ser Hale took fever the winter after. Ser Marren drowned—though I've sometimes wondered. The fourth was me."

"And the fourth name?" Paloma asked softly. "Who held the fourth anchor, Sef? You said four."

He couldn't look at her. He looked at the sigil on her wrist instead, glowing its slow coal-light, and understood suddenly—had perhaps always understood—that the universe had a sense of humor, and it was worse than Vesha's tea.

"There were only three of us in the room," he said. "Hale, Marren, me. The fourth anchor point was empty. Ondrell said the rite would compensate. He said the binding would reach out and take what it needed." He swallowed. "I stood in a circle under Caelmoor's old chapel, on the night of the twelfth turn, and I watched him put the king to sleep. And then the binding didn't stop. It kept reaching. It drank the whole chapel—candles, stone, Ondrell himself—and something came up through the floor of the world to fill the shape we'd made. Something crowned. Something that looked at Aldric sleeping in the center of the circle the way a man looks at a coat he intends to wear."

The lamplight flickered. Somewhere in the archive above them, Vesha's assistants moved through the stacks, sorting stolen names into their drawers, and Sef thought: *there but for the grace of a dead stranger's map goes Paloma Hartwell.*

"It put him on," he said. "Like a garment. Like you said in Merrow's Cross. I saw it happen. I stood four feet away with a sword I never drew, and I saw it happen, and then I ran."

"You ran."

"I ran." The words came out cracked. He let them. "Marren and Hale stood their ground—they tried to break the circle and the Crowned unmade their courage first, made them kneel, made them *smile*, and then it let them live, which I think was crueler. Me—it never even looked at me. I was beneath its notice. So I ran, and I kept running, and for three years I told myself I was searching for a way to undo it. And some of that was even true. I read every book. I tracked every rumor. I found the stranger who carried the map—the previous bearer, though I didn't know that's what he was then—and I was sworn to destroy the map, do you understand? My order gave me that charge before the binding ever happened. *If the rite fails or corrupts, destroy the key.* And when I found him dying at Vael Hollow's gate, I should have taken the map and burned it. Instead I stood on a ridge and watched a temple scribe give water to a dying man against every rule she'd ever been taught, and I watched him press the map into her hands, and I thought—"

He stopped.

"You thought what?" Paloma asked. Her voice was very steady. Too steady. The steadiness of someone holding something heavy and deciding whether to put it down.

"I thought, *good*. I thought, *let someone clean hands carry it*, because mine weren't clean anymore and I knew it. I let you take the burden because I couldn't bear to touch it myself. Every mile since the salt road, every lie I told you about why I was traveling north—'hunting the same enemies,' 'protecting the realm'—it was all one long act of cowardice dressed as chivalry. I used you, Paloma. I watched the sigil burn itself into your skin and I said nothing for days because as long as I said nothing, I didn't have to admit what I'd done."

The silence afterward lasted long enough for him to count the cracks in the ceiling plaster. Eleven. He'd counted them before, on other nights, in this same chair, rehearsing this conversation and chickening out of it.

"So," Paloma said at last. She refolded her hands. Unfolded them. Folded them again—a scribe's habit, he'd learned, the way other people drummed fingers. "Let me see if I have the ledger straight. You helped bind the king. You believed you were saving his life. The binding opened a door to the Crowned. Your fellow anchors died or broke. You ran. You spent three years hunting the key in order to destroy it, per your orders, and when you finally found it, you let a debt-slave scribe carry it instead because you couldn't face it yourself."

"That's..." He exhaled. "*Accurate.* Painstakingly accurate. You'd have made a fine auditor."

"Don't joke."

"I know. I'm sorry. It's what I do instead of screaming."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then, horribly, gently: "Why are you telling me this *now*, Sef? What changed?"

He had an answer ready for that—one about the ninety seconds in the garden, the king mouthing *help me* through eyes that were still, underneath everything, Aldric's eyes. It was a good answer. Noble, even. But she had just given him a lesson in painstaking accuracy, and he owed her the same coin.

"Wren," he said.

"Wren?"

"She draws pictures. In the margins of everything—your ledgers, my maps, the backs of Vesha's catalog slips. Little stick figures. Yesterday she drew one of you with ink-stained hands holding up a lantern, and one of me with a sword, and she labeled mine 'the sad sword.' And under it she wrote—she's learning letters, Vesha's teaching her—she wrote *he looks like he is carrying something heavy.*" Sef laughed, briefly, wetly. "Seven years old, nameless, hunted by the Crowned's agents, and she diagnosed me in charcoal. I've been carrying it since Caelmoor, Paloma. I will carry it whether you forgive me or not. The only question left is whether I carry it lying to you."

Paloma rose. He braced—for the slap, for the walking out, for the cold verdict delivered in that precise auditor's cadence she must have inherited from ten years of temple ledgers. Instead she walked around the table, picked up the teapot, and poured both cups full again.

"This pot's been sitting on Vesha's stove," she said. "It'll be warm. Barely. But warmer than the last cup." She sat back down and pushed a cup toward him. "Drink. Then finish it. All of it. If I'm to decide anything, I want the whole account, not the summary. Start with Ondrell's face when the circle began to drink him. Don't spare yourself. I'll know if you're sparing yourself; I've read enough doctored ledgers to smell one."

So he drank the warm-ish fish-adjacent tea, and he finished it. He told her about Ondrell's face—how the magus's expression hadn't changed at all as the Crowned consumed him, how he'd worn the same calm certainty to his dissolution that he'd worn proposing the rite, and how that was the detail that haunted Sef worst of all, because it meant Ondrell had never seen himself as wrong. He told her about the weeks after, when the "temporary" binding settled into permanence and the new king's first decrees came down from the palace smelling of ozone and honey. He told her about reporting to his order, being stripped of his knighthood in a cellar rather than publicly—for they feared the Crowned's agents more than they feared disgrace—and given the map-destruction charge as a kind of penance with a blade attached. He told her about the nights he'd stood outside inns where the king's progress was rumored to pass, watching lit windows, trying to catch the lucid hours. He told her about the garden. Ninety seconds. *Help me.*

Paloma listened to all of it without interrupting once, which for Paloma was a feat comparable to lifting a horse.

When he was done, the tea was warm and half-drunk and neither of them touched it again.

"Do you know what I keep coming back to?" she said quietly. "The fourth anchor. The empty point. The binding 'reached out and took what it needed.'" Her eyes came up to his, and he watched the thought land in her the way it had landed in him a hundred times and never stayed. "Sef. What does a binding take, when it reaches out? Names. Identity. Anchor-weight. What did it pull into that fourth point, three years ago?"

"I don't know."

"Somebody. Somebody somewhere lost something that night—something heavy enough to hold a door shut. And whoever they are, they've been carrying the other end of your sin for three years without knowing why." Her voice didn't rise. It got softer, which was worse. "You didn't just fail the king, Sef. You failed someone you'll never meet."

The truth of it went through him like a spear finding the gap in armor he hadn't known was there. He nodded, because speech had deserted him.

Paloma stood, crossed to the shelf, and took down one of Vesha's blank catalog slips and the stub of charcoal Wren used. For a moment he thought she was going to write him off—enter him into the record, close the account. Instead she wrote three lines, folded the slip once, and set it on the table between them like a card laid down in a game.

He unfolded it.

*The debt stands,* it said. *But debts are owed to creditors, and I am not your creditor. The king is. Get me to that throne room.*

Below it, smaller, in her cramped scribe's hand: *We leave at second bell. Sleep. You'll want your sword-arm steady—you're going to need it, and so am I.*

Sef read it twice. Then a third time, because his eyes had blurred and the first two readings might have been optimistic.

"Paloma—"

"Don't." She held up an ink-stained finger. "Don't thank me, don't swear oaths, don't do the sad-knight bowing thing, I've seen it and it takes too long. Just be worth the risk I'm taking on you. That's the whole of the contract. Initial the bottom."

He almost smiled. "There's no bottom."

"Then initial the air near where a bottom would be, and go to bed."

He stood, and—because he was apparently incapable of leaving well enough alone—said, "For what it's worth: if it comes to choosing between the mission and my life, choose the mission. I mean it. I've had three years of my life and it hasn't improved."

"Noted and rejected," she said, already turning away, already the meticulous scribe again, gathering her pens. "Your life is collateral on a loan I've decided to extend. I protect my investments."

She paused at the stair, her back to him, and for a moment the mask slipped and he saw how much it had cost her—her shoulders, her hands, the line of her neck all held a fraction too tight.

"Sef. The stranger at the gate. The one who gave me the map." She didn't turn around. "Do you think he knew? What I was? Who I'd become?"

"Yes," Sef said. "I think he chose you the way Ondrell chose me. Because you were the kind of person who gives water to dying men against all rules." He hesitated. "The difference is, I think he chose better."

Paloma stood very still on the stair. Then she climbed out of sight without another word, and Sef sat alone in the lamplight with two cold cups and a folded slip of paper, feeling lighter than he had in three years and more terrified than he had in three years, which he was beginning to suspect were the same sensation.

Above him, faintly, through the floorboards, he heard Vesha's voice sharp with alarm, and running feet, and a drawer of stolen names slam open.

By the time he reached the stairs, Paloma was already descending two steps at a time, her face pale, a scrap of paper crushed in her fist.

"They've taken Vesha," she said. "And they left a message. It's addressed to *the bearer*—and it knows my name."
