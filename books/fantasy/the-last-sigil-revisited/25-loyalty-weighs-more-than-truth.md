## 25. Loyalty Weighs More Than Truth

The tea had gone cold an hour ago. Paloma kept drinking it anyway.

She wasn't sure why. Perhaps because the small ritual of it—cup, saucer, the bitter dregs of Vesha's cheap leaf—gave her hands something to do that wasn't throwing the cup at Sef Corvane's head. The cup was chipped and ugly and belonged to a rogue archivist who was currently pretending to catalogue scrolls three rooms away while listening to every word. The cup deserved better than Sef's skull. Probably.

"Say it again," she said.

Sef sat across from her with his elbows on his knees, the posture of a man waiting for a blow he'd ordered himself. His grey eyes didn't waver. That was the worst part. If he'd looked away even once she could have told herself he was ashamed of lying, rather than simply sorry about being caught.

"I helped bind the king," he said. "Three years ago, in the vault beneath the throne room. I held the circle open while Magus Orenn worked the sigil. I believed—" his jaw tightened, "—I believed we were saving Aldric's life. There were assassins in the palace that night. Real ones. Two guards dead before we ever reached the vault. Orenn said the binding would hide the king inside himself, somewhere the assassins' magic couldn't find him. A hiding place made of his own name." He exhaled slowly. "I held the circle. I said the words. And when Aldric screamed, I did not let go."

"There," Paloma said quietly. "That wasn't so difficult, was it?"

"It was three years difficult."

She set down the cup. It clicked against the saucer, and the sound seemed enormously loud in the little archive room, where candles guttered behind protective glass and ten thousand condemned documents slept in their drawers like things buried alive.

"You lied to me," she said. "On the salt road. In Merrow's Cross. On the barge. At Thornbridge, where your forged nothing nearly got us both hanged because you were too busy carrying your precious secret to think clearly. You watched me learn this sigil grammar stitch by stitch, watched it burn itself into my skin, and you knew—you *knew*—what had been done with it before, and you said nothing."

"Yes."

"Don't *yes* me. Don't you dare be concise now. You were eloquent enough at Caelmoor, apparently, when you were chanting a man's soul into a cage."

He flinched. She noted the flinch with a scribe's precision and hated herself a little for how much satisfaction it gave her. Anger, she was discovering, was a ledger too. Every entry had to balance.

"The magus is dead," Sef said. "Orenn. He died the night of the binding—or something wore him out of his body and left the shell. I ran. I told myself I'd run to find help, to find a way to undo it, but that was a lie too. I ran because staying meant looking at what I'd done." His voice roughened. "Every year on the anniversary I told myself: next year. Next year I'll go back. Next year I'll find the courage. Then the stranger came through Thornbridge with a map, and I was ordered—I was still technically sworn—to destroy it. To protect the binding. Old oaths are sticky things, Paloma. They cling long after the person you swore them to has stopped existing."

"And so you hunted him. The dying man. The one who put this map in my hands."

"I tracked him to Vael Hollow intending to take the map and burn it."

"But he was faster than you. Dying made him fast."

Something flickered across Sef's face—not quite a smile, but its ghost. "Dying made him desperate. Desperation moves quickly."

Paloma stood up. She hadn't planned to; her legs simply decided, the way legs do when sitting becomes unbearable. She walked the length of the archive room, past shelves of seized genealogies and banned histories, past a jar of pickled something that Vesha refused to explain, and stopped at the window. Below, the capital sprawled in its evening gold—the gilded roofs, the smoke, the murals of a king whose face never quite matched itself from wall to wall. Somewhere under all that splendor, in a vault beneath a throne, a man was screaming inside his own skin and had been for three years.

Because Sef Corvane had held a circle and not let go.

"Do you know what I did," she said, without turning, "when my mother died? I was fourteen. We owed the temple eleven marks. Eleven. My father couldn't pay, so the debt passed to me, because in Vael Hollow grief is inherited along with everything else. Do you know what the auditor told me? That mourning was unproductive. That tears were hours I would owe back at interest." She turned around. "I have spent nine years learning that debts are the truest thing in the world. More true than love. More true than names. Everything can be sold, transferred, collected. So when you tell me you've carried a secret for three years, my first instinct—the honest one—is to calculate what it costs me. What I owe now that I know. Whether knowing you makes me complicit."

"Does it?" Sef asked softly.

"That's the question, isn't it." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I keep trying to enter it into the ledger and the columns won't balance. Because here's what I actually feel, Sef, underneath the arithmetic: I am *furious*. And underneath the fury—" she pressed a hand flat against her sternum, against the faint warmth of the blood-sigil sleeping there, "—underneath it I understand. Not forgive. Understand. You were a good soldier given a monstrous order wrapped in a kind lie, and you believed the kindness because believing was easier than suspecting. I have built my whole life on that same trick. The temple told me my debt was just, and I believed it, because believing meant I deserved it, and deserving it meant the world still made sense." Her voice dropped. "You didn't betray me, Sef. You betrayed yourself, every day, for three years, and I watched you do it and called it nobility. That's what wounds. Not what you did at Caelmoor. What I failed to see since the salt road."

He rose too, slowly, as if sudden movements might shatter whatever fragile thing was being built between them. "Paloma—"

"I'm not finished." She held up a hand. "I get to be angry for as long as I need. That's not a debt. It doesn't accrue interest, and you don't get to pay it off early with confessions or apologies or that tragic look you practice in polished breastplates. You will carry this. You will carry it *with* me, which is worse for you than carrying it alone, because alone you could pretend it was noble."

A pause. Then, with the careful flatness of a man testing ice: "Is that a yes, then? You'll continue?"

"It's a yes." She said it before the coward in her could intervene. "Not forgiveness. Forgiveness is a currency I don't hold yet. But the moon turns in five weeks—four, now. The Crowned grows stronger while we sit here trading truths over cold tea. And you—" she pointed at him, and her finger only shook a little, "—you are the only living soul who has seen the binding worked from the inside. You know the vault. You know the words Orenn used. You know where the circle's anchors were set, and how many there were, and which direction the king screamed." Her throat tightened. "Hating you and needing you are not mutually exclusive. Ask any indentured servant in Vael Hollow. We're experts at both."

"Genuinely the most romantic thing anyone has ever said to me," Sef said.

"Do not make jokes."

"I'm not. I'm making a note. For the memoirs. 'Chapter Nine: She Said Needing Me Was Like Debt.'" 

"*Sef.*"

"Sorry." He wasn't, entirely; the corner of his mouth betrayed him. But then the humor drained away and what remained was raw and young, thirty years old and suddenly boyish. "For what it's worth—and I know it isn't worth much, I know exactly what it's worth, I've been appraising it for three years—if you'd turned and walked out that door just now, I wouldn't have followed. I'd have gone to the palace alone and died in the attempt, and I'd have considered that fair accounting."

"Well," Paloma said, "that's morbid, and also terrible strategy. Alone, you'd last approximately four minutes against the Crowned's agents, and I say that with love."

"With—"

"Don't finish that sentence. I misspoke. Strike it from the record." But her ears were burning, and she turned away to fuss with the teapot, pouring a fresh cup she didn't want, mostly to have somewhere to put her face. Behind her she heard Sef exhale—a breath that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

* * *

They worked through the evening.

It was, Paloma reflected, the strangest form of reconciliation imaginable: two people who had just detonated the foundations of their trust sitting shoulder to shoulder at a scarred worktable, reconstructing a binding ritual from memory and marginalia. Sef drew the vault from recall—every pillar, every anchor-point, the exact spot where Orenn had stood. His hand was steady. His eyes kept sliding to hers, checking, always checking, whether the drawing was welcome. She let him wonder. It was petty. She found she could live with petty.

"The circle was triple-anchored," he said, tapping the charcoal sketch. "One anchor at each compass point, and the third..." He hesitated.

"The third on the king himself. Yes. Vesha's texts agree." Paloma smoothed the copied page flat. "Which means the counter-sigil must address all three. Two we can reach physically. The third lives inside Aldric—which means either the king must speak it willingly, or someone bearing the sigil grammar must carry it to him." She didn't say *me*. She didn't have to. The mark on her wrist pulsed faintly, as though it had opinions about being discussed.

"You've been studying the grammar," Sef said. It wasn't a question. "On the barge. I saw the ink on your fingers."

"You saw ink on my fingers and said nothing. Interesting policy, for a man who claims to have been protecting me."

"Ink on a scribe's fingers is weather. It tells you nothing except that the world is functioning." A pause. "What did you learn?"

"That binding-magic is a language, and languages have tenses." She pulled a page toward her—her own cramped copying, sigils rendered in ordinary iron-gall ink, annotated in the margins with a scholar's hunger she hadn't felt since childhood. "This clause, here. The anchoring clause. It specifies what may serve as payment for the working. Look at the construction—it's not a list, it's a *menu*. Life. Name. Memory. Anything of sufficient weight to the bearer." She tapped it twice. "Orenn chose to hide the king using his name. Which suggests names are the heaviest coin in circulation."

Sef went very still. "And what does the counter-working cost?"

"I don't know yet. The original text describes the binding. The reversal clauses are elsewhere—Vesha thinks they're in the sealed folios, the ones she calls her condemned things." She glanced up. "Tomorrow we ask her. Tonight we sleep, because tomorrow I intend to be insufferably precise and that requires rest."

He nodded. He gathered his sketches. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, back half-turned.

"Paloma. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank yourself in three weeks when we've won." She hesitated, then added, quieter, "And Sef? The next thing you fail to tell me—anything, however small—ends this. Whatever this is. Understood?"

"Understood." He almost smiled. "Though I confess I no longer know what to call whatever this is."

"Neither do I. It's very inconvenient." She waved him out. "Goodnight, Sir Corvane."

"Not sir. Not anymore."

"No," she agreed. "But you might be again, someday, if we pull this off. Kings are sentimental about the men who save them. Goodnight."

* * *

She didn't sleep.

Instead she sat by the candle with her copied pages spread around her like fallen leaves, and she read the anchoring clause until the words swam. *Anything of sufficient weight to the bearer.* The phrase circled her thoughts the way moths circled the flame—dumbly, persistently, drawn to something warm and dangerous.

Her life was light. She'd known that for years; the temple's ledgers had priced it often enough. Nine years of bond labor, and her total assessed value had never exceeded forty marks. A name, though—

A name was different. A name was every letter her father had taught her, every story Wren had already begun hoarding like a magpie with a grudge, every signature on every ledger she'd ever copied, every time Sef had said *Paloma* in that particular careful way, as though setting something down on high ground. A name was weight beyond appraisal.

She thought of the town of Merrow's Cross, where the residents had traded their names away piece by piece until they greeted each other as *neighbor*, *friend*, *hey-you*—and how the empty places where identities had been had ached like missing teeth. She thought of vials in the Undermarket, one labeled with her own letters. Someone had tried to sell her ahead of herself. The thought still made her hands curl into fists.

"If the price is a name," she murmured to the candle, "then the Crowned and I finally agree on something. Names are chains." She blew it out. "The difference is I intend to choose mine."

In the dark, the sigil on her wrist pulsed once—warm, patient, like a heartbeat waiting for instructions.

Down the hall, she heard Sef's door close, and then, after a long moment, open again. Footsteps. A pause outside her room. She held her breath. He didn't knock. He never would, now; he'd knocked on every door in his life and learned what it cost. But she heard him stand there, and she heard him say, so quietly it barely crossed the threshold:

"For the record. Given the choice again—I'd still hold the circle. And then I'd spend every hour since finding you, to undo it. Both things are true. I'm sorry they are."

His footsteps retreated.

Paloma lay in the dark with her heart doing something complicated and unledgered, and told herself firmly that it was exhaustion, and that whatever it actually was could wait until after the moon turned.

Outside, above the gilded teeth of the city, the thirteenth moon was rising—one night closer to conjunction. And far away in her archive of condemned things, Vesha was lighting lamps over a locked drawer, preparing, though none of them knew it yet, to show Paloma the clause that would change the shape of everything.
