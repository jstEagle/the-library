## 26. Vesha's Vault of Condemned Things

Vesha's archive smelled of dust, lamp oil, and the particular melancholy of paper that had outlived its authors.

Paloma felt it the moment she stepped through the door behind the fishmonger's stall—a feeling she could not have named a month ago but recognized now as *home*. Shelves climbed three stories into shadow, ladders on brass rails, thousands upon thousands of books and scrolls and bundles of letters tied with string gone soft with age. A skylight far above let down one column of gray afternoon light, and in it drifted motes like slow snow.

"It smells like the temple," Paloma said.

"Every library does," Vesha said. "Libraries smell like people who are finished talking." She was a small woman, sixty or thereabouts, with ink-stained fingers and spectacles pushed up into a cloud of iron-gray hair. "The condemned things are below. Mind your head, mind the candles, and touch nothing that hums."

"Nothing that *hums*?"

"You'll know it when you hear it."

The stairs went down farther than the building above was tall, which Paloma decided not to think about too hard, because thinking about it made her stomach tilt. At the bottom, iron doors banded in copper opened onto the vault: a long chamber lined with lead-fronted cabinets, each labeled in a hand so precise it looked printed. Here were the books no legitimate archive would keep—heretical genealogies, banned grammars of dead tongues, ledgers from guilds that no longer existed because the Crowned had eaten their names. Here was everything the empire wanted forgotten.

Here, Vesha said, was the original binding text.

She unlocked the third cabinet from the end with a key she wore on a cord around her neck, and drew out a folio wrapped in oilcloth, and set it on the reading table with the tenderness of a woman setting down a sleeping cat.

"Three years ago I paid an enormous sum to a palace clerk for a copy of this," Vesha said. "The clerk is probably dead now. Or worse—nameless. Either way, the money bought me the only complete transcription outside the royal vaults. And now you're going to read every word of it while I watch, because if you damage my folio, girl, I will invoice your ghost."

"That seems fair," Paloma said solemnly, and sat down.

Sef took up a position by the stairs, arms crossed, doing his impression of a man at ease, which had all the convincingness of a statue doing its impression of a man at ease. He had been like this since the confession over cold tea two nights past—since he'd told her everything about Caelmoor, about the binding, about how he had held the king's shoulder steady while the royal magus wrote the sigil into Aldric's living flesh. Everything except the part where he'd believed he was saving Aldric's life; that part he'd told her too, actually. The part he still hadn't said aloud was *forgive me*, and Paloma had decided not to make him say it until he meant it, because she'd spent enough years around priests to know the difference between repentance and relief.

They were not easy together yet. But they were *together*, which was more than she'd expected to be with anyone, ever, in any configuration.

Wren was asleep in a nest of blankets behind the cabinets, curled around a half-eaten honey bun Vesha had produced from somewhere, the way Vesha seemed to produce everything: grudgingly, and exactly when needed. The child had insisted on coming along—"the ink lady might need somebody to hold her lantern"—and had then fallen asleep within the first quarter hour, which Paloma considered the correct use of a child.

"Begin at the third leaf," Vesha said, pulling a lamp close. "The first two are invocation. You don't invoke anything. You *rewrite*."

The folio was written in the old court hand, dense and angular, but the language beneath it was the grammar Paloma had been teaching herself on the river—the same grammar the map had burned into her wrist, the same grammar her blood apparently spoke natively. She read slowly, lips moving, and Vesha watched her with the expression of a chess player watching an opponent make an unexpected good move.

The binding text described itself plainly enough. The Crowned was not summoned; it was *anchored*. It existed the way a ship exists at anchor—held in place by a chain, and the chain was a sigil, and the sigil required three things: a vessel of sufficient rank (a king), a night of sufficient darkness (the thirteenth turn), and a bearer of sufficient... Paloma paused.

"Sufficient what?"

"Read on," Vesha said.

*A bearer of sufficient weight.* Weight. Not power, not purity—weight. The sigil needed someone whose name carried mass, whose identity was heavy in the world. A scribe who had signed ten thousand documents. A debtor whose name was known to every creditor in Vael Hollow. Someone who could not be mistaken for anyone else.

"I always thought being nobody would be safer," Paloma said quietly.

"The Crowned agrees with you," Vesha said dryly. "That's rather the whole horror of it. Keep reading. The counter-rite is in the appendix."

The appendix was eleven pages. Paloma read them twice. The structure was elegant, terrible, symmetrical: where the binding anchored, the unbinding released; where the binding consumed a king's will, the unbinding demanded—

She stopped. Read it again. Felt her heart do something complicated, like a bird hitting a window.

*"The rite requires the life of the bearer,"* she read aloud, because saying it out loud made it real and therefore survivable, *"for only a soul freely offered can pay the debt the anchor incurred."*

The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs, faintly, a fishmonger shouted about prices.

"There's more," Vesha said. Her voice had changed—flattened, careful, the voice of a woman who had already read this page many times and hated it. "Keep reading. Bottom of the page."

Paloma looked down.

There was a clause. A small clause, tucked between two lines like a knife folded into a letter. In the old court hand, half the size of the surrounding text, almost apologetic:

*Or, should the bearer offer instead a name freely given—one spoken by its owner, wholly surrendered, never reclaimed—the debt shall be counted paid in full.*

Paloma read it four times. Then she read it a fifth time, because the fourth time she had been crying and the words had blurred.

"A name," she said. "Instead of my life."

"Yes."

"They take my name and I live."

"Yes."

"And the king wakes."

"*Yes,*" said Vesha, and then, gentler than Paloma had ever heard her: "And no one will remember you. Not your face, not your deeds, not the shape of you walking through a doorway. The name is the thread; pull it out and the whole garment comes loose. You'll be a stranger wearing your own boots. You'll look at your own hands and wonder whose they are."

Paloma stared at the clause. Hope rose in her chest, huge and warm—and then she saw the edge of it, the way you see the edge of a beautiful shell only when you pick it up and find the razor-thin fracture running through. Because she knew what names were worth here. She had walked through Merrow's Cross, where the Crowned's agents had scraped people empty of themselves. She had stood in the Undermarket beside vials labeled with stolen identities, one of them bearing her own initials. She knew exactly what it was to lose a name, because she had watched it done to others, and they had not died, and that had been the worst part—they had kept breathing, kept eating, kept smiling at strangers, hollowed out like bells with the clappers removed.

But she also knew ledgers. Debts and payments, columns and balances. And here, at last, was a line item she could afford.

"How freely?" she asked. Her voice sounded strange to her. "Freely given—it says freely given. Does that mean I can't be afraid? Does fear cancel it?"

"Fear doesn't cancel anything," Vesha said. "Fear is just the price tag showing. Freely means you choose it knowing the cost. Which—" she took off her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, a stalling gesture Paloma recognized because she used it herself when a ledger refused to balance— "which is why I'm telling you now, before you've had time to fall in love with the idea, and also why I'm going to say the next thing I'm going to say even though you'll hate me for it."

"Which is?"

"Don't decide tonight. Don't decide alone. And don't decide—" Vesha put her spectacles back on, and her eyes behind them were fierce and kind— "without telling the sad sword."

At the stairs, Sef went very still.

"I wasn't going to decide anything tonight," Paloma said, which was true, and also completely false, because some traitorous part of her had already signed the contract, had already dipped the pen, and was now furious at being caught doing paperwork ahead of schedule.

"Good," said Vesha. "Because there's one more thing. Look at the margin note. Left side, second appendix page."

Paloma turned back. There, in a different hand—an older hand, cramped and hurried, the hand of whoever had copied the folio three years ago—was a note in faded brown ink:

*The clause works both ways. What is freely surrendered may be freely re-given. But no record survives of anyone ever having done it. Ask yourself why.*

Paloma read it aloud. The vault absorbed the words the way libraries absorb everything: silently, permanently.

"Re-given," she said. "Someone could give the name back? Give *me* my name back?"

"In theory. If a name can be surrendered, it can be returned—that's the symmetry the whole grammar runs on. Binding and unbinding. Taking and giving." Vesha shrugged, a small unhappy motion. "But the note-writer is right to ask. Nobody's ever documented it. Maybe because nobody's tried. Or maybe—" she tapped the folio— "because a name, once out in the world, doesn't want to come home. Names are stubborn things. Ask any parent trying to get a child to answer to a nickname they hate."

Behind the cabinets, Wren stirred, mumbled something about honey, and slept on.

*

They worked until the lamps burned low.

Vesha spread out drafting paper and the two of them built the counter-sigil the way Paloma had once built ledger summaries—line by line, checking each stroke against the source, arguing about flourishes. ("That curve is decorative." "It is *not* decorative, it stabilizes the terminal node." "It looks like a duck.") Sef eventually came down from the stairs and stood behind them pretending not to read, which lasted approximately ten minutes before he pointed at a symbol and asked, very casually, whether that one was the same mark he'd seen on the magus's gloves three years ago.

It was not. It was much worse. He went pale in a way he clearly thought was invisible, and Paloma let him think so, because kindness sometimes meant letting a man pretend his hands weren't shaking.

By midnight—or what passed for midnight under the city, where the thirteenth moon hung somewhere beyond the smog like a coin at the bottom of a well—the drafts were done. Three copies, sealed against tampering, hidden in three places Vesha refused to disclose even under direct questioning, which she answered with a look that suggested she had been questioned directly by considerably worse people than Paloma.

Paloma sat back and flexed her aching hand and looked at the clause again, transcribed now in her own neat copyist's hand at the bottom of the master draft.

*A name freely given.*

It fit. That was the thing that frightened her most, how neatly it fit—like a key made for her lock, like the sigil had known her all along, like the dying stranger at the temple gate hadn't handed her a burden so much as a bill with her name pre-filled at the top. All those years copying other people's accounts, and here at last was her own entry, waiting for her signature.

Except.

Except Wren, asleep, who had named her "the ink lady" and meant it as a title of honor. Except Sef, standing guard at the bottom of the stairs because he didn't trust the stairs, bless his ridiculous honor. Except the version of herself she was becoming, the one who broke rules to give water to dying strangers, who forged imperial papers without flinching, who had learned to argue about decorative ducks. Was a name the summary of a person, or just the label on the bottle? Could you pour the wine out and keep the vintage?

*What is freely surrendered may be freely re-given.*

Maybe. Perhaps. In theory. With no evidence, in the whole history of the world, that anyone had ever come back from it.

Paloma blew out her lamp.

"We sleep here tonight," Vesha said, in the tone of a woman whose authority extended at least three floors underground. "There are cots behind the genealogies. They smell like mothballs and regret. You'll survive."

*

Paloma did not sleep.

She lay on the cot listening to the archive breathe—the settling of shelves, the tick of cooling lamps, Wren's slow child-sized snoring from the next cot over—and turned the clause over and over like a coin she couldn't stop flipping.

Give the name, save the king, live. Live as a stranger among strangers, watching Sef grieve for someone standing right in front of him. Watching Wren ask "where's the ink lady?" and getting no answer, forever.

Refuse, and die cleanly, whole, remembered. And Aldric stays a garment on a hanger for a thing that eats names, and Merrow's Cross multiplies across the empire, and everyone she has ever failed stays failed.

There was a third option, whispered the coin as it spun. Find the way to give the name and then take it back. Be the first. Break the symmetry. Write the ending nobody had ever written.

It would require, she thought drowsily, an enormous amount of nerve, a flawless memory of her own true name, and possibly a miracle.

Paloma Hartwell had never had much use for miracles. But she was, after all, a scribe. And scribes knew better than anyone that the difference between an impossible text and a difficult one was usually just a matter of who was willing to hold the pen.

She was nearly asleep when the dream came.

Not a dream, quite. More like a door opening in a wall she'd thought was solid. Warmth spilled through it—golden, honeyed, the warmth of debts forgiven, of ledgers wiped clean, of a valley with no auditors and a sky with no thirteenth moon. And from somewhere deep inside the warmth, a voice that sounded like every kind voice she had ever heard braided into one, said:

*Little scribe. We should talk about the price.*

Paloma's eyes snapped open in the dark.

Across the room, in the cold light of the banked lamps, every candle flame in the vault had bent sideways—toward her—all at once, like grass bowing before wind.

Like something bowing before a queen.

Like something making an offer.
