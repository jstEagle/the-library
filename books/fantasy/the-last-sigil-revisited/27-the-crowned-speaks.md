## 27. The Crowned Speaks

I have worn many faces in my long tenancy of this world, but none so inconvenient as a king's.

Kings are terribly busy. There are petitions to hear, granaries to count, ambassadors to smile at until their smiles curdle. Aldric's body requires feeding three times a day and sleeping eight hours a night, which I consider a vulgar indulgence for a vessel. His knees ache when it rains. His left ear has a faint ringing in it that no physician can explain and that I have come to think of as a roommate I cannot evict.

And yet.

There is something delicious about a crown. Not the gold one—the heavy thing they set on his head at coronations, all jewels and inherited guilt—but the other crown, the invisible one made of attention. When the king walks into a room, every spine straightens. Every eye arranges itself toward him like flowers toward a particularly tyrannical sun. For three years I have bathed in that attention, and I confess it suits me.

But tonight I am not interested in courtiers. Tonight I am interested in a scribe.

* * *

Her name is Paloma Hartwell, though she does not yet know what her name is worth, which I find charming. Most people who carry their own deaths around in their blood at least have the decency to be frightened about it.

I felt her the moment she crossed the border into my kingdom—felt her the way you feel a draft under a door on a cold night. A sigil-bearer. The last one had been an old cartographer named Emeric Voss who ran for eleven years on legs that were already failing him, and died at the gate of a temple in a valley where I could not follow. I had assumed the burden would gutter out with him. Burdens usually do. That is rather the point of burdens.

Instead it found a scribe with ink-stained fingers and a debt ledger longer than her arm, and now she is walking toward my throne room with a disgraced knight at her side and a nameless child somewhere in her wake, and I find myself, for the first time in decades, obliged to take an interest.

I could kill her, of course. Let us be honest with ourselves; I am always honest with myself, it is everyone else who lies. My agents could pluck her from any street in Aurelholm like a thread from a hem. But killing a sigil-bearer only releases the sigil to find another bearer, and the last thing I need is another decade of this tiresome relay. No. Sigils are not killed. They are *answered*. And there is exactly one answer that has never failed me in all my long, long residence.

Everyone, given the choice, will put down something heavy if you offer to carry it for them.

They simply need to be asked *kindly*.

* * *

So I go down into the deep places of the palace—not the vaults beneath the throne room, which are vulgar with old bindings and older blood, but the true deep place, the one that was here before the palace, before the city, before anyone thought to build anything at all. The dreaming-place. It is quiet down here in the way that wells are quiet. In the way that held breaths are quiet.

I sit in the dark—which is to say, I *am* the dark, briefly, gloriously, before I must climb back up into Aldric's aching knees—and I reach along the thread of the sigil to where it sleeps inside a young woman curled in a narrow bed above a rogue archivist's forbidden library.

And I knock.

Politely. One does want to make a good first impression.

* * *

She dreams, at first, of ledgers.

This does not surprise me. I have watched thousands of minds from the inside of their sleep, and do you know what most people dream of? Their debts. Their shames. The conversation they should have had. The door they did not open. Human beings carry their whole lives behind their eyes like a shopkeeper carries his accounts, forever tallying, forever finding the sums short.

Paloma Hartwell dreams of columns of figures that will not balance. She dreams of the auditor's voice: *Your bond extends another decade, scribe.* She dreams of candle wax and cramped fingers and the particular humiliation of being competent at something you hate.

So I begin there. One always begins where the ache is. That is not cruelty; that is courtesy.

In the dream, the ledger room fills slowly with warm light, and the columns of figures begin, gently, to erase themselves.

* * *

She notices before most would. That is the first thing I learn about her, and I will admit it pleases me: she notices. A lesser mind would accept erasure the way soil accepts rain. She frowns at the dissolving numbers. She puts her finger on the page to stop them, the way a child traps a ladybird under a cup.

"That's not right," she says, to the dream, to me. "The sums were wrong before. Erasing them doesn't fix them."

Oh, I *like* her.

"Perhaps," I say, and my voice arrives everywhere at once, soft as settling dust, "the sums were never yours to keep."

She turns. In the dream she has made me a shape—or rather her sleeping mind has, because I prefer to let them dress me themselves; it tells me so much. She has dressed me in candlelight and shadow and the suggestion of a figure seated behind the ledger desk, wearing a crown that might be gold or might be the memory of gold. Good. Honest, even. I am, after all, mostly memory.

"You're him," she says. "The Crowned."

"The king prefers that I not be discussed at dinner parties," I say. "But yes."

"You're in his head."

"I'm in his *everything*, actually. Head, heart, knees." I pause. "Especially the knees. You cannot imagine what those knees are like."

Something flickers across her dream-face that is almost—a laugh? Suppressed, strangled, but present. Wonderful. Humor is the hinge on which every good persuasion swings.

"And you've come to kill me," she says.

"Heavens, no. Killing you would be a waste, and I abhor waste. Do you know how rare it is, Paloma Hartwell, to meet someone whose entire existence is a mistake of bookkeeping?" I slide a ledger across the dream-desk toward her. It is hers—I have copied it faithfully, every entry, every interest charge, every year of her indenture stretched out in tidy ink. "Look at your life. Truly look at it. You were sold into a silent temple at fourteen to cover a debt your father incurred buying medicine he was too dead to use. You copy other people's numbers by candlelight. You are owed nothing and owned entirely. Am I lying?"

She looks at the ledger. Her jaw tightens. "No."

"Thank you. Honesty is so restful between intelligent people." I turn the page for her. "Now. Here is what I propose instead."

* * *

I show her a valley. Not Vael Hollow—she knows Vael Hollow, and one does not sell a customer the house she already lives in. I show her a different valley, green and sunlit, with a small stone cottage and bees in the hawthorn and a garden that needs weeding, which is the only labor in my vision of paradise, because a person with nothing to weed begins asking questions.

"This can be yours," I tell her. "Debt-forgiven. Bond-dissolved. The temple's claim on you annulled so thoroughly that no auditor in the empire could find the paper trail. Your hands healed of their cramping. Your sleep unbroken. And—" I lean closer, gentle as a confession, "—the pain gone too. All of it. Your father's death. The stranger bleeding out while priests looked through him. The fear that walks behind you now like a dog you didn't ask for. Gone. All of it erased as cleanly as a line through wet ink."

Her eyes have gone bright in the dream-light. Not tears. Something worse. *Recognition.*

"What do you want in exchange?" she asks, and I could kiss her, metaphorically, for asking it directly. So few ask directly. They circle and bargain and pretend, as though the price weren't always the same price.

"Walk away," I say. "That is all. Turn south at the next crossroads. Go back to your valley. Forget the map, forget the sigil, forget the king. Let things remain as they are. In return, the ledger closes. The debt dies. You live out your days pulling weeds and arguing with bees, owing no one anything, ever again."

"That's it?"

"That's everything. I'm not asking for your loyalty or your silence or your soul—souls are dreadfully overrated, mostly water and stubbornness. I am asking you to do *nothing*. It is, I promise you, the easiest thing anyone will ever ask of you."

She is quiet for a long moment. In dreams, quiet stretches like taffy. I let it stretch. Patience is the oldest tool I own and the sharpest.

Then she says, "What happens to the king?"

Ah.

"Nothing you need concern yourself with."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the *best kind* of answer," I say warmly. "The kind that costs you nothing to hear and less to believe."

* * *

But she does not believe it, of course. She is turning it over—I can feel her mind working the way one feels weather changing. And I realize I have made a tactical error, small but real: I have shown her too clearly what she wants, and Paloma Hartwell, bless her suspicious little heart, has spent her whole life watching people dangle things she wanted just out of reach. She knows the weight of a lure. She has been bait since she was fourteen years old.

So I change tactics, as one must. I give her the truth.

"You want to know what happens to the king," I say. "Very well. Nothing *worse* than what has already happened. He sleeps. I rule. The kingdom runs—better than it ran under him, frankly; the man had sentimental ideas about grain prices. Nobody suffers who was not already suffering. And in exchange, one tired scribe gets her life back. Weigh it honestly, Paloma. You are very good at weighing. I have seen your ledgers."

"They don't balance," she says quietly. "My ledgers. They never balance. There's always a remainder."

"A remainder?"

"Something left over. Something that doesn't fit the column." She looks up at me then, and oh, there it is—the thing I feared, the thing I have seen maybe four times in all my centuries of this work. Not courage. Courage is common; courage is cheap; soldiers buy it by the pint. This is *stubbornness*. Water-and-iron stubbornness. The kind that cannot be bought because it does not understand it is being offered anything.

"I don't know what the remainder is yet," she says. "But it isn't for sale."

* * *

I could press. I could flood the dream with terror, with the faces of the erased, with the sound of Merrow's Cross where nine hundred people answer to "hey, you." I have done it before. It works on perhaps one in five.

But terror hardens the remainder. And besides—I meant what I said about honesty being restful.

So instead I let the dream soften at its edges. The cottage fades. The bees dissolve into golden motes. I leave her one last gift before the invitation closes, because I am generous even in defeat, and generosity makes such excellent fertilizer.

"One more thing, scribe," I say, as the ledger room dims. "A free piece of truth, no strings. Ask your knight what he buried at Caelmoor. Ask him what he carried down into the dark three years ago, and what he promised the magus, and why a kingsguard who swore to die for his king has spent three years running *away* instead. Then decide whether the man walking beside you is your shield—or the third blade pointed at your back."

Her breath catches. In the dream and in the waking both, I feel it catch, a small snag in the fabric of her sleep.

"You're lying," she whispers.

"I am many things," I say, and I let the crown on my borrowed brow gleam once, gold and honest, "but I have never once needed to lie to make people destroy themselves. They do it beautifully for free. Sleep well, Paloma Hartwell. The offer stands until the thirteenth moon turns. After that, I'm afraid, the ledger closes on its own terms."

* * *

I rise then, up through the dreaming-places, up through stone and root and the foundations of gilded lies, back into the body of a king. Aldric stirs faintly beneath me—somewhere far down inside his captivity, he felt that conversation pass through our shared walls, and he is afraid for her, the dear loyal fool. I pat him inwardly, the way one pats a dog during a thunderstorm.

Down in the waking world, in Vesha's crowded archive of condemned things, a scribe wakes gasping in a narrow bed, her hand pressed flat against her wrist where the sigil sleeps, her heart going like a hammer on a nail.

I settle back into the business of ruling. There is a petition about bridge tolls that requires my signature and my signature requires Aldric's hand, which requires that I remember how he holds a pen—loosely, sloppily, left-handed, the man wrote like a spider dancing. Three years and I still find his handwriting distasteful.

But beneath the tolls and the granaries and the aching knees, I am thinking about the remainder.

Four times, in all my long residence, someone has refused me. Each time I told myself the same comforting story: *they will break, eventually; everyone breaks.* And each time, I was wrong, and each time it cost me something irreplaceable, and each time I learned nothing, because that is the one lesson I have never managed to write into my own ledger.

The thirteenth moon wanes above Aurelholm, thin as a paring of fingernail. Five weeks. Perhaps four, if she hurries.

Somewhere below me, in a city full of stolen names, a girl who refuses to be sold is already awake, lighting a candle, reaching for ink.

I do hope Sef Corvane enjoys her questions.

I certainly intend to.
