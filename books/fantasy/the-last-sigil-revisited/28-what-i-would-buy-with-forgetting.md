## 28. What I Would Buy With Forgetting

The dream began the way all of Vesha's archive did: in dust motes and candlelight.

Paloma stood between the shelves of condemned things—the confiscated ledgers, the banned genealogies, the jars of name-vials glinting like trapped fireflies—and everything was quiet. Too quiet. The archive was never truly silent; it breathed with paper and mouse and Vesha's muttering. But this version had no sound at all, and the candles burned without flickering, their flames standing perfectly straight, like soldiers at attention.

"You're dreaming," she told herself firmly. "Which means either you're asleep at your desk again, which Vesha will mock you for until the end of days, or something is talking to you."

"Both," said a voice from everywhere and nowhere. It was not unkind. That was the worst part. It had the texture of her own thoughts, warmed slightly, as if her inner voice had been left near a fire. "Though I'd argue only one of those things counts as talking."

Paloma turned slowly. Between two shelves stood a figure she could not quite focus on—a tall shape wearing light the way other people wore cloaks, its edges always a half-second behind where her eyes expected them to be.

"The Crowned," she said.

"A flattering title. I never asked for it." The figure drifted closer—not walking, simply being nearer than it had been. "You may call me what you like. Most people call me nothing at all. That's rather the point of me."

"I'm not interested in conversation with the thing wearing my king."

"Wearing," the Crowned repeated, tasting the word. "Such an ugly verb. I prefer *inhabiting*. Or *sustaining*. Aldric would have died three years ago, did you know that? Poisoned at Caelmoor, before your Sef ever arrived to help with the binding. The binding didn't imprison him. It kept him alive long enough for me to move in." A pause, almost delicate. "I am a lodger who pays rent in breath."

"Liar."

"Probably. But you can't prove it, and neither can he, and isn't that the whole trouble with kings?" The light shifted, and suddenly the archive was different—still Vesha's shelves, but warmer, the candles gold instead of white, and there was a desk. A good desk. Oak, wide, with a window beside it that looked out on—

Paloma's breath caught. Vael Hollow. Not the Vael Hollow of ledgers and debt-auditors and candle stubs hoarded like treasure, but the valley in high summer, green to the ridgelines, the temple bells (which never rang; the Silent Temple did not believe in bells) somehow ringing anyway, soft and sweet.

"This isn't real," she said, but her voice wobbled.

"No," the Crowned agreed pleasantly. "It's better than real. Real is just a draft. This is the fair copy." Something gestured—she felt it more than saw it—and the dream unfolded around her like a ledger opening to a clean page.

* * *

She saw herself at the desk. Older, or perhaps just rested. There was ink on her fingers—the good kind of ink stain, earned by choice—and stacks of her own work. Not copies. Originals. Histories, maybe, or stories, the kind of thing she'd once imagined writing back when she was small enough to imagine things without calculating their cost.

There was no debt-bond stamped on the doorframe of the little house visible past the window. No syndicate seal. No auditor with his thin smile and thinner mercy.

Her mother was there. That undid her. Her mother, dead six years, alive in the dream garden with dirt under her nails and flour on her apron, laughing at some joke Paloma couldn't hear but remembered the shape of. And beside her—

"Sef," Paloma whispered.

He looked younger in the dream. Unburdened. He was mending a fence badly and arguing about it cheerfully, and when he glanced toward the window he smiled—not the careful, rationed smiles she knew, the ones he spent like a man counting coins—but a whole smile, spendthrift and easy.

And there was no sigil burning on anyone's wrist. No thirteenth moon ticking down. No king suffocating inside his own body. No Wren flinching at loud voices. Just summer, forever, and everyone she had failed forgiven by the simple mechanism of never having failed at anything.

"What is this?" she asked, and hated how young she sounded.

"An offer," said the Crowned, gentle as a debt-forgiveness letter. "Walk away. Before the thirteenth turn. Take the salt road south, take any road, and by morning none of it will have touched you. Your debts—erased. Not paid. Erased, as if they were never written. Your mother's death—" the voice softened, and Paloma realized with horror that it was using her own cadence now, her own way of speaking, "—will hurt exactly as much as it hurts right after a very pleasant dream. Which is to say: it will fade. Grief is just a memory that hasn't been given permission to rest. I am very good at giving memories permission."

"And the king?"

"Will go on wearing himself, or I will go on wearing him, and either way you'll be free of it. You've carried other people's burdens since you could hold a quill, Paloma Hartwell. Has anyone ever once asked if you wanted the job?"

She opened her mouth to say *yes*, and found—to her genuine astonishment—that the answer was complicated.

Vesha had asked. In her brusque, ink-stained way, shoving tea at her: "Eat something, you ridiculous girl, nobody rewrites reality on an empty stomach." Sef had asked, in his broken, overcomplicated fashion, every time he stood between her and danger like a wall with guilt problems. Wren asked constantly, in the wordless way children ask, holding up scraped knees and odd-shaped rocks for inspection.

But the auditor had never asked. The Temple had never asked. Ten years of copying other people's accounts in other people's hands for other people's profit, and no one had asked whether Paloma Hartwell wanted to be Paloma Hartwell.

"That's the truth of it, isn't it," the Crowned said softly. "Not whether you can save him. Whether you want to be the one who does. And you don't. You're tired. You've been tired since you were nine years old. I'm offering you the one thing no god, no king, no knight-errant has ever offered you."

The dream-garden shimmered. Her mother laughed. Somewhere in the dream-house, a kettle whistled.

"Rest," the Crowned said. "That's all. Just rest. Say yes, and wake up light."

* * *

Paloma sat down at the dream-desk.

She told herself it was only to rest her legs. The chair was perfect, worn in exactly the shape of her, and the inkwell was full, and the pen fit her hand like it had been carved from her own bone.

She thought about the debt. Nine years remaining on her bond, if the auditor was feeling generous, and he had not been feeling generous lately. She thought about the salt-flats, and running, and the map burning itself into her skin, and Merrow's Cross where people walked around with holes where their names used to be. She thought about the vial in the Undermarket labeled *P. Hartwell*, sold ahead of her by someone whose identity she still didn't know, and how violated she'd felt, and how strange it was that part of her—some tired, traitorous corner of her—had also felt a flicker of *there but for fortune*.

If someone else could sell her name, why couldn't she give it away? At least then it would be hers to give.

"See?" said the Crowned, reading her thoughts with the ease of an old scribe reading a familiar hand. "You've already argued yourself halfway there. I barely had to try. Honestly, you're the easiest mark I've had in a century, and I once convinced a duke he'd never been born."

"What happens to Wren?" Paloma asked. It came out steadier than she felt. "In your version. In the garden. Is she there?"

A pause. The first hesitation the thing had shown.

"She could be—"

"Don't." Paloma stood up so fast the chair skidded. "Don't you dare fill her in like a blank space in your picture. She's not furniture. She's not a detail you add to make the offer pretty. If she's there because I said yes, then she's there erased too—no fear, no scars, no memory of Merrow's Cross, no—" Paloma's voice cracked and she pushed through it. "She held my hand on the barge when she had nightmares. Do you know why she has nightmares? Because men came and took her name while she slept. And you're offering me a world where that never happened, which sounds kind until you realize it means the little girl who learned to trust again afterward—*that* girl never gets to exist either. You're not selling peace. You're selling the grave of everyone we were on the way here."

The garden dimmed. Her mother's laugh stretched thin, like fabric pulled too far.

"It would still *feel* real," the Crowned said, and for the first time there was something underneath the warmth—something older and hungrier and very slightly wounded. "It would feel real forever. You would die happy. What does it matter what's true underneath?"

"Because I'm a scribe," Paloma said. "Underneath is the entire job."

* * *

She woke at the archive desk with her cheek stuck to a page of counter-sigil notes and her heart hammering like a debtor at the door.

Lamplight. Real lamplight, guttering honestly. Vesha's archive breathing around her—paper, mouse, the archivist's snores drifting down from the loft like distant thunder with opinions. For one disorienting moment Paloma couldn't remember which world was the offer and which was the price, and she gripped the edge of the desk until the wood's honest grain bit into her palms.

On the cot by the far shelf, Wren was awake.

The child watched her with those unnervingly steady eyes—eyes that had watched men steal names and had decided, somewhere in the wreckage, to keep watching things anyway. Wren didn't say anything. She just climbed off the cot, padded across the floor in her oversized borrowed boots, and climbed onto the bench beside Paloma with the matter-of-fact authority of a small general claiming territory.

"You were crying in your sleep," Wren reported. "Quiet-crying. Like the sad sword does."

"I wasn't—"

"You do a thing with your face." Wren demonstrated: a scrunching of the brows that was, Paloma admitted privately, devastatingly accurate. "The sad sword does it when he thinks nobody's looking. You do it when you think nobody's looking. Everybody here thinks nobody's looking. It's the most looked-at place I ever lived."

Paloma laughed—a wet, surprised sound—and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "What are you doing awake?"

"Bad dream." Wren shrugged, elaborately casual, the way children are casual about exactly the wrong things. "Men in nice clothes. Then it went away." She studied Paloma with sudden intensity. "Was yours about the garden? With your mum?"

Ice water, spine to skull. "How could you possibly know that?"

Wren picked at a splinter on the bench, considering her answer with the gravity of a scholar. "When the bad men took our names, some people got visited first. In dreams. My da' did. He talked about a garden after. Said it was lovely. Said he was thinking of saying yes." Her small shoulders rose and fell. "Then the men in nice clothes came, and he said yes, and now when Ma talks about him she cries, and when I talk about him I have to make him up out of remembering, which isn't the same, because half of him I invented and I can't tell which half anymore."

The lamp hissed. Paloma's eyes stung all over again.

"He offered you a garden," she said quietly. "And you said no."

"I didn't say no." Wren frowned, correcting the record. "I said, 'Can Ma come?' And it did the quiet thing, the not-answering thing, and I knew it was lying, because things that tell true don't mind questions." She looked up, fierce and certain. "Things that lie get cross about questions."

*Things that lie get cross about questions.* Paloma turned that over, and found it fit the Crowned's dream perfectly—every question she'd asked had made the garden flicker, had made the warmth strain at the seams.

"Wren," she said, "you may be the finest logician in this kingdom, and I intend to tell Vesha you said so, purely to watch her object."

Wren accepted this tribute with a nod, then leaned her head against Paloma's arm, all business again. "Did you say yes?"

"No."

"Good." A pause. "Was it hard?"

Paloma looked at the desk—at the honest mess of her own handwriting, the counter-sigil drafts, the ink-stained fingers that had copied ten thousand pages of other people's lives and were finally, terrifyingly, working on her own. She thought about her mother in the garden, and how badly she wanted that to be real, and how the wanting itself was proof of everything worth keeping. Grief was the receipt. You only grieved what you'd actually had.

"Yes," she said. "It was the hardest no I've ever said. And I'm going to have to keep saying it, probably. Right up until the thirteenth turn."

"That's alright," said Wren, with the vast tolerance of the very young for adult difficulties. "Saying no lots of times is just how you practice."

They sat together while the lamp burned down, and Paloma did not sleep again, but neither did she dream. She worked. Page after page, the sigil-grammar unfolding under her pen, and somewhere in the small hours she noticed that her hand had stopped shaking.

* * *

Dawn came grey through the archive's high windows, and with it came the sound that would end the quiet forever: boots. Many boots. Not the careful tread of Vesha's assistants, who shuffled like penitent cats, but the coordinated, confident stride of people who had never once been asked to leave a room they entered.

Vesha appeared at the foot of the loft ladder already dressed, already armed with a letter-opener she held like a dagger, and already furious in the controlled way that meant she was frightened underneath.

"Someone opened the outer gate last night," she said flatly. "Someone let them in. And Paloma—" her eyes went to the empty bracket on the wall where the counter-sigil drafts should have been hanging, and her face went grey, "—they weren't thieves. They took exactly one thing. They knew precisely what to look for."

Down the corridor, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of the Undermarket gate grinding open, and beneath it, Wren's small hand found Paloma's and squeezed.

"They're coming," Wren said.
