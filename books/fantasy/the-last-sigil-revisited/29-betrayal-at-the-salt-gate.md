## 29. Betrayal at the Salt Gate

Sef Corvane had survived three years on a single principle: trust no one's kindness longer than it takes to walk out their door.

It was a miserable way to live. It had also kept him alive, which he'd come to regard as less a blessing than a stubborn habit, like an old dog that refused to die simply because nobody had gotten around to burying it.

And yet here he was, three days in Vesha's archive-vault beneath a tannery that smelled of rendered fat and old secrets, trusting everyone.

That was the thing about Paloma Hartwell. She trusted the way other people breathed—automatically, constantly, without apparent effort—and somehow she made it contagious. He'd watched her hand Wren honey-bread without checking whether the child had already eaten. He'd watched her thank Vesha's assistants by name, all six of them, learning each one in a single afternoon as though names were a currency she refused to hoard.

He should have paid more attention to the seventh one.

* * *

The plan, as plans went, had been almost insultingly simple. That should have been his first warning. Simple plans were for honest people, and Sef had stopped being one of those somewhere around Caelmoor.

Vesha's counter-sigil required three components: the bearer's blood, the original binding text, and a draft of the rewritten clause copied onto vellum cured in moonwater. The first two traveled with them. The third—the drafts, plural, because Vesha insisted on redundancy the way other scholars insisted on breakfast—would be carried separately through the Salt Gate, the oldest and least-guarded entrance to Aurelholm, where smugglers had worn the customs officers down to a state of philosophical indifference.

"You'll go at dusk," Vesha had said, tapping the map with one ink-stained finger. "Dusk is when the gate guards change shifts, and changing shifts makes men stupid. Trust me. I've bribed my way past stupider."

"Have you bribed your way past smarter?" Paloma asked.

"No," Vesha said cheerfully. "Smart people can't be bribed. That's why I only ever meet stupid ones."

Wren had laughed so hard she snorted honey-bread, which was, in Sef's private accounting, the finest sound produced in the capital that week.

Now it was dusk, and they stood in the shadow of a shuttered dye-shop fifty paces from the Salt Gate, and everything was wrong in ways he could feel in his teeth before he could name them.

"The lamps," he said quietly.

Paloma followed his gaze. The gate lanterns were lit—which was normal—but between them stood four figures in pilgrim grey, standing too evenly spaced, the way soldiers stand when someone has told them to look casual and they have interpreted this as *stand at attention while thinking about nothing*.

"Pilgrims don't travel at shift change," Paloma murmured.

"Pilgrims don't stand like they're waiting for a cue." He turned. "Where's Tamsin?"

Tamsin was the seventh assistant. Young, eager, always underfoot, forever volunteering for errands no one wanted. Three days ago Sef had caught him lingering outside the map room, and the boy had flushed and claimed he'd been lost. In a building the size of a large pantry.

"He went ahead," Paloma said. "To signal the barge contact. Why?"

"Because he's been lost in the same corridor twice." Sef closed his eyes briefly, doing the arithmetic he'd spent three years hoping never to do again. Four pilgrims. Shift change. A boy who volunteered too eagerly. "He's sold us."

Paloma stared at him. Not with disbelief—with something worse. She was *checking*, running back over every kindness she'd extended, every name she'd learned, looking for the moment she'd miscounted. He watched her find it and watched it land on her like a dropped stone.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, no. Is he—did we—should we have *paid him more?*"

"That's not—" Sef pinched the bridge of his nose. "Paloma. The lesson is not that we underpaid our spy."

"It's a little bit that we underpaid our spy."

"*No.*" But there it was, the terrible warmth of her, reaching even now for the most charitable explanation of treachery, and gods help him, some traitorous corner of his chest found it endearing even as the pilgrims below began to move.

* * *

They ran for the north passage—the tanners' stairs, a route Vesha swore even the rats used on credit. Sef took point, Paloma behind him clutching the satchel with the blood-vials and the original text, Wren behind her because Wren had refused, absolutely refused, to be left in the vault with the assistants, and after what had just become clear about the assistants, no one argued.

They made the second landing before the trap sprang.

Not a trap, precisely. A person. Grey-cloaked, patient, leaning against the wall at the turn as if he'd been installed there by the architect. His face was smooth and pleasant and entirely forgettable, which was how Sef knew him for what he was before the man even spoke. Crowned agents came in two varieties: the kind who wore friendly faces badly, and the kind who wore them perfectly.

This one wore his perfectly.

"Evening," the agent said pleasantly. "Lovely night for a walk. Terrible night for a satchel, though. Hand it over and nobody learns anything painful."

"Sef," Paloma whispered.

"I see him."

"Do you want me to—"

"No." He stepped forward, putting himself between. "You run. Both of you. North stairs, down to the canal, the barge master owes me—"

"He doesn't owe you anything, you're terrible at debts," Paloma said, but she was already moving, pulling Wren with her, and Sef loved her a little for insulting him correctly under pressure.

The agent moved when she moved—not fast, just *certain*, the way water moves toward a drain. Sef drew his sword. It felt heavier than it used to, or perhaps his arm did; three years of running had not kept the edge on either.

"I know you," the agent said, tilting his head. "Corvane. The kingsguard who wasn't there when it mattered. They still tell stories about you in the palace. Sad ones."

"They tell stories about everyone down here," Sef said. "Mostly boring ones. Let's keep yours short."

What followed was brief, graceless, and effective, which described most of Sef's fighting and, if he was honest, most of his life. The agent was good—better than good, his blade work had the eerie economy of someone who had never once worried about consequences—but he fought like a man reciting, and Sef fought like a man apologizing, and apology, done properly, has a desperate sincerity that recitation cannot match. He took a slice across the forearm that would need stitching and a compliment he'd rather not examine, and he put his shoulder into the man's sternum and sent him down the stairs in a clatter of grey cloth and professional indignation.

Then he ran, because heroes linger and survivors don't, and he had long ago settled on survival as the more achievable ambition.

* * *

He caught up with them at the canal, and that was where the night stopped being survivable.

The barge was there. The barge master was there, arms crossed, face grim in the lantern-light. And beside the barge master, held by two more grey cloaks with perfectly forgettable faces, stood Vesha.

The archivist looked, Sef thought with distant horror, *annoyed*. Chained at the wrists, surrounded, captured—and annoyed, the way one is annoyed by rain or a poorly indexed book. Her eyes found Paloma and she spoke quickly, clearly, the voice of a woman who had rehearsed being arrested and found the reality disappointing.

"The drafts are gone. Tamsin took them at sundown—all three, the little rat, I taught him indexing myself, I should have taught him loyalty instead, it pays worse but—" She shook her head sharply. "Listen to me. The clauses are memorized. All of it. It lives in my head and my head is not for sale. Do you hear me, girl? *Memorized.* Whatever happens, the work survives."

"We're not leaving you," Paloma said, and her voice had changed—gone low and flat in a way Sef had heard only once before, at Merrow's Cross, standing among people whose names had been taken. "Sef. We're not leaving her."

"Paloma—"

"There are three of them," Vesha said, with the tone of a woman correcting arithmetic. "Two holding me, one on the towpath. You are one disgraced knight with a borrowed sword and one scribe with—" she gestured vaguely at all of Paloma, "—whatever that is. This is not a rescue. This is a donation."

"She's right," Sef said, hating it.

"She's usually right," Paloma said, "and it's unbearable," and then Wren made a sound.

Small. Sharp. The sound a child makes when hurt and trying very hard not to make a bigger sound about it, because bigger sounds bring adults, and adults bring decisions.

Sef turned and saw her: Wren, half-behind a mooring post where she'd been told—*told*, explicitly, in writing almost—to stay hidden, and a fourth agent he hadn't counted, the one from the towpath, standing over her with a knife that had already been used. Just a line of red along her arm, shallow, the kind of wound that bleeds dramatically and means little, except that it meant everything, because it was hers, because she was nine, because the Crowned's people had cut a child to make a point.

The point, apparently, was that they could.

Something in Sef went very quiet and very old. It was the same quiet that lived at Caelmoor, under three years of ash. He crossed the towpath in six strides, and the agent had time to raise the knife, and then did not have time for anything else. Sef did not kill him. He wanted to—that was the part he would carry, later, turning it over in the dark—but he broke the man's sword-arm instead, thoroughly, and put him in the canal, and stood breathing like a bellows while the ripples settled.

When he turned around, Paloma was kneeling by Wren, tearing a strip from her own sleeve, and her hands were steady, because of course they were, her hands were always steady, it was the rest of her that shook where no one could see.

"Look at me," she was saying gently. "Just me. Shallow cut. I've copied ledgers for eleven years; I've seen worse paper cuts. We're going to clean it, bind it, and you're going to complain about it, because complaining is how we know you're fine."

"It stings," Wren said, with tremendous dignity.

"Good. Sting away. Stinging means it's working."

The barge master cleared his throat. "Gate's closing in a quarter hour," he said. "After that, it's the river or nothing, and the river runs past the Pale Court's front door whether you like it or not."

"They took the drafts," Sef said numbly. "Without them, the counter-sigil—"

"Are you both deaf?" Vesha snapped. "I *memorized* it. I memorize everything; it's why they're taking me and not killing me. My head is worth more intact. Which means—" and here, finally, the annoyance cracked, and Sef saw under it, saw the fear, saw the woman who had spent a lifetime keeping records the empire couldn't touch suddenly understanding that *she* was now the record, "—which means they will take me apart very carefully to get it. So stop standing on my towpath having feelings and *go*."

"We can't just—"

"You can and you will." Vesha lifted her chin. "I have been robbed, sued, exiled, and once challenged to a duel by a man whose index I had corrected publicly. I am not afraid of a palace. Now give me your arm, Corvane, you're bleeding on my canal."

He did. She bound it with brisk, contemptuous efficiency.

"Vesha," Paloma said. Her voice cracked on the name, and Sef watched her try again. "We'll come for you."

"I know," Vesha said. "That's what worries me."

* * *

They went through the Salt Gate at a dead run, because the alternative was standing still, and the guards—who were, indeed, mid-shift-change and philosophically indifferent—decided that whatever this was belonged to someone else's jurisdiction. Sef carried Wren on his back; she weighed almost nothing and gripped like a drowning sailor, her bandaged arm tucked carefully against his shoulder, her face buried against his neck.

"Ink lady," she said into his collar, muffled. "Sad sword."

"I'm here," Paloma said, running at his left, satchel clutched to her chest.

"When the sad sword hit the mean man"—Wren's voice was small and terribly matter-of-fact—"his face went all surprised. Like a fish."

"Wren—"

"I liked it," she said. Then, after a pause, with the ruthless honesty of children: "Is that bad?"

Sef thought about Caelmoor. About the binding he'd helped perform, believing it salvation. About every road since. *Is that bad.*

"Ask me again someday," he said, "when I've got a better answer."

They came out onto the embankment above the river, and there Aurelholm spread itself before them, gilded and grinning, its thousand lamps lit for the evening like teeth in a beautiful jaw. Somewhere up there, in a palace whose gardens grew for no one, the Crowned sat wearing a king, and now it had Vesha, and the drafts, and a boy named Tamsin who had learned indexing and nothing else worth knowing.

Five weeks until the thirteenth turn. Five weeks minus one catastrophic evening.

"Paloma," Sef said. "The blood. Yours. If they took the drafts—if they have samples of the binding text and they have *you* in reach—"

She had gone pale in the lamplight. He watched her arrive at it a heartbeat after he did: the Crowned didn't need the drafts. It needed the bearer. And tonight, thanks to a boy who'd sold cheap, it knew exactly where to look.

Downriver, a bell began to toll—not the hour, but the alarm, rolling out across the water from the direction of the palace, deep and deliberate, a sound like the city clearing its throat to say *we know you're here.*

"They're not hunting us," Sef said quietly. "They're inviting us."

Paloma adjusted the satchel strap on her shoulder and lifted her chin, and even frightened—even bleeding-adjacent, friendless, hunted, and out of options—she managed to look like a woman opening a ledger to a fresh page.

"Then," she said, "I suppose we'd better accept."
