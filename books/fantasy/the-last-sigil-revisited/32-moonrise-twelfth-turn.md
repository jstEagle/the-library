## 32. Moonrise, Twelfth Turn

Sef had spent three years telling himself he was not a criminal. He had been a knight-errant, then a fugitive, then a man of no particular profession who happened to sleep rough and lie often, but never—never—a criminal. Criminals were the men he'd once dragged out of taverns by their collars in the name of a king whose face he could no longer trust.

Tonight he was going to shake hands with three of them and call it salvation.

The cell they'd put him in was, he had to admit, comfortable as cells went. Pale stone, pale light, a pallet that smelled faintly of lavender—the Crowned's idea of mercy, or its idea of theater. It wanted them comfortable. Comfortable prisoners made cooperative confessions. Sef sat on the lavender pallet with his back to the wall and counted what he still owned: one set of clothes, slightly bloodied; one boot heel that clicked when it shouldn't; and a memory of every corridor beneath the palace of Aurelholm, drawn in the sure hand of a young kingsguard who had once believed the place was worth dying for.

That memory was worth more than the boot.

He heard her before he saw her—of course he did; she moved like a cat burglar who had learned the trade from a book—and then the panel behind the wine rack slid aside and Paloma Hartwell looked at him through the gap with soot on her cheekbone and an expression he had come to think of as *her* expression: fury and hope sharing a single face, each pretending the other wasn't there.

"You're supposed to be locked up," he said.

"So are you." She squeezed through the panel, graceless and quick, and stood brushing off her sleeves as though she'd merely stepped out for air. "The guard outside mine fell asleep. Remarkably fast, actually. I only talked to him about ledger arithmetic."

"Paloma."

"He was snoring before I finished explaining compound interest." She crouched at the cell door and produced something from her bodice—a strip of black iron, thin as a knife, bent at one end. "I've been practicing. Don't look so alarmed, I'm very good at small precise work. I copied ledgers for six years. Locks are just ledgers that bite."

"That's not—" He lowered his voice. "How did you get out of your own cell?"

"The same way you're about to get out of yours." The lock gave a soft click, and the door swung. She looked up at him, and the humor drained out of her face, leaving something rawer underneath. "A guard named Ossen took a bribe from me three weeks ago at Thornbridge. He's stationed here now. People remember kindness, Sef. Even here. Especially here, where nobody's kind."

He should have been proud of her. He was. But underneath the pride sat the old familiar shame, because while she'd been charming corrupt guards with arithmetic, he'd been sitting on a lavender pallet cataloguing his honor like a dead man's effects.

"I know how to get us out of this palace," he said.

"I know you do." She held the door. "That's why I came to you instead of leaving without you. Although I want it noted that leaving without you was seriously considered."

"Noted," he said, and meant *thank you*, and hoped she heard it.

* * *

Ossen met them in the guard passage between the second and third cells, a heavyset man with a face like wet bread and eyes that kept darting to the corners as though the walls might be listening. Perhaps they were. Sef wouldn't have bet against it.

"The under-passage runs from the kitchens to the old chapel crypt," Ossen whispered, pressing a ring of keys into Paloma's hands and refusing, pointedly, to look at Sef. "Past the cistern it forks. Left goes down to the vaults. Right comes up by the salt gate. You want left, but the traps down there are keyed to names—I heard the pale priests talking. They say the stones ask you who you are, and if they don't like the answer—"

"They take it from you," Paloma said quietly. "Yes. We know."

"Then why would you go down?" Ossen hissed. "Go right. Salt gate, out of the city, run, live."

"Because Vesha's in the vaults," Paloma said, "and Wren is bleeding in a cellar in the Undermarket, and tomorrow night the moon turns thirteenth and everyone I—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "Because going right isn't an option. Thank you, Ossen. Truly."

The big guard looked at her for a long moment, and Sef saw something pass over his face—not courage exactly, but the shape courage leaves behind when it's gone. "My sister's name was taken last winter," he said. "In Merrow's Cross. She walked past me at the spring market and didn't know me." He swallowed. "If the stones ask who I am, I'd like someone to know the answer. Ossen. My mother called me Osric when I was good. Tell whoever keeps such records."

"I keep such records," Paloma said. "It's written down already. Osric, son of the wet-bread-faced man, brave past all sense."

Something cracked in his face that might have been a smile. Then footsteps sounded above, and he shoved them both toward the panel and was gone, back into his gray uniform and his gray life, carrying his sister's absence like a stone.

Sef followed Paloma into the dark, and thought: *this is what honor looks like when it stops caring who sees it.*

* * *

The under-passage smelled of cold stone and older water. Sef went first, because his feet remembered the way even when his conscience didn't. Three years ago he had walked these corridors with a kingsguard's cloak and a magus's orders, and every flagstone he crossed tonight seemed to remember him too. The palace had a long memory. So did he.

"Left at the fork," he murmured, though the fork was forty paces off. "There's a seam in the wall two men wide. Press the third stone from the floor—it looks like the others, but it's hollow."

"How do you know these passages?" Paloma asked. Not accusing. Worse than accusing: genuinely curious, in the tone of a scribe who intended to write the answer down.

"Kingsguard training. We were required to learn the service ways. In case the king needed to be moved quickly." Or trapped, or bound, or carried bleeding down to the vaults while a magus chanted and Sef Corvane held the door and told himself he was saving a life. "Every servant corridor, every spy hole, every drain. A king's safety depends on knowing his own house better than anyone."

"And you've kept it all? Three years?"

"A man can lose everything else," Sef said. "Useless knowledge stays."

She was quiet a few steps. Then, gently, in the voice she used on frightened children and cornered clerks: "You could have used it sooner. To get back in, I mean. To fix things. Why didn't you?"

He could have lied. He had gotten quite good at it, and she had gotten quite good at catching him, and the space between those two facts had become the truest conversation they ever had.

"Because using them meant admitting the palace was mine to fix," he said. "And I wasn't ready to be that man again. Every other road let me pretend I was just running away. These corridors don't allow pretending. You walk them, you're a kingsguard again, with everything that means." He touched the third stone from the floor. It sank, and a seam opened in the wall, breathing out cold air. "So I stayed out and hated myself in comfort. Tonight I've decided to hate myself productively."

"That," Paloma said, "is the most honest thing you've said since the river barge."

"I'm saving my best material for the vaults."

* * *

They were ten minutes into the descent when the first trap woke.

It didn't announce itself. One moment the stairwell was ordinary darkness; the next, the walls began to whisper—a sound like many voices reading aloud in different rooms, overlapping, patient. Sef felt the hair rise on his arms. Paloma pressed close to his shoulder.

"What is that?"

"The stones asking." His mouth had gone dry. "Old warding, from before the Crowned even. The magus told me they were decommissioned. He lied about a great many things." Ahead, the whispers gathered into words, and the words were questions, and the questions were simple and terrible: *Who are you? Who are you? Who walks here?*

"Don't answer," Sef breathed. "Whatever happens, whatever you hear—don't answer. It wants a name freely spoken. That's how it takes hold."

The whisper found his name first. Of course it did. It rose up around him like cold water, and it wore a voice he knew—his own, younger, from a night three years gone: *"Sef Corvane, kingsguard, second chair, who held the door. Who held the door. Who held the door while the king screamed."*

He stopped walking. He couldn't help it. The voice knew the worst room in him and was reading it aloud, and some animal part of him wanted to shout back *it was mercy, we were saving him* —and that was precisely how the stones won. Confession as bait. He clenched his jaw shut until his teeth ached.

Then Paloma's hand found his in the dark.

She didn't speak. She didn't need to. She simply held on, the way she had on the barge during the storm, the way she had in the Undermarket when the vial labeled *P. Hartwell* shattered on the floor between them—steadying, unimpressed by his demons, waiting for them to finish their performance. And slowly the whisper lost interest in him, the way a fire loses interest in wet wood, and moved on down the passage, asking its question of the empty dark.

"Third time you've pulled me out of my own head," he managed.

"Fourth. You forgot the ferryman." She tugged him forward. "Come on. It's nearly moonrise."

* * *

The fork. Left, down, toward the vaults where Vesha sat in a cell that probably also smelled of lavender. Right, up, toward freedom and cowardice, hand in hand.

They took the left, and the passage narrowed, and that was where they found the smugglers.

Two of them, wedged into a maintenance alcove like furniture that had been stored rather than hidden: a broad woman with a scarred scalp and no eyebrows, and a wiry youth picking his teeth with a lockpick. Both wore the ash-grey sash of the Undermarket's guilds. Both had knives out before Sef finished drawing breath.

"Well," said the woman. "Kingsguard. In the under-passage. On the twelfth turn." She looked at Paloma. "And a temple rat with guard keys. This is either a rescue or a very elaborate robbery, and either way, this stretch is ours. Toll's five silver a head."

"We don't have five silver," Paloma said. "We don't have five coppers. We have a plan to break into the vaults beneath the throne room, free an archivist, and stop an ancient entity from permanently binding the king tomorrow night, and frankly, madam, you should be paying *us* for the opportunity."

The youth stopped picking his teeth. The woman stared. And then, horribly, wonderfully, she started to laugh—a low, rolling laugh that she strangled immediately with both hands, eyes darting upward, because laughter carried.

"An entity," she wheezed. "She says *an entity.*"

"Marrow," the youth said warningly.

"Shut it, Finch. An entity. Tomorrow. Moon's turning and she says it like she's complaining about the price of bread." Marrow wiped her eyes. Then she looked at Sef—really looked, the way people in the Undermarket looked, weighing a man's use and discarding the rest—and something in her expression shifted. "You're Corvane. The sad sword. The little one talks about you. Wren. Red-haired, got herself stabbed being braver than she had any business being. She's laid up in Old Netty's cellar with a fever and a mouth full of opinions about you two."

Sef's chest went tight. "She's alive?"

"Alive and insufferable. Netty's poultices are famous and so is Netty's billing." Marrow jerked her chin toward the dark ahead. "You want the vaults, you'll never get through the ward-line alone. There's a sealed grate past the cistern, keyed to guild-blood—we cut our palms on it every season so it knows us. You'll bleed too, and it'll admit you, because blood's blood down here and the stones below are too old to be picky." She spat thoughtfully. "Five silver a head. Call it a professional courtesy, waived. But you'll owe the Ash Guild a favor, and the Ash Guild always collects."

Sef looked at her scarred head, her grey sash, her knives. Everything he'd spent thirty years despising from the high clean ground of a white cloak. Criminals. Smugglers. The people he'd been sworn against.

He put out his hand.

Marrow looked at it, then at him, and grinned with all the warmth of an open trap. "Oh, this is my favorite part," she said, and shook it.

* * *

The guild-blood worked as promised. Sef cut his palm on the grate's edge and felt the old metal drink, a sensation like being recognized by something that predated recognition, and then the grate rolled aside and they were through, into the deepest dark yet, where the air tasted of dust and candle-smoke and, very faintly, of incense burned to cover fear.

Vesha's cell was the fourth door on the crypt level. Paloma picked it in eleven seconds—she counted aloud, competitive about it—and when the door opened, the archivist looked up from the floor where she sat cross-legged amid a scatter of stolen paper, and said, with tremendous dignity:

"You're late. I've memorized four books. Two of them were tax codes. I want it noted that I suffered."

"Noted," Paloma said, hauling her up. "Can you still read the counter-sigil drafts from memory?"

"I can read them in my sleep. I have been. It's been very boring." Vesha swayed, caught herself on Sef's arm, and gripped hard. Her eyes, sunken as they were, went sharp. "Corvane. You came back for us. I confess I had you priced as a runner."

"So did I," Sef said. "New pricing."

They moved. Up through the crypt, past the cistern, Marrow and Finch peeling off toward their own roads with a nod that Sef found himself returning—actually returning, as one professional to another, which was a sentence he would have gagged on a year ago. The whispers in the walls had grown louder now, agitated, the whole palace seeming to murmur like a hive touched with a stick, because somewhere above them the sun was setting and the thirteenth moon was climbing toward its turn, and the thing wearing King Aldric knew, Sef was certain, exactly where they were and exactly what they intended.

It let them climb anyway. That was the part that scared him.

At the final stair, where the under-passage ended beneath the throne room itself, Paloma stopped and turned to him. Candlelight from Vesha's stub of tallow caught the soot on her face, the ink stains that never fully washed from her fingers, the set of her jaw that he had followed through checkpoints and ambushes and his own worst confession.

"When we go up," she said quietly, "you'll be standing between me and everything it sends. And I need you to promise me something."

"Anything."

"Don't die nobly. Die usefully, if you must, but preferably neither. I've had enough of men choosing dramatic exits over difficult mornings." Her voice cracked, just barely, on the last word, and she covered it by thrusting the tallow candle at him. "Hold this. My hands need to be free for the sigil."

He took it. Their fingers brushed, and neither of them mentioned it, which was its own kind of honesty.

Above them, muffled through the vaulted stone, bells began to ring the hour—and beneath the bells, faint but unmistakable, a sound Sef had heard only once before, three years ago in this same palace, on the worst night of his life: the deep, resonant hum of the binding-circle waking.

The thirteenth turn had begun early.

"It knows we're coming," Vesha whispered.

"Good," said Paloma Hartwell, and started up the stairs.
