## 33. The Long Climb Beneath the Throne

The passage behind the throne room's eastern pillar smelled of dust and older secrets, and it was exactly the width of Sef Corvane's shoulders plus two inches of regret.

"Hold this," he whispered, pressing a torch into my hands while he wedged himself through the gap in the stonework. There was a scrape, a grunt, the sound of a man negotiating personally with architecture. Then his voice floated back, muffled: "I want it noted that I was thinner three years ago."

"I'll have it inscribed on your memorial," I said. "*Here lies Sef Corvane. He used to fit.*"

"Your compassion is legendary."

Wren giggled — a small, bright sound she'd learned to make only in the last week, as if laughter were a language she'd been given late and was still practicing. She slipped through the gap after him like water finding its level. I went last, because I was carrying the important things: the counter-sigil written out in Vesha's careful hand against my own blood-marked wrist, a stub of chalk, three candles, and the increasingly heavy certainty that we had no idea what we were doing.

The passage sloped down. That was the first thing I understood about the vaults beneath Aurelholm — they did not go *down* so much as they went *in*, folding beneath the palace like roots under a tree, older than any throne that had been planted above them. The stones were fitted without mortar, each one bearing a mark I recognized from the map that had once lived in a leather tube and now lived in me. Binding-grammar. Old grammar. The kind that predated ink.

Sef moved ahead with a kingsguard's economy, touching walls, counting steps, muttering numbers under his breath. "Forty-one," he'd say, and then later, "forty-one again," and frown, because passages beneath palaces are not supposed to be honest about their length.

"You've been down here before," I said. Not a question.

"Once." His torch threw his shadow long and crooked up the wall. "The night of the binding. We came down this way to reach the ritual chamber. I remember thinking how cold it was." A pause. "I remember thinking a great many stupid things that night."

I let that lie where it fell. Three days since his confession over cold tea, and we had built something fragile out of the wreckage — not forgiveness, which neither of us could afford, but a working arrangement between two people who had stopped lying about the big things and were saving their dishonesty for smaller, more practical matters. Like whether the bread at breakfast had been stale. It had. We both knew it. Some lies are kindnesses.

"The traps start soon," Sef said. "Vesha's notes said the binding chamber is warded by name-work."

"Traps keyed to names," I repeated. "Which means what, precisely? They ask you your name and then arrest you?"

"Paloma—"

"I'm asking for tactical clarity, Sef."

He gave me the look he reserved for when I was being deliberately flippant about mortal danger, which was most looks, lately. "It means the wards will know who you are. They'll reach for your name the way a hand reaches for a handle. And what they find, they can pull."

I looked down at my wrist, where the sigil lay quiet under the skin, faintly warm, like a sleeping animal. My name. Paloma Hartwell. Indebted scribe of the Silent Temple of Vael Hollow. Daughter of nobody in particular. Owner of one cloak, half a candle, and a debt that no longer existed because the temple ledgers had burned along with everything else I'd been.

"They can't take it twice," I said. "I'm already spending it tomorrow night. At moonrise. It's spoken for."

"That's either very brave or very grim."

"I've been told I contain multitudes."

* * *

We came to the first door an hour down, or what felt like an hour — time behaved strangely in the roots of the palace, stretching and pooling. It was not a door so much as a suggestion of one: an archway of black stone carved over every surface with script that seemed to rearrange itself when I wasn't looking directly at it. Sef raised his torch and the light bent oddly, curving toward the arch like iron filings toward a lodestone.

"It knows we're here," Wren said quietly.

She'd been quiet for most of the descent, walking between us with her small hand occasionally finding mine or Sef's sleeve — whichever was closer, no preference, just the need to touch something alive. Since Merrow's Cross, since the ambush at the salt gate where she'd taken a knife-graze across her arm, she had developed a habit of stating obvious terrible things in a perfectly calm voice, the way children do, because children have not yet learned that naming a fear aloud is supposed to cost something.

"How can you tell?" Sef asked.

"The letters are breathing."

I looked. She was right. The script along the arch pulsed, slow and deep, like the flank of a resting beast. As I watched, a single line of characters peeled itself off the stone and hung in the air before me, glowing a pale hungry gold.

And it spoke.

Not aloud. Worse than aloud — it spoke *into* me, into the place where my name lived, and I felt it like fingers sorting through a drawer. *Paloma,* it hummed, tasting the syllables. *Hartwell. Scribe. Debtor. Daughter. Bearer.*

"No," I said, and my voice came out small.

The gold light brightened. The drawer began to open.

Then a small hand shot past me and slapped flat against the black stone.

"Wren!" Sef lunged for her —

— and nothing happened.

Nothing happened at all. The light guttered, flickered, and died like a candle pinched between wet fingers. The arch stood dark and dumb and ordinary. Wren stood there with her palm still on the stone, seven years old, bandaged arm, mud on her boots, looking back at us with the mild expression of someone who has just discovered the pantry is unlocked.

"Oh," she said. "That's all it does?"

Sef and I stared at her.

"It tried," she explained patiently, the way one explains weather to adults. "It reached for my name and there wasn't anything there. It's like—" she frowned, searching for it, "—like knocking on a house where nobody lives. It got confused and went to sleep."

The silence that followed had a particular quality. Sef broke it by sitting down abruptly on a loose block of stone, torch sagging, and putting his face briefly in his free hand.

"Three years," he said, to nobody. "Three years I've been afraid of these wards. Vesha wrote six pages of theory. And the answer is a child with no name."

"Don't feel bad," Wren said kindly, patting his knee. "You have lots of names. It must be very heavy, carrying all those."

I laughed — really laughed, the kind that comes up from somewhere below the fear, and Sef looked at me with an expression of wounded dignity that I intended to treasure for years, assuming I had years, which was uncertain, but hope springs eternal and so apparently does cruelty.

"She's right," I managed. "You're carrying Sir Sef Corvane, knight-errant, disgraced; Sef, kingsguard, oath-broken; Corvane, the deserter; and whatever name the Crowned's agents use for you in their reports. That's four names minimum. No wonder you walk like your pack is full."

"One of us should stay humble enough for the group," Sef said, hauling himself up, but the corner of his mouth had surrendered. He crouched to Wren's height. "Can you lead? Can you tell when they wake?"

She considered this with enormous seriousness, the way she considered everything, as though the world had handed her a task and she intended to perform it flawlessly. "They knock," she said. "When they're awake, I can feel them knocking. Looking for somebody home." She touched her own chest. "Nobody home," she said, matter-of-fact, and my heart did something painful and complicated that I did not have time to examine.

"Then you walk first," Sef said. "Paloma and I follow exactly where you step. Agreed?"

Wren nodded once, solemn as a coronation. Then she turned to the dark archway and walked through it as casually as stepping through a garden gate, and the darkness let her, and after a breath it let us too — but only in her footprints, I swear it, only along the path her small boots had made, the wards parting around her absence-of-name like water around a stone.

* * *

There were nine doors. I counted, because counting is what I am for; it's the one prayer I know.

Each door woke as we approached, and each time Wren laid her hand on the stone and said some version of *shh, nobody's home,* and each time the hungry gold light found nothing to grip and sank back into its carvings, baffled. Between doors, the passages narrowed and widened, spiraled and doubled back. Once we passed a gallery of statues — kings, I thought, or the idea of kings, their faces worn smooth by something older than weather — and the sigil on my wrist warmed so intensely I had to press my sleeve against it.

"It's pulling harder now," I said. "The nearer we get."

"To the chamber?"

"To whatever's left of the original binding. The map used to ache like this when we crossed old sigil-work. It wants to go home."

Sef glanced at my wrist, then away, guilt moving across his face with the ease of long practice. "I helped carve the anchor-stone," he said quietly. "Down here. I held the lamp while the royal magus worked. That's all I did, I kept telling myself. Held the lamp. As if the light weren't half the crime."

"Sef—"

"We're close. Listen."

I listened. Beneath our footsteps, beneath the breathing stone, there was another sound: a low, vast resonance, felt more than heard, like a bell that had been ringing so long the world had mistaken it for silence. The Crowned's anchor. Three years of stolen kingship humming in the bones of the mountain.

Wren had stopped at the tenth doorway.

This one was different. Larger. The black stone here was not carved but grown, seams of pale gold running through it like veins, and the whole surface trembled faintly, continuously, as though the door were less a barrier than a membrane. And the knocking — Wren flinched from it, actually flinched, first time all night.

"This one isn't asking," she whispered. "This one is shouting. It's shouting everybody's names. All at once." Her eyes came up to mine, wide and frightened. "It's shouting yours loudest."

Of course it was. I was the key walking up to the lock. Tomorrow night, at the thirteenth turn, I would stand in that chamber and speak myself into the binding and unmake the Crowned and lose every memory anyone had ever attached to the words *Paloma Hartwell*. The wards could smell it coming. They wanted their portion early.

"Then we don't give it," I said. "Wren. Can you get us through this one?"

Her jaw set. She stepped forward — and stopped. Pressed her hand to the stone. And this time the gold light didn't die politely. It surged, crawling up her arm, hunting, frantic, a starving thing thrown a locked pantry — and found nothing, nothing, nothing, and began instead to *pull*, dragging at the air around her, at her shadow, at the shape of her, trying to take anything at all—

"No!" I grabbed her other arm and hauled her back, and the light snapped off like a snuffed wick, and Wren stumbled into me shaking.

"It doesn't like me," she said, small now, all the calm gone. "The other ones got confused. This one gets *angry*."

Sef crouched beside us, torch low. Beyond the trembling membrane-door, the resonance swelled, and through the gold-veined stone I saw shadows moving — shapes pacing, patient, the Crowned's agents, stationed in the antechamber beyond. Waiting for us. Waiting, perhaps, for exactly this moment, when the least powerful among us would discover the limit of her strange gift.

"Then we don't knock," Sef said slowly. His eyes had gone distant, the way they did when three-years-old memories surfaced. "The night of the binding, we didn't come through this door. There's another way. A service stair — the magus used it to bring up the anchor-stone in pieces. It lets out inside the antechamber, past the ward-line. The wards guard the doors because the doors are the formal entrances." His mouth twisted. "Pride. Even in the trapwork. Nobody wards the servant's stair, because servants aren't considered worth stopping."

"And you remember where it is."

"I remember holding the lamp," he said. "I remember every step I took that night. God help me, it's the only road in my head I've never once wanted to forget, until tonight, and now it's the only one I need."

Wren wiped her nose on her sleeve, straightened, and tugged my hand. "Ink lady," she whispered, pointing at my wrist, where the sigil had begun to glow through the skin, bright as a coal. "It's not angry anymore. It's excited. It thinks you're coming home."

I looked down at my own burning blood, at the map that had become flesh, at the last page of a story someone else had started writing.

"Yes," I said. "Well. It's about to learn what happens when the book writes back."

Behind us, far above, through miles of palace stone, the twelfth moon began its final climb — and somewhere in the dark of the service stair, the first of the Crowned's agents turned toward the sound of our footsteps, and smiled at nothing.
