## 34. Aldric, Awake, Afraid

The first thing Aldric Vane knew of his own mind returning was the taste of dust.

Not metaphor. Actual dust—old stone ground fine, the particular grit of a palace vault that had been swept by no one in living memory. It sat on his tongue like an accusation. He realized he had been tasting it for some time without knowing it was *him* doing the tasting, which was rather like discovering you had been wearing someone else's coat for three years and only now noticing the sleeves were wrong.

He was sitting on a cold step in a corridor beneath the throne room, and he was alone.

Alone. He turned the word over carefully, the way one turns over a coin found in the street, checking both faces. For three years there had never been silence in his skull. There had been the Crowned—its attention like a hand pressed flat against the inside of his forehead, patient and vast and utterly without warmth. Even in sleep, even in those rare merciful hours when it let him drift far enough down to dream, he had felt it the way you feel a weight on your chest: present, proprietary.

Now the corridor was quiet except for water dripping somewhere, and his own breath, and—when he listened harder—the far-off sound of boots. Many boots. Running.

*An hour,* something in him said, and he did not know how he knew it, but he trusted the knowledge the way a drowning man trusts the direction of the surface. An hour. Perhaps less. The thing inside him was clawing its way back to the wheel, and it would be furious when it arrived.

Aldric got up. His legs held him, which surprised him pleasantly; he had half expected three years of captivity to have hollowed him into a scarecrow. Apparently the Crowned kept its garment pressed and fed. There was a grim courtesy in that he chose not to examine too closely.

He began to walk toward the sound of the boots, because after three years as a passenger in his own body, Aldric Vane had exactly one desire left, and it was this: before the hour ran out, he wanted to speak to someone. Anyone. To be *heard* by someone who was hearing him.

* * *

The antechamber opened off the vault corridor like a wound in the stone—a round chamber, ceiling lost in shadow, ringed with pillars carved so long ago that the figures on them had eroded into suggestions. Torches burned in iron brackets. Someone had been here recently; there were chalk marks on the floor, sigils half-drawn and scrubbed out again, and a scatter of paper covered in a small, precise hand.

And there was a girl.

She was crouched over the papers with a stub of charcoal behind her ear and another in her fingers, muttering to herself in the tone of a person arguing with a difficult sentence. She was perhaps twenty-five. Ink stained her fingers to the second knuckle. When she heard his step she went rigid—not the flinch of a coward, but the stillness of someone who has learned that sudden movement attracts worse things—and then she turned.

Aldric watched her face do several things in rapid succession. Recognition—that was the first, and it stung, because of course she recognized him, everyone knew the king's face from the murals, the coinage, the festivals where his image smiled down from banners while the man himself walked through crowds feeling like a portrait hung slightly crooked. Then fear. Then something else, something that made him stop two pillars away and wait, because he had learned in three years of watching through borrowed eyes to read faces very well indeed, and what he saw now was not awe.

It was grief.

"You're awake," she said. Her voice cracked on the second word.

"For the moment." His voice came out rusted. He hadn't used it—*used* it—in so long. "I'm told I have about an hour. Possibly less. I intend to spend it badly, so if you have expectations of regal dignity, I'd lower them now."

That startled a sound out of her—half laugh, half sob, the kind of noise people make when they've been holding themselves together with both hands and someone makes them use one hand to wave. "You're joking," she said. "You're actually—you're joking."

"I find," Aldric said, "that if I don't joke, the alternative is screaming, and I've done quite enough screaming internally for one reign."

She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, leaving a gray smear across her cheekbone. "I'm Paloma," she said. "Paloma Hartwell. I've come to unbind you."

And there it was. The reason for the grief. Because she said it the way soldiers say things before battles—bravely, and too loudly, and with the specific conviction of someone who has already done the arithmetic of the cost and decided not to share the total.

"I know what it costs," Aldric said quietly.

Paloma's charcoal stopped moving. She had picked up her papers again without seeming to notice, some scribe's reflex, organizing chaos while her heart did other things entirely. Now she looked at him with the wariness of a card player whose opponent has just named their hand.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Child," Aldric said, and then winced. "No—I'm sorry. That's what my grandmother called every person under sixty. May I call you Paloma?"

"...Yes."

"Paloma. I have spent three years watching the world through eyes that were mine but not mine. Do you understand what that means? I saw my wife—wept when she brought me figs because I couldn't make my hand reach for them. I watched my chancellor lean close and whisper treason into my ear, knowing I could hear every word and could do nothing but smile. I stood on balconies while crowds cheered me, and I screamed inside that glassy smile until I thought I would crack it like porcelain." He took a breath. "But I also watched the servants. The guards. The nameless poor in the lower city. And for the past month—" he stepped closer, and Paloma did not retreat, though her knuckles whitened around her papers— "for the past month, I have watched *you*. Through its eyes. It talks to itself, you see, when it's frightened, and it is very frightened of you. So it narrates. It plans. And I listen."

"Aldric—"

"The map-bearer died passing it to you. The archivist woman is in a cell below us right now, and the thing intends to use her as an anchor tonight. Your knight killed four men getting you down here and hasn't slept since Thornbridge. The child—" Aldric's voice caught, genuinely, unexpectedly— "the child has a burn on her arm from the ambush, and she told the knight she wasn't scared because 'sad sword needed someone to not be scared at.' I know all of it. I have been a fly on the wall of my own ruin, and I am telling you, Paloma Hartwell, that I know what the binding asks of its bearer, and I am asking you—"

"Don't," Paloma said.

"—to walk away."

"*Don't.*" Her voice came out fierce and wet at once. She jabbed her charcoal at him like a tiny sword. "Don't you dare stand there in your—your dusty crown and tell me to walk away. I didn't cross half a kingdom to—"

"To what?" Aldric asked, gentle. "To spend yourself? To become a story? Because that is what happens. I've *seen* it happen."

The torches guttered. Somewhere above them, distantly, a bell began to toll—not the hour, something older, a rhythm like a slow heartbeat, and both of them glanced upward before Aldric made himself continue.

"Three years ago," he said, "there was a woman named Maren Ostrewe. She was a laundress in the west wing. You wouldn't know her—no one does anymore, and that is precisely my point. The Crowned liked her name. It collected names the way misers collect coins, and Maren's was bright to it somehow, I never understood why. One morning she was hanging sheets in the courtyard and by evening she was standing in the kitchens holding a sheet she didn't remember washing, asking people—who she was. Not where her quarters were. *Who.* She had no name to answer to. Her face was the same. Her hands were the same. But everything she had been was simply... gone. Like a page torn out and the binding re-sewn so neatly you'd never know a page was missing."

Paloma had gone very pale. In the torchlight, for just a moment, Aldric saw the sigil-glow faintly trace itself along her wrist—the map written in her blood, stirring—and he hated it, hated the beautiful terrible thing that had chosen this ink-stained girl to spend.

"They found her a new one eventually," he went on. "'Sal.' Just Sal. She answered to it like a dog answers to a whistle. Polite. Empty-eyed. And every time I passed her in the corridor—every time, do you understand, *every time*—she would look at me for a moment too long. As if some drowned part of her remembered that we had once exchanged pleasantries about the weather. As if she was trying to recall a word that had been taken off the tip of her tongue." He closed his eyes briefly. "She looked at me the way you are looking at me now. With grief. And I swore, in whatever corner of myself remained mine, that if I ever got free of this thing, I would not permit my rescue to be purchased with anyone's self. Least of all someone who came *willingly*."

"Well, that's the stupidest oath I've ever heard," Paloma said, "and I transcribe temple ledgers, so I have heard some truly stupid oaths."

Despite everything—despite the bell tolling overhead and the hour draining away like sand through a fist—Aldric laughed. It came out cracked and rusty and real, the first true laugh of his in three years, and it hurt his face, muscles remembering work they'd been forbidden. "There it is," he managed. "Temple scribes. The Crowned fears you, and honestly, now I understand why."

"It fears me because I can end it."

"It fears you because you're *annoying*, and ending it is incidental."

"Aldric." She set down her papers. Stepped toward him, chin up, and this close he could see the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the dried blood on her sleeve that was not hers, the stubbornness sitting in her jaw like bedrock. "Listen to me. I know what I am. I've known since Vesha told me. I am not a laundress who got unlucky. Nobody tricked me. A dying man put this burden in my hands and I *chose* to carry it—through salt flats and checkpoints and a river full of smugglers, past men who wanted to peel it out of my skin. Every step of the way I chose it. That has to count for something. That has to mean the price is *mine to pay*, freely, which—" her voice wobbled and she steadied it— "which the old texts say matters. Willing blood. Freely given. It's the whole grammar of the thing."

"And what is it you think you're giving?"

Silence. The bell tolled again, closer now, or perhaps they were walking toward it—no, they weren't walking anywhere, they were frozen between the pillars like figures on one of these old carvings, mid-argument for eternity.

"My name," Paloma said finally. Quietly. "I give my name. Who I am gets... unwritten. People won't remember Paloma Hartwell. Records will go blank. My mother—" her breath hitched, and Aldric watched her wrestle it down the way one wrestles a door in a storm— "my mother sold herself into debt-bondage so I could learn letters. Everything she traded for me goes into the dark. And I'll still be *here*, walking around, eating supper, being alive—but I won't know why any of it mattered. I won't know *her*." She lifted her chin higher. "And I've decided that's worth it. A kingdom. A king. Three years stolen from one man returned. Yes. Yes, it's worth it."

"No," Aldric said.

"Yes."

"No. Listen to me—" and now his voice broke, and he let it, because there was no more time for dignity, the hour was bleeding out, and he seized her by the shoulders, gently, the way he had once held his newborn daughter, gods rest her, gently, gently, always gently, because everything precious breaks— "I have been *inside* the thing that unmakes people. Do you understand? I know its texture from within. It is not evil the way wolves are evil—it doesn't hate what it takes. It simply *takes*, the way the sea takes the shore, grain by grain, without malice, without end. And you propose to hand it the most precious grain on this entire coast. Yourself. Voluntarily. While telling me it's fine because you signed the ledger first."

"Somebody has to pay," Paloma whispered.

"Somebody already paid!" The word rang off the vaulted ceiling and came back changed. "*Me.* Three years. My wife's face going uncertain. My daughter's grave visited by a stranger wearing my grief like a costume. I have paid, Paloma, I have paid in the only currency I had left, which was witness—watching, remembering, refusing to forget a single damned thing it did—and if you erase yourself now, then it wins twice. It keeps my kingdom's memory of its own suffering *and* it takes the one person brave and stupid enough to come down here with a charcoal stub and righteous fury to stop it." His hands tightened. "I would rather wear this thing forever. Do you hear me? Forever. I would rather be its glove until I die in it, than watch you come out of that throne room alive and blank, asking 'who was Aldric Vane?' the way Sal asked who she was."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Water dripped. The bell tolled. And then Paloma did the last thing he expected: she reached up and patted his hands where they gripped her shoulders, twice, the way one pats a large anxious dog.

"You're very kind," she said. "And very tired. And I think you've confused being a victim with having a vote."

Aldric stared at her. "I beg your pardon?"

"It's my name," she said. "Mine. Not yours, not the Crowned's, not the temple's, not the debt-auditor's who tried to sell it before the Crowned could take it. Everyone in my life has owned a piece of me since the day I was born—my bond, my labor, my handwriting, even my blood apparently. This—" she touched her wrist, where the sigil glimmered like a vein of dawn under the skin— "this is the first thing that has ever been entirely mine to give or keep. And I am going to give it. Not because you deserve it. Not even because you want it. But because *I choose it*, and a choice freely made is the one thing in this whole rotten system nobody has ever let me have."

"That is," Aldric said helplessly, "an appalling argument."

"Thank you. I've been workshopping it on the sad sword for weeks. He hates it too."

"The sad sword."

"Ser Corvane. Long story. He cries when he thinks no one's looking."

"He does not."

"He absolutely does. Once into a stew, which Wren says improved it."

Against his will, against the terror climbing his throat like floodwater, Aldric felt his mouth twitch. Three years. Three years of silk and rot and the vast cold attention of a thing older than his dynasty—and here, in a dripping vault, a debt-scribe was making him almost laugh again, and telling him his sacrifice wasn't his to refuse.

"You should meet my chancellor," he said weakly. "You'd get along terribly."

Above them the bell changed. The slow heartbeat rhythm stuttered, quickened, became something urgent and arrhythmic, and the torches all leaned sideways at once as if a great breath had been drawn through the stone. The temperature dropped. Paloma's sigil flared bright enough to throw shadows, and far away—above, beyond, everywhere—something vast turned its attention downward, and Aldric felt the familiar pressure begin to rebuild against the inside of his skull, the hand pressing flat against his thoughts, the tide coming back in.

"No," he gasped, staggering. "No—not yet—listen to me, girl, there's no time, so hear the only thing that matters: the clause in the old text, the one about substitution—Vesha knows, ask her—there may be another way, a name *freely given* need not be yours, it only must be *given*—"

"What does that mean? Whose name, given how—"

"I don't know! Find her! Ask the archivist!" The pressure clenched; his vision whited at the edges; he felt his own mouth beginning, horribly, to smile. Through his teeth, fighting for each word like a swimmer fighting surf, he got out the last of it: "Paloma—if you must do this—then do it fast, and do it *angry*, anger holds shape better than grief—and when it's done, when I'm free and you're—when it's done—find the sad sword. Make him cry into something. He'll need it. And so—"

His voice was gone. The smile finished arriving, wide and gracious and utterly wrong, and the Crowned settled back into its garment like a king into robes, and the last thing Aldric Vane managed, in the shrinking cellar of himself, was to press one finger against his own palm—once, twice, three times—the old signal, the one he and his wife had used across crowded ballrooms to say:

*I am still here. Remember me.*

Then the hour ended, and the thing that wore King Aldric Vane straightened, adjusted its crown of borrowed bone, and looked down at the small furious scribe with interest.

"Well," it said pleasantly, in a voice like honey poured over a blade. "You must be the map."
