## 35. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

### Sef

The passage beneath the palace smelled of old stone and older secrets, and Sef Corvane knew every turn of it the way a man knows the scars on his own hands.

He had learned these corridors at nineteen, wide-eyed and freshly sworn, when Captain Ilsa Vane had walked him through them with a torch and said, *A kingsguard who doesn't know where the king can be stolen through is no guard at all.* He had laughed then. He was not laughing now.

Behind him came Wren, small feet quick on the flagstones, one hand fisted in his cloak because she had decided—somewhere between the salt gate and the cells—that holding onto him was safer than not. Her other arm was bandaged where the ambush had caught her. She had not cried. She had watched everything instead, with those enormous dark eyes that missed nothing, and Sef found her silence more frightening than any scream.

"How much farther?" Paloma's voice came low from behind him. She carried the counter-sigil in her blood; he could see it faintly even here, a pale shimmer along her wrist like light under ice. It had been growing brighter all day, as if it knew what the sky knew.

"Two turns and a stair," he said. "The vault opens into the antechamber beneath the throne room. From there—" He stopped.

From there they still needed a plan, and their plan had been seized at the salt gate along with Vesha and three months of Vesha's work. What remained of it fit inside Paloma's veins and nowhere else.

"From there," Paloma finished for him, "we improvise."

"You're very calm about that."

"I'm a scribe, Sef. Improvising is just writing without an outline." A ghost of her smile, there and gone. "The outline got arrested."

Wren tugged his cloak. "Sad sword," she whispered, "the lady's wrist is doing the thing again."

He looked. The shimmer had brightened to something almost audible—a hum he felt in his teeth rather than heard with his ears. Above them, beyond six floors of stone and ceremony, the thirteenth moon was climbing toward its sisters.

They were out of time. They had been out of time since Thornbridge, if he was honest, maybe since Caelmoor. They were simply running along the edge of the hour now, trying to arrive before it did.

"Keep moving," he said, and took the next turn at a run.

### Vesha

Vesha Ondrel had been tortured exactly once before in her long, well-documented life, and she had gotten through it by cataloguing. *Pain, sharp, left temple. Restraint, iron, poor craftsmanship. Interrogator, competent, unimaginative.* The archivist's mind was a vault, and vaults did not panic.

She was failing to catalogue now.

They had strung her up in the throne room itself—of course they had, the Crowned loved theater—and around her, in a ring on the marble floor, agents in pilgrim gray were drawing a circle of sigils that made her eyes water to look at. Not because they were bright. Because they were *wrong*, the way a word is wrong when someone has rearranged its letters into something that looks almost like language and means only hunger.

And above, through the great eastern windows, the sky was doing the thing the astronomer had described five weeks ago in a tower full of star charts: twelve moons rising in their appointed places, and the thirteenth—the small one, the shy one, the one most people lived their whole lives without noticing—climbing to meet them.

Conjunction. Once in nine hundred years. Twice, if you counted the night three years ago when the world had been quietly ruined.

"The grammar is sound," Vesha said aloud, because speaking kept her from screaming, and screaming would give them the satisfaction. "Whoever drafted this studied under Maravel. Sloppy terminal clauses, but sound grammar."

"Be quiet," said one of the gray pilgrims.

"I am an archivist," Vesha informed him with as much dignity as a woman hanging by her wrists can muster. "Quiet is for people with nothing to add."

The pilgrim raised his hand to strike her, and then stopped—stopped mid-motion, arm frozen, face going smooth as poured wax—and when he spoke again his voice came out layered, many voices wearing one throat like a badly fitting glove.

"She may speak," said the Crowned, through him, through all of them, through every gray figure in the circle at once until the whole room murmured with it. "I enjoy archivists. They understand that everything is a record. Everything is a ledger." The frozen man tilted his head, and his eyes were not his own. "You have kept such careful accounts of my work, little keeper of condemned things. Did you think I would not read them?"

"I hoped," Vesha said, "you'd be illiterate."

The laughter that moved through the circle was not kind. It was not unkind either. That was the horror of it—it was the laughter of something that had transcended cruelty so thoroughly that cruelty had become merely *efficient*.

"The girl is coming," the Crowned said. "She has been coming since the valley. Since the stranger bled out at your pretty temple gates. I let her come. Do you know why?"

"Because you're a monologuist," Vesha said. "All the ancient horrors are. It's in the literature."

"Because a binding needs two things," the Crowned went on, patient as tide. "An anchor, and a key. I have worn my king for three years, and he grows thin, thin, thin—a garment wears out, archivist, even a royal one. But a second binding, a permanent binding, done at the thirteenth turn with a living anchor freely offered—" the layered voices softened, almost tender, "—does not wear out. It does not fray. It simply *is*, forever, and no key can find the lock because the lock will be everywhere. In everyone."

"And you need me as the anchor."

"I need *willingness*. You have spent your life keeping names safe from me. There is a poetry in turning the keeper into the keystone. And there is a practicality." The frozen man smiled. "Your records name ten thousand people the empire forgot. Ten thousand anchors' worth of memory, stored in one stubborn skull. When I take you, I take the archive."

Vesha's mouth went dry. All her life she had believed knowledge was armor. She had never considered that a person could be a treasure hoard, and hoards could be looted.

Above the eastern window, the thirteenth moon cleared the last of the clouds.

The sigils on the floor began to glow.

"Oh," Vesha said quietly. "Oh, that's not good at all."

### Aldric

Inside, it was always dusk.

That was how Aldric thought of it—not a cell, not a dark, but a perpetual evening in a country with no name, where he wandered halls built from his own memories while something vast and patient wore his body upstairs. Three years of dusk. Three years of watching his own hands sign decrees he hated, his own mouth smile at men he knew were monsters, his own face—no, *its* face, the face that never quite matched itself—staring down from murals in every city square.

But tonight the dusk was flickering.

He felt it first as cold, real cold, the first true sensation in months, and then as pain in his wrists and shoulders that was *his* pain, borrowed back for a moment, and he understood: the thing was busy elsewhere. It was pouring itself into a new vessel, a new binding, and while it poured, its grip went slack.

*Aldric.*

His wife's voice. Dead eleven years, but in here, memory had a pulse. *Aldric, get up.*

He ran—not walked, *ran*—through the dusk-halls toward the surface, the way he had learned to do in the rare minutes when the leash loosened. Ninety seconds in the garden once. Thirty seconds over breakfast another time, enough to mouth *help me* at a knight he'd thought dead. Each time it cost him days afterward, crushed flat under the weight of the Crowned settling back into place.

He didn't care. He ran.

He surfaced like a drowning man breaking water, and for one staggering instant he *was* King Aldric IV, in his own throne room, feeling the cold marble through his boots and the ache of old rope-burns on his wrists that were not his rope-burns, seeing the gray circle and the woman hanging in its center and the sigils blazing on the floor—

—and seeing, reflected in the dark glass of the eastern window, the door behind the throne standing open, and figures slipping through it.

One of them moved like Sef Corvane.

*Sef,* he thought, and the name was a wound and a prayer. *Sef, you finally came. Three years late, you impossible, cowardly, loyal fool—*

Then the cold closed over him again, and he sank.

But not all the way. Not tonight. Tonight, somewhere far below the dusk, Aldric pressed his palms against the underside of his own mind and pushed, and pushed, and refused—absolutely, royally, with every scrap of the man he had been before—to go quietly.

If this was the last night of the kingdom, the king would be awake for it.

### Wren

The stair went down and then up, which Wren thought was very rude of stairs.

She held the sad sword's cloak with both fists now, because he had started moving fast, and fast people sometimes forget small people. Behind them the ink lady climbed with one hand pressed to her wrist, where the light was getting stronger. It wasn't scary light. Wren had decided that. It was candle-light, hearth-light, the kind of light that meant someone was home.

"The traps," the sad sword was saying. "Paloma, the traps are keyed to names. When we reach the antechamber, whatever happens—don't answer anything that calls you."

"Calls me?"

"They'll ask. The vault asks. It asked me, three years ago." His jaw did the tight thing. "It will use your name. Your real name. Don't answer."

Wren filed this away with all the other grown-up rules, which mostly seemed to be *don't* things, and thought about how glad she was to have no name at all. The gray men in Merrow's Cross had taken hers, or her mother had given it away, or it had dissolved somehow—she couldn't remember, remembering hurt—but the result was that Wren was empty where names went, and empty things slipped through locked doors like wind.

The ink lady had explained it once on the barge, kneeling down to Wren's level with her ink-stained fingers: *"Nothing can hold you, little wren, because you were never written down."*

At first Wren had cried about it. Being unwritten sounded lonely.

Now, climbing through the dark beneath a palace full of sleeping traps, being unwritten felt like being a secret. And secrets got to go everywhere.

"Light ahead," the sad sword breathed.

There was. A thin gold line under a door of black stone, and beyond it a humming, and beneath the humming—so faint Wren almost missed it—a sound she knew. Someone breathing hard. Someone alive.

"Vesha," the ink lady whispered.

They pushed the door open together, all three of them, and looked down into the burning circle, and Wren saw the hanging woman lift her head, and saw her face do something complicated—hope and horror fighting for the same ground.

"*No,*" Vesha mouthed. "*Go back.*"

Above, through windows tall as trees, thirteen moons stood in a perfect ring, and the smallest one began, very gently, to shine.

* * *

### Paloma

Later—if there was a later—Paloma would remember the throne room in pieces, the way scribes remember pages: fragments, margins, notes in the gaps.

The circle of gray pilgrims, thirty of them, motionless as statuary. Vesha suspended at its heart, silver-haired and defiant, blood on her lip and fury in her eyes. The sigils on the floor burning a color Paloma had no word for—not red, not gold, but the color of a page being erased.

And the hum. The terrible, beautiful hum, rising out of the stones themselves, out of the moons, out of her own blood, all three singing the same note: *too late, too late, too late.*

"Sef," she said. "Tell me you have a plan."

"I have a sword and a staircase."

"That's not a plan."

"It's the only thing I've ever had that worked."

The nearest pilgrim turned. Then the next. Then all of them, in perfect unison, and the room filled with that layered voice she had heard once in a dream, offering her a clean slate, a debtless life, a self with no suffering stitched to it.

*"Welcome, bearer,"* said the Crowned, through thirty mouths. *"Welcome to your coronation."*

Paloma's wrist blazed. The sigil under her skin was no longer a mark; it was a map, a key, a sentence waiting for its final word—and she understood, suddenly and completely, the way one understands falling, that the drafts were gone, that Vesha's research was captured, that the clause about names freely given was locked in a vault across the city, that every preparation they had made lay in ruins at the salt gate.

Everything they had was in this room. Her blood. Sef's sword. Wren's hand in hers, small and warm and utterly, miraculously unwritten.

And ninety seconds of a king, surfacing somewhere behind those distant eyes, mouthing words she could not yet read.

The thirteenth moon reached the center of the sky.

The second binding began.
