## 36. Sigil Written in Willing Blood

The doors of the throne room were taller than the temple at Vael Hollow, and older than any debt Paloma had ever owed.

They stood open, which was the worst part. She had imagined forcing them, breaking them, bleeding on them—some grand resistance from a palace that did not want her there. Instead the great bronze leaves had swung wide at their approach like a host greeting expected guests, and beyond them the throne room blazed with candlelight and cold silver, and the Crowned sat upon the throne wearing King Aldric's face like a well-loved coat.

"Come in," it said warmly, in a voice that had once commanded armies and now commanded only her fear. "I've been expecting you since before you were born."

Paloma's legs nearly failed her on the first step. It was Sef who steadied her—not with his hands, which were full of sword, but by simply being beside her, close enough that his shoulder brushed hers, an old habit of the road that had become, somewhere along five hundred miles of salt and river and running, something she leaned on without thinking.

"Don't listen to it," he murmured.

"I'm not listening."

"You're listening."

"I'm *hearing*," she said, through her teeth. "There's a difference. I'm a scribe. I know the difference between hearing and listening. One is what sound does to you. The other is what you do to it."

Something that might have been a laugh escaped him—brief, terrified, real—and then the doors closed behind them with a sound like a book shutting, and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

The throne room was wrong in ways she could only half name.

It was beautiful, first of all—vaulted ceiling painted with constellations, banners of gold and white, the whole gilded splendor of Aurelholm concentrated into one room designed to make every person entering feel small. Paloma had felt small her entire life. Ledgers did that to you. Debts did that to you. But this was different: this room had been built to make her feel small, deliberately, architecturally, and beneath its beauty ran a current of something hungry.

And the court—the pale, silent court. They lined the walls in rows, nobles and guards and servants, dozens of them, standing perfectly still. Their eyes were open. Their chests moved. But no one blinked, no one shifted, no one so much as swallowed. Vesha had told them about the Court of the Kept—the people whose names the Crowned held as surety, bodies walking around with nothing behind the eyes but obedience and a faint, terrible patience.

"They're alive," Wren whispered at her hip. The child's hand had found Paloma's sleeve without asking permission, the way she always did when frightened, gripping fabric instead of flesh because gripping flesh meant admitting you needed someone. "All of them. I can hear them breathing. Like sleeping, except standing up."

"I know, little wren."

"Are we going to wake them up?"

"That's the plan."

"What if the plan is bad?"

"Then," said Paloma, "we'll need a better one, and fortunately I have ink."

Wren looked up at her with those enormous serious eyes, and for one absurd heartbeat, in the middle of everything, the child almost smiled. Then her gaze slid past Paloma to the throne and went flat with animal fear, and her hand tightened on the sleeve.

Because the Crowned had risen.

King Aldric's body descended the three steps from the throne with unhurried grace, robes whispering against marble, and up close the wrongness was worse. The face was handsome. The face was kind. The face was arranged into expressions the way a scribe arranges letters—correctly, legibly, without meaning a single one of them.

"Paloma Hartwell," the Crowned said, and her own name in its mouth felt like fingers inside her chest, sorting through her ribs. "Do you know how long I have waited for someone to come? Three years, this body has sat in my chair, and in all that time not one soul has climbed down to ask why. They bring petitions. They bring taxes. They bring complaints about grain prices." A smile, perfectly placed. "You brought yourself. Do you know how rare that is? How *precious*?"

"It's come up," Paloma said.

Her voice didn't shake. That surprised her. Somewhere in the last weeks—on the salt flats, on the barge, in Vesha's vault of condemned things—her voice had learned not to shake, and apparently the lesson had stuck even here.

"The map found you," the Crowned continued, circling her slowly, courtiers' eyes tracking it in perfect unison, a field of heads turning like wheat. "Or perhaps you found the map. The distinction stopped mattering generations ago. You are the last page of a book I began writing long before your birth, little scribe, and I confess—" it spread Aldric's hands, "—I am fond of how you turned out. Stubborn. Careful. So many debts, and still you kept writing. I offered you freedom, remember. In your dreams. A life with nothing owed. And here you are anyway, climbing my stairs, carrying your pain like a lantern."

"I thought about your offer," Paloma admitted.

"And?"

"And I decided I'd rather keep my suffering than give up the parts of me that earned it." She shrugged. "Also your dream-messenger snored. It was very off-putting."

Sef made a strangled noise beside her that he converted, barely, into a cough.

The Crowned regarded her for a long moment. When it spoke again, the warmth had drained out of Aldric's voice, leaving something vast and cold underneath, the way a lake looks friendly until you see what lives at the bottom.

"Tonight the thirteenth moon turns," it said. "Tonight I complete what I began. This body has served, but a garment wears out, and I have grown tired of wearing one man. Tonight I bind myself into the world itself, anchored through the archivist's blood, and after tonight there will be no unbinding, no sigil, no last page—only me, everywhere, forever, and none of you burdened with being anyone at all." It tilted its head. "Unless you stop me. Which brings us to the unpleasant part."

It snapped its fingers—or rather, Aldric's fingers—and the Court of the Kept moved.

* * *

Later—much later, in another life, in whatever life came next—Paloma would try to remember exactly how the fighting started, and find that memory had blurred it into shapes and sounds: steel ringing on marble, the terrible synchronized shuffle of forty kept courtiers moving as one creature, Wren's small body darting low along the wall like a mouse along a wainscot.

But some things memory kept sharp.

Sef stepping in front of her, sword drawn, planting himself between her and the wave of blank-eyed bodies the way he had planted himself between her and debt-collectors, and river-pirates, and every other thing the road had thrown at them. "Go!" he shouted, and his voice cracked on it, the knight's composure finally burning off and the man underneath showing through—scared, stubborn, loyal past all sense. "Paloma, *go*, I'll hold them—"

"You can't hold forty!"

"I don't have to hold forty! I have to hold them for as long as it takes you to do the thing!" He parried a guard's blade, twisted, shoved the man back—not killing; even now, even here, he was pulling his strokes, fighting to disarm and disable, because Sef Corvane had spent three years deciding what kind of man he wanted to be when he grew back into one. "I'm very good at buying time! It's practically my entire skill set!"

"He is correct," said the Crowned, watching with academic interest. "He was always better at holding lines than winning battles. Why do you think I chose him, three years ago, to stand guard while the magus worked?"

Sef flinched. Paloma saw it land—the old wound torn open at the worst possible moment—but he did not fall, did not falter, met the next blade with a grunt and a counterstroke, and shouted over his shoulder:

"He lies! That's all he does! Don't listen—*do the thing!*"

And Paloma turned toward the throne, because doing the thing was all she had left.

* * *

She had rehearsed this a hundred times in Vesha's vault. She had written the counter-sigil on parchment until her wrist ached, memorized its grammar, its turns, its terrible arithmetic—a name freely given, weighed against a binding unwillingly made. She knew it the way she knew her letters. She knew it better than her letters.

What she had not rehearsed was the anchor.

Vesha hung above the dais in a cage of pale light, suspended like a moth in amber, and her eyes were open, and they found Paloma's, and they were furious.

*"Took you long enough,"* the archivist mouthed.

"I got lost," Paloma mouthed back. "Big palace."

*"Excuses. Left side. There's a catch in the lattice—I can feel it—third node from the top. Break the third node and I drop free."*

"How do I break a node?"

*"You're a scribe, girl. Everything is text. Strike through it."*

Of course. Of course it was that simple and that impossible. The binding-lattice was language made light, and Paloma had spent her whole life learning that language could be edited. She pulled the stylus from her sleeve—black iron, plain, the cheapest tool the Silent Temple had ever issued her, the one object she had carried across five hundred miles because it was the only thing in her possession that was entirely, uncomplicatedly hers—and she reached up into the light-cage's edge where it came nearest the wall, and she struck through the third node the way she had struck through ten thousand ledger errors, one clean decisive line.

The node shrieked. Light scattered. Vesha dropped three feet, caught herself, dropped again, hit the marble rolling, and came up swearing with a fluency that would have gotten her exiled from three academies.

"Wren!" Paloma called. "Help her clear the floor!"

"On it!" The child materialized out of nowhere, seized Vesha's hand, and hauled her toward the shadow of the pillar where the fighting hadn't reached yet. For a moment the two of them crouched together—old archivist, small thief—checking each other over with brisk hands, and then Vesha pressed something into Wren's palm, whispered something fast, and Wren nodded and began dragging a fallen shield toward the center of the room.

Toward Paloma.

No. Toward the space in front of the throne. Where the original binding lived.

* * *

Here is what nobody had told her, because nobody knew: the final page of the sigil was not written in the vaults below. It was written in the air above the throne, hanging in the dark like a constellation no astronomer had charted, and now that Paloma looked for it—now that she was, herself, the lens it had been ground for—she could read it.

It was elegant. That was the horror of it. The Crowned had bound itself into King Aldric with a structure of such beauty that Paloma's scribe-heart ached to look at it, clause nested in clause, anchor folded into anchor, a sentence three years long and still unbroken. Whoever had helped draft it had been brilliant.

Sef, she thought, ducking instinctively as a sword sang past her ear, answered by the ring of Sef's parry. Someone brilliant had helped. Someone who had believed he was saving a king.

"No," said the Crowned, suddenly right behind her—it moved too quietly for a king, for a man, for anything wearing a body. Its hand closed near her shoulder, not quite touching. "Don't read it. Reading leads to editing. Editing leads to *presumption*."

"It's a good binding," Paloma said, still reading. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, calm, professional, the voice she used for difficult ledgers. "Genuinely. Whoever wrote it understood the grammar. But there's a flaw, and you know there's a flaw, because you've spent three years hunting every bearer who could exploit it. You killed the stranger who gave me the map. You sold my name in the Undermarket before I even knew I had one worth stealing. You're afraid of me."

"I am *interested* in you."

"You're afraid of me," Paloma repeated, "and you should be, because I've found the flaw, and the flaw is *me*."

She turned to face it. Up close, Aldric's eyes were not eyes at all but windows, and behind the glass something enormous pressed its face, something that had been alone so long it had forgotten the difference between company and consumption.

"Your binding requires a living bearer," she said. "A key in the lock, forever, generation to generation, because you couldn't trust the world not to change the wording. But a bearer isn't just a key. A bearer is a *hand*. And a hand can turn the lock either way."

The Crowned went very still.

"You cannot," it said. "You lack the weight. Unbinding requires payment. A life. Your life, little scribe—that is the price, and we both know you've never once in your indentured, indebted, borrowed life paid your debts in full—"

"She doesn't have to."

Vesha's voice, ragged, from the pillar. The old woman was leaning against the stone, gray-faced, one arm around Wren, and she was smiling the fierce smile of someone who has spent a lifetime hoarding knowledge waiting for the exact right moment to spend it.

"Clause nine, you magnificent parasite. *A name freely given may be substituted for a life.* I told her weeks ago. I've been *waiting* to tell her again in front of you, because I wanted to watch your face." Vesha spat on the marble. "You should have erased mine when you had the chance."

The Crowned looked at Paloma.

And Paloma looked at the sigil hanging in the air above the throne, and understood, finally and completely, what she was about to do.

* * *

A name is a strange thing to hold in your mouth when you know it's the last time.

*Paloma.*

Her mother had chosen it. She had never known her mother, had only the word itself, inherited like a debt or a keepsake—*paloma*, dove, a bird of peace given to a girl raised in a valley of silence. She had resented it, sometimes. She had worn it out of duty, the way she wore everything. And now, standing in a stolen king's throne room with a war crashing around her, she discovered she loved it—loved every syllable, loved the small clumsy weight of it, loved the girl it named, the girl who copied ledgers by candlelight and broke rules for dying strangers and fled across salt flats with nothing but a map written into her skin and somehow, somehow, became the most dangerous page in the kingdom.

She would not remember this. She understood that. The name was the payment, and the payment was the whole of it—all of it, every memory tied to every moment the name had touched, which was all of them. She was spending herself. Not dying—worse, or better, or both: continuing without continuity, becoming a stranger in her own surviving body.

Wren would remember for her. Wren, who kept every story. That was why Vesha had sent the child to the front of the room. Not to fight. To *witness*.

"Paloma!" Sef's voice, raw, from the melee—he had taken a cut, she could hear it in the wet edge of his breathing, and he was still standing, still holding forty kept courtiers at bay with nothing but a disgraced knight's stubbornness. He couldn't see her face. Maybe that was mercy. "Whatever you're about to do—*do it!*"

The Crowned lunged.

Paloma spoke her true name into the space between them.

"*Paloma Hartwell.*"

The word went out of her like blood from a wound. She felt it leave—not the sound, the *substance*, the thread of self running from her chest into the air, and the sigil above the throne caught it the way dry ground catches rain, drinking, opening, every clause of the three-year binding suddenly lit and legible and *editable*.

And she rewrote it aloud.

She struck the anchor clauses first—one clean decisive line, strike through, as Vesha had taught her. The lattice screamed. The Court of the Kept shuddered as one body. She rewrote the bearer-clause, striking her own designation, *key*, replacing it with *free*. She rewrote the term of service from *forever* to *ended*. Clause by clause, line by line, in a voice that grew rougher and less certain as the name bled out of her and she forgot, gently, progressively, why she was speaking—only that the words mattered, only that her hands remembered the grammar even as her heart forgot the reason—

"*Unbound,*" she finished. "*Released. Free.*"

The Crowned shrieked.

Not with Aldric's voice—with its own, if it had ever had one, a sound like a library burning, like every erased name crying out at once. It tore backward from her, out of the king, out of the body it had worn for three years, and stood for one impossible moment in the air above the throne: a shape like a crown with nothing beneath it, a form made entirely of titles, and having no title left to be, it had nothing left at all.

Nameless, it unraveled.

It went the way ink goes into water, gracefully, helplessly, threads of darkness loosening and dissolving, and in its last instant of coherence Paloma—already forgetting why—felt something almost like pity reach back at her from it. *So that's what it is to be no one,* the unraveling seemed to say. *How do any of you bear it?*

Then it was gone, and King Aldric fell.

* * *

He fell like a man, not a king—crumpling down the throne steps in an untidy heap of gold robes, and the Court of the Kept collapsed with him, dozens of bodies dropping to the marble all at once like puppets with cut strings, and then, one by one, beginning to stir, to groan, to blink at the ceiling and whisper their own names in wonder, testing them like coins bitten to check for counterfeit.

Sef caught the king before his head struck stone. Of course he did. Even bleeding, even sobbing—she could hear him sobbing, great ugly gulps of it—even hollowed out by three years of guilt, the man's arms moved on their own toward anyone falling.

"Your Majesty. Your Majesty, it's Sef—Sef Corvane—you're free, it's done, you're—"

"Sef." The king's voice was rust and gravel, unused. His hand rose, shaking, and gripped the knight's forearm. "I remember. Caelmoor. I remember *everything*, and I remember you carrying me afterward, and I have waited three years to say this: you were lied to, boy. As was I. Whatever you've been punishing yourself for—" Aldric coughed, laughed, wept, all at once. "We will speak of it. At length. With wine. But not—" his gaze drifted across the chaos, the stirring courtiers, the scattered light of a broken binding, "—not yet. Where is she? The girl. The one who paid."

Sef turned.

Paloma Hartwell was standing at the foot of the dais, stylus still in her hand, looking at the room with the polite bafflement of a traveler arrived somewhere unexpected. She looked at the fallen king. She looked at Sef, kneeling in his own blood, and tilted her head with faint, distant curiosity, the way one looks at a painting of someone one almost remembers.

"Hello," she said. "I'm sorry—do I know you? I seem to have..." She frowned, patting her own pockets, her sleeves, as though the missing thing might be on her person after all. "I seem to have forgotten something important."

At the base of the pillar, small and silent, Wren clutched the shield in her lap with both arms, and began—very quietly, the way children cry when they have learned crying must be economical—to weep, and to remember everything.
