## 37. Who Was Paloma Hartwell?

The bells did not stop for three days.

Sef counted them the way he had once counted watch rotations—out of habit, out of a soldier's need to measure time in something other than dread. The great bells of Aurelholm rang from dawn to dusk and sometimes past it, and nobody ordered them silenced, because nobody could quite believe they were allowed to stop. A kingdom waking up after three years of sleepwalking apparently needed to make noise about it.

He heard them through the window of a physician's house on Candlemaker's Row, which was not where he expected to be sitting vigil over anyone, least of all a woman who had unmade an ancient god-thing with nothing but her own voice and her own name.

"Stop staring at me," the woman said.

Sef blinked. "I wasn't."

"You were. You've been staring at me since yesterday. It's very rude." She turned another page of the book in her lap—a book of shipping manifests she'd pulled from the physician's shelf because, she said, everything else was poetry, and poetry was lies with rhythm. "Do I know you? You keep looking at me like I owe you money."

"You don't owe me anything," Sef said.

"Everyone owes someone something." She said it lightly, without bitterness, as though quoting a proverb she'd found somewhere and liked the shape of. Then she frowned at the page. "That was a strange thing to say. I don't think that's mine. That sounds like someone else's saying."

His chest did something painful. He kept his face still. Three years of practice at keeping his face still.

* * *

They had carried her out of the throne room herself.

That was the part the ballads would get wrong, Sef thought—not that there would be ballads, though there were already two, and one of them gave him a singing voice he had never possessed and hair down to his waist. The ballads would say the hero collapsed dramatically into the knight's arms while the palace crumbled around them. In truth, the palace had not crumbled at all. The Crowned had come apart like smoke deciding it no longer wished to be shaped like anything, and the stones hadn't even shivered. And Paloma Hartwell had walked—walked, on her own feet, steady as a temple scribe crossing a courtyard—until she reached the foot of the dais, looked up at King Aldric standing free for the first time in three years, and said:

"I'm sorry. Have we met?"

And then her knees folded, and *that* was when Sef caught her, so technically the ballad was half right, but only about the falling.

The physicians called it spent-name sickness. Vesha—the real Vesha, recovered from her cell with bruises on her wrists and murder in her eyes for whoever had sold them out, currently conducting a one-woman purge of the archive guilds—called it what it was. "She paid," Vesha had said flatly, standing over Paloma's sickbed on the first night. "Everything she was, into the ledger, to balance the Crowned against the world. The sigil doesn't take lives, Corvane. It takes weight. And nothing weighs more than a self."

"Will it come back?" Wren had asked. Wren, who should have been asleep hours ago, sitting cross-legged on the floor with her arm bandaged where the salt-gate ambush had caught her, refusing to leave.

Vesha had been quiet a long moment. "Names can be given back," she said finally. "By witness. By record. By love, if you want to use the vulgar word for it. But it won't be the same name. It will be a new one wearing the old stories. Whether that's a tragedy or a mercy—" she had shrugged, and it had not looked like indifference; it had looked like a woman carrying more grief than her shoulders were rated for, "—depends on the teller."

* * *

On the second day, King Aldric came.

Not in procession—that was the remarkable thing. Sef heard the commotion on the stairs and braced for courtiers, heralds, the machinery of a restored monarchy grinding into motion, but the man who came through the door wore a plain gray coat with a missing button and looked like he hadn't slept since the reign of his own usurper, which, in fairness, he hadn't.

"Sef Corvane," Aldric said.

"Your Majesty." Sef started to rise, started to kneel, got tangled somewhere between the two and ended in an undignified half-crouch that made the king wave impatiently at him.

"Sit down before you hurt yourself. Where is she?"

"Asleep, mostly. Awake in stretches. She doesn't—" Sef stopped. Started again. "She remembers the mission. She remembers Vesha's vault, the counter-sigil drafts, the climb beneath the throne. She remembers all of it the way you remember a battle you fought in armor: every move, none of the feeling. What she doesn't remember is herself. Her mother. Vael Hollow. The debt. Me." His voice cracked on the last word, barely, and he hated it. "Us."

Aldric pulled up the room's only other chair and sat heavily, elbows on knees, a king studying a problem the crown had never trained him for. Up close, Sef could see what three years of captivity had left behind—the hollows under the eyes, the tremor in the right hand that he kept closing into a fist when he noticed it moving. But the eyes were his own. That was the miracle everyone kept forgetting to be astonished by. The eyes were *his*.

"Ninety seconds," Aldric said quietly. "Every few months, she'd lose her grip for ninety seconds, and I'd get my body back long enough to scream into a pillow or steal a walk in the garden. Do you know what I did with those ninety seconds? I memorized things. The grain of the banister. The smell of the hedge roses. My own handwriting, because I was afraid I'd forget it." He laughed, short and terrible. "And now I have three years of memories that aren't mine, and everyone wants their king back, and I keep thinking: the woman who bought me out of that—she doesn't even get to remember doing it."

"She knew the price," Sef said. "There was a clause. She could have substituted a freely given name instead of her own. Anyone's. She chose hers."

"Why?"

"Because taking someone else's would have made her the same kind of thief as the thing she killed." Sef heard his own voice go rough. "She told me that once. Not in those words. But that was always the shape of her. Every rule she broke, she broke cleanly."

Aldric studied him for a long moment. "You loved her."

"That's not—" Sef began, and then, because the last month had stripped him of every comfortable lie he owned, finished honestly: "I don't know if I loved her. I lied to her for most of the road. I helped bind you. I ran for three years. She forgave me—or decided to work with me, which may be harder—and then she spent the only thing she had left, and now she looks at me like I'm furniture with opinions." He exhaled. "So I'm going to sit here until she remembers, or until she decides she likes the furniture well enough to keep it. Whichever comes."

"Hm," said the King of Vail-and-the-Nine-Marches, in exactly the tone of a man being handed a problem he intended to solve with paperwork. "Then I'll tell you what I'm going to do. Tomorrow I dissolve the Court of Bonds and Ledgers. All of it. Debt-bondage ends by decree—first decree of the restoration. No one else spends ten years copying ledgers to buy back their grandmother's funeral costs. No one else gets sold to a syndicate over a map." His jaw tightened. "Her file crossed my desk once, you know. Three years ago. A scribe petitioned for relief of bond, denied by the auditor on grounds of 'insufficient contrition.' I signed the denial. Or—the Crowned signed it, wearing my hand, but it used my seal and my authority, and I've decided those are the same crime. So yes. Justice outside the old courts. Starting with hers."

"The auditor," Sef said. "Merrick. He fled the city during the uprising."

"He fled to a fishing village two days north, where he is currently pretending to be a man named Gulliver Pemm." Aldric's smile was thin and entirely human and slightly terrifying. "My new marshal found him in an afternoon. The old courts took three years to notice the throne was occupied by something with too many teeth. The new courts will be faster."

* * *

Paloma woke near dusk, and this time she didn't reach for the shipping manifests. She lay still, watching the ceiling, and when she spoke her voice had the careful quality of someone testing ice.

"I dreamed about a valley," she said. "Salt in the air, but not sea salt. Stone salt. And candles everywhere—thousands, and nobody talking. There was a rule about talking. I knew the rule. I could feel how much I hated the rule." She turned her head toward him. "Was that mine? Was that a place I lived?"

"Yes." Sef set down the whetstone he'd been pretending to need. "Vael Hollow. The Silent Temple. You were a scribe. The best copyist they'd seen in a generation, according to a very drunk ferryman I once interrogated."

"A scribe." She flexed her fingers, looking at them as though they might confess something. "My hands do want to hold a pen. They're very insistent about it. It's the strangest feeling—knowing a skill and not knowing owning it." She paused. "Did I have family? A mother? A husband? Someone who'll be looking for me?"

"No one living. Your mother died when you were young. The funeral debt was why you were bonded." He delivered it gently, factually, the way she seemed to prefer facts—as though facts were stones she could rebuild with. "No husband. You were engaged to the work, mostly. And to arguing with your auditor."

"I feel like I would have argued with an auditor," she said, with such immediate conviction that Sef laughed—an unexpected sound, rusty, startling them both.

"There it is," he said. "There's a piece of you. You once told a syndicate enforcer that his arithmetic was wrong and then corrected it for him, mid-threat. It confused him enough that we escaped."

"I did *not*."

"You absolutely did. I was there. Well—I was there from the second day onward. Before that you fled alone across the salt-flats with a stolen map and no boots worth the name."

She was quiet. Outside, the bells had finally, mercifully stopped, and the evening filled up instead with ordinary city noise: carts, gulls, a neighbor's argument about a ladder.

"Sef," she said.

"You know my name?"

"It came back this morning. Like a stone that had been sitting in my shoe." She sat up, wincing, pushing hair out of her face. "Here's what I want to understand. Everyone who comes to this room looks at me like I'm a funeral. The little girl—Wren—cries when she thinks I'm asleep and then pretends to be cheerful when I'm not, badly, like a child pretending. The archivist woman talks to me in a tone people usually save for graves. The king visited and looked at me like I was a war memorial. And you sit here sharpening a sword that hasn't needed sharpening since Tuesday, watching me like I might evaporate." Her chin lifted, and something in the gesture was so precisely, recognizably *her* that Sef's throat closed. "So tell me plainly. What did I do, and what did it cost, and don't soften it, because I can tell when people are softening things. I could always tell. I'm almost sure I could always tell."

* * *

So he told her.

All of it. The stranger dying at the gate with her name already in his mouth. The map that turned out to be written in her blood. The salt road, and his own interception of her, and the lies they traded like currency because neither of them could afford the truth. Merrow's Cross, where names had been peeled off people like bark. The astronomer with the clock made of moons. The barge, and Wren, and Thornbridge, and her forged papers that saved them where his sword failed. Caelmoor—he made himself say it, the binding night, his hand on the ritual circle, his belief that he was saving the king, his three years of running—and he watched her take that in without flinching, watched her weigh it with those quick ledger-keeper's eyes, and whatever verdict she reached she kept behind her teeth for later, which was also, he suspected, exactly what the old Paloma would have done.

And then the end. The throne room. Vesha freed, Wren's small fierce hands working at the shackles, Sef holding the doorway against agents of the Pale Court with a sword and three years of guilt finally burned as fuel. And Paloma at the center of it, speaking her own true name aloud—*Paloma Hartwell, born of Vael Hollow, debtor, daughter, scribe*—and rewriting the binding in the grammar she'd taught herself from stolen pages on a river barge, each clause of it priced in pieces of herself, paying and paying and paying, until the Crowned, which had worn kings like coats and unmade thousands of names, found itself holding nothing of hers left to grip. Until it unraveled, because it had no name of its own, and a thing with no name cannot survive being refused.

"And then you asked if we'd met," Sef finished, "and fell over, and I caught you, and the ballads are going to make it far more romantic than it was, because I was crying, and ballads hate crying knights."

Silence. She looked at her hands again.

"So I'm the price," she said at last. "The whole bill came due, and I paid it, and the kingdom got its king, and I got—" she gestured vaguely at herself, at the room, at the enormous blankness where a life had been, "—this. Shipping manifests."

"You got freedom," Sef said. "Actual freedom. No debt, no bond, no auditor. You're the only person in nine kingdoms with a completely clean ledger."

"How grimly funny of fate." But her mouth twitched. Then it steadied, and she looked at him with that unnerving directness. "You said you helped bind the king. Before. Why are you still here? Why does the king let you sit at my bedside? Men who do what you did usually end up on scaffolds or in monasteries."

"Because I confessed, and she—" he nodded at the empty chair where Wren had fallen asleep an hour ago, bandaged arm and all, "—decided loyalty weighed more than truth. And because the king, in his first lucid hour, remembered that I tried to stop it afterward. Tried, failed, ran. Apparently trying and failing counts for something when the alternative was succeeding and staying." He rubbed his jaw. "Also he needs people the old courts didn't own. Turns out that list is very short and includes disgraced knights and nameless scribes."

"Hm." She filed that away; he could see her filing it. "And what am I to you? Exactly? Since I clearly mattered enough to sharpen swords at."

Sef considered lying. It was his oldest skill. Then he considered that she had just unmade a god with the truth, and lying to her felt like bringing a knife to a comet fight.

"You were the first person in three years who looked at me and saw something worth lying *to*," he said. "People either pitied me or hunted me. You negotiated. You were infuriating. You copied sigils in secret on a barge because you couldn't stand not understanding things. You named a child, fed an archivist, and threatened a god's agent with correct arithmetic. And when you had a way out—when it offered to erase your debts, your whole past, everything that hurt—you looked at what it was selling and said no." His voice went low. "So I don't know what you are to me. But I know who you were. And I intend to introduce you to her properly. All forty chapters' worth."

"Forty?"

"Give or take. Wren's been keeping count. And keeping everything else—every story, every detail. The child has a memory like a courthouse." He hesitated, then said the thing he'd been circling for three days. "Tomorrow, if you're strong enough, Wren wants to start telling you who you were. From the beginning. She's been rehearsing. She has *notes*, Paloma. Actual notes, in charcoal, on a slate. She's very serious about it."

Paloma was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone strange and bright.

"A valley full of candles and silence," she murmured. "A mother I paid for with ten years of ink. A map in my blood. A knight who cries. A child with a slate." She pressed her palm flat over her sternum, as if checking whether anything echoed. "None of it feels like memory. But it feels like... homework. Like a life I've been assigned, and I haven't read the text yet."

"That's more than yesterday," Sef said.

"Yes." She looked toward the window, where the first stars were coming out over a capital busy with the loud, unbelievable business of being free. "It is. And tomorrow there's a girl with notes, and a king abolishing ledgers, and—" she paused, and the ghost of the old dry humor surfaced, unmistakable, incorrigible, hers, "—apparently a ballad in which you have beautiful hair that I simply must hear performed, for science."

"It's a terrible ballad."

"Then I'll write a better one. I have it on good authority these hands know how to hold a pen."

Outside, the bells began again—one soft, single toll, as if the city itself had decided that some silences deserved marking. And in the pause between that toll and whatever came next, Sef Corvane, knight unmade and remade, keeper of a stranger's vigil, finally let himself hope that the next chapter would not be a eulogy—

—because down in the courtyard, faintly, he could hear Wren's voice, already beginning: *"Once upon a time, there was an ink lady..."*
