## 38. Debts Outstanding

The morning after the world was saved, Paloma Hartwell woke in a bed she did not recognize and did not know her own name.

She knew this the way one knows rain is coming—not as a shock but as a weather report of the self. She lay still under a quilt that smelled of cedar and old paper, watching dust motes climb through a shaft of light, and performed the small inventory that had become her morning ritual these past three days: I am alive. I have hands. My hands know how to hold a pen. Everything else is negotiable.

Somewhere below, someone was frying onions, and someone smaller was narrating the process with great authority.

"—and then you turn them *over*, Vesha, because otherwise they burn on one side and stay sad on the other—"

"They're fine, Wren."

"They're *burning*."

"They are *caramelizing*."

Paloma smiled at the ceiling. She did not remember learning to smile, exactly—or rather, she remembered smiling the way one remembers a story heard secondhand—but the smile came anyway, unbidden, aimed at nothing, and it felt like a room in a house she'd never seen but somehow owned.

That was the shape of it now. All the furniture gone from the house of herself, but the rooms remained, and her feet knew where the floorboards creaked.

She got up. The dress someone had left for her—a plain grey thing, temple-cut, which Sef must have fetched because he was the sort of man who noticed such things and said nothing about noticing—fit well enough. She went down the narrow stairs of Vesha's archive-house, past shelves of condemned books stacked like sleeping animals, following the smell of onions and the argument about their condition.

The kitchen was small and crowded and full of morning. Vesha stood at the stove looking like a scholar betrayed by breakfast. Wren sat cross-legged on the counter itself, which no one had the energy to forbid anymore, swinging her feet. And Sef Corvane stood by the window in a clean shirt, holding a cup of tea he had clearly forgotten to drink, watching the street below with the vigilance of a man who had not yet told his body the war was over.

He saw her first. Something moved through his face—the thing it always did now, grief and relief fighting to a draw—and then he stepped aside from the window and said, "Morning," as if it were any morning at all, which was its own kindness.

"Morning," she said. "Your tea has died of neglect."

He looked at the cup, surprised, as though the tea had acted alone. "So it has."

"There's more," Wren announced. "Vesha made too much because Vesha always makes too much when she's worried, and Vesha is always worried."

"I am not worried," Vesha said, pouring a fresh cup without being asked, which proved the point better than an admission would have. "I am *contemplating*. It looks similar but costs less."

Paloma took the cup and sat, and for a while they ate eggs and onions and bread, four people in a kitchen, and none of the four could have explained to a stranger what they were to each other. Companions. Survivors. A ledger whose entries had all been torn out but whose binding still held.

It was Wren who broke the comfortable silence, because Wren always did.

"Are we going today, then? Back to the ink lady's valley?"

Paloma set down her fork. She had been circling this question for three days, walking around it the way one walks around a locked door, and here was a child of perhaps nine summers simply picking it up and setting it on the table between the plates.

"I don't—" she began, and stopped. *I don't know* was true but incomplete. "Tell me again what it's like. Vael Hollow."

Wren lit up like a lamp. "You ask me that every day."

"I know."

"And every day I tell you, and every day it's new to you." Wren counted off on her fingers, a habit she'd caught from watching Paloma copy sigils. "There's fog that comes down the mountain in the evening like it's tucking the valley in. There's a temple full of quiet people who don't talk because talking costs money there—I liked that part. There's salt-flats so flat and white they look like the sky fell down and is resting." She paused, considering. "And there's a scribe who copied ledgers every night until her candle burned to a stub, and she gave water to a dying stranger even though it was against the rules, and that's why everything happened."

Paloma looked down at her hands. They were steady. That still astonished her—that the hands that had rewritten a god out of existence now trembled only at ordinary things, like cups of tea held too long.

"That sounds like someone worth meeting," she said quietly.

Sef made a sound across the table—not quite a laugh, not quite anything else—and when she glanced at him he was studying his dead tea with tremendous concentration. He had been doing that for three days: listening to Wren tell Paloma who Paloma had been, and saying nothing, and carrying whatever he carried behind his eyes like a man hauling stones uphill in silence.

"You could come meet her," Wren said, with the terrifying directness of children. "We're going back. You could stand next to yourself."

"Wren," Sef said gently.

"What? It makes sense. If you lost your house you'd go back and look at where the house was."

"The house isn't gone," Paloma said, before she thought about it. Then she thought about it, and said it again, firmer, testing whether it held weight. "The house isn't gone. It's just empty. Empty houses can be filled again."

Vesha snorted from the stove. "Spoken like a true archivist. Most people grieve loss. You lot look at an empty shelf and think, *ah, room for acquisitions.*"

"Is that wrong?" Paloma asked.

"No," Vesha said, and something raw flickered under her dryness. "No, it's the sanest thing anyone's said in my archive in thirty years. Which is a low bar, granted. But still."

* * *

They went to the palace that afternoon, because Aldric had asked—or Aldric's letter had asked, delivered by a page who saluted Sef with the particular reverence soldiers reserve for men they've been ordered to revere and have decided to actually respect—and because some things ought to be heard standing up.

The throne room had been cleaned since the thirteenth moon. The scorch-marks were gone, the broken marble replaced, the great doors rehung. Only the crown above the throne had been removed, and in its place hung a simple banner of white cloth, blank, waiting for whatever the king would choose to put there. Paloma found she approved. Blankness, she was learning, was not emptiness. Blankness was a page.

Aldric met them in a smaller chamber off the hall—not the throne room, he'd written, *because I have spent three years being addressed in that room by something wearing me, and I find I'd like to talk to friends somewhere with chairs.*

Friends. He'd written *friends*. Sef had read that line four times and then folded the letter very carefully, as if it might bruise.

The king looked better than the last time she'd seen him—when he'd been a hollowed thing propped on a throne, or afterward, when he'd knelt in the vault antechamber begging her not to spend herself on his rescue. Now he wore plain clothes, no regalia at all, and there were lines around his eyes that spoke of sleepless nights, but his gaze was his own. When he looked at you, someone was home.

"Sef Corvane," he said, and took the knight's hand in both of his own. For a moment neither man managed words. Then Aldric said, roughly, "Three years. Every time that thing let me surface, I looked for your face in the court. Do you know how many times I saw it?"

"No," Sef said hoarsely.

"Neither do I. But I looked." He gripped harder. "And then you came up out of the dark with a scribe and a child and an archivist, and I understood that looking hadn't been wasted." He released him, cleared his throat, and turned with visible relief to the others. "Lady Paloma."

"Your Majesty." She started to curtsy; he waved it off before she'd bent two inches.

"None of that. Not from you. Not ever." He studied her with an expression she couldn't name—grief and gratitude braided together, the look of a man seeing a debt he could never repay and knowing the debtor hated debts above all things. "I'm told you don't remember our conversation in the vaults."

"Wren tells me I refused to be saved unless it cost nothing. That I said you'd watched enough people be unmade."

Aldric's jaw tightened. "You did. And then you spent yourself anyway, and I have had to live with the arithmetic of that." He gestured them all to seats, and waited until they'd taken them, and only then did his manner shift—from king among companions to king at work, the difference visible like a change in weather. "Which brings me to why I asked you here. What do you know of the decrees I signed this week?"

"Rumors," Sef said. "Mostly that the courts are terrified."

"The courts should be terrified. I spent three years watching that creature govern, and do you know the worst of it? It didn't rule badly. Efficiently. Cruelly in small ways that never reached the histories. It sold people." Aldric's voice flattened. "Debt-bondage. Indenture. It kept the temples' lending practices untouched—encouraged them, in fact. A valley full of people bound to silence and labor, generation after generation, because their grandparents borrowed grain in a bad winter. It found the whole arrangement *useful*. Souls in ledgers are souls that cannot look up."

Paloma's hands had gone still in her lap. She thought of the auditor's face, the candle burning to a stub, the decade extending like a wall rising around her life.

"So," Aldric said, "my first decree—not the hundredth, the *first*—dissolves every bond of debt-indenture in the realm. All of them. Every contract voided, every ledger closed, every debtor freed as of the seal's drying. The temples will scream. Let them. I have been screamed at by worse."

The room was quiet. In the quiet, Paloma became aware that she was crying—without drama, almost absently, tears simply falling as if some inner clerk had finally balanced an account and closed the book. Wren, sitting beside her, took her hand and held it and asked no questions, which was its own form of wisdom no adult in the room possessed.

"I'm sorry," Paloma managed. "I don't know why—"

"Yes, you do," Aldric said gently. "You just don't remember why. Those aren't the same thing." He leaned forward. "I cannot give you back your name. I have asked Vesha, I have asked every scholar who survived the Pale Court, and the answer is the same: what was freely given cannot be taken back, only newly given. But I can do this much." He drew a folded document from beside his chair. "This is a royal warrant. It names no one—which seemed fitting. It grants the bearer and her chosen company passage anywhere in the realm, lodging where needed, and protection under my personal seal. It says, in effect, that whoever holds this paper is a friend of the crown, and the crown does not forget its friends." His mouth twisted wryly. "Even when the friend forgets everything else."

Paloma took the warrant. The seal was warm red wax, pressed with a sigil she recognized now—she'd spent weeks learning the grammar of such marks, and this one read, in the old formal language, simply: *remembered*.

"Thank you," she said. It was inadequate and they both knew it, and he nodded as though she'd delivered an oration.

"One more thing." Aldric rose, and so did they. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "Corvane. The commission to reform the kingsguard goes out tomorrow. I want men who failed once and learned from it. Men who know exactly what their honor costs." A pause. "I'm told you intend to travel north instead. The offer stands regardless. It will keep."

Sef's voice, when it came, was gravel and something softer underneath. "Keep it a year, Majesty. Maybe less. There's something I have to see about in Vael Hollow first."

Aldric glanced back, one eyebrow raised, and looked from Sef to Paloma to Sef again with the swift understanding of a man who had worn a parasite for three years and therefore knew precisely what free human hearts looked like when they were busy pretending not to be entangled.

"A year," he agreed solemnly. "The paperwork will wait. Paperwork is patient. Believe me, I have developed opinions on paperwork."

* * *

They left Aurelholm three days later, on a bright cold morning that smelled of woodsmoke and river.

The city saw them off in its own indifferent way: fishwives arguing over eels, a mural painter touching up the king's official portrait—for the fourth time, according to Wren, who had appointed herself supervisor of all public works they passed—so that the face might finally match the living man. Below the gilded streets, in the low quarters, people were already telling stories about the night the pale court fell. Paloma heard fragments as they rode out: a woman with ink on her hands, a sword that remembered mercy, a child who walked through walls of names. The stories were already wrong in every detail and already truer than the details, which is how stories work.

Wren rode in front on a fat pony named Turnip, whom she had selected personally and over Vesha's objections regarding dignity. Vesha herself stayed behind—someone had to keep the archive, she said, and someone had to catalog what they'd pulled out of the Pale Court's vaults, and besides, she claimed, four days in a carriage with these three would kill her, and she refused to die before finishing the index. But at the gate she had pressed a parcel into Paloma's arms: blank books. Twelve of them. Good paper, good binding.

"For the ledger," Vesha had said gruffly. "New accounts need somewhere to live."

Now the road unwound northward, and the land began to change. The soft green country hardened into hills, the hills into moor, and the air took on a mineral sharpness that Paloma found she loved without knowing why—her body remembering what her mind could not.

On the third evening they camped in the lee of an old boundary stone, and Wren, wrapped in blankets, demanded a story. This was ritual now. Every night, a story about the ink lady. Paloma suspected the child understood exactly what she was doing: stitching a person back together out of anecdote, one seam per evening, patient as any scribe.

"Tell the one about the barge," Wren said. "When you taught yourself the sigil-language and Sef caught you and pretended he hadn't."

"Oh, I like that one," Sef said, settling against his saddle with the contentment of a man about to enjoy someone else's embarrassment. "Go on, Wren. Be thorough."

"You be quiet, sad sword, it's not your story." Wren frowned in concentration. "Right. So the ink lady used to copy things. Ledgers, mostly, boring ones, but she copied them *perfectly*, because copying perfect was the only power she had, back then. And on the barge she stole the map's pages—copied them secret-style, late at night—and she taught herself the whole grammar of binding-magic from stolen notes, which is either the bravest or the naughtiest thing anyone's ever done, probably both." Wren paused for effect; she'd learned effect from somewhere and deployed it shamelessly. "And the sad sword caught her. And he didn't yell. He just sat down next to her and said, 'If you're going to do something forbidden, at least do it where I can watch your back,' and then he stayed awake the rest of the night so she could practice."

Paloma listened to this stranger the way she'd begun to listen—as to a beloved legend, half envy, half ache. *She* had done that. That woman with her name and her nerve and her candle burned to a stub. She tried to feel like her, and couldn't, and was beginning to make peace with the fact that she wasn't supposed to. Vesha had said it best, the last night in Aurelholm: *"You're not her ghost, girl. You're her heir. Heirs don't imitate. They build."*

"And then," Wren continued, yawning hugely, "she rewrote a god. Out loud. With nobody's permission." Another yawn. "That's my favorite part. Nobody's permission."

"Go to sleep, Wren," Sef said.

"'M going. 'M going." Her eyes were already shut. "Tomorrow we see the salt-flats. Ink lady, you'll like them. They look like a page nobody's written on yet."

Paloma sat by the fire long after the child slept, turning the phrase over. A page nobody's written on yet. She looked at the mountains ahead, black against the stars, with the valley somewhere beyond them—the Silent Temple, the fog that tucked the world in, the cell where a stranger named Paloma Hartwell had spent ten years of nights copying other people's accounts by guttering light.

She didn't want to recover that woman. She wanted, she realized, something both harder and easier: to sit down at that same desk, in that same valley, open one of Vesha's blank books, and begin a new entry. Not a record of debts owed. A record of debts outstanding—the kind you carry forward not because you must but because you choose to. To Wren, for the stories. To Sef, for the watching-back. To a stranger who had given her everything and gotten, as far as Paloma could tell, exactly what she'd wanted: a world where no one else had to pay.

Beside her, Sef fed the fire another branch. He'd been quieter on the road than usual, if such a thing were possible, and tonight the quiet had edges.

"You'll tell me, eventually," Paloma said, not looking at him, "what you're not saying about going back to the valley."

The fire popped. He was silent long enough that she turned, and found him watching the flames with an expression she'd learned to read in fragments: guilt, worn smooth now, like a stone carried too many years.

"When we get there," he said. "There's a door in the temple archives I helped lock, a long time ago, before any of this. Before Caelmoor, even. It's the reason I knew the valley at all—the reason I could track the stranger there. And I think..." He exhaled. "I think whatever's behind it is the reason the valley is magic-dead in the first place. And I think your predecessor's map leads through it."

Paloma looked at him for a long moment. Then, slowly, deliberately, she reached into her pack and drew out one of Vesha's blank books, and laid it on her knee, and picked up her pen.

"Well then," she said. "Best start a fresh page."

North of them, unseen in the dark, the pass to Vael Hollow opened its stony throat, and somewhere beyond it, in a temple of silence, an old door waited to be unlocked.
