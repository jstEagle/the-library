## 39. The Valley Remembers Differently

Wren had a trick, and the trick was this: she never forgot anything anyone ever told her.

Not on purpose. She'd tried to forget some things—Merrow's Cross mostly, and the smell of the name-wagons, and the sound her mother's voice made the last time it said her name before the collectors took it—but forgetting wasn't something Wren's head knew how to do. Everything that went in stayed in, stacked up like the barge master's cargo crates: labeled, tied, waiting. The ink lady once said that was called *a memory*, and that most people lost theirs like loose coins. Wren had decided then that she was rich.

So when they came down out of the pass into Vael Hollow—the sad sword walking stiff as a fence post, the ink lady walking light as if she weighed nothing at all, which was almost true now, which was exactly true now—it was Wren who stopped on the ridge and said:

"I know this place."

The ink lady looked at her with those eyes that didn't remember anything, not even themselves. "You do?"

"From stories." Wren counted them off on her fingers, because counting helped the remembering come out orderly instead of all at once like soup spilled from a bowl. "The barge master said there's a valley where magic can't get in. He said his grandfather smuggled salt through it during the Border War because no spell could find you there. And the astronomer—he talks too much, did you notice? He talks too much—he said the Silent Temple keeps its ledgers so clean that even the gods couldn't fudge them, and that's why the gods don't visit." She grinned. "And Sef said—" she glanced up at the tall man, who was doing his best to look like he hadn't been listening, which he always was, "—Sef said somebody scribe-y lived here who wrote so small you needed a beetle's eyes to read it."

The sad sword cleared his throat. "I may have mentioned it."

"You definitely mentioned it. You mentioned it four times. Once when we were hiding under the barge and once when you thought I was asleep and twice more for no reason at all." Wren took the ink lady's hand. It was thin and warm and it flinched a little at being held, the way hands do when they've forgotten what holding is for. "Come on. If I'm going to know a place, I want to actually see it."

They went down into the valley together.

* * *

Vael Hollow in autumn smelled like woodsmoke and wet stone and the particular dusty sweetness of old paper, which Wren learned later came from the temple itself, breathing it out through every window like a sleeping animal. The priests met them at the outer gate—you could tell they were priests by their gray robes and their faces, which were arranged in expressions of profound disapproval that somehow also meant welcome, because apparently in Vael Hollow even kindness had to be done grudgingly or it didn't count.

The oldest priest looked at the ink lady for a long time. Then at the sad sword. Then at Wren.

"The debt-rolls say a Paloma Hartwell finished her bond," he said at last, in a voice like a drawer closing. "Paid in full. Unusual method of payment. Not one we're eager to have explained."

"She can't explain it," Wren said helpfully. "She spent her name. That was the payment. Didn't you read your own ledger? Honestly."

"Wren," the sad sword murmured, in the voice that meant *we are trying not to be arrested*.

But the old priest only made a sound deep in his chest, somewhere between a sigh and a laugh, and stepped aside. "The valley remembers differently than ledgers do," he said. "Come in out of the cold. All of you. Yes, even the child. Especially the child; children are the only honest witnesses left anywhere."

Wren decided she liked him enormously.

* * *

That first night they were given a cell-room with three cots and a candle that burned without flickering, because nothing in Vael Hollow flickered—no wind got in, no magic, nothing restless. Wren lay awake between the snoring of the two grown-ups (the sad sword snored like a landslide arguing with itself; the ink lady barely breathed at all) and listened to the silence of the temple, which wasn't empty the way Merrow's Cross had been silent after the name-takers came. This silence was full. It felt like a room where everyone had agreed, all at the same moment, to be quiet so that one person could think.

And Wren thought: *she doesn't know who she is. Nobody told her yet properly. They keep saying "you'll remember" and "give it time" and "the valley might bring things back," but the valley can't bring anything back, it's a valley, valleys don't talk.*

Well. Valleys didn't talk. But Wren did.

In the morning she found the ink lady in the copying hall—a long stone room where rows of desks faced windows full of gray-gold light, and scribes bent over their work like plants growing toward the sun. The ink lady stood at an empty desk with a pen in her hand, turning it over and over, and her face was doing the thing it did sometimes, the crumpling thing, like paper someone had smoothed out but the creases still showed.

"It won't work," she said when Wren climbed onto the stool beside her. Her voice was careful, like she was carrying water in both hands. "I hold the pen and my hand knows the shape of it. But when I try to write, there's nothing underneath. No—I write, and then I look at what I wrote, and it's like reading a stranger's letter. Neat handwriting. Empty words." She set the pen down very gently, the way you put down something you're afraid of breaking. "Whoever I was, she lived in there." She touched her temple. "And she's gone."

Wren swung her feet. This was the moment. She'd been saving it since the ridge, the way you save the biggest sweet in the bag for last.

"That's not true," she said.

The ink lady looked at her.

"You said I should tell you if I ever noticed anything important. You said it on the river, night four, when you were teaching me knots and you thought I wasn't listening, except I'm always listening." Wren took a breath, because this part mattered, and important things deserved breath. "You said: *a witness is worth ten memories, because a memory lives in one skull and dies there, but a witness carries it out into the world.*"

"I said that?" The ink lady's mouth did something complicated.

"Night four. You also said the knot I tied was 'an insult to rope,' which was mean but fair."

A sound came out of the ink lady that Wren recognized, because she'd heard it on the barge, and in Vesha's archive, and once through a locked door while the sad sword sat on the other side with his back against it. It was the laugh that comes out sideways when crying tries to sneak through the same door.

"So here's what I'm going to do," Wren said. "I kept everything. Every story anybody told on the road. Every word you said. I've got—" she spread her arms wide, nearly knocking over the ink pot, "—all of it. Stacked up. Labeled. So I'm going to give it back to you. One piece at a time. Not fast. Fast would be rude."

The ink lady stared at her for a long moment. Then she pulled the second stool around so they faced each other, knee to knee, the way people sit when something real is about to happen.

"Start wherever you like," she said.

Wren grinned until her face hurt.

* * *

She started, of course, with the stranger at the gate.

"Day one," Wren announced, because everything deserved a proper beginning. "Before I met you, obviously, this part is hearsay, but the sad sword heard it from the temple gatekeeper, and the gatekeeper saw it happen, so it counts double. There was a dying man at the outer gate and everybody said turn him away and one scribe broke the rules and gave him water anyway. Gray robe. Ink-stained fingers. Small handwriting, allegedly readable only by beetles."

The ink lady's hand drifted to her wrist without her seeming to know it. "That was me?"

"That was you. And the man pressed a map into your hands, and whispered your name—which he shouldn't have known—and died. And you hid the map under your desk while guards came, and your heart was going like a drum, and you were terrified, and you were curious, and the curiosity was bigger." Wren leaned forward. "That's the part that matters. The curiosity was bigger. It's always bigger with you. I watched it be bigger for the whole whole trip."

"I don't feel brave," the ink lady said quietly. "Is that strange? Everyone keeps looking at me like I'm a story. But inside I just feel... new. Like a house nobody's lived in yet."

"Good," said Wren. "Because houses are better empty. Houses you can fill yourself. If it comes furnished you're stuck with somebody else's chairs." She said it with total confidence, though she wasn't entirely sure what it meant. It sounded wise. Wise things often arrived in her head wearing borrowed clothes and she'd learned not to question them.

* * *

Over the following days, Wren gave it back piece by piece, and she learned something wonderful, which was that telling a story well was a kind of work, maybe the best kind.

She told her about the salt-flats, running barefoot with the map burning cold against her ribs. About the sad sword appearing out of nowhere on the salt road with his terrible lie about being "a traveling guard," and how she'd lied right back about being "on temple business," and how they'd each known the other was lying within a minute and traveled together anyway for eleven days pretending otherwise.

"He's a bad liar," Wren reported. "He does this thing with his jaw. Like a cow chewing. You always knew."

"I gathered that," said the ink lady, and there was something warm and complicated in her voice whenever the sad sword came up, and Wren, who was nine and had heard every story ever told in the underworld of a great city, understood that warmth perfectly well and filed it away for later use.

She told her about the border crossing, when magic flooded back into the world and the map woke up and wrote itself across her skin. "You screamed," Wren said honestly. "Then you laughed. Then you screamed again. Then you looked at your arm and said—" Wren closed her eyes to get it exact, because exactness was the whole job, "'*It's not a map I'm carrying. It's a language.*' And Sef went white as milk, and you asked him why, and he lied, jaw-cow, jaw-cow, but you let him."

She told her about Merrow's Cross, softening nothing, because the ink lady had already seen the town and besides, softening was lying and lying to a witness-in-training was a crime. She told her about the vial in the Undermarket labeled *P. Hartwell*, and how the ink lady had gone so quiet and so furious that even the barge master, a man who feared absolutely nothing, had taken a step backward.

"You bought it back," Wren reminded her. "Spent half our money. You said, '*No one gets to own me, especially not the past tense of me.*'" She paused. "We buried it outside the city walls. You said names shouldn't live in bottles."

The ink lady was crying a little now, silently, the way she seemed to do everything, neatly, without fuss. "Go on," she whispered.

* * *

On the fifth evening, Wren saved the hard ones for last, and she told them gently, sitting cross-legged on the end of the ink lady's cot with the candle burning steady between them.

She told her about Caelmoor—not the details, those belonged to the sad sword to give himself, and Wren suspected he was working up to it, in his slow rock-slide way—but about the confession over cold tea, and how the ink lady had raged and grieved and then, at dawn, packed their bags and said, "*Loyalty weighs more than truth, and I refuse to pretend otherwise. Get up. We have a king to unbind.*"

"Why did you stay with him?" the ink lady asked. "If I was angry. Why?"

Wren considered. "Because you said—night nineteen, on the barge, watching him sleep standing up against the mast like a horse—you said, '*Everyone I've ever trusted has been broken in some load-bearing place. He's just the only one honest enough to show me where the cracks are.*'" She shrugged. "Also you liked him. It was extremely obvious. Even the barge master knew. The barge master knew everything, though. He was like a fat wise cat."

The ink lady laughed, wetly, and wiped her eyes with her sleeve like a person and not like a legend.

And then Wren told her the last part. The throne room. The thirteenth moon coming down like a coin dropping into a slot. The way the ink lady had stood in the wreck of the binding-circle with the Crowned unraveling around her, speaking her own name aloud, syllable by syllable, spending it like the last coin in the purse—willingly, clearly, with her chin up.

"And then you turned around," Wren said, "and looked at all of us like we were strangers." She reached out and took the ink lady's hand. "Which you were. Which we all were. Except—" and here Wren leaned in close, because this was the thesis, the point, the thing she'd been building toward across five days and forty miles and one entire war, "—except you weren't. Because I remembered you. And the sad sword remembered you. And the king remembers you, he sends letters, awful letters, they take him three drafts each. And now *you'll* remember you, because I'm telling you. See? A witness is worth ten memories. Your own words. You can't argue with your own words. I checked."

The ink lady sat very still for a long time. The candle didn't flicker. Nothing in Vael Hollow flickered.

"Paloma Hartwell," she said at last, slowly, tasting the sounds like a foreign fruit. "Debt-bond scribe. Broke rules. Kept water for strangers. Insulted rope. Bought back a bottle. Chose a liar. Spent everything." She looked up, and her eyes were shining and dry at the same time, which Wren hadn't known eyes could do. "That's quite a woman."

"She was," Wren agreed. "And the good news is—" she patted the hand she was holding, briskly, practically, "—you're her heir. Everything she did, you get to keep. And everything she didn't get to do, you get to start."

* * *

They went down to the copying hall together the next morning, early, while mist still pooled in the courtyard like spilled milk. The ink lady sat at the same empty desk. She picked up the pen. Her hand didn't shake.

"What should I write?" she asked. "It feels like it should matter. First words."

Wren thought hard. She dug through the great stacked warehouse of everything she'd ever heard, past the barge master and the astronomer and the fat wise cat of the river, past her mother's voice and the name-wagons and all the dark crates she kept locked at the bottom. She found what she wanted. She always found what she wanted. That was the trick.

"Write what you told me at Thornbridge," she said, "when the guards were coming and you handed me the forged papers and your hands were steady and mine weren't. You said: '*Paper remembers what people can't afford to.*'"

The ink lady—no. Not anymore. The woman who used to be her, and who was now becoming someone new out of everything Wren handed her, piece by labeled piece—dipped the pen and wrote, in small neat letters that a beetle could certainly have read:

*Paper remembers what people can't afford to.*

She looked at it for a long time. Then she turned the page and began a fresh line, and her voice, when she spoke, was quiet and certain and entirely her own.

"Now then," she said. "Tell me about the day we met. Start from the beginning. Don't leave anything out."

And Wren, richest girl in the world, folded her hands, took a breath, and began.
