## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The border stones of Hollowmere stood in a crooked line down the western pass like teeth that had once belonged to something larger, and every traveler who crossed them left a little of themselves behind without ever knowing it.

Wendell Vane knew this the way he knew his own hands — intimately, and with no memory of learning it.

He crouched now behind the third stone from the road's bend, the one furred with grey lichen and warm from the afternoon sun, and watched the traveler come down out of the hills. A woman, forty perhaps, walking with the loose-hipped ease of someone who had spent her life on ships. Salt-stained boots. A coat too heavy for autumn in the valley. She was humming as she walked — a low, rolling tune that bent in the middle the way sea-songs did, and Wendell felt the familiar hunger open in him like a hand.

*There she is,* he thought. *There's my bread.*

Magic could not cross the border stones. That was the whole of Hollowmere's covenant, the bargain struck so long ago that no one alive remembered who had struck it or with whom. Inside the valley, no spell would light, no charm would hold, no sorcery of any kind would answer to a name. The stones drank it all. Travelers came down out of the high passes trailing enchantments like burrs — blessings sewn into hems, wards knotted into hair, charms against fever strung around necks on leather cords — and by the time they reached the valley floor they were ordinary. Clean. Stripped.

But memories were not magic. Memories were only meat, and meat the stones ignored entirely. And so a man who had learned — from whom? from where? he had never known — the old touching-work could sit behind a lichen-furred stone and lift the past right out of a passing head, the way you'd lift a purse, if your fingers were quiet enough.

Wendell's fingers were very quiet indeed.

The woman stopped at the fourth stone to drink from her flask. She tipped her head back, throat working, still humming, and the sun caught the fine lines at her eyes and the salt-white streak in her dark hair. Wendell breathed out slow, the way he'd taught himself to breathe — long exhale, empty lungs, an emptied self being the easiest thing for another mind to pour into — and reached.

Not with his hands. The reaching was lower than hands, older, somewhere in the soft floor of him where thought became body. He touched the edge of her attention, feather-light, and felt her awareness ripple the way water ripples when a moth lands on its surface. She frowned faintly at the middle distance. She looked up at the sky, puzzled, as if a cloud had passed over a sun that was still shining.

And there it was. Her past, spread out under her present like coins under a cloth.

He didn't rummage. Rummaging left bruises — a traveler would walk on into Hollowmere feeling vaguely robbed, checking pockets that weren't pockets, and some of them talked, and enough talk brought the valley's few hard men asking questions Wendell preferred not to answer. No: he went in gentle, one thread at a time, reading the tags of things the way a merchant reads the marks on bales. *Harbor childhood. Storm survived at nineteen. A wedding, brief. A funeral.* He moved past griefs — griefs were heavy and left a dragging hole in a person, and besides, they sold poorly; nobody wanted to carry somebody else's mourning home at the end of the day.

What he wanted sang in her still. He found it near the top, kept close and polished with handling: *the sea.*

Not a description of the sea. The sea itself, as she carried it — the green-grey swell off some coast whose name he didn't catch and didn't need, the deck lifting under her feet, spray cold as a slap, gulls hanging in the wind like scraps of thrown paper, and underneath everything that enormous patient sound, the sound the land never made, the breathing of a thing too big to have a name worth saying.

Wendell lifted it the way you'd lift an egg.

It settled into him with a weight and a chill that made his breath catch. For a moment — always just a moment — he stood on her deck instead of kneeling behind his stone. The horizon ran all the way around the world. The air tasted of iodine and distance.

Then the moment closed, as moments did, and he was a thin man in a worn coat crouching in the grass of a valley that had never seen a sea and never would, holding something beautiful that wasn't his.

The woman capped her flask. She blinked twice, looked at the sky again — the small confusion already smoothing off her face, because minds are merciful and paper over their own gaps — and walked on down toward the village, humming a tune that no longer bent quite the same way in the middle. By tomorrow she wouldn't notice anything missing at all. People rarely missed a single thing out of a whole life. There was always more life underneath.

That was the trick of it, Wendell thought, rising, brushing off his knees. Nobody missed what they couldn't name. He was living proof.

* * *

Hollowmere had two streets, a mill, a shrine nobody tended, and an inn called the Crooked Wether where the fire was always lit and the gossip was always three days stale. It had been Wendell's whole world for as long as he could remember, which was longer than most people's, and shorter than he pretended.

He walked in through the back way, past the woodpile, up the outside stair to the room he rented from the innkeeper's widow, who asked no questions because he paid in coin that raised no eyebrows and kept his boots clean of mud. Two rules governed his life, and he recited them to himself on those stairs every evening the way other men said prayers.

*Never steal from children.*

*Never leave Hollowmere.*

The first rule needed no explaining, even to himself. Children's memories weren't finished yet; taking from them was theft from a house still being built. Also, children noticed. Children felt the gap like a pulled tooth and worried it with their tongues for years. He had done it exactly once, in his early days, from a boy of nine or ten — a memory of a dog, nothing more, a yellow dog and a stick — and the way the child had stopped in the road afterward and looked back down the valley with tears starting and no idea why had stayed with Wendell like a splinter working toward his heart. He'd given the memory back. That was harder than the taking; giving back meant going in again, and going in twice doubled the risk. But he had done it, and he had never touched a child's mind since, and when travelers came through with children in tow he worked the parents and let the young ones pass untouched, unburdened, whole.

The second rule everyone in the valley understood without him saying it, because everyone in the valley lived by it too. Magic stopped at the stones. Whatever else was true of the world beyond them — and travelers' stray surface-thoughts suggested wars, plagues, sorcerers, kingdoms rising and falling like weather — none of it could follow you in. Hollowmere was a pocket of nowhere, a knot tied against the world. You stayed inside the knot or you took your chances with everything outside it.

Wendell had taken inventory of his own courage many times over the years and found it sufficient for staying put. There was nothing out there for him. There was nothing in here either, strictly speaking, but in here the nothing was quiet.

In his room, he drew the curtain, lit the lamp, and opened the cabinet.

The vials caught the lamplight and threw it back in small amber pieces. Dozens of them, ranked in wooden racks he'd built himself, each cork sealed with wax, each labeled in his small precise hand. This was the other thing the valley didn't know about him — or rather, the thing it chose not to look at directly, the way you don't look directly at the moon. Old Marench the broker kept a back room behind his tannery where such goods changed hands, and Marench had clients in the low country beyond the pass — collectors, the lonely, the dying, people who wanted to feel a storm or a first kiss or a mother's kitchen one more time in a borrowed body. Memory was currency. Wendell Vane was the richest poor man in Hollowmere.

He uncorked no vial tonight except the new one. He poured the woman's sea into a fresh glass tube — not literally; the memory lived in him until sold, the vial was only a vessel for the wax and the tag and the theater of it, but the theater mattered, Marench insisted, buyers liked to see goods that looked like goods — and held it up to the lamp.

Green-grey. Cold. Enormous.

His best work in months. He felt it sitting inside him like a swallowed stone, that great patient breathing, and he thought, as he thought every time: *this is the closest I will ever come to owning my own life — holding someone else's.*

Because here was the arithmetic of Wendell Vane, and he did it nightly, the way misers count: he owned the memory of a desert caravan, three separate oceans, a mountain funeral, a wedding in a snowed-in chapel, the taste of forty unfamiliar fruits, the sound of eleven languages, a duel at dawn, a city on fire seen from a rooftop (he did not sell that one; some goods spoiled the whole shelf), and the face of a woman laughing in a garden, though he'd forgotten which traveler she'd belonged to and sometimes, in bad hours, whether she'd belonged to anyone at all or had drifted loose between vials.

He owned everyone's past but his own.

Before Hollowmere — before the stones, before the room above the inn — there should have been a *before*. Everyone had one. But when Wendell reached for his, his hand closed on smooth nothing, the way a tongue finds the socket of a tooth pulled so long ago the gum has healed over. He had woken, as far as his memory went, at the age of — he estimated twenty? — standing at the border stones with sore hands and no name he could swear to, and *Vane* had simply been there, waiting, the way names wait in valleys for men who need them.

He had theories. In his early years he'd wasted whole winters on theories: that he'd fled some crime, that the stones themselves had stripped him like they stripped enchantments (though memories crossed free, so why not his?), that he had done the stripping himself with his own touching-work, which was the theory that kept him up at nights and that he fed to the fire whenever it got too loud.

Nowadays he didn't theorize. Nowadays he bought bread with other people's yesterdays and kept his racks dusted and told himself that a man with a full cabinet wanted for nothing.

Most nights he almost believed it.

* * *

Marench's back room smelled of hides and lamp oil and, beneath both, of something sweeter and wronger — the smell of the broker's trade, which clung to the walls the way tallow clung to a chandler's apron. Shelves lined the room, and on the shelves sat jars that Wendell did not let himself examine too closely, because Marench dealt in more than memories and had never once offered, and Wendell had never once asked, and the not-asking was the foundation of their entire arrangement.

"Sea," Marench said, when Wendell set the tagged vial on the scarred table. "Again."

"It sells."

"It sells because you bring me sea like no one else brings me sea." Marench turned the vial in his thick fingers without opening it — he never opened them in front of sellers, a courtesy or a warning, depending on the hour. His eyes were pale as pickle brine and about as warm. "You've a touch for it, Vane. Always have. Makes me wonder sometimes where a valley man learned to swim so deep."

"In other people's heads," Wendell said. "Same place you learned to count."

Marench smiled with the bottom half of his face only. He named a price. Wendell countered. They settled where they always settled, a fair figure dressed up as a grudging one, and Marench counted out coin across the table with the deliberation of a man who wanted you to watch every piece.

At the door, with the coins in his pocket and the transaction finished and the evening going blue outside the single window, Marench spoke without looking up from his ledger.

"The woman. Today, on the west road. Brown coat."

Wendell paused. "What of her?"

"You took deep. Deeper than your usual cut." Marench's pen scratched. "I can always tell. The good ones leave a shine on the buyer's fingers." He looked up then, and the lamplight did something unfriendly to his brine-pale eyes. "Careful how deep you reach these days, Vane. There's word moving along the passes. Word about things that were erased. Kingdoms that got unwritten. Folk who collect what falls out of history when a name comes loose." The pen scratched again. "Word travels faster than wagons. And word says some of what fell out is looking for somewhere small and safe to hide."

"Hollowmere isn't hiding anything," Wendell said. "Nothing crosses the stones."

"No," Marench agreed pleasantly. "Nothing does."

Wendell walked home through streets going dark, past windows lighting one by one, past the shrine where the little god's features had weathered blank generations ago, and the coins in his pocket weighed what they always weighed and somehow less. Bread money. He'd buy the good loaf tomorrow, and cheese, and maybe the dried apples Sela the grocer got from the pass caravans, and eat his supper above the Crooked Wether with a sea inside him that no one in the valley had ever seen.

That night he dreamed, which was rare, and in the dream there was no sea at all. There was a knock at his door, and cold coming through the wood like a draft from a deeper country, and a voice speaking words he did not know and, some buried part of him screamed, should know, words that burned —

He woke before dawn with the taste of frost in his mouth and his heart going hard, and lay listening to the valley's silence, and told himself it was only a dream, the way men do, right up until the knocking starts for real.
