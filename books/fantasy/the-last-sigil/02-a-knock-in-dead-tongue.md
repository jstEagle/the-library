## 2. A Knock in Dead Tongue

There were three rules Wendell Vane lived by, and he recited them the way other men recited prayers: never steal from children, never leave Hollowmere, never keep what you couldn't sell.

The third rule was a lie he told himself every night before bed, because under his floorboards, wrapped in wool and wax, sat eleven vials he had never sold and never would.

That night was moonless — the dark so complete it felt less like absence of light than presence of something heavier. Wendell sat at his table with a candle stub and a cup of barley tea gone cold, doing the arithmetic of a small life. Four coppers left. Bread would cost two. The sea-memory from the tinker would fetch six if Marench was feeling honest, three if he wasn't, and Marench hadn't felt honest since the winter of the drowned mill.

He was counting coppers when the knock came.

Three knocks. Soft. Almost polite.

Wendell went still in the way he'd learned to go still — not moving like a man afraid, but like a man listening with his whole body. Travelers didn't knock on his door. Travelers came to the border stones, where he worked them gentle as a pickpocket lifting a watch, and they walked on into the valley without ever knowing what they'd lost. Nobody knew where Wendell Vane slept. He had made sure of it for nine years.

Another knock. Then a voice — small, cracked, speaking words that made no sense and yet made his teeth ache.

The language was old. Not old like his grandmother's sayings, old like *buried*. Old like something that had been killed on purpose and buried face-down so it couldn't climb back up. And the terrible thing, the thing that raised every hair on Wendell's arms, was that some animal part of him understood it. Not the meaning. The *weight*. The words fell with a cadence he knew the way you know a song you've never heard — a lullaby in a tongue that should have been dust.

"Who's there?" he said, and hated how his voice caught.

The answer came again, those same syllables, patient now. Pleading.

Under the floorboards, the vials began to shudder.

He heard it clearly — a fine glass rattling, eleven small voices chiming against their cork stoppers, and Wendell was across the room before he'd decided to move, hauling up the boards, kneeling in the cold earth with the candle held low. The vials trembled in their wool. Inside each one, the memory swirled faster than memory should — the tinker's sea churning green and furious, the cooper's wedding flickering like a struck match, all of them agitated, all of them *afraid*.

Memories didn't do this. Memories were quiet things once you bottled them. They settled like silt. In nine years of thieving, Wendell had never once seen one move without his hand on the glass.

The knocking came again, and this time the door handle turned — slowly, without force, the way a child turns a handle when they've been told doors are heavy things.

Wendell stood, blew out the candle, and put himself between the door and the floorboards with a fire iron in his fist.

*Never steal from children.* He'd made that rule the first year, after lifting a memory from a girl no older than ten and watching her walk away lighter, emptier, smiling at nothing. Some thefts you can't price. Some holes don't close.

The door opened onto darkness.

A child stood on his step. Small — eight years old, maybe nine, though hunger and road-dust made it hard to read. Barefoot despite the season. A coat three sizes too large, buttoned wrong, that might have been cut for an adult and shrunk by grief. Hair the color of river clay, matted flat, and eyes that caught the last grey light from the sky and gave none of it back.

"You shouldn't be here," Wendell said. "Whatever village you've come from—"

The child spoke again. Those same buried words, and this time Wendell felt them land — not in his ears but somewhere behind his sternum, cold as a coin left overnight in snow. His breath fogged. The night was cold, yes, but not *that* cold, and the chill came from inside the words themselves, radiating outward like the opposite of a hearth.

"I don't understand you," he said, which was true and also, somehow, a lie.

The child looked at him. Really looked — the way you look at a locked door when you've been walking for a very long time. Then those eyes moved past him, into the dark of the cottage, and fixed on the floorboards over the vials, and the child said one more word.

One word only. Soft as a bruise being pressed.

Every vial under the floor rang at once — a clear, terrified chord, glass singing — and Wendell's knees nearly buckled, because he recognized the word's shape even without its meaning. It was a name. Or close enough to a name that it didn't matter. It was the sound a thing makes when it is called home.

"Get away from my house," Wendell said, and his voice came out wrong, thin and high, and the child flinched as if the words themselves had struck.

Then the child swayed, and folded, and Wendell caught them before they hit the step — catching them without deciding to, hands moving on their own old errand — and the weight of the boy in his arms was almost nothing. Nothing but bones and a coat and a fever burning under skin like banked coals. Up close, the child smelled of reed-water and woodsmoke and something else, something older, the smell of the air before lightning.

*Never steal from children,* Wendell thought, holding a child who had stolen into his life through a locked door on a night with no moon. *And apparently never turn one away either.*

He carried the boy inside and laid him on the bed, which was really a straw pallet he himself barely used, and built up the fire, and did all the small domestic things a man does for a guest while telling himself he was doing them for no reason at all. Fever-wracked children died in ditches. That was not his business. Nothing was his business. His business was four coppers and a bottle of someone else's ocean.

But when he knelt to pull off the boy's ruined shoes, the child's hand closed around his wrist. Weak as a bird's foot. Eyes half-open, fever-bright, fixing on him with terrible clarity.

The boy spoke again — a full sentence this time, slow and deliberate, each syllable placed like a stone in a wall. And though Wendell understood nothing, he understood everything: *I have come a long way. I have nowhere else. You know what I am.*

"No," Wendell said aloud, to the empty room, to the fevered child, to whatever had sent this thing to his door. "No, I don't know what you are."

Under the floorboards, the vials had gone silent. But when he checked them — kneeling in the dark, candle low, telling himself it was habit and nothing more — he found the sea-memory had changed. The tinker's ocean, which had been grey-green and ordinary when he'd lifted it, now burned blue at its center. Deep blue. Vael-blue, said a voice in his head that he did not own, and he clamped down on the thought so hard his jaw ached, because he did not know that color, had never known that color, had no business knowing it.

He closed the floorboards. Sat by the fire. Watched the child breathe.

* *

Sleep did not come, so Wendell worked instead, because work was the shape his fear took. He laid out his trade on the table by candlelight: the tinker's sea in its lead-lined case, ready for morning; three smaller memories in reserve; the tools of his quiet craft — a sliver of polished bone for the temple touch, a twist of lavender and ash to smooth the edges of a wound. He arranged them neatly, the way a carpenter arranges chisels, and told himself the ritual would steady him.

It didn't. Because twice during the night, the vials sang.

Not loudly. Just a thread of sound, glass humming at the edge of hearing, both times when the child stirred in sleep and murmured those dead syllables. Both times, Wendell felt the sound in his fillings, in the marrow of his fingers. And both times, when he pressed his palm flat against the floorboards, he could swear the earth beneath his cottage was humming too — a lower note, deeper, as though the valley itself had begun to listen.

Hollowmere kept magic out. That was the whole point of the place, the reason a thief had chosen it nine years ago and never left. The border stones ringed the valley like a promise: *nothing enchanted crosses here*. No sorcery, no curses, no wonders. Just mud and barley and people too dull to dream big. It was the safest boring place in the world, and Wendell Vane had bought his safety with other people's pasts and had never once, in nine years, regretted it.

So why did the stones feel far away tonight? Why did the air in his single room feel *thin*, like a membrane stretched over something vast?

Near dawn, the fever broke. The child surfaced from sleep like a swimmer coming up through dark water, blinked at the fire, at the cottage, at Wendell sitting rigid in his chair, and then — deliberately, with the gravity of a much older soul wearing a young face — pressed one small hand against his own chest and spoke.

Two words this time. Wendell caught them like splinters.

Then the boy pointed at Wendell. Pointed at his chest. And waited.

"You want my name," Wendell said slowly. "Wendell. Wendell Vane."

The child shook his head. Not that. Tried again — pointed at Wendell, then made a sound, one of the dead-tongue syllables, shaped around something that was clearly a question. Asking who Wendell was. Asking *what* Wendell was. And there was something in the boy's expression that stopped Wendell's breath — recognition, or the ghost of it, the way you squint at a stranger because they wear the coat of someone you buried.

"I'm nobody," Wendell said. "I take memories off travelers and sell them to a fat man in town. That's all I am."

The child considered this. Then reached out, took Wendell's hand — the one calloused from nine years of temple-touches — turned it palm-up, and traced one finger across it. Slowly. Drawing.

A shape. A circle crossed by three lines, like a wheel seen through frost.

Wendell snatched his hand back as if scalded. Because he knew the shape. Not from any book, not from any traveler's mind he'd rifled — he knew it the way you know your own signature. His hand knew it. Somewhere in the muscles and tendons, in the body that remembered what the mind had been denied, that sigil lived like a scar lives in skin.

"I don't know that," he said hoarsely. "I've never seen that."

The child looked at him with those light-swallowing eyes, and for the first time since arriving, smiled — small, exhausted, unbearably sad. As if to say: *you will.*

Outside, the first grey hint of dawn touched the reeds. And Wendell Vane sat in his chair with a stranger's child sleeping in his bed and a symbol burned into his muscle-memory and eleven vials under the floor that had spent the night singing in a dead language — and he understood, with the cold certainty of a man reading his own death warrant, exactly what he had to do.

Marench would know. Marench knew every strange thing that entered the valley, and Marench paid best for strangeness, and Marench was the only man in Hollowmere who might recognize a tongue that everyone else believed had never existed.

Which meant carrying the child — the feverish, barefoot, impossible child — straight to the one person Wendell trusted least in the world.

*Never steal from children,* Wendell thought, looking at the sleeping boy. *Nobody ever said anything about selling them.*

He hated himself for the thought. He got up anyway, and began packing.
