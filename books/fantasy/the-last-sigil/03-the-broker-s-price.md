## 3. The Broker's Price

The broker kept his shop where the river forgot to be a river.

That was the joke of it — Hollowmere's one piece of honest geography. Upstream the water ran loud and brown through the willows, and downstream it picked itself up again and went on its way to the lowlands, but for a quarter mile in the middle it simply stopped, seeped into the chalk, and left a dry channel of smooth grey stones that everyone called the Riverbed and no one called anything else. Marench had built his shop against the bank of that nothing, because a man who traded in memories understood better than anyone the value of an absence.

Wendell walked the dry stones at midmorning, three days after the child arrived, with the vial riding warm in his inner pocket like a small dishonest heart.

He had not slept well. That was the truth of it, though he'd have bitten his tongue off before admitting it aloud. The child slept on his floor by the banked fire, curled around the rag doll he'd made from a flour sack, breathing in the slow even rhythm of someone who trusted the room they were in. Wendell had sat up most of the night watching him, waiting for the words to come back — those cold syllables that had burned through his door on a moonless night and made every vial on his shelves tremble in their wax like insects waking too early.

They hadn't come again. The child said nothing now. He looked at Wendell with eyes the color of rainwater over slate, and he ate what was given him, and he slept by the fire, and he was silent as a stone. Whatever had spoken through him that night had gone back down into whatever deep place it lived.

But Wendell had done something foolish in the dark hours, something he told himself was prudence and knew was greed.

He'd taken a scrap of it.

Not from the child — never from children, that rule held even when the child was a riddle wrapped in a dead language — but from the air. From the doorframe, if you wanted to put it crudely. When the child had spoken, the words hadn't just made sound; they'd left something behind, the way lightning leaves its smell. And Wendell Vane, who could lift a traveler's memory of the sea as gently as picking up a coin, had found that he could scrape the residue off the wood of his own threshold. A fragment of phrase. A single line of the dead tongue, still humming with whatever power hummed in it, sealed into a vial of smoked glass with hands that shook worse than they had since his first year in the trade.

It was worth money. That was what he told himself, walking the Riverbed with his collar up against the morning damp. It was rare beyond reckoning, and rarity was price, and price was bread and lamp oil and a new latch for a door that had been knocked on by something old enough to frighten him. The fact that part of him — a part he did not examine — simply wanted to know what Marench would do when he heard it was a weakness he could afford, so long as he didn't name it.

Marench's shop had no sign. Brokers didn't need signs; their customers came by other means. The building leaned into the empty riverbank like a drunk against a wall, timber gone silver with age, and its single window glowed with a light that was warm from outside and, Wendell knew from long experience, cold within — light filtered through glass stained with tinctures that made memory keep longer.

The door opened before he knocked. It always did.

"Wendell Vane." Marench's voice was soft the way silt is soft, the way things are soft when they've settled at the bottom of deep water. "Three weeks. I'd begun composing your elegy."

"You don't compose, Marench. You appraise."

"True." The broker stepped back to let him in. "Elegies are for things with only one version."

* * *

The inside of the shop was dim and smelled of beeswax and cold iron and, underneath both, faintly of the sea — because Marench stocked sea memories the way other men stocked brandy, and the smell of them leaked. Shelves rose floor to ceiling on every wall, hundreds upon hundreds of vials, each stoppered with red wax and tagged in the broker's cramped hand: *harvest festival, 40 years held. First snow, child's-eye view, pristine. Mother's voice, singing.* Rows and rows of other people's lives, catalogued and priced, and somewhere among them — Wendell had never asked which shelf, would never ask which shelf — the inventory Marench held on Wendell himself. Every thief who sold to a broker sold collateral. That was the trade. Your own oldest memories sat in the man's cabinets, and your good behavior sat with them.

Wendell had eleven vials in Marench's keeping. He tried not to think about them. He thought about them constantly.

"So." Marench lowered himself onto his stool behind the counter, a narrow man folded up like a folding ruler, all joints and angles under a coat the color of wet ash. His face was forgettable in the professional sense — a face designed to slide out of memory, which Wendell suspected was not natural and had never once said so. "What have you brought me? And please don't say another fisherman's honeymoon. I have forty honeymoons. I could pave the road with them."

Wendell set the vial on the counter between them.

He watched Marench look at it, and he saw something he had never seen in twenty years of doing business with the man. He saw Marench go still.

Brokers were trained to stillness — appraisal required it — but this was different. This was the stillness of a man who has heard a floorboard creak above him in a house he believed empty. Marench's hand came out slowly, turned the vial in the lamplight, and Wendell watched the smoked glass catch the glow and hold it wrong, the way it had caught the firelight wrong all night in his cottage, bending it toward colors that weren't in any flame.

"Where," Marench said quietly, "did you get this?"

"A scrap," Wendell said. "Residue. You know how strong feelings leave traces on places. Well — strong words leave stronger ones. I scraped the threshold."

"The threshold." Marench set the vial down with exaggerated care, as though it might bruise. "Your threshold. On Fennel Lane. The cottage with the crooked chimney."

"Marench—"

"There is a child in your house." It wasn't a question, and the softness of the broker's voice had changed quality entirely, the way water changes when it goes from cool to freezing. "A mute child, arrived three nights past, on a night with no moon. Who speaks — or spoke, once — a language that has not been spoken in this world for longer than this valley has existed."

The shop was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, a vial ticked as its wax seal contracted in the cold.

"How do you know that?" Wendell asked.

Marench smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes, which remained fixed on Wendell with the flat attention of a man reading a ledger. "Because I am a broker, Wendell. Knowing things is the entire shape of my life. Because the ferryman's boy saw them cross at dusk and told the miller's wife, who tells me everything for a discount on her mother's memories. Because when the moon goes dark over Hollowmere, people talk, and I listen, and listening is my trade as lifting is yours." He paused. "And because four days ago, before your child ever reached your door, I received a letter asking after exactly such a child. And now here you stand, selling me a piece of the answer."

The cold went through Wendell then, slow, starting at the soles of his boots. "A letter. From whom?"

"That is the interesting question." Marench rose from the stool and moved along the counter, trailing one finger across the shelf edge, disturbing a fine grey dust. "You know I deal mostly in the local trade. Fishermen. Farmers. The occasional lovesick fool wanting to sell the worst of his heartache and keep the best. Small goods, fair prices. But I have correspondents elsewhere. Collectors. People for whom rarity is not a price but a hunger." His finger stopped moving. "One of them wrote to me about the kingdom of Vael."

The word landed in the shop like a stone dropped into a well, and Wendell waited for the splash and heard none. The name meant nothing to him — less than nothing, some hole in the world shaped like meaning — and yet his body reacted before his mind could refuse. His mouth had gone dry. His hands, resting on the counter, had curled inward like something trying to become fists or trying to hide.

"Vael," he repeated. The word felt strange in his mouth, familiar the way a stranger's coat is familiar — cut for someone else's shoulders.

"Erased," Marench said, with relish. He enjoyed this; Wendell realized that with distant clarity. The broker enjoyed knowing things that unmade people. "Every record, every ruin, every grave and grandchild. Not destroyed — erased. There is a difference, and the difference is why my correspondent pays so well. Destroyed things leave bones. Erased things leave nothing, and therefore anything that remains is priceless." He tapped the vial with one yellowed nail. "This is a remainder of Vael. Perhaps the last remainder in existence. And it walked to your door on two small feet and knocked."

"I'm not selling the child," Wendell said. "I'm selling the scrap. If you want it."

"Oh, I want it." Marench turned the vial again, and the light inside it bent, and for half a heartbeat Wendell thought he heard — faintly, impossibly, from within the sealed glass — a woman's voice singing a single rising note. His skin prickled. "But you misunderstand the market, Wendell. You always have. You think you're a seller. But there are buyers in this world whose appetite does not negotiate. My correspondent did not ask me to find scraps of the Vaeli tongue." He leaned forward across the counter, and up close his forgettable face seemed suddenly full of details, the way a page seems full of print when you finally look. "They asked me to find the vessel. The one that carries what they call — I copy the letter exactly — *the Last Sigil.*"

The shop tilted. Or Wendell tilted. He gripped the counter's edge.

"I don't know what that is," he said.

"No," Marench agreed pleasantly. "Neither did I, four days ago. I know somewhat more now. Enough to know that when the letter asked, I replied honestly: no such thing had passed through Hollowmere. And then three nights ago the moon went black, and a child knocked on a thief's door, and the thief came running to sell the proof." He spread his thin hands. "So now I must write again. And this time my letter will say: *it is here. It sits by a thief's fire eating his bread. Come.*"

Something in Wendell's chest closed like a door slamming. He heard himself speak from a great calm distance, the calm he found when lifting memories, the calm of a man with his fingers inside someone else's mind. "Who is the correspondent, Marench?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters if they're coming here."

"They are already—" The broker stopped. Something crossed his face, quick as a fish under ice, and Wendell understood two things at once: first, that Marench had already sent the second letter, days ago perhaps, the moment the ferryman's boy reported a mute child on the road; and second, that the broker regretted saying as much as he had, which meant the truth was worse than what he'd said.

"Already what?" Wendell asked softly.

"Already interested," Marench finished smoothly. Too smoothly. "Which brings us to terms. The scrap I'll buy — five crowns, and don't insult us both by haggling, you need bread more than pride. But the child—"

"Is not for sale."

"Everything is for sale. That is not cynicism, Wendell, it is physics. The question is only the currency." Marench's eyes flicked upward, toward the shelves, toward the eleven vials that Wendell had put in this man's keeping years ago against bad seasons, against the day the trade dried up, against — though he'd never said it, never let himself say it — the day he might need to buy his way out of his own past. "You have collateral with me. Old stock. Very old. Some of it, I'm told by colleagues who understand these things, is not entirely ordinary stock at all. A memory you hold that you have no record of acquiring. Curious, that. Curious how a careful man keeps books and yet one vial appears in his collection with no provenance."

The floor of the world shifted again, gentler this time, like the valley itself leaning to listen. "What vial?"

"The oldest one. The one you've never sold because you've never been able to part with it, though you couldn't tell anyone why." Marench smiled his silt-soft smile. "You should open it sometime, Wendell. Before my correspondent arrives. I suspect you'll want to know what you were, before you decide what you'll be."

"I came to sell a scrap," Wendell said. Each word came out flat and separate, coins laid on a table. "Take it. Take your five crowns. And take this too, free of charge: if anyone comes looking for that child in my keeping, I'll know who told them, and I will spend the rest of my life making sure you remember everything you've ever wanted to forget. I'll pour it all back into you, Marench. Every grief. Every shame. Neat, labeled, in order. You'll drown in your own inventory."

For a moment — just a moment — Wendell saw fear move through the broker's forgettable face, and it was the most human thing he'd ever seen there.

Then Marench laughed, short and dry as a cough. "There he is. There's the thief." He swept the vial off the counter and produced five crowns from a drawer without looking, counting them onto the wood. "Take your money, Wendell. Go home to your foundling. Feed him, keep him warm, teach him your rules." He paused, and his voice dropped to almost nothing, and somehow that was the worst sound in the whole cold shop. "But I'll tell you what I told my correspondent in my reply, which went out with yesterday's ferry. I wrote: *the vessel is in the keeping of the memory-thief Vane, of Fennel Lane, Hollowmere. He will not give the child up willingly. But he will give up anything rather than let his valley be harmed. He believes this place protects him. Correct that assumption at your convenience.*"

Wendell stood very still. "You told them I believe the valley protects me."

"I told them the truth. Which is crueler." Marench folded his thin hands. "The border stones keep magic out, everyone says so. Everyone comforts themselves with it. But ask yourself, Wendell, as you walk home — ask yourself what kind of border lets a dead kingdom's child through, on a moonless night, straight to your door. Ask yourself whether the stones keep danger out." His smile returned, thin as wire. "Or whether they sometimes act as a funnel."

Wendell took the crowns. They were colder than coins should be.

At the door he stopped, hand on the latch, and did not turn around. "When does your correspondent arrive?"

Silence, long enough that he thought the broker wouldn't answer.

Then, from the dimness behind him, soft as sediment settling:

"Listen for singing, Wendell. They always sing."

* * *

He walked home fast, faster than care allowed, taking the high path above the Riverbed with the five crowns clenched in his fist and the broker's words turning over and over in him like stones in a mill. *Funnel. Vessel. Sigil.* Words that meant nothing and weighed everything. Behind him the shop's pale window shrank against the grey bank of the river that wasn't there, and ahead the willows of Fennel Lane bent in a wind he couldn't feel at ground level, and the whole valley lay quiet under its afternoon light — the pastures, the mill, the crooked chimneys, the border stones standing in their ancient patient ring like teeth around something swallowed.

Safe, he had always told travelers, when they asked. Nothing magical gets in. That was the whole promise of Hollowmere. That was why he'd stayed twenty years in a valley where his trade was legal and his loneliness was cheap.

Nothing magical gets in.

He crested the rise above his cottage and stopped so abruptly that his boot skidded in the wet grass.

Smoke was rising from his chimney — fine, because he'd banked the fire, expected to be gone hours, and the child wouldn't touch the hearth without leave. Fine. But beneath the smoke-smell, carried on the wind down the lane, was another scent. Beeswax. Cold iron. And underneath both, faint and impossible, thirty miles from any coast—

The sea.

No. Not the sea. Something older, wearing the sea's smell the way a thief wears a stolen coat.

The child stood in Wendell's doorway, small against the dark interior, and he wasn't silent anymore. He was singing — the same rising note Wendell had heard through the smoked glass, climbing now toward a word, toward many words, in the language with no living speakers — and every vial on Wendell's shelves, every stolen life in his keeping, was ringing in sympathy like struck glass.

And down the valley road, still distant but closing, came the sound of many voices singing back.
