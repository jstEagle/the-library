## 4. The Pale Choir

The knock came at dusk, and Wendell Vane knew before he opened the door that the world had ended.

Not his world — his world had ended years ago, quietly, in a way he'd never been able to name. This was the other kind. The loud kind. The kind that arrived with a sound like a hymn heard through water.

He had spent the day doing nothing, which was how he spent most days now that the child lived in his house. Sef sat by the hearth arranging pebbles in patterns that meant nothing to Wendell and everything to someone dead, and when the knock came the child's head snapped up like a deer catching wind of hounds.

Wendell put a finger to his lips. Old habit. The child couldn't speak anyway.

The knock came again. Three strikes, evenly spaced, with the patience of something that had never once needed to hurry.

He crossed the room and looked through the shutter's gap, and what he saw made his hands go cold in a way that had nothing to do with the season.

There were five of them on the road. Tall, robed in white so pale it was almost not a color at all — the white of frost on a window, of milk gone thin with water, of bone left long in sun. Their hoods were up. Where faces should have been there was only shadow, and in the shadow, nothing. Not darkness. Nothing. His eyes kept trying to focus and kept failing, sliding off them like rain off glass.

They stood at the border stones.

That was the thing. That was the impossible, valley-breaking thing. Hollowmere's border stones had stood for as long as anyone remembered, older than the chapel, older than the mill, older than any family name, and they did one job: they kept magic out. Travelers came in hollow and ordinary. Witches who wandered too close found their spells dying on their lips like breath in winter. The stones were the reason Wendell had chosen this valley, back when choosing was something he could still do — because here, at least, nothing could reach into your head but a man with steady hands and a knife's-edge talent.

And now five figures stood among the stones, and the stones were singing.

No. The figures were singing, and the stones were *letting* them.

The hymn was low and wordless at first, a hum that Wendell felt in his teeth more than heard. Then it resolved, the way fog resolves into shapes, and he understood two things at once. First: it was a language. Second: it was the language the child spoke.

Vaelish. The dead tongue. Sung by things with no faces, at the border of a valley that had never once allowed magic through its skin.

Behind him, Sef made a sound.

Wendell turned. The child was standing now, both hands pressed flat against the hearthstone, and every vial on Wendell's shelf — forty-one vials of stolen memory, each stoppered and waxed and labeled in his own cramped hand — was rattling in its rack as if the shelf itself were shivering. He watched the wax seals pulse. He watched the liquid inside them turn, all together, from silver to a gray like drowned flesh.

*They can feel it,* he thought. *My stolen memories can feel that song.*

One vial — the oldest one, the one he never touched, the one he told himself he kept out of sentiment — cracked straight down the middle. Not shattered. Cracked, clean, deliberate, like an egg tapped by a careful thumb. A thread of silver light leaked out and hung in the air, and in the light, for half a heartbeat, Wendell saw a city burning.

Then it sealed itself, or seemed to, and the light was gone, and Sef was looking at him with eyes too old for the face they lived in, and outside, the hymn climbed another note.

Wendell did the only sensible thing a man does when the rules of his whole life break at once. He moved.

* * *

There was no time to pack properly. He took the vials — all of them, even the cracked one, wrapped in flannel in the satchel he used for work — because a thief does not leave his stock behind, and because some part of him, deeper than sense, whispered that those vials were the only proof he existed at all. He took bread. He took the good knife and the ugly knife. He took Sef's hand, small and cold and gripping back harder than he expected, and he went out the back door into the last gray light.

The marshes lay south and east of his cottage, a thousand acres of reed and black water where the valley sloped down toward the fens. No one went there. No one had reason to. The reeds grew taller than a man in autumn and the paths were memories even the locals had let go of, which meant, in Hollowmere, that they barely existed at all.

It also meant the Pale Choir would move slowly there. Things that fed on erased things, he thought wildly, sprinting down the rutted lane with the child's hand in his — things like that would find a marsh full of forgotten paths either a feast or a maze. He was betting everything on *maze.*

The hymn followed them.

That was the horror of it. It didn't echo — echoes fade, bounce, break apart against hillsides. This came from everywhere at once, level and patient, growing no louder and no softer, as though distance were a courtesy it declined to observe. And now that he listened — now that he couldn't help listening — Wendell began to catch the words.

It was naming things.

*Stone,* sang the voices, and somewhere behind them the mill's foundation stone, the one laid by the valley's first settlers, crumbled to sand with a sound like a sigh. He felt it go. Everyone in Hollowmere must have felt it go — a small absence opening in the world, a hole shaped like a stone that no one would remember missing.

*Road,* sang the voices, and the lane beneath Wendell's feet softened, its ruts smoothing over, its history erasing, until he was running on ground that had never been walked and didn't know how to be a road anymore.

*Name,* sang the voices, and Wendell stumbled, because for one terrible instant he felt the hymn reach for *him*, slide over him like fingers over a shelf of jars, searching for a label—

—and finding none.

The hymn hesitated. A note held too long. Somewhere in that faceless harmony, something paused, puzzled, the way a clerk pauses at a ledger line that won't add up. *Wendell Vane* was a name he'd taken, worn, inhabited for eleven years, but underneath it — and he had never once let himself think this thought all the way through — underneath it there was nothing. A blank page where a man should have been written.

The hymn moved on. It lost interest. But in the moment of its attention, Wendell learned something about himself that he would spend the rest of this night not thinking about: the Pale Choir could not sing him, because there was almost nothing left of him to sing.

Sef pulled his hand. Hard. The child pointed south, into the deepest reeds, eyes wide and wet and furious — and it was the fury that decided him. Not fear. Fear froze you. But that look said *run*, in the language of someone who had run before, who had run from these exact singers once already, across distances Wendell could not imagine, carrying something worth more than every memory in his satchel.

They ran.

* * *

The marsh took them in like it had been waiting.

Reeds closed over their heads, eight feet tall, whispering in the wind with a sound like distant audiences. The path — if it was a path — was a seam of firmer mud winding between pools of black water that reflected a sky going purple with early stars. Wendell counted turns the way he counted everything: one for the fork by the lightning-split willow, two for the pool where the heron stood like a sentry, three, four, five, banking each in the vault of his mind where he kept other people's pasts.

Sef knew the way better than he did.

That unsettled him more than the hymn. The child had been in Hollowmere nine days. Nine days, most of them spent inside a cottage arranging pebbles. And yet Sef moved through the marsh like water remembering downhill — left here, around the dead oak, along the spine of the dike — pulling him by the hand with a certainty that had nothing to do with Hollowmere and everything to do with something older underneath it.

At the sixth turn Wendell finally understood, and stopped dead, and the reeds hissed around them.

Beneath the marsh, under centuries of silt and fen-water, there were foundations. He'd seen them once at low drought — lines of dressed stone in the black mud, too regular to be natural, too old to be anything the valley admitted to. He'd thought then: *old mill footing, maybe. Something drowned.* Now, with Sef tugging him along a route no living soul could know, he saw the truth with awful clarity.

The paths weren't forgotten. They were *mourned.* The marsh wasn't a marsh. It was a grave, and they were running along the bones of something buried so deep that even the border stones had grown over it — something the valley had agreed, without knowing it agreed, to forget.

The child's kingdom had not ended far away. Some part of it had ended *here*, and Hollowmere had been built on top of the silence.

"Down," Wendell breathed. "Get down."

Because the hymn had changed.

It had stopped naming stones and roads. The new words were slower, richer, and they carried a quality he recognized from his trade: the quality of a voice speaking directly into a mind. He pressed himself and Sef flat in the mud between reed-clumps, cold water soaking through his coat, and listened to the Choir sing a name he knew.

*Marench.*

The broker's name, unwound syllable by syllable, held up to the dark like a lantern. And then — worse, so much worse — the hymn sang *the thief of Hollowmere* and *the house by the mill-lane* and *forty-one vessels of stolen light*, and Wendell understood that Marench had sold him. Sold him completely, catalogued him, handed the Choir everything: his trade, his address, his inventory. The broker hadn't just known what Sef carried. He had priced it, packaged it, and posted it to the first buyers who could pay.

And the Choir had come to collect.

The hymn shifted again, and now it named the child — not *Sef*, which was perhaps not even a real name, but a description, a string of Vaelish that made the cracked vial in Wendell's satchel hum against his ribs like a struck bell. *The vessel. The last vessel. The one who carries the closing word.*

Closing word. Closing *what?*

He didn't know. He filed the question away with all the others, in the vault where he kept things too large to open, and he did what he did best: he focused on the immediate, the practical, the next ten feet of mud.

The voices were moving along the mill-lane. He could tell now, could track them the way he tracked travelers' thoughts — not hearing so much as feeling pressure, a slow tidal drag passing north of the marsh. They were walking his road. Toward his house. Patiently. They had all the patience of things that had already unmade a nation; one thief's cottage would take them moments, and then they would begin the search in earnest.

Unless they found nothing to search. Unless the thief of Hollowmere had never existed.

An idea came to him then, rising out of eleven years of craft like a fish out of black water, and he hated it even as he took hold of it.

*Hide us the way the valley hides its grave.*

He couldn't do what the Choir did — couldn't unmake. But he had spent his life doing the small cousin of unmaking. He lifted memories out of heads; he knew exactly what forgetting felt like from the inside, its texture, its weight, the way a mind smooths over a wound. And the marsh — the mourned, buried, half-erased marsh — was already soaked in that texture. It was a landscape made of forgetting. If forgetting had a current, this whole fen flowed with it.

He didn't have magic. He had craft, nerve, and a child's hand in his. He pressed his palm flat to the mud, closed his eyes, and did the closest thing to prayer a thief knows: he *listened* the way he listened at a traveler's temple, and asked the marsh, in whatever language drowned kingdoms dream in, to forget two more people.

For a moment — nothing. Water dripped. Reeds whispered. The hymn named houses along the mill-lane, and somewhere his home lost its windows, its chimney, its history, one detail at a time.

Then the mud breathed.

It was faint, and it was probably imagination, and it did not matter at all, because Sef's small hand covered his own where it pressed the earth, and the child *sang* — one note, low, Vaelish, a single syllable that the cracked vial echoed like a tuning fork — and the marsh *agreed*. He felt it the way you feel a door latch: something closing, softly, on the two of them. The reeds above bent and wove themselves into a roof of ordinary green. Their footprints behind them filled with water and forgot their shape. Even Wendell's own sense of where they were began to smudge, and he gripped Sef's hand tighter, anchoring himself to the one solid fact in the world.

Above the marsh, passing close enough that the reed-tops stirred though there was no wind, the Pale Choir walked on. Five white shapes against the last light. And as they passed, one of them — the tallest, the one whose hood held the most perfect nothing — stopped.

Turned its head. Or the place where a head would be.

And listened.

Wendell stopped breathing. Sef's grip crushed his fingers. The whole marsh held its stillness, two heartbeats hidden under a blanket of borrowed forgetting, while the faceless thing tasted the air above them for a gap in the world.

Ten seconds. Twenty. Long enough for Wendell to compose, absurdly, the eulogy for his own unlived life — *here lies a man whose name was never his, who owned everyone's yesterdays and none of his own* —

The figure moved on. The hymn faded toward the village, patient as tide, and began, very gently, to sing the valley's name.

Hollowmere's true name — the old one, the one under the maps — and as it sang, Wendell felt the valley itself begin to forget what it was.

He lay in the mud in the dark with a child's hand in his and a satchel of other people's pasts against his ribs, and he finally said the sentence he had been refusing to say since the border stones:

*The valley doesn't protect anyone. It never did. It only hides things — and tonight it hid me, and tomorrow it might not.*

Sef looked at him across the black water. And in a voice rusty with disuse, cracked with fear, in the dead tongue of a dead kingdom, the child spoke for the first time since arriving at his door — one soft phrase, repeated twice, urgent as a heartbeat.

Wendell didn't understand the words.

But the cracked vial in his satchel warmed against his chest, and deep in the mud beneath them, something ancient turned over in its sleep, listening — and far to the north, the hymn stopped mid-note.

Something had heard the child speak.
