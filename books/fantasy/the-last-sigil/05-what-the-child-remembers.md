## 5. What the Child Remembers

Sef remembers the bridges first.

That is how memory works now, after everything. It does not start with mother, though mother is at the middle of every memory like a stone at the middle of a fruit. It starts with the bridges, because the bridges were everywhere, and because they were beautiful, and because beautiful things are what break loudest.

The bridges of Vael were made of glass that was not really glass. Mother said it was song, cooled down until it forgot how to sing and just stood there holding people instead. You could see through them into the water below, and the water below held the sky, and so you walked on the sky twice, once under your feet and once over your head, and everyone in Vael walked this way all day long, on the sky both ways, and nobody thought it was strange.

Sef was small then. Small enough that the bridge-keepers carried a little brass bell and rang it when the fog came up off the river, so you would hold the rail. The rail sang under your hand. Not a tune. More like a cat purring, if a cat could purr a whole city.

Sef has not told anyone this. Sef cannot tell anyone anything. The words went away on the night the words burned, and what is left is a mouth full of ash-shaped silence. But inside, where no one can take it, Sef still walks the bridges every night before sleep, barefoot, because in Vael you could be barefoot all year, even in winter, because the bridges were warm. The bridges were warm because the whole kingdom was one song, and songs are warm if you are inside them.

* * *

Mother's name was Ileni, but she said names were only the handles of things, not the things themselves, and so she mostly called Sef *little lamp*. Because Sef glowed. That is what she said. She said it so many times that Sef believed it, and believing it may be the reason it came true, later, in the bad way.

Mother worked in the Vault of Names. This is what she did: when a Vaeli child was born, someone had to write the child's name into the kingdom, so the kingdom would know to hold them. Mother wrote names. Her pen never touched paper. She wrote them into the air, into the stone, into the bones of the person herself, and the writing did not look like writing. It looked like nothing. It sounded like nothing. But afterward you were written, and you belonged somewhere, and you could not fall out of the world.

She taught Sef the letters by drawing them on Sef's back with one finger while Sef pretended to sleep. She thought Sef didn't know what she was doing. Sef knew. Sef learned the whole alphabet from her finger before learning it from any book, and that is why, even now, with every living tongue gone quiet in Sef's mouth, the dead tongue sits in the chest like a second heart. Mother put it there. Mothers do that. They put languages in you the way they put bones in you, before you are born almost, before you can refuse them.

Some nights she sang. Not lullabies. She said a lullaby is a song for one child, but what she sang was a song for a whole nation, and she happened to start with her own child. The song had no name she would tell. She said naming it would make it smaller.

She sang it into Sef's collarbone first. Then the ribs, one rib a night, left side going down and right side coming up, like she was stitching a coat closed around a lantern. Sef asked her once — mouthed it, hands shaping the question in the dark — *what are you putting in me?*

And mother stopped singing, and looked at Sef a long time, with the lamp turned low, and said:

*I am putting the door in you. Doors have to live somewhere, little lamp. A door that lives in a book can be burned. A door that lives in a wall can be broken. But a door that lives in a person —*

She didn't finish. She never finished that sentence. Sef spent many nights after the ending trying to finish it for her, and the endings kept changing, and none of them were right.

* * *

Here is what the grown-ups said, in the last season, when they thought the children weren't listening.

They said the Choir was coming. They said the Choir had already eaten three kingdoms, but nobody remembered which ones, and that was the whole horror of it — the Choir ate the remembering too. A kingdom fell, and then the falling itself fell out of the world, and there was just a smooth place where a country used to be, like skin healed over a wound, and travelers walked across the smooth place and felt nothing at all.

They said the Choir did not hate anyone. That was worse. They said the Choir loved everything so much it wanted to keep it forever, and the only way to keep a thing forever was to take it out of time, and the taking-out was the unmaking, and the unmaking was sung. They said you could hear them a day before you saw them. They said the hymn sounded almost lovely, right up until it started naming things.

They said someone had stolen from the Choir. A fragment. A piece of the great spell, the one the Choir was building note by note across the world, the one that needed every kingdom's name sung in turn. Someone had taken the piece meant for Vael and hidden it, and hidden it well, and the Choir could not finish the song without it, and so the Choir was coming to find it.

Sef knows now what the grown-ups did not say out loud.

Nobody stole it. Mother was given it. Or mother took it, depending on how you tell it, and maybe those are the same thing when the alternative is everyone dying. The Last Sigil. A single syllable of the unmaking song, pulled out like a tooth from the hymn's jaw, and a syllable pulled from a spell is a door, and doors have to live somewhere.

Mother chose the somewhere.

Mother chose Sef.

* * *

The night the Choir came, the bells rang wrong.

Sef remembers that. All the bridge-bells in the city rang at once, but not their fog-rhythm, not the slow hold-the-rail pattern. They rang fast, all together, like a thousand hearts all startled at the same moment, and every bridge in Vael went cold for the first time in living memory, and people stood up from their dinners and looked at each other across tables and knew.

Mother did not scream. Sef watched for it and it never came. She moved through the house like water finding its level, gathering things that mattered — and here is the thing Sef cannot stop returning to, the thing that hurts in a new way every time: she gathered almost nothing. A knife. A flask of oil. And Sef.

Everything else she left. The house with its painted door. Sef's shoes by the mat. The bowl with tomorrow's breakfast already covered in cloth. She left it all like a woman stepping out of a story she intended to come back to, and Sef understands now that she was leaving it as an offering, as if tidiness might appease whatever was coming, as if the Choir might take the house and leave the rest.

From the high window you could see them coming down the river road. Pale figures, hooded, walking in step. They did not hurry. Hurrying, mother had said once, is for people who fear failure. They walked the way frost forms. And ahead of them, rolling out slow like spilled milk, came the sound.

The hymn.

It named things. That was the worst part, the part Sef will carry past the end of everything. It did not roar. It *named*. It sang the names of the river, and the river went silent. It sang the names of the eastern towers, and the towers did not fall, they simply became places where towers had never been decided on. The glass bridges began to lose their warmth one span at a time, going cool, going cold, going to ordinary dumb glass, going toward nothing.

Mother knelt in front of Sef in the doorway and put her forehead against Sef's forehead, the way she did every night, the way that meant *I am writing you again, you belong, you belong.*

Then she said, very quietly, in the dead tongue, the language she had stitched into Sef bone by bone:

*Sing it back to me.*

And Sef did. Sef opened the small scared mouth and sang the Sigil — the stolen syllable, the door, the thing that lived in the ribs now — back into mother's hands, and she caught it the way you catch a moth, gently, both palms cupped, and Sef felt it go, felt the ribs go hollow and ringing like a struck bell after the sound, and mother folded the syllable into a fold of her shawl and it vanished, and she smiled at Sef with the whole of her face, and said:

*Good. Now it can't find you. Now you're only a little lamp with nothing lit. Now run when I say, and don't stop, and whatever you hear behind you — do not answer it. Nothing that calls your name loves you. Remember that. Nothing that calls your name loves you.*

She said *run*, and Sef ran.

* * *

Sef does not remember running. Memory keeps that part dark, the way a hand goes dark over a candle. There is the doorway, and there is — after — a ditch full of reeds, alone, and the city behind glowing in a way cities should not glow, gold and white and getting whiter, and the hymn rising and rising and then, at the top of its rise, changing key.

It found the missing note.

Everyone always said it couldn't. Everyone said a spell with a hole in it stays broken forever. But the Choir stood in the burning center of Vael and sang around the absence, sang the shape of the gap, sang the hole itself, and the hole filled in with silence, and silence, it turns out, is also a note.

The kingdom did not burn down. Burning leaves ashes, and ashes remember fire. Vael went the other way. It got quieter and quieter and then it got *less*, and the light went out of the glass, and the bridges were just cold air over cold water, and then they were not, and then there was a valley where a country had been, and the valley did not know it was standing on anybody.

Sef lay in the reeds with wet mud in ears and mouth and watched the sky where the moon rose over a place that was no longer anywhere, and understood, in the body, in the bone-stitched alphabet way, what had been traded. Mother had taken the door out of Sef and stood in the gap of the song herself. She had become the missing note. She had let the hymn finish through her, so that it would finish *wrong*, so that it would close the kingdom shut like a fist instead of erasing it clean — buying, with the whole of Vael, one thing only:

One child, unwritten. Unfindable. A little lamp with nothing lit, carrying a door that could not be opened without a key the Choir did not have.

The key, Sef knows now — has always known, in the wordless way of the bones — is the rest of the song. The Sigil is not one syllable. It is one syllable *plus the person it was sung into*. Mother made sure of that. She stitched herself into the working alongside the kingdom. The door out of Vael does not open for power. It opens for grief, freely given, by blood that remembers.

Which means Sef is not a vessel. Sef wants the pale hooded men and the brokers and even the tall sad thief who took Sef in to understand this, someday, if mouths ever work again: a vessel gets poured out. A survivor carries. There is a difference, and the difference is everything, and the difference weighs as much as a drowned kingdom.

* * *

Now there is a man called Wendell, in a valley between stones that keep magic out, and he feeds Sef bread and eggs and does not ask questions, and his house is full of small bottles that whisper when Sef walks past them. He thinks Sef doesn't know what the bottles are. Sef knows. Bottles of other people's yesterdays — the man is a thief of the gentlest kind, stealing only the past, never the present, and Sef has decided this makes him almost honest, the way a dog that steals food is almost honest.

Sef lies on the pallet under the eaves of his crooked house and listens to him move around below, muttering, counting coins, being afraid in the particular way of a man who has just realized fear has finally found his address. Sef heard what he heard at the border. Hooded voices, crossing stones that should have turned them back. The valley's rules bending. The smooth healed-over places of the world starting, very softly, to hum.

Tonight the moon comes up nearly round through the little window. One more turning after this one. Sef can feel it in the ribs, the hollow bell-place mother left, keeping its own count. Thirteen. Everything ends or begins at thirteen.

And lying there, Sef does the bravest thing done since the reeds.

Sef opens the mouth that has held only silence since the burning, and aims it at the floorboards, at the man below, soft as breath, soft as fog on a warm bridge — and speaks. Three words of the dead tongue. *I remember you.* Words mother said every night. Words meant, somehow, already, for this stranger who owns everyone's past but his own.

Below, a bottle shivers against its shelf and rings, faintly, like a bridge-bell.

Footsteps stop. Then climb the stairs.
