## 6. The Thief's Own Theft

The vials lived in the floor.

Not under it — in it. Wendell had pried up three boards behind the woodstove years ago and hollowed out a cavity lined with felt, and there they slept: forty-one small glass phials stoppered with wax and cork, each one holding a memory he'd lifted from a traveler's mind at the border stones. He catalogued them the way other men catalogued coins. A fisherman's first storm. A bride's wedding morning. A soldier's last order, never obeyed. Forty-one lives he had borrowed without asking, and not one of them his own.

He counted them every night. It wasn't superstition. It was accounting. A thief who didn't know his inventory was already caught.

That night — the fourth since Sef had arrived at his door speaking a language that made the glass ache — the count came back wrong.

Forty-two.

Wendell stood over the open floor, candle in hand, and looked at the stranger among his treasures. It sat at the far end of the row, where the oldest memories lived, the ones he'd taken in his first years in Hollowmere before he'd learned to be careful, before he'd built his rules like a wall around himself. *Never from children. Never outside the valley. Never the same traveler twice.* The rules were how he'd stayed human, or something adjacent to it.

But this vial — this one he did not remember taking.

It was older than the others. The glass had gone faintly amber, the way old honey does, and the wax seal was cracked along one side as if it had been opened once, long ago, and resealed by someone in a hurry. Or by someone who wanted to forget that they'd opened it.

He crouched and lifted it out. Cold. Colder than glass had any right to be in a room with a banked fire. And when his fingers closed around it, the other forty-one shivered in their felt bed — a sound like wind through winter reeds, like the noise Sef's dead tongue made when the child murmured in sleep.

"Where did you come from?" he said aloud. His voice sounded thin in the one-room house. Sef was asleep in the loft above, breathing slow, and Wendell kept his voice low anyway. Some things you don't say loud near a sleeping child who carries a spell inside their bones.

There was only one way to know what was in the vial, and he hated it, because it meant doing to himself exactly what he did to travelers on the road. He uncorked vials with a silver needle — touched it to the wax, drew the memory up into the metal, then pressed the needle to his own temple and let the memory pour in. It was an intimate violence. You became someone else for a minute or an hour, wore their grief like a coat, and then took the coat off and went about your night. He had done it hundreds of times. To others.

Never to himself.

His hands were steady. That surprised him. He'd have expected them to shake.

* * *

The needle went in cold and came out burning.

He was standing in a street he had never walked, and the street was on fire.

Not burning the way hollows burn — greedy, orange, ordinary. This fire was white. It burned without heat and without smoke and it ate the color out of everything it touched. Buildings of pale stone and glass — actual glass, whole walls of it, panes taller than two men — stood glowing like lanterns with the light going out of them. Bridges arced overhead between towers, bridges made of the same glass, and people were running across them, and where the white fire reached them they didn't scream. They simply grew fainter. Like ink in rain. Like something being erased rather than killed.

A city dying of forgetting.

Wendell knew — in the way you know things in dreams, without learning them — that this memory was not stolen from anyone else. There was no second mind behind these eyes. No stranger's fear wearing him like a glove. This was first-person. This was *his*. The street tilted the way streets tilt when your own body is running down them, and his breath came ragged in his own chest, and his own arms were full of —

Glass. A case no bigger than a bread loaf, sealed in lead, heavy as sin. He clutched it against his ribs and ran, and the weight of it was so familiar that his shoulders ached around a shape they'd held for years.

Behind him, the white fire found the glass bridges, and the bridges began to forget themselves mid-span. People fell into nothing. Not into a river, not onto stone. Into *nothing*, and the nothing closed over them like water.

And a woman's voice — ahead of him now, not behind, she was ahead of him calling back over her shoulder, her hair coming loose from its braids, her face lit by the light that unmade — shouted a name.

"*Corven!*"

The name hit him like a thrown stone.

"*Corven, run!*"

He knew her. That was the vertigo of it, the floor-of-the-world-dropping-out of it. He knew the exact angle of her jaw, the way her left eye turned slightly when she was frightened, the smell of her — cold iron and some flower he couldn't name because the name had been scrubbed out of the world along with everything else. He loved her. The memory said so. The memory said it with the flat certainty of a thing that has no need to argue, the way a scar doesn't argue about whether it happened.

She pressed something into his hands — into *his* hands, Wendell's hands, Corven's hands, the same hands — and her mouth formed words the fire was already eating: "*Take it through the door. Don't look back. Whatever you hear, don't—*"

Then the whiteness took her.

Not all at once. From the edges. Her fingers went translucent first, still reaching toward him, still shaped around the ghost of whatever she'd given, and her eyes stayed on his until the very end — and there was no terror in them. That was the worst part. There was only a terrible, specific trust, the kind you can only put in someone you have known long enough to be certain of.

She trusted him to run.

He ran.

* * *

He came back to himself on the floor beside the open boards, the amber vial loose in his grip, the candle guttered down to a thumb's height, and he did not remember falling.

Sef was awake. Of course Sef was awake. The child stood at the top of the ladder to the loft, wrapped in the blanket Wendell had bought off a tinker two winters back, watching him with those too-old eyes. In the dark they couldn't see much — Wendell made sure of that — but children see more than we build rooms for.

"You cried out," Sef said. Not in Vaeli. In valley-speech, which the child had been picking up with frightening speed, word by word, like a magpie building a nest.

"I didn't."

"You said a name." Sef came down the ladder without hurry, the way the child did everything, as if the world had already ended once and there was no sense rushing through the sequel. "You said it wrong. You said it like a stranger."

Wendell sat up. His whole body ached, deep-muscle aches, the ache of having run a long way in a dream. The vial was warm now in his palm, warming as though ashamed of its earlier cold.

"What name?"

Sef considered him for a long moment. Then the child spoke — one word, two syllables, in the dead tongue, and the sound of it went through Wendell like the needle had gone through the wax. He didn't need a translation. Some words carry their meaning in the bone.

*Corven.*

"How do you know that?" His voice came out wrong. Scraped. "How do you know that word?"

"It's on you." Sef touched their own sternum, then pointed at his. "Under. Under everything. I heard it the first night. When you opened the door." The child's brow furrowed, searching for valley-words and finding most of them missing. "Everyone has a sound. Under. Most people are quiet underneath. You are not quiet. You are—" the child made a gesture, both hands opening outward like a door swinging wide, "—a big empty place. With one word in the middle of it."

"A big empty place," Wendell repeated.

"With a door in it." Sef pointed at him again, then, slowly, at the vial in his hand. "And that is a key to the empty place. It was always yours. It was waiting for you to be brave enough."

"I'm not brave."

"No," Sef agreed, with devastating mildness, and climbed back up to the loft.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat at the table with the vial standing in front of him like a witness, and he did what he did best, which was take apart a thing to see how it worked.

Here were the facts, laid out like tools on leather.

Fact: the memory was his own. First-person, no seams, no borrowed edges. Every stolen memory he owned had fingerprints on it that weren't his — the little distortions where another mind's attention had lingered or skipped. This one had none. It fit him like his own skin.

Fact: he did not remember stealing it, because he hadn't. You couldn't steal from yourself. Which meant it had been *sealed*. Someone had taken this out of his head — or he had — and corked it away, and the seal was cracked, and cracks let things leak, and maybe it had been leaking into him for years, drop by drop, which might explain the dreams he'd never mentioned to anyone. The glass bridges he sometimes saw when he blinked. The feeling, on certain nights, that his own name was a coat hung on a hook just out of reach.

Fact: Vael. The child's dead kingdom, the one that was not on any map, the one whose language no living person should speak. The city in the memory had glass bridges. Ossa — no. He hadn't met Ossa yet; that name would come later. But Marench had gone gray as ash when Sef sang, and the broker had said one sentence before Wendell walked out: *"They burned the whole country out of the books, Vane. Books don't burn that hot unless someone was very afraid."*

The city in his memory was Vael. The day Vael died.

And he had been there. Running. Carrying a lead-sealed case full of something heavy enough to matter, while a woman who loved him burned herself into nothing so he could get away.

He thought about the Sigil. The word Marench had used, half-whispered, half-laughing, the way men say dangerous things to make them smaller. *The Last Sigil.* The fragment of the spell that unmade Vael, still unfinished, still hungry, riding inside a mute child who had appeared at a thief's door on a moonless night.

If the Sigil was a fragment of the unmaking — then someone had broken it into fragments. And fragments get carried. Hidden. Smuggled out of dying cities by whoever loves you enough to stay behind and hold the door.

The case in the memory. Heavy as sin. Sealed in lead.

"What did you give me?" he asked the empty room, in the woman's direction, in the direction of twenty years ago and a country that wasn't there. "What did you make me carry?"

The vial sat on the table and said nothing, but its silence had a shape now, and the shape was an answer: *you know.*

He knew.

He was not a man who had stumbled into this story. He was not a bystander the storm had chanced to find. He had run out of a burning nation with a piece of the thing that was killing it in his arms, and somewhere between there and here he had done the one theft he'd never counted — the theft of himself. He had taken his own past, sealed it in amber glass, and buried it under the floorboards of a valley that forgot, and then he had spent twenty years stealing other people's memories like a man trying to fill a hole he refused to look at.

The hunter had been running from the hunt since before the hunt began.

Wendell put his head in his hands. Above him, Sef's breathing had gone slow again, trusting, and the sound of it was unbearable, because now he understood why the child had come to *him*. Out of all the doors in Hollowmere. Out of all the hollow men in a valley full of them. Something in the child — that strange hearing, that listening-under-everything — had found the one word buried in him and steered by it.

He hadn't sheltered a stray. He'd been found by his own unfinished errand.

Outside, far off toward the border stones, something began to sing.

It was very faint. It could have been wind. It was not wind. It was many voices in perfect, terrible unison, and they were singing a hymn in no language Wendell knew, and yet he understood every word of it, because he had heard that song once before — from inside a burning city, through a woman's fading eyes.

They were singing names. Names of erased things. And one of the names, threaded soft through the verses like a wire through wax, was his.

Not *Wendell Vane.*

*Corven.*

The candle went out. In the dark, the forty-two vials shivered together in the floor like teeth, and Wendell Vane — Corven — reached down and closed the boards over them, gently, the way you close a coffin, and began to pack.
