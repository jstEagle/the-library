## 7. The Road Out of Nowhere

The border stones of Hollowmere did not look like much. That was the point of them.

Seven gray pillars in a crooked line across the western pass, each one taller than a man and older than the valley's oldest lie, their faces worn smooth as river pebbles. Travelers walked past them every day without a glance, because to glance was to wonder, and to wonder was dangerous. The stones kept the valley empty of magic the way a good lock keeps a house empty of thieves — quietly, absolutely, and only so long as nobody tested them.

Wendell Vane had spent eleven years not testing them.

Now he stood in the bracken above the pass with a child's hand wrapped in his own and watched the sun go down behind the stones like a coin dropping into a well, and understood that he was about to test everything he had built his life on never testing.

"They'll feel it," he said. It wasn't the first time he'd said it. He said it again anyway, because saying it aloud was cheaper than thinking it. "The Choir. The moment we cross, they'll feel us."

Sef looked up at him. The child hadn't spoken since the marshes — hadn't spoken at all, really, except in that dead tongue that burned cold in the air — but the look said everything words would have wasted breath on. *Then let them feel.*

"You understand there's no coming back." Wendell crouched, bringing his face level with the child's, because this was the part that mattered, the part he needed Sef to carry the way Sef carried the Sigil: somewhere deep, somewhere unstealable. "I've lived my whole life inside those stones. I know every road, every roof, every face worth robbing. Out there—" He gestured west, where the light was dying into country he had no map for. "Out there I'm nothing. No name, no tricks, no valley keeping me safe. Just a man and a kid walking into a world that eats men like me."

Sef's grip tightened on his hand. Small fingers. Stubborn as roots.

Wendell exhaled through his teeth. "Right," he said. "Well. Nothing new about being a fool."

* * *

They came down through the bracken as the last light failed, moving the way Wendell had taught himself to move in eleven years of thieving — weight on the outside edges of the feet, breath shallow, eyes reading the dark in slices rather than staring straight into it. Not that it mattered. There were no watchers on the pass. Hollowmere had never needed watchers; the stones did all the watching anyone could want.

Up close, the border stones hummed. Not a sound exactly — more a pressure behind the ears, the way a held note lives in the walls of a room after the singer stops. Wendell had heard it his whole life the way you hear your own heartbeat, which is to say he'd stopped hearing it at all. Tonight it rang loud as a bell.

He stopped an arm's length from the nearest stone and made himself look at it properly for the first time since childhood. Someone had carved it once — you could see the ghost of lines under the weathering, spirals within spirals, a pattern that hurt to follow with the eye because it seemed to turn when you weren't looking directly at it. Ward-work. Old, old ward-work, older than any kingdom Wendell knew the names of, and cut by hands that had wanted one thing only: a hole in the world where magic couldn't reach.

A safe place. That was what Hollowmere was. A place where nothing could burn you down with a word, where no sorcerer's hymn could find you, where memory stayed put in the heads that owned it. He'd told himself that story for eleven years. Safe. Quiet. His.

Standing here now, with the Choir's hymn still crawling in his ears like something under his skin, he saw the story plain for what it was: a wall is also a cage, and everyone who ever praised the wall was standing on the inside of it.

"Stay close," he murmured, and stepped forward.

Nothing happened.

That was the joke of it — he'd braced for thunder, for the stones to cry out, for some great door to swing shut behind him forever, and instead it was just grass, just gravel, just his own boots crossing an invisible line in the dirt between one stride and the next. One step he was a thief in Hollowmere. Next step he was —

Magic touched him.

There was no other way to say it. Later he would try to describe it to himself a hundred times and fail every time, but in the moment it was simple and total, like being born. The world *came alive*. Every blade of grass in the pass lit up with its own small green attention. The wind wasn't air anymore; it was a current, and things moved in it — vast slow presences turning over beneath the hills like whales under a boat, and smaller bright ones stitching through the grass, and above it all a pressure, a *regard*, as if the sky itself had noticed him for the first time in his life and found him interesting.

Wendell made a sound. He was ashamed of it later — some small cracked noise in the back of his throat, half gasp, half laugh — and he staggered, and Sef caught his hand harder and held him up.

Because it was awful. That was the thing he hadn't been ready for. All those years inside the stones, he'd imagined magic the way the valley imagined it: a story, a rumor, a warmth. Nobody told him it would be *loud*. Nobody told him it would press against him like deep water, that every stolen memory in his chest would suddenly feel like coins in a pocket with a hole in it, rattling, loose, visible. The vials he kept — the sea, the wedding, the burning city, all forty-odd years of other people's lives he carried like a miser carries gold — he felt them all at once, felt them straining toward the living world around him as if recognizing kin.

And underneath the awfulness, worse: it was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever felt, and he had once owned the memory of the sea.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that's —"

Sef was smiling. First time. A small, fierce, gap-toothed smile aimed up at him, and Wendell realized the child felt none of the dread — or felt it and didn't care. To Sef, this was home. This was what the world was supposed to taste like. The valley had been the wrongness; this was the correction.

"Come on, then," Wendell managed, voice rough. "Before I change my mind."

* * *

They walked through the dark and the dark walked with them.

By full night the initial shock had worn down to something he could function inside, though functioning was generous — it was like learning to walk on the deck of a ship. Magic soaked into everything out here. He learned fast, because he was a thief and thieves learn surfaces quickly, which things were merely things and which things were *watching*. An old rowan at the pass's foot was watching, in a patient vegetable way. A stream they forded had opinions. Once, crossing a fallow field, Wendell felt something enormous pass overhead in the upper dark — not a bird, not weather — and he pulled Sef flat into the stubble and lay there with his heart hammering until the presence slid away westward, unhurried, incurious.

The Choir. Or one of theirs. Or something older that even the Choir answered to. He didn't know, and not knowing was a sensation he hated with his whole body.

What he knew was this: the hymn they'd sung in the valley square three nights ago had named things, and the things it named had come apart. Reed and rope and the broker Marench's front window, gone like breath off glass. And now they were out here, in the choir-loft of the world, two small named things — *the thief*, *the vessel* — walking west through country that could hear itself think.

"We need distance," he told Sef, and Sef nodded, and they walked faster.

* * *

Near midnight they crested a long bare ridge, and Wendell stopped, because behind them the land had changed shape.

Hollowmere sat in its bowl of hills like water in a cup, and he had always known its borders the way you know the edges of your own skin. But seeing it from outside — truly outside, from the high ground where the valley became a thing among things — undid him in a way the crossing hadn't. From up here it was small. It was a handful of roofs and lamplight and the pale scatter of the border stones, seven gray teeth in a dark jaw, holding back nothing now that he'd stepped over. Smoke rose from Marench's quarter in a thin straight line. Somewhere down there was his cottage with its shelves of vials, each one stoppered and labeled in his neat thief's hand: *sea, storm, first love, mother's voice.* Forty years of other people's lives, sitting in the dark waiting for him.

He would never see them again. He understood that with a strange calm. You didn't run from the Pale Choir and come home to collect your things. Home was a door that locked behind you, and he had chosen the running.

Eleven years. He'd arrived with nothing — that was the official story, the one he told travelers when they asked, the one he'd told himself so long it had calluses. Arrived with nothing, built a trade, built rules, built a life the size of a coffin and called it peace. And the truth was the truth he'd drunk around and slept around for eleven years: he couldn't remember arriving. Couldn't remember before. His own history started mid-sentence, a man waking on the south road with a talent for theft and a head full of gaps, and he had made his peace with that the way you make peace with a limp — by never looking down at it.

From the ridge, with magic singing in his blood for the first time, the limp was all he could feel. Whatever he'd been before Hollowmere, it belonged to this side of the stones. Whatever had erased him — and some animal part of him, the part that had flinched at the burning city in his oldest vial, knew it had been *erased* and not merely forgotten — whatever had done that lived out here too.

Maybe the same people who were hunting them now.

"Keep walking," he said softly, to himself as much as to the child, and turned his back on the lamplight.

* * *

Dawn found them in a birch wood, silver-trunked and dripping, and Wendell allowed them an hour's rest because exhaustion made thieves sloppy and he refused to be sloppy. He built no fire. They ate hard bread and a wedge of cheese bought with the last of his honest coin — a phrase that described roughly four coins of his acquaintance — and Sef fell asleep sitting up against a birch trunk, mid-chew, the way children do when the body simply closes the shop.

Wendell sat watching the light come up through the leaves and took stock like a man counting a purse.

Assets: one child, asleep, carrying the last fragment of a spell that could finish erasing a kingdom. One knife. One talent that worked better out here than it ever had inside the stones — he could feel that already, could feel memories hanging off the sleeping world like ripe fruit, and the knowledge sat in him warm and shameful, the old engine turning over at the smell of fuel. One name — *Vane*, a made-up name, a thief's name — and behind it a locked room he was increasingly afraid to open.

Debts: the Pale Choir, who sang things out of existence. Marench, who had sold them both for the price of whatever the Choir paid brokers for children. And whatever debt his own erased past owed to the burning city, the woman's voice, the name he didn't remember answering to.

He looked at Sef — cheek smudged with ash, fists curled even in sleep, guarding something invisible — and felt that unwilling protectiveness again, the one that had ambushed him the first night at his door and hadn't let go since. He'd spent his whole life taking. Taking memories, taking coin, taking the easy exits. And here he was walking away from the only home he'd ever known to deliver a mute child to a door out of a dead kingdom, armed with bread crumbs and bad judgment.

If the Choir caught them, they wouldn't kill Sef. That was the thing he kept circling back to, the thing that made his hands cold. Killing was quick; killing left a body, a grief, a grave. The Choir didn't kill. They *unnamed*. They would sing the child out of the world's memory, and every person who had ever seen that small stubborn face would wake tomorrow with a soft blank place where Sef had been, and no one would mourn, because mourning requires a name to hang the grief on.

And no one — this was the part that made Wendell stand up and shoulder the pack and nudge the child awake with a gentleness that surprised them both — no one would even know there had been anything to forgive themselves for.

"Not today," he said.

Sef blinked up at him, sleep-heavy, questioning.

"Nobody gets unnamed today," Wendell said. "I've decided. I decide things now. It's a whole new life."

Something that might have been a laugh flickered in the child's eyes — there and gone, quick as a fish — and then Sef was up, brushing off leaf-mold, hand finding Wendell's without being asked, and they went on westward while the wood filled up with ordinary morning birdsong, each note carrying faintly, sweetly, impossibly alive.

* * *

They smelled the inn before they saw anything else: woodsmoke, and under it something frying, and under *that* — Wendell stopped dead in the middle of the track, nostrils flaring, thief's instincts ringing like a struck wire.

Under all of it, old paper. Ink. The dry dust smell of maps.

Which was impossible, because there was no inn here. There was no *here* here — he'd have sworn it, an hour ago, standing on the road that ran through empty moorland with nothing ahead but heather and sky. But the road bent where no road should bend, around a hill that hadn't been there a moment ago, and at the bend stood a low stone building with smoke rising from its chimney and a sign swinging in the windless air.

Wendell read the sign twice, because the first time he assumed his eyes had been damaged by the crossing.

**THE INN AT THE END OF NAMES**, it said, in letters that rearranged themselves slightly whenever he wasn't looking directly at them.

Below it, smaller, in a different hand: *Travelers who have lost a place may inquire within.*

Sef tugged his hand. Pointed. Looked up with an expression Wendell was beginning to know well — the expression that meant the child knew something and was deciding how much of it to spend.

"That's not possible," Wendell said. "Inns don't just — places don't—"

But even as he said it he could feel the truth of it humming in the new-lit world around him, the way he'd felt the rowan tree watch and the stream opine. Some places, out here, existed because someone believed in them hard enough. And a building at the end of all names, on the edge of a kingdom that had been sung out of the world—

The door opened. A woman stood in it, broad-shouldered, gray-stubbled scalp, one sleeve pinned neatly at the shoulder where an arm used to be, regarding them with the level gaze of someone who had been expecting them for years and was mildly annoyed they'd taken so long.

"Well," she said. "You'd be the ones carrying Vael."

She spoke the dead tongue's sister-word like it was nothing. Like it was a greeting.

Wendell's hand found his knife out of pure habit. The woman watched him do it and smiled the smile of a cartographer who has just confirmed a coastline.

Inside, she said, "we should talk about the moon."
