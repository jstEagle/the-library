## 8. The Inn at the End of Names

The road out of nowhere had a way of making you doubt your own feet.

Wendell had been walking since the last grey light of dusk, Sef's small hand tucked in his, and the land around them had grown stranger with every mile — hills that seemed to lean in when he wasn't looking, trees whose shadows fell the wrong direction, a river they crossed twice without ever turning back. Magic clung to him like wet wool. He could feel it now, the way a man who'd been deaf all his life feels sound: overwhelming, painful, impossible to ignore. Out here beyond the border stones, everything hummed.

Sef hadn't spoken since the marshes. Not in Vaeli, not in anything. The child just walked, and watched the road behind them more than the road ahead, and held on to Wendell's hand hard enough to leave marks.

"You're crushing my fingers," Wendell said, not meaning it as a complaint.

Sef didn't loosen the grip.

It was full dark when the inn appeared. That was the only word for it. One moment there was nothing on the moor but gorse and wind and a sky bruised purple with stars, and the next moment there was light — warm, gold, window-shaped light — spilling across the grass maybe two hundred yards ahead.

"That wasn't there," Wendell said aloud, because saying it made it slightly less mad.

He stopped. He'd survived Hollowmere for eleven years by trusting his suspicion more than his hunger, and every instinct he owned was telling him that an inn which materialized out of empty moorland was either a trap or a miracle, and that traps dressed as miracles were the oldest kind.

But Sef was pulling him forward. The child had seen the light and something in that small face had changed — a loosening around the eyes, like a fist unclenching after days. Sef tugged again, insistent.

"All right." Wendell exhaled. "All right. But we watch first. We always watch."

They crouched in the gorse at the edge of the inn's yard and watched. It was a low stone building, older than any architecture Wendell knew, with a roof of turf gone green and wildflowers growing from the eaves. A sign hung over the door on iron brackets, but the letters on it wouldn't hold still — he looked directly at them and they slid away like fish, so that he caught only fragments. *End.* *Names.* Something else that might have been *of.*

There were horses in the small stable. Smoke rose from the chimney. Through the windows he saw shapes moving, unhurried, ordinary. A woman carrying plates. Someone feeding wood to a fire.

"Nobody's posted lookout," Wendell murmured. "Nobody's watching the road. Either they've got nothing to hide, or they're very good."

Sef stood up out of the gorse and started walking toward the door.

"Sef—" Wendell scrambled after, caught the child's shoulder, turned them around. "We don't know these people."

Sef looked up at him. In the light from the windows, Wendell saw something he hadn't seen before in that face: recognition. Not fear, not hope exactly. Recognition, deep and aching, the way you look at a thing you lost a long time ago and had stopped believing existed.

Then Sef spoke. One word. Vaeli, soft and certain, aimed at the inn itself.

And the door opened.

* * *

Inside smelled of woodsmoke, tallow, stewed onions, and — Wendell's breath actually caught — old paper. Ink and vellum and the particular dusty sweetness of maps rolled tight for years. His stolen memories stirred in their case against his ribs like birds feeling weather. Somewhere in this building were things that remembered places.

"Sit where you like," said the woman with the plates. She was broad, grey-streaked, unbothered by the arrival of a gaunt man and a mute child out of the night. "Stew's a copper, bread comes free if you don't waste it. Beds are two silvers and I don't want to hear about the lumps."

Wendell sat with his back to the wall, Sef pressed close beside him, and counted the room. Three travelers at a corner table playing dice, too absorbed to look up. An old man asleep by the fire with a dog across his boots. No one who moved like a Choir cantor, no one wearing the pale grey robes, no one whose attention lingered too long. The woman — the innkeeper, presumably — brought their stew without asking what they wanted and left without asking their names.

"Where are we?" Wendell asked her quietly when she passed again.

"Here," she said.

"I mean—"

"I know what you mean." She set down a second spoon for Sef, though the child already had one. "This is the inn at the end of names. It sits where things stop being called anything. You'll find it or you won't. Nobody finds it twice unless it wants them back." She looked at Sef then, and her face did something complicated. "Eat," she told the child, gently, in a tone Wendell recognized — the tone of someone speaking to a survivor of something. Then she was gone into the kitchen.

Sef ate. Wendell ate too, mechanically, tasting almost nothing, one ear on the room. It was only when the bowls were empty and the fire had burned down to a red murmur that the cartographer came out of the back room.

She came through the doorway sideways, out of habit, and Wendell understood why before he understood anything else about her: she had one arm. The right sleeve of her coat was pinned and folded neat as a folded wing. What remained of her left arm was inked from wrist to shoulder in lines so fine they looked, at first, like shadow — and then he realized what he was looking at and nearly dropped his spoon.

Maps. Her skin was covered in maps. Coastlines crawled down her forearm; mountain ranges ridged her knuckles; a river delta spread across her collarbone and vanished under her shirt. Roads stitched her wrist. Some of the ink was black, some faded blue, some a strange silver that seemed to move when he wasn't looking straight at it.

She carried a lamp in her single hand and a leather roll under her remaining elbow, and she crossed the room to their table without hesitation, the way people cross toward the thing they came for.

"You," she said, standing over them. Her voice was dry and precise. "You came off the moor tonight. Off the *empty* moor. And the child spoke to my door."

Wendell put his body slightly between hers and Sef's. "It's late. We're just passing through."

"No one passes through here. That's the entire point of here." She set the lamp down. Light moved across her mapped skin; a coastline glinted. "I heard the word the child said. I have spent thirty years collecting dead words, sir, and I know perhaps four hundred of them, and I know what that one was. It means *shelter* in a language that has been dead for longer than most kingdoms live. It means shelter in Vaeli."

The name went through the room like cold water. At the dice table, one of the players fumbled and the dice scattered. Even the sleeping man's dog lifted its head.

"Keep your voice down," Wendell said.

"Why? Half this room can't hear it, and the half that can has been waiting twenty years for someone to say it." She pulled out a chair — awkwardly, one-handed, with a practiced economy that told him she'd been doing it a long time — and sat down uninvited. Up close, Wendell could see her eyes were the grey of pencil lead, and tired, and sharp as a compass point. "My name is Ossa. I make maps of places that no longer exist. Which is to say, I am the world's busiest cartographer and also its least employed."

Wendell said nothing. Beside him, Sef had gone very still, staring at the woman's inked arm.

"You're wondering how I found you," Ossa said.

"I'm wondering what you want. People who find you are either selling something or taking something. Sometimes both."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile. "Eleven years in Hollowmere teaches a man that, does it?"

His whole body went rigid. "How do you know—"

"I don't. You just told me." Now the smile came, thin and dry. "You said *eleven years* like a man quoting a sentence. And you flinch at the word *sell*. And you keep one hand on that satchel the way a miser keeps a hand on coin. Memory-thief. I thought so the moment you sat down. You have the look of men who own other people's lives." She leaned back. "Don't reach for whatever's in that case. I'm not interested. Stolen memories are worthless to me. I deal in geography, not grief."

"The Pale Choir deals in both," Wendell said quietly.

At the name, Ossa went still in a different way. The tiredness in her face settled deeper, became something older. "Yes," she said. "They do." She looked at Sef for a long moment. "And they're coming for this child. Aren't they."

It wasn't a question. Wendell found he couldn't lie to her, and hated that. "Three nights ago they crossed the border stones. Nothing crosses those stones. They sang a hymn while they did it, and the stones let them, and I have been running since."

"They'll have marked you both by now. Their songs stick." She turned her left arm over, studied the map inked there — a coastline, worn faint with age. "I lost this arm to the Choir, if you're wondering. Twenty-two years ago. There was a place called Callowmere. Lovely little country. Marshes, glassmakers, a language with nine words for fog. The Choir decided it was redundant. I drew its maps. I was the last person alive who could have drawn its maps, and they wanted the place forgotten completely, and my arm was holding the pen." She said it flatly, the way you recite something you've said so many times it's worn smooth. "So. Yes. I know what they do. And I know what that child is."

"You don't," Wendell said.

"Don't I?" She reached out — slowly, telegraphing every inch of the movement, letting Wendell see her open palm — and touched the table between them, and began, with one finger, to trace a shape in the spilled salt near Sef's bowl. A circle. Inside it, lines radiating outward like a wheel, like a sun, like—

Sef made a sound. Small. Broken. Both hands flew to their own chest.

Ossa withdrew her finger immediately. "I thought so," she said softly. "That's the Sigil. Or part of it. Drawn from memory, and my memory is forty years old and secondhand." She met Wendell's eyes. "You're carrying the Last Sigil of Vael across the world in the body of a child, and you're doing it with a memory-broker's satchel and a thief's instincts, and you haven't the faintest idea where you're going. Am I wrong?"

The fire popped. The dice players had given up pretending not to listen.

"We're going away from them," Wendell said. "That's a direction."

"It's not. Away from the Choir is everywhere. They're not chasing you across space, memory-thief. They're chasing you across time. The moon turns thirteen times, and on the thirteenth turn—" She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. Looked at Sef again, and something in her expression shifted, softened by a degree, the way ice shifts before it becomes water. "No. Not here. Not in front of the child. Come to the back room."

"Why would I trust you?" Wendell asked. It came out rougher than he intended. "Everyone sells. Brokers sell memories. The Choir sells salvation. You'll sell us a map and be no different."

Ossa stood, gathered her lamp and her roll of leather with her one arm, and looked down at him with those pencil-grey eyes.

"Twenty-two years ago," she said, "I sold a map. A man paid me well for the location of Callowmere's last refuge, where its scholars were hiding, and I took the money because I needed to eat, and three weeks later the Choir sang that refuge into nothing, and everyone in it, including a girl who used to bring me bread." She flexed her empty sleeve. "I've never sold another thing in my life. Everything I've drawn since, I've given away. Ask anyone in any erased country's diaspora — if such a thing exists — ask the ghosts. They'll tell you the one-armed mapmaker works for free and hates it." She turned toward the back room. "Come or don't. But come soon. The moon is waxing, and I count better than I sleep."

* * *

The back room was paper. It was floor to ceiling, wall to wall, rolls and flats and drawers and maps pinned thick as feathers on every surface — coastlines of countries Wendell had never heard of, cities rendered in loving detail that he somehow knew had no living counterpart anywhere on earth. A kingdom of bridges built entirely of colored glass, drawn so precisely he could see the individual panes. A capital city on a lake, terraced and white-walled, its streets labeled in flowing script.

In Vaeli.

"This one," Ossa said, following his gaze, "took me six years. I finished it four years after the Choir finished the country. Every street from memory. Every bridge." Her voice was steady. Her single hand, resting on the map's edge, was trembling. "I have never once shown it to anyone who could read the labels. Until tonight."

Behind him, Wendell heard Sef's breath change.

He turned. The child was standing in front of the map, head tilted back, and tears were running down Sef's face silently, steadily, the way rain runs down glass — no sobbing, no sound at all, just water, as if grief had become simply a function of the body, like breathing.

Sef raised one hand and touched the painted glass bridges.

Then the child spoke. Quietly. Fluently. A whole sentence of Vaeli, addressed to the map, and Wendell — who had spent his life stealing other people's languages and understanding none of them — felt the words go through the room like wind through wheat.

Ossa answered.

Not fluently. Slowly, haltingly, her accent terrible, her grammar assembled like a child stacking blocks. But she answered. In the dead tongue. Four hundred words, she'd said, and she spent them like a miser spending her last coins, and Sef stared at her as though the sky had opened.

"What did you say?" Wendell asked. "What did either of you say?"

"I said," Ossa replied, not looking away from the child, "that I never saw Vael. That I only know it from other people's remembering. And the child said—" her voice caught, just barely, just for a moment, "—the child said, *then I will remember it for you.*"

Silence. The lamp hissed. Outside, far off across the moor, something that might have been wind and might not have been carried a long, thin note — almost like singing.

Ossa's head came up sharply. She crossed to the shuttered window and put her eye to the gap, and Wendell saw her whole mapped body go still.

"How long," she said, "has that been following you?"

"Since the marshes. I think. I hoped it was birds."

"It's not birds." She turned from the window, and the dry precision of her voice cracked open onto something harder underneath. "Get away from the shutters. Both of you. And sit down, memory-thief, because you're going to need the whole story, and the story starts with the moon."

She began pulling maps from the wall — fast, decisive, one-handed, laying them across the great table in overlapping sheets until the surface vanished beneath kingdoms that did not exist.

"On the thirteenth turn," Ossa said, "the moon finishes what the Choir started. And there is exactly one road in the world that leads out of a country that has been erased — and I am the only person alive who knows where it begins."

Far off across the dark moor, the thin note came again. Closer now. Almost patient.

Like something that knew exactly where they were, and was content to wait for the turn.
