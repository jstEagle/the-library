## 9. Thirteen Turns

Ossa had drawn maps for kings who paid in gold and smugglers who paid in blood, and she had learned to read a customer before she ever unrolled the leather. The tall one with the thief's hands and the hollow eyes — he was easy. Men like him wanted a road out of trouble. But it was the child she couldn't stop watching.

The child sat on the bench by the inn's cold hearth with her knees pulled up, watching Ossa's remaining hand as if the fingers themselves were speaking. And when Ossa said the word — *Vael*, just once, testing it — the child flinched the way a body flinches when an old wound is pressed.

Not surprise. Recognition.

Ossa set down her cup. "Say it again," she told the tall one. "What she said at your door. All of it."

"She doesn't say much of anything," Wendell Vane said. "That's rather the point of her."

"Then sing it. You said she sang."

"I didn't say that either." His jaw worked. He was deciding how many truths to spend, and Ossa recognized that arithmetic because she'd done it herself in a hundred border towns: *how much do I tell this stranger, and what will the telling cost me?* "One line," he said finally. "She sang one line, the first night, and my vials shook on their shelf. Every one of them. Like something trying to get out."

Ossa closed her eye. Opened it again. Some things you needed to see plainly or not at all.

"Child," she said, turning to the girl. "My name is Ossa. I make maps of places that don't exist anymore. That's not poetry — it's the trade. When a place is erased, it leaves a scar, and scars have shape, and shape can be charted. I've mapped eleven dead countries. There were twelve." She let that sit in the smoky air of the inn, where no map had ever hung and no name had ever been asked for. "You're the twelfth. Or what's left of it."

The child looked at her without fear. That was the thing that undid Ossa, later, when she thought about it. Most people, hearing about erased nations, went pale or laughed or reached for superstition. This child just listened, the way you listen to someone describing your own house.

Then Sef opened her mouth and sang.

It was four notes. Maybe five. The fire in the hearth — cold all evening, because the inn's keeper fed it only when paid — guttered upright as if remembering flame. The tankards on the bar hummed against their wood. And inside Ossa's chest, in the place where her missing arm still ached on wet mornings, something answered. Something old. Something that knew the tune from the other side of its own death.

She gripped the table until the wood creaked.

"*Don't*," she said, too sharp, and the song stopped, and Wendell's hand came down on the girl's shoulder like a shutter closing, and for a moment the two men-and-a-child of them faced her across the table like enemies.

"No," Ossa said, softer. "I'm sorry. Not don't ever — just not here, not yet. Do you know what you're carrying, thief?"

"I know what everyone tells me she's carrying. A sigil. The last piece of some spell." He said it like a man reciting rumors he didn't believe, which meant he believed them completely. "The Choir wants it. The broker sold them our direction. Beyond that—"

"Beyond that," Ossa said, "you've been walking around with a lit fuse and no idea there's a candle anywhere near it." She pushed back her bench and stood. "Come to the back room. Both of you. What I have to show you doesn't go on display."

* * *

The back room of the Inn at the End of Names held a table, two chairs, and a wall of maps so dense they overlapped like scales on a fish. Ossa had spent nine years collecting them, drawing them, stealing them from archives that no longer remembered why they'd kept them. Maps of Thessaly-before-the-flood. Maps of the Amber Coast, which had been unmade by its own kings. Maps of a desert that used to be a sea that used to be a god's drowned face.

And in the center of the wall, behind a sheet of oilcloth, the map she showed to no one.

She pulled the cloth away.

Wendell stared. The child made a small sound, low in her throat, like a door opening on a dark room.

"That's nothing," Wendell said. "That's blank vellum."

"It's not." Ossa traced the empty rectangle with one finger. "This is Vael. Glass bridges between seven hills. Vineyards on terraces cut by hands older than any living alphabet. The Library Under the Water, where they kept songs instead of books, because they believed a sung memory can't be stolen." She watched his face. "A country of thieves' nightmares, actually. Nothing there could be taken because everything belonged to everyone. They'd have hated you."

"Charming. It's still blank."

"It's blank *because* it was erased, not before. Look closer. Hold it to the light."

He did, grudgingly, tilting the vellum toward the lamp. And then Ossa saw it catch him — saw his breath change, saw the thief's steadiness crack into something younger and more frightened. Because under the right light, the blankness moved. Shadows swam where nothing was drawn: the ghost-press of hills, the negative image of rivers, a coastline that existed only as the absence of ink.

"You see it," she said.

"What am I seeing?"

"The world remembers Vael. It's not allowed to say so. Everything erased leaves a shadow on the page — the shape of the hole it left. The Choir calls it residue. I call it the truth, written backwards." She took the map from him and laid it flat. "Now. The part that matters. Sit down, thief. Both of you. This takes as long as it takes."

The child climbed onto the second chair. Wendell remained standing, arms crossed, a man guarding a door even when there was no door. Fine. She'd talk over his defenses; she'd had practice.

"Moon cycles run twelve turns," Ossa began. "Twelve full faces, and then the year turns over and starts counting again. Everyone knows this. Farmers, sailors, priests. Twelve is the number of the world's patience." She tapped the blank map. "But moons are like anything else. They wear. And every so often — once in a very long while, once in the lifetime of a nation — the moon shows a thirteenth turn. A full face that shouldn't exist. The thirteenth moon of a thirteenth month that lasts three nights and then is forgotten by everyone except the people it happens to."

"I've never heard of a thirteenth turn."

"No. Almost no one has. The ones who know are the ones it's for." She met his eye. "The Pale Choir knows. The Choir wasn't built to cast the spell, thief. It was built to *finish* it. Unmaking a whole country isn't one act — it's a process. You pull the threads out of history one at a time. Names first. Then records. Then buildings, borders, graves. Then language. Then memory, family by family, mind by mind. It took the Choir forty years to erase Vael. Forty years of hymns. And at the end of it, one fragment remained — one piece of the counter-song, the Sigil, the thread they couldn't find. So the spell sits unfinished. Incomplete. Like a knot pulled almost through."

"And the moon?"

"The moon is the loom." Ossa spread her hand flat on the blank vellum. "Every cycle of twelve turns, the knot tightens a little more. Vael fades a little further. Fewer dreams of glass bridges. Fewer grandmothers humming tunes they can't name. The spell feeds on the turns the way ivy feeds on a wall. And on the night of the thirteenth turn — when the moon shows the face that shouldn't be — the spell completes itself. Whatever fragment remains gets pulled through. Whatever carries it—" she glanced at the child, and made herself keep her voice level, made herself say it plain, because this child deserved plain more than she deserved gentle, "—gets pulled through with it. And then it isn't just that Vael is gone. It's that Vael was never gone, because Vael was never *anything*. No shadow on the page. No scar. No songs half-remembered. Even the forgetting gets forgotten. Even the hole closes up."

Silence. The inn creaked around them, that particular silence of a building that listens.

Wendell had gone very still. Stillness was clearly his native language. "How long?" he said.

Ossa had been dreading the question since the child first sang. She'd counted the nights on the road here, marked them against the almanac of turns she kept in her head because writing such things down was how the Choir found you.

"Nineteen days," she said. "We're in the twelfth turn now. Twelfth full moon rises in nineteen nights. The thirteenth comes three after that."

"Twenty-two days."

"Twenty-two days. Yes."

The child's face did something complicated and terrible — hope and grief fighting over the same ground — and Ossa understood suddenly that for this child, the deadline cut both ways. Twenty-two days to save a kingdom. Also twenty-two days left in which a kingdom still existed to be saved. After that, even the mourning would be stolen from her. Even the loss would be unwritten. She would stand in a world with no wound in it and no way to know she had ever bled.

Ossa had watched countries die. She had never before watched someone realize they were the last person alive who would ever grieve them.

She put her hand over the child's small fist on the table. The child didn't pull away.

"There's a way," Ossa said. "There has to be, or I wouldn't waste your twenty-two days telling you this. The spell completes only if the Sigil stays in the world. If it's returned — given back, freely, through the door the Vaeli built for exactly this — the knot unties instead of pulling through. The spell breaks. Vael stays erased, yes. Dead is dead, and I won't lie to you about that. But it stays *remembered*. The shadows stay on the page. The songs stay half-hummed. Grief keeps its shape. That's not nothing. That's the difference between a grave and a disappearance."

"What door?" Wendell said. "Where?"

Ossa almost laughed. Of course that was his next question. Where. The man thought in destinations, in roads, in the geography of escape. Well. Geography was her trade, and for once the trade and the truth pointed the same direction.

"Not where," she said. "*Whether.* The Door Out of Vael stands in the middle of the space where Vael used to be — the true middle, the heart of the scar, out past the border stones and the reed marshes and every road anyone's ever walked. And the only way to reach it is the Road Out of Nowhere, which doesn't exist." She held up her finger before he could object. "I mean that literally. It exists only while it's being believed in. Step onto it doubting, and there's no step. The Choir knows this. It's why they don't simply wait at the gate — they can't reach it any better than we can. Instead they'll spend those twenty-two days doing what they do best. Singing at you. Naming things to unmake them. And the first thing they'll try to unmake is the simplest: your trust in each other. Because the road holds only as long as the walkers do."

Wendell looked at her for a long moment. Then at the child. Then back at her, and Ossa watched him do the thief's arithmetic one more time — except this time the columns didn't balance, and she watched him notice it.

"Why do you care?" he said abruptly. "Nine years mapping dead countries. One arm. No army. Why stick your neck into a Choir hymn for a stranger's kid?"

Because I drew the map of the Amber Coast and watched the ink fade while I held it. Because I once refused a commission from hooded patrons and woke the next morning with my arm gone and no memory of losing it, only a scar and a hymn stuck in my head for a week. Because someone has to keep the ledger of what was taken.

"Because erasure offends me," she said. "Professionally."

It wasn't the whole truth. He knew it wasn't the whole truth. He let it go anyway, which was its own kind of courtesy, and Ossa decided — provisionally, cautiously, the way she trusted anyone — that the thief might be worth the ink.

"So," she said, and pulled a fresh sheet of vellum toward her, and uncapped the ink she carried in a vial at her throat, the vial she'd sworn never to open for anything less than this. "Nineteen days until the twelfth turn. Three weeks of walking, and not along any road you know. I need to chart us a path across the scar, and I need provisions, and I need money, and I need—" she looked at Wendell's hands, long-fingered and quick and made for other people's pockets, "—a thief. Which means we need a city. And the nearest city is Varrow, and Varrow is two days east, and Varrow is crawling with Marench's kind and worse."

The child tugged her sleeve. Small fingers, urgent. Sef pointed at the blank map, at the swimming shadows of the seven hills, and then pressed her palm flat against her own chest — over the sternum, where the bones were — and looked up at Ossa with an expression that needed no translation in any tongue, living or dead.

*It's in here. It's been in here the whole time.*

"I know, little one," Ossa said quietly. "I know what you're carrying. And in twenty-two days, we're going to carry it home."

Outside, through the inn's one small window, the moon was rising — fat, white, ordinary, the twelfth face beginning its slow climb toward its final fullness. Ossa watched it clear the treeline and felt the countdown start in her chest like a second pulse.

Somewhere out in the dark, she knew, hooded throats were already warming up.

* * *

She worked through the night.

Mapping an erased country was unlike any other cartography. You couldn't survey what wasn't there. You worked from residue — from the negative shapes, from the dreams people reported, from the half-hummed songs collectors had written down without knowing why. Ossa had spent nine years assembling the fragments, and now, with the child asleep on a pallet in the corner and the thief pretending not to watch from the doorway, she began the real work: laying the pieces against each other, finding where the roads of Vael had run, searching the shadow-text for the one road that mattered.

The Road Out of Nowhere appeared on none of her sources. Of course it didn't. It was never a road in Vael; it was the seam in the erasure itself, the one path the Vaeli had left unbuilt so that it could never be unmade. You didn't find it. You believed your way onto it, one step at a time, and the believing had to be shared, because no single heart held firm enough alone.

Which meant, Ossa realized somewhere past midnight, quill hovering, that the map she was about to draw was not really a map at all.

It was instructions for trust. Drawn in ink, but made of something else entirely.

Behind her, the child murmured in her sleep — a syllable or two of the dead tongue, soft as riverstones. On his pallet by the door, the thief slept badly, the way guilty men sleep, one hand curled near the satchel of vials he still hadn't explained.

Ossa dipped her quill and drew the first hill.

Nineteen days. She'd charted dead kingdoms before.

Never one with a clock attached.
