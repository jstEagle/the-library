## 10. The Market of Stolen Things

The city had no name, which suited Wendell fine. Named things could be found. This one simply accumulated at the mouth of a dead river like silt — a sprawl of lantern-light and wet stone that appeared on no map and answered to no lord. The locals called it the Shambles when they called it anything at all, and mostly they didn't call it anything, because calling attention to yourself in the Shambles was how you ended up floating face-down in the brackish water with your pockets turned out.

Sef waited in the room above the tallow-chandler's shop, third floor, window facing the alley, exactly as instructed. Wendell had made the child repeat it back to him twice before he'd leave: *Room over the chandler's. Don't open the door for anyone. If I'm not back by moonrise, don't wait.*

The last instruction was a lie, and they both knew it. Sef would wait. That was the trouble with children who had lost everything — they clung to whatever was left, even when what was left was Wendell Vane.

He pulled his hood up and went down into the market.

* * *

The Market of Stolen Things wasn't a place so much as an agreement. Somewhere north of here, memory-trading happened in back rooms between brokers with ledgers and licenses and a thin veneer of respectability. Here it happened in the open, under strings of paper lanterns that bled red light onto the cobbles, because here there was no one left to steal from who mattered and no law that cared.

Wendell walked the rows slowly, hands in his sleeves, doing what he'd done ten thousand times in Hollowmere: reading faces.

It was different outside the valley. In Hollowmere, magic couldn't cross the border stones, which meant his work had always been artisanal — close, quiet, hand-to-hand. He'd press two fingers to a traveler's temple at the ferry crossing or the coaching inn and lift the goods gently, the way you'd lift an egg from a hen's nest. Clean work. Honest work, if theft could ever be honest.

Here, the market did the work for him. Half these people were already selling. The other half were already buying. All he had to do was stand in the current and skim.

He passed a woman haggling over a vial of her own wedding day, trying to buy back something she'd sold three winters ago and regretted every morning since. He passed a boy no older than fifteen offering fresh-drawn memories from a tray like sweetmeats — first snowfall, a mother's lullaby, the taste of honey straight from the comb — each one going cheap because the boy was new and didn't know his own stock was precious. Wendell kept moving. He'd made himself one rule years ago and it still held: never take from someone already selling. There was no craft in that. No art. Just scavenging.

But the others. The others were everywhere.

A merchant by the fish stalls, fat and complacent, his mind leaking images like a cracked barrel: a warehouse full of saffron, a mistress in a green dress, a night in Vell harbor when he'd watched ships come in with their sails full of sunset. Wendell drifted past, let his sleeve brush the man's shoulder, and took the harbor.

It came away clean. The ships, the light on the water, the smell of tar and salt — he folded it into himself where he kept such things, behind the door in his chest that only opened inward. The merchant blinked once, felt a small absence he'd never be able to name, and went back to arguing over the price of eels.

Wendell stood very still in the crowd.

*Oh,* he thought. *I've missed this.*

That was the thing he hadn't understood until this moment, walking a foreign market with a stolen sunset-sail in his head: in Hollowmere he had told himself he stole out of necessity. Bread money. Rent. Survival. A tidy story, well-worn, comfortable as an old coat. But here — here there was no need yet, not really; the passage money was short but not desperate, Ossa had said she knew a captain who owed her, and the debt could likely be settled for less than Wendell carried. He was stealing tonight because he wanted to.

Because it felt like coming home.

His fingers were steady. His breathing was even. He moved through the crowd the way water moves through a net, touching, taking, leaving no ripple, and every touch was perfect, every take was clean, and somewhere underneath the pleasure of it a voice was saying *look at yourself, look how easily the glove fits* — and he couldn't stop.

He lifted a soldier's memory of his daughter learning to walk. He lifted a drunk's memory of the morning he'd stopped drinking, eleven years ago, one whole day of clarity preserved like a fly in amber. He lifted a courtesan's memory of a client who had wept while she played the harp, and the strange tenderness of it, the way pity and desire had braided together in her chest.

He took fourteen memories in under an hour. It was the best hour of work he'd done in years, and he hated it, and he loved it, and both feelings sat in him side by side like strangers forced to share a bench.

* * *

The broker worked out of a booth near the market's heart, a lean man with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of dishwater. He weighed vials on a little brass scale and paid in coin that might have been real. He didn't ask questions, which in the Shambles was its own kind of courtesy.

"Passage south," Wendell said, laying the memories out on the counter one by one. "Two travelers. Quiet ship. No manifest."

The broker glanced at the goods, then at Wendell, then back at the goods, longer this time. "Harbor at dusk. Soldier's girl. Drunk's salvation." He tapped the vials with a fingernail. "Good pulls. Clean pulls. You've done this before."

"I've done this before."

"Where?" The question was casual. The eyes weren't.

"Hollowmere," Wendell said, because lying about a town that appeared on maps was easier than lying about a town that didn't, and the broker's eyebrows rose with the mild interest of a man filing information rather than using it.

"Marench country," the broker said. "Memory-brokers up there run a tight guild. You working independent?"

"Retired."

The broker smiled without warmth. "Nobody retires. They just change markets." He counted coins onto the counter — a fair price, Wendell noted, almost generous, and generosity in the Shambles was more alarming than robbery. "For this lot? Passage south is yours. Captain's called Saltmarsh, berth at the drowned pier, tell him Doss sent you."

Wendell swept the coins into his pouch and turned to go.

"One more thing," Doss said, still weighing nothing on his little brass scale. "Man came through yesterday asking after a thief from Hollowmere. Traveling with a child. Described you close enough to spit a portrait." He looked up, dishwater eyes mild as milk. "Didn't catch the name. Paid well, though. Marench's agents pay well."

The market noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater. Wendell kept his face still. Ten thousand hours of practice held it still.

"What did this man want with me?"

Doss shrugged. "Wasn't me he wanted. Wanted to know if you'd been here, where you'd go next, whether you were selling." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "I told him you hadn't come through. Man pays better for being lied to than most pay for truth."

"Why?"

"Because," Doss said, "he described the child too. And the way he described it—" the broker's tongue touched his teeth, "—wasn't the way you describe a kid. Was the way you describe cargo. Or a cure." He slid his scale aside and leaned forward, lowering his voice to something almost human. "Whatever you're carrying, thief, half the market will sell you and the other half will sell you cheaper. Get to your ship."

Wendell was already moving.

* * *

He didn't go straight back. That was discipline, not bravery — running flat-out through the market would draw every eye he was trying to lose. So he walked, fast but walking, doubling through the fish stalls, down an alley that stank of rot and lamp oil, up a stairway that groaned under his boots, cutting a wide loop around the booth district in case anyone was watching the chandler's street.

They were watching the chandler's street.

Two men, positioned badly — one idling by a doorway, one pretending to examine the chandler's shutters — wearing the particular patience of people being paid to wait. Wendell read them from the shadows across the lane and felt something cold slide down his spine that had nothing to do with the river wind.

Not Choir. The Pale Choir didn't loiter; they arrived like weather. These were hired men, muscle-for-coin, and muscle meant Marench. Which meant Marench hadn't just marked him back in Hollowmere. Marench had tracked him. Across the border, out of the valley, through country Wendell had never walked — tracked him the way you track something valuable.

Or the way you track something dangerous.

There was a back stair to the chandler's rooms, a rickety iron ladder down into the yard behind the shop, and Wendell took it two rungs at a time, heart hammering now, all the stolen memories in his head sloshing like ballast in a storm-tossed hold. He let himself in through the kitchen door with the key under the loose brick, took the interior stairs quietly, and knocked their pattern on the third-floor door — soft, soft, pause, soft.

Sef opened it instantly. Had been waiting with a hand on the latch, probably, all night. The child's face did its usual work of showing nothing and everything at once: fear, held carefully, like a bird cupped in two hands.

"We're leaving," Wendell whispered, already gathering their things — two bundles, a waterskin, Ossa's map-case with its blood-dark lines. "Marench found us. Two men downstairs, more probably coming. We go out the back, through the yards, down to the drowned pier, and we're on the water before midnight."

Sef nodded. Then, in the dead tongue that still made Wendell's teeth ache to hear, said something short and questioning.

"I don't know what you asked," Wendell said. "But if it was *are we safe* — no. Not yet. Maybe not for a long time. Come on."

They went down through the sleeping house, past the chandler's snoring bulk behind his curtain, out into the yard where the river smell was thick enough to chew. Wendell paused in the shadow of the wall and listened. Behind them, faint through the building, footsteps on the front stairs. Fast. Unhurried men had become hurried men.

"They're inside," he breathed. "Move."

They ran. Through the neighbor's yard, over a fence that tore Wendell's sleeve, along the drainage channel toward the water, the paper lanterns of the market glowing behind them like a wound in the dark. Sef ran well — light, silent, the endurance of a child who had learned that stopping meant dying. Wendell followed the sound of the small feet ahead of him and thought, absurdly, of the harbor memory he'd stolen an hour ago: ships coming in with their sails full of sunset. He'd sold it to Doss. He wished suddenly, fiercely, that he hadn't. He wished he could look at it one more time, some beautiful thing in his head that wasn't fear.

The drowned pier stretched out into the black water on pilings gone soft with rot, half of it submerged at high tide, which was now, which was the point — a berth nobody official could reach without swimming. A boat waited there, low in the water, sails furled, and at its rail a shape Wendell recognized from Ossa's description: broad, weathered, missing two fingers on the left hand.

Saltmarsh.

"Ossa's friends," Wendell called softly, when they were twenty feet out on the planking. "She said to say she remembers the favor."

The shape laughed, low and rough. "Ossa doesn't have favors. She has debts, and she collects them like taxes." A lantern came up, hooded against the wind, illuminating a face like a cliff face. "Two berths. Coin first."

Wendell paid. Every coin Doss had given him and three of his own besides, and Saltmarsh counted it with his eight fingers and grunted, which from a man like that was a hymn of welcome.

Then Wendell heard footsteps on the pier behind them.

Four sets. Heavy. Running.

"They're on the boards!" he shouted. "Cast off — cast off *now*—"

Saltmarsh moved faster than a man his size should, hauling the mooring line free, and the boat swung out from the pier on the tide just as the first of Marench's men reached the gap. The man made a grab for the gunwale, missed by a hand's width, and stood there panting on the rotten planks as the water widened between them. In the lantern light Wendell saw his face clearly for the first time.

He knew it. Not the man — the *mark*. A small brand below the left ear, a spiral with a line struck through it. The mark of Marench's house. The mark Wendell had seen on the ledger in Marench's back room, beside a column of figures, beside a word written in careful ink:

*Sef.*

And beneath that word, another entry, older, in a different hand, that Wendell had seen and dismissed and spent every night since trying to forget:

*A. Vaeli. Price on retrieval, doubled if living.*

The man on the pier shouted something across the water — not a threat, not a curse. An offer. "Vane! Marench says come back and keep your name! Come back and be paid!"

The boat slid out into the dark. The lantern shrank. The Shambles sank behind them into the fog it had risen from, and Wendell stood at the stern with his fists white on the rail, hearing it again, the words from Marench's ledger, the ones he'd refused to read properly, the ones he'd told himself meant nothing:

*A. Vaeli.*

Not a stranger. Not some nameless erased soul.

Himself.

Beside him, Sef tugged his sleeve and pointed back at the shrinking shore, then at Wendell, then pressed a small hand flat against his chest, and asked a question in the language of a kingdom that no longer existed.

This time, Wendell thought he almost understood it.

*I know you.*
