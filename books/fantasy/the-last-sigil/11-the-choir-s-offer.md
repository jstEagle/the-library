## 11. The Choir's Offer

The city of Vetch had a smell Wendell never got used to: tallow and wet stone and the sweet rot of memories too old to sell. He'd been there three days. He'd meant to be gone in one.

It was the stealing that kept him.

He told himself it was necessity. Passage north cost coin, and coin in Vetch came from only two trades — memory and flesh — and he had no intention of learning the second. So each evening he'd gone down to the Lanternmarket with his sleeves full of tricks and his hands remembering their old work, and each evening he'd come back to the rented room above the dye-shop lighter in his chest and heavier in his purse. A fishwife's wedding day. A soldier's last letter home, unsent, still folded in his mind like paper. A boy's first sight of snow.

Good work. Clean work. The best he'd done in years.

That was what frightened him. Not the Choir at his heels, not Marench's agents moving through the markets like eels through kelp — but how easily his hands fell back into it. How good it felt. In Hollowmere he'd told himself for eleven years that he stole because he had to, because bread was bread, because the border stones gave him no choice. Three days in Vetch had stripped that story down to the studs. He stole because he was *good* at it, and he was good at it because some part of him had never wanted to stop.

Sef was asleep when he came up the stairs that night, curled on the far bed with one hand under their cheek and the other closed around the little tin whistle they carried everywhere and never played. The child slept like someone who had learned not to trust sleep — shallow, ready, eyes opening at the smallest wrong sound.

Wendell stood in the doorway a long moment before coming in. Counting breaths. Fourteen a minute, steady. He did this every night now. He'd stopped pretending it was practical.

"You're late," said a voice from the dark corner by the window.

Wendell didn't move. His body wanted to bolt — old instinct, older than Hollowmere — but bolting would leave Sef alone with whatever sat in that corner, and that was no longer a thing his body was permitted to want.

"I'm often late," he said. "The market runs long."

"The market." The voice was dry and pleasant, like pages turning. "Yes. We've watched you in the market."

The lamp on the table lit itself. No match, no flint — just flame arriving out of the air the way sound arrives after an explosion, late and terrible. Sitting beneath it was a figure in grey robes so pale they were almost white, hood drawn back to show a face that was ordinary in the way a well-made mask is ordinary: symmetrical, calm, faintly smiling. Ageless. Genderless, or simply past the bother of it.

A cantor. Wendell had seen them once before, years ago, across the border stones at dusk — distant as weather. Never this close. Never indoors.

"Magic doesn't cross into rooms," Wendell said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "I've lived my whole life next door to what you are. I know the rules."

"The rules bend," the cantor said, "where we have already unmade something. This city has lost three things this year. A bridge. A bell-founder. A name-day festival. Where erasure has been, our song echoes." It tilted its head. "You know something about echoes, Wendell Vane."

"That's not my name."

"It is the name you bought," the cantor agreed. "Fourteen silver pieces and a memory of your mother's kitchen, sold to a broker in a town that no longer appears on maps. You were thorough. I'll grant you that. Most people who erase themselves leave threads. You left almost none."

"Almost?"

"Almost." The cantor rose. It moved without weight, robes settling around it like smoke deciding to be cloth. "You are wondering why you haven't screamed. Why you haven't run. I'll tell you: because some part of you has been waiting eleven years for us to find you. Guilt does that. It leaves the door unlatched."

Wendell's hands were cold. He thought about the knife in his boot and knew, with perfect clarity, that it would do nothing. You can't cut a hymn.

"If you've come to kill me," he said, "do it quietly. There's a child sleeping."

"We know there is a child sleeping. We have listened to the child sleep for six nights. Do you know what we hear, Wendell Vane?" The cantor drifted a step closer, and the lamplight bent toward it, hungry. "We hear the Sigil. It sings in the child even now, in sleep. A lullaby in a dead tongue. Every note of it is a note of our own great work — the final verse, the closing bar. When the moon turns her thirteenth face, the spell completes. Vael will not merely be fallen. Vael will be *unfallen-from*. Nothing left. Not ruins. Not grief. Not even the shape of a hole where something used to be." Its smile deepened by a degree. "We are very nearly finished. Eleven hundred years of patient singing. And then you walked out of your valley with the last verse tucked under your arm like a stolen loaf."

"So take it," Wendell said. "You're magic. I'm a thief with a knife that won't help. Take the child and be done."

Something flickered across the cantor's face — not anger. Disappointment. That was worse.

"We cannot take," it said. "That is the whole architecture of the work. What is given may be unwritten; what is taken remains written, forever, somewhere in the world's ledger, a stain, a debt. The Sigil must be surrendered freely or the spell fails and must begin again — another eleven centuries, another thousand singers. We are not prepared to wait." The cantor spread its pale hands. "So we bargain. And of all the creatures in creation, thief, we wished most to bargain with *you*."

* * *

Wendell became aware, gradually, that the room had changed. Not the furniture, not the sleeping child — but the air had thickened, gone syrupy, and the lamplight had turned the color of honey held up to sun. Memory-light. He knew it from the other side; he'd made it himself a hundred times, coaxing a traveler's past loose into visible form before drawing it down into glass.

The cantor was making it too. But it wasn't drawing anything *out*.

It was offering.

Images hung in the air between them like lanterns at a festival. A walled city of white stone and glass bridges strung between towers like frozen rivers. A harbor full of ships whose sails were painted with constellations. A woman with ink-stained fingers laughing at something off-frame. A library where books sang themselves aloud in the dark so no story would ever be lonely. A man — young, dark-haired, with a ward-keeper's iron chain around his neck — running through a burning gate with something wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, weeping as he ran.

Wendell knew the man's face. It took him a moment to understand why, and when he understood, his knees nearly went.

It was his own.

Not his as he was — thinner, younger, unbroken by valley years — but his. The jaw he shaved every morning. The scar on the left forearm he'd always assumed came from a scythe.

"You were there," the cantor said softly. "At the fall. You were a keeper of wards, third rank, sworn to the Sigil's protection. And when the walls burned, you ran — not away. *Out.* Through the last door, carrying a fragment of the great work, believing to your dying day that you were stealing it to save it." The cantor laughed, low and warm, a horrible sound, like a friend's laugh heard underwater. "You were half right. You saved it for us. Eleven years you sat in your little valley growing old around it, and you never once wondered why the fragment found *you*, of all the roads in the world. Things that are erased drift toward their own kind, Wendell Vane. You are not hiding from Vael. You are Vael's last piece. Its final ward. Its unfinished errand."

"Stop." His voice cracked on it.

"And here is what we offer." The cantor gestured, and the images rearranged themselves — the whole erased country assembling in the air like a mosaic rebuilding itself midair. "Give us the child. Freely. Walk them to us by the hand, tell them it will be painless — it will be, we are gentle with verses — and we will give you back everything. Your name, which you sold. Your mother's kitchen, which you traded for a new life. Every memory you ever parted with, restored, whole, *yours*. And more." The cantor stepped close enough that Wendell could see its eyes were the grey of old ash. "We will restore what *we* took from you. The night you fled, you did not merely run — you asked us, in your terror, to make you forget. You knelt in the mud outside the gate and begged the Choir to unmake your knowing. We obliged. We are generous with penitents. Everything you have spent eleven years wondering about yourself — we hold it. Complete. Intact. We will pour it back into you like water into a cracked cup, and you will be *whole*, Wendell. You will remember being loved. You will remember being brave. You will remember why you wept as you ran, and you will finally, finally know who you are."

Behind him, on the far bed, Sef breathed. Fourteen a minute. Steady.

Wendell found that he was crying. It annoyed him distantly, the way weather annoys a man with work to do. He hadn't cried in eleven years; he'd assumed the equipment was broken.

"You're lying," he said.

"About which part?"

"Any of it. All of it. Cantors lie — it's in the—"

"In the songs? Yes. We lie the way a river lies about being still." The cantor's tone didn't change at all. "But consider, thief: what do we gain by lying? You will give us the child or you will not. If you will not, we take the city apart looking for you both, and we are very good at taking cities apart — ask anyone from Vael. Oh." The smile again. "You can't. They're not *anyone* anymore. Ask the child, then. The child remembers."

The images hung there. The woman with ink-stained fingers. The singing library. His own young face, running, weeping, alive with a purpose he could not recall ever having felt.

And Wendell understood, with the cold clarity of a man reading his own death warrant, that the cruelest thing about the offer was not that it might be false.

It was that it might be true.

Every stolen memory he'd ever held in glass had taught him one law, and the law was this: people do not miss what they cannot name. He had built his whole life in that law's shadow. And now the Choir stood in his rented room offering to name everything he'd lost, and the wanting that rose in him was enormous and shameless and *familiar* — it was the wanting of the Lanternmarket, the wanting of the thief's high craft, the wanting that fit him like a glove. Give us the child. Be whole. Be named. Be the man who was loved.

All he had to do was what he had always done.

Take from someone smaller.

Sef stirred in sleep and murmured something in the dead tongue — two soft syllables, rising, the cadence of a question. Asking, even dreaming, if someone was still there.

Wendell looked at the cantor. The ash-grey eyes waited with the patience of mountains.

"No," he said.

It came out small. He made himself say it again, louder, and the second time it filled the room and cracked the honeyed light like ice giving way.

"No. Find another verse."

The cantor regarded him for a long moment. Then it sighed — genuinely, almost fondly — and the hanging images went out one by one, the white city, the harbor, the woman with ink-stained fingers, his own weeping face, until only the lamplight remained, ordinary and yellow and cheap.

"Pity," the cantor said. "You would have made a magnificent penitent." It moved toward the window, and where it passed, the wall forgot itself briefly — plaster going smooth and featureless, then remembering. "Then hear the terms of refusal, Wendell Vane, so you may weigh them honestly. We do not need your surrender. We need only the child's, and children surrender *constantly* — to hunger, to fear, to whoever feeds them. You have made yourself the child's protector. Very good. Protectors can be removed. And when the child stands alone and frightened in a world that has forgotten their kingdom ever existed—" the cantor paused at the sill, half-turned, hooded in shadow, "—they will give us the Sigil just to have one voice, any voice, sing to them kindly in the dark."

The window opened onto nothing. The cantor stepped through it like a man stepping off a pier, and the night closed over the place where it had been, seamless.

* * *

Wendell sat on the floor for a long time. He didn't remember sitting down.

Sef woke near dawn — Wendell heard the breathing change before he saw the eyes open, dark and watchful in the grey light. The child looked at him sitting there among the shadows, and something in his face must have shown, because Sef slid off the bed, crossed the floor, and stood in front of him. Not touching. Just standing. Guarding him, maybe. Or asking to be guarded.

"Sef," Wendell said. His voice was wrecked. He tried again. "We leave today. Now, if we can. There's a woman — a cartographer — Ossa. At the inn on the north road. She knows things. She can draw us a way."

Sef studied him. Then, slowly, the child reached out and touched the tear-tracks on his cheek with one finger, frowning at the damp, and said something in Vaeli — soft, questioning, the same two rising syllables from the dream.

*I don't understand you,* Wendell thought. *I don't understand you and I would burn down every market in this city rather than let them have you, and I don't know when that happened.*

"We go north," he said aloud, and began packing.

They were down the stairs and into the street before the dye-shop opened, into a morning still blue with night, and Wendell told himself the tightness in his chest was fear of pursuit. It was partly that. But underneath the fear, quiet as a coin slipped into a pocket, was something else — a memory the cantor had shown him that he couldn't put down. Not the burning city. Not the woman.

His own face. Running. Weeping. Carrying something wrapped in oilcloth.

*Believing to your dying day that you were stealing it to save it.*

Half right, the cantor had said.

Wendell walked north with the child's small hand in his, and for the first time in eleven years, he was afraid not of what he'd forgotten —

but of what, if he ever remembered, he might find he'd done.
