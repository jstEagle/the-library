## 12. Sef's Song

The inn had a room with two beds and one window, and Sef counted the window before anything else.

One window. That was good. Windows were doors that had forgotten how to open, and doors that forgot could not let the Choir in. Sef checked it the way Mother had taught — not with hands, hands made noise, but with eyes, slow, from the corner of the room where no shadow could stand up behind you. The glass was old and wavy. Through it the fields went on being fields, gray-gold, ordinary. Nothing burned.

Sef counted anyway. Counting was the other thing Mother taught. *When you are afraid, count what is real.* One window. Two beds. One man sleeping in the far bed, breathing slow, his boots by the door because he said a thief sleeps better knowing exactly how far he'd have to run barefoot.

That was Wendell. He told jokes like that — jokes that were also instructions.

Three vials on the table, wrapped in cloth so they wouldn't catch light. Four apples in the bowl, one going soft. Five steps from bed to door. Six — six was the number of turns in the first phrase of the Sigil, and Sef stopped counting at six the way you stop walking at the edge of a well. Some numbers had teeth.

The moon was almost full. Ossa had said it plain, her one hand flat on her map: *Twelve turns done. One left.* Sef did not understand all of what the Sigil was, only that it lived under the breastbone like a swallowed coal, warm when the Choir was far and cold as a key in winter when they came near. Tonight it was warm. Tonight the coal was almost kind.

Tonight Sef wanted to give Wendell something.

It was a strange want. Sef turned it over like a river stone. All the wanting since the burning had been small wants: water, sleep, a door with a lock, Wendell's coat around the shoulders when the marsh wind came through. Wants for staying alive. This was a different animal. This was a want to *give*.

Because of the apples. That was where it started. Yesterday Wendell had bought five apples and put four in the bowl and eaten none himself, and when Sef asked with a look, he'd shrugged and said, "I ate mine on the road," which was a lie, because Sef had watched him not eat it. He'd put it back in his pocket and carried it all day like a coin he couldn't spend.

People who lied to feed you were people you could trust. Mother had said something like that once, or maybe Sef had decided it alone, in the years alone, but it felt true the way the Sigil felt true: low down, under words.

So. A gift. And there was only one thing Sef owned that was worth anything.

* * *

Wendell woke because the singing had started.

Not aloud — Sef was careful, lips barely moving, sitting cross-legged on the floor with the blanket around the shoulders like a hooded ghost. But the Sigil did not need volume. It needed only a voice willing to shape it, and even whispered it moved through the room like water finding cracks.

He lay still and listened, and hated himself for listening, because this was exactly the thing he'd promised Marench's memory-brokers he would never do: taste a memory that wasn't for sale. But it wasn't stealing if it was given. Was it? It wasn't stealing if the child sang it into the dark of a shared room, trusting, eyes half-shut, rocking slightly with the rhythm of a song older than every kingdom Wendell had ever robbed.

The words were Vaeli. He didn't know Vaeli. No one knew Vaeli. That was the whole point of Vael — it had been erased so completely that its language was a hole in the world shaped like a country.

And yet.

And yet the sounds went into him like keys into locks he didn't know he had. Each syllable landed somewhere below thought and turned. His chest hurt. Not the coal-hurt Sef must have felt — a different ache, an ache of recognition, like hearing your own name spoken in a crowd by someone who died long ago.

The fire came without warning.

It rose behind Sef's closed eyes and poured out into the room — not flame, nothing so honest as flame, but memory given shape, the way a stolen memory looked inside a vial, except this one filled walls instead of glass. Wendell sat up and the bedroom was gone and he stood in a street that was screaming.

Vael was burning.

Towers of green glass going soft in the heat, bending like candlewax. Bridges between them — bridges of glass too, arched high over avenues full of running people — cracking, shedding glittering rain. The sky was wrong, bruised purple, and through it came the singing, the Choir's hymn, beautiful and bottomless, and everywhere the hymn touched, things stopped being remembered. People didn't die so much as become unmentioned. Whole houses went quiet mid-scream. A woman dropped a child's shoe in the street and the shoe remained after both of them were gone, lying on the cobbles like a question no one would ever ask again.

Wendell knew the street.

That was the impossible part. He knew the baker's awning, striped blue. He knew the fountain with the chipped dolphin. He knew — his stomach turned over, turned inside out — he knew the tall house on the corner with its shutters open, because he had climbed those shutters, because—

Because a voice was calling from that house. A woman's voice, cutting through the hymn, and it was not calling for help. It was calling a name.

"Wendell—"

No. Not that name.

"*Oren—! OREN!*"

The name hit him like the fountain's cold water. Oren. The word meant something in his body before it meant anything in his mind — it meant climbing, it meant a satchel heavy with something wrapped in oilcloth, it meant a woman in a doorway holding out her arms and him running the *other* way, running toward the gate, running away, the satchel against his spine like a second heartbeat—

"Oren, come back, you have to take me with it, you have to—"

He had not gone back.

The memory showed him everything at once now, all of it, the floodgates broken: himself, younger, a stranger's face he somehow wore, sprinting through the burning avenue with the fragment of the Sigil strapped to his back while the Choir sang the city out from under everyone's feet. Himself choosing. Choosing the errand over the arms. Choosing the kingdom over the woman, telling himself she'd follow, telling himself the ward-keepers' plan would hold, telling himself lie after lie as the glass towers fell behind him like slow tears.

He had run out of Vael carrying the last piece of it. And the price of carrying it out had been that no one who stayed could ever say his name again.

The vision folded. The street collapsed inward like a held breath released, and the room at the inn reassembled itself around him — table, apples, window, boy.

Sef was looking at him.

The child's face had gone white around the eyes. Lips still parted on the last unfinished note. And Wendell watched — watched in real time, helpless — the understanding arrive. Saw it land. Saw Sef's gaze go from his face to the space where the vision had hung and back again, doing the arithmetic children do faster than adults, arriving at the sum no child should have to carry:

*You were there.*

*You ran.*

*You left her.*

"No," Wendell said, and his voice came out ruined. "Sef. Listen to me. I don't remember any of it, I swear it, I erased myself, I—"

But the child had already stood up. Already stepped back. And the coal under the small breastbone had gone cold — Wendell felt it go cold, felt the temperature of the whole room drop, felt the Sigil recoil from him the way a dog recoils from the hand that once struck it.

Sef's mouth worked. No sound came. The muteness that wasn't muteness — the silence the Choir had carved — seemed suddenly less like a wound and more like a verdict. There was nothing the child wanted to say to him badly enough to break the silence for.

Then, slowly, deliberately, Sef took the blanket off the shoulders and laid it on the empty bed. Wendell's bed. Not throwing it. Laying it. The way you return something borrowed.

The window was one story up. Sef went through it without hesitating, quick and silent as smoke, dropping into the yard below, and was gone into the gray-gold fields, toward the marshes, toward anywhere Wendell Vane was not.

"Sef—!"

He got to the window in time to see a small figure running, getting smaller, getting harder to believe in, out along the hedgerows under the fat and nearly-finished moon.

Behind him, on the table, the three vials in their cloth wrapping shivered once, softly, like things dreaming of home.

Wendell stood in the cold coming through the open window and understood that he had finally stolen something he could never sell and never give back: he had taken a child's faith, and spent it.

And somewhere out in the dark, alone, the Last Sigil was walking toward the marshes — where the Choir's hymn carried farthest at night.
