## 13. What I Was Before

The inn was ash by the time Wendell got back to it.

He stood in the road and looked at what remained — the black ribs of the roofline, the chimney standing like a headstone, the smell of burnt oak and something else underneath, something sweeter and wrong, the smell of erased things burning. Ossa's cartography table had been thrown into the yard. Her maps were scattered across the mud, half-charred, and he could see even from a distance that the ink was lifting off them, the lines of dead roads fading as if they'd never been drawn.

Sef wasn't there.

Wendell checked the well first. Then the root cellar, which was more a hole than a cellar, then the stand of alders behind the inn where Sef liked to hide when the world got loud. Nothing. Just crows, and the creak of the sign swinging on its one surviving bracket — *THE INN AT THE END OF NAMES*, the paint scorched but legible, stubbornly legible, as if the name itself refused to be unmade.

*Ossa*, he thought. *They took Ossa.*

And Sef had run. Of course Sef had run. The child had watched him kneel in the marsh mud and confess everything — *I was there when your kingdom burned, I carried a piece of the thing that killed it* — and Sef had offered forgiveness the way children offer everything: completely, without checking whether it was wise. And then the Choir had come, and Wendell hadn't been fast enough, hadn't been anything enough, and now the child who trusted him was alone in the dark with people who fed on erased things.

His hands were shaking. He made himself stop looking at the ruin and start thinking, because thinking was the one trade he'd never been able to steal from himself.

They would have taken Ossa toward the border of the old kingdom. Everything the Choir did moved toward that absence, that hole in the world shaped like Vael. And Sef — Sef would go too, or be drawn there, because the Sigil wanted to go home the way water wants downhill. Which meant Wendell didn't need to find them so much as get ahead of them.

But not yet. Not without knowing what he was walking toward.

He went back to the alders, to the hollow trunk where he'd hidden his satchel before going into the inn — an old habit, thief's habit, never sleep next to your valuables — and took out the vials.

There were thirty-one of them. He knew each one blind: weight, warmth, the color of the light through the glass. A fisherman's wedding day. A woman's memory of her mother's hands kneading bread. The sea. Always at the front, wrapped in wool, the sea — the stolen horizon he'd carried for eleven years, the most beautiful thing he owned, because he owned nothing else that hadn't been someone else's first.

And at the very bottom, wrapped in oilcloth older than any of the others, the vial he had never once opened.

He didn't know where it had come from.

That sentence had always felt like a lie when he thought it, so mostly he didn't think it. But it was true. Every other vial in his collection he could name — the traveler, the night, the price paid or the theft managed. This one simply existed in his satchel the way a scar exists on skin. It was darker than the others, the glass thick and greenish, sealed with wax stamped with a sigil he'd never been able to read. When he held it up to the firelight, nothing showed inside. No image. No shimmer of remembered light.

Just cold. A deep, patient cold, like holding a stone pulled from the bottom of a well.

He sat down against the alder with the vial in his palm and let himself feel how afraid he was. That was another rule — his rules were all small and practical, built over years the way a man builds a fence against wolves: never steal from children, never leave Hollowmere, never open the dark vial. Rules kept him alive. Rules kept the shape of his days intact.

But the valley had burned its illusion in front of him already. The Pale Choir had crossed stones that no magic had ever crossed, singing, and the world had bent around their song like a reed in wind. His rules had not saved Ossa. His rules had not saved Sef.

And somewhere out in the dark, a cantor had told him the truth with a smile: *we can give you back what you lost, Wendell Vane. Your name. Your life. All you have to do is give us the child.*

He'd said no. He'd said it fast and fierce and he'd meant it. But the wanting hadn't left him — the want was still sitting in his chest like a swallowed coal. They knew his name. Whatever he'd been before Hollowmere, whatever self he'd buried so thoroughly that even he couldn't find the grave, the Choir knew it. They fed on erased things. He was an erased thing.

Which meant the answer had been in his own satchel all along, hadn't it. If he was erased, then somewhere there was a record of the erasing. Somewhere there was the original.

The dark vial.

"Right," he said aloud, because the crows had gone and the silence needed breaking. "Right."

He cracked the wax seal with his thumbnail.

* * *

The memory did not pour into him. It detonated.

He was standing on a wall — white stone, warm under his hands, a wall that ran along the edge of everything — and below him spread a city made partly of light. Bridges of glass arched between towers, and the bridges sang when people walked them, a low harmonic hum, thousands of footsteps becoming music. Vael. He knew it was Vael the way you know your own face in a mirror: not by learning but by recognition. The city of the glass bridges. The city in the child's remembering.

Except this was no child's view. He was tall in this memory. He wore a grey coat with silver thread at the cuffs, the livery of the ward-keepers, and there was a ring on his hand — iron, plain, worn smooth — and he was running along the top of the wall while the sky above the city turned the color of an infected wound.

He was carrying something. He could feel it against his sternum even now, even across eleven years and an erasing: a fragment, a shard of something vast, humming the same note as the singing bridges but wrong, inverted, a chord with the joy scraped out of it.

The Last Sigil. Part of it. Carved free of the greater working and given to *him* —

— and here the memory stuttered, tore, and he saw why the Choir wanted it, saw what the whole spell was for, saw the great circle of the unmaking laid out beneath the moon like a chart: thirteen turns, thirteen fragments scattered to thirteen keepers, and when the last piece came home the working would complete itself and Vael would not fall but *unhappen*, would slide out of history like a splinter drawn from flesh, and no one would grieve it because grief requires memory, and there would be no memory —

Twelve pieces had already been surrendered. Taken. Given up by keepers who broke, who bargained, who loved the wrong person too much. He had seen the twelfth go, three nights ago in this remembering — seen a tower come apart like wet paper while men in pale hoods sang.

And the thirteenth — the last — the ward-mothers had split into two pieces rather than trust any single soul. One piece they gave to a child too young to speak, singing it into her bones where no cantor could reach. The other piece they gave to him.

*Run,* the woman had said — a ward-mother, grey-haired, her voice steady in a way that cost her everything; he could see that steadiness costing, see her hands shaking behind her back where no one would witness it. *You are the fastest and the least known. Run east, past the border stones, into the dull lands where our works cannot follow. Hide it. Bury it. Forget it. Forget us, if you must. Forgetting is a kind of shield, Wendell.*

*Wendell.* So the name had been his even then. Or — no. He felt the truth of it as the memory turned over: *Wendell* was the shield. The name she'd given him to wear into the dull lands was a false one, a plain name, a name that fit through doors that true names couldn't. She had renamed him the way you rename a hunted animal.

And he had run. He had run east with the shard burning against his chest, and behind him Vael had begun to sing — the whole city singing back at the Choir, every bridge, every bell, a defiance so beautiful it made the memory blur with tears that were eleven years old — and he had run anyway. He had done what she told him.

Mostly.

Because hiding it wasn't enough, was it? Because the Choir found keepers. Found them through dreams, through longing, through the shard's own hunger to return to the working. Twelve keepers, twelve failures. He lay awake in ditches and haylofts doing the arithmetic of doom and arrived, in his exhaustion and his guilt and his terror, at the only solution that seemed to hold:

If the shard could find him through what he was, then he would stop being anything at all.

There was a working for that. Small, ugly, forbidden — a keeper's working, carved into the wall-stones of Vael for exactly one purpose, though no keeper had ever dared use it. You couldn't erase yourself entirely; something had to remain to carry the shard. But you could cut away everything *around* it. Every memory of Vael. Every memory of the errand. Every memory of the woman with the steady voice and shaking hands. Leave only a husk with a false name and a parcel it couldn't recognize, stumbling east into a valley where magic couldn't follow, and let the husk live out its small dull life stealing small dull memories from travelers until the world forgot there had ever been anything else to steal.

He had stood at the border stones of Hollowmere with the working half-spoken and the shard pressed to his lips like communion, and he had said the words, and the words had eaten him.

The last thing the man on the wall had seen was his own hands opening the vial.

* * *

Wendell came back to himself on the ground beside the alders, cheek in the mud, the empty vial rolling slowly away from his open fingers. His whole body hurt, as if remembering were a bone being reset. The crows were back. Night had come down fully while he lay in the wreck of his own past.

He didn't move for a long time.

So that was the answer. That was what the Choir had smelled on him from the first moment Marench whispered his name into the dark. He wasn't a thief who had stumbled into a war. He was the last ward-keeper of Vael, self-made amnesiac, the coward who had solved the problem of his own courage by cutting it out.

*You cannot undo the past,* the woman's voice said in him, steady, costing. *But an errand unfinished is not the past. An errand unfinished is still happening.*

He laughed — a single ugly bark of sound that scared the crows off the wall of the ruined inn. Eleven years. Eleven years of rules. Never steal from children, because some part of him, buried deeper than the working could reach, knew what had been done to a child. Never leave Hollowmere, because the shard slept better where magic was thin. Never open the vial — and that rule hadn't been caution at all, had it? It had been the working itself, still running, a ghost instruction circling the husk it had made, keeping him from the door of his own house.

He sat up. Wiped his face with both hands. Made himself take inventory, because inventory was what he was good at.

One: he knew what he carried now, or had carried — the shard had gone into the working with him, and the working had bound it into his bones the way the ward-mother had bound Sef's piece into song. He was not just the last keeper. He was a keeper who *was* the keeping. The Choir couldn't take the fragment from him by force because force wouldn't find it; they needed him to surrender it, the way the gate would need a freely given memory. Everything about this ending required consent. That was the shape of the whole terrible design — the unmaking worked only on the willing, the grieving, the tired.

Two: Sef had the other piece, sung into bone, and Sef trusted him again — barely, freshly, a sprout of trust growing through rubble. He had knelt in marsh mud and told the truth and the child had put a small hand on his head like a blessing, and Wendell had felt something he had no stolen word for.

Three: Ossa was in the Choir's hands, alive — they don't feed on the living quickly, they like to draw it out — and Ossa knew the way to the door. Chapter by chapter, map by map, the one-armed cartographer had been drawing a road that must be believed in, and the road led to the only door out of a country that no longer existed. If Ossa broke — and everyone broke, twelve keepers out of twelve — the Choir would learn the door's location.

Four: the moon. He looked up and found it rising over the black treeline, swollen, a day short of full. The twelfth turn had passed. There was one full moon left in the count, and on the night of the thirteenth, if the Sigil was not returned through the door, the working would complete itself with or without the last fragment — the twelve pieces would pull the thirteenth home like a tide pulls a boat off a sandbar, and Sef's piece, sung into bone, would tear free of the child the way a tooth comes out, and everything Vael had been would end even as a memory in a mute child's mouth.

One moon. Maybe twenty-eight days. Probably fewer, because the Choir would not wait politely for astronomy.

Wendell got to his feet. His knees cracked. He was forty-something in the way of men who've stopped counting, and he had never in his life been brave — he had specifically, surgically, removed his bravery along with everything else — but he discovered, testing the feeling like a man probing a broken tooth, that resolve is not the same organ as courage. Courage leaps. Resolve just refuses to sit down.

He gathered the vials, all thirty-one, and considered them. A fisherman's wedding. Bread-kneading hands. Thirty-one lives' worth of other people's best moments, hoarded like a dragon's pile of candles. He had thought of them as wealth. Now, with the memory of the white wall fresh in him, they looked like what they were: evidence. A museum of everything he'd taken because he couldn't bear the emptiness the working had left in him.

He kept the sea. He couldn't explain it even to himself — maybe because it was the first thing he'd ever stolen after Hollowmere remade him, maybe because somewhere under the erasure the man on the wall had loved horizons, had run along one toward the east — but the rest he stoppered and packed with a new intention forming, cold and clean, at the base of his skull.

There was a lawless city three days south where memories traded like coin. There were agents of Marench in it. There were roads the Choir would watch, and roads it wouldn't think to, because roads only mattered to people who believed destinations were fixed.

And there was a door that opened only for a memory freely given, and he was going to need to understand that mechanism completely before he reached it, because he was beginning to suspect — with the flat certainty of a man reading his own confession — that he already knew which memory the door was going to ask for, and he was beginning to suspect he might actually be capable of giving it.

Not yet. First: find Sef. First: get ahead of the Choir. First: finish the errand, eleven years late, badly, in a false name, with a coward's borrowed legs.

The woman with shaking hands would have understood. He was almost sure of it. He was counting on it.

Wendell slung the satchel, looked once at the ruined inn — *I'll come back for you, Ossa* — and turned south, toward the dark, walking with the unhurried stride of a man who has finally stopped running from the thing he is.

Behind him, above the black trees, the moon rose one night fuller, and began to turn.
