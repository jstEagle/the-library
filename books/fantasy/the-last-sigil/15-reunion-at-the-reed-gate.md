## 15. Reunion at the Reed Gate

Sef had been gone since before dawn.

Wendell knew it the way you know a tooth has broken — not by looking, but by the sudden wrongness of everything. The inn's back room was cold. The blanket was folded, and Sef did not fold blankets; Sef left them in ruins like something slaughtered. On the pillow, weighted by a river stone so it wouldn't blow away, was a single reed cut clean at both ends.

An offering. An apology. A goodbye.

"Damn it," Wendell said to the empty room, to himself, to the whole crooked architecture of what he'd done. "Damn it all."

He'd told Ossa he would handle it. He'd said the words *I'll talk to the child* with the same smooth confidence he used on travelers at the border stones, the voice of a man who had never once been honest about anything that mattered. And then he hadn't talked. He'd rehearsed instead, standing in the yard with his hands jammed in his pockets, composing sentences like a man building a bridge out of rotted planks, and every version collapsed under its own weight, and the sun went down, and Sef watched him not-speak from the doorway with those dark patient eyes that held a kingdom's worth of grief, and Wendell had thought *tomorrow*.

There would be no tomorrow now. There might not even be a tonight.

He took the reed. It smelled of the marsh, green and bitter, and he closed his fist around it hard enough to bite the skin, as if pain could stand in for penance. Then he went to find Ossa.

She was in her map room, which is to say she was in the kitchen with the tables pushed together and half the inn's crockery displaced, drawing. Her one hand moved with terrible precision, the stump of her other arm tucked into her belt like a tool holstered. She looked up before he spoke. She always looked up before he spoke. He suspected she heard him thinking from three rooms away.

"He's gone," Wendell said.

Ossa set down her pen. For a long moment she just looked at him, and there was no anger in it, which was worse. Anger you could argue with. What was in her face was the particular sorrow of a woman watching someone else make the mistake she'd already made once herself.

"You didn't tell him," she said.

"I was going to."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "You were going to keep meaning to. That's different. That's the coward's country, and you've lived there your whole life, haven't you? Even before you can remember living anywhere."

The words went in clean, the way only true ones do. Wendell didn't flinch. He'd learned long ago that flinching just showed people where to aim.

"Which way?" he asked.

Ossa rose, crossed to the window, and pointed east, where the land fell away into the reed marshes that bordered the dead space where Vael had been. The morning mist still lay over them like a sheet over a body.

"The boy walks toward home when he hurts," she said. "They always do. Even when home is nothing but a hole in the world." She turned back to him, and now there was iron under the sorrow. "Go. And Wendell — don't go to explain. Explanations are how thieves talk. Go to confess. There's a difference, and children smell it like smoke."

* * *

The marsh took him an hour to cross and gave him back a different man than it took.

It was slow going, black water between tussocks of reed taller than his head, the mud sucking at his boots with every step as if the ground itself were reluctant to let anyone leave Hollowmere. Birdsong everywhere, thin and indifferent. The mist burned off by midmorning and the sun came down flat and white across the water, and Wendell walked with the stolen reed still clenched in his fist because he could not think what else to do with it.

He tracked Sef the way he tracked travelers — small kindnesses of attention, the habits of a thief. Broken reed stems at shoulder height: the child was small but pushing through rather than around, moving fast, not caring about noise. A smear of mud on a stone where a small hand had steadied itself. Once, a single shoeprint in the black silt, toes pointed east, dug deep at the ball of the foot — running, then. Running hard.

And then, near noon, the tracks simply stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. At the edge of a wide pool where the reeds opened onto open water, the last print sat full and sharp as if pressed there minutes ago, and around it, nothing. No exit prints. No return. Wendell stood very still, and the marsh stood very still with him, and he felt the old cold crawl up his spine, the cold of the night a mute child had knocked on his door speaking the language of the dead.

"Sef," he said aloud.

The word came out cracked. He tried again.

"Sef. I know you can hear me. You've been able to hear me since the door." His voice sounded enormous and stupid against the birdsong. "I'm not going to chase you. If I chase you, I'm the thing you think I am."

Silence. Water moving in small idioms against the reeds. And then — so faint he almost missed it, almost convinced himself he'd imagined it — a sound. Not a word. A breath, hitched, swallowed, the sound of someone who has been crying for hours and has gotten very good at hiding it.

Wendell looked at the pool. At the reed bank opposite, dense and shadowed. And he understood.

The child hadn't run east toward the dead place. The child had gone to ground like a hunted thing, because that was what the child had learned the world was: hunters, and places to hide from them. Sef wasn't walking home. Sef was waiting to see if the person who'd betrayed him was the kind of monster who searched.

Wendell looked down at his boots, caked black to the knee. At the pool between them and the reeds. Then he did the only thing left that was true.

He got down on his knees in the mud.

It was colder than he expected, and deeper — the muck took him to mid-thigh, soaked through his trousers, gripped him like a thousand small hands. He knelt there in the filth of the marsh, in full view of the reed bank, and he made no move to cross the water. He put the cut reed down on a hummock between them, an offering returned.

"My name isn't Wendell Vane," he said.

The silence changed shape. Listening became attention.

"I don't know my name. I erased it. I took it out of myself the way I take memories out of other people, and I buried it so deep that even I couldn't find it, and I have lived in Hollowmere for longer than I can honestly count, being no one, stealing everyone." He laughed, and it came out ugly. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? You knocked on the door of the one man in the world whose whole life is a theft, because I stole the first piece of you. Before the Choir ever found you. Before Marench ever sold your name. It was me. I started this."

The reeds didn't move. But they didn't part, either, and he took that as permission to keep talking, because God knew he'd spent a lifetime not talking and the words had backed up behind his teeth like floodwater.

"There was a city," he said. "I saw it burn. I don't remember seeing it burn — that's the trick of it, the filthy elegant trick — I only remember that I remember, the way you feel a scar ache without knowing the wound. Glass bridges. Towers that sang when the wind came off the water. People in the streets with their hands raised, singing something, and the sky coming apart above them like wet paper." His voice was shaking now and he let it shake. "I was there. I know I was there, because when you sang in your sleep two nights ago — that little scrap of the Sigil, the part your mother put in your bones — I heard the city again. From the inside. And I wasn't a stranger watching it burn, Sef. I knew the streets. My feet knew the stones. Whatever I was, whoever I was, I was *there*, and I ran, and I took something with me when I ran, and I made myself forget so thoroughly that I built an entire life out of the forgetting."

His hands were fists in the mud. He forced them open. Thieves' hands, scarred, clever, steady — hands that had never shaken until this year.

"I should have told you the first night. I should have told you the day I understood what you carried. Every hour I didn't was me choosing my comfort over your safety, and I don't have an excuse, and I'm not going to offer one, because excuses are how thieves talk." He stopped. Breathed. The marsh breathed with him. "Here's the truth, all of it, and then you can decide what I deserve.

"I was going to give you to them."

The words landed in the silence like a stone in a well.

"Not — hear me — not the way you think. When Marench told me what the Choir wanted, some rotten practical corner of me did the arithmetic. One child, weighed against a valley. One life against thousands. And I stood in that broker's den and I *did the sum* and I hate that I did it, I hate that the answer even briefly looked like a number." He wiped his face with a muddy hand, which accomplished nothing except making it muddier. "But then I walked home and you were asleep in my chair with your feet pulled up like the world was a place that had ever once been kind to you, and I looked at you, and the sum broke. It just broke. Because you're not a fragment of a spell. You're a person. You're a person who folds blankets wrong and eats too fast when you think no one's counting and cuts reeds as apologies because you have no voice to say sorry with. And I would burn the whole valley down before I handed you through that door, and I don't know what that makes me, but I know what it makes the arithmetic: worthless."

Stillness. The long white light lay on the water. Somewhere a bird cried twice and quit.

"So that's the confession," Wendell said. "All of it. The man you trusted is the man who broke your kingdom. The man who broke your kingdom is the man who chose you anyway. Both things are true and I can't unpick them and I'm not going to try." He spread his empty hands, dripping. "I'm kneeling in the mud because I don't get to stand while you decide. Take whatever time you need. I've stolen lifetimes from strangers; I can spare an afternoon to be judged by a child."

* * *

The sun moved. The shadows of the reeds swung slowly west like the hands of an indifferent clock. Wendell knelt, and the cold worked up through the mud into his bones, and he let it, because discomfort was the smallest coin he had and he meant to pay with all of it.

Twice he heard movement in the far reeds — small shifts, the whisper of stems — and each time he kept his eyes down and his mouth shut. Confessing was done. Begging would undo it.

When Sef finally came, it was not dramatically. The reeds parted halfway along the bank, thirty yards off, and the child stepped out into the light — mud-smeared to the waist, eyes red-rimmed and furious and exhausted, hair full of seed-down. Standing straight. Not approaching. Making Wendell look at the distance between them and own it.

Wendell stayed on his knees. He said nothing.

Sef stared at him for a long time. Then the child reached up and touched two fingers to the throat — the old gesture, the one that meant *words won't come, listen with your eyes* — and pointed at Wendell, and pointed at the burning city that lived behind both their eyes, and drew a line through the air with one small finger. *Cut.* Severed. Ended.

Then the fingers moved again, slower, and Wendell watched them the way he'd once watched travelers' lips for the memories worth taking, and what the hands said was this: *I saw. In your sleep, after I sang. Your face when the city burned. You weren't smiling. You were screaming. Nobody screams like that unless they lost something.*

The child's hands hesitated. Then finished:

*I'm not saying it's forgiven. I'm saying I saw you scream.*

And Wendell — thief, liar, ward-keeper of a dead nation, kneeling in the marsh filth with his whole false name dissolving around him — put his face in his muddy hands and wept like the last citizen of Vael, which was, he was beginning to understand, exactly what he was.

He didn't see Sef cross the water. He only felt the small weight arrive beside him, sinking into the mud at his side, shoulder touching his shoulder — not an embrace, not yet, maybe not ever quite that, but a position taken. A choice of proximity. The fragile beginning of a thing neither of them had a language for, and one of them no voice besides.

They knelt together in the reek and the cold and the ordinary birdsong, two survivors of the same fire, and the marsh held them without judgment, the way wild places hold everyone.

It was Sef who ended it. The child stiffened suddenly, head turning east, and Wendell followed the look — and felt his stomach drop through the mud and the water and the bones of the drowned world beneath.

Far off beyond the marshes, where the land fell away into the nothing that had been Vael, the horizon had changed color. Not sunset — it was barely past noon. This was a different light, a pale and sourceless luminescence, like moonrise in the wrong quarter of the sky. And riding on the wind across the water came a sound that made the reeds tremble and the birds fall silent all at once, every throat in the marsh closing as one:

singing.

Layered voices. Hymnal. Patient. Coming from the direction of the inn.

From the direction of Ossa.

Wendell was on his feet before he knew he'd moved, hauling Sef up with him, mud streaming off them both, and the last thing he saw before he turned north and ran was the child's face — and the fierce, terrified resolve in it, the face of someone who had already decided that whatever was coming, they would not be separated again.

The Pale Choir had found the inn. And the moon was nearly full.
