## 16. The Choir Descends

The moon came up fat and white over the inn, and Wendell watched it through the window of the room he'd been sharing with Sef, and he did not like the look of it at all.

Twelve full turns since the child had knocked on his door speaking the dead tongue. One more and everything Vael had ever been would be gone even from memory. Ossa had marked it on her one good map — the thirteenth turn, circled twice in ink she'd mixed from ash and something darker — and for eleven of those turns they had lived at the inn at the end of names as if living were a thing they could simply keep doing.

He should have known better. He had spent his whole life knowing better. Safety was a story travelers told themselves before he took it from them.

The fire started an hour past midnight.

Wendell woke to the smell first — not woodsmoke, which he knew the way he knew his own hands, but something sweeter, wronger, a burning that had no honest fuel behind it. Then light against the wall, orange where there should have been dark, and then Sef's small hand shaking his shoulder, and then the sound.

The Choir was singing.

He had heard them once before, across the reed marshes of Hollowmere, a hymn that unmade what it named, and he had told himself the distance had softened it. It hadn't. The song came through the walls of the inn like water through a cracked jar, and every word of it was a word for a thing, and every thing it named grew thinner. He could feel it working on him — not his body, nothing so kind, but his *edges*. The place where Wendell Vane stopped and the world began was going soft as wet paper.

"Sef." His voice came out gravel. "Up. Now. Don't ask."

Sef didn't ask. That was the thing about the child that still undid him — no questions, just those dark eyes taking the measure of a situation faster than most grown men, and then moving. They were out the door and into the corridor with the smoke already crawling along the ceiling in a low grey tide.

Ossa's room was at the end of the hall. Wendell pounded the door with the heel of his hand. "Cartographer! We're leaving!"

The door opened before the third blow. Ossa stood in the gap with her single sleeve knotted above the stump of her missing arm and her map case strapped across her back, and her face was terrible and calm. Behind her, out the window, the night was burning.

"They came over the fields," she said. "No road. No gate. They walked straight across my wards like they were puddles." She spat the last word as if the wards had personally betrayed her, which, Wendell supposed, they had.

"How many?"

"Enough." She looked past him to Sef, and her voice changed — gentled, the way it only ever did for the child. "You remember the back way, little one?"

Sef nodded once.

"Then go. Both of you."

"Not without you," Wendell said, and heard how it sounded, and didn't care. Somewhere between Hollowmere and here the thief who never left the valley, never took risks, never carried anything heavier than a vial, had turned into a man who said things like *not without you* and meant them down to the marrow.

Ossa almost smiled. Almost. "I'm slower than I look, Vane, and I look slow. Go."

Then the corridor wall came apart.

Not burned — *unmade*. The plaster didn't crumble; it forgot itself, peeling backward into nothing, and through the gap stepped three figures in hooded robes the color of old milk, and their mouths were open, and they were singing.

The nearest one looked at Wendell, and though he couldn't see its face in the cowl's shadow, he felt seen — seen all the way down, catalogued, priced. The hymn shifted key. And in the new key, Wendell understood — not the words, the language was dead, dead as Vael — but the *intent*, the way you understand a knife:

*Give us what you carry.*

"Down," he shouted, and pulled Sef flat as the second cantor raised a hand, and the air where they'd been standing went white and silent. Not fire this time. Silence — a hole in the world's hearing, a wound shaped exactly like the space two people had occupied half a heartbeat ago. The floorboards under it aged a hundred years and rotted through.

They ran.

Down the back stairs, which were already smoke, through the kitchen where the innkeeper's boy crouched crying under the table — Wendell grabbed him by the collar and shoved him toward the cellar on instinct, some old rule surfacing, *never steal from children* transmuted now into something larger — and out into the yard.

The inn at the end of names was dying beautifully. Fire climbed its walls in ribbons, and the Choir's song wove through it, and Wendell realized with a lurch that the flames themselves were singing — that the Choir had set the fire as an instrument, that every crackle and roar was a note naming the building, naming its beams and its beds and its ledgers of impossible maps, unmaking it syllable by syllable.

And in the yard, ringed in pale robes, stood Marench.

The broker looked smaller outside Hollowmere, the way a fish looks smaller out of water. But his voice was the same, smooth as a ledger's spine. "Vane." He spread his hands, apologetic, reasonable, the gesture of a man discussing interest rates. "You made this difficult. It didn't need to be difficult. A scrap of language, a fair price, everyone walks away whole — that's all I ever offered."

"You sold the child," Wendell said. The rage came up through him like groundwater, cold and total. "You sat at my table. You drank my—"

"I *priced* the child," Marench said. "There's a difference. There's always been a difference; that's why you brought the scrap to me in the first place. We're the same trade, you and I. You just never had the stomach for scale."

Behind him, the cantors stood in their circle, singing, and Wendell saw now what they were singing *at*: Ossa.

She had come out the front after all — of course she had, stubborn as a border stone — and she stood between the inn and the tree line with her map case held against her chest like a shield, and the hymn was wrapping around her, and she was *thinning*. Not burning. Fading. Her outline going soft, her red hair draining toward grey toward colorless, the way a memory fades when someone else holds it too long.

"No—" Sef's voice, cracked open, the first word Wendell had heard the child scream since the marshes. Sef lunged forward and Wendell caught the small body around the waist and held on, held on while the child fought him with a strength that grief lent, and the cantor nearest them tilted its hooded head with something like curiosity.

It began to sing *at Sef*.

Three notes. That was all it got. Three notes aimed at the child, and Wendell felt them pass through his own chest like ice picks, and then—

Sef sang back.

One syllable of the dead tongue, high and clear and furious, cutting through the fire-roar like a blade through silk. The cantor staggered. Actually staggered, its hymn breaking stride, and in the gap Wendell heard — or thought he heard, or *remembered* hearing, the distinction had stopped mattering weeks ago — the ghost of glass bridges, a city of white towers, a mother's voice.

The Last Sigil did not like being named by its unmakers.

"Take her and go!" Ossa's voice, ragged, from the fading edge of the circle. She had dropped to one knee. Her left hand — her only hand — was pressed flat to the ground, and where it touched, grass was growing, impossibly, furiously, green spears forcing up through the Choir's silence-wounds. "The map is *here*—" she struck her own forehead with her fist, once, hard, "—in the blood-memory, Vane, it was always in the blood-memory, you don't need the case, you don't need me, GO—"

"We're not leaving you," Wendell said, and meant it, and knew even as he said it that they were, because the cantors were closing, because Sef was sobbing in his arms, because the moon stood overhead at its twelfth full face like a coin about to be spent, and because he was, underneath everything, underneath the name he'd stolen for himself and the man he'd tried to become, still fast enough to know when running was the only move left on the board.

A cantor reached Ossa. Its hooded head bent close to hers, and it sang one soft line, and Ossa — fierce, one-armed, blood-mapped Ossa — went quiet. Not dead. Quiet. Folded down into herself like a letter being closed.

Then the cantor picked her up, weightless as ash, and the circle began to withdraw toward the burning fields, and Marench gave Wendell a little bow, a broker's bow, the bow that closes a transaction.

"She'll keep," the broker called back. "Erased things keep beautifully, if you store them right. Come find us before the turn, Vane. You know the price by now. You've always known the price."

Then they were gone — robes and prisoner and song dissolving into the smoke — and the inn came down behind them with a sound like a held breath finally let go, and Wendell ran into the dark with Sef in his arms and the map of a dead kingdom beating behind his own eyes, and he did not stop until the trees swallowed the firelight.

* * *

They stopped at dawn in the hollow of a fallen oak, and Wendell built no fire, and Sef sat with knees drawn up, staring at nothing.

Grief has a texture. Wendell knew it the way he knew every traveler's sorrow he'd ever lifted and bottled: it lives in the throat, and it makes the world go slightly out of focus at the edges, and it wants a name to fix itself to so it can begin the long work of becoming bearable. He watched it take root in the child across from him and felt his own answer rising to meet it, and for a moment he let himself hate — cleanly, simply, with his whole heart — Marench, the Choir, the moon, the valley that had taught him safety was the highest law, himself most of all, for every step of the road that had led a cartographer who chose kindness to a circle of pale robes.

"We go back," he said.

Sef looked up.

"For her. After. Or during — during, maybe, I haven't worked out the order yet." He heard his own voice and barely recognized it; there was no thief's caution left in it, no broker's arithmetic. Just a man talking, badly, to a child, because the alternative was silence and silence was where the Choir lived. "But we can't go back yet. If we walk into them with nothing, we lose everything — her, the Sigil, all of it. Thirteen turns ends tomorrow night, Sef. Tomorrow night. Which means today we finish the errand."

The child's eyes were dry and enormous. Then, slowly, one small hand came out from around the drawn-up knees and found Wendell's and held it, and neither of them said anything else for a long time, because some things don't need translating out of any language, dead or living.

When the sun cleared the trees, Wendell closed his eyes and reached — carefully, the way you reach into a stranger's sleeping mind, except this time the stranger was himself — for the blood-memory Ossa had pressed into her own skull and spoken of with her fist.

It was there.

Of course it was there. It had been there since the inn, maybe since the reed marshes, maybe since the day a mute child knocked on his door: a road that must be believed in, walking west out of the fields of nothing, and at its end a door standing alone, humming with everything a kingdom used to be. He could see it now as clearly as if Ossa had inked it onto the inside of his eyelids. The road. The door. The price.

His most precious theft. The sea. He'd known it was the price since the night the cantor's hymn had shown him his own edges going soft, and he had not let himself think it since, and he thought it now, fully, and it hurt worse than the hymn had.

One more full turn. One more night of that fat white face hanging over the world.

Wendell stood, and brushed the oak-hollow dirt from his knees, and held out his hand to the last fragment of a murdered kingdom.

"Come on," he said. "We have a road to believe in."
