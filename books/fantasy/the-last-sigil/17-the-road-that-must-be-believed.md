## 17. The Road That Must Be Believed

### Wendell

Ossa's map showed a road where no road was.

She'd pressed the scrap of hide into his hands at the burning edge of the inn's ruins, her remaining arm shaking, her eyes already going toward the treeline where the Choir's hymn still hung like smoke that wouldn't clear. "West," she'd said. "Through the hollow where Vael used to be. The road is only there if you believe it. Both of you. All the way."

"And if we stop believing?"

"Then you'll be standing in a field of nothing," she said, "and the Choir will find you standing in it."

Now they were walking in it.

Wendell felt the wrongness of the ground beneath him the way a man feels a floorboard about to give — every step a small act of faith that the world would hold. There was grass underfoot, technically. There was sky above. But the grass was the color of forgetting, gray-green and thin as old thread, and the sky had no birds in it, and the horizon on either side of them simply stopped, as if someone had drawn the edge of the map and then lost interest.

The road itself ran ahead of them: a ribbon of pale stone, cracked and ancient, wide enough for two carts or one small kingdom's worth of refugees. It hadn't been there an hour ago. It appeared when Ossa had spoken its name, and it would stay, she said, for exactly as long as they kept it in their hearts.

Sef walked beside him, small hand fisted in the hem of his coat. The child hadn't spoken since the inn burned. Hadn't sung. The Sigil sat behind those dark eyes like a coal banked under ash, and Wendell found himself watching the child's face the way he used to watch travelers' faces at the border stones — reading for cracks, for doubt, for anything that might undo them both.

Because that was the thing about this road. It didn't just need belief. It needed *shared* belief. Two people agreeing, step after step after step, that the stone was real and the nothing on either side was not. One mind faltering was a crack. Two minds faltering together was a door, and doors were exactly what the Choir wanted.

"Talk to me," Wendell said.

Sef looked up.

"Anything. The glass bridges. Your mother's kitchen. What rain sounded like in Vael." He kept his voice low and even, the voice he'd used on frightened travelers, except now he was the one who needed steadying. "I want to hear all of it. I've got room. More room than you know."

Sef's mouth worked. Then, in the dead tongue first — words that made the air shiver faintly, sweetly, like a struck bell — and then in the valley's plain speech, halting but growing stronger with every sentence:

"The bridges sang. When the wind came off the sea, they sang."

"Sang how?"

"Like this." And Sef hummed — three notes, four, a phrase of something old — and the road beneath their feet *brightened*, the pale stone flushing warm gold for a dozen paces in either direction before settling back into gray.

Wendell stared down at it. "Do that again."

"It costs something," Sef said quietly. "Songs cost."

"Everything costs. That's the whole of the world." He walked on, matching the child's short stride. "Sing anyway. I'll pay whatever it takes. I'm good at paying other people's prices."

That was a joke. He could tell by Sef's face that it hadn't landed as one.

* * *

They walked through what might have been afternoon. In the hollow where Vael had been, the sun did things it wasn't supposed to do — it moved too fast, then not at all; it came from the east once, briefly, out of pure spite. Wendell kept count of their steps instead of the hours. Four hundred. Eight hundred. Twelve hundred.

At fifteen hundred steps, the hymn began.

It came from everywhere and nowhere, the way it had come over the reed marshes, but here in the erased country it was louder, closer, as though the Choir's voices traveled faster through emptiness. The melody slid into his skull without asking permission, and with it came the words — translated instantly, horribly, into the language of his own heart:

*You have never trusted anyone. You have never been trusted. The child walks beside you because the child knows no better. The child will learn.*

Wendell's foot came down on air.

For half a heartbeat there was nothing under him — no road, no ground, only the gray nothing yawning open like a mouth — and then Sef's hand clamped around two of his fingers and hauled, surprisingly strong, and his boot found stone again.

They stood very still, holding hands like children crossing a stream.

"It lies," Sef said. Not a question. A confirmation, given calmly, the way a soldier confirms the enemy's position.

"It lies," Wendell agreed. His heart was slamming against his ribs hard enough to hurt.

"It said the same thing to me. About you." Sef's grip tightened. "I didn't listen."

Something moved through Wendell's chest that had no business existing in a man like him — something warm and terrified and enormous. He crouched so they were eye to eye. Up close, Sef looked older than a child should look. Everyone did, out here.

"Listen carefully," Wendell said. "When the song comes — and it will keep coming — we say true things at each other. Out loud. True things are heavier than lies. They hold the road down."

"True things," Sef repeated.

"You start."

Sef thought. The hymn swirled around them, patient, coiling, waiting for the gap in their faith to widen into a canyon. Then the child said, in a clear voice pitched deliberately against the melody:

"You gave me your bread on the second night even though it was the last of it. You pretended you weren't hungry. You're bad at pretending."

The road flared gold from horizon to horizon.

Wendell laughed — a rough, startled sound, rusty from disuse. "Your turn's coming, thief. I've got years of true things saved up about you."

* * *

### Sef

The true-things game worked for a long time. Sef kept count the way Mama had taught counting, on knuckles and stars: forty-one true things said between them, and the road held, and the hymn grew frustrated enough to change tactics.

It started using Mama's voice.

Sef knew it was a lie immediately. Mama's voice had gone into the ground with the glass city, had gone into the Sigil, lived now only in Sef's bones. Nothing outside Sef could speak it truly. But knowing a thing and hearing a thing were different countries, and the border between them was exactly where the Choir liked to stand.

*Little one,* the false Mama sang, gentle as evening. *The tall man took something from you. Look in his pockets. Thieves always take. He stole your language once, to sell it. He would steal your name next.*

And the terrible thing — the thing that made Sef's stomach go cold and heavy — was that it was *almost* true. Wendell *had* tried to sell a scrap of the dead tongue to Marench, back at the beginning, before any of this. Sef had seen the vial. Small and green and glowing, holding a piece of home inside it like a firefly in a jar.

The road under Sef's feet went soft. Grainy. Like sand pretending to be stone.

*No,* Sef thought. *No, no—*

But doubt doesn't care about *no*. Doubt is a door that opens from the inside.

A hand closed over Sef's shoulder. Wendell. Kneeling again, bringing his tired gray face level, and there was something in his expression Sef had never seen there before — not the careful merchant mask, not the guarded flatness of a man who owned everyone's past but his own. Something rawer.

"It's talking to you about me," Wendell said. Not a question either. "I can tell because the road is dying under your feet and not mine."

Sef couldn't answer. The hymn poured Mama's voice into the silence, and Wendell flinched — he heard it too, or heard his own version — and for one awful moment they stood in the lie together, two people being unmade at each other.

Then Wendell reached into his coat and drew out the green vial.

He held it up between them. Inside, the scrap of stolen language glowed like a captive dawn — a fragment of the singing of the glass bridges, taken from Sef's sleeping mind in the early days, when Wendell had still been what the hymn said he was.

"I did take this," Wendell said. Loudly. Clearly. Into the teeth of the hymn. "I stole it while you slept, and I carried it to a broker to sell, and that's true, and you should be angry about it, and you should doubt me about it, because doubt earned honestly is worth more than trust handed out blind."

The road flickered. Sef felt it sway.

"But here's the rest of the truth," Wendell went on, and now his voice cracked down the middle, and he let it. "I couldn't sell it. Because when I held it, I remembered — something. A woman's voice calling a name that wasn't Wendell Vane. A burning city. I've been carrying that memory my whole life without knowing whose it was." He pressed the vial into Sef's small hands. "It was yours. Take it back. I'm done taking."

And Sef, holding the little jar of home, felt the doubt turn over like soil and show the clean earth underneath. Because the man kneeling in the gray field, trembling, offering back the only valuable thing he had ever successfully stolen — that man was telling the truth. Sef had grown up in a kingdom built on true names and true songs. Sef knew the weight of truth the way a mason knows stone.

The road roared back into being beneath them, wider than before, golden-edged, humming faintly with the memory of bridges.

Sef uncorked the vial and breathed the stolen language home.

* * *

### Wendell

They made camp that night — if it was night; the sky had gone a deep bruised violet that might have been dusk or might have been the color of the country's grief — in the lee of a waymarker. The marker was real, or believed-in enough to lean against: a leaning pillar of white stone carved with letters in the dead tongue, worn almost smooth. Sef read them aloud in a whisper. They said: *VAEL IS NOT GONE. VAEL IS WAITING.*

Beyond that, the road bent westward, and Wendell could see it now in the failing light — the place where the road ended. Not faded. Ended. A clean terminus, maybe half a day's walk on, where the pale stone simply stopped and beyond it stood—

A door.

Even at this distance he could see it: a free-standing gate in the middle of the nothing, tall and dark and patient, with no wall around it and no house behind it. Just a door, standing up out of the erased world like a headstone for a country.

"That's it," he breathed. "That's the way out of Vael."

Sef nodded against his shoulder. The child was nearly asleep, worn hollow by the day's walking and the day's resisting. Before sleep took them fully, Sef murmured one more true thing, barely audible:

"When we get there. You'll stay. Won't you? Until the end?"

Wendell looked at the door a long time. He thought about what he was — a bundle of stolen memories wearing a borrowed name, a man who belonged to no country, not even the one that had erased him. He thought about thresholds and which side of them men like him got to stand on.

"I'll stay until the end," he said, and found, to his surprise, that it was the easiest promise he'd ever made.

He settled Sef against the waymarker and wrapped his own coat around the both of them, and he was just drifting into the shallow half-sleep he'd learned in the marshes when the hymn changed key.

This time it wasn't lying about Mama. This time it wasn't lying about him.

This time it sang a name — a true name, a Vaeli name, a ward-keeper's name, *his* name, the one buried under thirty years of theft and erasure — and it sang it in celebration, in welcome, in triumph.

And out past the door, at the far western edge of the nothing, lights kindled one by one. Pale figures in a long procession, hooded and swaying, dozens of them, scores, the whole Choir come at last to the door they had spent a century trying to reach — arriving from the other side.

Between Wendell and the door, and between the Choir and the door, lay half a day of road.

The moon, rising thin and swollen over the rim of the erased world, was one night from full. Its twelfth full face. Its last.

Wendell shook Sef awake.

"We walk now," he said. "We don't sleep. We sing the whole way if we have to. We get to that door before they do — or before the sky finishes turning, whichever comes first."

Sef scrambled up, eyes wide and clear, fear and resolve burning together in the small face. Behind them the hymn swelled, joyful and vast, and ahead of them the door waited in the dark like the only honest thing left in the world.

Hand in hand, thief and vessel, they stepped onto the road and began to run.
