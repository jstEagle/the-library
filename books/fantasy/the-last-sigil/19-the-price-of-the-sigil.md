## 19. The Price of the Sigil

The door stood in a field of nothing.

That was the only way to describe it. Not a meadow gone grey in winter, not fog, not dark — *nothing*, a country-sized absence that pressed against the eyes like looking too long at the sun's afterimage. The grass of the believed-in road ended three paces behind them and would not grow one blade further. Ahead there was only the door: oak gone silver with age, iron hinges furred with lichen, standing upright in the void as if it had been planted there by a god with a sense of humor and no follow-through.

And it was humming.

Not a sound so much as a memory of sound — the way you remember your mother's voice years after she's gone, all the words worn off and only the warmth left. Behind that door was everything Vael had been. Glass bridges singing in the wind. Harbor bells. A language that had once been spoken by two million throats and now lived entire in one small mute child who had finally stopped being mute because there was no one left to be silent for.

Sef stood at his side, small hand wrapped around two of Wendell's fingers, gripping hard enough to hurt. He didn't mind. Pain meant he was still here, still solid, still a man made of stolen things standing on the last road he might ever walk.

Behind them — far behind, but not far enough — the hymn had begun again.

* * *

The Pale Choir had found them at dusk, as Ossa had promised they would. She had bought them an hour with fire and blood-memory, staying behind at the edge of the field with her one arm raised and her voice raised higher, shouting the names of erased places like curses, like blessings, like ammunition. Wendell had wanted to carry her. She had looked at him with her one grey eye and said, *You carry the child. I'll carry the argument.* And then the Choir's cantors had come over the horizon like a tide of white robes, and there had been no more time for arguing about who carried what.

Now their hymn rolled across the nothing, and where it touched, the nothing *moved*. That was the horror of it, the thing Wendell's mind kept sliding off of like a boot off wet clay: the song didn't just unmake things. It unmades *space*, *time*, *the idea of a thing having ever been*. He could hear it working at the edges of the road behind him, hear the believed-in stones going soft, going doubtful, going gone. The road existed only as long as they refused to doubt it, and the hymn was very good at planting doubt. It sang in a voice like his mother's, like Marench's, like his own — it sang *you are a thief, you have always been a thief, thieves do not open doors, thieves only take what better men have built—*

"Don't listen," Sef said.

The child's voice came out cracked and small. Two days ago Sef hadn't spoken at all; the dead tongue had been locked behind teeth since Vael fell, and it had taken a burning city shown from the wrong side, a confession in marsh mud, and a night of weeping that Wendell would deny to his grave to unlock it. Now the words came haltingly, in two languages braided together, Vaeli and Common trading places mid-sentence like children sharing a secret.

"I'm not listening," Wendell lied.

"You are. Your face does a thing." Sef pointed at him. "Like this." The child made a face — hollow-cheeked, eyes narrowed, mouth turned down at one corner. It was, Wendell realized with distant horror, a devastatingly accurate impression of him.

"That's my thinking face."

"It's your *listening-to-bad-songs* face." Sef tugged his fingers. "Walk."

So they walked. Three more paces. Five. The door did not seem to come closer, and then it did, all at once, the way doors do when they've decided you're ready — or when they've decided you're out of time. Up close, Wendell could see that the wood was carved. Of course it was carved. Every inch of it was covered in the Sigil, the great spell-that-unmade, its final fragment glowing faintly under Sef's collarbone like a coal wrapped in skin. But around the edges of the unmaking were older carvings, deeper cuts, almost worn away: bridges. Bells. Gulls. A woman holding up a lantern.

A warning, maybe. Or an invitation.

The humming grew louder, and beneath it Wendell began to hear something else — voices. Thousands of them, layered like sediment: people laughing, arguing, haggling over fish, calling children home for supper. The whole dead kingdom, pressing against the inside of its own front door.

Sef went rigid. "They're waiting."

"I know."

"They've been waiting a long time." The child's chin trembled. "Mama said the door would open. Mama said—" The sentence collapsed into Vaeli, fast and low, the way grief always fell back into the mother tongue when the second language ran dry.

Wendell crouched down to the child's height, knees cracking, mud from the impossible road still drying on his trousers. He thought about everything he'd learned about doors in forty-odd years of stealing through them. Most doors wanted keys. Some wanted blood. The worst ones wanted names.

This one, he already knew, wanted worse than any of those.

"Tell me what it says," he said quietly. "On the wood. The old writing."

Sef wiped their nose on a sleeve and read, lips moving. Then stopped. Looked up at him with those enormous dark eyes, and Wendell saw the understanding arrive in the child's face before the translation did — saw it land like a stone dropped into still water.

"*The door opens for a memory freely given,*" Sef recited. "It doesn't say freely given *by whom*." A pause. The child was smart. Smarter than Wendell had ever been at that age, smarter than he was now, probably. "But it says *freely*. And it says *given*." Another pause. "You can't steal your way through."

"No," Wendell agreed. "I don't suppose I can."

Behind them, the hymn changed key.

* * *

Here is the thing about the sea:

Wendell had stolen it eleven months ago from a wool merchant named Corvan Hale, at the border stones, on a Tuesday. He remembered the theft itself perfectly — he remembered every theft perfectly, that was the curse of the trade, the thief forgets nothing except himself. Corvan Hale had come down from the hill country with salt-crusted boots and a head full of a coastline he'd visited once as a boy, and Wendell had knelt beside him in the pretense of helping with a lame horse, and had slipped two fingers to the man's temple, and had *pulled*.

It came out clean. They didn't always. Some memories fought — griefs mostly, and shames, and first loves. But this one came out like a ribbon sliding off a gift: a beach of white sand, a green-green water, gulls hanging motionless in wind that smelled of kelp and cold. A boy of nine running along the tideline with his arms spread wide, pretending to be a gull himself, while somewhere behind him his father laughed at something his mother said.

Wendell had never seen the sea. No one in Hollowmere had; the valley sat four hundred miles inland and the border stones kept out anything that might bring the world in — including, presumably, the world's oceans. He had carried the vial home in his coat pocket like a stolen egg, and that night, alone in his house with its single lamp, he had held the vial to his forehead the way the brokers taught, and he had *lived* it.

He had lived it forty times. Four hundred. He knew the exact temperature of that water. He knew the way the sand held heat after sundown. He knew that if you stood with your back to the dunes and closed your eyes you could hear the surf breathing, in and out, in and out, patient as God. It was the first beautiful thing he had ever owned. Everything else he owned was other people's leavings — other men's weddings, other women's funerals, other children's birthdays, sold by the ounce to brokers who paid in bread money. But the sea was *his*. He had never sold it, though Marench had offered three times market rate. He had told himself it was because the price wasn't right. He had known, in the place where he kept the truths he couldn't afford to look at, that it was because selling it would leave him with nothing beautiful at all, and a man needs one beautiful thing or he stops being a man and becomes just a set of hands.

And now here was a door that wanted it.

"The Choir will reach us before moonrise," Sef said. The child had gone very still, watching him with the terrible patience of the young. "Ossa said the thirteenth turn comes tomorrow night. If the Sigil isn't through the door by then..." The child didn't finish. Didn't need to. When the cycle completed, the fragment would finish what the Choir had started: the unmaking would go from the world into *memory itself*, and Vael wouldn't even be forgotten. Forgetting implied there had been something to forget. Vael would simply never have been. Sef would wake up — or not wake up — a hole shaped like a child, in a world with no shape for them.

"Yes," Wendell said. "I heard the terms."

He took off his coat. He laid it carefully over a stone that wasn't quite a stone — the road's last effort at existing — because a man should meet his sacrifices dressed properly. From the inner pocket he drew the roll of oiled leather that had ridden against his ribs for eleven months, and unrolled it, and there they were: the vials. His life's work. Other people's yesterdays, glinting amber in the light that came from nowhere.

And there, third from the left, stoppered with wax from Hollowmere's chapel candles, the sea.

It looked so small. That was the joke of it, the cruelty of it. Forty years of thieving and the sum of everything he cared about fit in a tube of glass no longer than his thumb.

"You could do it," Sef said suddenly.

Wendell looked up.

"A memory freely given. Anyone's. Mine — I'd give one. I'd give the glass bridges, I remember them fine, I don't need—" The child's voice climbed, cracked, steadied. "I don't need all of them."

"No."

"You don't even know which one it wants. Maybe it wants a small one. Maybe—"

"Sef." He said the name gently. It was the first name he'd ever bothered to learn instead of steal, and saying it felt like setting something down after carrying it a long way. "It's not asking for a *payment*. Look at the carving again. Freely given. You don't get to give away someone else's grief to buy a door. And you especially don't get to give away yours, because you're going to need every scrap of it on the other side. Grief is inheritance, remember? I told you that in the marshes, and I was right, and I'm right now." He picked up the vial. It was warm. Eleven months against his heartbeat had warmed it, or the memory inside had warmed itself, the way living things do. "Besides. It's mine to give. That's rather the point."

"But it's the sea," Sef said, and the child's face did something complicated — the face of someone who has been given exactly one gift in their life and is watching the giver prepare to throw it away. "You love it."

"I do," Wendell said. "That's why it works."

He explained it the best he could, kneeling there at the end of the world, because the child deserved to understand the arithmetic. A door that opened for any trifle would open for anyone, and it had stayed shut through the fall of a nation. It wanted weight. It wanted the thing that, if you gave it, would actually cost. The Choir's cantor had offered him his stolen past, his true name, his erased life — and the offer had tempted him precisely because it asked for nothing beautiful in return, only a child. That was the difference between a price and a bargain, he was learning. A price you pay with your own hand. A bargain someone else pays for.

"There's another thing," he added, because honesty was apparently a habit now, God help him. "If I give it away, I won't remember it. That's how giving works, at a door like this. It's not like selling. Selling, the broker copies and the original stays put. This is different. This is the whole memory, root and branch, poured out of me and into the wood. Tomorrow morning I won't know what the sea looks like. I won't know I ever knew." He smiled, and found, to his surprise, that the smile was real. "Which means tomorrow morning, some traveler will walk through Hollowmere with a coastline in his head, and I'll kneel down by his horse, and I'll lift it, and it'll be brand new. First beautiful thing I ever owned. Again."

Sef stared at him. Then the child laughed — a wet, broken, furious laugh. "That's the most thief-brained thing I have ever heard."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a compliment!"

"It absolutely was."

But the laughter died quickly, the way laughter dies at doors, and then it was just the two of them and the hum and the hymn coming closer, and Wendell stood up, and pulled the wax from the vial with his teeth.

* * *

He had expected the giving to feel like loss. That was what he had braced for: the cold slide of it leaving, the sudden poverty, the amputee's phantom ache where the beautiful thing used to be.

It didn't feel like loss.

It felt like *opening a hand*.

He pressed the vial to his forehead the old way, but reversed — not pulling, *pushing* — and the sea rose up in him one last time. White sand. Green water. A boy running the tideline with his arms flung wide. The surf breathing, in and out, patient as God. He held it the way you hold a bird — firmly enough that it stays, loosely enough that it lives — and then he spoke aloud, to the door, to whatever old carver had cut *freely given* into the wood a thousand years ago:

"This is mine. Nobody gave it to me and nobody lent it to me. I stole it fair, and I loved it badly, and I kept it selfishly, and it's the only thing I've ever had that I didn't take from someone who needed it more." His voice shook. He let it shake. "Take it. Please. Not because it's owed. Because it's *wanted*."

And he let go.

The memory went out of him like light going out of a lamp — no, like light going *into* a lamp, a wick catching, a flame standing up. He felt it pass through his skull, down his arm, out through the glass, and the vial emptied between one heartbeat and the next, and for a moment he stood there holding a little tube of nothing, feeling the shape of the hole where the sea had been.

Then the door exhaled.

There was no better word for it. The whole silvered oak drew breath — the humming swelled into a chord, the chord into a choir, and oh, that was a bitter irony, that the true Choir sounded like *this*, like ten thousand voices saying *come home, come home, come home* — and the lichen fell away from the hinges, and the iron swung inward without a creak, and light came out.

Not sunlight. Not lamplight. *Remembered* light — the gold of a Vaeli afternoon, the shimmer of glass bridges, the particular brightness of a harbor at festival time. And out of that light spilled the smell of bread and brine and cut grass, and the sound of bells, real bells, and a voice — a woman's voice, high and glad, calling a name down a street that had waited centuries to be called down again.

Sef made a sound Wendell had never heard a human make. Half sob, half song. The child stepped toward the light, and stopped, and turned around.

"You're coming too," Sef said. Not a question. A demand, delivered with the full imperious authority of a nine-year-old heir of a dead kingdom, and it broke Wendell's heart cleanly, like a wishbone.

"Sef—"

"You're *coming too*. You gave the door your — your sea. Doors don't take payment from people they're going to turn away. Even I know that. Ossa would know the rule. *You're coming—*"

"Sef." He crouched one last time. Took both the child's hands in his, thief's hands, keeper's hands. "Look at me. There's something I have to tell you, and you're going to hate it, and I need you to listen anyway."

Behind them, at the edge of the field of nothing, the hymn crested — and broke, like a wave against rock, as the Choir reached the border of the believed-in road and found that the road no longer believed in *them*. But Wendell barely heard it. He was looking at the child. He was memorizing the child, the way he'd once memorized the sea, storing it somewhere no broker could touch.

"When you get inside — when your people see you, when your mother's sisters see you — someone is going to ask who brought the Last Sigil home." His throat closed. He forced it open. "Tell them the truth. Tell them a thief carried it the last hundred miles. And then tell them he stayed outside, because a man made of stolen things belongs nowhere wholly — but a man can stand in a doorway, Sef. A man can keep a door. Someone should keep it. So that if anyone else ever runs out of a burning country with a spell in their bones, there's a light on for them."

Sef was crying now, silently, furiously, the way children cry when they refuse to be comforted. "That's a stupid plan."

"It's a magnificent plan."

"It's a *stupid*—" The child hurled themselves forward and hit him like a wave hits a seawall, arms around his neck, face buried in his shoulder, and Wendell held on and let the seawall crumble. "You're supposed to come *home* with me. That's how it works. That's how it worked in every story Mama told. Somebody carries somebody home."

"Some stories," Wendell said into the child's hair, "somebody keeps the porch light lit instead." He pulled back. Held the small tear-streaked face in his hands. "Go home, Sef of the glass bridges. Go home and be loud in two languages. Grow up insufferable. Come argue with an old man through a door someday, when you're tall and the kingdom's rebuilding and you've got grievances to air. I'll be here. Keeping the light."

For a moment the child searched his face — hunting for doubt, for a lie, for the listening-to-bad-songs face — and found none. Then Sef stepped backward over the threshold, into the gold, into the bell-sound, into the country that had been waiting, and the Last Sigil rose up under the child's skin like dawn under a horizon, and the door began, slowly, gently, with the tenderness of all endings done rightly, to close.

Through the narrowing gap, Wendell heard a woman's voice cry out in Vaeli — joy, pure and enormous, a mother-tongue welcoming a child home — and he heard Sef answer, clear and unafraid, in the same tongue, and then the gap was a seam, and the seam was a line, and the line was gone.

The door stood shut in the field of nothing. And the hymn behind him — the false hymn, the hungry hymn, the Choir's long unmaking — stopped.

Stopped dead.

Wendell turned. Far across the void, the white-robed figures stood frozen mid-note, and the frozen note began, horribly, beautifully, to turn inward, folding back on itself like a snake swallowing its own song —

And the moon, rising over the edge of the nothing, began to show its thirteenth face.
