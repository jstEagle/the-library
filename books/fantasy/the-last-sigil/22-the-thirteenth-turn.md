## 22. The Thirteenth Turn

The valley remembered Vael the way a man remembers a scar he never earned.

Wendell noticed it first in small things. A shepherd at the border stones paused mid-sentence and said, *glass bridges*, though he had no reason to know such words existed. A woman selling plums hummed three notes of a song that had not been sung for a hundred years, and when Wendell asked where she'd heard it, she only frowned and touched her throat as if something had passed through it on its way out.

The world's memory was knitting itself back together, thread by thread, and Hollowmere — valley that forgets — was forgetting how to forget.

He kept the door open now.

That was the change people remarked on most, when travelers came down from the pass. The thief's cottage at the edge of the reed marshes, once shuttered and smokeless, stood with its door wide to the autumn wind. And they came to it. Not all at once — word travels slowly into a valley that resists being found — but they came. Pilgrims, mostly, at first: folk who had dreamed of a kingdom of glass bridges and woken weeping without knowing why. Then others. A scholar carrying a broken slate inscribed in the dead tongue. An old soldier who remembered standing in a harbor that had never existed, waiting for ships out of Vael.

They brought offerings.

That was Sef's doing, though the child didn't know it. Word had gone out through whatever channels erased things travel — through Ossa's maps, through the flickering restoration of a kingdom's name on old charts — that there was a keeper in Hollowmere. That he had carried the Last Sigil to its door. That he had given up the sea to do it.

So they brought him memories. Freely given, which was the part that undid him every time. A memory of a wedding. A memory of a mother's hands kneading bread. A memory of snow falling on a city whose name was coming back letter by letter. They set them on his doorstep like eggs in a wren's nest, wrapped in cloth and wax, and Wendell carried them inside and shelved them, and he did not sell a single one.

Marench would have laughed himself sick.

* * *

He thought about Marench more than he expected to, that first year. Not with fear — the broker had fled the valley the night the Choir burned, and no one had seen his fat rings or heard his soft voice since — but with something like gratitude curdled by grief. Marench had taught him the trade. Had shown a half-starved boy with quick fingers and an empty head how to slip a stranger's past loose like a purse from a belt. Everything Wendell knew about touching another mind, he'd learned in that smoke-filled shop behind the tannery, and everything he knew now — the gentleness of it, the care — he'd learned alone, after, in the years when stealing was the only warmth available to him.

He wondered sometimes if Marench had known what he was making. Whether you could train a ward-keeper's hands to thieve and expect anything but this: a man who spent his whole life taking until someone taught him, at last, how to give.

The vials lined his walls now, hundreds of them, each labeled in his careful hand. He drank almost none. He had learned that a memory held is worth more than a memory swallowed — that keeping other people's pasts safe was its own kind of life, even if it wasn't the one he'd have chosen.

Even if some nights he still woke reaching for the sea.

* * *

It was gone. He had to keep reminding himself of that, or he'd catch himself standing at the shelf where it used to live, hand raised toward the space between two vials of ordinary joy. The smell of salt. The green-black swell of water under a gray sky. The gull crying over a harbor he'd robbed from a traveler eleven years ago — the first beautiful thing he had ever owned, the thing that had made him a thief in truth, because once you've tasted the sea you cannot rest until every horizon belongs to you.

He had poured it into the door out of Vael, and the door had opened, and Sef had walked through into the arms of a kingdom returning from nothing, and Wendell had stood in the field of un-being and watched the light close over the threshold like water closing over a stone.

He did not regret it. That was the strange part, the part he turned over at night like a coin worn smooth. He missed the sea the way you miss a limb — constantly, uselessly, with a phantom ache that flared at the sound of wind through reeds — but he did not regret it. Some things you steal so long you forget they were ever yours to give away. And then you give them, and find out what they were for.

The moon was rising full when the knock came.

* * *

He counted the turns now. Ossa had taught him that before she left — before she'd shouldered her one-armed pack of blood-drawn charts and walked out of the valley with the whole restored map of Vael humming against her back. *Thirteen turns make a cycle,* she'd said, tapping the twelfth mark on her wrist. *The Choir is ash. But cycles don't stop for anyone. Next year, when the thirteenth comes round again, stand at your door and be somebody worth knocking on.*

Twelve full moons since the door closed. Tonight made thirteen.

The knock was small. Two raps, polite, the way children knock when they're afraid the answer might be no.

Wendell crossed the room and stood with his hand on the latch, and for a moment he was back on that moonless night a year ago, listening to words burn cold through the wood, feeling his stolen memories shudder in their vials like birds in cages. Fear had come first, then. Fear always came first with him; it was the oldest thing he owned.

But he was not that man anymore. Or he was, and also this one. Both true. He opened the door.

Sef stood in the moonlight, taller — a year does that, even across the border of a returned kingdom — hair longer, braided in the Vaeli style, glass beads clicking softly at the ends. Thirteen years old now, maybe fourteen. Wearing a traveler's coat too big in the shoulders and boots scuffed from a long road, and grinning — grinning — with tears already running freely down both cheeks.

And speaking.

"Hello, Wendell," the child said, in the clear common tongue of the valley, each word chosen with visible care, savored like fruit. Then, laughing at the wonder of it, in the dead tongue that was dead no longer: *words in Vaeli*, bright and flowing, and Wendell understood maybe one in ten and understood all of it anyway.

"You talk," Wendell said. Stupidly. Wonderingly.

"A year of teachers." Sef shrugged, self-conscious suddenly, the grin folding inward. "The kingdom came back, and with it everyone who remembers it. There are singers now. Real ones. They say my mother's song is in half the cradles in Vael." The child's voice dropped. "I told them about you."

"I'm not sure I want to know what you told them."

"That you stole the sea," Sef said solemnly, "and gave it back to the world." A pause. "They liked that better than the true version."

"The true version is that I stole everything else, too."

"They know that too." Sef looked past him into the cottage — at the shelves, the hundreds of labeled vials catching the moonlight, the keeper's careful handwriting on every one. Something moved across the young face. Not judgment. Recognition. "You kept them. All this time. You never sold them?"

"Not one."

"Why?"

Wendell leaned against the doorframe and considered the question honestly, because Sef deserved honesty the way a garden deserves rain — not occasionally, but as a habit. "Because I finally understood what Marench never did," he said. "A memory isn't a purse. It's a person, folded small. You can carry them. You can keep them safe. You can even give them back." He tapped the nearest shelf, where a farmer's memory of his wedding day waited to be returned to a man who'd wept with gratitude last spring. "But you don't own them. I spent thirty years learning that. You learned it at six. Show-off."

Sef laughed — and it was a different laugh than a year ago, deeper, less startled by itself. The child stepped forward and hugged him, quick and fierce, face pressed into his coat, and Wendell stood stiff as a fence post for three full heartbeats before his arms remembered what they were for and came around the narrow shoulders and held on.

The moon climbed. Neither of them moved for a long time.

* * *

Later, with soup steaming on the stove and the door still open to the autumn night — open, always open now — Sef wandered the shelves reading labels aloud, translating some into Vaeli just to hear the old words live in the air. Wendell watched from his chair and felt something settle in his chest that he had no name for. Not peace exactly. Peace was too finished a word. This was more like a fire kept burning overnight: banked, tended, ready.

"Ossa sent this." Sef produced a rolled chart from the coat, sealed with wax pressed in the shape of an eye. "She said — her words — 'Tell the keeper his road held.' She drew it herself. From memory." Sef's voice went quiet on the last word, because they both knew what Ossa's memory-maps cost, and what they were worth.

Wendell broke the seal and unrolled the chart, and there it was: the impossible road, inked in brown that was unmistakably blood-memory, running from the border stones of Hollowmere through the field of un-being to a door drawn in fine gold line. And around the door, in Ossa's cramped sure hand, a note:

*The road exists as long as it is believed in. So do you. — O.*

He read it twice. Then he hung the map above the hearth, next to the empty hook where the sea-vial had lived for eleven years, and decided the wall looked better this way.

"There's something else," Sef said. The child had gone still by the window, looking out at the marshes silvering under the thirteenth turn. "That's partly why I came. Not just to visit. Though — yes, to visit. Mostly to visit." A breath. "Wendell. The singers found something. In the deep archive, under the glass bridges, where the Choir couldn't reach. Records. Ward-keeper records." The child turned. Moonlight lay along one cheek. "Your name is in them."

The room went very quiet. The soup ticked on the stove. Out in the marshes a heron cried once and fell silent, as if listening too.

"I had a name," Wendell said carefully. "Before I erased myself. Before I ran. I don't remember it. That was rather the point of erasing myself."

"I know." Sef crossed the room and sat on the floor beside his chair, knees drawn up, the way the child used to sit a year ago, small and mute and burning with a language no one alive should speak. "I know what you did. What you ran from. I know all of it — you confessed in the mud at the reed gate, remember? You don't get to pretend I came here innocent of you." A ghost of the grin. "But Wendell. The record doesn't say what you're afraid it says. It says a ward-keeper took a fragment of the Sigil and ran — yes. And it says why." The child's voice steadied, became almost formal, the cadence of recitation learned from singers. "*He stole the fragment to keep the Choir from finishing the spell. He hid it in the only vessel they could not unmake: a child who could not be found. He erased his own name so that no hymn could ever call him home and take it back.*"

Wendell's hands had gone cold around his cup. "That's not how I remember—"

"You don't remember. That's my point." Sef reached up and touched the old vial on the shelf above the hearth — the oldest one, the burning city, the woman's voice calling him by another name. The one he'd opened in chapter and chapter of guilt. "You've been punishing yourself for a crime you invented. The Choir needed you to believe you were a coward and a thief, because a man who believes that will never come looking for the truth of what he actually did." The child's eyes were bright and fierce. "You weren't the fall of Vael, Wendell. You were the reason there was anything left to bring back. You were the errand. I was just — I was just the package."

The cup trembled in his hands. He set it down.

For a long moment he couldn't speak. Thirty years of vertigo, of self-loathing transmuted into resolve and resolve into service and service into this quiet room with its shelves of other people's joy — and underneath it all, the wound he'd built his whole second life around, the belief that he had run, abandoned, betrayed — and a child of thirteen was telling him, in two languages, with the authority of a kingdom's archive behind it: *you stayed. In the only way that mattered, you stayed.*

"The name," he managed at last. His voice came out rough. "What was it?"

Sef smiled, slow and certain, and spoke a single word in Vaeli — and then, gently, translated it into the tongue of the valley, giving him back the sound of himself like a coat returned after a long winter.

And it fit.

Of course it fit. It had always fit; he had simply refused to wear it.

Wendell Vane — no. Wendell *something else*, ward-keeper of Vael, the man who stole the Sigil to save it, who gave the sea to open the door, who kept the door of a forgotten valley open ever after — sat in his chair while the thirteenth turn rode high over Hollowmere, and let the name settle into him the way rain settles into ground that has been dry a long time. Not a transformation. A return.

"Well," he said finally, and his voice cracked on it, and he found he didn't mind. "Well."

"Will you come?" Sef asked softly. "To Vael. Someday. They'd want — I'd want—"

Wendell looked around the little room. At the shelves. The open door. The dark valley beyond it, breathing, remembering, filling slowly with pilgrims and plum-sellers and dreams of glass bridges. At the hook above the hearth where a map now hung instead of a theft.

"No," he said, and smiled, and meant every kind thing in it. "Not someday. But you'll come back. Every turn of the moon, if you can manage it. Every thirteenth turn at the very least. Knock on this door, and I'll have soup on, and you can tell me the new songs." He reached out and rested his hand briefly on the child's braided head, the gesture of a keeper, not a thief. "Family isn't where you end up, Sef. I learned that late. It's who keeps coming back."

Sef beamed, wiped both sleeves across both cheeks, and said something in Vaeli that Wendell — who was, as of tonight, a man with a name and a kingdom and a language slowly returning to him — understood completely.

Outside, the moon began its slow climb down the sky, ordinary and whole, and the valley of Hollowmere slept without forgetting, and somewhere far off, at the edge of hearing, a new song was being written with room in it for everyone who had ever been lost.

Then Sef, still grinning, nodded toward the dark road beyond the marshes.

"One more thing," the child said. "There's someone on the road out there. Been following me since the pass. Says he knows you." A pause, and the grin sharpened into something older, warier, borrowed straight from Wendell's own face. "Says his name is Marench — and says he's brought your sea back."
