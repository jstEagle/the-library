## 1. Salt on the Pass

The snow came in from the west like a debt collector — quiet at first, then all at once, and never alone.

Yara Lockridge had learned to read weather the way other people read faces. This one she did not like. It had that flat grey patience of a storm meaning to stay the week, and she still had six miles of high switchbacks between her and Hollowmere, with a mule who hated winter more than she hated taxes and a cargo worth more than both their lives.

"Come on, Sorrel," she muttered, hauling the lead rope over a lip of blue ice. "You can hate me at the bottom."

Sorrel expressed her opinion with a flattened ear and a plume of breath like smoke off a forge, but she climbed.

Below them, the Thornfell passes fell away in white terraces, each ridge stitched with the ghost-lines of old roads. Some were guild roads, cut and graded two centuries back when the Lockridges had been a name instead of three people and a ledger full of bad paper. Most were older than that — older than roads had any right to be — and those were the ones Yara's grandmother had taught her to find: the paths nobody sold because nobody remembered they existed. Marette Lockridge called them the quiet ways, and she'd taught Yara to walk them with her eyes half-shut, listening for something under the wind.

*Listen for what?* young Yara had asked once, ten years old and frostbitten at the ears.

*For whether the mountain's asleep,* Marette said. *It usually is.*

Tonight it wasn't. Tonight there was a hum in the stone beneath the snow, faint as a struck bell heard through a wall, and Yara told herself it was water moving under ice, or wind in the scree chimneys, or the ordinary nonsense of a body too long in the cold. She was a practical woman. Practical women did not hear mountains humming.

She carried medicine.

That was the whole of the job, this last run before the passes closed: forty pounds of willowbark tincture, feverfew paste, honey packed in waxed jars, and three small bottles of something the herbalist in Lowmark had called *greywort compound* and refused to discuss further. The fever in Hollowmere had started with the first hard frost and by now, if the letters were honest, had taken one person in five. Children first. The village couldn't pay much — a few coins, a promise of spring wool, a carved token Yara could sell to a collector down-country. Marette had waved the payment ledger shut when Yara raised it.

"Take it anyway," she'd said, coughing into the crook of her elbow. Her knuckles had gone the wrong color lately, grey as old pewter. "A road you don't run in a famine isn't a road anymore. It's a story."

So Yara ran it, because that was what Lockridges did, even when there were only three of them left and the youngest of the three spent her nights doing arithmetic about how many winters the guildhall roof had left in it. The answer was two. Maybe three if she re-lagged the north chimney herself.

The storm caught them an hour past the saddle, exactly as she'd known it would.

It came down the eastern face in a single white sheet, erasing the trail behind her like a hand wiping slate. Yara got Sorrel's hood up and her own scarf over her face and walked by feel and by memory, counting paces between cairns she could not see. This was the part of the work no one romanticized — the blind hours, the cold working inward like a slow honest loan, the moment when your own breath became the loudest thing in the world.

Somewhere in the third blind hour, the humming stopped being deniable.

It came up through her boot-soles now, a deep patient tone, and with it — and this was the part that made her stop dead in the drift, mule steaming beside her, snow hissing down — with it came warmth. Not sunlight warmth. Hearth warmth. The specific, unreasonable warmth of standing near a fire someone else built, in a house where someone expects you.

Yara stood in a whiteout on a mountain pass where nothing lived above the treeline, and felt *welcome*.

"Right," she said aloud, because saying things aloud kept the mind on its side of the fence. "You're frozen. You're hallucinating. Keep walking."

She kept walking. The warmth kept walking with her.

And here was the thing she would not tell Marette, would not tell Brenn, would barely admit to herself in the privacy of her own skull: the path got easier. Not merely easier to follow — though it did that, her boots finding bare stone where there should have been four feet of fresh snowfall — but easier *to walk*. The grade gentled. The wind parted around them like a crowd around a funeral bier. Twice she crossed stretches of trail where the snow lay undisturbed in perfect sheets except for a single line of prints already melted open ahead of her, going the way she was going, made by no feet she could imagine.

Sorrel followed the prints without being asked. That frightened Yara more than anything else. Animals knew things. Sorrel walked that night like a creature following a hand she trusted.

They came down out of the storm at moonrise into the hanging valley of Hollowmere, and the village below was burning with fever-light — candles in every window, which was either prayer or vigil, and in Hollowmere it had always been hard to tell the difference.

* * *

Hollowmere prayed to gods that everyone knew were dead.

That was the joke of the low country, told over beer in every tavern from Lowmark to the coast: up in the Thornfells the mountain folk still lit candles to the Nine Old Ones, still left cream out at Bracknight, still touched iron to their lips before swearing oaths — all for a pantheon that had gone silent before anyone's grandmother's grandmother drew breath. A hundred years of silence. Two hundred. The priests of the low cities had declared the old gods dead so long ago that the declaration itself had become scripture, and the free cities had gotten very comfortable with a heaven that demanded nothing and answered less.

Yara had never thought much about it either way. Gods were weather to her — something that happened overhead, that you dressed for, that didn't care about your ledgers. She believed in rope, in ice, in the honesty of a well-loaded mule. When the Hollowmere folk pressed little carved spirals into her hands and asked her to carry prayers down-mountain to *anyone who might listen*, she took the tokens because tokens sold, and she promised because promising cost nothing, and she did not believe a word of it passed anywhere further than the bottom of her pack.

The village headman met her at the gate, a stooped man named Aldric with frost in his beard and panic in his eyes, and he nearly wept when he saw the mule.

"You came." He said it the way people said *the tide turned* or *the war ended*. "In this. Nobody comes in this."

"The Lockridge roads don't close for weather," Yara said, which was true, and which was also the closest thing to a creed she had.

They unloaded in the headman's hall while the sick moaned in the houses around them — a sound Yara knew from a bad winter in her childhood, the sound of a village holding its breath. She counted the cargo out jar by jar, and when she came to the three small bottles of greywort compound, Aldric went still.

"That's the one," he whispered. "The dream I had. Three bottles, dark glass, a red seal."

Yara looked at the seals. They were red. They were also, she happened to know, red because the herbalist sealed everything with red wax, having bought a lifetime supply of it cheap.

"It's just medicine," she said. "Willowbark and feverfew and honey. The greywort's for the worst cases, and honestly, I don't know what's in it. Take it or leave it."

"We'll take it." He handled the bottles like relics. "We've been praying, mistress Lockridge. Every house, every night, since the fever started. Praying the old way. My grandmother's words, and hers before her." He laughed, wet-eyed and embarrassed. "I know how it sounds down-country. Dead gods. But my grandmother always said the Nine weren't dead, only sleeping, and that sleepers can be woken, and that the mountain remembers who fed it."

"People say a lot of things on mountains," Yara said. "Thin air."

But she dreamed badly that night in the headman's loft, dreams of a lamp in a dark vault and a voice like a struck bell saying her name — not Yara, not Lockridge, but some older name, a name she almost caught, the way you almost catch a word spoken in the room next door.

* * *

By morning, the medicine had worked impossibly well.

Yara understood medicine the way she understood freight rates: practically, and with a clear eye for what it could and couldn't do. Willowbark brought fevers down a degree and bought time. Feverfew helped with the aching. Honey kept wounds sweet. None of it — none of it — emptied a sickhouse overnight.

She walked through Hollowmere at dawn and found the sick sitting up. Not all of them; not yet. But children who had been delirious at midnight were drinking broth and complaining about it, which was the most hopeful sound a child could make. An old woman Yara had seen carried to her bed the evening before was standing in her doorway, wrapped in blankets, watching the sunrise come up the eastern peaks like a person greeting a friend she'd expected to die before seeing again.

"The greywort," the woman said when Yara paused. "One spoon, and I dreamed of my mother's kitchen. Haven't dreamed true since I was a girl. And in the dream my mother said, *tell the runner the mountain knows her name.*"

"I'm not a runner," Yara said automatically. "I'm a carrier. Runners are lowland work."

The old woman smiled with too few teeth and said nothing, and her saying nothing was somehow worse than an answer.

At the headman's table, breaking fast on barley porridge, Yara laid it out plainly, because plainness was a tool like any other. "Whatever was in those three bottles, it wasn't willowbark. I want to know where your grandmother's recipe came from, and I want to know why the fever broke in a single night in houses where we used it and nowhere else."

Aldric wouldn't meet her eyes. "Greywort grows on the high faces. It's old craft. Mountain craft."

"It doesn't grow on the high faces. I cross the high faces eleven times a year. I know every plant up there that survives the altitude, and greywort isn't one of them."

"Then perhaps the mountain grew it special," Aldric said, with the mild stubbornness of a man defending a miracle against a bookkeeper, "for folk who asked nicely."

Yara set down her spoon. Outside, the valley lay silent under new sun, and the snow on the peaks burned white-gold, and underneath the ordinary morning sounds — hens, axes, a child laughing, actually laughing, two days after the whole village had been writing names on scraps of cloth to remember the dead by — underneath all of it ran that hum again. Fainter down here. Patient. Like something enormous turning over in its sleep, getting comfortable, deciding to stay awake a while longer.

She thought of the melted footprints on the pass. Of Sorrel following a hand she trusted. Of the dream-name she hadn't quite caught, sitting in her chest like a coin in a crack in the floor.

"Mistress?" Aldric said.

"When I come back through in the spring," Yara said slowly, "if your sick are all alive and your gran's recipe has grown another miracle, I'm going to ask you again. And you're going to tell me the truth, because I'll have earned it. Agreed?"

"Agreed," he said, and then, quietly, with the air of a man paying a small debt early: "Mistress Lockridge — the tokens. The prayers you carried down for us last autumn, and the autumn before. Did you deliver them?"

"I told you. There's no one—"

"Not whether there was anyone. Whether you delivered them. Where you put them."

Yara opened her mouth to give the practical answer — *I keep them in a box, they're inventory, they sell to collectors* — and found that the practical answer had developed, overnight, a taste like a lie.

"There's a shelf," she heard herself say. "In the guildhall. North wall, where the morning light comes in. My grandmother puts them there. I always assumed it was sentiment."

Aldric nodded as if this explained everything, which was the most unsettling thing that had happened all morning, and Yara had spent the night following ghost-prints through a blizzard.

* * *

She left Hollowmere two days later, ahead of schedule, telling herself the early start was about beating the next storm front. It was partly true. Storms were real. Ledgers were real. The two winters left in the guildhall roof were brutally, comfortingly real, and Yara held onto them the whole ride home like a woman gripping a rail in rough water.

But the mountain hummed under her the entire way, and on the second afternoon, crossing the saddle in clear cold light, she saw something that she would turn over in her mind for years afterward.

On the far side of the pass, at the place where the old quiet way branched off from the guild road — the branch Marette had shown her once and sworn her to silence about, the one that went *down*, though down to where Marette had never said — the snow had melted.

Not thinned. Melted. A circle thirty feet across, bare black stone steaming gently in the freezing air, green shoots already pushing up through the cracks as if it were April instead of the teeth of winter. At the center of the circle stood a shrine Yara had never seen before, though she knew this stretch of trail better than she knew her own hands. Small, ancient, older than the guild, older maybe than roads. A spiral carved into a standing stone, and at the spiral's heart, a niche.

Someone had left a lamp burning in the niche. A little clay dish of oil, flame steady in the wind that should have killed it.

And laid neatly beside the lamp, arranged with the care people gave to offerings, was a single item that turned Yara's blood to meltwater:

A ledger page. Torn out. Old paper, old ink, folded twice.

Her name was written on it.

Not *Yara*. The other name — the dream-name, the almost-caught word from the room next door — written in a hand she recognized with a lurch, because she had grown up watching it fill the guild's books column by careful column. Her grandmother's hand.

Yara sat Sorrel in the snow at the edge of the warm circle for a long time, wind pulling at her hood, and did not dismount. Everything practical in her said: *ride on, get home, burn nothing, touch nothing.* But she had carried prayers down a mountain for nine years without delivering them, and something in the mountain had apparently decided the account was overdue.

She swung down. Walked to the stone. Took the page with fingers that did not entirely obey.

She read it once standing up. Then she sat down in the snow, inside the warm circle where the wind couldn't reach, and read it again, slower, while the little lamp burned and the mountain hummed beneath her like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

Then she folded it away inside her coat, next to her heart, mounted up, and rode for the guildhall as fast as Sorrel could travel, because her grandmother owed her an explanation, and Marette Lockridge was going to give her one if Yara had to shake it out of her dying body.

Which, by the look of the grey in her knuckles, might not take long at all.
