## 2. The Guild of Three

The guildhall came into view at dusk, the way it always did — a dark shape against the darker mountain, one chimney smoking, the carved lintel over the door worn smooth by three generations of hands that had touched it for luck before stepping inside. Yara had been touching that lintel since she was tall enough to reach it on her toes. She touched it now, and found her fingers trembling in a way she refused to name.

The medicine was still in her pack. She'd delivered most of it to Hollowmere, as contracted, and watched with her own eyes as fevered children drank the bitter tincture and stopped shivering within the hour. Not eased. Stopped. The fever broke like a wave hitting rock, clean and total, and old Widow Sarn had wept over the empty bottle and said, *the gods are listening again*, and Yara had laughed at her because there was nothing else to do.

But she'd kept back two vials. She told herself it was standard practice — reserve stock, contingency, any of the hundred phrases a smuggler uses to dress up superstition in workman's clothes. She'd kept them back because something in her chest had gone cold watching those fevers break so fast, and cold things wanted holding close.

She pushed open the door.

The hall smelled the way it had smelled her whole life: woodsmoke, tallow, old leather, the ghost of every cargo ever stacked along its walls. But thinner now. A smell remembered rather than lived in, like a coat hung up too long. The great room where forty runners had once taken their meals stood half-empty, benches pushed against the walls to make room for dust, and the long table where Marette Lockridge had held court over ledgers and grudges alike held only a single lamp and a single cup.

Brenn sat by the fire with his boots off, feet wrapped in rags, mending a harness strap that had been mended so many times it was more stitch than leather. He looked up when the door opened, and his face did something complicated — relief first, bright and boyish despite his seventy years, then quickly shuttered behind the flat expression he wore for bad news.

"You're back," he said. "Roads were kind?"

"Roads were roads." She set down her pack. "Hollowmere's clear. Fever's gone through the whole village like it was never there."

"Good," Brenn said, too fast. "That's good."

Yara looked at him. Brenn had taught her to read a man's hands before his face, because faces lied and fingers fumbled, and Brenn's hands had stopped working on the harness entirely.

"Where's my grandmother?"

* * *

Marette Lockridge was in the ledger room, because of course she was. She had slept in that room some nights toward the end, Yara would learn later, unwilling to be more than an arm's length from the books. She sat now behind the great table with a blanket over her shoulders and the winter volume open before her, quill in hand, and for one merciful moment Yara thought: *she's fine, she's working, Brenn's gone soft.*

Then Marette looked up, and Yara saw the color of her.

Her grandmother had always been the color of iron and weather — grey-eyed, grey-streaked, hard-edged, but alive underneath, the way iron is alive under rust. Now the aliveness was gone. Her skin had the translucence of paper held to a flame, and her eyes were sunk deep, burning brighter than they should, the way eyes burn when the body around them is failing and something inside refuses to admit it.

"Yara." Her voice was rough gravel. "You're late."

"Storm on the high shoulder cost me a day." Yara crossed the room and knelt beside the chair before her grandmother could object, which Marette immediately began to do. "Don't. Let me look at you."

"I'm not a cargo to be inspected—"

"You're exactly a cargo to be inspected, and you've been mislabeled as 'fine' for months, and I'm correcting the manifest." Yara took her grandmother's wrist. The pulse underneath fluttered like a moth against glass. "How long?"

"How long what?"

"Don't."

Marette looked at her for a long moment. Outside, wind worried the shutters. Somewhere in the walls, a beam settled with a sound like a sigh.

"Since midsummer," Marette said finally, and pulled her hand free, and returned to the ledger as if the conversation were concluded. "There's a debt from the Kestrel family outstanding since spring. I want you to ride out—"

"You'll ride nowhere and neither will I, not until—" Yara stopped. Steadied herself against the chair. She had faced armed toll-men, avalanches, a customs officer in Vaskal's colors with a suspicious mind and a good memory, and none of it had made her stomach drop the way her grandmother's handwriting did just then — the letters wobbling, drifting off the ruled lines, the hand of a woman who could no longer trust her own body to hold a straight course.

"Brenn," Marette called, without looking up. "Tell her."

"Tell me what?" Yara stood. "Tell me what?"

Brenn appeared in the doorway with a bowl of broth he'd clearly been sent in with twice already. He set it down on the corner of the table with exaggerated care, as if it might detonate.

"The guild," he said. "It's three of us now. Has been since harvest. Torv went south for the winter routes and never sent word. Denna took her family over the border to her sister's. The Corvane contract fell through — their whole operation's dissolved. And the lowland buyers..." He rubbed his jaw. "Nobody's buying passes anymore, girl. They're buying soldiers' loyalty instead. Times are changing down there."

"Times change every year. That's why people need smugglers."

"Not like this." Brenn's voice dropped. "Shrines are coming down in the valley towns. Old shrines. Stone ones nobody remembered who built. Vaskal's priests are paying good coin for them broken. And folk are lining up to swing the hammers."

Yara felt the cold thing in her chest again, the one from Hollowmere. She thought of the medicine working impossibly well, of Widow Sarn weeping over prayers answered, of fevered children saved by herbs that should have only eased. The world was tilting somewhere beneath her feet, slow as a glacier, and everyone seemed to know about it except her.

"And her?" She nodded at Marette. "How long has she been — what is it? What does the physicker say?"

"There's no physicker left within thirty miles who isn't dead or fled." Brenn wouldn't meet her eyes. "And your grandmother won't tell me what she knows. Says it's guild business. Says it's in the ledger."

"In the *ledger*?"

"A bargain comes due," Marette said quietly.

They both turned. She had closed the winter volume and was sitting with both hands flat upon its cover, and she looked suddenly very old and very upright, a tower leaning that hadn't yet decided to fall.

"Marette," Yara said, and it wasn't granddaughter-to-grandmother; it was runner-to-mistress-of-the-guild, and they both heard it. "What bargain."

"After supper." Her grandmother's mouth twitched toward the ghost of her old imperiousness. "A Lockridge doesn't discuss debts on an empty stomach. It's bad manners and worse arithmetic."

* * *

Supper was barley soup and the heel of a loaf gone hard at the crusts, softened in the broth. They ate at the long table, all three of them at one end of it, and the emptiness of the other end was louder than conversation. Yara caught herself counting chairs. Forty seats once. She counted anyway, out of some stubborn refusal to stop counting, and arrived at the same number she always arrived at, and hated it.

"You should see the ledgers," Brenn said, when the silence got unbearable. "Half these debts are owed by dead men. Whole villages emptied out. The Harrowdale account alone — eleven years of carried interest on grain runs nobody can collect."

"We honor our contracts," Marette said.

"Honoring contracts is easy when there's someone left to honor them *with*." Brenn set down his spoon. "Marette, I've been saying it since autumn. Write them off. Close the book. Sell the hall if it comes to that and split—"

"*No.*"

The word cracked across the table like a whip. Marette had gone rigid, her thin hands gripping the edge of the board, and for a moment Yara saw not the dying woman but the woman from the stories — the one who had held Thornfell pass through three wars and a famine, who had stared down a Vaskalite magistrate until he apologized, whose name opened doors in five valleys and closed them in a sixth.

"The Lockridge Guild does not default," Marette said, quieter now, but no less absolute. "Not on coin. Not on goods. Not on anything. Whatever this hall is worth, it's worth that. My mother built the roads. My father kept them. I *sealed* them — sealed them so well that armies can't find the true ways and never will. And Yara will keep them after me. That is the inheritance. That is all of it. And I will die owing nothing to anyone."

"You're dying owing everything to somebody," Brenn said. "I've seen you look at that ledger. There's a page you keep turning past, and your face when you turn past it—"

"Brenn."

"—and I've mended your harness straps and buried your husband and carried your cargo up passes that would kill a mountain goat, and I am telling you, whatever it is, the girl deserves—"

"*Enough.*" Marette pushed back from the table. She rose slowly, one hand braced on the wood, and stood swaying, and neither of them moved to help her because helping her would have been a wound worse than any sickness. "I'll be in the ledger room. Yara. When you've finished sulking into your soup."

She went. Her footsteps down the corridor sounded like someone walking underwater.

Brenn stared into the fire. "Stubborn old woman," he said softly, and there was more love in it than grief, and more grief than anything.

"You knew," Yara said. "About her. How long?"

"Long enough." He poked at the coals. "You know what she asked me last week? Sat me down right where you're sitting and asked me if I remembered the famine winter. The Hunger Year, before you were born. I told her nobody who lived through it could forget it. And she said —" he shook his head — "she said, *I forget nothing, Brenn. That's the curse of being the one who signs.* And then she laughed. Your grandmother laughed, and I haven't heard her laugh like that since your grandfather was alive, and it frightened me worse than the coughing has."

"What happened in the famine winter?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Brenn looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and afraid. "Go ask her. And when she tells you — and she will, tonight, because she's been waiting for you to come home so she can tell you — you remember one thing. Remember that everything that woman ever did, she did for this guild. For the roads. For *you*. Whether it was right or wrong, it was never small."

* * *

The ledger room was warmer than the rest of the hall; Marette had fed the stove until it roared. She had cleared the table of everything except the winter volume and one other book — older, smaller, bound in leather gone black with age, its corners worn round as river stones. Yara had seen that book perhaps four times in her life. It lived in the locked drawer of Marette's desk, and the drawer's key had hung around Marette's neck for as long as Yara could remember, and no one, not even Yara, had ever been permitted to touch it.

"Sit," Marette said.

Yara sat.

Her grandmother lowered herself into her chair by degrees, like a ship taking water, and when she was settled she rested her hands on the old black book and was silent for so long that Yara began to count the things she'd never noticed in this room: the water stain shaped like a bird on the ceiling beam. The notch cut into the doorframe where her grandfather had marked Yara's mother's height, and beside it, smaller, Yara's own height at six years old, cut by Marette's own knife.

"I'm going to tell you a story," Marette said, "and then I'm going to show you a page, and after that you may hate me, and I will deserve it. But you will listen to the whole of it before you decide. That much you owe me. That much I will take."

"Grandmother—"

"The Hunger Year." Marette's voice had gone somewhere distant, reciting. "You've heard the songs about it. Everyone has. What the songs don't say is how it started. Bad summer. Blight in the low fields, early snow on the high ones. By midwinter, the valley towns were eating seed grain. By late winter, they were eating bark. And the free cities closed their granaries, because the free cities are generous with everything except food."

"I know the history."

"You know the history. You don't know what I did." Marette opened the black book. The pages inside were dense with writing in several hands, decades of it, but she turned past all of them to the back, to pages so old the ink had gone brown. "Your grandfather was dead eight months. Your mother was a baby screaming for milk that wasn't there. The guild had forty runners and no cargo, because there was nothing left to carry and no one left to pay for it. And I had forty families looking at me across that table out there, waiting for me to fix it, because I had promised them I would fix it, because that is what a Lockridge does."

She turned the final page.

What was written there wasn't ink. Or it had started as ink, but something else had finished it — letters pressed deep into the parchment as if written from beneath, in a script that hurt to follow, each character slightly wrong in a way Yara couldn't name, the way a face in a dream is almost a face. At the bottom of the page was a signature. Marette Lockridge, in her young strong hand.

And below that, another signature, in that same wrong-pressed script, followed by a seal stamped in something that had dried black-brown and waxy and had never, in all these years, faded.

"This is a contract," Yara said slowly.

"It is the contract." Marette laid her palm flat over the page, covering the lower signature, though Yara had already seen enough to feel the cold crawling up her arms. "I signed it on the longest night of the Hunger Year, in this room, with the snow drifted to the windowsills and forty families starving around me. A herald came to the door. Out of the storm, out of nowhere. Dressed fine as a magistrate, courteous as a priest, and it offered me terms."

"It."

Marette met her eyes. Her grey gaze was steady and terrible.

"You've been running my roads for twelve years, girl. You've walked the passes at night. You've felt things in the high places that you told yourself were wind. Don't stand there pretending you don't know the mountains have landlords." She lifted her hand from the page. "It offered me the roads. Open, safe, secret, forever. Grain moving, gold moving, the guild rich and every family fed through the famine and after it. In exchange—"

The coughing took her then, sudden and awful, doubling her over the table. Yara was up and around it before she knew she'd moved, holding her grandmother's shoulders, feeling the fragile architecture of ribs under her hands, and when Marette straightened and wiped her mouth with the back of her hand, her palm came away red.

Bright red. Impossible red, for a woman who should have been grey as ash by now.

"Medicine," Yara gasped, reaching for her pack by the wall. "I brought back — there's a tincture from Hollowmere, it worked on fevers, it'll—"

"It won't." Marette caught her wrist with a grip that still had iron in it, and held her still, and smiled, and it was the worst thing Yara had ever seen, that smile: proud, and fond, and utterly without hope. "Nothing will. Because it isn't a sickness, child. It's a collection. The bargain is coming due, and the interest is being paid in advance."

"In advance of *what*?" Yara's voice came out high and strange. "Grandmother. In advance of what?"

Marette looked at her — looked at her the way you look at a thing you have guarded for twenty years and must now surrender — and pressed the red-stained hand gently against Yara's cheek.

"In advance of you," she said.
