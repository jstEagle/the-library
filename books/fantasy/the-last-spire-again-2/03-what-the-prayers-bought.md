## 3. What the Prayers Bought

The stranger came up the pass three days after the medicine, on the last day the road was open.

Yara saw him first as a smudge against the snowfield below the switchbacks — a dark figure walking where no sane traveler walked in late Thawmonth, when the cornices hung over the trail like the jaws of some white beast waiting to close. She had stopped at the Widow's Ledge to check the marker cairns, and there he was, climbing steadily, unhurried, as though the mountain were a staircase in his own house.

She watched him for a long moment before she moved. Smugglers learned to watch first and decide after. Most people who came up Thornfell in winter came for one of two reasons: desperation or greed, and both of them made a man walk wrong — too fast or too slow, head down or eyes darting. This man walked like neither. He walked like someone who had made his peace with wherever he was going.

By the time he reached the guildhall gate, the light was failing and the wind had come around to the north, carrying the smell of deep ice. He stood at the gate with his hood back, and Yara saw that he was younger than she'd guessed from the distance — thirty perhaps, maybe less, with the kind of face that weather had already finished arguing with. Grey at the temples though the rest of his hair was dark. A scar through one eyebrow, old and clean. His eyes were the color of slate under water, and they did not move past her the way most strangers' eyes did, cataloguing exits and valuables. They stayed on her face as if her face were a text he was trying to read in bad light.

"Lockridge Guild," he said. Not a question.

"What's left of it," Yara said. "You want passage?"

"I want passage."

"Where to?"

"Down."

The word sat strangely in the air between them. *Down* meant the lowlands, the free cities, the coast — everyone coming up the pass said *up*, even when they were going home. Nobody said *down* like it was a direction you could want.

"The lowlands are burning," Yara said. "Or so the last courier said. You're walking toward it."

"I'm not going to the lowlands." He pulled something from inside his coat — not coin, not papers, but a small square of grey stone, unmarked, and held it out flat on his palm the way a merchant shows a sample. "I'm going beneath them."

Yara looked at the stone and felt, absurdly, the way she'd felt at Widow Hesk's village — that prickle along the spine, that sense of standing in a room where the furniture had been rearranged by no one. The stone was ordinary. That was what was wrong with it. It was so perfectly, deliberately ordinary that it could only be hiding something.

"We don't run that cargo," she said.

"You don't know what cargo I carry." He closed his hand. "And I'm not buying. I'm asking. There's a difference, and the difference is that I can pay, but I'd rather be let through than bought."

Behind her, from inside the hall, came the sound of Marette coughing — that wet, tearing cough that had gotten worse all month, the one Brenn pretended not to hear and Yara couldn't stop hearing. The stranger's head turned slightly toward the sound, and something crossed his face that looked, impossibly, like grief.

"Your grandmother is dying," he said quietly. "Isn't she."

"That's none of yours."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. But the reason she's dying is."

The wind gusted. Somewhere above them, a cornice calved off the ridge with a boom like distant artillery, and snow-smoke curled up into the violet sky. Yara found that her hand had gone to the knife at her hip without her deciding anything, and the stranger noticed, and did not flinch, and did not reach for any weapon of his own, which frightened her more than steel would have.

"My name is Cael," he said. "Once I served in the Undercourt's priesthood. Before your grandmother ever signed her name in that ledger of hers. I've come a long way to tell her the bill has come due — and to tell you, if she hasn't the strength left, exactly what she paid."

* * *

They let him into the kitchen because Marette told them to.

That was the thing Yara kept turning over later: not that Cael knew things he shouldn't, but that Marette — proud, iron Marette, who had turned away tax assessors and guild inspectors and once, famously, a Vaskalite missionary at knifepoint — heard the name Cael spoken at her door and said, in a voice worn down to gravel, "Let him in. Put the kettle on. And bring me the ledger."

The ledger. The black oilcloth-bound book that lived under the floorboard beneath her grandmother's bed, that Yara had carried across half the Thornfells in flood years and famine years, that held every debt, every route, every secret price the Lockridge Guild had ever charged or been charged. Yara fetched it because Marette asked, and her hands were not steady doing it, and she hated that.

Marette sat propped in her chair by the fire, wrapped in two blankets despite the heat, her skin gone the color of tallow. The blood-cough had taken most of her voice; what remained came out in fragments, each one costing her. But her eyes were still her eyes — sharp, black, unappeasable — and when Cael entered and bowed his head slightly, the way a soldier bows to an officer of equal rank, those eyes went narrow and bright.

"You're Halvard's boy," she rasped. "The one they cast out."

"I'm the one I cast out," Cael said. "Halvard just did the paperwork."

He sat down across from her without being invited, laid the grey stone on the table between them, and Yara — standing by the hearth with the ledger still in her arms — watched her dying grandmother and the defrocked priest regard each other like two duelists who had been circling for years and had finally, wearily, arrived.

"Say it plain," Marette said. "I haven't breath for ceremony."

Cael folded his hands on the table. "The roads are opening, Marette. All of them. The passes you sealed — the ones you were never supposed to know about, the ones that only answered because of what you gave — they've started answering again without being asked. The shrines in the lowlands are working miracles. Sick children waking whole. Bread multiplying in famine villages. The old gods are stirring in their sleep, and the bargain that kept them asleep—" he touched the ledger, one finger, lightly, "—is failing. Because she's failing." His gaze flicked to Yara. "Because the collateral is maturing."

The fire cracked. Outside, the wind moaned down the chimney throat.

"Collateral," Yara repeated. The word came out flat, toneless, the way words come out when they're too large for the mouth. She looked at her grandmother. "What collateral? What is he talking about?"

Marette didn't look away from Cael. "You said you'd come to tell her."

"I came to give you the chance to do it yourself. A courtesy. One priest to one bargainer."

"She's nineteen," Marette said, and there was something in her voice then that Yara had never heard in it — something that might have been shame, if shame could survive in that woman's chest. "She's a child."

"She's a smuggler with a dead guild and a soul that doesn't belong to her. Those stopped being compatible a long time ago." Cael leaned back. "Tell her, or I will. But either way, tonight, she hears it."

"Marette." Yara set the ledger down on the table. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, thin as wire. "Grandmother. What did you do?"

For a long moment Marette Lockridge looked at the ledger as if it were a grave she'd dug herself and lain down in. Then she began to talk, in that broken gravel voice, stopping twice to cough blood into a rag Brenn silently held out, and Yara stood very still and listened to the story of her own life turn out to have been a lie.

* * *

It had been the famine winter. Twenty-two years ago — the year before Yara was born, though she hadn't known until this moment that the timing mattered.

The passes had been closed. Not snow-closed — closed. Sealed by something that pushed back against rope and pick and prayer, walls of storm that stood in clear weather, trails that looped back on themselves, compasses that spun. The Lockridge Guild, which had run the Thornfells since before the free cities had names, had woken one morning to find its arteries tied off, and the mountain's new masters had sent a herald to explain the terms.

"It wore a coat of office," Marette whispered. "Grey, with silver thread. Looked like any assessor from any capital. But its shadow fell the wrong way, and when it smiled there were too many teeth behind the smile, arranged like keys on a ring." She coughed, steadied. "Vaskal's herald. The usurper-god. He'd taken the Undercourt the century before, put the old gods under, and now he wanted the surface too. The passes were his choke-point. Control Thornfell and you control every road between the sea and the plains. He offered to sell them back."

"And you bought them," Yara said.

"With what did I have?" Marette's laugh turned into a cough. "Coin? The guild was starving. No. He didn't want coin. Coin is nothing under the mountain. He wanted surety. A lien. Something of ours that would keep us honest for as long as he needed us honest." Her black eyes lifted, and for the first time in Yara's life, her grandmother could not hold her gaze. "You weren't born yet. Your mother was six months gone. The herald said: *a child unborn, unbaptized by any god, unclaimed. Give us her name in the ledger, and the roads stay ours to walk.*"

The kettle screamed on the hob. Brenn moved to take it off, and his big hands shook, and nobody spoke until he'd done it.

"They said the debt would sleep while you slept," Marette went on, faster now, the words tumbling like rocks down a scree slope, as if she'd been holding them twenty-two years and her body was done holding anything. "While you were small. While you ran the roads and never asked why they opened for us when they wouldn't open for anyone else. Why our cargoes passed through storms that ate other caravans. Why the mountain seemed to — to know us." She pressed the bloodied rag to her mouth. "Every mile you ever ran, girl. Every winter we ate when others starved. Every toll we never paid, every pass no army ever took from us. It was all built on you. On your name in that book, marked *payable upon maturity*. And the maturity date—" her voice failed, came back thinner, "—the herald said the debt comes due when the collateral comes of age, or when the guarantor dies. Whichever comes first."

Yara heard herself say, very calmly, from a great distance: "So it wasn't the medicine. At Hesk's village. The prayers working — that was—"

"The seal cracking," Cael said quietly. "The bargain fraying. When a contract of that weight begins to fail, everything it held down starts to rise. The old gods under the peaks. The miracles leaking through like light under a door. Your grandmother's lungs." He glanced at Marette, and there was no mercy in how gently he said it, which was its own kind of mercy. "Her body is the guarantor's body. The debt is collecting interest."

"You used me." Yara's calm held. It was the smuggler's calm, the calm she wore through avalanche country and checkpoint inspections, and underneath it something was moving that she refused to look at directly, because if she looked at it she would not be able to stand in this room one more minute. "My whole life. Every time you taught me a route, every time you said *the Lockridges keep their contracts* — I was the contract. I was the cargo."

"Yara—"

"Don't." The word cracked out of her like a whip, and the fire guttered, and all three of them — Brenn frozen by the stove, Cael motionless at the table, Marette with her hand half-raised — went still. "Don't say my name like that. Don't say it soft. You sold it. You don't get to use it anymore."

Marette's hand dropped. And Yara saw — she would see it for the rest of her life, in dreams, in the space before waking — the exact moment the fight went out of her grandmother's face. Not death. Worse. Recognition. The look of a woman who had run the arithmetic twenty-two years ago, alone, at night, and had known even then that there was no column in which she came out clean.

"Aye," Marette said. "I suppose I don't." She turned to Cael. "Get out. Take your stone and your truths and get out of my hall. I'll settle with her myself. What's left of me."

Cael rose, unhurried, and collected his grey stone. At the doorway he paused.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the herald cheated her. The terms were worse than she understood. They always are — that's how courts work, mortal or divine. Which is why I'm here. Not for the guild. For the ledger." He looked at Yara, and those slate-water eyes held no pity at all, which she found she could bear better than pity. "There's a clause she never read. Debts to the Undercourt can be contested — but only in the court itself, and only by the debtor. If you want your soul back, Yara Lockridge, you'll have to go down and take it. And you'll need someone who knows the gates."

The door closed behind him. The wind took up its long moan in the chimney again.

Brenn cleared his throat, a rumble like far thunder. "Girl—"

"Keep her alive," Yara said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, at her sides, were fists hard enough to ache. "Whatever it costs, whatever he wants for it — keep her alive until I'm back. I'm going to find out what a soul goes for these days. And then I'm going to make the Undercourt regret it ever learned our name."

Under the floorboard, beneath the bed, the ledger lay shut — and in the silence of the kitchen, faint as a heartbeat, Yara would have sworn she heard it breathing.
