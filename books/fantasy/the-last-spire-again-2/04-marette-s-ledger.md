## 4. Marette's Ledger

The cough came at moonset now, regular as a tax collector. Marette Lockridge had learned to time her days around it the way she had once timed her runs around the mountain weather — accept it, plan around it, never let it see you flinch.

She sat up in her bed in the loft above the guildhall's great room and pressed a cloth to her lips. When she took it away, the cloth was spotted red, dark in the lamplight, like wine spilled on an old map. She folded it small and tucked it under her pillow. Brenn didn't need to see it. Yara certainly didn't.

Yara was downstairs. Marette could hear her through the floorboards — the particular rhythm of her granddaughter's footsteps, quick and hard-heeled, moving between the ledger desk and the hearth and the door, checking all three as though one of them might solve the others. Checking the door most of all. Yara always checked the door last, the way her mother had, the way Marette herself did. You looked at the way out before you looked at anything else. That was the first lesson of the passes, taught before children learned their letters: *know where the road leaves from.*

She had taught that lesson to three generations now. Two of them were dead, and the third was down there counting debts no living soul could pay, and Marette's chest hurt worse than the cough ever managed when she thought about what she had done to make sure there *was* a third generation to teach at all.

The lamp guttered. Outside, wind came down off the Thornfells with the smell of iron and coming snow — early this year, too early, another sign she pretended not to read. The world had been sending signs for months now, and she had spent a lifetime being paid not to read them.

She reached beneath her mattress and drew out the ledger. Not the guild ledger — the other one. The true one. Bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, its corners worn round from forty years of her thumb. There was no lock on it. A lock would have been an admission that someone might want to look, and Marette had built her whole life on the principle that the safest hiding place was the thing nobody believed existed.

She opened it by feel, though her eyes were still good enough for this page. Only this one mattered anymore. The rest was history; this was arithmetic.

*One soul,* said the entry, in her own young hand, in ink she had mixed from ash and pine pitch because the good ink had run out in the famine winter along with everything else. *One soul, freely given by the bearer of this book, to be collected at the pleasure of the Court Beneath, in exchange for: the roads.*

The roads. As if you could write it so small. As if two words could hold thirty years of grain carts crossing the high passes ahead of the snows, of medicine reaching Hollowmere before the fever did, of the free cities eating through winters that should have starved them. As if two words could hold the price.

Marette closed the ledger and held it against her sternum, over the place where the cough lived, and let herself remember — because she was dying, and the dying are owed their memories whether they want them or not.

* * *

The famine winter had come in her twenty-ninth year, though everyone afterward called it the Winter of the Grey Bread, which was kinder. The crops failed lowland and highland both. The war in the south choked the river roads. And the Thornfell passes — the only arteries left into the free cities — filled with snowfall so deep and so sustained that even the Lockridges, who claimed descent from the mountain's own midwives, stopped swearing they could get through.

Marette remembered standing in the guildhall doorway watching the snow bury the shrine stones by the gate, one by one, like the mountain closing its eyes. Her husband Torv had been dead two years by then, taken by a rockslide on a route he'd sworn was safe, and the guild had been hers alone: four runners, a cellar of seed-grain meant for spring planting, and a ledger full of promises.

Hollowmere had sent word by the last runner out: eleven children dead of hunger in one week. Eleven. She remembered sitting with the seed-grain cellar open in front of her and doing the arithmetic that would define her — the arithmetic she would spend the rest of her life pretending had been simple. Feed five villages for a month and lose the spring planting, or keep the seed and lose the villages. Either way the guild died. Either way the name ended with her.

That was the night the herald came.

She hadn't heard the door. That was the first wrong thing — no door in Thornfell opened without noise, not in winter, not ever — but she looked up from the grain sacks and there was a figure standing in the great room among the shadows, tall and courteous, wearing a coat the color of wet slate. Its face was pleasant the way a frozen lake is pleasant: smooth, reflective, and concealing depths that went down past any honest measurement.

"Lady of the Roads," it said, and its voice was warm as a banked fire, which was the second wrong thing. Nothing that warm comes up a mountain in famine winter uninvited.

"We're closed," Marette said. Even starving, even alone, she said it like a merchant. "Come back in spring."

"I have come about your spring," the herald said. "And every spring after."

It knew things. That was the horror of it, looking back — how much it knew. It knew the number of sacks in her cellar. It knew the names of the dead children in Hollowmere, and recited them gently, the way you'd read a psalm. It knew that her ledger showed the guild owing more than it owned, and it knew something else besides, something it laid on the table between them like a coin: that the Lockridge road through the heart of Thornfell — the true road, the old road, the one her grandmother had shown her under oath and darkness — was not merely a smuggler's route. It was a wound. A way *through* the mountain that was also a way *into* it, and the thing that slept beneath the stone had been dreaming uneasily ever since mortals learned to use it.

"You want the road," Marette said.

"I want what the road leads to," the herald said. "You want what the road carries. We are well matched, Lady of the Roads. I am empowered to offer terms."

And here was the part she had never told anyone, the part that made the memory unbearable rather than merely terrible: she had not been tricked. She had not been seduced or deceived or caught in a moment of weakness. She had been a merchant presented with an offer, and she had done what merchants do. She had haggled.

She got the roads open by morning — all of them, the high passes and the hidden ways, snow falling behind her runners like a curtain drawn shut. She got a guarantee written into the bargain's bones: the guild's people, and those who traveled under the guild's mark, would pass safe while the bargain held. She got the famine lifted from the free cities within the season, and she got it *quietly*, so that no priest would come asking questions the answers to which would burn the whole arrangement down.

What she gave — after long negotiation, after the herald's patience proved bottomless and its smile proved fixed — was not her own soul, though she had offered that first and been refused with something almost like pity. *Yours is spent already, lady. Half of it burned on these roads, the rest promised to grief.* No. The court wanted collateral with a longer term. The court wanted a life not yet fully lived, a soul still green and unformed, something that could ripen in the vault below until it was worth devouring.

The court wanted the unborn daughter of her dead son.

Torv's girl had died with him on the mountain, but his wife had been carrying again, three months along, and the herald had known that too. Had counted it, the way you count stock.

"I will give you anything else," Marette had said, and heard her own voice crack on the word *anything*, and hated herself for the crack, and hated the herald for hearing it.

"The terms stand or fall entire," the herald said. "Eleven children in Hollowmere. More next week. The snow does not haggle, lady. Why should I?"

So she had signed. In ash-and-pitch ink, on the grey page, with the name of a child not yet born — a name she chose herself, right then, in that moment, because the ledger required a true name and the child had none yet. *Yara.* It was her own mother's name. She wrote it slowly, beautifully, the finest penmanship of her life, thinking: *if I must sell her, at least she'll be sold under a name that was loved.*

The bargain held. The roads opened. The famine broke. Her granddaughter was born in the spring to a mother who never knew why the midwife wept at the naming, and Marette held the child in her arms in this same great room and felt the two halves of her life split clean down the middle: everything before, when she had been merely a woman who ran dangerous roads; everything after, when she was a woman who had fed ten thousand strangers with one grandchild's soul.

She had told herself, for thirty years, that she would find a way to buy it back. That the ledger had a second half waiting to be written. She told herself so long that telling herself became the same as believing it, and believing it became the same as having done it, and that is the mercy and the treachery of a merchant's mind.

Then the prayers started being answered, and she knew the term was coming due.

* * *

Dawn was close. She could tell by the quality of the silence outside — the wind had dropped, the way it does in the last hour of dark, leaving the mountain holding its breath.

Marette climbed down the ladder from the loft one slow rung at a time, ledger wrapped in oilcloth and strapped across her back beneath her shawl, because she could not bear to have it out of her reach and could not bear either to leave it where Yara might find it before Marette found the words. There were words. There had to be words. Thirty years of silence and she had composed a hundred speeches on the stairs at night, and every one of them turned to ash in her mouth by morning.

Yara was asleep at the ledger desk.

Marette stood at the bottom of the ladder and looked at her, and the sight of her hurt in a way that had nothing to do with lungs. Yara slept the way she did everything — efficiently, defensively, boots on, chair angled toward the door, one hand loose near the ledger as if even unconscious she was guarding the guild's accounts. Twenty-six years old and she had the face of a woman who had never once been permitted to be careless. Marette had done that. Not the mountain, not the trade, not the hard years. *Her.* Every sacrifice she'd made for the guild had been paid for twice over out of that girl's hide, and the girl didn't even know she'd been paying.

There was a stranger coming. Marette had seen him in the fire-mood three nights running now — a man with a priest's ruined hands and a hunted animal's walk, carrying a god's name around his neck like a millstone. He would come up the pass within days, maybe hours. She knew what he carried. She knew what it meant: the old gods stirring, the bargain straining at its seams, the court below growing hungry for its collateral. Cael, the fire-mood named him. A defrocked priest walking toward her door like a debt collector in a cassock.

He would tell Yara. Or Yara would read the ledger. One way or another, the truth was coming up the stairs, and Marette had run out of winters to outrun it.

"Gran?"

Yara stirred, blinked awake, and was instantly, infuriatingly alert — checking the door, checking the window, checking Marette. Always checking. "You shouldn't be down here. It's not dawn yet."

"Neither are you," Marette said, and lowered herself into the chair across the desk, and allowed herself one long look at her granddaughter's face in the banked firelight. The stubborn jaw. Torv's jaw. The dark eyes that missed nothing except, blessedly, the one thing that would break her.

"There's tea," Yara said, already rising.

"Sit down, child. I've drunk enough tea to float a barge." She patted the desk between them. "I want to talk about the ledgers. All of them. While my head's clear."

Something flickered in Yara's face — hope, quickly smothered, the hope of a girl who had spent her whole life trying to read her grandmother and being told, gently and repeatedly, that some pages stayed shut. "The debts can wait—"

"The debts can't wait. That's rather the point of debts." Marette smiled, and knew the smile was the old merchant's smile, the one that sold the bridge along with the crossing. "Every account comes due, Yara. Every single one. That's not cruelty. That's just the shape of the world. The trick — the only trick there's ever been — is choosing what you're willing to pay before the collector arrives. Do you understand me?"

"No," Yara said honestly. Then, because she was her grandmother's girl: "But I'm listening."

*Good girl.* Marette put her hand flat on the desktop, over the wood, over the place where the guild's marks were carved into the grain by hands three generations dead. *Hold on. Hold on just a little longer. Let me get you ready.*

Outside, the sky was going grey at the eastern edge, the color of ash and pine-pitch ink. Inside, the fire ticked and settled. Somewhere down beneath miles of sleeping stone, in a vault of lamps, a small bright light was burning that had her blood in it and her sin on it, and Marette Lockridge, who had out-haggled a herald of hell, found that she had nothing left to bargain with except the morning.

She began, as she had begun every hard transaction of her life, with the oldest line in the guild's book.

"When I was twenty-nine," she said, "there was a famine..."

* * *

She talked until the grey light came through the east windows, and Yara listened without interrupting once, which frightened Marette more than any interruption could have — because Yara interrupted when she was comfortable, and went silent when she was assembling something sharp. Marette gave her the shape of it without the heart of it: the famine, the herald, the bargain sealed, the roads bought at a price she named only as *"more than I ever meant to spend."* She watched her granddaughter's face go through disbelief and anger and arrive somewhere cold and patient, and she thought: *not yet. She's not ready to hate me yet. Give me till dawn. Give me till I'm past the point where her hating me can cost her anything.*

Because that was the last piece of the arithmetic, the final column she had kept hidden even from herself: Yara must learn the truth, but she must learn it *angry*. Anger would carry her down the mountain and into the dark and back out again. Grief would not. Grief would make her stay at a dying woman's bedside and hold her hand and waste the weeks that were all the world had left. So Marette would give her a betrayal instead of a goodbye, and let her granddaughter's fury do what love, gentler employed, could not.

It was a terrible thing to calculate. It was the kind of thing Vaskal's herald would have calculated. Marette saw that clearly, in the last hour, and did it anyway, and understood at last that this was how the damned are made: not in single grand bargains, but in a thousand small sensible ones, each one defensible, each one a little cold, until the sum total of your choices wears a crown of slate and calls itself necessity.

The light strengthened. The fire burned down to a red seam. Marette felt the cough gathering far down in her chest like water rising in a mine, and this time she knew it for what it was — not the illness, but the tide. The term. The collection.

Yara had gone to the hearth to feed it. Her back was to her, shoulders straight under the patched wool, and the dawn light came through the window and caught the edges of her hair, and Marette thought: *there. That's what I bought. That's what it cost. Worth it. God forgive me — whatever's left up there to do the forgiving — worth every grain of it.*

"Yara," she said.

Her granddaughter turned.

The cough took her then, all of it at once, the whole flooded depth of her, and she gripped the arms of the chair and rode it down and knew, with a merchant's clarity, that she would not ride back up. The room tilted. The fire swam. She heard Yara's boots on the flags, fast, hard-heeled, coming for her, and she had time for exactly one thing more, and she spent it the way she had spent everything for thirty years.

"Yara," she said again — her mother's name, her granddaughter's name, the truest entry in the ledger — and the dawn came through the windows like snowfall in reverse, rising, and the grey page of the world turned over, and the collector stepped quietly into the room.

Downstairs, at the foot of the pass, a man with a priest's ruined hands had begun, in the freezing dark, to climb.
