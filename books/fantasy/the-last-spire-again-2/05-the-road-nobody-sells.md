## 5. The Road Nobody Sells

Yara did not weep. She had learned young that grief was a luxury the passes never stocked, and she was not about to begin importing it now, standing in a guildhall that smelled of ash and old blood and her grandmother's last hours.

She buried Marette at dawn instead.

The ground above Thornfell was iron-hard with frost, so Brenn and she took turns with the pick while Cael stood apart, hat in hand, saying nothing — which Yara noted, and filed away, and decided not to be grateful for yet. They laid Marette in a hollow beneath the rowan tree where three generations of Lockridges had gone into the dirt before her, wrapped in the guild's oldest banner because there was nothing else large enough and Marette would have laughed herself sick to be shrouded in anything so ceremonial. Brenn spoke the few words he knew. Yara filled the grave herself, refusing help, and when it was done she stood over the raw mound with her hands blistered and her jaw set like the mountain around them, and felt precisely nothing, which she understood to be a kind of lie, and let it stand.

"She'd have hated that," Brenn said quietly, beside her. "The banner."

"She'd have hated being dead more." Yara brushed the soil from her palms. "This way at least she's annoyed."

Brenn made a sound that might have been a laugh if grief hadn't gotten its hands on it first. He was an old man made suddenly older; overnight his shoulders had acquired the stoop of a man carrying something heavier than ledgers. "What will you do, girl?"

Yara looked out past the tree line, down the long white throat of the pass, toward the lowlands where Cael said Vaskal's armies were gathering like stormwater behind a dam. Somewhere down there, beneath the roots of the mountains, in a court she had learned of only three days ago, a thing that was hers — that *was* her, some essential bright piece the world had been shortchanged of since before she could walk — sat locked in a vault among thousands of others.

Her grandmother had sold it. Had signed for it in the ledger, in Marette's cramped, beautiful hand, and taken payment in open roads and full bellies, and then spent twenty years watching Yara grow up knowing exactly what the interest would come due as.

*Betrayal* was too clean a word. Betrayal implied she'd been given something first worth keeping whole.

"I'm going to go get it back," Yara said.

Brenn looked at her. The wind moved through the rowan's bare branches, rattling them like dice in a cup. "Down," he said. Not a question.

"Down."

"You've heard what the priest said. What lives under there. What it does to people who—"

"I've heard." She turned to face him fully, and saw him take stock of her the way he'd taught her to take stock of cargo: weight, condition, whether it would survive the journey. Whatever he found in her face seemed to satisfy him, or at least to resign him. "Marette bought twenty years with my soul, Brenn. Fine. That was her arithmetic, and I'll carry the debt of loving her anyway, because I don't get to choose that any more than she chose the famine winter. But the principal is mine. And I have never in my life left goods unclaimed that were owed to this guild."

"It isn't goods, Yara."

"No," she agreed. "It's worse. It's *mine*."

* * *

Cael was in the hall when she came back in, sitting where he'd sat every day since Marette died: at the long table, with the ledger open in front of him, reading it the way other men read scripture. He'd hardly slept. She could see it in the gray under his eyes, in the careful stillness of his hands, as though he'd learned that his own body was something to be managed from a distance.

"You should know," he said without looking up, "that I've been through every page. Twice. Your grandmother recorded everything. Dates, amounts, terms. She kept accounts the way—" He stopped. Started again. "She kept accounts better than most priests keep faith."

"She was a Lockridge." Yara pulled out the bench across from him and sat, laying her knife flat on the table between them — not a threat, just a habit, just the way she thought best. "Tell me about the descent. The gate. You said priests remember it. Which means you do."

His finger stopped on the ledger line it had been tracing. For a long moment he didn't move at all, and Yara watched him decide, visibly, whether to lie to her — watched him weigh it and find, whatever else he was, that he no longer had the stomach or perhaps the right.

"There is a chapel," he said. "Three days' climb northeast of here, above the tree line, in a fold of the mountain where the snow never quite settles. It was a shrine once, before Vaskal. A place where the old gods were worshipped openly. When the Undercourt was sealed beneath us — when *he* took it — the faithful built downward from their holy places. Chapels became doors. Doors became stairs. Most were closed when the courts fell silent. One wasn't."

"Why not?"

"Because closing it required a priest willing to walk down and seal it from below." His mouth did something that was almost a smile and had nothing of humor in it. "None volunteered. The order chose instead to forget it existed. Unwritten, unspoken, struck from the liturgies. That is how you hide a door from a god, Yara. You make your own people ashamed to say its name."

"And you remember it anyway."

"I remember everything I was made to forget." He closed the ledger gently, as if it might bruise. "It's why they defrocked me. Why they— no. It's one reason." He met her eyes then, and she saw again what she'd seen the night he arrived: a man carrying his own vault of stolen things, all of them labeled with his own name. "I can take you to the gate. I want to say that plainly, so you understand what you're agreeing to. I am not doing you a kindness. I am settling an account. Everything that happens below is a debt I owe to someone in that book, and possibly to everyone."

"That's fine," Yara said. "Debts are the only honest language left in these mountains."

"It isn't fine." For the first time something moved in his voice, something hot under all that ash. "You're a smuggler. You think everything is a route, a toll, a cargo. You think if you're clever enough and hard enough you can run anything across any border. But that place doesn't work by borders. It works by *terms*. By bargains struck in words you won't fully understand until they've already cost you. Your grandmother was cleverer than either of us, and look what her cleverness bought."

"My grandmother negotiated from hunger," Yara said. "From a famine winter, with three starving guildsmen and a road full of dying villages. I'm negotiating from fury. It's a better starting position." She leaned forward. "Here are my terms, priest. You guide me to that gate. You tell me true about everything you know of what's below — every rite, every ward, every trick of the Undercourt's tongue. In exchange, when this is done, I'll get you out of the mountains alive, even if I have to carry you. That's the contract. Shake on it or don't, but don't waste both our time pretending you have a better offer."

He studied her for a long moment. Then, slowly, he extended his hand across the table — and hesitated above the ledger, and drew back, and opened the book instead to a page near the end, turning it so she could see.

A name was written there. Not hers. Not Marette's. A name in a different ink, older, half-faded, in a hand Yara didn't recognize.

"What's this?" she asked.

"The toll," Cael said quietly. "Every gate below demands a true name to pass. It's how Vaskal keeps his ledger — souls entered, souls owed. Your grandmother knew that. I think she always meant for someone to make this descent someday, and she left this in case it was you." His jaw tightened. "We'll need it. And Yara — when we reach that gate, whatever happens, whatever it offers, however much easier it makes the road — do not give it yours."

Outside, the wind shifted, and came down off the high peaks carrying a smell that didn't belong to winter: green, growing, impossibly warm, like spring rising up through the bones of the mountain from somewhere far below.

Yara looked at the faded name on the page, and at the priest who feared it, and at the door of the hall where the strange warmth pooled like spilled light.

"Pack light," she said. "We leave within the hour."

* * *

They left Brenn with the guildhall and the ledgers and instructions written out in Yara's own hand — routes to warn, debts to call, and, sealed separately, a letter she did not explain to either of them, addressed simply *For whoever comes after*. If she didn't come back down, someone should at least inherit the truth cleanly, without a court's fingerprints on it.

Brenn walked them as far as the switchback where the trail split, north to the salt road, northeast to nothing anyone mapped anymore. He embraced Yara briefly, gruffly, pressing something small and cold into her palm: her grandmother's tally-ring, iron worn smooth by sixty years of counting.

"She'd say you were a fool," Brenn said. "And then she'd have packed you provisions twice as heavy as you needed and told you the second half was for luck."

"Tell her when you see her," Yara said, "that I'm collecting."

Then she and Cael climbed.

The first day was ordinary country — scree and pine and frozen stream, the kind of ground Yara had crossed a thousand times and could cross blindfolded. She watched Cael walk it and revised her opinion of him upward: he kept pace without complaint, picked footing like a man who'd done fieldwork in worse places than chapels, and asked intelligent questions about the terrain without pretending to already know the answers. There was a humility in it that sat strangely on him, like borrowed clothes — or perhaps, she thought, like clothes finally returned after years of wearing richer ones that never fit.

The second day the trees gave out, and the world went white and silent, and the strangeness began.

It started small. A crow flew overhead — ordinary, black, expected — except that it called three times in a rhythm Yara somehow recognized without ever having heard it, a pattern like a knock, like a question asked at a door. Cael stopped walking and watched it until it vanished, and his face was pale under the windburn.

"They used to say the birds carried prayers up," he murmured. "Before. When the gods could hear."

"And now?"

"Now I don't know what they carry." He resumed walking, faster. "Pray it isn't answers."

By the third day the snow behaved wrongly. It fell in the folds of the valley ahead but never accumulated in the particular hollow Cael led them toward, dusting the slopes on either side and leaving a bare crescent of dark stone, as though the mountain itself held that one spot out of the weather — or as though something beneath it exhaled warmth through the rock, patient as a banked fire. The air grew warmer as they descended into the hollow, warm enough that Yara pulled off her gloves, warm enough that she could smell earth instead of ice, deep mineral earth, the smell of cellars and graves and roots.

And there it was.

The chapel was smaller than she'd imagined — little more than a shepherd's hut of ancient stone, roofless, its walls furred with moss that should not have survived this altitude and was green as June. No icon remained. No altar. Only the walls, and in the floor at its center a ring of seven stones, waist-high, arranged like a broken crown. Snow lay on six of them.

The seventh was bare, and faintly, unmistakably, warm.

"This is it?" Yara said. "This is the gate nobody sells?"

"Nobody sells it because nobody owns it." Cael knelt beside the warm stone and did not touch it. Up close she could see his hands were shaking, and that he was making them stop through sheer discipline, the way a man holds a wound closed. "The old gods owned the mountain. Vaskal stole the deed. This chapel stands on land neither of them currently controls, which makes it the only free ground left in Thornfell." He looked up at her. "Beyond this point, Yara, I have to teach you things the priesthood burned men for teaching. Words. Names. The grammar of bargains below. Once you know them, you can't unknow them, and they change how you hear everything — prayer, promise, contract, even plain speech. Are you certain?"

Yara crouched across from him, on the opposite side of the warm stone, and laid Marette's tally-ring in her palm where they could both see it. Sixty years of counted debts and honored payments, worn smooth as a river stone.

"My grandmother signed my soul away to feed a village," she said. "Someone down there has been holding it in inventory ever since. I'm a Lockridge. We don't abandon stock." She met his eyes. "Teach me."

Something in Cael's face broke open then — not despair, not relief, but the expression of a man watching someone step voluntarily onto ground he'd sworn never to let another soul tread. He reached into his coat and drew out a small, battered book of his own, pages soft with handling, and opened it across the warm stone between them.

"Then we begin," he said, "with the first law of the Undercourt: *nothing given freely is binding, and nothing bound is free*. Every bargain below turns on that hinge. Learn it, and you may survive. Forget it—" he glanced up at the roofless walls, at the sky going violet over the peaks, "—and you'll end up a lamp in a vault, same as the rest."

Above them, the evening star came out — except it wasn't the evening star. It hung lower, brighter, and pulsed slow as a sleeping heartbeat, directly over the heart of the mountain.

And beneath Yara's boots, faint but undeniable, the ground breathed.
