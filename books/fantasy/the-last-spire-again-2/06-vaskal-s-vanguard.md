## 6. Vaskal's Vanguard

Brenn had buried three guildmasters and outlived two wars, and in all his sixty-one years he had learned exactly one lesson worth the learning: when the birds leave the lowlands all at once, do not ask why. Pack. Walk uphill.

He was salting a haunch of mutton in the guildhall's cold kitchen when he saw them go — a black ribbon of starlings lifting off the fields below Thornfell's last shoulder, rising not in their usual wheeling confusion but straight, purposeful, like smoke drawn up a flue. He set down the salt jar. He wiped his hands on his apron with great care, because old hands wanted something to do when they began to shake, and he went to the south window where the glass was warped and grey with age and looked down at the valley of Harrowmere.

The morning fog was burning off the way it always did, slow as syrup, and beneath it Brenn could see the river road, and on the river road — where there should have been nothing at that hour but a milk cart and perhaps a peddler's mule — there was a column of men.

Not refugees. Refugees straggle. These moved like water finding its level, rank after rank after rank, and above them floated banners Brenn had hoped never to see outside of a woodcut: a black tower split by a white lightning-stroke, stitched on cloth so dark it seemed to drink the light around it. The mark of Vaskal. The mark that hadn't flown over mortal soil in a hundred years, or so the priests had said, back when anyone bothered to say anything about gods at all.

"Well," Brenn said to the empty kitchen. "There it is, then."

He did not waste time on prayer. Prayer was Yara's grandmother's department, and Marette was dying upstairs by inches, coughing her life into red-stained cloths that Brenn collected each morning and burned each evening because she'd asked him to and because some things you did even when they made no sense.

He took the stairs two at a time anyway, which cost him a stitch in his side he felt for the rest of the day, and pushed open Marette's door without knocking, because they were long past knocking.

"They've come," he said. "The usurper's lot. On the Harrowmere road, marching north."

Marette lay propped against her pillows like a coat hung on a hook, small and folded and yellowed, her hands — those great strong smuggler's hands that had once bent iron locks open like bread — lying quiet on the quilt. She turned her head with terrible slowness and looked at him, and her eyes were still sharp. That was the worst of it. The body going to ruin around eyes that stayed bright as a knife in winter.

"Which road," she said. Not a question. A demand.

"The low road. Through Harrowmere proper." Brenn crossed to the window of her room, which faced south, and stood aside so she could see. It cost her visibly to lift her head, but she lifted it.

"Conscripts," she said at last. "See how wide they're spread? An army marches tight. They're sweeping. Taking every man between fifteen and fifty off the farms as they go." Her breath caught, held, released. "That's how Vaskal fights. Not with soldiers first. With our own sons, handed a spear and told their families burn if they drop it."

"You sound like you've seen it before."

Marette's gaze came away from the window and found him again, and there was something in her face then that Brenn had only seen once before, thirty years ago, the night a herald had come to this same hall wearing a smile like a wound. He'd been young then. He'd thought the fear on her face was for the bargain itself.

"I've seen enough," she said, which was a lie, and they both let it stand as one.

* * *

By noon Brenn had done everything an old man alone can do, which is more than most people think and less than the world requires. He fed the goats. He filled every water vessel in the hall. He checked the cache under the hearthstone — the guild's emergency purse, three hundred crowns in mixed coin and a letter of credit from a free-city banker who was probably dead by now — and he took out the ledger instead.

Marette's ledger. The true one, not the false one they kept in the front room for tax-men and thieves. It sat in its oilcloth wrapping behind a loose stone in the pantry wall, and Brenn held it in both hands and felt its weight the way you feel the weight of a sleeping child or a loaded crossbow: carefully, and with respect for what it could do.

He did not open it. That was the discipline. Forty years in the Lockridge Guild and the deepest secret of the trade was knowing which books you were not permitted to read. But he weighed it, and he thought about roads — about the false roads and the true road, the one no ledger named outright, the one that lived in Marette's head and now, apparently, in whatever pages she'd inked in her last lucid months — and he thought about columns of conscripted farm-boys marching north toward the passes.

Then he went up to see whether the pigeons were still alive.

They were. Three of them, fat and ill-tempered in their cote behind the stable, descendants of birds that had carried guild messages through two wars and a plague. Brenn wrote the message four times before he got it right, because his hand had developed a tremor he refused to acknowledge, and because the wording mattered.

*Vaskal's vanguard on the Harrowmere road. Conscription sweep, moving north. Estimate main host behind. Shrines burning in the lowlands — Marta's chapel at Fordell gone, the standing stones at Cray's Mill pulled down. You are carrying medicine into country that will be occupied within the fortnight. Come home or go east to the free cities, but do not come back down the pass. Whatever you went to find — the answer is coming up the mountain to meet you.*

He read it over twice. Then he added, in smaller letters, because he was an honest man even in war:

*Marette is failing. If you mean to see her again, come quickly.*

He tied the message to the oldest bird's leg, kissed the top of its stupid feathered head, and threw it into the grey sky. It circled once, twice, caught the wind off the peaks, and became a speck, and then nothing.

Brenn stood in the yard with his face turned up until his neck ached.

* * *

The shrines started burning on the third day.

He knew because of the light. At dusk, looking south from the ridge above the goat pasture, he counted the glows along the valley floor the way a shepherd counts his flock: one at Fordell where old Marta had kept a chapel to the river-mother for sixty years; one further out at Cray's Mill; one whose position he couldn't place at all until he realized, with a cold turning in his stomach, that it must be the wayside cairn at Hollow Bridge, the little heap of stones where travelers left a copper and a wish.

They weren't burning churches, precisely. Nobody in the lowlands had worshipped in earnest for generations. They were burning *remembering*, and Vaskal's army understood, better than the people themselves did, that remembering was the thing to kill.

On the fourth day the first refugees came up the pass road, and Brenn learned what the sweep looked like from underneath.

They were eleven, all told — two families and a widow and a boy of maybe sixteen walking alone with a look on his face that Brenn recognized because he'd worn it himself at that age, the look of someone who has recently discovered that the world is larger and crueler than advertised. The father of the larger family, a miller named Ossen with flour still ghosting his sleeves, told it in bursts while Brenn fed them barley soup in the hall's front room, the room with the false ledger and the honest fire.

"They came at dawn. No horns, no warning — just the drums starting up the valley, and riders at every crossroads by midmorning. A captain read a proclamation in the square. Said the true god had returned to claim his mountain, said the old things were lies and the lying would end. Then they gave every man a choice." Ossen stared into his soup. "Take the white cord, or watch your house burn. My boys took the cord. I—" His voice failed and restarted. "I ran. I'm not proud of it. I ran, and my sons are down there wearing Vaskal's white, and I'll never—"

"You'll see them again," Brenn said, because that was what you said, and because it might even be true.

"No." Ossen shook his head slowly. "You don't understand what they're doing. The ones who take the cord get marched north with the column. And the ones who refuse—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "They make the family watch. And then they don't burn the house. They leave it standing. Empty. So everyone who passes knows what refusal costs, and knows the army doesn't rage, doesn't gloat. It just *removes* you. Like a stain."

Brenn looked at the boy sitting alone by the wall, the one with the hollow face, and didn't ask. Some stories you let people keep.

The widow spoke up near midnight, when the others slept. She was called Sera, and she had walked with the refugees out of pure stubbornness, having no kin among them, and she sat by the fire with her hands folded like a woman in prayer who has given up on being answered.

"I saw what they did at the standing stones," she said. "At Cray's Mill. I watched from the tree line — I know, I know, foolish, but I wanted to see. Twenty soldiers and an officer in grey, and they pulled the stones down with ropes and horses. And when the tallest one fell — the Mother-stone, the one with the cup-carving — the ground *shuddered*, master warden. Not like an earthquake. Like a—" she searched, frowning, "—like a dog turning over in its sleep. And the officer laughed. He heard it too, and he laughed, and he drove a spike into the fallen stone and nailed a banner to it, and the shudder stopped. Just like that. Like someone had put a hand flat on a drumhead."

Brenn said nothing. There was nothing to say. But that night, in his narrow bed in the loft, he listened to the mountain the way he hadn't since he was a boy — really listened, past the wind and the settling stone — and he thought he could feel it, faint as a pulse under a wrist: something vast down in the roots of Thornfell, turning over, trying to wake, and something else pressed down on top of it, holding it under.

Holding it under, and running out of strength to hold.

* * *

On the sixth day, Marette asked him to bring her the ledger, and Brenn understood that the clock he'd felt ticking since the starlings rose had just ticked louder.

She was worse. The cough had deepened into something wet and laborious, and the cloths he burned each evening were redder each time, and she had begun, in the hours when the fever took her, to speak to people who were not in the room — to a herald with a wound-smile, to her dead husband, once, terribly, to a young girl's voice saying *Grandmother, what have you done* — though whether that last was memory or prophecy, Brenn couldn't tell and didn't ask.

But today she was lucid. Brightly, dangerously lucid, propped up with her knife-sharp eyes fixed on him as he set the oilcloth bundle on the quilt beside her.

"You sent the bird," she said.

"Six days ago."

"And you haven't opened it." She laid one ruined hand on the ledger. "Forty years, Brenn, and you never once peeked. I used to test you, you know. Left it out twice where you'd find it. Never touched."

"Didn't need to read it. Needed to trust it." He sat on the edge of the bed, careful of his knees. "Same as now. Tell me what you need me to do with it."

Marette was quiet a long moment. Outside, wind moved through the pines with a sound like the hall breathing, and far to the south — so far it might have been thunder, except there were no clouds — something rumbled against the base of the world.

"Yara will come back," she said finally. "Or she won't, and then none of this matters. But if she comes back, she'll come back angry. She'll have learned what I sold, and she'll want to go down after it, and Cael — the priest, the broken one — will tell her it can't be stolen back, and she'll try anyway, because she's mine." Something moved across her ruined face that was almost a smile. "And when she goes, she'll need to travel a road that isn't on any map. The true road. And the key to the true road is in this book, and I am out of time to teach it to her myself."

"So give it to her plain."

"No." Marette's grip tightened on the oilcloth, sudden and fierce, the old smuggler flashing up through the dying woman like flame through a grate. "Plain words are the first thing Vaskal takes. He reads. Oh, he reads — his heralds read every ledger they ever seized, every contract, every debt, and that's how he owns half the lowlands, not with spears but with signatures. What's in here is written the way the guild writes everything. Layered. Keyed. It looks like grain shipments and toll schedules, and only a Lockridge can find the road in it — and Yara is a Lockridge if she is anything at all." She pushed the bundle toward him, and her arm shook with the effort. "Keep it. Keep it close. When she comes — if she comes — put it in her hands and tell her: *the debts are real, the interest is paid, and the vault remembers.* She'll know what it means. Her grandmother taught her numbers before she taught her letters."

"The vault remembers," Brenn repeated.

"She'll know." Marette sank back against her pillows, spent, and for a moment her eyes lost their focus and fixed on the ceiling, on something past it. "Gods forgive the arithmetic," she whispered. "Gods forgive the sum."

Brenn took the ledger. It weighed more than it had six days ago, or his arms were weaker, or grief has mass — he didn't know which, and suspected all three.

Downstairs, he wrapped it in fresh oilcloth and considered the hiding places: the hearthstone cache, the pantry wall, the hollow leg of the kitchen table. Then he looked at the window, at the southern sky where a smudge of smoke had appeared above the ridgeline — closer today than yesterday, a day's march closer, maybe less — and he made a different decision entirely.

He cut the hem off his oldest cloak, sewed the ledger flat against his own ribs where a knife wouldn't reach and a search wouldn't think to press, and stitched it shut with waxed thread, swearing under his breath the whole time because his fingers were stiff and the needle fought him and because it is easier to swear than to weep.

Because he already knew. Had known since the starlings rose, if he was honest, and certainly since the widow described the mountain turning in its sleep. The warning bird wasn't enough. Paper wings didn't outrun an army. If Yara was coming home into the teeth of this — and she was, she was his guildmaster's blood and she'd come, right into the mouth of it — then somebody had to carry more than a message down the mountain to meet her.

Somebody had to carry the guild itself. Its book. Its secret. Its stubborn, unkillable arithmetic of debts owed and debts honored.

Brenn banked the fire, filled his pack with hard bread and dried meat, laced on his boots, and stood in the doorway of the hall where he had lived for forty years, looking at the warm room and the cold road and choosing, without ceremony, the cold road.

Behind him, faint through the floorboards, he heard Marette cough, and then call his name — clear, urgent, awake.

"Brenn. *Brenn.* Come quick. It's starting."

He turned back toward the stairs, and through the high south window he saw it: light rising off the peaks of Thornfell, not dawn-light, not lightning, but something slow and green-gold climbing the mountainside like sap rising in a tree — and beneath his stitched-up ribs, against his heartbeat, the ledger seemed to grow warm.

The clock had not merely ticked. It had begun to strike.
