## 7. The Hollow Chapel

The chapel had been swallowed by the mountain the way a bone is swallowed by a dog — slowly, patiently, and with every intention of keeping it.

Yara saw it first as a shape against the snowfield, a black ribcage of roof-beams rising from a drift like something that had died mid-gallop. Then, as they crested the last shoulder of the pass, the rest of it emerged: walls of grey stone furred with frost-lichen, a bell tower snapped off at the waist, and doors — or what had been doors — hanging by one hinge, creaking in a wind that came from *inside* rather than out.

"You're certain this is it," she said. It wasn't quite a question. She'd stopped asking real questions somewhere around noon, when Cael had led her off the smugglers' map entirely, up a goat track no ledger had ever recorded, and she'd realized he knew these mountains the way she did — not from trade routes but from something older and less forgiving.

"The gate is here." Cael stood at the edge of the drift, snow to his knees, hood back despite the wind. He was thinner than he'd been three days ago; whatever the descent cost a man, it seemed to be collecting payment in advance. "Whether we open it is another matter."

"Everything's another matter with you."

He almost smiled. Almost. "That's the first honest thing you've said since Marette's funeral."

Yara pushed past him toward the doors because if she'd stayed a moment longer she would have said something worse.

* * *

Inside, the chapel smelled of cold iron and old smoke, as though a fire had happened here once and never fully finished happening. The nave was choked with drifted snow that had blown in through the broken tower and hardened into strange pale sculptures, half-melted and refrozen until they looked less like weather than like kneeling figures. Rows of pews rotted along either side. And at the far end, where an altar should have been, there was only a hole.

Not a hole in the floor, precisely. A hole in the *world*. A circle perhaps ten feet across, its edges rimmed with carved stone worn nearly smooth, descending into darkness so complete it seemed less empty than *attentive*. The air above it moved — a slow breathing draft, warm where everything else in the chapel was frozen, carrying a smell Yara couldn't name: loam, and candlewax, and something faintly sweet like fruit gone over.

She had grown up on stories about the Undercourt. Every child in the free cities had. They were the stories you told to keep each other from wandering — the court beneath the roots where debts were collected in flesh and names, where the usurper Vaskal sat on a throne of his own making and counted what he owned. She had filed them with the other useful lies, the ones that kept cargo moving and fools careful.

Now she stood over the door to one, and it breathed.

"Say it," Cael said quietly behind her. "Whatever you're thinking. Say it aloud. The gate listens better to spoken things."

"It's smaller than I imagined," Yara said. Her voice sounded flat and practical, and she hated how much comfort that gave her. "All this. The bargain, the roads, my whole life mortgaged against — that. A hole in a dead chapel."

"Gates usually are small. Doors are small. It's what comes through them that isn't." He came up beside her, close enough that she could hear his breath, see the frost melting on his lashes from the warmth rising out of the dark. His eyes were fixed on the circle with an expression she hadn't seen on him before. Not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man greeting someone he'd hoped never to meet again.

"You've been here," she said.

"I opened this one." The words came out level, practiced, the way a man recites a confession he's said so many times it has lost all its blood. "Thirty-one years ago. I was twenty-two, and I thought I was doing the god's work."

* * *

They sat in a rotted pew near the front, facing the gate, because Cael said you didn't turn your back on it, and Yara found she agreed without knowing why. She broke out the hard cheese and the heel of black bread and split it unevenly, giving herself the larger share, because grief or no grief she wasn't a saint, and Cael took the smaller piece without comment.

"My name then was different," he said, after the silence had gone on long enough to become its own kind of speech. "Cael is what I've called myself since. The old one — I won't give it to you, so don't ask. Names have weight down here, and I've spent thirty years setting mine down."

"I don't want your name. I want the story."

He turned the bread over in his fingers. Snowmelt ticked somewhere in the ruined rafters. Out beyond the broken doors, the wind had dropped, and the silence of the high pass settled over the chapel like a lid.

"There were four gods in the mountains once," he said. "You know the names even if you don't pray them. Orren of the deep roots. The twin sisters, Sela and Sune, who kept the snows and the thaw. And Vaskal." He paused on the name the way a tongue finds a cracked tooth. "Youngest. Loud. Beautiful, if you like that kind of beauty — the kind that makes you feel chosen just for looking. He was the god of thresholds. Doors, gates, borders, bargains. Everything that passes from one state to another passed through his hands, or so his priests said. I was one of his priests. Acolyte at nineteen, full orders at twenty-one. The youngest in a century."

"And the others?"

"Asleep already, by then. That's the part nobody remembers right. The songs make it sound like Vaskal murdered them in their beds, some great war in heaven. It wasn't like that. They were *tired*, Yara. Gods wear out like anything else, and worship had been thinning for generations — the free cities growing fat and forgetting, the villages keeping only the little household prayers, the guilds—" he glanced at her, and she felt it like a finger pressed to a bruise, "—the guilds praying to roads and tolls and safe passage, which is a thin diet for a god of roots and rivers. So they slept. And sleeping gods leave their duties undone, and undone duties rot, and Vaskal — who was awake, who was always awake, because a god of thresholds cannot sleep without unmaking himself — Vaskal saw the rot and decided he could do their work better than they could. All of it. All of them."

"He ate their duties."

"He ate *them*. Slowly. While they slept, he drank what was left of their worship, and he told himself it was mercy. Told us it was mercy. We believed him because believing was easier and because he made belief feel like sunlight." Cael's hands had gone still around the bread. "There were those who objected. Priests of the old rites who said the sleepers must be left to sleep and wake in their own season, that a throne built on stolen worship would curdle. Vaskal called them traitors and sealed them beneath the mountain with the bodies of the gods he'd devoured. And those of us who stayed loyal — we became the court. Heralds, gatekeepers, collectors. We kept his books."

"His books." Yara thought of Marette's ledger, the columns of debts no one could collect, and felt the two documents slide together in her mind like pages of the same volume. "My grandmother signed a book with one of you. A herald. The famine winter."

"I know which winter you mean." He said it too quickly, and she caught it, and he knew she'd caught it. For a moment neither of them moved. The warm draft from the gate stirred the frost-sculptures of snow, and one of the kneeling shapes slumped silently into powder.

"Say the rest," Yara said.

"The famine winter, the lowland harvests failed twice running, and Thornfell closed early — avalanches, dead passes, the roads the Lockridges ran sealed under twelve feet of ice. Villages starved. Your grandmother's own village among them; I know that too, don't ask me how, the court keeps records." His voice had gone very quiet. "Marette Lockridge walked up to a shrine that everyone said was empty, and she prayed loud enough to be heard below, and something answered. Something wearing a beautiful face and holding out a contract. Open roads through any winter. Safe passage for the guild forever. In exchange—"

"In exchange for a soul not yet born." Yara's hands were fists in her lap. She made them open, deliberately, one finger at a time, the way Brenn had taught her to calm herself before a negotiation. "Mine. I was in her belly. She sold me before I drew breath."

"She saved four hundred lives with you," Cael said. "I've read the terms. I helped write terms like them. Four hundred souls fed and sheltered and alive to make more souls, weighed against one. It's not a monstrous sum. Any merchant would call it fair."

"Don't." The word came out of her like a blade leaving a sheath. "Don't you dare dress it up in arithmetic. You weren't the collateral. You weren't the *cargo*."

"No," he said. "I was the one holding the pen. Which is worse, though it took me twenty years and a great deal of blood to understand why."

* * *

The argument, when it truly began, started small — the way avalanches start with a footstep.

"You said cast out," Yara said. "In the guildhall. You said you were cast out for mercy. But you just told me you kept his books for years. So which is it? Were you thrown out, or did you finally grow a conscience?"

Cael looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him decide — watched the calculation move behind his eyes like weather — and she understood suddenly that this was the real gate. Not the hole in the chapel floor. This. Whether he would hand her the truth or the version of the truth that kept him safe.

"Both," he said finally. "Neither. Sit with it a moment and I'll give it to you straight, and you can hate me however you like after."

"I already hate you. Get on with it."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more like the ghost of relief. "There was a village. Karst-hollow, under the eastern peaks, a place that still prayed properly — to Orren, to the sisters, the old words their grandmothers taught them. Vaskal wanted it emptied. The villagers' devotion was leaking down to the sleepers in their dreams, and he was — hungry. He is always hungry. He ordered a consignment. Souls owed against births, the standard instrument, enough to strip the village's children for three generations." His hands had begun to shake, very slightly, and he flattened them against his knees. "I carried the contract. I stood in their shrine while the elders wept and argued, and there was a girl — fifteen, maybe, pregnant, unmarried, the sort of soul a court loves because no one will fight for her — and she asked me, she looked at me with his light still in my eyes and asked me whether her baby would suffer."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her the truth. That the courts don't torment souls. They use them. Which is worse." He exhaled. "And then I burned the contract. In front of the elders. On the altar of a god who was asleep and could not protect me, and I sang the old litany — the one for waking, not for sealing — and I put the girl and her family on a road south and told them never to stop walking."

"And Vaskal?"

"Vaskal doesn't punish. Punishment ends. He *reassigns*." Cael's voice went flat and distant, a man reading from his own ledger. "I was given a choice. Serve in the vaults, tending the lamps — the souls, the collected ones — or be unmade quietly, no pain, no memory, as though I'd never been born. I chose the vaults. Three years in the dark counting other people's souls by lamplight, and then—" he stopped.

"And then?"

"And then the lamps started singing. Very softly. Souls shouldn't sing, Yara. Collected souls go quiet as coins. But these sang, faintly, in harmonies I recognized from the litany — the waking-litany, the forbidden one — and I understood that somewhere far above, the sleepers were turning over in their graves, and everything Vaskal had built was beginning, at last, to crack." He met her eyes. "So I ran. Up the rootways, through gates I had keys to, out into a world that had forgotten my order ever existed. Cast out for mercy — that's the version I told you. It's even true. What I left out is that I'd earned my place in hell honestly, and served there gladly, for years."

The silence afterward was enormous. Somewhere in the ruined tower, snow slid and struck the stones with a soft final knock.

Yara found that she was furious — genuinely, tremblingly furious — and that the fury was not at the confession but at the shape of the man sitting beside her. Because she had needed him to be simple. A disgraced priest, a guide, a tool with legs; that was a thing she could carry up a mountain and use and set down. What sat next to her instead was a mirror. A man who had held the pen, who had believed in the bargain, who had done the arithmetic — her grandmother's arithmetic, Vaskal's arithmetic, the arithmetic of sensible, decent, practical people — and who had needed years and blood to understand what she had understood in a single morning in a dying woman's kitchen: that some sums are monstrous no matter how neatly they balance.

"You let me walk three days with you," she said, "without telling me you built the machine that owns my soul."

"Yes."

"You could have gotten us both killed. You could have—"

"I could have. I told myself the road was dangerous enough without adding my history to your pack. I told myself you'd turn back." He shook his head. "Those were true reasons. They weren't the real one. The real one is that I'm a coward, and I hoped to reach the gate before you made me say it out loud, because saying it makes it yours as well as mine, and I have spent thirty years trying to carry it alone."

"Well," Yara said, "that's done, then."

She looked past him, at the gate. The warm darkness breathed. And she found — this surprised her more than anything the day had produced — that the fury was settling, not fading but condensing into something denser and more usable. Trust, her grandmother used to say, isn't given. It's *audited*. You check a man's books year after year, and trust is what remains when the numbers stop surprising you.

Cael's numbers had finally stopped surprising her. A coward, a collaborator, a pen-holder, a runner, a keeper of lamps — every entry ugly, every entry consistent. There was no false column anywhere in him. She had known lords and merchants and guildmasters whose books were prettier and whose hearts were forgeries top to bottom.

"Audited," she said aloud.

"What?"

"Nothing. Guild habit." She pulled her pack around and dug to the bottom, past the rope and the rations, to the oilcloth bundle she had carried from Marette's deathbed: the ledger. Not the whole book — the loose pages, the ones Marette had pressed into Brenn's hands at the end, the ones Yara had taken a copy of by candlelight while her grandmother's body cooled, because she was a Lockridge and Lockridges don't leave records lying about uncounted. "If we're going to do this, we do it with everything on the table. You held his pen. Fine. Now hold mine."

She unfolded the pages between them on the pew: Marette's cramped, iron-nibbed script, the terms of the bargain, the clauses, and there at the bottom — she watched Cael's face as he read it — a line neither of them had expected.

*Paid in full upon delivery,* Marette had written, in ink gone brown as old blood. *One soul, unbaptized, unnamed. The herald accepted. Witness: the gate itself.*

"Unnamed," Cael whispered.

"She never named me," Yara said. "Wouldn't. My mother wanted to call me after her sister, and Marette refused, and no one knew why, and I grew up thinking the old woman was just stubborn." She laughed, and it came out wrong, ragged at the edges. "She was stubborn. She was stubborn like a lockpick. She left the contract with a flaw in it, thirty years ago, waiting for someone to find the seam. My soul was sold *unnamed*. Which means, priest — you being the expert — what does that mean?"

Cael had gone very pale, and very still, and when he looked up at her there was something in his face she had never seen there before: hope, raw and terrified, like a lamp carried through wind.

"It means," he said slowly, "that the debt may not be collectible. An unnamed soul can't be entered in the vault. It may have been held in escrow all this time — or it may have slipped the ledger entirely. It means your grandmother may have out-bargained a god with nothing but a christening font and thirty years of silence." He rose, joints cracking, and stood at the edge of the gate, and the warm dark breathed up at him like an old dog scenting its master. "It also means the court will want to know why we've come. And Vaskal does not forgive clerical errors."

"Good," Yara said, standing beside him, her shadow falling into the circle along with his, two shadows braiding at the rim. "Then we'll get along. I've never forgiven one either."

Below them, far down in the attentive dark, something shifted — a sound like a vast page turning — and the gate began, softly, to open.
