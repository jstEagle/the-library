## 8. Down the Rootways

The chapel floor opened like a mouth.

Yara had expected a stair — something cut, something built, evidence that hands had made this passage and could be trusted to have made it well. Instead the stones simply parted beneath Cael's touch, folding aside in slabs the size of coffin lids, and beneath them was not darkness but a slow green dimness, the color of light strained through deep water. Roots came down through it. Enormous roots, thick as temple pillars, twisting into the earth with the patience of things that had been growing since before anyone thought to count.

"That's not a tree," she said.

"No," Cael agreed. He stood at the edge with his hood back, and in the green light his face looked older than she'd yet seen it, carved rather than merely worn. "It's what trees were trying to be. Hold the rope."

"I don't have a rope."

"Then hold on to me."

She did not answer that, and he did not press it, which was becoming the shape of their whole acquaintance: an offer made, a silence kept, both of them pretending the silence was agreement. She stepped down onto the first root instead, testing it with her smuggler's weight the way she'd test an ice bridge or a rotten plank. The root held. It was warm under her boot. That was the part her hands understood and her mind refused — stone was cold, wood was dead, and this was neither. It gave slightly, like the flank of a sleeping animal, and when she shifted her weight she felt — or imagined she felt — a pulse move somewhere far below, slow as a tide.

"Half-asleep," Cael said behind her, reading her stillness. "That's how I'd put it. Not dead. Dreaming badly."

"You keep saying *I'd put it* like you're guessing."

"I am guessing." A pause, and then, quieter, the honesty he rationed out like hardtack: "I served the court above its dreams for eleven years. I never once went down the way we're going now. Priests descend by rite, through the Gate of Names, with permission. This—" he gestured at the roots, the green dark, the vast unhurried breathing of the mountain around them, "—this is the body itself. Nobody's supposed to walk the body."

Yara looked up at him over her shoulder. "You said the chapel gate only priests remember."

"I remember it. That's different from being welcome."

Everything about him was different-from-being-welcome, she thought. The defrocked collar scarred into his skin where the holy mark had been burned away. The way he touched the roots — gently, apologetically, the way a man pets a dog that has bitten him before. She turned forward again and began to climb down.

* * *

The descent took the day and gave back no sky.

They moved through galleries of root and hollowed stone where the air tasted of loam and iron and something sweeter underneath, faint as a memory of honey. Fungus lit their way in patches — pale shelves glowing a soft corpse-green along the walls, brightening as they approached and dimming behind them, so that they traveled always inside a moving room of light. Yara had run the Thornfell passes since she was nine years old. She knew every kind of dark: the honest dark of a cave, the dishonest dark of a blind canyon at night, the professional dark of a cellar full of contraband. This was none of those. This dark was inhabited.

Not by anything she could see. But the mountain murmured. That was the only word for it. Deep vibrations rose through the roots and the walls and the soles of her boots — not sound exactly, more like the residue of sound, the way a hall holds the ghost of a voice after the speaker has gone. Sometimes it almost resolved into rhythm. Once, horribly, it almost resolved into her own name, and she stopped walking until the feeling passed.

"Does everyone hear that?" she asked finally.

"Everyone hears something different," Cael said. "That's how you know it's real. Echoes repeat. This improvises."

At the bottom of a long spiral gallery the roots braided together into a floor, and the floor sloped down toward the sound of water.

The river ran black and silver in the fungal light, wide as a millrace and moving without hurry, without any hurry at all, as if speed were a thing it had considered and declined. It filled a channel of smooth stone that might have been carved or might have been worn, and where the current bent around standing rocks it made no splash. The water simply flowed around the stones and continued, silent as poured ink.

Cael knelt at the bank and did not drink.

"What is it?" Yara asked.

"A tributary of the Underroot. In the old rites we called it the Answering Water." He glanced back at her, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before — not fear exactly. Reverence with the safety removed. "It answers questions. Truthfully. That's the whole of the danger."

"Answers questions." She crouched beside him, keeping her boots dry out of habit even though habit meant nothing here. "How?"

"Ask it something small. Something you don't mind knowing."

She almost didn't. Twenty years of smuggling had taught her that answers were currency and questions were debts, and here was a river offering to make her rich beyond sense. But curiosity in Yara Lockridge had always been stronger than caution — it was why she was alive at thirty-one, and also why she'd been arrested twice, and she leaned out over the black water and asked the smallest question she owned:

"How many people are left in the Lockridge Guild?"

The water did not speak. That was the terrible elegance of it. No voice, no lips, nothing so crude. Instead the current changed — braided itself briefly into shapes on its surface, three bright lines of reflected fungus-light converging in the black, holding, then dissolving.

Three. Her grandmother, dead these six days. Brenn, left behind at the guildhall with warning fires and a crossbow and legs too old for running. And herself.

The number had already been true when she asked. She had known it. But seeing the water agree — seeing the mountain itself do arithmetic on the ruin of her family — put a cold wire through her chest that no amount of breathing loosened.

"One more," she heard herself say. Her voice came out low and rough. "My grandmother. Marette Lockridge. Did she—"

"*Careful,*" Cael said, sharp as a whipcrack.

"—what did she trade me for?" Yara finished, because she was going to finish it whether the priest liked it or not, and some questions, once started, would carry you over the falls.

The river slowed.

It slowed the way a person slows when they must decide how much cruelty the truth requires. The surface stilled to a mirror of black glass, and in it Yara saw — not reflections. Memories, or the mountain's version of memories, rendered in light and shadow on the water's skin: a famine winter, snow to the eaves, a younger Marette with black hair and a face like a closed fist kneeling in a hall of mirrors while something vast and courteous spoke terms she could not refuse. Then a child's soul drawn out like a lamp-flame from a lantern of flesh. Then roads — roads opening through the Thornfells where no road could be, snow melting in impossible patterns, patrols sleeping at the wrong hours, every pass lying open for the Lockridges alone.

A generation of open roads. Bought with one small bright flame.

The images dissolved. The water resumed its motion, indifferent as weather.

Yara sat back on her heels. She found she was shaking, and made herself stop, because shaking was a luxury and she had never once been able to afford luxuries. "You knew," she said to Cael. "You knew what I'd see."

"I suspected. There's a difference." He said it steadily, meeting her eyes, refusing to flinch, and she hated him a little for the steadiness and was grateful for it in the same breath, and the two feelings sat in her chest like stones in a sack, knocking. "I've watched people ask that river things they couldn't survive knowing. If I'd told you what you'd see, you'd have seen it anyway. Now at least you saw it on your feet."

"On my feet," she repeated bitterly. "That's the mercy. Fine. Noted for the ledger." She stood. Her knees held, which surprised her. "Keep moving, priest."

They followed the Answering Water downstream, and Yara did not ask it anything else. Some ledgers you close yourself, before the debt collects you.

* * *

Past the river's bend the character of the tunnel changed, and Yara noticed it the way a smuggler notices a change in a border town's mood — not by any single sign but by the accumulation of small wrongnesses. The fungal light grew warmer, tinged gold. The roots overhead thickened until they wove a ceiling like the vaulting of a cathedral, and between them hung clusters of pale nodules that pulsed, very slowly, all in time. The same time. The same slow tide-beat she had felt in the first root under her boot, except now she could hear it everywhere, feel it in her teeth, in the wet walls of her own heart trying to fall into step.

And there were stones that remembered names.

She found the first one by accident. A flat slab beside the path, knee-high, its surface worn smooth — and when her hand brushed it in passing, the stone sighed. Not wind through a hollow; a sigh, human-shaped, weary and content at once, and out of the contact point bloomed a name in letters of faint golden light: *Sorrel, who carried water up the high path, and did not spill it.*

Yara snatched her hand back. The glow faded. The sigh faded. The stone was just a stone again, except that it obviously, catastrophically, was not.

"Cael."

"I see it." His voice had gone careful, the way it went careful whenever the mountain did something that confirmed his worst private theories. "Memory-stone. They're all through the deep ways, in the oldest songs. The mountain keeps what mortals give it freely. A name given in love doesn't die here. It just... settles."

"Settles where?"

"Into the god-flesh." He said it plainly, and let the word sit between them.

God-flesh. He'd used the phrase before, in the chapel, in his flat recitation of theology she'd assumed was half madman's cant: *the old gods did not die when Vaskal sealed them. You cannot kill what a mountain is made of. You can only put it to sleep.* She had filed it under things priests say to frighten smugglers. Now her hand still tingled from a rock that remembered a water-carrier's name, and the filing system was failing.

They walked on, and there were more. Dozens. Hundreds — a whole stratum of the tunnel lined with them, each one a small grave-marker for a name that wasn't dead. *Bren of the Nine Valleys, who sang to the goats.* *Ilset, who forgave her sister on her deathbed.* *Tammas, who loved the mountain better than his own mother, and said so.* Some glowed at a touch. Some glowed on their own, pulsing softly with the great slow beat, and Yara walked among them with the hairs on her arms standing like a frightened animal's, because she understood two things at once, the way you understand lightning and thunder separately before you understand they are one event.

First: these were prayers. Every one of them. Names offered up in love and grief and gratitude, whispered into shrines everyone knew were dead, pressed into stones and wellsprings and high lonely places by villagers like the ones she'd carried medicine to, generation after generation, with no expectation of answer. Prayers don't vanish. Nobody had ever bothered to wonder where they went. They went *down*. They soaked into the mountain the way rain soaks into a field.

Second: the medicine had worked impossibly well. The fever-blight that should have taken half that village had broken overnight, clean, like snow off a roof in spring. She'd told herself it was a strong batch. She had known, even then, in the practical certainty she'd built her whole life on, that it wasn't.

"They're not returning," she said.

Cael stopped.

"The old gods." Yara turned in a slow circle, taking in the glowing names, the vaulted roots, the pulsing dark that breathed around them like the chest of something vast turning over in its sleep. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears — small and enormous simultaneously. "Everyone says they're coming back. Returning. Rising. Like they went somewhere and they're walking home." She looked at Cael. "But you can't return if you never left. They've been *here*. The whole time. Asleep. And every prayer, every name, every desperate little candle lit at a dead shrine has been falling on them like rain on a seed, and now the bargain's failing and the seal's cracking and—"

"And they're waking," Cael finished quietly. "Yes."

"Not returning." The word felt important enough to say twice. "Being woken. By us. By everyone who ever prayed to a dead god because praying was all they had left." She laughed, and it came out wrong, ragged at the edges. "All those years I hauled contraband past ruined shrines and thought the people lighting candles in them were pitiful. Superstitious. Wasting good tallow." She pressed her palm flat against the nearest memory-stone, deliberately this time, and it warmed under her hand and breathed out its little golden name — *Ilset, who forgave her sister* — and she felt the mountain feel it, a shiver of attention passing through the dark like light through water. "They weren't wasting anything. They were doing the one work that mattered, and nobody knew, and I *hauled cargo* past them."

"Yara."

"Don't tell me it's not my fault. I know it's not my fault. That's not what this is." She pulled her hand back. Her fingers trembled; she watched them do it with a kind of clinical interest. "This is the other thing. The thing where you realize everything you were sure of was a coin someone clipped, and it spent fine your whole life, and now you've weighed it." She looked up at him, and the gold light caught the planes of his face, the burn-scar at his throat. "How awake are they? Right now. Under us. How close?"

He hesitated.

"Cael. You owe me at least the same honesty the river gives."

"Close," he said. "Closer than the rites ever predicted. When the seal fails — when your soul comes out of the vault — the keystone goes, and what's under us stands up. All of it. At once." His eyes went to the pulsing nodules overhead, counting heartbeats. "Which means everything between here and the court happens inside a creature that is dreaming its last dream. Every tunnel we walk, every stone that knows our names — we are inside the waking mind, Yara. It is starting to notice us the way you notice a fly in a dark room. Not with malice. With attention."

"With attention," she repeated.

"Attention," Cael said, "is how gods love. And how they kill. From where it stands, the two look identical."

They walked on. Below them, the Answering Water plunged over a lip of stone into deeper dark, and the sound it made going down was almost — almost — a word. The gold-lit gallery narrowed ahead into a throat of root and rock, and from somewhere far beneath, rising through the soles of Yara's boots and the marrow of her bones, the great slow pulse quickened. Just slightly. Just enough.

Something down there had rolled over in its sleep.

And far ahead, at the edge of the fungal light, Yara saw the thing she had been dreading without knowing its shape: a doorway of black stone, ancient and deliberate, framed in carved letters she could not read but recognized anyway, the way you recognize handwriting on a bill.

Cael stopped. "The threshold," he said. "The Gate of Names. And Yara — it will want a toll."
