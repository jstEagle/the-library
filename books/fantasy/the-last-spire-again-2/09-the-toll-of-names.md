## 9. The Toll of Names

The gate was older than the chapel above it.

Cael had known it would be — he had helped seal three like it in another life, back when his hands still smelled of incense instead of road dust and fear — but knowing a thing and standing before it were different countries. The descent did not open into the mountain so much as *into* the mountain's memory: a stair of living root spiraling down through darkness the color of deep water, each step ringed with pale fungus that breathed light in slow pulses, like something dreaming.

They had walked down for the better part of a day. Yara had not spoken for the last two hours. That silence had a shape he was learning to read — not anger, not anymore, but a kind of coiled attention, the way she must have watched passes for avalanches her whole life. Listening for the shift before it came.

The stair ended at a threshold that should not have existed underground: a doorway of black stone framed by carved lintels worn nearly smooth, set into nothing, opening onto a plain of ash-gray grass beneath a sky that was not a sky. Above the door, in letters older than any alphabet Cael had sworn himself to, ran a single line of script. He could still read it. That was the cruelest part. Vaskal had taken everything else from him — his orders, his name in the rolls of the priesthood, the right to speak the litany aloud — but he had left him the languages. Perhaps because they were useless without the faith. Perhaps because Vaskal liked keeping knives on the shelf.

*What is given freely cannot be taken,* the lintel said.

"Here we are," Cael said quietly. "The threshold."

Yara came to stand beside him, close enough that he could feel the warmth coming off her shoulder. After days of cold root-tunnels, it seemed almost indecent. She looked at the door the way she looked at ledgers — weighing, auditing, finding the lie in the numbers.

"That doesn't look like a gatekeeper," she said. "That looks like a door."

"The gatekeeper comes with the toll." Cael exhaled slowly. "I've stood at doors like this one. There's always a keeper. And there's always the same price."

He felt her turn to look at him. He didn't return it yet. He kept his eyes on the gray plain beyond, where shapes moved at the edge of sight — tall, patient, wrong — gathering the way crows gather before they decide what they are waiting for.

"A true name," he said. "That's the toll. Not a name you answer to. Not a name your mother called you. The name underneath all of those — the one that says what you actually *are*. Give it freely, and the door opens. Refuse, and the door stays shut forever. Try to force it—" he touched the black stone lightly, and the cold of it went up his arm like a confession, "—and the mountain takes the toll out of your hide instead. I've seen what's left after. It isn't much."

"So we give it a false name." Her voice was flat, practical, smuggler-certain. "We've been lying our whole lives. How hard can one more be?"

"It will know." He finally turned to face her. In the breathing light of the fungus her eyes were very dark and very steady, and he found, as he always did, that looking at them made honesty easier than it should have been. "It isn't a lock, Yara. It's a scale. It weighs what you offer against what you carry. A lie weighs nothing, so it tips nothing. You'd stand here until you died of thirst, and then it would take your name off your corpse, and it would be true anyway."

"Then how do people get through?"

"They don't want to badly enough. Or—" and here his voice betrayed him, going quiet, going careful, "—or they pay."

The silence between them stretched. Somewhere far below, or perhaps very near, something exhaled — a long sound like wind through a throat of stone.

"I'll pay it," Cael said. "My true name. It's worth almost nothing now — I'm defrocked, excommunicated, my old name burned off every register Vaskal keeps. But it's still *true*, and truth is all this door cares about." He managed something that was almost a smile. "Consider it the last tithe of a bad priest. One honest payment, at the end, for everything dishonest I ever did in service."

"No."

He blinked. Of all the things he had expected from her — grim agreement, a bitter joke, that flinty pragmatism that carried her through everything — he had not expected the word to come so fast, so hard, so *final*.

"Yara—"

"You don't get to do that." She stepped between him and the door as if the black stone itself might reach out and take him if he lingered too long in its sight. "You don't get to spend yourself like coin just because you've decided you're worthless. I've heard you talk about yourself, Cael. You talk about your own soul like it's a debt already written off. Like no one would miss it."

"Would anyone?" The question escaped him before he could dress it up. It came out rawer than he intended, and he hated that — hated how easily she cracked the shell he'd spent ten years building. "Be honest, Yara. What am I? A guide who got lost. A priest of a god he helped chain. Everything I touch turns to either ash or confession. My name is the only thing I have left to give, and it happens to be exactly what's needed. Let me be useful once. Let me end the ledger square."

Something moved across her face — fury, he thought at first, and then recognized it as its twin: grief wearing fury's clothes.

"You listen to me." She grabbed a fistful of his collar and pulled him down until they were eye to eye, and her breath was warm against his chin, and her hands were shaking, and he realized with a jolt that the shaking wasn't rage. "Marette spent my soul to keep a road open. Do you understand what that did to me? Finding out that the person who loved me most in the world had priced me, weighed me, and paid me out like cargo?" Her voice cracked and she pushed through it. "I will *not* stand here and watch you do the same thing to yourself. Not for me. Not for the gods. Not for anything."

"And what would you have us do?" he asked softly. "Turn back?"

"I don't know. I'll think of something. I always think of something." Her grip didn't loosen. "But you don't go through that door as a payment. If you walk through it, you walk through it *with* me, whole, or not at all."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The gray light pulsed. Far off across the ash-plain, the patient shapes drifted closer, unhurried as weather.

Cael had spent his entire adult life believing he understood the arithmetic of worth — that souls were weighed, that mercy was a subtraction, that he himself had come out on the wrong side of the sum long ago. And here was this woman, this smuggler, this thief of roads, telling him the ledger was wrong. Telling him she would rather fail her quest than balance it with him.

No one had ever refused a sacrifice on his behalf. It undid something in him that ten years of exile hadn't touched.

"Marette," he said at last, hoarsely. "You said she hid things in the ledger. Codes, routes, prices."

Yara's grip loosened, though she didn't let go entirely. "What about it?"

"She bargained with Vaskal's herald face to face. You don't survive a negotiation like that without learning how these courts think. If she sold your soul to seal the roads, she'd have planned for the day someone came to unseal them. She was too good a merchant not to leave herself an exit clause." He swallowed. "Is there anything in that book you couldn't read? Any page that didn't make sense?"

Her brow furrowed. Slowly, reluctantly, she let go of his collar and swung her pack around, digging past rope and ration tins until she drew out Marette's ledger — the fat, water-stained volume they had carried half a mile under the earth like a relic. She opened it across her forearms and turned pages with a thumb gone suddenly gentle.

"Here," she murmured. "Last page before the blank ones. It's not in her cipher. It's not in any cipher I know. Just a single line, over and over, like she was practicing it." She angled the book toward him. "*Veyra Ashfall, daughter of no house, keeper of the ninth road.* Over and over. Thirty times. I thought it was a prayer."

Cael stared at the page. The letters wavered slightly in the fungal light, and as he looked at them, they seemed to look back — the way words do when they are not quite words.

"It's not a prayer," he said. "It's a *name*. A constructed name — a true name built the way heralds build them, piece by piece, meaning stacked on meaning. Daughter of no house. Keeper of the ninth road. Ashfall — grief that falls and covers everything and lets green things grow through it after." His mouth went dry. "She built herself a second soul, Yara. A forged one. True enough to tip the scale, because every word of it was real. She *was* a daughter of no house. She did keep a ninth road nobody knew. This isn't a lie — it's an autobiography distilled until it burns."

Yara's breath caught. "She left it for us. For whoever came down here."

"She left it for *you*." He looked up from the page and found her eyes again. "She couldn't give you back what she took. So she gave you the tool to take it yourself. That was her apology. That was probably the only apology she was capable of making."

Yara closed the ledger with exaggerated care, the way one closes a coffin, and pressed it briefly to her chest. For a moment her composure slipped entirely, and Cael saw the girl underneath the guild-master — a girl who had loved a difficult, brilliant, terrible woman, and had been loved back in the only currency that woman had ever learned to count.

Then the moment passed, and Yara Lockridge squared her shoulders, and walked toward the door.

"Hey!" Cael lunged after her. "You can't just—"

"Watch me." She stopped before the black stone, lifted her chin, and spoke clearly into the listening dark. "Gatekeeper! I bring a name for the toll. Given freely, as your law requires."

The ash-plain shivered. The distant shapes stopped moving. And from everywhere and nowhere came a voice like roots growing through rock — vast, slow, neither cruel nor kind, the voice of a thing that has held one job since before the idea of jobs.

***A*** ***name***, it said. ***Offered*** ***freely***.

"Freely offered," Yara said. "And hear the terms before you weigh it. I give the name *of a dead woman*. Marette Lockridge, daughter of no house, keeper of the ninth road. Every word of it true. She built it with her own hands and left it in her ledger, signed and sealed, for whoever needed passage." She opened the book and laid it flat upon the threshold stone. "Read it. Weigh it. Take it."

The silence that followed was enormous. Cael felt the gate attend — felt the whole weight of the Undercourt's attention bend toward that small stained book like a river bending toward a gap in the hills. The letters began to glow, faintly, a gold like lamplight seen through fog.

And then the voice spoke again, and this time there was something in it that Cael, in all his years of serving courts divine and damned, had never heard from a gatekeeper before.

***This*** ***name*** ***is*** ***spent***, it said. ***Its*** ***bearer*** ***is*** ***dust***. ***A*** ***dead*** ***soul*** ***cannot*** ***pay*** ***a*** ***living*** ***toll***.

Yara didn't flinch. "Then check your own law. Does your law say the name must belong to the living? Or only that it must be *true*?"

Another silence, longer. The gray grass rippled in a wind that had no source. Behind the door, something vast turned over in its sleep, considering.

***...The*** ***law*** ***says*** ***true***, the gatekeeper said at last, and there was, unmistakably, reluctance in it — the tone of a clerk finding a loophole used against him for the first time in ten thousand years. ***The*** ***name*** ***is*** ***true***. ***The*** ***gift*** ***is*** ***free***. ***The*** ***scale*** ***accepts***.

The black stone split down the middle with a sound like a held breath released, and the doors swung inward onto a stairway descending into a light that was not light — a golden murk, warm and terrible, shot through with drifting motes that might have been dust or might have been the last bright fragments of ten thousand stolen souls.

Cael grabbed Yara's arm. "Wait. Wait — you can't just hand it a dead woman's name and walk through. There's always a price beyond the price. There's always—"

"There's always another ledger entry," Yara said. "I know. I grew up reading them." She picked up the ledger, closed it, and slung her pack. Then she paused, and something flickered across her face — not doubt. Decision. "But you're right about one thing. Nothing in this place is free. So before we take one more step, you tell me the rest of it, Cael. All of it. What you did for Vaskal. What you really were. Because I just bet my grandmother's ghost on this door, and I'm not walking into that court with one more secret between us."

The golden dark waited. Somewhere below, faintly, Cael could hear singing — a choir of voices with no throats, humming the old litany wrong, note by note, like a memory rotting.

He told her. Standing at the threshold, with the gate open and the court listening, he told her everything he could bear to say and some of what he couldn't: the sealing of the gods, the chains he had sung tight, the mercy that had saved him and damned everyone else. He watched her face while he did it, waiting for the revulsion, the withdrawal, the cold.

Instead, when he finished, she simply nodded once, took his hand — took it, deliberately, fingers laced through his like a knot meant to hold — and said:

"Good. Now we both owe the same god. Let's go collect what's ours."

And hand in hand, they stepped down into the light of the Court Beneath, while behind them the gatekeeper's voice followed like a closing ledger:

***Enter***, ***debtors***. ***The*** ***court*** ***has*** ***been*** ***expecting*** ***you***.
