## 10. The Court Beneath

The last stair was made of salt.

Yara felt it before she saw it — a fine grit under her boots that crunched with every step, and the taste of it rising in the dark like the ghost of a sea no mountain should have known. She had crossed the Thornfells in every season of her life. She knew snow and scree and the blue ice that hid its teeth under a skin of meltwater. She did not know salt.

"It's from the tears," Cael said quietly behind her. He had not spoken for hours, not since the river of names had finally gone silent beneath their feet, as if even it were afraid to be overheard this close to the court. "The sleeping ones. The mountain weeps brine where they lie."

"Mountains don't weep."

"This one does." A pause, and then, softer: "You'll want to stop being surprised now. It only costs you time down here."

She wanted to tell him she'd stopped being surprised three days ago, back when the stones had murmured her grandmother's name in a voice like gravel settling. But the truth was that surprise kept finding her anyway, patient as winter, and all she could do was keep walking.

The stair ended at a door.

That alone would have been strange enough — nothing else in the Rootways had doors, only thresholds, only places where one kind of dark became another kind. But this was a door in every particular: oak gone black with age, iron hinges furred with frost, a lintel carved with letters worn nearly smooth. Yara ran her thumb along them and felt the shape of words she almost knew, the way you know a song from before you could speak.

"What's it say?"

"'All accounts settled at the threshold,'" Cael read. His voice did something complicated on the last word. "It's the old toll-law. Older than Vaskal. The courts have always run on debt, Yara. He didn't invent that. He just made himself the only creditor."

She looked at him. In the grey light leaking through the door's seams, his face was hollowed and watchful, a man standing at the edge of his own grave and reading the inscription with professional interest. She thought about asking — again — what he'd been, when he'd served here. She thought about what he hadn't said in the chapel, in the root-tunnels, on the long silence of the descent. She decided it could wait until after. Everything could wait until after. That was how smugglers survived: one border at a time.

"Then let's settle our account," she said, and pushed the door open.

* * *

The guildhall above stood at the meeting of two passes, low-roofed and stubborn, built by hands that expected to defend it. It smelled of tallow smoke and wet wool and Brenn's terrible pipe-weed. Its great hall held one long table scarred by fifty years of knives, and above the hearth hung the guild's ledgers in oiled leather, and the light came from windows set high so that no one on the road could see who sat within.

The hall Yara stepped into was that room turned inside out.

The table was there — but it stretched farther than any hall could hold, running away into a distance that bent wrong, its far end lost in a haze of gold light that never flickered. The benches were there, but no one sat at them; instead the spaces between were crowded with pillars of pale stone, dozens of them, hundreds, each one wrapped in chains of a metal that was not iron and not silver and moved when she wasn't looking directly at it. The ledgers were there — shelved along walls that climbed into darkness, thousands upon thousands of them, spines stamped in a script that hurt to read sideways. And where the hearth should have been, where Marette's fire had burned down to stubborn embers every night of Yara's childhood, there was instead a vast shape lying coiled in shadow: something enormous and slow-breathing, scaled in stone, its flank rising and falling once for every hundred of Yara's heartbeats.

God-flesh. Sleeping. Chained to the floor of the court with those moving links, and dreaming — she could feel it dreaming, a pressure against her mind like weather coming.

"Don't look at it too long," Cael breathed. "And don't touch anything. In here, touching is *signing*."

"I know how contracts work," she muttered, but she put her hands in her pockets anyway.

They walked. There was nothing else to do; the door had shut itself behind them with the soft finality of a ledger closing, and the hall's one visible exit lay at the far end, past the table, past the pillars, past the thing that slept. Their footsteps made no sound. That frightened her more than the chains. Above ground, Yara Lockridge's boots announced her on gravel, on boardwalk, on the frozen mud of the high passes — she had always taken a certain pride in being heard coming. Here she was a rumor even to herself.

The pillars, as they drew closer, resolved into something worse than stone.

They were lamps.

Each pillar was a case of glassy crystal, and inside each case hung a small flame that did not behave like fire — it didn't rise, it didn't gutter, it simply *was*, steady as a held note. And around each flame, faint but unmistakable, moved shapes. A woman's laugh, heard from another room. A boy running downhill. Hands kneading bread. Grief. A first snow. The small unrepeatable moments that make a life into a life rather than a span of days.

Souls, kept as coin.

Yara stopped walking. She couldn't help it. Her hand found the nearest crystal and hovered an inch away, close enough to feel the cold coming off it, close enough to see her own reflection warped in the curve beside the little flame within — a soul that had belonged to someone, that someone had laughed and grieved and kneaded bread, and here it hung in a vault between a pillar of chains and a shelf of debts, inventory in the court of a usurper god.

"That one's fresh," Cael said, very quietly. "See the color? Bright yellow-white, still moving. They dim as they're spent. Spent souls go grey, then clear, then the case gets emptied and refilled." He said it flatly, the way he said everything about this place, and Yara realized he was doing it on purpose — giving her the facts like cargo manifests because if he gave them any other way he might not give them at all.

"How many cases are there?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. The shelves ran up into blackness, and the pillars went on past counting, and somewhere in that gold haze at the end of the table something stirred that was neither sleeper nor lamp nor ledger.

"Keep your eyes forward," Cael said. "We're meant to arrive. Let's arrive looking like we belong."

* * *

They did not belong. Yara knew it the way she knew when a customs officer had already found the false panel in her cart: some instinct older than thought, reading the room's attention settling on her like frost forming. The hall knew they were there. Perhaps the hall had always known.

The throne — if that was the word — stood where Marette's chair stood in the guildhall above, at the head of the long table. But where Marette's chair was a kitchen chair dragged to the head out of practicality and never replaced, this was a construction of black basalt and bone-white veining, tall as three men, its back carved into the likeness of a mountain range with its peaks snapped off. And seated in it —

She had expected a monster. She had spent the whole descent building one out of Cael's silences and her own nightmares, some vast horned thing dripping dark, and she clung to that expectation like a handhold right up until Vaskal looked at her.

He looked like a man who owned things. That was all. Middle-aged the way a well-kept house is middle-aged, broad and comfortable in robes of undyed grey wool, with a merchant's open face and a farmer's heavy hands folded on the table before him. His hair was going grey at the temples. He could have been anyone's uncle, anyone's guildmaster, anyone's neighbor across the pass — and that was the horror of him, Yara understood suddenly, that was the whole architecture of the horror. No one ever signed a bad contract believing the other party was a monster. Monsters got refused. This — this smiling patience, this air of a man who had never once lost money — this got *signed*.

"Daughter of Lockridge." His voice filled the hall without effort, the way cold fills a valley. Around the pillars, the little flames shivered. All of them, at once, thousands of souls flinching together, and Yara's stomach turned over. "You've come a long way down for a piece of property."

"It's mine," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she'd earned. "Bought and paid for by no one. I'm here to withdraw it."

Something moved at the edges of his face — not quite a smile, but the space where a smile would go on a man. "Withdrawn. A smuggling word. I do admire the craft of you people. Your grandmother had it too. Did you know that? Marette Lockridge sat where you stand, thirty years ago, and bargained like a woman with nothing left to lose, which is the most dangerous kind." He unfolded one heavy hand and gestured, gently, at the vault of lamps around them. "Do you know what these are, in my accounting? Not prisoners. Not treasure. *Collateral*. Every soul in this hall secures a debt, and every debt keeps something running — a road open, a famine ended, a war unfought. Your grandmother understood collateral better than any mortal I've dealt with since. She put up the one thing she had that I could not take."

Yara's hands had closed into fists inside her pockets. She could feel her nails through the lining. "Me."

"You." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow the worst available option. "A child unborn, pledged against the roads of Thornfell. And the roads stayed open, did they not? Thirty years of winters your guild survived that should have buried it. Every route through those passes, safe, when every other road in the mountains bled. You ate off that bargain, daughter of Lockridge. You grew strong on it. You are standing here today on credit your grandmother extended in your name." He leaned back, and the broken-peaked throne creaked like a ship. "I am not your enemy. I am your *creditor*. There is a difference, though I grant few mortals live long enough to appreciate it."

Behind her, she heard Cael shift his weight, and heard — or imagined she heard — the faint rattle of a chain adjusting somewhere in the dark, the sleeping thing turning in its dream.

"The debt's failing," Yara said. "That's why the gods are waking. That's why your court smells like tears and salt. The bargain's coming apart, and you need me down here to re-sign it."

For the first time, something flickered behind the merchant's open face — quick, quickly mastered, but she'd spent her life reading men across negotiation tables and she caught it the way she caught a false weight in a coin purse.

"Clever," Vaskal said softly. "Yes. Come. Walk with me, daughter of Lockridge. If you're going to refuse me — and you will refuse me, your kind always does, it's what makes your kind worth the trouble — then refuse me knowing exactly what it is you carry. What it is I've been keeping safe for you all these years." He rose, unhurried, and gestured toward a door in the far wall she was certain had not been there before. "Every debtor deserves to see the vault where her collateral sleeps."

* * *

The vault door was iron, and the lock on it was beautiful.

Yara hated herself a little for noticing, but noticing was the trade — she'd picked locks since she was seven, and this one was a masterwork, seven tumblers nested inside one another like the rings of a tree, each one shaped differently, each one requiring a different pressure and a different lie. She could have stared at it for an hour. She suspected that was part of its design: a lock that invited admiration while making theft unthinkable.

Vaskal touched it with two fingers and it opened like a flower.

Beyond lay a round chamber, domed, lit by no source she could find — the light simply lived in the air, sourceless and even, the way it lives underwater. Shelves curved away in concentric rings, and on the shelves stood lamps. Thousands. Tens of thousands. More souls than Yara had ever imagined existing in the world, more than all the free cities could hold, each flame burning its own color, its own tempo, and the combined effect was a sound — she realized after a moment that the chamber *hummed*, a chord made of ten thousand lives being held in suspension, a chord that was almost music and was unbearable precisely because of the *almost*.

"There," Vaskal said, and pointed.

One lamp, on the third ring, near eye height. Small. Bright. Burning a clean hard white-gold that cut through the hum like a whistle through crowd-noise, and around it, faintly, moving in its glass: a girl on a frozen pass with medicine on her back. Salt on a doorstep. A grandmother's cough through a thin wall. An argument in a ruined chapel, and the moment — the exact moment, preserved like an insect in amber — when a disgraced priest looked at her in the dark and she stopped being afraid of him.

Recognition hit her like a fall onto ice.

She knew that flame. Not the way you know a possession. The way you know your own heartbeat, your own breath, the specific ache behind your eyes when you cry — she knew it from the *inside*, and seeing it outside herself, caged, catalogued, humming in a chord of ten thousand captives, tore something open in her chest that she suspected would never fully close again. This was what Marette had sold. Not an abstraction. Not a coin. *This.* The girl on the pass. The salt. The anger and the arithmetic and the way she loved people without telling them. All of it, hanging in a vault, drawing interest for a smiling man in grey wool.

Her legs carried her forward before she decided to move. Her hand rose toward the glass.

"I wouldn't." Vaskal's voice, mild as milk. "Touching is signing, remember? Though—" and now the smile arrived in full, warm as a hearth, dreadful as a landslide— "in your case, daughter of Lockridge, the signature was written long ago. Look closely at your lamp. Look at what it's *doing*."

She looked. And she saw it.

The lamp was not merely burning. It was *anchored*. Threads of light ran out from it — fine as spider silk, fine as hair — threading away through the vault, through the walls, down into the stone, and every thread terminated in one of the sleeping chained shapes she had glimpsed in the hall beyond. One thread per god. Dozens of threads. The small bright lamp that was Yara Lockridge was stitched into the flesh of the old gods like a keystone in an arch, holding them down, holding them shut, holding them *asleep*.

"Do you understand now?" Vaskal asked gently. "Your grandmother sold me a lamp. What she sold me, unknowing, was a keystone. When the old bloodline's soul burns in my vault, the sleepers cannot wake. Pull it free—" he spread his heavy hands, a merchant displaying the flaw in honest goods— "and the arch falls. Either direction. Wake them, and they rise hungry after a thousand years of chains. Break it, and the arch comes down on everything beneath these peaks. There is no version of your rescue, child, that does not cost the mountains." He tilted his head, kindly, terribly. "So. Shall we discuss terms?"

Behind her, at the mouth of the vault, Cael made a sound — small, involuntary, the sound of a man watching a door he helped build swing open again.

And deep beneath the floor, beneath the shelves, beneath ten thousand humming flames, Yara felt something vast turn over in its sleep — and one thread of light, just one, trembled like a plucked string.

The lamp flickered.

Not dimming. *Reaching.*
