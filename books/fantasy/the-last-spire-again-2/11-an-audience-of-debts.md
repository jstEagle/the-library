## 11. An Audience of Debts

They did not drag her. That was the first insult.

Yara had expected chains, had braced for the bite of iron on her wrists the way she'd once braced for the snap of a snare-line on the high passes — with a certain grim familiarity. Instead the wardens of the Undercourt simply walked on either side of her, tall and gray as winter pines, and the corridor opened before them like water parting around a stone. No doors were locked. No locks were needed. The court beneath the mountain was so certain of itself that escape was not a category of thought.

She catalogued everything anyway. Old habit. The vault-wardens wore masks of hammered tin, featureless except for eye-holes, and their hands were wrong — too many knuckles, folding like the legs of spiders when they gestured her forward. The corridor walls were veined with something pale that pulsed faintly when she looked at it directly and went still when she didn't. Rootways, Cael had called them, back when he'd still been talking to her. The roots of something older than Vaskal. The sleeping flesh of the old gods, threaded through the whole belly of the mountain, and the usurper had built his palace on top of it like a man building a house over a grave he meant to rob.

The lamps came next, and those she could not catalogue without her chest tightening. They hung from hooks of black iron along the passage at intervals — small glass vessels, each holding a flame that did not flicker. Some burned white. Some burned gold, or green, or a blue so deep it hurt. And some, here and there, guttered low, nearly out, and the wardens walked past them without a glance because what was guttering had no one left above ground to remember it.

*Souls as coin,* Cael had said. *Vaskal keeps his treasury burning.*

Yara counted them as she passed. It steadied her, counting. Two hundred and forty-one lamps between the gate and the doors of the hall, and every one of them a person who had been sold, or gambled away, or bargained off by someone who loved them just enough to damn them.

Her grandmother's arithmetic. She could almost hear Marette's voice: *Everything costs, girl. The only question is whether you pay in coin or in blood, and blood is always cheaper.*

The doors of the audience hall were carved from a single slab of mountain-glass, and they showed her herself as she approached — a thin woman in a smuggler's oiled coat, hair escaping its braid, eyes red-rimmed from the river that answered questions. In the glass she looked exactly like what she was: a Lockridge, down to the last three, walking into the one market where she had no goods worth selling.

Except that wasn't true anymore, was it. She had one good. One lamp in a vault of thousands.

The doors swung inward without being touched.

* * *

The hall beyond was her guildhall turned inside out.

She knew it immediately, the way you know your own face in a warped mirror — the long trestle tables become benches of obsidian, the great hearth become a pit of coals that gave no heat, the ledger-shelves along the north wall become niches, hundreds of niches, each cradling a lamp. Even the smell was right and wrong: woodsmoke and tallow and cold stone, the guildhall's smell, but underneath it something sweeter and fouler, like honey poured over a wound.

At the far end, where Marette's chair had stood — the high-backed chair with the broken left armrest that nobody had ever fixed because fixing it would mean admitting the guild couldn't afford a carpenter — sat Vaskal.

He was not what she had feared. He was worse, because he was ordinary. A broad man in dark robes, silver-streaked beard trimmed short, hands folded on the table before him like a merchant waiting out a slow season. His crown was a simple band of black iron, unornamented, and it sat on his head the way a yoke sits on an ox: not worn, but borne. Only his eyes betrayed him. They were the color of the lamps that guttered low, and when they moved across the room, the nearest flames leaned toward him like wheat toward wind.

"Yara Lockridge." Her name in his mouth sounded like a debt being called in. "Granddaughter of Marette, daughter of the roads. Sit."

There was a chair opposite him, placed precisely at the middle of the long table, and she understood that this too was theater — the seat of an equal, offered to soften her before the hook went in. She stayed standing. "I've walked a long way to stand," she said. "I'll do my business on my feet."

Something flickered across his face. Not anger. Interest, which was more dangerous.

"So did your grandmother," said Vaskal. "Stand, I mean, through the whole of our first negotiation. Famine winter. You would have been — what, four? Five? She stood in this same hall, in a body already half-starved, and told me the Lockridge Guild does not kneel." He unfolded his hands. "I admired her enormously. I want you to know that before we speak of what she cost me, and what she cost you. Admiration makes the accounting cleaner."

"I'm not here for accounting. I'm here for a withdrawal." Yara kept her voice flat, the voice she used at border crossings with guards who liked to play games. "One item. Small. Bright. You'll know it when you see it."

"The lamp with your name on it." He nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something he'd long suspected. "Yes. You've come to steal yourself back. The priest told you the way down, and you thought — what? That a woman who runs salt past tax-men could run her own soul past me?"

"He told me nothing useful. Neither did the river, nor the stones. I figured the rest out myself." This was mostly true and entirely bravado, and from the faint tilt of his head she knew he knew it. Fine. Let him know. Smugglers survived on bluff longer than they survived on steel.

"You misunderstand your position, Yara Lockridge, and I think your pride has made you stupid about it, so let me be plain." He rose, and the room rose with him — the coals brightening, the lamps swaying though there was no draft, the pale veins in the walls pulsing faster, like a sleeper's heartbeat quickening toward nightmare. "You are not a thief in my hall. You are the deed to my hall. Do you understand? Your soul is not *coin*. Coin I have. Your soul is the *keystone*."

The word landed in her like a dropped anchor.

"Marette came to me in famine winter wanting three things," Vaskal continued, pacing now along the length of the table, and she noticed his footsteps made no sound at all, not even on stone. "Open roads through Thornfell that no army could close and no blizzard could bury. Prosperity enough that the guild's name would keep its people fed. And time — a generation of it, safe and quiet, while the world forgot the old ways and the old gods slept deeper." He stopped beside a niche and touched one lamp, tenderly, the way Brenn touched the guild's last good mule. "In exchange she offered me collateral. Not her soul — hers was spent, half of it, and the rest too old and tough to be of use. She offered me something unborn. Unformed. A child of her line, not yet come, whose soul would be — "

"Mine," Yara said. "You already told Cael this. Get to the part he doesn't know."

That earned her a real look, sharp and pleased. "Direct. Good. Then here is what no one knows, not the priest, not your dead grandmother, not the sleeping things in these walls." He turned to face her fully, and up close his eyes were terrible in their ordinariness, the eyes of every customs officer and warlord and creditor she had ever hated. "When I sealed the bargain, I did not take a random soul. I took the first soul of the new generation of your line — and the first soul of a new generation, freely promised by its elder, is a key that fits every lock the old gods ever built. Their prisons are all one prison, Yara. Their seals are all one seal. And the lock at the center of it — the one that holds the others shut — was never forged in heaven or hell. It was forged in *bloodline*. In inheritance. In the thing mortals pass hand to hand without ever knowing what they're carrying."

The veins in the walls pulsed. Somewhere far below, deep under the floor of the world, something vast shifted in its sleep, and the coals in the pit flared green.

"Your grandmother sold me the keystone," Vaskal said softly. "She thought she was selling a smuggler's collateral. She was handing me the pin that holds the mountain's heart shut. And now the old gods are waking — you've felt it, you've seen the river answer, the stones remember — and every year they wake further, they strain against a seal that is wearing through. Because the seal *is you*, child. Your soul, kept separate from you, kept burning in my vault, is the only reason this mountain has not already torn open and swallowed both my court and your precious free cities. When they wake fully — and they will wake fully; I can feel them turning over down there like men turning in a bed too small — there will be one moment. One instant when the old seals fail and the new order must be written before the fire spreads. And whoever holds the keystone writes that order."

He let the silence stretch. Around the hall, the lamps burned. Two hundred and forty-one had been the corridor; in here there were thousands, a horizon of small fires, each one a life reduced to light in glass.

"So," Yara said, because her mouth was dry and saying anything was better than letting him watch her think. "You want me to hand it over willingly. That's the catch, isn't it. Stolen souls are just lamps. But a keystone given freely — that's a door you can build with."

"Your grandmother read the same ledgers you do," Vaskal said, with something almost like warmth. "Blood tells. Yes. A taken soul resists. A surrendered soul *cooperates*. If I seize your lamp and crack it open by force, I get maybe half its power and a mountain range full of rubble. But if you give it — if you stand in this hall and say *take it, it's mine to give* — then when the gods wake, I don't fight them. I *eat* them. All of them. Every name the mountains ever whispered. And I rule what's left forever, in a peace so total it will look like silence."

"And if I say no?"

"Then the seals fail on their own, in chaos, in agony, and the waking gods rise mad and starving, having slept too long, and everything dies screaming — my court, your cities, your roads, your priest." He shrugged, a merchant's shrug. "Or perhaps the struggle unmakes the mountains entirely. The stones themselves have told me both endings. They remember the future the way they remember names. Poorly, but often enough."

* * *

And then he offered her the chair again, and this time it was not theater.

"Sit, Yara. Hear the rest before you decide to be noble. Nobility is cheap when you haven't seen the price list."

She sat. Not because he asked — because her knees had quietly decided the matter, and she was too much her grandmother's granddaughter to pretend otherwise.

"At my right hand," Vaskal said, "there is a place that has stood empty since the beginning. My herald held it for a century and grew bored of it. The priest you travel with held it for one mortal lifetime and threw it away for the sake of one village of strangers." Something moved behind his eyes at the mention of Cael, old and complicated — hatred wearing the mask of regret, or regret wearing the mask of hatred; after a hundred years beneath the mountain she supposed the two wore each other's clothes. "The seat is yours. Not as a slave — as a partner. When I devour the waking gods, their worship comes to *me*, and through me, and I will need administrators. Wardens. A covenant, if you like — I know how fond you've become of the word. The free cities stand. The passes stay open. Your guild, restored — not three members in a rotting hall, but the Lockridge Guild as it was in the founding years, arbiter of every road from the Salt Coast to the Icewall, with your grandmother's debts paid and her name honored and her mistake forgiven, because her mistake will have *built the peace*."

He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the black iron of the crown, and for one horrible moment she saw what he must show everyone eventually: not a monster. A tired king who genuinely believed he was the only adult left standing.

"Think of what you've lost," he said gently. "A grandmother who lied to you your whole life. A guild of three, drowning in bad paper. A priest who follows you because following you is his penance, not because you matter to him. Think of what I'm offering. Everyone lives. Everything you loved is restored. The mountains keep their shape, the cities keep their walls, the roads keep their snow-free passes. The only cost is—" he gestured, elegantly, at the thousands of burning lamps, "—the same cost your grandmother already paid on your behalf. You would simply be agreeing to terms that were signed before you could walk. Survival, Yara. Offered at the price of everyone else. It is the oldest bargain there is, and I have never once found a buyer who could afford to refuse it."

The hall was very quiet. Down in the deep places, the sleeping things breathed.

And Yara sat in the usurper's chair-side seat, in the inverted guildhall, and was ashamed to discover that the temptation was real.

Not the power. She had never wanted power; power was just another kind of cargo, heavy and liable to shift. Not even the restoration — though grief for Marette sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, and the promise of a guildhall full of living voices instead of two coughs and a silence was a sweet, sweet poison. What tempted her was simpler and filthier: *everyone lives.* The villages on the lowland roads. The free cities. Brenn, up top with his bad hip and his crossbow, keeping watch against an army he couldn't stop. Cael, who had confessed to her in the dark of the Hollow Chapel with a voice like a man reading his own death warrant, and who believed — she had seen it in his face, plain as a brand — that whatever came for him next, he deserved.

If she said yes, Cael lived. If she said yes, the armies marching on the border towns turned home. If she said yes, the world kept its rough, unfair, familiar shape, the only difference being that somewhere beneath it a handful of ancient hungry things stayed asleep forever, and one woman's soul burned in a lantern instead of beating in her chest.

Was that so terrible? Her grandmother hadn't thought so. Her grandmother had stood in this hall, starving, four years old Yara dreaming in her mother's belly, and done the arithmetic, and the arithmetic had said *one soul against a generation of open roads,* and Marette Lockridge had never in her life refused a bargain the numbers favored.

"Your grandmother hesitated too," Vaskal murmured, watching her face the way a card-player watches a mark. "Right about there. Right at the moment when she understood that refusing would cost more than accepting. Do you know what she said to me?" He smiled, and it was almost kind, and that was the worst thing in the whole buried hall. "She said, *Tell me she'll never know.*"

The coals ticked. A lamp guttered somewhere in the far niches, and a warden drifted to it and did not relight it.

"She was wrong about that," Yara said at last. Her voice came out low and steady, and she was grateful to it, because the rest of her was shaking. "About a lot of things, but especially that."

"Ah," said Vaskal. "So you *do* know. The priest told you after all."

"No. The ledger told me. Page one, in her own hand, dated the year of the famine: *Item received, one soul, condition unborn. Item owed: everything.*" Yara stood, slowly, pushing the chair back, and was distantly pleased that her legs held. "She knew I'd read it someday. That's why she wrote it down. Marette Lockridge never hid a debt in her life — she just deferred payment. So here's my counter-offer, and listen close, because I'm only making it once."

Vaskal's eyebrows rose, and around the hall the lamps leaned in.

"You keep the keystone. For now. I'm not cracking my own soul out of your vault tonight — I've seen what's fused to it, and even I don't gamble cargo that size on a first run." *Let him chew on the fact that she'd been in the vault,* she thought savagely; let him spend a sleepless hour wondering how. "But I'm not giving it either. Freely, unwillingly, any way at all. Because I've done your arithmetic, Vaskal, son-of-nothing, usurper, and your numbers are wrong. You count the waking gods as a cost. You count the free cities as assets. You count *me* as a key. But you never ran the books on what happens when the things you're counting start counting *back*." She was walking now, backward, toward the doors, and the wardens did not move to stop her, and that frightened her more than chains would have. "The old gods are waking whether I burn or shine. Your seal is failing whether I cooperate or not. So the smart money isn't on the king who ate a heaven's worth of souls to buy himself a throne. The smart money's on whatever's strong enough to wake up *underneath* him."

At the doors of mountain-glass, she stopped, and gave him the only thing she had left to give: the truth, delivered like a blade.

"My grandmother regretted this bargain every day of her life. She died coughing blood with my name on her lips, and the last thing she tried to tell me was how to undo it. So no, Vaskal. I won't sit at your right hand. But I'll make you a promise, free of charge, the way the best threats always are: when this hall comes down — and it's coming down — I'm going to be standing in the doorway when it does. Counting the lamps as they go out. Starting with yours."

For a long moment the usurper-god of the Undercourt said nothing. Then he laughed — a short, genuine bark of a laugh, the worst sound yet — and settled back into Marette's inverted throne, and the lamps throughout the great hall dimmed all together, like a held breath.

"Take her back to the cells," he said to the wardens, conversationally. "The soft one. Put her near the priest — they'll want to talk, and talking is how keys turn." His eyes found hers one last time across the dying light. "We'll resume negotiations when you've had time to reconsider, Yara Lockridge. You will reconsider. They always do. And when you do, ask your defrocked friend what he did the *first* time someone offered him a kingdom for a soul. Ask him why the gods' prisons hold as well as they do. Ask him what he built for me."

The doors closed on the sound of his laughter, and the corridor of guttering lamps swallowed her whole, and Yara walked back through the dark with Vaskal's words lodged under her ribs like a smuggled knife:

*Ask him what he built for me.*

She had thought Cael's confession in the chapel was the bottom of him. She had thought she'd already forgiven the worst.

She had been running contraband her whole life, and she knew, with a sick professional certainty, that the most dangerous cargo is always the one declared honestly — and that somewhere in the cells ahead, the man she had started, against every rule she owned, to love was sitting on a secret heavier than any she had ever carried down a mountain.
