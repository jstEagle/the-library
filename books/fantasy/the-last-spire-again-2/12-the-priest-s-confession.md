## 12. The Priest's Confession

The cell they gave Cael was not cruel, which frightened him more than cruelty would have.

It had a bed—a real one, with a mattress stuffed with something that smelled of cedar and cold iron—and a basin of water that never clouded no matter how long he left his hands in it. There was a candle that did not gutter and a window that did not open, though through it he could see a sky that belonged to no hour of any day: a deep violet dusk frozen mid-fade, the stars pinned in place like flies to a board. He knew that sky. He had prayed under it for eleven years of his life, back when prayer still meant something other than apology.

They had taken his boots, his knife, the little iron charm Yara's grandmother had pressed into his palm at the guildhall door. They had not taken his voice, which meant Vaskal wanted him able to speak. That, too, was an old pattern. Cael remembered it from the first time—the interrogations conducted like conversations between old friends, the way the court made its violence feel like courtesy.

He sat on the bed with his back against stone that was warm as skin, and he waited for her.

She came at the end of what he judged to be a day, because time down here moved only when someone needed it to. He heard her before he saw her—no guards announced her; there were no guards, or none that wore shapes a man could name—but there was a particular sound Yara Lockridge made when she walked, a rhythm like water finding its way downhill, patient and certain and impossible to dam. The door opened without hinges. She stood in it with her arms crossed and her jaw set, and behind her eyes was the look of someone who had spent the hours since their capture doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"They gave you a *bed*," she said.

"They gave me a bed," he agreed.

"And a candle."

"And a candle."

"In the prison of a god who wants to eat every soul in the mountains." She stepped inside. The door closed behind her without being touched. "Cael. What exactly were you to him?"

And there it was. The question he had been circling since the hollow chapel, since the rootways, since the river that answered questions had asked him, in its voice of wet stone, *why do you come home?* He had lied around it, knelt beside it, let Yara fill the silence with her own conclusions—that he was a defrocked priest, a failure, a man whose god had cast him out for one weak moment of mercy. A small sin. A survivable story. He had let her believe it because the truth was a weight he had carried alone for thirty years and had convinced himself he deserved to carry alone forever.

But they were both of them prisoners now, and her soul was a lamp in a vault below them, fused to thousands of others, and if she was going to trust him with the next part—if there was going to be a next part—she would have to know what she was trusting.

"Sit down," he said.

"I'll stand."

"You'll want to sit." His voice came out quieter than he intended. "Please, Yara."

Something in the *please* reached her. She crossed the cell—not to the bed but to the wall beneath the impossible window, where she lowered herself onto the floor with her knees drawn up, smuggler-fashion, ready to move. Ready, always, to run. He envied her that. He had never once managed to run when it mattered.

"My name wasn't always Cael," he began. "That's true, at least. It was Caelyth. Third voice of the Vespers. Do you know what the Vespers were?"

"Evening prayers."

"The *evening* prayers." He almost smiled. "That's what the free cities remember, if they remember anything. Songs at sundown, candles in windows. But the Vespers weren't songs, Yara. They were instructions. The old gods sleep in the bones of the mountains—they've slept since before men learned to count—and their dreams leak up through the stone as weather, as luck, as the small mercies your villages pray into. The Vespers were how we kept the dreaming gentle. Twelve voices, sung in the old tongue, tuned to twelve sleeping hearts. When we sang well, the gods dreamed kindly. Harvests came. Rivers stayed in their banks. Children were born whole."

"And when you sang badly?"

"We didn't sing badly. We sang *away*." He looked at his hands. They were a workman's hands now, scarred from thirty years of roads and rope and honest labor, and he could hardly bear them. "There were thirteen of us once. The thirteenth voice was not for the sleeping gods. It was for the thing that watches them sleep. Vaskal was not always the usurper, Yara. He was the warden. The keeper of the vault. The one who tended the gods' bodies while they dreamed so that nothing else could feed on them. And somewhere across the long centuries, tending became keeping, and keeping became owning, and the warden decided that a treasure this great should not merely be guarded."

"He wanted to eat them."

"He wanted to *become* them." Cael heard his own voice go flat, the flatness of a recitation learned by rote in a place like this one. "A sleeping god is the greatest store of power in creation. Devour one and you hold its dominion. Devour all twelve and you are not a usurper anymore—you are simply the mountain entire, and everything that lives on it lives by your sufferance. But the gods could not be eaten while the Vespers held, because our song kept their souls knit to their flesh. So long as we sang, the vault was sealed against him from the inside. He could not touch them. He could not even wake them. He needed the song broken first."

Yara was very still. In the frozen light of the impossible window, her face might have been carved. "And you broke it."

"I *wrote* the breaking." The words came out of him like splinters, each one leaving a piece behind. "I was his. Not his servant—his. I was raised in the Undercourt, Yara. I never saw the sun until I was nine years old. My mother was a Vesper before me, third voice of her generation, and when my voice came in they found I could hold two tones at once, and Vaskal took personal charge of my training. He called me his keystone. He said—" and here Cael's voice failed him for the first time, and he had to begin again, lower, "he said I was the finest instrument he had ever built. I thought it was love. I was a child. He made sure I thought it was love."

The candle flame bent, though there was no draft. Below them, far below, something vast shifted in its sleep—or in its waking—and the stone under Cael's hands trembled once, gently, like the flank of a breathing animal.

"For years I helped him erode the song. Nothing obvious. A rest where a note belonged. A word mispronounced just enough to loosen its root-meaning. One by one, the twelve voices went sour, and the other singers blamed themselves, and I comforted them, and all the while I was the rot in the wood. Eleven years I did this. And then—" He stopped. He made himself go on. "Then came the night of the last sealing. Vaskal had finally unmade the twelfth verse, and the oldest of the gods, the one the lowlanders call the Mother-under-the-Snows, began to stir. Not wake. Stir. Like a sleeper rolling away from a hand. And Vaskal brought me to the vault itself, put the seal-chains in my hands, and told me to bind her while she half-dreamed. To sing her back under. And I stood in front of her, and I looked into the dream behind her eyes—"

"What did you see?"

"Homes," Cael said simply. "I saw homes. I saw every village in the mountains, every hearth that had ever been warmed by her dreaming, every prayer ever whispered into the snow. She wasn't a power, Yara. She was a grandmother. She was Marette. And I understood in that moment that everything I had been told about wardenship and order and necessary sorrow was a lie told by a thief standing over a sleeping family with a knife. And I dropped the chains, and I ran."

Yara exhaled slowly. "You said mercy got you exiled."

"I said what was safe to say." He met her eyes and did not look away, though everything in him wanted to. "Here is the truth, and I will give it to you plain because you have earned it and because I am done curating my sins. I did not merely fail to bind her. As I ran, I passed the Vesper hall, and I sang the counter-song—the unbinding, the verse that exists to undo the other twelve. I sang it alone, badly, with a boy's cracked voice. It should not have worked. By every law of the art, it should not have worked. But the Mother heard it, and she turned her face fully toward waking for the first time in ten thousand years, and Vaskal has spent every year since trying to push her back under. That is why the vault must stay sealed. That is why he needs a keystone. Your soul, Yara. The last thing ever bound with the original seal-work, made by the last hand he trusted. Mine."

The silence that followed was long enough to have weather. Cael sat in it and felt strangely lighter and more empty at once, the way a room feels after furniture has been carried out.

"So when they cast you out," Yara said at last, and her voice was careful in a way he distrusted, "when you told me in the chapel that your god threw you into the dark for one act of mercy—"

"That was a coward's version. Yes."

"And the truth is you escaped. You stole out of the Undercourt with the knowledge of how to find the gates, and you have spent thirty years walking the passes looking for a way to finish what you started, and you never once told me because you believed—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Because you believed what?"

"That I deserve whatever comes." It was easier to say than he had feared. He had rehearsed it so many times in thirty years of lonely inns and lonely roads that it had worn smooth as river stone. "Vaskal built me to break a song that held the world together, and I broke it, and the mending will cost the mountains either their gods or their foundations, and either way the bill comes due on work I did with these hands. Whatever he does to me in this court, Yara, it is not punishment. It is arithmetic. I have made my peace with—"

"*Stop.*"

She was on her feet. He hadn't seen her move—one moment kneeling, the next standing over him with her fists clenched and her eyes bright with something that was fury wearing grief's clothing, or grief wearing fury's, he couldn't tell and didn't dare guess.

"Don't you dare," she said. "Don't you *dare* stand in front of me and do what my grandmother did."

"Yara—"

"She did it too. Sat me down with her ledger full of debts and told me suffering was just sums, that if the price was paid then the crying was finished, that guilt you carry quietly is somehow nobler than guilt you spend trying to fix things. And look where her arithmetic landed us. Look where *we* are." Her voice cracked and she pushed through the crack without slowing, the way she pushed through drifts on the high passes, head down, relentless. "You were a *child*, Cael. A child raised in a cellar by a thing that ate the concept of love and wore its shape. Everything you did before the night you ran was done with a chain around your neck, and the night you ran—the one thing you did free, the one choice that was wholly yours—you used it to *sing the gods awake*. Thirty years you've punished yourself for your chains and refused yourself credit for your freedom, and I am so tired—do you hear me—I am so *tired* of watching people I love kneel at altars of their own guilt like it's some kind of holiness—"

She stopped. The word hung between them. *Love.* She had thrown it out into the frozen air of the cell like a coin tossed to a beggar, carelessly, and then heard herself do it, and gone still.

Cael rose slowly. He was taller than she was, but he felt smaller; he felt like a man standing in a cathedral he had forgotten how to enter.

"You shouldn't forgive me," he said softly. "You don't know enough yet to forgive me. There's more. There's always more with me. The herald who came to Marette's door the famine winter—I trained him. The seal-formula that bound your soul—he wrote it in my hand, from memory, because I was the only living creature who still knew it. Every ruin in your life that leads back to the Undercourt leads back to me twice over. If you want to hate me, I will stand here and hold still for it. But don't forgive me out of pity, Yara. I couldn't survive that. I've survived everything else."

For a long moment she just looked at him, and the frozen starlight caught the wet on her cheeks, though whether she knew she was crying he doubted.

"Pity," she repeated. "You think this is pity." She stepped closer. Close enough that he could see the salt-cracked skin on her knuckles, the smuggler's calluses, the small scar on her chin from a fall she'd taken at fourteen that she'd never told anyone about but had told him, once, on a cold ledge above the tree line, because he listened the way starving people eat. "My grandmother sold my soul to save three people and a road, and everyone in that guildhall has spent twenty years calling it love. You helped a tyrant break the world and then spent thirty years carrying the mending on your back through every pass in the Thornfells, and you're calling it debt. Well, I'm a ledger-keeper's granddaughter, Cael, and I know bad bookkeeping when I see it. You've been entering the same payment on every page. It doesn't balance. It never balanced. And I am telling you—" her voice dropped, fierce and low, "—as the last member of the Lockridge Guild, authorized to settle accounts: the debt is *paid*. Paid past owed. Paid beyond reason. Whatever you owe, you've been paying it since before I was born, and I am sick of watching good people drown themselves in a well that ran dry decades ago."

"Yara."

"And if you argue with me," she said, "I will remind you that I am the one holding the lockpicks, and you are the one in the cell."

Despite everything—despite the vault below them and the waking mountain and the frozen sky and the arithmetic of endings—a laugh broke out of Cael, sudden and helpless, the first in longer than he could measure. It hurt on the way out, the way laughter does when it has rusted. Yara glared at him, and then the corner of her mouth betrayed her, and for one breath the cell was just a room and they were just two tired people in it, laughing at the edge of the end of the world.

Then her face changed.

"Cael," she said quietly. "The guards. Or whatever they are. When they brought me here, the one who locked my door leaned close and said a word to me. Just one word. I thought it was nonsense." She swallowed. "It was in the old tongue. It was *keystone*. And it was smiling when it said it."

Below them, deeper than the vault, deeper than the roots of the world, the Mother-under-the-Snows turned over in her long dream—and the whole mountain shuddered, and the candle went out, and in the dark Cael heard, unmistakable, the sound of Vaskal beginning to sing.
