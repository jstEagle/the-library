## 13. The Vault of Lamps

The lock on the vault door was a work of art, and Yara hated it on sight.

She crouched before it in the cold corridor beneath the Court, Cael's stolen cloak around her shoulders, and studied the thing the way she'd once studied the customs-locks of Dunmarrow: not as a barrier, but as a sentence someone had written, waiting to be read. The mechanism was grown, not made — pale bone-white metal that might have been silver or might have been something's actual skeleton, braided into a knot the size of her two fists. Seven tendrils curled inward toward a center that pulsed faintly, slowly, like a heart deciding whether to bother.

"It knows what you are," Cael murmured behind her. "It will taste your soul through your fingertips."

"Then it'll find a lot of smuggling and a little prayer," she said, "and be very confused." She flexed her fingers against the chill. "How long do we have?"

"The court sleeps shallow after an audience. An hour, perhaps two, before the heralds make their rounds." He glanced down the corridor, where the walls breathed a slow amber light, like the inside of some enormous throat. "Yara. If we're caught here—"

"We won't be caught here." She said it the way Marette used to say *the roads hold* — flat, certain, a lie you told until it became true. "Because I've spent my whole life taking things from places that didn't want me to have them, and this is just one more warehouse with worse lighting."

He almost smiled. It was the first time in three days he'd come close to anything resembling a smile, and she found she was keeping count without meaning to. She turned back to the lock before the counting could mean too much.

The tendrils were the problem. Touch any one of them wrong and the whole braid would wake screaming — she'd seen a thief's hand in the low markets once, years ago, locked in a guild-trap until the flesh went grey. But locks were locks. Locks wanted to be opened; that was the dirty secret no locksmith ever admitted. A lock was just a door that had been taught to be shy.

"Talk to me," she said quietly, drawing her picks from the lining of her boot. Three slivers of black iron she'd traded a week's rations for in the market of mirrors, where the vendor had looked at her hands and laughed. "Not warnings. Talk. Anything."

Cael was silent a moment. Then, because he was learning her, he began to talk about the chapel where he'd been ordained — the smell of tallow and wet stone, the way the dawn service sounded when forty voices hit the third verse of the Litany of Waking at once. His voice was low and even, and it did what she needed it to do: gave her hands somewhere quiet to live while they worked.

First pick into the leftmost tendril. The metal was warm. That was the worst part, how warm it was, like touching a sleeping animal's flank. She eased it aside by degrees, feeling for the counterweight within, the little dishonest balance every lock carried somewhere in its heart.

"Marette kept a ledger," she said, mostly to herself. "Everything written down. Every debt, every favor, every jar of pickled eel owed to Brenn since the famine winter." The second tendril yielded. Sweat ran cold along her spine. "I used to think it was meanness. Now I think it was the only way she knew how to love things. Write them down so they can't be lost."

Third tendril. Fourth. The braid shivered, and somewhere far above them, through leagues of stone, the mountain sighed in its sleep.

Fifth. Sixth.

The seventh tendril did not move. It sat coiled at the center, and when she probed it, gently, with everything she had, it probed back — a pressure against her mind like a finger pressed to the inside of her skull, tasting. *What are you,* it seemed to ask. *Whose are you?*

And that was the trap, wasn't it. Because there was only one true answer, the answer she'd learned three days ago in her grandmother's kitchen, and if she gave it, the lock would know exactly which lamp in the vault was hers, and seal itself around it forever.

"No," Yara whispered, and thought of mislabeled cargo, of salt-barrels stamped FLOUR, of the thousand small lies that had kept three people alive in a dying trade. "Wrong ledger. Try again."

She fed it a different truth instead. *I am Yara Lockridge, of the Lockridge Guild, and I am owed.* Debts were real. Debts had weight. The Undercourt ran on debts — Vaskal had said so himself, smiling, in his hall of sleeping god-flesh — and so the seventh tendril hesitated, tasting the shape of an account it half-recognized, a claim filed in the proper form.

The lock opened like a flower that had never wanted to bloom.

The door swung inward without a sound, and cold air rolled out over them — not the cold of stone, but a colder, cleaner cold, the cold of a place where nothing was allowed to burn.

* * *

The vault was a cavern, and the cavern was full of lamps.

Yara stopped on the threshold and forgot, for a long moment, how to breathe.

They hung from the ceiling by chains that grew out of the rock like roots, thousands upon thousands of them, receding into dark beyond the reach of sight. Each lamp was a glass vessel no bigger than a fist, and each held a flame — but *flame* was the wrong word. They burned without consuming, without heat, without flicker. Steady. Patient. Some burned white, some gold, some the deep bruised blue of a winter dusk, and the whole vast space of the vault glowed with them like a sky seen from underwater.

"Souls," Cael said hoarsely, beside her. He had his fist pressed to his mouth. "This is where he keeps—"

"I can see what it is." Her voice came out strange and thin.

She walked between the hanging lamps. The aisle was narrow, and the lamps swung faintly as she passed, though there was no draft, turning to follow her like flowers following a sun that had betrayed them. She did not look closely at any single one. She couldn't yet. Looking closely meant seeing faces in the flames, and she wasn't ready to see faces.

"How many?" she asked, though she already knew the number would be unbearable.

"More than the living remember," Cael said quietly. "Some of these are older than Vaskal's reign. Souls pawned across generations, the way your grandmother pawned yours. Families who sold a daughter's soul for a good harvest and told themselves the gods were dead anyway, so what harm." His voice cracked on the last words. "I told myself the same things. I stood in his court and recorded these entries. I wrote the labels."

That stopped her. "Labels?"

He touched one lamp — carefully, with two fingers, the gesture of a man touching a wound he'd inflicted. A small tag hung from its chain, bone-white like the lock, marked with characters that shifted when she tried to read them. "Name. Debt. Terms. Every lamp is a line in a ledger." He looked at her, and his face in the lamplight was terrible and young. "Your grandmother's ledger has a twin, Yara. It's this room."

*Mislabels,* she thought suddenly, the smuggler's instinct rising sharp through the horror. In thirty years of running the Thornfells, the Lockridge Guild had survived not on speed or luck but on paperwork — on knowing which inspector checked seals and which checked weights, on barrels marked to attract exactly the right kind of careless glance. A vault was a warehouse. A warehouse had a system. And systems could be lied to.

But first she had to find her own lamp, and she had no idea how, and the longer she stood in this cold the more certain she grew that she did not want to find it at all.

* * *

She found it by accident, the way you find anything true.

She had worked inward for what felt like hours, reading tags by touch — most were in the shifting script, but some, older ones, were scratched in plain characters, names she almost recognized, names from songs her grandmother had sung half-under her breath while kneading bread. And then the aisle opened into a wider chamber at the heart of the vault, a round space like the hub of a wheel, and Yara stopped walking because the light changed.

It was brighter here. Warmer. And all the lamps in the inner ring leaned, subtly, toward a single point at the chamber's center — a knot of chains converging, and among them, one lamp that burned differently from all the rest.

It was small and bright and stubbornly golden, and it did not hang still. It strained. Against its chain, against the glass, against the dark — a flame leaning hard toward the doorway where Yara stood, the way a dog leans toward the sound of a familiar step.

"No," she said aloud. Not a word, really. Just air shaped like refusal.

She crossed to it anyway. Of course she did. Her feet decided before her fear finished arguing, and then her hands were around the glass — warm, finally, warm in all this cold — and the flame inside flared up against the surface like a palm pressed to a window, and she felt it. She felt *her*. Not saw. Felt. A lifetime of mornings on cold passes. The specific ache of her own laugh. Marette's kitchen. The taste of snowmelt. All of it burning behind the glass, trying to get home.

"That's you," Cael said softly, at her shoulder. He sounded like a man at a graveside.

"It's me." She turned the lamp over with shaking hands. The tag on its chain was newer than the others, the script still legible: *LOCKRIDGE, Y. — HELD AS SURETY — TERMS PER CONTRACT, FAMINE WINTER — DO NOT RELEASE.*

"Do not release," she repeated. Her breath fogged the glass, and the flame inside bent toward the fog like a child chasing breath on a winter pane. "Well. Grandmother always said the guild's ledgers were the honest ones."

Then she looked past her own lamp, and understood, and wished she could un-understand.

Her lamp's chain did not end at the ceiling. It ran downward, into the floor, into the rock — and it was not one chain. It was woven. Threaded through it, fused to it at a dozen points, ran hundreds of other chains, and those hundreds joined thousands, the whole inner ring of the vault knotted together around hers like threads around a spindle's eye. Where her lamp burned brightest, the nearest lamps burned a little brighter too. Where the flame in her glass guttered — and it guttered now, as her hope did — a hundred small lights dimmed in sympathy, a tide going out across the dark.

"Yara." Cael's hand closed over her wrist. Gentle. Warning. "Look at the weave."

"I'm looking."

"Then you see it."

"I see it." Her voice was very flat, the voice she used on the passes when the weather turned and lying to yourself became a survival skill. "My lamp isn't a lamp. It's a keystone. Pull one thread and—" She made herself follow the fusion with her eyes, chain to chain to chain, outward and outward into the dark, thousands of souls bound through hers like rivers bound through their source. "Pull one thread and the whole weave comes apart. Or doesn't. Or burns."

"There may be a way to cut yours free alone," Cael said, and she heard the priest in him saying it, the old litany-voice, the voice trained to deliver comfort that the comforter didn't believe. "Vaskal's smiths—"

"His smiths built it this way on purpose." She was thinking now, coldly, clearly, the way she hadn't let herself think since Marette died. "Think about it. Why fuse them? Why not keep my lamp in a box by itself?" She answered herself, because the answer was already sitting in her chest like swallowed ice. "Because it makes me useless. Because anyone who comes for their soul finds ten thousand others tangled in theirs. Because freeing mine frees them all — and freeing them all wakes the old gods, and waking the old gods unmakes the mountains, and *that's the terms per contract.*" She laughed, one short ugly note. "He didn't just take my soul, Cael. He took it and made it the pin holding everyone else's in place. My grandmother sold me, and he turned the sale into a cage for the world."

The vault was silent around them, ten thousand patient flames, ten thousand souls that had waited decades in the dark for someone to come, and now someone had come, and she was standing there doing arithmetic.

Free one, save one. Free all, risk everything. Walk away, save yourself, and leave ten thousand lamps burning in a room under a usurper's throne.

Marette's arithmetic had been the same shape. One girl's soul against a guild's survival. The famine winter against the generation after. Terrible sums, done in terrible ink, and Yara had spent three days hating her grandmother for them, and now here she stood with the same pen in her hand.

"I can't choose," she heard herself say. It was the most honest thing she'd said in the Undercourt. "Do you understand? Whatever I pick, I pick for all of them. That's not a choice, that's — that's being the contract." Her hands were still around her lamp's warm glass, and she could not make them let go. "He knew. When he offered me a place at his right hand, he knew I'd end up here, staring at this, understanding that refusing him means gambling ten thousand lives on whether the weave breaks clean or burns. Survival isn't the price, Cael. Survival is the *currency*, and he's been spending mine since before I could walk."

Cael said nothing for a long moment. Then he stepped closer, into the light of her lamp, and she saw that he was crying — silently, without drama, tears simply present on his face as if they had always lived there.

"When I served him," he said, "I told myself the sealing of the old gods was mercy. Sleep instead of slaughter. Order instead of hunger. I wrote the liturgy of the sealing with my own hand." He reached out and laid his palm flat against her lamp, beside hers, and the flame leaned toward his hand too, and something in his expression broke open. "It's warm. Yara — it knows me. Even sealed, even labeled, it knows the man who helped put it here."

"Don't," she said. "Don't do that. Don't stand there cataloguing your sins while mine hasn't even happened yet."

"Someone has to say it, since you won't. What you're holding — what you came here to steal back — it isn't just yours anymore, and it never was. You've known since the gate." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, brutally practical. "So don't decide tonight. Deciding tonight is what he wants — a rushed choice is a choice made for you. Cut us a road out of this room, smuggler. Take the lamp's measure. Learn the weave. And when the moment comes — and it will come, it's coming faster than either of us wants — you'll know which sum is yours to do."

She stared at him. Ten thousand lamps hung around them, and her own burned between their hands like a small furious sun, and somewhere above them the mountain slept with gods in its bones.

"You're asking me to steal my own soul slowly," she said.

"I'm asking you to case the job," he said, and this time he did smile, tired and crooked, and she discovered she would burn a great many things to keep that smile aimed at her. "Like your grandmother would have."

"My grandmother," Yara said, "would have found a way to mislabel the whole vault."

And that was when the light changed.

Every lamp in the inner ring guttered at once — a single indrawn breath across ten thousand flames — and far away, back up the corridor they'd come by, iron rang against stone. Not the soft chime of the heralds' rounds. Something heavier. Something urgent. Voices followed, echoing down the throat of the passage, and one of them rose above the rest, cracked and ragged and unmistakable, a voice that had no business being alive in this place, shouting a word that was a name that was hers:

*"YARA!"*

Cael's head snapped toward the sound. "That's—"

"Brenn." Her blood went cold, then hot. "That's impossible. That's *impossible*, he was left at the hall, he was—"

The shouting cut off. And in the silence after it, from somewhere above, the whole mountain seemed to lean — a vast, slow shift of weight, like a sleeper rolling over — and every lamp in the Vault of Lamps flared bright as day.
