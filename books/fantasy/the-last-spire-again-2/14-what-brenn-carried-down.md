## 14. What Brenn Carried Down

Brenn had carried many things down the Thornfell passes in his sixty-one years. Salt and steel. Letters sealed in wax that could hang a man. A newborn once, wrapped in a smuggler's blanket while her mother bled out two switchbacks above — he'd never learned what became of the child, and some nights he still wondered. But he had never carried anything like this: a ledger bound in cracked green leather, heavier than any book had a right to be, and the knowledge folded inside it like a knife wrapped in cloth.

The mountain did not want him going down.

He felt it in his knees first, then his hips, then the long bones of his legs, as if the stone itself were pressing back against him with every step. The rootways — Cael's word for them; Brenn would have called them the deep roads, or nothing at all, because a sensible man didn't name the inside of a mountain — narrowed and widened without sense, breathing almost, expanding and contracting like the throat of something vast and slow. Twice the passage ahead had simply been shut, sealed by roots thick as oxen, and twice the ledger had grown warm against his chest and the roots had drawn apart like curtains, grudgingly, the way an old man moves aside for someone he owes money to.

"You knew her," Brenn told the dark, because talking kept him upright. "Marette Lockridge. You took her soul and you let her live forty years on your credit, so don't pretend you don't know the weight of this book."

The mountain said nothing. The mountain had been saying nothing since before Brenn was born, and he respected it for its consistency.

He'd left the guildhall nine days ago. He remembered the leaving clearly, which surprised him, because everything since had gone soft at the edges like ink left in the rain. He remembered the snow coming down sideways off the high shoulder of Thornfell, the kind of snow that erased roads rather than covered them. He remembered Yara standing in the courtyard with her jaw set in that particular way she had — Marette's way, the whole family's way, the look of a Lockridge about to do arithmetic that would cost somebody everything. She'd tried to order him to stay.

"Somebody has to hold the hall," she'd said.

"The hall is four walls and a leaking roof," Brenn had answered. "The hall is wherever the ledger is." And he'd taken the ledger out of the strongbox and strapped it under his coat, over his heart, where a man carries the things he cannot afford to lose, and Marette's last secret with it, written in her own cramped hand on the final page in ink she'd mixed herself from ash and tallow so that no scrying could find it.

Yara had looked at him then the way you look at a door closing.

"Brenn."

"Girl," he'd said — though she wasn't a girl anymore, hadn't been for years, but grief makes children of everyone around it — "your grandmother sold her blood and yours to keep these roads open. I've got no soul worth selling and no blood anybody wants. What I've got is legs that have walked every pass in these mountains since before you were whelped, and I've got the one thing Vaskal's armies have marched ten years and never found." He'd patted the ledger through his coat. "I'm not letting the Undercourt read it secondhand."

She'd hugged him. That was the part that came back sharpest now, sharper even than the pain in his side. She'd hugged him quick and hard, the way you hug someone when you're both pretending it isn't goodbye, and she'd smelled of woodsmoke and road dust and the particular oil she used on her lockpicks, and she'd said, "Come back," into his shoulder, and he'd lied to her, easily, the way he'd lied to her mother before her, and said, "Always do."

Nine days. The lie had lasted six.

* * *

It happened at the river that answers questions.

Brenn hadn't meant to stop there — the water spoke in a voice like wind through a keyhole, and it asked things, and he was in no mood to be asked things by a river — but the passage forked three ways and none of the forks matched anything Cael had sketched for him on the back of a supply manifest, and pride was a luxury he'd stopped affording around day four, when the wound opened properly.

So he'd knelt at the bank, black water sliding past without a single ripple, and the river had said, in a voice that seemed to come up through his boot-soles rather than his ears:

*What do you carry?*

"A book," Brenn said.

*Whose book?*

"Marette Lockridge's. Guildmistress of the Lockridge Guild. Last of the name, near enough."

The water went still. Not calm — still, the way a held breath is still. And then, softer, almost tender, in the voice of someone reading an old letter:

*She owed us a song. She promised it at the famine winter, and she never sang.*

"She sang every night," Brenn said. "Badly. Off-key as a cartwheel. Forty years, every night at the hearth, whether anyone wanted to hear it or not."

*That,* said the river, *was not our song.* And then, after a long moment in which the black water seemed to consider him: *You are bleeding, old keeper.*

"I'm aware."

*The third passage. Left of the standing stone. Do not drink. Do not sleep more than a breath at a time. The mountain dreams, and dreamers are pulled into the dream, and the dream does not know how to give things back yet.*

"How do you know all this?"

*I am the mountain remembering itself,* the river said, and resumed its slide into the dark, and that was the longest conversation Brenn had had in nine days, and he'd had it with water, and the state of his life was such that he found it comforting.

He took the left passage. The wound in his side — a spear-point, low and deep, from one of Vaskal's outriders who'd caught him on the last stretch of open ground above the descent chapel — opened again anyway. It opened the way wounds do when a body decides it has done enough and begins, quietly, to file its complaints. He packed it with moss and the last of the yarrow salve and bound it tight with a strip cut from his cloak, and he kept walking, one hand pressed flat against the ledger beneath his coat, and he talked to Marette the whole way, because dead women make better company than silence.

"You always said the guild would outlive us," he muttered, ducking under a root that hung across the passage like a lintel. "Forty years I heard it. 'The Lockridges endure, Brenn. We're like the mountain.' Well, love, the mountain's waking up, and I'm not sure either of us endures much longer, so I hope you're satisfied."

The ledger warmed against his chest. It did that sometimes. He'd stopped flinching from it around day five. It was not warmth exactly — it was more like being *acknowledged*, the sensation of a hand resting briefly between your shoulder blades. Marette had mixed her ink from ash and tallow, and she'd steeped the pages in things Brenn preferred not to think about, and somewhere in the binding there was enough of her that the book knew its bearer the way a hound knows its household.

"Hold together," he told it. "Both of us."

* * *

On the eighth day he slept too long.

It was only meant to be a breath — the river's instruction, a breath at a time, no more — but his body had stopped taking instructions around the same time it had stopped taking suggestions, and he woke to find the passage walls moving. Not shifting, not settling. *Moving*, slow and liquid, the veins of stone pulsing faintly gold, and the air was full of a sound like a snore the size of weather, and the floor beneath him rose and fell in a rhythm far too large and far too patient to belong to anything with a name.

A dream. He was inside the dream.

And the dream noticed him.

He couldn't say how he knew. There were no eyes. But the great rhythm stuttered, caught, and the pressure in the air changed the way a room changes when someone wakes in it, and far off in the dark — miles off, maybe farther, distance being a rumor down here — something turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, with the terrible curiosity of the half-conscious, to pay attention.

Brenn did the only thing a Lockridge man would have done. He opened the ledger.

Not to read it. His eyes weren't good enough for that anymore and the light was worse. He opened it and laid his palm flat on the page with Marette's secret on it, the true road, the road nobody sells, and he said aloud, in the voice he'd used for thirty years to talk down panicked clients and drunken toll-wardens and, once, an avalanche:

"No. Not yet. This one's mine to deliver."

The mountain breathed. The gold veins pulsed. Somewhere in the vast drowsing dark, attention weighed him — measured him the way a merchant measures goods, thumb on the scale, unhurried — and then, mercifully, withdrew, folding back into sleep like a wave pulling back from shore.

But it left something behind. In the black of the passage, hovering at the edge of his failing sight, a shape: small, bright, steady. Like a lamp. Like a soul seen from the outside, if souls could stand watch.

It stayed with him after that. He named it Marette, because who else would it be, and it never answered, and it never left, and between the lamp and the ledger and pure Lockridge stubbornness, Brenn came down the last miles of the rootways the way a bad coin comes down a mountainside — tumbling, ringing, refusing on principle to stop.

* * *

The gates of the Undercourt were taller than cathedrals and made of a darkness that had texture, that had grain, that looked like it had been planed smooth by hands the size of seasons. They stood open — of course they stood open; Vaskal wanted guests, wanted tribute, wanted the world walking willingly into his vaults — and beyond them the court spread out in mirrored splendor: a guildhall the size of a country, corridors of polished obsidian reflecting corridors of polished obsidian forever, and everywhere the lamps.

Souls. Thousands upon thousands of them, shelved in vaults behind glass like inventory, each one a small bright flame, each one labeled. Brenn had spent his life around cargo manifests and the sight of organized wealth, and what he felt looking at the vaults was not awe. It was the cold professional recognition of a warehouse, and then the sickness that follows such recognition when you understand what's stored.

He came through the gates leaning on a spear he'd taken off a dead outrider, using it as a staff, trailing blood in a line the mirrored floor reflected into infinity so that it looked like ten thousand Brenns had bled their way in before him. Guards moved toward him — things in gray armor with faces like unfinished sculpture — and stopped, because the lamp at his shoulder had brightened, and the ledger under his coat had begun to glow through the wool like a coal through ash, and the guards of the Undercourt, creatures of debt and contract to their marrow, recognized a creditor when they saw one.

"I'm here," Brenn announced to the vast mirrored hall, to the court, to whatever usurper-god sat listening at the heart of it, "to see about an account."

His voice echoed and echoed and did not die.

"Marette Lockridge's account. Forty years outstanding. I've brought the ledger." He drew it out with hands that shook, held it up, and the green leather threw light across the nearest pillars like a struck match. "And I've brought what's written in it. The true road through Thornfell. The road your master's armies have bled ten years trying to find and never will, because it isn't on any map and never was — it's in here, in her hand, in ink she made from her own hearth-ash, and there's exactly one copy in all the world."

He began to walk. One step. Another. The spear clicking on the mirror-floor. Somewhere deep in the court, doors were opening; something vast was rising from a throne; the whole inverted world was turning its attention toward the bleeding old man in the doorway the way the mountain had turned toward him in its dream.

"So we're going to talk terms," Brenn said, and grinned with bloody teeth, because it was true, it had always been true, the thing Marette understood and Yara was learning and he himself had known since the famine winter when he'd stood witness in the snow: *everything* was terms, gods and men alike, the whole world was a market, and the only question that ever mattered was what you were willing to put on the table.

"And I'll tell you the price right now," he said, louder, as the shadows gathered and the throne-room doors swung wide and a presence older than the mountains' memory leaned down into the light to look at him. "It's not the road. The road's not for sale. The road's the *collateral*. You want to know the price?"

The lamp at his shoulder burned steady. The ledger burned brighter. And from very far above — impossibly far, through miles of stone and root and sleeping god-flesh, from a chapel in the deep places where two people he loved more than his own life were, at this moment, alive — Brenn felt, or imagined, or refused to doubt, an answer: a flicker, a warmth, a girl's voice saying *come back*.

"That's the price," he said. "Them. Safe. Out. Every debt cleared, every lamp freed, and my people walking home."

He planted the spear. He stood as straight as the wound allowed, which was not very, and did not care. And the darkness of the Undercourt gathered close around him like a tide around a rock that refuses, on principle, to move —

— and the great voice of Vaskal, when it came, sounded almost amused.

"An auditor," it said. "How delightful. Come in, little keeper. Let us review the books."

Brenn gripped the ledger tighter, felt Marette's lamp settle warm against his shoulder, and walked forward into the light of ten thousand stolen souls — knowing, with the cold clarity of a man whose account is nearly closed, that whatever happened next, he had already carried it far enough.
