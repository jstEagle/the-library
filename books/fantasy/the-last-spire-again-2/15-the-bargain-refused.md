## 15. The Bargain Refused

The court had stopped pretending to be anything but what it was.

Yara felt it the moment the guards — if you could call them that, tall figures of smoke and bone-lattice whose faces were smooth as river stones — came for her again. The mirrored guildhall around her had grown more honest in the hours since she'd been locked away: the false hearths had gone dark, the counterfeit ledgers on their shelves had begun to bleed ink like wounds, and the long table where Vaskal held his court now stretched into shadow at both ends, longer than any hall built by mortal hands could hold.

They walked her through corridors that remembered being a guildhall. She knew the shape of them the way she knew the shape of her own hands. There was the alcove where Brenn used to hide the good brandy behind a false wall of account books. There was the window seat where Marette had taught her to read cargo manifests by candlelight, tracing routes with one cracked fingernail while the snow piled up outside like an unpaid debt.

Here, every one of those places existed, but wrong. The alcove held a cage instead of brandy. The window seat looked out not on Thornfell's white shoulders but on a darkness so complete it seemed to press against the glass like something drowning.

*He built this from stolen memories,* she thought. *From souls he's kept. From mine.*

Her soul. Somewhere below her feet, past floors that were not floors, in a vault lined with ten thousand small bright lamps, hers was burning. She had seen it. A lamp no bigger than a fist, gold-white, steady as a hearthfire, with a flame that leaned toward her when she entered the vault — leaning the way a dog leans toward its person after years apart. She had reached for it and the wards had flung her back hard enough to crack a rib, and even that pain had felt strangely sweet, because pain was proof of something, and the lamp had flickered in answer, and she had thought: *I know you. I have been carrying you my whole life without knowing I was empty.*

Now they brought her up to the high court, and Vaskal was waiting.

He did not sit on a throne. That was the first thing she'd noticed, days or hours ago — time moved strangely here, thick as tallow — and it was still the first thing. He stood at the head of the endless table like a merchant standing at the end of a bargaining bench, which was, she understood now, exactly what he was. He wore the shape of a broad-shouldered man in traveling leathers, the kind of man you'd trust with your goods and your life, and only his eyes betrayed him: they were the color of the space between stars, and they never blinked, and when he looked at you, you felt *appraised*.

"Yara Lockridge," he said, and his voice was warm as a fire in winter, as a road home, as everything safe she had ever lost. "Sit. Eat. You've been fed nothing but court-food, and I know what court-food is. It's memory without substance." He gestured, and a chair drew itself out from the table. On the plate before it: bread, real bread, with a crust that cracked the way Marette's did, and butter, and salt from the pass.

Yara didn't sit.

"You've been talking to Cael," Vaskal said. Not a question. "I can smell him on the air of your cell. Guilt has a scent, did you know? Like wet ash." He smiled, and the smile was kind, and that was the most frightening thing about him. "I want to make you an offer. A true offer, under seal, the kind your grandmother understood. The kind that *holds*."

"I know what your bargains hold," Yara said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "My whole life was one."

"And a good life, wasn't it?" Vaskal tilted his head. "You ate. You thrived. The roads stayed open through famine winters when every other route through the Thornfells was buried or bloodied. Three generations of Lockridges lived because one old woman understood arithmetic." He spread his hands, the gesture of a man presenting an obvious truth. "I don't ask for worship, Yara. Worship is a currency for gods who are starving. I ask only for *order*. Debts paid. Contracts honored. Everything in its place."

"Everything in its place," Yara repeated. "And the free cities?"

Something shifted behind his unblinking eyes. "Ah. You've heard the drums."

Everyone had heard the drums. For two days they had come up through the stone, faint and rhythmic, the sound of an army marching somewhere far above — or perhaps not above at all; distance meant nothing here. The vanguard of the usurper, sweeping down out of the lowlands toward the free cities, burning shrines, taking conscripts, doing what armies do. Brenn had sent warning up the pass before Yara ever descended. She wondered if it had arrived. She wondered if there was anyone left to receive it.

"They will burn," Vaskal said simply. "Unless."

"Unless."

"You give me what I was always owed." He stepped closer, and though he made no threatening motion, the temperature of the hall dropped, and the shadows at the ends of the table leaned in like eavesdroppers. "Your soul, willingly surrendered. Freely given — that word matters, freely, it matters more than you can know. Your grandmother gave me yours under duress, and a soul taken under duress is a flawed coin, Yara. It spends poorly. It *resists*. But a soul given freely—" his voice softened, went almost tender, "—given freely, by its owner, with full knowledge? That is a keystone that fits. With it I can finish what I began a thousand years ago. I can devour the waking gods as they rise, drink the mountains' marrow, and rule until the rock itself forgets it was ever anything but mine."

"And the cities?"

"Spared. Sealed under covenant, as your grandmother sealed the roads. No army touches them. No plague, no famine, no usurper's tax. They will flourish." He said it easily, smoothly, and the terrible thing — the thing Yara would carry for the rest of her life, however long that proved to be — was that she believed him. He was a creature of contracts. He could not lie in a bargain. Whatever he promised, he would keep.

That was what made it a temptation instead of a trap. Traps you could see. This was just arithmetic — Marette's arithmetic, the same cold beautiful ledger-math that had bought thirty years of open roads with a granddaughter's soul. *One life weighed against thousands.* One girl's innermost light against every city from here to the sea.

She thought about the medicine she'd carried through the pass last month, the medicine that worked impossibly well, the village praying to dead gods and the dead gods answering anyway. She thought about the river underground that had answered her questions in a voice like water over stones, and the stones that remembered names, and how the mountain had felt half-asleep and *dreaming*, dreaming of being awake.

She thought about Cael, sitting in his cell two doors down, singing the old litany under his breath like a man keeping a promise to someone who couldn't hear it anymore.

"No," Yara said.

Vaskal's smile did not falter, but the hall grew colder. "You should consider the terms before—"

"I did consider them." Her heart was hammering and her cracked rib ached and her mouth was dry as chalk, and none of it mattered, because underneath all the fear was something harder, something forged in twenty years of passes and avalanches and border patrols and ledgers full of debts no one could collect. "You want to know why your bargain is failing? Why the old gods are waking despite everything you did to seal them? Because my grandmother cheated you. Not in the contract — she was too smart, too scared, she kept every letter of it. She cheated you in the only way that matters. She loved me. Every day of my life, she loved me, and she hated herself for what she'd done, and that hatred poisoned your perfect little keystone. A soul that knows it was sold doesn't shine right. Isn't that true?" She took a step forward, reckless, furious, alive. "You've been holding a lamp with a crack in it for thirty years, and you're only now realizing the flaw isn't damage. It's *love*. And you think I'll hand over the rest willingly?"

"I think," Vaskal said quietly, "that you love the free cities too. And I think you'll find, in the end, that love is just another ledger, and every ledger balances."

"Then we're about to find out which of us reads numbers better."

For a moment — one long, frozen moment — the court was utterly silent. Even the drums above seemed to hold their beat. Then Vaskal laughed, a short, genuine bark of sound, and it was somehow worse than rage.

"Oh, Marette," he said, to the air, to the dead, to the ghost of a woman who had out-stubborned a god. "You see it? You see what you made?" He turned back to Yara, and the warmth drained out of his face like light from a closing door, leaving something ancient and vast and terribly patient. "Very well. Refusal noted. Witnessed. Sealed." Around the hall, the shadow-court stirred, and Yara heard, distantly, the sound of a great ledger page turning. "Then the cities burn, the gods wake into my teeth, and your lamp stays in my vault until the last heat leaves the world. Guards—"

That was when the doors blew open.

Not the grand doors — the *small* door, the servant's entrance at the far end of the hall, the one Yara hadn't even known existed until that moment except that of course she had, because it was the servant's entrance of the guildhall, the one Brenn used when he came in late from checking the traps, and it stood open now, and half-collapsed in its frame, bleeding from a dozen wounds and burned across one whole side of his body, was Brenn.

"*Brenn!*"

He looked terrible. He looked like a man who had walked through the vanguard's fires and the mountain's rootways and the court's own gauntlet of smoke-and-bone guards on a leg that had been broken since the lowlands. His beard was singed to the skin on the left side. One eye was swollen shut. He was leaning on something — clutching something to his chest with both arms like a man holding a child, or a shield, or the last of everything he loved.

Marette's ledger.

"Brenn, you stupid, stubborn old—" Yara started forward and the guards moved, smoke coalescing between her and him, and Vaskal raised one finger and the whole hall stilled.

"Well," the usurper-god said softly. "What is this? A courier? At my court?" His starless eyes fixed on the ledger, and Yara saw understanding bloom in them, slow and terrible, like frost spreading across a window. "Ah. *Ah.* The guild's last secret. The true road." He laughed again, quieter this time, delighted and appalled in equal measure. "Thirty years I've wanted that road. My armies have broken themselves against the Thornfells for thirty years because one old woman hid the map inside a book of debts and never told a living soul where." His gaze cut to Brenn, and the tenderness that came into his voice then was the most obscene thing Yara had yet witnessed. "Old man. You carried it *here*? Into my court? Give it to me, and I will make you whole. New legs. New years. I will write your name into the covenant of my kingdom and it will stand as long as stone stands."

Brenn spat blood on the floor. It steamed.

"You don't know her at all," he rasped, and it took Yara a heartbeat to understand he was talking about Marette. "You think she hid the road to *keep* it. Fool. She hid it so that someday someone could *give it away*. So the passes wouldn't die with us." He looked past the guards, past the god, straight at Yara, and his one good eye was bright and fierce and absolutely calm. "Girl. You remember what I told you. About the ledger."

*Burn the page and the road dies with it. Burn the page and no army ever finds it. Some things are worth more as ashes than as inheritance.*

"No," Yara whispered. "No, Brenn, don't you *dare*—"

"The free cities," Brenn said. "The waking gods. The whole rotten beautiful world." He fumbled at his belt with shaking fingers, and Yara saw the striker there, the flint and steel he'd carried for forty years, the one he used to light his pipe on cold watch nights. "Marette's arithmetic was wrong, girl. She counted lives like coins. But some things aren't *in* the ledger. They're what the ledger's *for*."

"Brenn—"

"I'm already dying, Yara." Gentle now. Terribly gentle. "Been dying since the lowlands. This way I get to choose what I die *of*." He grinned with bloody teeth, and it was the grin he'd worn every morning of her childhood, the grin that meant the traps were checked and the road was clear and the world, whatever else it was, was workable. "Tell Cael he owes me a pipe. Tell the gods—" his voice broke, just once, just slightly, "—tell the gods Brenn Lockridge kept his books."

He struck the flint.

The spark caught the ledger's oil-soaked spine — he must have drenched it, sometime on that terrible road down, prepared for this the whole way, faithful to the end — and flame went up the old pages like sunrise going up a white peak. Vaskal moved then, faster than sight, faster than thought, smoke and fury crossing the hall in a single stride, his hand closing on Brenn's shoulder—

But the ledger was already burning. Page after page curling, blackening, the debts and credits and routes of three generations of Lockridges feeding the fire, and somewhere in that roar of flame one particular page — the page, the *true road*, the secret that had outlasted famine and armies and a god's thirty-year hunger — went up in a single breath of light.

Brenn looked at it burn. He looked at Yara.

"Roads stay open," he said. "That's the whole job, girl. Roads stay open."

Then Vaskal closed his hand, and the light went out of Brenn the way a candle goes out — suddenly, completely, leaving only the dark where it used to be. What fell to the floor was small and heavy and still, and the fire kept burning on top of it, consuming the last of the ledger, and the god stood over both, and for the first time since Yara had entered his court, Vaskal looked *afraid*.

"You fool," he breathed. "You magnificent, pointless fool. Do you know what you've—"

The hall shuddered.

Deep beneath them — deeper than the vault of lamps, deeper than the roots of the rootways, in the sleeping flesh of the mountain itself — something rolled over. The drums above faltered. The stones in the walls began, very faintly, to remember their names aloud.

And in the sudden ringing silence, Yara heard Cael's voice rising from the cells below, clear and steady, beginning the old litany — the one sung to gentle waking gods, the one he'd been keeping warm in his chest for a thousand years:

*"Wake soft. Wake slow. We are here. We are here. We are here."*

The mountain opened its eyes.

* * *

The guards came apart like smoke in a gale. Yara didn't wait to learn why. She ran — past Vaskal, who stood transfixed over the burning ledger as though the flames were speaking to him in a language he'd forbidden himself to learn; past the long table where the counterfeit ledgers bled their ink; past the window seat where her grandmother had once taught her to read, its glass now showing not darkness but *dawn*, impossible dawn, red-gold light flooding up from below as though the sunrise had gotten lost and decided to rise from underneath the world.

She took the servant's stair down three steps at a time, ribs screaming, lungs full of ash and grief, and behind her she heard Vaskal begin to shout — orders, threats, invocations — and beneath his voice the deep groan of stone remembering it was bone, and beneath *that*, growing louder with every heartbeat, ten thousand small bright lamps beginning, all together, to sing.

The cells were open. Of course they were; nothing in this court could hold against what was coming. She found Cael in the corridor, on his feet, litany spilling from him like water, his face streaked with tears he didn't seem to know he was crying.

"Yara—" He saw her face. He saw past her face, to the corridor beyond, to what lay at the end of it. "Oh," he said softly. "Oh, Yara. Who?"

"Brenn." Her voice cracked clean down the middle. "He burned it. He burned the road. He—" She couldn't finish. Twenty years of watching the old man check traps and light pipes and complain about her handwriting, and all of it was ash now, along with the last secret of the last guild of the last smugglers in the Thornfells.

Cael took her face in both hands. His palms were shaking. "Then the road is gone," he said, "and the mountain is awake, and the god who killed him is standing in a burning house." His eyes were ferocious and wet and absolutely certain. "So we finish it. For Marette. For Brenn. For everyone whose name was in that book."

Above them, the ceiling split along lines no mason had ever drawn, and the first true light of the waking gods poured through like honey, like blood, like morning.

Yara wiped her face with the back of her hand, the way Brenn had taught her, and started running.
