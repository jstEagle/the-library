## 16. Riot of the Sleeping

Cael had spent thirty years learning how prisons worked, and so he knew this one: a cell of black stone with no door he could find, warm as a held breath, lit by nothing. Vaskal's court did not need chains. It needed only certainty, and certainty was the one thing Cael had never managed to keep.

He had been counting. Not hours — hours meant nothing down here, where the mountain's pulse replaced the sun — but words. He recited the old litany under his breath, the one they had beaten out of him in seminary and then, later, out of the world entirely. *Root below us. Root above us. The mountain dreams, and we are its dreaming.* Nine verses. He had reached the fourth for perhaps the two-hundredth time when the stone beneath him flinched.

Not trembled. *Flinched* — the way a sleeper flinches when someone says their name in a room where everyone swore they were alone.

Then the sound came, and every prayer he had ever memorized left him at once.

It began far off, or perhaps very near; distance was a lie the Undercourt told constantly. A ringing, high and thin, like a wineglass about to break. Then another glass joined it, and another, hundreds, thousands, until the air itself seemed made of shattering. And threaded through the ringing came voices.

He knew what they were before he let himself know. Souls. The vault of lamps, the hoard Vaskal kept behind mirrored doors — thousands of them, harvested over centuries, each one taken from someone who had prayed at the wrong altar or owed the wrong debt. He had helped build that vault. He had carried the first lamp himself, when he was young and certain and called it *keeping safe* instead of *stealing*.

The ringing climbed toward a pitch that hurt his teeth. And then Yara's voice cut through it all, clear as a blade laid flat against a whetstone.

"Ledger's open," she shouted, somewhere beyond the walls, beyond everything. "Every debt in this house is *paid*."

The wall facing him did not shatter. It *opened*, peeling back like skin from muscle, and light poured in — not the cold silver of the court's mirrors but gold, warm, ragged, alive. Light that had been locked away for lifetimes and remembered exactly what it was.

Cael stood in the doorway of his cell and watched the riot begin.

* * *

It was not like anything the seminaries had described, though they had described everything. They had taught him that souls were orderly things, that they went where gods sent them, that the Undercourt's vault was a treasury and treasuries were quiet. They had never taught him what a soul looked like after three hundred years in the dark when the dark finally broke.

They came up through the floor of the court like water finding its level. Lights first — small bright lamps streaming through corridors of black glass, bouncing off mirrors, doubling and redoubling until the whole court blazed with a thousand thousand reflections of joy. And inside each light, if you dared to look, a shape: a farmer still holding a scythe of pure intention, a child running on legs made of laughter, an old woman whose light flickered in patterns that Cael slowly understood were *singing*.

And the god-flesh woke.

He felt it before he heard it, a shifting in the deep rock like a whale turning over beneath a ship. The sleeping gods — the ones Vaskal had bound beneath the mountain, the ones Cael himself had helped seal with hymns inverted and names unwritten — began to stir, and their waking was not gentle. How could it be? They had been asleep for an age, dreaming of hands and harvests and rain, and now they woke into a body full of screaming light.

The mountain screamed with them.

Stone split overhead. The mirrored ceiling of the great court crazed like lake ice, and through the cracks poured not darkness but *roots* — vast pale roots pushing down from the world above, feeling blindly through the wreckage, searching. Somewhere in the deeps something enormous exhaled, and the exhalation smelled of loam and snowmelt and the green memory of forests that had died before the free cities were built.

Cael ran toward the noise.

He did not decide to. His legs decided for him, carrying him across a court coming apart at every seam, past mirrors that had begun to weep mercury, past Vaskal's throne where the usurper-god stood rigid as a man hearing his own name read from a death warrant. Past Yara.

She stood at the center of the flood with her hand still pressed against the broken vault door, hair whipping in a wind that had no source, her face lit gold and terrible. Their eyes met for half a heartbeat. She was looking for an answer in him, and he had none to give except the only thing left.

"Sing," she said. Not a request. "You said you remembered it. *Sing.*"

So Cael sang.

* * *

His voice cracked on the first verse. Thirty years of silence will do that to a throat; so will terror, and so will shame, and he had a lifetime's supply of both. But the litany did not care whether it was sung beautifully. That was the thing the seminaries had gotten wrong, or maybe the thing they had lied about on purpose: the litany was not a performance offered upward. It was a lullaby offered *down*. It was what you sang to something vast and wounded and half-mad with waking, and it worked the way a mother's voice works — not because it is skilled, but because it is *there*.

*Root below us. Root above us.*

The nearest soul — the old woman, her light stuttering with grief and freedom tangled together — drifted toward the sound. Cael sank to his knees on the shattered mirror-floor so she would not have to look up. He opened his arms the way you open your arms to a bird that may fly away or land, and either answer must be borne.

*The mountain dreams, and we are its dreaming.*

She landed against his chest like a coal wrapped in silk. Warmth spread through him, and with the warmth came *her* — not her name, he didn't get her name, but her last morning: bread baking, a grandchild's weight against her hip, sunlight through a window that some herald had knocked upon with terms of a bargain she'd been too poor to refuse. Three hundred years, and the first thing she chose to spend her freedom on was leaning against a stranger who had helped put her in the dark.

Cael wept and did not stop singing.

*We dream the rain, and the rain dreams rivers, and the rivers remember our names.*

More of them came. He could not hold them all; no one could hold them all. So he changed the song as he went, the way he had been trained to change it long ago, before mercy got him cast out — no. Before mercy got him *out*. He turned the litany outward, made it a road instead of a cradle, and the souls began to move along it, up through the splitting ceiling, up along the pale roots, toward a sky most of them had forgotten they'd ever been robbed of.

And beneath it all, the god-flesh responded.

It was agony at first. He would not pretend otherwise, not even now, not even in the remembering. The waking gods thrashed, and mountains have no concept of gentleness; whole galleries of the court folded like wet paper, and somewhere a river of answers — the one Yara had told him about, the one that spoke — burst up through the floor and ran shrieking between the pillars, every drop of it shouting questions it had stored for centuries. The roots whipped. The mirrors exploded into silver rain.

But the litany did what it had always done. It gave the pain a rhythm. And rhythm is the difference between a scream and a song.

Slowly — by degrees measured in verses — the thrashing eased. The great bulk beneath them rolled over once more and then lay still, listening. Cael sang the ninth verse, the one about dawn, the one the usurper's priests had forbidden on pain of unmaking, and as he sang it he felt the mountain's attention gather around him like weather.

It knew the song. It knew the traitor singing it. And it listened anyway, because a lullaby does not ask the singer to be innocent. Only present.

* * *

Yara found him there, kneeling in the ruin, hoarse, drenched in silver rain and gold light, with the old woman's warmth still fading against his chest. She crouched beside him, took his bleeding hands in hers, and looked at him the way she had looked at him in the hollow chapel when he confessed what he was — measuring him, and finding the measure sufficient.

"The deep ones are waking," she said. Her voice was raw. "All of them. And I think—" She stopped, swallowed, started again. "I think Vaskal's still standing. And I don't think he means to lose quietly."

Above them, at the crown of the ruined court, the mirrors had fallen away entirely, and through the hole showed not sky but *Vaskal* — vast now, grown terrible, drawing the court's collapsing architecture into himself the way a dying fire draws in air. His eyes found them across the chaos, and his voice, when it came, did not come through the air but through the stone, through Cael's bones, through the litany itself:

"You sing for them, little priest. Then sing for *this*."

The mountain shuddered. Beneath them, in the deepest dark, something began to rise — and Cael, who had spent his life learning the grammar of gods, understood with perfect clarity that whatever came next would be paid for in blood.

He tightened his grip on Yara's hands and kept singing anyway.
