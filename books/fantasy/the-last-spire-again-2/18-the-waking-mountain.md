## 18. The Waking Mountain

The true road was not a road at all.

Yara had known that, in the way she knew the weight of her own pack or the pull of her own breath — something carried so deep it never needed saying. Marette had told her once, when she was small enough to sit on the guildhall floor and trace the pass-routes with a finger dipped in spilled salt: *the roads we sell are roads anyone can walk, girl. The road nobody sells is walked by the mountain itself.* She had thought it a smuggler's riddle, the kind old women tell children to keep them from asking where the real cargo goes.

Now she climbed it, with Cael dying against her back, and understood that Marette had been literal all along.

The way opened out of the collapsing court like a seam splitting cloth. Where Vaskal's halls had been mirrored and cold and lit by stolen souls, the true road was rough stone veined with living root — pale roots, thick as her waist, pulsing faintly with light the color of dawn seen through ice. The mountain breathed around her. She could feel it in the soles of her boots: a slow, vast rhythm, inhale and exhale, as if Thornfell had been holding its breath for a thousand years and had only just remembered how.

Behind her, far below through the shaking stone, the court was coming down. She did not look back. She had looked back exactly once, at the mouth of the seam, when the vault's light had still been pouring up out of the depths like water finding its level — ten thousand lamps' worth of freed souls rising through the roots of the world — and she had seen the god-flesh beneath the court begin to move. Great sleeping limbs, mountains themselves, flexing after an age of enforced slumber. It had been beautiful. It had been terrible. She had not had time for either feeling, because Cael had been bleeding out on the shattered mirror-floor beside her, and love, it turned out, was very practical.

So: she ran. Or she walked, when running would jolt him too hard. Or she crawled, in one place where the seam narrowed and the breathing stone pressed close, and she whispered apologies to the mountain the whole way through, and the mountain, impossibly, seemed to listen — widening itself by a hand's width, by two, the way a sleeper shifts to make room.

"Almost," she said into Cael's ear, though she had stopped believing in *almost* somewhere around the fourth mile. "Cael. Almost there."

He weighed nothing and everything. A grown man, lean from years of exile, and yet he hung off her shoulders like a bundle of wet wool — no, heavier than that, because wet wool doesn't whisper your name in a voice gone thin as candle-smoke.

"Yara." A bubble of red at his lips. "Yara, put me down. You can't—"

"I can," she said. "I'm a Lockridge. We carry what can't be carried. It's in the ledgers."

A sound that might have been laughter, if laughter could be drowned. His head lolled against her neck, and his blood soaked warm through her coat, and she kept climbing.

* * *

She came up out of the seam onto the true road proper, and stopped, and forgot, for three full breaths, that Cael was dying.

They were inside the mountain still — but the mountain was awake now, and it showed. The passage before her was a spine of granite arched high as a cathedral, and down its length the pale roots burned brighter, and between them grew things that had no business growing underground: white flowers like frozen stars, moss that glittered with frost-light, pools of water so clear they showed not their own depths but the sky — the actual sky, wheeling overhead, stars dragging westward though night should have been hours from falling. And everywhere, everywhere, the pulse. In the walls. In the air. In her own chest, keeping time whether she willed it or not.

And through it all, rising, the gods.

She felt them before she heard them, felt them as a pressure change like weather coming over the pass — and then she heard them: voices without throats, songs without mouths, rolling up through the roots of the world from every peak at once. Not words, not quite. More like the memory of words, the shape meaning makes in the mind before language arrives. The old gods, waking in their bones of stone and glacier, stretching limbs that were ridgelines, opening eyes that were lakes catching the first impossible dawn.

One of them was directly above her. She knew this because the ceiling of the great spine-passage thinned, and through it, filtered by fifty feet of rock, she saw light — golden-green, alive, moving — and a vast slow attention turned downward, and touched her, and passed on, gentle as a hand brushing a fly from a horse's shoulder. Small. She had never felt so small, nor so held.

"Hold on," she told Cael, uselessly, tenderly. "Hold on. Look what's waking up."

His eyes were open. When had his eyes opened? They were fixed on the thin place in the ceiling, on the moving gold-green light, and his ruined lips were shaping something — a word, half a word — and she bent close to catch it.

"*Ashevan,*" he breathed. "*Ashevan rises.*"

"The river-god?" She remembered the name from the rootways, from the pool that had answered questions. "That's who's over us?"

"No." A shudder went through him; she tightened her grip. "Bigger. The mother-current. Every river is her finger." His hand found hers, weak as a child's. "Yara. Listen to me while I can still say it. They're waking wrong. Hurting. Vaskal chained them under their own flesh — you saw what was under the court. They'll rise in agony, and agony makes gods do—" He coughed, and the cough was bad, wet and long. "Makes them do what armies do."

"Then I'll talk to them," Yara said. "I've out-bargained a usurper-god and a gate made of names. I can talk to a mountain."

He laughed then, properly, and the laughing cost him, and she felt the cost in the slackening of his hand, and terror went through her like cold water.

"Cael. Stay with me. That's not a request, priest, that's a term, and I always get my terms—"

"*Smuggler,*" he murmured, fond as a benediction, and his eyes stayed open, and she took that and climbed.

* * *

The true road came up out of the mountain's shoulder near midnight, which was also somehow dawn — the sky itself confused now, the stars wheeling too fast, and along the eastern peaks a glow that no sun had ever made, green and gold and climbing. Yara stood on a shelf of bare granite with the whole Thornfell range laid out around her, and watched the old gods rise.

There was nothing else to do. There was no more road, no more descent, no more plan. There was only this: a ledge, a dying man, and a world being unmade and remade at once.

To the south, the peak above the Undercourt split like an egg. No fire came out. What came out was light and root and water — a fountain of living silver that climbed and climbed until it stood taller than any spire man had built, and it had a shape, almost, if you squinted at it kindly: shoulders, a crown of glacier, arms opening. Ashevan, or the body Ashevan wore the way mortals wear coats. Farther north, another light answered, blue-white, crackling; then another, deep amber, rising out of a valley with a groan she felt in her teeth. One by one, seven, eight, nine — the old gods surfacing through their own mountains like whales breaching a dark sea.

And below, in the lowlands, where Vaskal's armies marched on the free cities—

Yara saw it happen. She wished she hadn't. She was glad, forever after, that she had.

The host of the Undercourt stretched across the valley floors like a spilt ink-bottle, ten thousand torches in ordered ranks, siege-engines crawling toward the border towns, the black banners of the usurper snapping in a wind that had, until tonight, belonged entirely to him. And then the wind changed. Then the rivers rose. Then the snowfields let go all at once — not as avalanches, not as destruction, but as release, whole slopes of winter sliding down into the valleys as meltwater, clean and sudden, and where the meltwater met the army it did not drown the soldiers. It *unmade* what the usurper had made of them. Because that was the thing Yara saw, the thing she would carry: the front ranks turning to ash from the boots up — ash, and then snowmelt, and then simply gone, like frost surrendering to morning — and the men behind them dropping their weapons and falling to their knees, weeping, as the songs rolled over them. Not killed. *Freed.* Whatever Vaskal had bound them with — oaths, chains, souls pawned like hers — the waking of the old gods broke it, the way dawn breaks a bad dream.

Some few, she knew even then, had been willing. Some few loved their master. Those ones the mountains swallowed, quietly, without ceremony, and the mountains did not apologize.

The banners went out one by one, like candles pinched by a careful thumb.

By the time the last black banner fell, Yara was on her knees on the granite shelf with Cael cradled in her lap, and she was crying without any memory of having started, and the songs of the rising gods washed over her and through her and filled every cracked place in her the way meltwater fills a dry streambed.

Then the mountain spoke to her.

* * *

It was not words. It was never going to be words. But she had spent her life reading terrain — reading scree-slopes and snow-bridges and the lies men wrote in ledgers — and this was reading of the same kind, only vaster. The attention she'd felt earlier, the gentle brush of a god's regard, returned and settled on the ledge, on her, on the dying man in her arms, and it did not pass on this time. It stayed.

*Small thing,* said the mountain, in the voice of pressure and warmth and the particular quality of the silence under snow. *Thief of roads. Granddaughter of the woman who bargained.*

"I'm not a thief," Yara said. Her voice came out wrecked. She didn't care. "I'm a Lockridge. We keep accounts."

*You do,* the mountain agreed, and there was something in it that might have been amusement, ancient and enormous and dry as summer stone. *We have read your grandmother's ledger. All of it. Every debt, every entry, every page your Brenn carried down through fire.* The attention shifted, briefly, toward Cael — toward the blood, the failing breath — and grief moved through the mountain's regard like a shadow of a cloud crossing a valley. *This one sang our litany in the dark. This one gentled our waking. He is owed.*

"Then pay him," Yara said. Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against Cael's chest, as if she could hold his life in by force, a smuggler guarding cargo. "Pay him whatever he's owed. I'll sign for it. I'll countersign. I'll—"

*It is not ours to pay,* the mountain said. *What breaks cannot be unbroke by us. What bleeds—* The pause was long. Somewhere below, a river changed course, singing. *What bleeds can only choose to stop, or choose not to. That freedom is the one thing even gods cannot give or take. He is choosing, little thief. Right now. Do not interrupt him.*

Yara looked down at Cael. His eyes were closed, but his lips were moving — the litany, she realized, the old one, the one he'd sung to the rioting souls, worn smooth as a river-stone from a lifetime of guilt and devotion. Choosing. Even now. Even here.

Her heart broke cleanly, like ice at thaw, and she found she could stand on the broken pieces.

"Then hear my terms," she said to the mountain. Loudly. Into the face of something older than language. Because grief had burned away the last of her fear, and what was left underneath was pure Lockridge — stubbornness refined to its final temper. "You're waking up hungry. I can feel it. You want worship. You want what Vaskal wanted — everything, all of it, the lowlands and the towns and the people kneeling in the mud. I watched your rivers take his army apart and I know power when I see it, and I know appetite when it's wearing power's clothes."

The mountain's attention sharpened. Around her, on the other peaks, other attentions turned — Ashevan's green-gold gaze, the blue-white crackle of the storm-mother, the deep amber burn of the valley-walker. Nine pairs of eyes made of lake-light and glacier, all of them settling on one exhausted woman kneeling on a rock with a dead-or-dying man in her lap.

Yara lifted her chin.

"You won't get it," she said. "Not the way he wanted it. Not taken. Because I have something better, and I'm the only one alive who can offer it." She spread one hand, palm up, toward the passes — the hidden ways, the salt-roads, the thousand secret threads the Lockridge Guild had woven through these mountains for six generations. "Our roads. All of them. The sold ones and the unsold one. Every route my grandmother sealed with her bargain and my soul — I'll open them. Not as smugglers' ways. As *sacred ground*. Pilgrim roads. Shrines at every waypoint, tended by the guild, kept by the guild, forever. People will come to you, mountain. Not driven. *Walking.* Prayers said freely on ground given freely — isn't that worth more than anything Vaskal ever stole? Worship with clean hands?"

Silence. The kind of silence a cathedral has. The kind of silence a judge keeps while weighing.

*You offer us the wild places,* the mountain said slowly, *and ask us to leave the rest.*

"I offer you the passes," Yara corrected. "The hardest, highest, holiest parts. Let the mortals keep the lowlands and their farms and their stupid little wars. Let them come up to you when they need something bigger than themselves. That's the covenant. That's the terms. Take them or don't, but know this—" and here her voice finally broke, and she let it, "—I have paid for these mountains with my grandmother's life and my own soul and maybe his, and I will not watch them become Vaskal's kingdom with better weather. I am the last Lockridge, and I am telling you: *no.*"

Wind moved across the shelf. Snow, fine as flour, swirled up from the granite and hung in the air around her, catching the green-gold light of the risen gods, so that for a moment she knelt inside a cloud of tiny burning stars.

Then the mountain laughed.

It was not a frightening sound. It was the sound spring makes when it cracks a frozen river — relief, release, the joy of a thing long-bound remembering motion — and it rolled from peak to peak to peak, and the other gods caught it and passed it on, until the whole Thornfell range rang with something that had not sounded in a thousand years: the old gods, *delighted.*

*Done,* said the mountain. *Sealed. Witnessed by all nine and the waters between. Keeper of the covenant, last Lockridge — rise. Your roads are holy now. Walk them tall.*

The pressure lifted. The attention withdrew, gently, folding itself away like a letter returned to its envelope — but not entirely. Yara felt it stay, faintly, the way warmth stays in stone after sundown. A presence at her shoulder. A partner.

She looked down at Cael.

* * *

The blood had stopped.

That was the first thing. The second thing was the light — faint, green-gold, seeping up out of the granite beneath him, out of the roots that ran everywhere now, through everything, threading even the shelf where they knelt. It gathered in the wound in his side the way water gathers in a cupped hand. Not healing. Not exactly. *Deciding,* perhaps. Weighing, the way the mountain had weighed.

Cael's eyes opened. They found hers immediately, the way they always did, as if the space between them were the shortest distance in any world.

"What did you do," he rasped.

"Bargained," Yara said. Her voice cracked again. She let it. "You should see the contract. It's written in snow."

"Yara." He caught her wrist. His grip was stronger than it had been an hour ago, or she was weaker, or both. "Whatever they offered you — the guild restored, the debts cleared, everything Marette lost handed back — you didn't—"

"It's done, Cael." She covered his hand with hers. "The roads are sacred ground. The guild keeps them. That's the inheritance now. Wardens, not smugglers." She smiled, and it hurt, and it was real. "Marette would hate it. Which means she'd secretly love it."

Below them, in the valleys, the first pilgrims were already on the old paths — freed soldiers, weeping townsfolk, walking upward toward the lights on the peaks with their faces tipped back like plants finding the sun. The roads Yara's family had run contraband down for six generations were filling with prayers. Somewhere south, a village whose medicine had worked impossibly well all winter was lighting candles on a shrine that had stood empty for a century, and the candles were catching, one by one, with no wind to help them.

Cael watched it too. His breathing was easier. Not well — she could see the cost of the night written all over him, and she suspected she would spend years paying off what the mountain's mercy had only deferred — but easier. Present. *Choosing,* still, to stay.

"They'll want a priest," he said quietly. "First of the new order. They'll offer me anything."

"I know."

"And I'm going to refuse," he said, "and ask instead for a mortal life beside a woman who out-bargained nine gods on a rock in the middle of the night, and I want you to know I've thought this through and the math is terrible and I'm doing it anyway."

Yara laughed — a raw, cracked, unstoppable sound — and leaned down, and kissed him among the tiny burning stars of the snow, while the mountain kept its warmth in the stone beneath them like a promise.

Far to the north, past the last peak, past the edge of the maps, something else stirred. Something that was not one of the nine. Something the mountain's delight had momentarily drowned out — a tenth note in the litany, low and patient and wrong, waiting in the wild places beyond the covenant's edge where the old gods' new hunger would soon come looking.

But that, as Marette used to say, was tomorrow's ledger.

Tonight, the mountain was awake, the roads were open, and Yara Lockridge — soul her own, debts rewritten, inheritance transformed — carried her love down the sacred way toward home.
