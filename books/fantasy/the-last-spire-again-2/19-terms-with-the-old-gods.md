## 19. Terms with the Old Gods

The mountain had a voice now, and it would not stop talking.

Yara heard it through her boots, through her teeth, through the marrow of every bone she owned: a low grinding chorus like millstones turning beneath the world, like ten thousand rivers deciding at once which way to run. The old gods were awake, all of them, rising through the roots of the Thornfells the way sap rises in a tree cut down too long ago — slow, groaning, furious with life.

She sat on a shelf of granite halfway up the true road, Cael's head in her lap, and watched dawn come up over a range that was no longer sleeping.

Snow was falling upward.

Not all of it — just along certain ridgelines, certain spires, where the stone breathed out plumes of white that climbed instead of drifted down. Where the flakes rose, Yara could feel something underneath looking back at her. Not eyes. Something older than eyes. Attention, vast and unhurried, settling on her the way weather settles on a valley.

Cael stirred against her knee. The wound Vaskal had dealt him ran from shoulder to hip, a black seam that no bandage seemed to hold shut, but his breath still came, shallow and stubborn, and when she touched his forehead it was cool — cool as riverstone, cool as the deep places of the world. She chose to believe that meant something good. She had spent three days believing things because she needed them to be true, and so far the practice had kept them both alive.

"You're staring at me again," Cael murmured, without opening his eyes.

"I'm checking whether you're dead."

"Still not." A ghost of his old dry smile. "Disappointing everyone."

"Everyone being me."

"Mostly you." His voice cracked on the last word, and she fed him snowmelt from the waterskin, one careful sip at a time, until he slept again.

Above them, the upward-falling snow thickened into a column, and the column took a shape, and the shape looked down at her with the patience of a thing that had waited four hundred years under stone and could certainly wait out one exhausted smuggler.

Yara sighed, shifted Cael gently onto her folded cloak, and stood up to meet it.

* * *

It did not come as a body. That was the first thing she understood, standing there with her hands empty and her knife — uselessly, absurdly — in her belt. It came as pressure, as presence, as the sense of a face too large for any single shape, assembled out of avalanche and pine-shadow and the blue ice of the high cirques. When she tried to look directly at it, she saw instead everything it was made of: a glacier calving, a wolf track in old snow, the green insistence of lichen cracking rock, a cave mouth breathing warm air in winter. The old gods were not one god. They were the mountains entire, waking, and they had gathered their attention into this one place because of her.

Because of what she carried.

Her soul sat behind her ribs again — she felt it constantly now, a small bright weight, a lamp relit — and the mountains felt it too. Everything that had happened below, the shattering of the vault, the flood of freed souls, the unmaking of Vaskal, had passed through her like current through wire. She was, whether she liked it or not, the keystone still. Just pointed in a new direction.

*You bargained,* said the mountain, and its voice was not sound at all but the memory of sound, avalanches she had never witnessed, her grandmother's voice saying *terms* over a ledger, the creak of the guildhall door. *The usurper offered. You refused. Now we offer, little thief, and see what you are made of.*

"I'm made of stubbornness and bad sleep," Yara said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "And I'm not a thief today. Today I'm a merchant. There's a difference, and it matters."

A rumble that might have been laughter moved through the rock beneath her feet. Somewhere far below, a hot spring that had been cold for centuries burst open and steamed into the morning.

*Name your terms.*

"Not yet." She held up a hand, palm out, as if she could stop a mountain range the way she'd once stopped a mule. "First I want to know what you want. All of it. No heralds, no fine print, no bargains made in famine winters by desperate women who don't understand what they're signing. I've seen how that ends. I buried her last week."

The attention upon her sharpened. Snow rose faster along the ridgelines. For a long moment the only sound was wind, and Cael's ragged breathing, and somewhere far down-slope the melt beginning — whole drifts sloughing off at once as warmth returned to slopes that had forgotten it.

Then the god spoke, and it spoke honestly, because she had asked it to, and honesty turned out to be more terrible than any threat.

*We want what he wanted,* it said. *Worship. Altars. Kneeling. Four hundred years underground while they burned our shrines and sang his name, and now we are awake and the hunger is enormous, little thief, and we do not know how to be smaller than it.* A pause, and within the pause, something almost human: *We want the valleys. We want the cities. We want the prayers of every living soul between here and the sea.*

"And you'll take them," Yara said, "the way he would have taken them. Armies of snowmelt. Shrines raised over free-city hearths. A different crown on the same head."

*We are not him.*

"No," she agreed. "You're worse. He was one greedy god. You're a whole range of them, and you've been starving for four hundred years, and starving things don't negotiate well." She crouched, picked up a handful of ordinary snow, let it fall through her fingers. "But here's what you seem to have missed, because you were asleep and no one told you. Worship taken by force isn't worship. It's fear wearing worship's clothes. Vaskal had an entire empire kneeling, and what did it feed him? Nothing. He got hungrier. He had to steal souls by the vault-full just to keep his court lit. Fear is thin gruel, my lord. It keeps you alive and it never fills you."

The mountain went very still. Even the upward snow slowed.

*Speak plainly,* it said, and there was something in the voice now that had not been there before — something young. These gods were ancient and newborn at once, and no one had ever dared talk back to them, and Yara realized with a lurch that she was doing the thing she had been doing her whole life: walking a dangerous pass alone, reading the terrain, finding the line between the cliff edge and the road.

"Freely given worship is meat," she said. "Taken worship is smoke. You can gorge yourselves on smoke and starve anyway, or you can learn to raise actual herds. Which means you leave people room to choose you. Which means—" she spread her hands, "—you don't get the cities. You get the wild places. Every peak, every forest, every river nobody's dammed. The places people already go to feel something bigger than themselves. Let them come to you. Make it worth the walk."

*And the passes?*

"The passes stay mortal." She said it flat, final, the way Marette used to say *the ledgers balance*. "Trade roads, shepherd tracks, the true road through Thornfell. People have bled and died for those roads for six generations. My grandmother sold her granddaughter's soul to keep them open, and I am not letting her arithmetic come to nothing. Mortals keep the passes. You keep everything else. And in exchange—"

*In exchange?*

"In exchange, you answer prayers." Her voice caught, and she let it. "Real ones. Small ones. A village in the high valley prayed to you all winter with medicine that worked impossibly well, and none of them knew why, and they thanked the gods everyone said were dead. That prayer reached you, didn't it? I carried it. I carried a lot of them, over the years, without knowing that's what contraband weighed." She swallowed. "Answer the honest ones. Heal what you can heal. Wake the springs, bless the herds, show up in dreams when a shepherd's lost in a whiteout. Be worth praying to, and mortals will pray. That's the whole covenant. That's the trade."

Silence. Long enough that she checked over her shoulder to make sure Cael still breathed.

When the god answered, it answered with all its voices at once — glacier, wolf, spring, storm — and the words came down like the first thaw.

*Nobody has ever asked us to be worth loving,* it said. *They have only ever asked us to be feared, or begged us to be elsewhere. You ask us to be worthy.* Another rumble, deeper, considering. *This is why the keystone was yours. This is why the bargain broke in your hands and not ours.*

"So we have terms?"

*We have terms. Swear them, keeper of the roads, and swear them in the old manner — blood on stone, name spoken twice.*

Yara drew her knife. It was a smuggler's knife, short and practical, nicked from a hundred hard miles, and it had opened everything from cargo crates to the seal on Marette's ledger. It seemed suddenly the most appropriate instrument in the world.

She cut her palm, pressed it to the granite, and spoke her name twice — *Yara Lockridge, Yara Lockridge* — and felt the oath sink into the mountain the way water sinks into thirsty ground, felt it travel down and down through strata older than any kingdom, accepted, recorded, remembered. The stones remember names; she had learned that in the dark below, and now the stones held hers.

*Done,* said the god. And then, with what she could have sworn was wryness: *You drive a hard bargain, little merchant. The usurper never asked for anything. That was always his flaw.*

"He never loved anything he couldn't own," Yara said, wrapping her bleeding hand. "I've met his type. They die lonely."

* * *

They descended the rest of the true road together over two days, and the road itself helped.

That was the strangest part — stranger than the upward snow, stranger than the voices. Switchbacks that should have taken half a day unspooled in an hour. Rockfalls that had blocked the trail for years lay neatly tumbled aside, as if a patient hand had cleared them overnight. At a stream crossing where the bridge had rotted out before Yara was born, flat stones surfaced in the shallows, spaced exactly a stride apart, steaming faintly warm.

Cael walked most of it leaning on her shoulder, refusing — with the particular mulish dignity of the gravely injured — to be carried. But at the stone crossing he stopped, looked at the impossible stepping-stones, and laughed: a cracked, disbelieving sound that ended in a cough.

"They're making a pilgrimage of it," he said. "Look at this. They're laying the road out like a litany. Step, grace, step, grace." He shook his head slowly. "Four hundred years I studied their rites, and I never once saw them build anything. Only seal things. Bury things. Sleep." His hand found hers and gripped it, careful of her cut palm. "You taught a mountain range to say yes, Yara."

"I taught it to shop around," she said. "Don't make it poetry. I'll get sentimental and then who's going to carry the ledger."

The ledger rode in her pack, wrapped in oilcloth. Brenn's blood had dried brown on its cover, and she had not cleaned it and would not. Some debts you carried forward.

On the second evening they came down out of the high passes into the wide saddle above Thornfell village, and Yara stopped dead.

Every shrine on the slope was lit.

The little stone cairns, the carved waymarkers, the moss-eaten niches where travelers had left offerings since before the guild existed — each one held a flame. Not fire, quite. A light like the lamp her soul had been, gold-white and steady, burning without fuel in the open wind. And the villagers were coming up from the valley in twos and threes, silent, awed, leaving bread and wool and salt at the shrines' feet, and where the light touched the sick — Yara saw it happen, saw an old man with a walker straighten at a waymarker like a man setting down a burden he'd carried twenty years — where the light touched, things mended.

"They're answering already," Cael whispered. "The small prayers. Look how fast. They're *hungry* to answer."

"Good," Yara said, and meant it, and underneath the meaning it there was a splinter of something colder that she examined honestly and did not flinch from: hungry things that learn to feed well become hungrier still. Covenants held only while both sides kept counting. She would count for the rest of her life. She had, in fact, just sworn to.

Marette would have understood that. Marette, who had counted every coin and every soul and every year of a bargain's interest, and died owing one debt she'd spent her last breath trying to confess. Yara touched the ledger through the oilcloth and started down toward the lights.

* * *

The guildhall, when they reached it, had not burned.

She had braced herself for ash — Vaskal's reach, the vanguard's torches, some final spite from a dying court — but the hall stood whole against the darkening sky, and the door stood open, and lamplight spilled out onto the frozen mud of the yard.

Brenn sat in the doorway.

For one horrible heartbeat she thought he was a corpse propped there, another debt the world meant to hand her. Then he raised his head, and one eye found her — the other was bound with filthy linen — and he grinned through a beard stiff with old blood and newer frost.

"Took your time," he rasped. "Roads were clear. No excuse."

"Brenn—" Her voice failed entirely. She crossed the yard in five strides and knelt and gripped his shoulders, feeling the bones under the rags of him, the wounds beneath the wrappings, cataloguing damage the way she'd catalogue cargo: bad, bad, survivable, bad. "You're supposed to be dead. I watched you burn a page and fall down in a collapsing hell-court. I *carried* you partway. You weigh as much as a piano."

"Marette always said I was too mean to bury." He patted her arm with a hand missing two fingers. "Mountain gods owe you favors now, aye? Figured they'd not let me die on their new doorstep. Bad manners. First impression and all." His grin faded into something rawer. "The ledger. Tell me the page took. Tell me he never read the true road."

"He never read it. It's ash. The secret burned with you holding it." She took his ruined hand carefully in both of hers. "You kept the roads, Brenn. Right to the end. Marette would have—"

"Don't." His eye glistened. "Don't say it yet. Say it inside, by the fire, where I can pretend I'm not crying in front of the priest."

Inside, the hall smelled of woodsmoke and old cedar and, impossibly, of bread. The great hearth was lit. On the long table where the ledgers of six generations had been tallied, someone had set out cups — and standing at the hearth, feeding the fire, was a woman Yara had never seen, gray-haired and broad-shouldered, dressed in traveling wool gone shiny with years of weather.

The woman turned. Her eyes were the gray-green of glacial ice, and they held the same vast unhurried attention Yara had felt on the mountain shelf, scaled down, folded into a face.

"You swore in blood and name," the woman said. Her voice was the mountain's voice, but quiet now, domesticated to a kitchen. "So we sent a doorkeeper. Every covenant needs a doorkeeper. Call me what you like — Serra serves, if names must serve too." She nodded toward the fire. "Your hall sits on our oldest root. We will keep your hearth lit, keeper of the roads. Consider it the first payment on a very long account."

Yara stared at her. Then at Cael, who had gone very pale and very still, the way he did when theology was happening to him.

"That's generous," Yara said slowly, because in her experience generosity was the most expensive thing anyone could offer.

"It is fair," said Serra. "Which is better." She wiped her hands on her apron — an apron, on an avatar of an avalanche — and looked at Yara with something that in a mortal face Yara would have called kindness. "But fairness has a second clause, and you are a merchant, so you will want it spoken aloud rather than buried in the back of the contract. The gods keep one further claim. A small one. A priest, to stand between us and the towns, to teach the rites, to keep the covenant honest on both sides. Every covenant needs a priesthood, Yara Lockridge. And there is exactly one man alive who knows our old liturgy complete — who sang it to gentle a thousand waking souls in the dark, and whose voice the sleeping ones still remember."

The fire popped. Outside, the upward snow glowed along the peaks like a procession of lamps climbing forever.

Yara did not look at Cael. She kept her eyes on Serra and felt the splinter of cold turn in her chest, because she had known this was coming, had known it since the mountain shelf, had perhaps known it since the river that answered questions. Institutions ate people. Guilds, courts, churches — they all found the one person who belonged to them and sent the bill.

"We'll discuss it," she said. "Tomorrow. Terms get discussed. Nobody signs anything in a doorway."

Serra smiled, and the smile was four hundred years deep and entirely patient.

"Of course," she said. "Discuss it tonight, then. Over supper. Because he is already dreaming our dreams, keeper — and the gods, newly woken, have decided what they mean to offer him."
