## 20. What Cael Owes

Cael had stood before gods before. He had done it young, kneeling in the white cold of the high sanctuaries, when Vaskal still wore the shape of a merciful king and the litany came out of Cael's mouth like his own breath. He had thought, in those years, that he understood what it was to be in the presence of something vast. He had been wrong. What he had known then was architecture — the theater of divinity, incense and gold and a voice pitched to echo.

This was different.

The waking gods did not perform. They simply *were*, the way weather is, the way stone is, and standing among them felt less like an audience than like being held — gently, terribly, the way a mountain holds a seed it has decided to let grow.

They had gathered on the shoulders of Thornfell where the true road broke open into daylight: shapes of living granite and snowmelt and green fire, half-glimpsed even now, even awake, because mortal eyes could not hold them whole. The one who had spoken for the others called itself by no name but answered to *the First Woken*, and its voice came not through air but through the soles of Cael's boots, up through his bones, into the place behind his ribs where he had spent thirty years keeping accounts of what he owed.

*You,* it said, and the word landed on him like a hand laid flat on his chest. *Priest of the sealed doors. Singer of the litany. You woke us with mercy, and mercy was never your office.*

He knelt. It was reflex older than thought; his knees simply went, and the broken flagstones of the old shrine bit through his trousers, and he let them. Yara stood beside him — she had refused to kneel, of course she had, she would kneel for no one living or dead or newly divine — and he felt her glance down at him, sharp as a blade laid against his neck.

"Get up," she muttered. "You don't owe them that."

"I owe them everything," he said quietly.

"That's what I'm afraid of."

The First Woken turned its attention from Cael to her, and the temperature of the morning changed, the way a room changes when someone important enters it. Green fire moved beneath the god's stone skin like blood beneath frost.

*Lockridge woman. Soul twice bargained, twice reclaimed. You hold the covenant we have just made.*

"I hold it," Yara said. "And I'll hold you to it. Wild places for the gods, passes for the living. That was the deal."

*Mortals bargain well when they are desperate,* the god said, and there was something in the vast voice that might, in a smaller throat, have been amusement. *But this matter concerns the priest. We did not call you here for the covenant alone.*

Cael's stomach tightened. He had known this was coming. From the moment the vault shattered and the souls poured out like a breached dam, from the moment he opened his mouth and the old litany came out of him — not Vaskal's litany, not the sealing-song, but the *older* one, the waking-song, the one the first priests had sung before the usurper taught them to sing mountains shut — from that moment, he had known the gods would come looking for their singer.

He had hoped, with a coward's hope, that they'd forget.

*We offer restoration,* said the First Woken. *First Priest of the Waking. Voice at every shrine, hand at every consecration. The sanctuaries will be rebuilt. The high places will be filled. And you will stand in them as you were always meant to stand — not servant of a usurper, but herald of the true return. Every debt you carry will be transfigured into purpose. This is what we owe you, and what you owe us.*

The words settled over him like snowfall, soft and suffocating.

He could see it. That was the cruelty of it — he could see it perfectly. White sanctuaries rising out of ruin. Bells recast. His own voice trained again into the instrument it had once been, carrying across valleys, calling the faithful home. Purpose. Absolution. A life in which every terrible thing he had done became merely the prologue to something radiant.

Thirty years ago he would have wept with gratitude.

Now he looked up at the god — really looked, the way you look at a contract before signing — and saw what was written in the fine print of glory.

"And if I say yes," he said, "I stop being a man."

*You become more than—*

"No." He rose to his feet. His knees ached and his hands shook and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "Forgive me. I mean no disrespect. But I've been *more* than a man once already. I was Vaskal's priest, and *more* meant sealing the old gods under a mountain while they screamed. *More* meant teaching villages that suffering was holy. *More* meant watching people starve and calling it purification." He swallowed. "I did those things because I believed I was serving something greater than myself. I'm not going to make the same mistake wearing a different crown."

Silence. The wind moved along the ridge, and somewhere below, snowmelt ran silver down the new-made streams, and the god regarded him with an attention that should have crushed him and somehow did not.

Yara had gone very still beside him. He could feel her looking at him now not with suspicion but with something rawer — the look of a woman watching another person walk out onto ice she'd been afraid to test herself.

*Name your price, then,* the First Woken said at last. *Every soul who stands before us owes and is owed. What do you ask, priest?*

Cael turned to Yara.

It was, absurdly, the hardest thing he had done in a year of impossible things — harder than the descent, harder than the gatekeeper's toll, harder than singing three thousand screaming souls into gentleness while the world came apart around him. Because this he could not do with craft or courage. This he had to do naked.

"A mortal life," he said. "That's all. Let me grow old. Let my knees fail honestly instead of being made incorruptible. Let me owe debts I can actually pay — to her, mostly, which will take the rest of my life and won't cover it." His voice cracked on the last words and he let it crack. "I don't want absolution. Absolution is just another way of saying it didn't happen. I want to live with it. Beside her. If she'll have me."

The green fire in the god's veins flickered. And then — Cael would swear this until his dying day, which he now intended to make a long one — the vast stone face did something no god of Vaskal's court had ever done.

It smiled.

*A priest who refuses heaven,* it said. *Perhaps there is hope for worship after all.* The attention swung to Yara. *And you, Lockridge. We would restore your guild also. The halls rebuilt. The roads chartered under our protection. Your grandmother's name cleared, your debts struck from every ledger in the mountains. Say the word and the Lockridge Guild rises from its ashes as the first house of the new covenant.*

Cael watched Yara's face do the thing he knew it would do — the flinch, the hunger, the grief all fighting for the same ground. The guild. Her whole life had been the guild. Her grandmother's cough and Brenn's grumbling and the smell of lamp oil in the guildhall and the ledgers full of names nobody else remembered. Everything she had lost, offered back to her with divine interest.

She looked at the god for a long moment.

Then she looked at Cael.

"You absolute idiot," she said, and her voice broke in the middle of it, and she grabbed the front of his shirt with both hands. "You're going to spend your whole life paying me back? Fine. But you're not doing it as some glowing statue-voice on a mountaintop. You're doing it here. Where I can reach you. Where I can *yell* at you."

"Yara—" he started, and then her mouth found his, and whatever he had been about to say dissolved into the taste of salt and woodsmoke and the astonishing, ordinary fact of her.

When they broke apart, breathless, the First Woken made a sound like a glacier laughing.

*So it is witnessed,* the god rumbled. *Two mortals who choose each other over crowns. We have waited a long age to see it.* The great head inclined toward Yara. *Your guild stays dead, then. Its debts stay paid. But hear this, Lockridge: the covenant does not need a smuggler's guild. It needs wardens. Mortals who know the roads, who know the bargains, who know how to speak to things that answer prayers. Build what you build. We will not command it. We will only watch, and hope, and occasionally knock.*

"That sounds suspiciously like every traveler with an impossible request I've ever turned away," Yara said.

*Good,* said the god. *Then you'll know how to handle us.*

The green fire dimmed, folded inward, drew itself down into the stone of the mountain like a lamp being carried deeper into a house. The presence withdrew — not gone, never gone again, but *settled*, the way a great beast settles at the fireside of a home it has decided belongs to it too. The wind resumed. The sun, unremarkable and golden, continued to climb over the shoulder of Thornfell.

And then it was just the two of them on the broken shrine, the mountain breathing slowly beneath their feet, and Cael realized his legs had stopped holding him and sat down hard on the flagstones, laughing — or crying, possibly both, the distinction having collapsed sometime during the past week.

Yara sat down beside him. She didn't touch him, not yet. She just sat, shoulder almost touching shoulder, and pulled a strip of dried venison from her coat and tore it in half and handed him his share, because that was Yara: communion in the currency she trusted.

"The guild's really gone," she said after a while. Not bitter. Just stating it, the way you state the weather.

"Marette would have hated that."

"Marette would have hated it for a week and then renegotiated with the mountain inside a month." Yara chewed, stared out at the valley where the free cities glittered small and alive in the spring light. "Brenn died burning a page so Vaskal couldn't have the road. Grandma sold my soul to keep the roads open. Everyone I loved gave everything to the guild, and the guild's just... done." She was quiet a moment. "And I find I don't mind. Isn't that strange? I keep waiting to feel like a traitor and it doesn't come."

"It's not betrayal," Cael said. "It's inheritance. They gave everything so that something could survive. Turns out the thing that survived is you. That's how inheritance works — it changes shape every time it changes hands."

She snorted. "Listen to you. Priest to the last."

"Defrocked," he corrected. "Thoroughly, gloriously defrocked. Twice over, if we count this morning."

"Three times," Yara said. "I'm defrocking you myself later. There'll be a ceremony."

He laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, his first in longer than he could remember — and the sound went rolling down the slope toward the waking world, and nothing struck him dead for it.

They stayed on the mountain until the shadows grew long. Then they walked down together, not by any road marked on any map, but by the true road, the road nobody sells, which now belonged to everyone and no one — and as dusk came on, Cael saw, far below in the gathering blue, a light kindle in the ruined guildhall. One lamp in a dark window. Then a second.

Someone had gotten there ahead of them.

Smoke rose thin from the chimney. A horse stood tethered at the rail, travel-worn, saddlebags heavy. And in the doorway, silhouetted against the lamplight, a figure waited — hat in hand, road dust on its coat, the posture of a person who had come a very long way carrying something that could not wait for morning.

Yara stopped at the edge of the yard and Cael felt the change come over her, the old instinct waking, the smuggler reading a stranger's burden by lantern-light.

"Well," she said, and he heard the smile in her voice before he saw it. "Looks like the guild's open after all."
