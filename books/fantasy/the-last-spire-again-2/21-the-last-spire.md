## 21. The Last Spire

Spring came up the pass like a rumor that would not die.

Yara stood in the guildhall doorway with her hands wrapped around a mug of barley tea gone lukewarm, watching the snowline retreat up the Thornfells one gray shoulder at a time. Three weeks ago the peaks had been white to their teeth. Now the lower slopes showed through in patches of wet black stone and the first stubborn green — moss first, then the small yellow flowers nobody had bothered to name because nothing had ever grown there before. Nothing was supposed to grow there. The high passes were too cold, too thin of soil, too cursed by every story told about them.

The flowers grew anyway. They grew in the wheel ruts of the old smuggling road. They grew out of the mortar of the shrine Brenn had died carrying a ledger toward. And when Yara walked the road each morning — a habit she could not have explained and had stopped trying to — she noticed that they turned, very slightly, to follow her.

She had decided not to think about it. She was getting better at deciding not to think about things, which was either wisdom or surrender, and she suspected the mountain found the question funny.

Behind her, inside the hall, Cael was arguing with a door.

"You're holding it wrong," he said.

"I'm holding it exactly as it's been held for two hundred years."

"Two hundred years of holding it wrong, then." A scrape of wood on stone. "The hinge-side's warped. If you lift instead of push—"

The door swung open with a sound like a relieved sigh, and Cael stepped through into the morning light, sawdust clinging to his forearms, his hair tied back with a strip of red cloth that had once been a priest's sash and now served no purpose except that Yara liked seeing him wear it. He looked thinner than he had in the Undercourt. He looked older. There were lines around his eyes that had not been there in autumn, and some mornings he woke gasping from dreams of a vault full of lamps, and some mornings he did not wake at all until she shook him.

But he was alive. That word still felt like contraband. Something precious carried over a forbidden line.

"The door works," he announced.

"The door worked before."

"The door *grinded* before." He came to stand beside her, shoulder warm against hers, and followed her gaze up the pass. The wind off the snowfields smelled different this year. Everyone said so. Cleaner, somehow, and underneath it something green and vast and patient, like breath drawn through an open window after a long illness.

"Any more signs?" he asked quietly.

"Flowers turning to watch me walk by," Yara said. "A stream down in the low cut that runs uphill for ten paces and then thinks better of it. And last night I dreamed the whole mountain was a sleeping animal, and I was walking along its spine, and it knew my name." She drank the cold tea. "So. Normal spring."

Cael laughed — short and startled, the way he still laughed, as if amusement were a sin he kept relapsing into. "They're gentle," he said. "The dreams. That's how you know it's them and not him. Vaskal never let anyone sleep enough to dream."

*Them.* Six months ago the word would have meant corpses and stories. Now Yara said it too — *them, they, the waking ones* — and the grammar sat easier in her mouth each time, the way a new road sits easier under a mule's hooves with every crossing.

Down in the yard, the sound of hammering started up: Brenn's nephew Doss, who had come up from the border towns in the thaw with four others, all of them young, all of them hungry for work that meant something. They were rebuilding the south wall of the stable, where the mountain's exhale had cracked the stones like eggshell. Doss hammered the way Brenn had hammered — steady, unshowy, each blow placed like a coin counted out. Yara watched him and did not look away, because looking away had never once brought anyone back.

"Marette would have hated this," she said.

Cael glanced at her. "Would she?"

"Hated the flowers. Hated the singing stones. Hated the fact that we're—" Yara gestured vaguely at the hall behind them, at the changes. "Renovating."

"And yet," Cael said, "she sold her granddaughter's soul to keep this hall standing. Whatever Marette loved, it wasn't smuggling."

That landed the way truth always landed — flat, heavy, undeniable. Yara set down the mug on the doorpost and went inside.

* * *

The great room of the Lockridge guildhall had not changed much in two hundred years, which was the problem. It had been built by smugglers for smugglers: a long table scarred by knife-points and candle burns, ledgers stacked in a locked case against the north wall, a map of the Thornfell passes above the hearth with the false roads inked in bold and the true road marked only by a smudge of grease from someone's thumb, decades ago, deliberate as a signature. The founders had designed the room so that a stranger entering it would learn everything and understand nothing.

Yara had spent the winter learning to see the room as strangers now saw it.

Because strangers came now. That was the change no one had planned. Pilgrims, at first, in the early snows — ragged folk climbing the pass to leave offerings at the shrine where the god-flesh ran nearest the surface, leaving bread and buttons and, once, an entire wedding dress folded into the snow with terrible care. Then travelers with practical problems. A shepherd whose flock had walked into a whiteout and walked out again, every ewe accounted for, who wanted to know whom to thank. A widow from the free cities whose son had been conscripted into Vaskal's army and returned to her in the ashmelt with no memory of marching, only of "a song under the ground." Merchants asking whether the passes were safe to cross now that the mountains had opinions.

And always, sooner or later, the same question, asked with the shy greed of people who had heard rumors:

Is it true the Lockridges can carry word to the gods?

The honest answer was no. The truer answer was worse: the Lockridges could carry word *from* them, sometimes, when the mountain chose to speak through root and stone and running water — but what the old gods wanted, mostly, was not messages. What they wanted was what they had always wanted. To be remembered. To be met. To have their wild places left wild and their names spoken correctly, without flattery, without fear, the way you'd speak the name of a neighbor you intended to live beside for a long time.

Hence the covenant. Hence the renovations.

Doss and the others had cleared the smugglers' false ledgers out of the locked case and replaced them with a single book, bound in oiled leather, its first page written in Yara's own hand in the days after the bargaining:

*Terms sworn between the Waking Ones of the Thornfells and the Free Peoples of the Lowlands, witnessed beneath the Last Spire:*

*To the gods: the high places, the deep roots, the nameless valleys, the snows, the silence between prayers.*

*To mortals: the passes, the roads, the low meadows, the right to travel, trade, grieve, and go home.*

*Neither shall take the other's soul. Neither shall take the other's name. Both shall knock before entering.*

*Signed in lamp-light, in the court beneath, by Yara Lockridge; and in root and river and rolling stone, by those who woke.*

It was not law. No kingdom had ratified it; no kingdom knew it existed. It was older than law and younger than memory, and it held the way a well-made knot holds — not because anything forces it, but because both ends are pulled taut and neither side wants it to slip.

Below the terms page, the book had filled slowly through the winter with records of a kind no guild had ever kept. Not debts. *Meetings.* Where the gods had been seen or heard. What they asked for. What mortals owed and what had been paid. A shepherd in the eastern pass had heard the river-god complaining that fishers took without thanking; Yara's people had posted notice at the ford, and the complaints stopped. A landslide had buried the chapel road, and when the wardens sang the litany Cael remembered, the mountain shifted the slide aside — grudgingly, the way an uncle moves furniture he thinks you've arranged stupidly. Everything was negotiation now. Everything was relationship. Yara had grown up believing the world was made of cargo and contracts, and the joke of it, the bitter beautiful joke, was that the world was made of cargo and contracts after all — just with parties she'd never thought to include in the ledger.

She crossed to the hearth wall, below the old map, where two iron hooks had been driven into the stone.

On the first hook hung a ledger. Marette's ledger, its cover scorched where Brenn had burned the page with the true road, its remaining pages soft as cloth from handling. On the second hook hung Brenn's hammer, the handle worn to the shape of his grip, head bright where Doss had polished it but never dared swing it.

Guild tradition had names for such things. Dead partners' tools went into the storeroom; dead partners' debts went into the fire. You did not display your losses. Display invited bad luck the way spilled salt invited thirst.

Yara had hung them anyway, the week the snows began to break, and when Doss had asked — carefully, the way you ask about a wound — she had said: "New guild, new rules."

What she had not said: that Marette had spent sixty years keeping the hall's history hidden, and Brenn had spent sixty years keeping Marette upright, and if their names lived nowhere but in Yara's memory, then the mountain's long waking would eventually swallow even that. Stone remembered names — she had learned that in the Rootways, where the walls whispered back everything ever spoken against them. But stone needed someone to speak the names aloud first.

So every evening, last thing before the lamps went out, Yara stood at the hearth wall and spoke them.

She did it again tonight, after supper, when the hall had gone quiet and the fire had burned down to a red heart. Cael stayed by the table, pretending to read, giving her the privacy of his turned back, which was its own kind of tenderness.

"Marette Lockridge," she said to the wall. "Keeper of the roads. Sold a soul to save a guild, and saved a world by accident, and never once said sorry because she didn't believe in it. My grandmother. My debt."

The stones drank the words. Somewhere far under the floor, faint as a pulse, something listened.

"Brennan Ostrek. Called Brenn. Carried the ledger down a burning road with a spear through him and got it to the gate. Loyal past death, which sounds like a blessing and isn't. My teacher. My friend. The hammer stays sharp."

Silence, and then — she was almost sure, she was always almost sure — the faintest warmth from the hearthstone, like a hand laid briefly on the wall. Like acknowledgment. Like *received.*

She stood there a moment longer than usual. Then she banked the fire and went up to bed, and Cael blew out the lamp behind her without being asked.

* * *

Three days later, the first real traffic of the season came over the pass, and with it the test Yara had been dreading since midwinter.

She heard the mules before she saw them — the particular tired rhythm of animals that had climbed hard country — and stepped out into the yard to find six riders and a string of pack-beasts descending the switchback below the shrine. Not pilgrims. Pilgrims traveled light and looked up. These folk looked around, measuring sightlines, counting roofs, and the leader rode ahead of his own dust with the easy seat of a soldier.

Yara's hand found her knife out of pure habit. Beside her, Cael had gone very still, in the particular way he went still around anything wearing authority.

The riders reined up at the yard's edge. Their leader was fifty-odd, gray-shot, weathered, with a chain of office around his neck that Yara recognized from the free cities' council seals — silver links stamped with the five towers. An envoy. A real one, with real weight behind him, come all the way up into the mountains now that the mountains had become news.

"Lady Lockridge?" he called up.

"Warden Lockridge," Yara said. "There's no lady here."

Something flickered across his face — amusement, or its diplomatic cousin. "Envoy Serrec Halven, out of Corvess, bearing the council's commission." He dismounted and bowed, correctly, the depth of the bow calibrated to say *we have heard things about you and chosen to be polite.* "I'm told you speak for the powers of these peaks."

"No one speaks for them. That's rather the point of the arrangement." Yara came down the steps. Behind her, Doss and the others drifted out of the stables, hammers quiet, watching. "I speak *with* them. Occasionally. When they feel like being spoken with."

"Then perhaps," Halven said, and here his voice changed, lost its polish, gained something rawer, "you'll forgive me for climbing half the known world to ask in person. Because the councils have petitions. Dozens of them. Hundreds." He pulled a rolled document from his coat, thick as a fist, ribboned and sealed. "Cities that want the old gods' favor for their harvests. Towns that want protection sworn against whatever comes next. Lords who want to know—" his mouth twisted, "—who want to know if worship can be *bought*, Warden, the way everything else is bought. And beneath all of it, one request the council itself debated for nine days before sending me."

He held out the roll.

Yara did not take it. "Say it."

Halven looked up at the peaks, at the snowline retreating day by day, at the small yellow flowers turning in the yard like faces at a window. When he spoke again it was barely above the wind.

"They want to know if the gods will come down. Out of the mountains. Into the cities. Into the temples, the courts, the markets — into *civilization,* Warden. Because half the council believes the age of miracles has returned and we should seize it. And the other half believes that what woke under this mountain nearly ate the world once already, and will do it again the moment it gets hungry, and that you—" his eyes came back to hers, level, afraid, "—are the only person alive who has bargained with it and lived. So. Will they stay in their high places? Or should our cities prepare for a second conquest, gentler and holier, but a conquest all the same?"

The wind moved through the yard. The flowers turned. Far overhead, a sheet of snow let loose from the Last Spire itself — the highest peak, the one the old maps named and the new maps had begun naming again — and slid down the mountainside in a long white whisper, unhurried, like a sleeper turning over.

Yara looked at the sealed petition she had not taken. She thought about Marette's arithmetic — *one soul against one guild* — and about the covenant's first line, and about every hard bargain she had ever struck, and about the question underneath all questions, the one Halven was too careful to ask outright and too honest to hide:

*Are we safe from you?*

"We don't answer for the gods," she said at last. "But I'll carry the question up. Properly. With witnesses." She nodded at the ledger hanging inside the hall, at the book of meetings on its stand. "Come in, Envoy. It's a long climb and you'll want tea, and if the councils sent hundreds of petitions, we'd best start reading. The guild of three is dead." She turned for the door, and heard Cael rising behind her, and Doss and the others, the new blood, the inherited blood. "The wardens of Thornfell are just getting started."

Above the hall, high and clean in the spring air, the Last Spire caught the sun — and for one breath, unmistakably, the whole peak seemed to lean toward the valley, listening, the way a grandmother leans toward a child who has finally said something worth hearing.
