## 22. Prayers Answered, Roads Open

Spring came late to Thornfell that year, the way it always did — grudgingly, from below, the snowfields on the high shoulders of the mountain holding out like old men refusing to leave a warm room. But come it did. By the time the crocuses pushed through the mud of the courtyard in stubborn yellow fists, Yara Lockridge had learned to stop flinching at every sound the mountain made.

The mountain made a great many sounds now.

It breathed. That was the word Cael used, lying awake beside her some nights when the deep stone hummed beneath the guildhall's foundations like a struck bell too low for ordinary ears. *Breathing.* The old gods waking in their long beds of granite and glacier-root, stretching limbs no cartographer had ever charted, murmuring in their sleep-turned-waking the way any creature does when it has slept too long and dreamed too darkly. Some mornings the well water tasted of iron and lightning. Some evenings the sunset came up green along the eastern peaks, and the shepherds in the lower valley took off their hats without being told.

The guildhall had changed too. It had been a smuggler's den once — Marette's den, and her mother's before her, and whoever had first scratched the Lockridge mark into the lintel three generations back — all false walls and hidden ledgers and cargo mislabeled on purpose. Now the false walls stood open, their compartments swept clean, lined instead with candles that never guttered because the mountain liked them lit. The ledgers were new ones, bound in grey leather that Cael swore was not skin no matter how much he squinted at it, and the entries in them were not debts but prayers.

That was what the guild did now. It carried prayers up and down the true road, the road through the heart of Thornfell that Marette had sold her granddaughter's soul to keep secret and Brenn had burned his life to keep out of Vaskal's hands. The gods got their wild places — the high crags where nothing built by human hands would ever stand again, the valleys where the rivers answered questions if you asked politely, the caves where stones remembered names. Mortals kept the passes. And the Lockridge Guild, down to its last member and one disgraced priest who had asked for no restoration and been given something stranger instead, kept the door between.

Yara had hung two names in the great hall on the first morning of spring. Marette Lockridge, cut into oak in letters as plain and hard as her grandmother's arithmetic. Brenn Colvard beneath it, with the words *he carried what mattered* underneath, because Yara had insisted and Cael had agreed and there had been no one left to argue. Travelers coming through the hall paused sometimes to read them. Most of them didn't know the story. A few thought they did, and told it wrong in the way stories get told wrong — Vaskal a demon instead of a god who wanted to be the only one, Yara a warrior-queen instead of a woman with cold fingers and a smuggler's talent for finding the flaw in any lock, including the lock around her own heart.

She let them tell it wrong. Stories were cargo too, and cargo shifted in transit. What mattered was what arrived.

* * *

On the morning the traveler came, Yara was in the courtyard arguing with a goat.

The goat had belonged to a widow in the village below — the same village that had prayed to dead gods for medicine and received it impossibly, the first crack in the world Yara had ever noticed and been too practical to name. The widow had sent the goat up as an offering, which was a thing people did now, and the goat had opinions about being an offering. It had eaten half the herb bed Cael was trying to coax back to life, headbutted the covenant-stone twice, and now stood regarding Yara with the flat golden insolence of a creature that knew exactly whose mountain this really was.

"You," Yara said, "are not sacred. I don't care what the widow says."

The goat chewed. Somewhere under their feet, faint as a heartbeat through six fathoms of stone, the mountain chuckled. Yara felt it in her boots, that low amusement, and scowled at the ground.

"Don't encourage it."

"She has good instincts," said Cael from the doorway. He came out with dirt to his elbows and seedlings in a wooden tray, his hair tied back with a strip of the grey cloth he still wore though there was no temple left to defrock him from. He looked thinner than he had a year ago, and older, and easier. The lines around his eyes had softened into something that might, given time and enough springs, become laugh lines. His soul sat behind them quiet and whole and entirely his own — or theirs, depending on how you counted, since he had asked the gods for mortality and they had granted it the way mountains grant things: permanently, and with interest. "Everything on this mountain has good instincts. That's why everything on this mountain is terrified of you."

"Everyone should be terrified of me. It cuts down on nonsense." She grabbed the goat's collar and hauled it toward the far paddock, where the other offerings lived — three chickens, a very confused mule, and a beehive that had swarmed onto the covenant-stone in autumn and refused to be moved by anything short of divine intervention, which apparently it had received, because the bees were unnaturally calm and made honey that tasted faintly of starlight, which Cael said was impossible and then ate anyway. "And you shouldn't be encouraging me either. You're supposed to be my conscience."

"My conscience retired," Cael said. He set the tray down in the recovered corner of the herb bed and knelt in the mud without ceremony. "It filed a complaint about working conditions and went south for the winter. I've been making do with you."

Yara snorted, tied off the goat, and came back to stand over him. For a moment she just watched him work — the careful hands, the priest's patience transplanted without complaint into gardener's soil. A year ago she had forced him at knifepoint-adjacent stubbornness to guide her down into the Undercourt, and he had gone because his guilt weighed more than his fear, and somewhere between the river that answered questions and the vault where ten thousand lamps had burned in the dark, guilt had worn thin enough to see through, and what was underneath had turned out to be this: a man who planted things and sang litanies to soothe waking gods and looked at her as if she were a question he was glad not to have finished answering.

"Your hands are shaking," she said.

"They're always shaking. It's the cold. It's spring. It's the residual spiritual trauma." He glanced up, and the humor in his face was real but not complete; underneath it ran the thread that never fully left him, the memory of what he had helped Vaskal seal and what it had cost to unseal. It would never leave him entirely. She had stopped wishing it would. It was part of the freight. "Also, the mountain's restless today. You feel it?"

"I feel it." She crouched beside him, knees in the mud, uncaring. Below the courtyard, below the rootways, below even the hollow chapel where the descent gate had sealed itself into plain wall after the fall — she could feel the turning-over of something vast and slow and only half tame. The old gods honored the covenant. They honored it the way weather honors a promise: sincerely, until the day it inconveniences them. "Third time this week."

"The third time this week that it's been strong enough for you to notice." Cael pressed a seedling into the earth and patted the soil around it with a gentleness Yara had once watched him extend to a gatekeeper demanding his true name as toll — the gentleness of a man handling something that could destroy him and choosing trust anyway. "The northern peaks are arguing about something. The river won't say what. Rivers are terrible gossips except when it matters, and then they're maddeningly discreet."

"Sounds familiar."

"I have no idea what you mean." He brushed off his hands and looked at her, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because a year of almost-dying together teaches you when silence is the honest conversation. Then, softly: "You're worried."

"I'm a warden of the covenant. Worrying is in the charter." She stood, pulled him up after her, and held on to his muddy hand longer than strictly necessary. "But yes. Something's moving up north. And I'd rather know what before it comes down here wearing its Sunday voice."

As if summoned — and on this mountain, Yara had learned, things often were — the bell above the gate rang.

* * *

They had rehung the bell themselves in the first week. It was the old smuggling bell, cracked down one side, and Cael had offered to sing it whole, and Yara had refused. Cracked bells ring true, Marette used to say; they just ring honest. So the bell sounded slightly wrong, slightly broken, unmistakably itself, and everyone within earshot of the pass knew that when it rang, the Lockridge Guild was open for business.

One ring meant a traveler. Two meant trouble. Three meant the mountain wanted a word, and those days Yara mostly pretended not to hear.

It rang once.

Yara wiped her hands on her coat — the old coat, salt-stained and patched, because some garments earned their keep — and walked through the courtyard, past the covenant-stone humming quietly to its bees, past the lintel with the Lockridge mark scored deep and fresh-cut beside it the mark of the open road that the founders had never imagined, and into the great hall.

The traveler stood just inside the doors, dripping.

Not rain-drip. Spring-melt drip, the particular soaked misery of someone who had crossed the high passes in the last week of snowmelt, which no sane person did, because the melt-swollen fords killed experienced climbers every year and the safe crossings were weeks away yet. They were young — younger than Yara, barely more than a kid — wrapped in a shepherd's cloak gone grey with road-grime, and they stood in the middle of Yara's hall leaving an expanding puddle on her clean floor and looking at the two names on the wall with an expression she recognized from her own mirror.

Grief with its sleeves rolled up.

"Door was open," the traveler said, when they noticed her. Their voice cracked on the second word. "Sign said... the sign at the crossroads said the guild takes requests."

"We take requests." Yara crossed to the hearth and put the kettle on without asking whether they wanted tea, because the answer to that question was always yes, and hospitality was a muscle you kept or lost. "Sit down before you fall down. You crossed the ford at Grey Stone, didn't you. In melt season."

"It was the only crossing nobody was watching."

"Nobody was watching it because it kills people." She handed them a cup of tea strong enough to tan hides. They wrapped both hands around it and drank like a drowning man, and she waited, because she had been a smuggler for twenty years and the first rule of the trade was that cargo tells you what it is if you're patient enough.

The hall was quiet around them. Candlelight. The two names on the wall. Beneath the floorboards, so faint the traveler surely couldn't hear it, the mountain listened too. Yara felt it listening — felt the vast attention tilt toward her hall like a wolf turning one ear — and kept her face smooth.

"Name?" she said at last.

"Sef. Sef Andric." The kid swallowed. Looked at the fire instead of at her. "My sister's sick. Fever, since midwinter. The doctors in Lowmark can't name it. The temples can't cure it — the new temples, the Vaskal ones that got rebuilt after—" A quick, guilty glance up. Everyone knew what happened to Vaskal's rebuilt shrines when the mountain exhaled. "There aren't old temples anymore. Not ones with anyone in them."

"There's one here," Yara said. "Though the priest mostly grows herbs."

"And the medicine works," Sef said. "I know it does. The widow Marta's boy had the wasting and your medicine fixed him in a night. Everyone in the valley knows. That's why I'm here. That's why I crossed Grey Stone in melt." Their hands shook around the cup. "Except it isn't why. Not only."

Yara set down her own cup. "Say it."

Sef looked at her straight then, and there was something underneath the grief and the exhaustion that Yara knew intimately, that had driven her down a rootway into the court of a devouring god with a knife and a ledger and no plan worth the name. Desperation with a spine in it.

"When my sister sleeps now," Sef said, "she talks. In her fever. Words nobody taught her. Old words — the kind the grandmother songs use, the ones about the mountain having a name." The kid's voice dropped, went ragged. "Three nights ago she sat up in bed with her eyes closed and said, clear as church-bell, *the lamp is lit, send someone to the door.* And then she said the door has a mark on it." Sef reached into their cloak with a hand that trembled and drew out a scrap of birch bark, charcoal-scribbled in a child's fevered hand.

Yara took it.

She knew the mark before her eyes finished traveling its lines. She had known it her whole life, scratched on ledgers and lintels and the inside of her own eyelids: the Lockridge mark. Except this was not the mark as her family had cut it. This was the mark as it appeared on the covenant-stone, on the new ledgers bound in grey leather, on the gates of the passes where the waking gods kept their word — the smuggler's sigil grown a crown of small flames, the way the mountain had redrawn it the night the old gods rose and named the Lockridges wardens.

The kettle ticked on the hearth. Candles burned in their sconces without guttering. Under the floorboards, the mountain held its breath.

"That's our mark," Yara said quietly.

"I know whose it is. Everyone knows whose it is now. Half the valley thinks your guild are saints and the other half thinks you're witches, and my money's on both." Sef leaned forward, and the puddle around their boots spread, and their eyes were bright with fever-fear that wasn't even their own. "Here's the impossible part, mistress. Here's why I climbed Grey Stone in meltwater. My sister's fever breaks whenever anyone prays over her. Breaks clean, like a snapped branch. And the moment they stop — it comes back worse. Whatever's in her needs tending. Needs *keeping*. Like a flame." The kid's voice broke entirely. "She said someone lit a lamp in her. She said it's yours."

The word *lamp* landed in Yara's chest like a stone dropped down a well.

She had seen lamps. Ten thousand of them, burning in a vault beneath the world, souls kept as coin by a usurper who had wanted hers for a keystone. She had shattered that vault and flooded a court with freed light and sung-down grace, and she had carried a dying man up a road that no one sells, and in all the year since, she had told herself the lamps were free, all of them, every light loosed to go wherever souls go when they finally belong to themselves.

But the vault had been vast. And Vaskal had been thorough for a thousand years. And freedom, Yara knew better than anyone alive, was sometimes a door you had to walk through yourself, with help.

Behind her, she heard Cael come into the room. She didn't turn. She heard him stop when he saw the birch bark in her hand, heard the breath go out of him slow, heard the old litany start under his breath — three notes of it, the soothing cadence, the one he'd used to gentle a riot of the sleeping — before he caught himself and went silent.

"Mistress Lockridge," Sef said. "Will you take the job?"

Yara looked at the mark. At the two names on the wall, Marette and Brenn, watching her with oak-wood patience. At the cracked bell hanging in its frame, ringing honest. At Cael, when she finally turned — mud to the elbows, eyes already resigned to the fact that his quiet spring of planting things was over, mouth already curved despite everything into the shape of a man who had chosen this life knowing exactly what it cost and would choose it again.

The mountain hummed beneath the floor. Not chuckling, this time. Waiting.

Yara picked up her tea, drained it, and set the cup down with a smuggler's decisive click.

"Finish your tea," she said. "Then tell me everything your sister said, word for word, and don't leave out the parts that scared you. Especially those." She was already reaching for the new grey ledger, opening it to a clean page, dipping the pen. Her hands weren't shaking at all. "The Lockridge Guild takes requests. We'll need provisions, rope, and whatever your sister sleeps easiest under — and Sef?"

The kid looked up, hope and terror fighting openly across their face.

"Tell your sister," Yara said, writing the first entry of the next road, "*the wardens are coming.*"

Outside, the snowmelt roared down the pass, and under it, deeper than water, deeper than stone, something old and waking began — very softly, very distinctly — to hum along.
