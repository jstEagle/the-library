## 1. Charms for the Godless

The bell above Odile's stall had not rung in three days, and this was, on the whole, a good sign.

It was a small bell, tin, bought from a scrap merchant who swore it had once hung in a temple. Odile had filed the inscription off the clapper and now it rang with a flat, anonymous note—no blessing in it, no history, just the sound of commerce. In Corvane, that was the only kind of sound a person wanted to make.

Odile opened the stall at half past eight, as they did every morning, arranging the wares with the care of a jeweler and the conscience of a fraudster. Luck-knots, braided from gray market twine. Carved buttons shaped like acorns, "for prosperity." Little clay birds with their wings folded shut—"for patience," though Odile privately suspected they were mostly for people who couldn't afford real pets. Nothing here worked. That was the point. That was the law.

Corvane had outlawed magic eleven years ago, back when Odile was still a name attached to a sword attached to a god, and the city had been thorough about it. Not merely forbidden—*unwritten*. The Church of the Unwritten kept its censors in gray coats on every corner, men and women whose job was not to punish magic but to pretend it had never existed at all. They carried ledgers instead of swords. They struck words out of books, then struck the memory of the words out of the people who'd read them. It was, Odile had to admit, elegant work. They didn't burn witches anymore. They simply made it so that no one could remember there had ever been anything to burn.

Which left the charm-sellers. A whole gray economy of them, huddled under awnings along Ash Street, selling knots and buttons and folded paper cranes to a population that knew perfectly well none of it worked, and bought it anyway. Hope, it turned out, was legal so long as you sold it in a form that couldn't do anything.

Odile had gotten very good at selling nothing.

"Morning, Blackwood." Old Tess from the fish stall next door, arriving with her usual offering of gossip wrapped around a herring like a gift. "You hear about the Ferrow girl? Third house down? Says she dreamed her grandmother's name last night—a name nobody's spoken since the census."

Odile's hands did not pause over the luck-knots. This was important. Hands that paused were hands that cared, and hands that cared got noticed. "Dreams aren't against the law," they said mildly. "Yet."

"The censor came by asking after it." Tess leaned in, lowering her voice to the frequency reserved for dangerous subjects. "Halloway. You know him? Gray coat, little round spectacles, walks like he's counting the paving stones."

"I know the type."

"He's a strange one, that Halloway. Polite. *Too* polite. Asked the Ferrow girl what she remembered, and she said the dream, and he wrote it all down in his ledger, and then he told her thank you, and then—" Tess made a small erasing gesture in the air, a flick of the wrist, like brushing crumbs off a table. "Well. She doesn't remember telling him. Doesn't remember the dream either, I'd wager, by supper."

Odile nodded without nodding—the small motion of the head that acknowledged a statement while committing to nothing in it. It was one of perhaps forty such motions they had perfected over three years, an entire vocabulary of plausible deniability conducted from the neck up.

"And how's business?" Tess asked, retreating to safer ground.

"Dead," Odile said cheerfully. "Yours?"

"Rotten. The mackerel's gone off. Here—" and she pressed the offending herring into Odile's hands with the unstoppable generosity of the poor, "—you look thin."

Odile looked thin because Odile *was* thin, having spent the better part of three years perfecting the art of taking up less space in the world. It was a discipline. Eat less, speak less, want less, be less. Every year a little smaller, a little grayer, a little more like the stall itself: weathered, unremarkable, easy to walk past. There had been a time when Odile Blackwood could stand in a marketplace and every eye would find them—not because they were loud, but because sworn soldiers of Vess carried themselves a certain way, spine straight as a drawn blade, and the god's warmth sat visible under the skin like banked coals. People had stepped aside for that. People had *prayed* to that.

Now Odile slouched. Had taught themselves to slouch the way other people taught themselves instruments—daily, patiently, until it became second nature. Shoulders curled inward, chin tucked, gaze fixed on the middle distance where nothing interesting ever happened. The vow-marks that once blazed along both forearms like rivers of light had faded to something you'd miss unless you knew to look: faint warm lines, old scar-warmth, the ghost of a promise. On cold mornings they ached. Odile had learned to be grateful for the ache. An ache meant the bond still existed somewhere, even if the god on the other end of it had gone silent as a closed door.

*Three years*, thought Odile, wrapping another knot. Three years since the Last Spire sealed. Three years since the sky over the eastern border went blank, since the maps lost a country the way a book loses a page—cleanly, completely, no torn edges, no evidence anything had ever been printed there. Three years since Odile had stood on a hillside outside Corvane with two hundred other survivors and watched the horizon forget them.

Most of those two hundred were gone now. Scattered, renamed, dead, or worse—*edited*. The Church had a gentle word for what happened to people who remembered too much. They called it "correction." Odile called it what it was: being unwritten while still breathing. Your neighbors stopped knowing your face. Your own mother looked through you. Somewhere in the Church's gray ledgers, your name was struck through with a line so fine and final it might have been drawn by a spider with excellent penmanship.

So Odile had done the sensible thing. Odile had become boring.

* * *

Midmorning brought the first customer, a boy of maybe ten with his mother's shopping list crumpled in one fist and three coppers in the other.

"Do the knots really work?" he asked, in the way children ask—honestly, rudely, with no concept of the minefield beneath the question.

"They work exactly as much as you believe they do," Odile said, which was the standard answer, legally vetted by three years of surviving inspections. "And belief is free."

"So they don't work."

"They're decorative," Odile said, steering hard toward safety. "Very handsome. Matches your eyes."

The boy bought the knot anyway, because children always buy the knot anyway, and Odile made change with a smile calibrated to seem friendly without seeming memorable. This was the trick of it, the whole art of the thing: be pleasant enough to keep customers, dull enough to escape ledgers. Never let them remember your face the way you'd remember a story. Be the background. Be the awning. Be the gray twine.

By noon the sun had come around to warm the cobblestones, and Ash Street had filled with its usual theater of the harmless: Tess arguing with a customer about the freshness of fish that had died Tuesday; the knife-grinder spinning his wheel and singing songs whose lyrics changed every verse, possibly because he'd forgotten them, possibly because forgetting was contagious in Corvane; a pair of gray-coated inspectors strolling the far end of the street at the pace of men being paid by the hour.

Odile watched the inspectors the way sailors watch clouds. Not fear, exactly. Weather-awareness. The gray coats came down Ash Street twice a week on average, walking slowly, pausing at stalls at random, asking cheerful questions with no answers that weren't traps. *Good morning. How is trade? What do these charms do? Nothing? Wonderful. And where did you learn to braid so neatly?*

That last question was the hook. Odile had watched a knot-seller on Cordwainer Lane answer it too quickly, too precisely—"the pattern is traditional"—and had seen the inspector's spectacles catch the light as his pen moved. *Traditional.* Traditional according to whom? Traditions came from somewhere. Somewhere meant somewhere else. Somewhere else meant the east, and the east meant the erased country, and the erased country did not exist, and therefore neither, increasingly, did the knot-seller on Cordwainer Lane. His stall was reassigned within the month. Nobody spoke of him afterward, partly from fear and partly because nobody could quite recall him.

So when the inspectors reached Odile's end of the street, Odile was ready, in the way that a duelist is ready: relaxed, empty-handed, thinking of nothing.

"Afternoon." The taller one, a woman with a ledger under her arm and the patient expression of someone who has heard every lie in the city and found most of them adequate. "How is trade?"

"Slow," Odile said. "People are careful with their coppers."

"Aren't we all." She picked up a luck-knot, turned it over with two fingers. Her touch was light, professional, the touch of someone handling evidence or produce—it took Odile a moment to decide which. "Pretty work. Where do you source the twine?"

"The chandler's, on Mill Street."

"And the pattern?"

"Mixed," Odile said, and shrugged—one of the forty shrugs, this one meaning *I am a simple person with simple tastes and no history*. "I make some up. Whatever looks nice. It's decoration, mostly. People like something pretty to hang by the door."

"Mm." She set the knot down. Her partner, a young man whose gray coat still had creases in it from the shop, drifted his gaze across the wares with the thorough blankness of youth pretending to be experienced. "And business is honest? No... claims made?"

"Only claims about prettiness," Odile said. "I leave the rest to the buyer's imagination. Imagination's legal."

"For now," said the young inspector, with the smugness of a man who believed he'd just been witty, and his partner's mouth twitched in a way that suggested she'd heard the joke before, possibly from herself.

They moved on. Odile exhaled slowly through the nose, a controlled release, no visible sigh—sighs were memorable—and re-tied a knot that didn't need re-tying, because hands needed employment the way hearts needed denial.

Under the skin of both forearms, faint as breath on glass, the old marks warmed.

It happened sometimes, at odd moments. Never at prayer—Odile had stopped praying three years ago, or rather had stopped expecting answers, which amounted to the same religion. But in quiet moments, in the pause between customers, in the gray hour before sleep, the marks would stir with a heat like a hand resting on Odile's from underneath the table. *Still here,* the warmth said. Or perhaps: *still yours.* Or perhaps it said nothing at all and Odile's body was simply remembering a debt the way a missing limb remembers itching.

Vess, the silent god. The god of the sealed door. Once, Odile had knelt at the foot of the Last Spire with a blade flat against their tongue and sworn service in a language that predated the law, predated the Church, predated the maps that no longer showed the place where they knelt. Once, the god had answered—warmth, presence, the unmistakable pressure of being *known*. Now the god was behind a door that the world had agreed to forget, and the world was very good at agreeing.

Odile had made peace with it. That was the phrase they used, anyway, in the privacy of their own skull. *Made peace.* As if peace were a thing you made once, like a chair, instead of a thing you remade hourly, badly, out of whatever scraps were lying around.

* * *

The afternoon light went long and amber, and the street emptied by degrees, and Odile counted the day's take: nine coppers and a button. The button had been traded by a woman who swore it was brass and Odile was fairly sure was pewter, but trade was trade, and pride was for people with countries.

Tess packed up her fish beside them, humming something tuneless. "You ought to eat more," she said, apropos of nothing, the way she said everything. "You're all edges. A strong wind'll take you off to—"

She stopped. Odile felt the stop like a change in wind direction.

"To?" Odile asked lightly.

"Nowhere," Tess said, frowning at the middle distance, at the place where a sentence goes when the censor gets to it first. "Funny. I had a—never mind. Goodnight, Blackwood."

"Goodnight, Tess."

Odile watched her go and felt the familiar cold settle behind the sternum, the specific chill of watching erasure happen in real time, gently, to someone kind. Tess had been about to say *east*. Odile would have bet the whole stall on it. *A strong wind'll take you off to the east, back where you came from*—something like that, some small careless kindness, and the sentence had been plucked out of her mouth mid-word by machinery older and quieter than any censor's pen. The Church didn't need to arrest anyone. The city itself had been taught to flinch.

Odile packed the stall in the dark, folding the canvas with the economy of long practice, stacking the unsold knots in their wooden box. Nine coppers. Tomorrow there would be bread to buy, and coal if the price held, and the rent was due at the week's end, and the arithmetic never quite worked, and somehow it always worked, the way it does for people who have learned to need almost nothing.

The lamp in the shop window—Odile kept a room above a chandler's storeroom, cheap because the tallow smell never left your clothes—was lit by the time they climbed the stairs. Odile ate standing up, standing meals being another discipline of smallness, and stood a while at the window looking down at Ash Street as it emptied.

Below, the lamplighter made his rounds. Two doors down, the Ferrow house stood dark. Somewhere in the city, Magistrate Halloway was writing in his ledger, striking lines through names, and each struck line was a small death that no one attended.

Odile touched the faint warmth on their forearm, the way you'd touch a bruise to check whether it still hurt.

It did.

"Well," Odile said aloud, to the empty room, to the silent god, to no one. "Another day."

The words felt thin. Everything felt thin lately—the soup, the light, the self. Odile had whittled themselves down so carefully, so patiently, that some nights they wondered what was left at the center of all that carving. A mercenary with no war. A sworn soldier with a silent god. A citizen of a country that officially had never existed, living in a city that had outlawed the only language their heart still dreamed in.

They blew out the lamp and lay down in the dark, and the vow-marks ached their low, faithful ache, and Odile slept the way the careful sleep: lightly, facing the door.

* * *

It was near midnight when the storm arrived, rolling in off the western hills with the particular violence of autumn, rain hitting the roof tiles like thrown gravel. Odile woke to the noise and lay listening, cataloguing it out of habit—wind from the west, heavy rain, no thunder yet—which was when they heard the other sound.

A knock. Small. Fistsized. At the street door below, not the shop.

At midnight. In a storm. In Corvane, where nobody knocked at midnight unless they were dead, drunk, or sent.

Odile lay still for the count of thirty, doing the arithmetic every hidden person knows by heart. Answering doors killed people. Ignoring doors also killed people, occasionally, and more memorably. The knock came again—smaller, somehow. More desperate. Less like a demand than like the last of something.

Against every discipline Odile had spent three years carving into their bones, they lit the lamp and went down the stairs.

Through the pebble-glass pane of the street door, by lightning-light, Odile saw a shape no bigger than a child. Mud-caked, soaked black, swaying on its feet like a small tree in the wind.

Odile opened the door.

The child looked up. Rain ran off its chin. Its lips were blue, its eyes enormous, and when it opened its mouth, what came out was not a cry, not a plea, not any word of any language legally permitted inside the walls of Corvane.

It was Old Tongue. Fluent, urgent, unmistakable—the language of the Spire, of the oath, of the country that had never existed—and Odile understood every word of it without trying, the way you understand your own heartbeat.

*The door remembers me,* the child said. *Please. It's coming to erase me too.*

Behind the child, the storm raged. Under Odile's skin, the old marks flared hot for the first time in three years—bright, sudden, alive—and somewhere in the dark of the city, Odile knew, someone was already writing their name in a ledger with a very fine line through it.
