## 2. The Child at the Door

For three years, Odile Blackwood had made a religion of smallness.

Small stall. Small voice. Small life, folded up neat as a charm-knot and tucked where no inspector would think to look. It was, they had come to believe, the only honest prayer left to them: *make me forgettable*. And the city of Corvane, bless its gray heart, had answered. Nobody looked twice at the charm-seller on Cinder Lane who sold luck that was mostly string and hope that was mostly knots. That was the whole trick. You didn't hide by being invisible—you hid by being boring.

Which is why, when the child on their doorstep spoke the dead language of an erased kingdom in a voice like a struck bell, every careful year of Odile's life collapsed at once.

*The door remembers me,* the child had said. *Please. It's coming to erase me too.*

And Odile had understood it. Not translated it—understood it, the way you understand your own name, your own heartbeat, the ache in a scar when rain comes. The words went straight past the part of the brain that handles language and landed somewhere older, somewhere Odile had boarded up and painted over and told everyone, including themselves, was a load-bearing wall.

"No," Odile said aloud, to the storm, to the child, to whatever god was listening out of spite. "No, absolutely not."

The child blinked up at them through the rain. Mud to the eyebrows. Bare feet blue with cold. In one small fist, clutched so tight the knuckles had gone white, was a shard of black glass no longer than a finger—and even soaked, even shivering, even half-dead with fear, the shard drank the lamplight and gave nothing back.

Odile knew that glass. You didn't forget the color of the Last Spire. You didn't forget anything about it, which was rather the problem.

"Inside," Odile heard themselves say, because three years of self-erasure apparently hadn't survived contact with a freezing child. "Before someone sees you. Quickly."

The child bolted past them into the shop like water finding a crack, and Odile stood one moment longer in the doorway, looking out at the storm as if it might apologize, or explain itself. It did neither. Thunder rolled down the Spireward hills—the real hills, not the ones on any map—and somewhere under Odile's skin, the old oath-marks flared hot for the first time in three years, bright and sudden and alive, like embers someone had breathed on after swearing they were cold.

Odile shut the door. Bolted it. Then, feeling ridiculous and doing it anyway, dragged the luck-charm rack in front of it, which would stop precisely no one but made a fine statement of intent.

* * *

The shop—if you were generous—was one room: a counter, a cot behind a curtain, a stove that sulked, and shelves of charms arranged by how much trouble each one would cause if inspected. Row one: harmless. Knotted cords for luck in love, luck in dice, luck in cows. Row two: technically harmless. Charms that kept bread fresh a day longer than bread had any right to keep. Row three, hidden behind a false panel and a decoy shelf of "souvenirs": nothing at all, because Odile had burned everything in row three the week they'd opened the shop, and kept the panel anyway, as a kind of memorial to common sense.

The child stood dripping in the middle of the floor, leaving a puddle shaped vaguely like a country Odile refused to look at too closely.

"Right," Odile said, in Corvani, the safe tongue, the legal tongue. "Let's establish some rules. Rule one: you don't speak. Not here, not anywhere, not a word of—not *that language*, do you understand me? Rule two—"

The child said something in Old Tongue. Softly. Apologetically, if a language could apologize.

"I said rule one—" Odile stopped. Pressed two fingers to the bridge of their nose. "You don't understand Corvani, do you."

Headshake. Small. Miserable.

"Of course you don't. Why would you? No one speaks it. No one's spoken it since—" Since the fall. Since Vess went silent and the kingdom went with him, unwritten out of every ledger and map and memory the Church could reach, until the whole country was a hole shaped like home. "Wonderful. I'm arguing rules with a child in a language the law says doesn't exist."

The child watched this tirade with the enormous, unblinking patience of the very young and very frightened, and then held up the black shard, offering it. An explanation. Or a plea. Or possibly, Odile thought wildly, a trade; children bartered in whatever they had.

"Don't—" Odile's hand came up before they'd decided to move. They did not touch it. They wanted to touch it, which was exactly why they wouldn't. The oath-marks along their forearm were warm as a fever, and Odile knew, with the flat certainty of old soldiers recognizing old sounds, that the warmth wasn't theirs. Something on the far side of that glass was leaning against the door of it, listening. "*Don't* hold that up to the light. Don't set it down near the stove. Don't do anything with it except keep it wrapped and quiet." A pause. "Please," they added, in Old Tongue, because apparently rules one through three had already died.

The child's face changed—lit, actually, the way a window lights when someone inside finally comes to the door—and answered in a rush of that liquid, falling-bright speech. Too fast. Odile caught maybe one word in four, and hated how much of them woke up just hearing it.

"Slowly," Odile said. "Slowly. My—my Old Tongue is rusty." It wasn't. That was the lie they'd be telling all night, probably. Their Old Tongue was sitting right there under their skin, fully armed, waiting.

The child tried again, slower, pointing at the shard, then at the door, then—at Odile, with both muddy hands framing something in the air. Tall. A shape. A spire.

"You came *from* there?" Odile asked carefully. "From the Spire?"

The child nodded, then hesitated, then made the gesture again, smaller, and shook their head, and Odile got the strange, sliding sense that the answer was somehow both yes and no, and that the truth of it was worse than either. Fine. Fine. Interrogation could wait until the child wasn't vibrating with cold.

"Bath first. Questions later." Odile filled the basin from the ewer, found the cleanest rag they owned—a low bar, that rag, but standards mattered—and pointed. "Wash. There. Clothes—" They dug through the chest and came up with a shirt that had fit them at fourteen, which on this child would hang like a tent. It would do. "And *that* goes in the box." They indicated the iron-bound strongbox under the counter, empty these three years, holding nothing but a false bottom and old regrets. "Wrapped in wool. Where I can see it."

The child considered the box. Considered Odile. Then, with the grave deliberation of someone signing a treaty, wrapped the shard in the offered cloth and placed it inside, and Odile shut the lid and felt the marks on their arm cool by a single degree, which was its own kind of answer, and one they did not enjoy reading.

* * *

Later—much later, with the child asleep in the cot behind the curtain, breathing in the whistling, hiccupping rhythm of the finally-safe—Odile sat at the counter with a cup of chicory gone cold and did the arithmetic they'd been avoiding since the door opened.

Item: a child speaking fluent Old Tongue, in a city where the Church of the Unwritten paid silver for reports of "unlicensed speech," and worse than silver for the speakers.

Item: a shard of Spire-glass, which was not supposed to exist outside the Spire, because the Spire was sealed, because sealing it had been the point, the whole point, the last act of a dying god's last sworn servant.

Item: the marks. Three years of quiet, and tonight they'd flared like a signal fire. Which meant either the child had woken them—or the shard had—or the thing on the other side of the door had noticed, at long last, that someone had left a window open.

Odile turned their wrist in the lamplight. Along the inner forearm, faint as watermarks, the oath-script lay coiled: words sworn at nineteen in a shrine of black glass, in a country that had been real on a Tuesday. *My hands, my blade, my silence, yours.* Everyone sworn to Vess wore them. Most of the others were dust now, or scattered, or—like Odile—doing their best impression of furniture in cities that hated what they'd been.

"We had a deal," Odile murmured to the marks. To the god, if gods bothered eavesdropping on charm-sellers. "I hid. You slept. Everybody wins."

The marks said nothing. But they stayed warm, and warmth, from Vess, had always been a kind of sentence—one word, and never the one you wanted.

From behind the curtain came a sound: the child, talking in their sleep. Low, urgent, in that bright falling language—and here was the thing that turned Odile's blood to well-water, the detail they'd be chewing on at dawn and every dawn after: the frost. As the child dreamed, frost was blooming on the inside of the window glass above the cot. Feathering out in spirals and long deliberate strokes. Shaped, if you squinted, like writing.

Old Tongue, written in winter on the inside of a window in Corvane, in a room with a sealed strongbox and a sleeping key.

Odile sat very still and read it. They couldn't have stopped themselves; the reading happened the way breathing happens. Four words. Simple ones. The kind of thing a parent writes on a slate for a child learning letters.

*It knows where you are.*

"Right," Odile said to the empty shop, in the tone of a woman revising her entire week. "New plan."

There was no new plan. There was, however, half a wheel of hard cheese, a knapsack with a false bottom, a comrade named Bren who owed them a favor and ran maps to places that weren't on maps, and approximately six hours before the morning inspectors made their rounds of Cinder Lane. Odile ate the cheese, because decisions are best made fed, and listened to the storm trying the shutters like a debt collector.

Behind the curtain, the child slept on, and the frost-words slowly melted, running down the glass like the window itself was weeping, and Odile thought—with a weariness that had a strange, stubborn warmth buried at the bottom of it, the way a hearth keeps a coal under ash—*three years. I gave it three good years. I was starting to be nobody. I was almost *good* at it.*

Then the knock came. Not at the shop door—at the shutter. Two raps, a pause, two more. A rhythm Odile knew, from another life, from walls and watches and nights that smelled of woodsmoke and fear: *friend at the gate, friend in the dark.*

Nobody in Corvane knocked like that. Nobody in Corvane *could*.

Odile blew out the lamp, took the carving knife from beside the cheese—it had never carved anything but cheese, but courage is where you find it—and crossed the dark room, one eye on the curtain, one ear on the child's sleeping breath.

Through the shutter's seam, rain-lashed and grinning like sin, said a voice Odile had last heard across a burning ford:

"Blackwood. Open up. You're not going to believe whose name I saw on a Church list today."
