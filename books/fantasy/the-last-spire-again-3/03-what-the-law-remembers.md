## 3. What the Law Remembers

There are three ways to know when the Church has come to Corvane's charm-quarter, and Odile had learned all of them the hard way.

The first is the bells. The cathedral rings its hours in bronze, but the censors travel with a small hand-bell of white iron that makes a sound like a spoon tapped against a tooth. You feel it in your fillings before you hear it with your ears.

The second is the dogs go quiet. Not gone—quiet. They sit on their thresholds and watch the street with the particular patience of animals who have decided that whatever is coming down the cobbles is not their problem.

The third is Mrs. Ferrow stops gossiping. This was, historically, the most alarming sign of all.

Odile noticed all three within the space of a minute on a grey morning three days after the child arrived, and swore with a fluency they hadn't permitted themselves since leaving Vess's service.

"Under," they said to Sef, who was sitting cross-legged on the counter sorting luck-knots by color with the grave seriousness of a general reviewing troops. "Back room. Under the floorboard. You remember."

Sef remembered. The child went without argument, which was itself unnerving—most children Odile had known argued as a matter of principle—and the floorboard settled back into place over a hollow that smelled of dust and old tallow, just deep enough for a small body and shallow enough to pass casual inspection, because Odile had dug it the second night, before even learning the child's name.

Three years of hiding had taught Odile this much: dig first, ask questions later. Or never.

The white-iron bell rang at the end of Ash Street.

* * *

Magistrate Halloway was not what Odile expected, which was annoying, because Odile prided themselves on expecting everything.

Censors were supposed to be grey men. Grey robes, grey faces, grey voices reciting grey law until your soul filed itself down to fit inside the regulations. Odile had met dozens. They blurred together like rain.

Halloway came up the street in a good coat the color of strong tea, hatless despite the damp, with his hands clasped behind his back like a man strolling through his own garden. He was perhaps fifty, with a scholar's stoop and a face built for disappointment—the kind of face that made you want to apologize before he'd said anything. Two wardens flanked him at a respectful distance, looking bored, which meant they were dangerous. Bored wardens were the ones watching everything.

He stopped at Odile's stall, looked at the luck-knots, the weather-charms, the little braided cords guaranteed—within strictly non-miraculous limits—to keep moths out of wool, and smiled the smile of a man reading a very familiar book.

"Master... Blackwood, is it?"

"It is." Odile kept their hands visible and their voice pleasant. Shopkeeper's voice. Harmless voice. They'd worn it so long it fit better than their skin.

"You have a license."

"I do." Odile produced it from the box under the counter before being asked. Third drawer down, beneath the moth-charms, where a license belonged: technically legal, spiritually apologetic. Halloway read it with the unhurried attention of a man who had all morning and intended to use every minute of it.

"Charm-seller, third class. No divination, no invocation, no unlicensed speech." He looked up. "You understand the last one?"

"Perfectly, magistrate. I sell knots. Knots don't talk."

"Some do, apparently." Halloway set the license down and aligned its corner with the edge of the counter, a small fastidious gesture that Odile distrusted immediately. "We've had reports. From this street. Of speech in an unknown tongue. Unlicensed speech carries penalties regardless of the speaker's intent—you'll know the law."

"I know people say a lot of things about this street," Odile said. "Half of Ash Street talks in tongues after the third drink at the Drowned Bell. Last month Widow Prentiss swore the fishmonger addressed her in High Liturgical. Turned out he was drunk and she was deaf and both were insulted by the suggestion."

Something flickered at the corner of Halloway's mouth. It might have been the ghost of amusement; it might have been indigestion. With censors you could never be sure, and Odile had learned not to bet on either.

"And yet," Halloway said, "the report specified a child."

Odile's heart performed a slow, deliberate maneuver, the way a cart wheel does when the axle is thinking about failing. They kept the shopkeeper's face on. Three years of practice. Invisibility was an art, and Odile was, if nothing else, an artist.

"There's no shortage of children on Ash Street. There's the Cobb twins, who scream in what I'd generously call language. There's Tam Ostrey, who sings. Badly. If bad singing were a crime, half the quarter would be in chains."

"The report described a child alone. Mud to the knees. Carrying something dark." Halloway's eyes were pale grey and did not blink often enough. "Seen during the storm, three nights past."

"Frightful night," Odile agreed. "I boarded my shutters and didn't open them till morning. I'd have seen nothing but lightning, and I'm told even that's illegal now if it speaks."

That got the flicker again, stronger this time. Halloway turned it into a cough.

"Master Blackwood. May I look at your back room?"

And there it was. Odile felt the question settle over the shop like frost forming—not the dramatic kind, the creeping kind, the kind that gets into mortar and splits stone by winter's end.

"Back room's full of mold and my laundry," Odile said. "You're welcome to inspect it, though I warn you the smell has been reported to the city twice already, once by me. But I'll need to see paper authorizing entry to private premises. My cousin's a scrivener at the Hall of Records, magistrate, and he does love his forms filled out properly. He once rejected a tax filing over an ink blot. Took the man three months to recover."

It was a lie. Odile had no cousin anywhere. But the beauty of inventing a scrivener relative was that everyone believed it, because everyone had met that person, and nobody wanted to deal with them.

Halloway studied Odile for a long moment. Under the counter, hidden from view, Odile's hand rested flat against the wood, and beneath the wood, beneath the floor, a small heartbeat kept time with their own.

"No," Halloway said at last. "No need today. The forms can wait." He straightened his coat. "But I'll tell you what I tell every honest trader on this street, Master Blackwood, because I believe you're honest, and honesty deserves warning rather than traps."

"Go on."

"The Church doesn't fear magic." He said it quietly, almost kindly. "Magic burns out. Magic exhausts itself, like any fire. What the Church fears is words. A word can be taught. A word survives the person who spoke it. A word passed mouth to mouth down forty years becomes a country again, and countries—" his gaze drifted down Ash Street, at the shutters, at the silent dogs, at the windows where ordinary frost was already gathering in the ordinary way of ordinary mornings— "countries are very hard to burn twice."

He put on his gloves.

"If such a child comes to your door, Master Blackwood—and children do come to doors, it's practically their profession—I trust you'll do the lawful thing."

"Lawful things are my entire inventory," Odile said.

Halloway nodded, satisfied or pretending to be, and moved on down the street with his bored wardens trailing behind him and the white-iron bell ringing softly, softly, like a spoon against a tooth, like winter counting its money.

* * *

Odile waited until the bell faded, then until Mrs. Ferrow resumed gossiping audibly two doors down—a sound like a dam breaking—and then lifted the floorboard.

Sef looked up at them from the dark, calm as a stone at the bottom of a well. Children who hid well had learned hiding somewhere. That thought joined the queue of thoughts Odile was refusing to examine.

"He's gone," Odile said. "For now."

Sef climbed out, brushed dust off their knees with great dignity, and then pointed at the window.

Odile followed the pointing finger and felt the cold arrive a beat behind it.

Frost covered the inside of the glass. Not outside—in. It climbed the pane in delicate feathered whorls, spreading outward from a single point at the center, and the pattern it made was not the random lacework of weather. Weather did not repeat itself. Weather did not make the same shape twice.

This shape had been made before. Odile knew it the way you know your own handwriting: spiraling inward, branch upon branch, narrowing toward a point—

A spire. Frost, drawing a spire on the inside of Odile's window, in a room where the only other occupant was a mute child who had been under the floorboards since before the magistrate reached the street.

"Sef." Odile crouched to the child's level, keeping their voice light, the way you speak near a frightened animal or a lit fuse. "Did you say anything? Down there? Even to yourself?"

Sef shook their head slowly. Then hesitated, and held up one finger—one moment—and touched their own lips, and made a small motion, breathing out.

Not speaking. Almost speaking. Thinking too loudly, maybe. Dreaming with their mouth closed.

The frost began, gently, to melt. Odile watched it go, and the spire-shard in Sef's pocket—if it warmed, neither of them mentioned it, and Odile was becoming an expert at the things neither of them mentioned.

"All right," Odile said. "New rule. When the bell rings, you don't just hide. You sleep. Real sleep, if you can manage it, or the nearest thing. No dreaming out loud."

Sef looked at them with those old, old eyes in that young, young face, and nodded, and Odile understood with terrible clarity that the child understood perfectly—that Sef knew exactly what they were and what they leaked and how much danger it made for the person sheltering them—and that the child would carry that knowledge silently, the way children carry everything, in the place where grown-ups don't think to look.

Which was somehow worse than frost.

* * *

That evening Odile did what Odile always did with problems too large to solve: made soup.

Soup was strategy. Soup bought thinking-time. And besides, there were two mouths now, a fact the larder had begun registering with visible alarm. Odile chopped onions with the efficiency of someone who had once field-dressed wounds by torchlight and now applied the same skills to root vegetables, and tried to assemble the day into sense.

Halloway had come for the child. Fine. Expected. The Church always came eventually; that was less a policy than a law of nature, like water finding cracks. What bothered Odile was the *how*. Reports of unlicensed speech were common enough, but Halloway hadn't canvassed like a man chasing a rumor. He'd canvassed like a man following instructions. Specific ones. *Mud to the knees. Carrying something dark.* Somebody had seen Sef arrive in the storm. Somebody had talked.

Or somebody had known already, and the reports were theater.

Neither possibility improved the mood, so Odile salted the soup and refused both.

They fed Sef, who ate with the focused gratitude of a child who had known hunger recently enough to respect food, and afterward the child sat by the banked fire turning the black glass shard over in their fingers, and Odile sat across the room sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening, and between them stretched the silence of two people conducting an entire negotiation without a shared word.

Except they did share a word. One.

"Say it again," Odile said, before they could stop themselves.

Sef looked up.

"Your name. Say it again. Quietly."

Sef considered this for a long moment, head tilted. Then, barely above breath, they said it—that name in the Old Tongue, two syllables like a door opening and closing, like a place remembered—and the fire leaned toward the sound, and the window fogged at its edges, and Odile's oath-mark ached beneath the wrappings on their forearm with a dull, familiar grief, the ache of a vow made to a god who had gone silent three years ago mid-word, as if interrupted.

As if, Odile thought for the thousandth time, *called away.*

"Thank you," they said gruffly, and went back to the knife.

Later, banking the fire, laying out bedding—the child took the cot, Odile the floor, and the argument over this had been conducted entirely in gestures and lost entirely by Odile—Odile lay awake listening to Sef breathe and doing arithmetic they hated.

Hiding a past was easy. A past fit in a locked box under the floor, next to the tallow. You could starve a past; it asked nothing of you.

But a child needed feeding. Needed washing, mending, teaching, walking to places without being seen. A child left footprints in mud and smells in blankets and frost on windows, and grew louder and taller and more visible by the day, and sooner or later some neighbor—kind, curious Mrs. Ferrow, probably, whose kindness was functionally indistinguishable from surveillance—was going to notice that the charm-seller had acquired a shadow.

Hiding a person was a debt that compounded daily.

And underneath the arithmetic, quieter and worse, ran the other current, the one Odile kept trying to drain and couldn't: the shard was warming. Not much. A degree, maybe, since the storm. But warmth traveled in one direction here, and Odile knew where it led. Everything warm in this story led to the Spire, and everything that led to the Spire demanded payment, and Odile had sworn three years ago—over ash, over silence, over the bodies of everyone they'd failed to save—that they would never stand at that door again.

The knife was sharp. The knife had been sharp for an hour.

Outside, far off toward the cathedral, the watch bells rang midnight, and beneath their bronze voices—so faint Odile might have imagined it, except Odile had stopped trusting their imagination years ago and started trusting their instincts instead—the white-iron bell rang too. Once. Twice. Still out there, working the far streets in the dark, patient as winter.

In the cot, Sef murmured something in sleep. The window bloomed, briefly, with feathers of frost, and melted, and Odile lay in the dark watching it happen and understanding, finally, that this could not hold.

Not because the Church would find them.

Because the Church was already looking elsewhere—and whatever was calling the child eastward, toward the borderlands, toward the country that officially did not exist, was calling more loudly every night, and frost was only its whisper.

Three streets away, a dog began to bark. Then another. Then, all along Ash Street, every dog at once, barking at nothing anyone could see, and Odile sat up in the dark with the shard's warmth prickling faintly through the floorboards like a pulse, and reached for their boots.
