## 4. Old Tongue, Older Debt

The child slept in my bed and I slept in the chair by the window, which was backwards from every sensible arrangement, but I had learned long ago that sensibility was a luxury for people whose gods stayed put.

I kept the lamp low. Every time I looked at the sleeping shape under my spare blanket — mud still crusted at the hem, one fist curled around that shard of black glass like it was the last true thing in the world — I heard the language again. *Vess kaelen tor.* Three words, small and cracked with cold, and my whole body had answered them before my mind caught up. That's what Old Tongue did. It didn't travel through your ears. It went straight down through the floor of you, into whatever cellar you kept the things you'd sworn.

Three years since anyone had spoken it aloud to me. Three years since Vess's final silence, since the Spire closed its eye and took a kingdom with it.

And now there was a child in my bed who spoke the dead tongue as if it were milk-warm.

* * *

You don't forget the day you swear. The Church would have you believe oaths are ink and parchment, signatures and seals. But an oath to a god is a wound that heals around something. You carry the shape of it forever.

I was nineteen when I climbed the Last Spire. Nineteen and all elbows and certainty, one of forty recruits brought up the mountain in the last autumn of the kingdom of Vel Sarenne — though we didn't call it erased then. We called it home, because nobody had thought yet to imagine losing it.

The Spire didn't look like a door. It looked like a needle of black glass taller than any tower in Corvane, standing alone on the shoulder of the mountain where nothing grew, and the wind around it made a sound I can only describe as listening. The priests said Vess lived in that sound. Vess, our silent god, the Quiet One, the god of soldiers and midwives and everyone else whose work happened between heartbeats. Other gods demanded songs and burnt offerings and public weeping. Vess asked only this: that you keep watch over the small mercies. That you stand between harm and the harmable, and do it without applause.

Forty of us knelt on the frost-bitten stone. Forty of us pressed our palms flat against the Spire's base, and it was warm. Not sun-warm. Blood-warm. Skin-warm, like pressing your hand against the chest of someone sleeping.

The oath wasn't words. That surprised me. I'd rehearsed the liturgy for a year — the old formulas, the responses, the whole architecture of devotion — and none of it mattered. What mattered was that moment when the warmth came up through your palm and asked, without language, *will you be a door for mercy even when no one opens you?*

And you answered yes. However you answered it. Some wept. Some laughed. Old Sergeant Corvel, bless his crooked heart, simply nodded once, the way he did at everything, including artillery.

The mark appeared where your hand had rested: a thin line across the base of the palm, silver-grey, like an old scar that hadn't happened yet. The vow-mark. It never hurt — not exactly. It *ached*. The way a healed bone aches before rain. The way an empty chair aches at supper.

Mine ached for three years now, steady as a pulse.

* * *

The child stirred and murmured something, and I caught myself leaning forward like a fool, hungry for it — the vowels, the soft falling ends, the way Old Tongue rolled its r's like a stone down a well.

*Vess kaelen tor,* they'd said on my threshold. *God-house open.* Except *kaelen* isn't just "house." It's house-the-way-a-hand-is-a-house-for-a-finger. It's shelter-with-intention. There is no clean translation because Old Tongue doesn't translate; it *reminds*. It reminds you of things you didn't know you remembered.

I'd spent three years being very good at not remembering. I'd built a life out of not-remembering the way a mason builds a wall, course by course, careful as prayer. Charm-seller. Harmless. Luck-knots and traveler's blessings and little braided cords that worked about as well as wishful thinking, which is to say: sometimes, if you squinted. The Church inspectors came through Ash Street twice a month and found nothing worth writing down, which was the finest compliment a magic-outlaw city could pay me.

But the vow-mark didn't care about walls. When the child spoke, it had flared hot enough that I'd had to bite my cheek to keep quiet.

Duty, it turns out, doesn't end. It just waits.

* * *

Here is what I remembered about the last day, and I want you to understand that I remembered it every single night whether I wanted to or not, usually somewhere between the second and third hour of lying awake:

The silence came at dusk.

We felt it in the mark first — all of us, the whole garrison at the mountain foot, four hundred sworn soldiers touching their palms at once without a word passing between them, because there was no word for it. The ache stopped. That sounds like relief. It was the opposite. It was like a heartbeat stopping. For three years the mark had been a thread pulled taut between me and something vast and patient, and in one dusk-hour the thread went slack.

Then the horizon changed. I know how that sounds. But ask any survivor of the fall and they'll say the same strange thing: the sky over Vel Sarenne went *blank*, like a page turned face-down. The mountains were still there. The valleys were still there. But looking at them was like looking at a room after the furniture's been carried out — the shape of everything remained, and the meaning had been removed.

The Unwritten had come. White-robed censors of the Church of the Unwritten, carrying no weapons that bled, walking the border of the kingdom and *unmaking the record of it*. Not the land. The *record*. Every map, every ledger, every memory outside the border, softened and rewritten until the world agreed that no such country had ever stood between the sea and the high passes. Trade routes bent around empty space. Treaties referred to signatories that no longer existed. Mothers in neighboring towns woke unable to say why their kitchens held too many bowls.

Inside the border, we who were sworn still remembered. That was the cruelty of it — or the mercy, depending on the hour. The Spire had sealed itself and poured what remained of Vess into the vow-marks of its servants. We became the last archive of a nation. We became, each of us, a book the Church wanted burned.

Most of us ran. Some fought, and are unrecorded even in my memory now, because some prices get paid in installments. I ran south, through a country that officially had never existed, past checkpoints where clerks frowned at my papers because the papers named a homeland no clerk had ever heard of, and I learned the great survival art of the erased: I made myself dull. I made myself harmless. I opened a stall in Corvane selling luck-knots to people who needed luck more than they needed knots.

And every night, the mark ached like a healed bone before rain.

Every night but tonight. Tonight it burned.

* * *

Dawn came grey and thin. I'd dozed maybe two hours in the chair, neck at an angle I'd be billing myself for later. In the corner, the child sat up all at once — no slow surfacing, just awake, eyes finding mine immediately, the way animals wake.

"Good morning," I said, in Common, carefully.

The child watched my mouth. Then, in a voice like a rusted gate learning to move: "*Sela veth?*" Good-sleep? Asking if *I* had slept well. A mud-caked fugitive, hunted by white robes, asking after my rest.

"Sela," I confirmed. Then, because apparently three years of discipline meant nothing: "What's your name?"

They understood. I saw understanding move across their face like weather. And then they did something that undid me more thoroughly than anything the Unwritten ever managed.

They hesitated. They looked at their own hands. And they gave me a name the way you'd hand someone a wounded bird — carefully, as if it might die in transit.

"*Sef.*"

One syllable. It hit the vow-mark like a struck bell. I actually swayed on the chair. Because names in Old Tongue aren't labels, they're *sentences*. *Sef* — the root meant *what remains*. What remains after burning. What remains after grief. What remains when everything else has been unwritten and somebody, somehow, is still sitting in the rubble keeping watch.

A child named What-Remains, speaking a dead language, carrying a shard of the Spire, arriving at the doorstep of the one person in Corvane who could understand them.

Coincidence is a story cowards tell themselves. I'd stopped believing in it around the same time I stopped believing in safety.

"You're hungry," I said instead of all that, because some truths need bread first. I put water on the little stove and cut the heel off yesterday's loaf, and Sef ate like a soldier — fast, watching the door between bites — and something in the manner of it squeezed my heart in a way I pretended not to notice.

We managed, that morning, a kind of conversation built from gesture and the few words of Common the child had clearly picked up fleeing through the world. Enough to learn this much: they had come a long way north-to-south. They had come *from* something. When I touched the shard of black glass — gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal — the mark on my palm sang, and Sef flinched, and both of us pretended otherwise.

It was the Spire. A piece of it. Which should have been impossible, because the Spire was three hundred leagues away behind a border of forgetting, and also because the Spire did not *break*. Doors that demand payment do not shed splinters like a cheap table.

Unless the door wanted to be found.

I wrapped the loaf, filled a flask, and got out my good boots, the ones with no holes, and while I laced them I caught Sef watching my hands with an expression I recognized because I wore it in mirrors for years: the look of someone deciding whether the person in front of them is safe, and hating that they have to decide at all.

"It's all right," I told them, in Old Tongue, badly — my accent had gone soft with disuse, vowels blurring at the edges. "*Im sela. Im kaelen.*" You're safe. You're housed. Shelter-with-intention.

Sef's face did something complicated and young. And then the child pointed one small finger at the frost-feathered window, and said, in a whisper, in Old Tongue, a sentence I will hear until I die:

"*Sel vethan im. Tor vethan sel.*"

The white ones are coming. They walk the remembering.

I crossed to the window so fast I knocked the flask over. Down in Ash Street, morning traffic, carts and fishwives and the usual grey parade of Corvane going about its lawful, forgettable business.

And at the far corner, unhurried, pale as paper, two figures in white robes, moving shop to shop. Asking questions. Erasing nothing yet.

Just taking attendance.
