## 5. The Unwritten

Sef does not like the dark under the stairs, but the dark under the stairs is where names go when they are being taken away, and Sef has learned to hide in the places where taken things are kept, because hunters never search for what they have already thrown away.

That is a clever thought. Sef holds onto it the way you hold onto a warm stone on a cold night.

The white robes come at dusk. Sef knows this because dusk is when the light goes thin and everything looks like it is made of paper, and paper is what the Unwritten love best. Paper remembers nothing. Paper can be folded until a name disappears inside its own creases.

*Run,* says the memory that is not a memory but a lesson, learned before learning was possible, in the warm dark before Sef had hands. *When the light goes thin, run.*

So Sef runs, in the dream that is also not a dream, bare feet on cobbles that are soft as bread, past windows full of frost-flowers spelling out words nobody alive can read anymore, and behind — behind, always behind, patient as weather — the whisper of white hems sweeping the street clean of names.

They do not chase. That is the worst part, the part Sef cannot explain to the kind woman with tired eyes who gives them soup. The Unwritten never chase. Chasing means you believe the thing can get away. The Unwritten simply *walk*, and wherever they walk, things stop having ever been there. A door they pass forgets it was a door. A cat they pass forgets it was anyone's cat, and sits in the road looking puzzled about itself until it fades like breath off glass.

Sef has watched a whole house forget itself. It took only the length of a song.

* * *

Before — and *before* is a strange country, bigger than any country on a map, and Sef has been to both — before, there was the Spire.

Sef remembers it wrong, probably. Memory in a child is a jar of fireflies: you open it and some light gets out and some beetles get in, and after a while you can't tell which was which. But here is what the fireflies say:

There was a tower made of black glass, and it sang.

Not music exactly. More the way a kettle sings just before it boils, or the way a mother hums when she thinks no one is listening. The Spire sang to itself, low and endless, and inside the singing were words so old they had stopped being words and become *weather*. Sef lived inside the weather. Sef was — this is the part that makes the soup-woman frown when Sef tries to say it — Sef was *poured*, the way you pour candle wax into a mold. Someone very tall and very quiet stood at the foot of the Spire with cupped hands, and the Spire tipped, and what came out of it went into the hands, and the hands warmed it, and the warming became a child.

Chosen, not born. Made, not made-up. There is a difference and the difference matters, though Sef couldn't say why yet.

The tall quiet one had a face like a door that has been shut for a long time. When Sef asks the fireflies what the tall one said, the fireflies only flicker in a pattern that feels like:

*You will be given back. That is what being a gift means.*

Sef did not understand then. Sef understood it all at once later, in the bad moment, the running moment, and understanding went through them like a swallowed stone.

* * *

Here is how the world ended. Not with thunder. With *tidying*.

Sef remembers — or the fireflies remember, or maybe it is only the shard of black glass they carry against their chest, warm as a sleeping bird, doing the remembering for everyone — a great plain of people, kneeling, and above them the Spire singing its kettle-song, and around the edges of everything, white robes walking inward like frost creeping across a window.

And the white robes did not fight. Fighting is for things that exist. The white robes *edited*.

A man stood up from the kneeling crowd and shouted something fierce and wonderful, and the white robes walked toward him without hurrying, and one of them opened a book that had no writing in it, and the man's shout came out of his mouth and went into the book and was gone, and then his *name* went, and then the shape of his face went soft, like wax near a flame, and by the time the robes had walked past him he was a smear of almost, a person-shaped hole in the air, and the other kneeling people looked right at the hole and felt their eyes slide off it, because to look too long at an unwritten thing is to start wondering if you might be one.

Sef hid. Sef was small even for being poured, and the Spire had tucked them into a fold of itself like a coin into a purse, and through the black glass Sef watched the tidy frost creep closer and closer.

Then the god spoke.

Not aloud. Gods don't bother with aloud. The speaking happened *inside* everything at once, the way cold happens inside everything at once, and the words were Old Tongue, the first tongue, the tongue that tastes like snowmelt and iron and somebody's hand on your head, and the words said:

*Enough. I will be the payment. Close the door.*

And the Spire went dark. And the country around it — the fields, the roads, the little towns with their bread-smells and their arguing grandmothers — all of it went with the dark, gently, like a lamp being carried out of a room. Not destroyed. *Unwritten.* Put away between the pages of the blank book, where even God must knock politely to visit.

And Sef fell out of the fold, out of the purse, out of the Spire's last thought, into a world that had already forgotten the room the lamp used to live in.

* * *

Sef wakes in the charm-shop with salt on their face and doesn't know how long they've been crying, only that the kind woman — Odile, Odile, hold the name tight, names are eggs and eggs break — has put a blanket over them and is pretending very hard to be asleep in the chair by the door, which is a lie, because her eyes are open a crack, watching, the way she always watches, like a person guarding a door even though the door is only a door.

The shard is warm against Sef's chest. Warmer than yesterday. Every day warmer.

Sef lies still and counts the good things, because the tall quiet one taught counting as a prayer. One: the blanket smells of smoke and mint. Two: the woman left water within reach without being asked. Three: outside, the rain is doing its rain-sound, and rain has never once tried to erase anybody.

Four: four is harder. Four takes a while.

Four: somewhere far away, under all the forgetting, a door is waiting, and the door knows Sef's name, and being known — even by a door, even by a door that wants to eat you — is not nothing.

Sef holds the number four like a warm stone and finally sleeps.

* * *

In the next dream, the white robes have found a way to follow.

They walk down the middle of the charm-shop street, hems whispering, and the shop-fronts close their shutters like eyes, and the frost comes off their footsteps and climbs the walls and writes on every window in letters that Sef, alone in the whole city, can read. The letters say the same thing over and over, patient as weather:

*WHERE IS THE KEY. WHERE IS THE KEY. WHERE IS THE KEY.*

And Sef wants to shout, *I'm not a key, I'm a child, keys don't get scared of the dark under stairs* — but the dream-voice won't come, and anyway, in dreams you always know more than you want to know, and the knowing says: *you are both. You have always been both. That is why you were poured.*

One of the white robes stops. Turns. Under the hood there is no face, only a page, blank and waiting, and somehow the blankness is worse than any face could be, because a face can hate you or love you or be bored of you, but a blank page is *hungry* in a polite way, hungry the way an empty chair is hungry.

It extends one white sleeve, and in the sleeve-cuff, written small and neat, is a single word in Old Tongue, and the word is Sef's name — Sef's true name, the long one, the one that means *the-gift-that-returns* — and the robe says, in a voice like paper turning:

*We are not your enemies, little vessel. We are your editors. Come home to the blank page and be finished.*

And Sef, in the dream, in the terror, does the bravest thing Sef has ever done anywhere, dream or waking, which is to say:

*No.*

Just that. Small and cracked and absolutely stubborn. *No. I'm not finished. Nobody's finished. Even books get sequels.*

The blank face pauses. Somewhere behind it, deep in the hood, something that might be surprise happens. And the frost-letters on all the windows shiver, and rearrange themselves, just slightly, into a new sentence, and the new sentence says:

*THEN WE WILL COME FOR YOU IN THE WAKING WORLD, WHERE CHILDREN CANNOT ARGUE.*

* * *

Sef wakes for true this time, heart going like a bird in a box, and the shop is dark except for the banked coals, and the rain has stopped, and the silence afterward is the thick listening kind.

Odile is awake now, really awake, standing at the window with her shoulders tense as a drawn bow. Down the street, lamplit, unhurried, a figure in magistrate grey is knocking on doors. Asking questions. Writing things down.

Sef slides off the cot and comes to stand beside Odile, and together they watch the grey man work his way up the street, door by door, and Sef feels the shard pulse against their chest like a second heartbeat, quickening, and understands with terrible child-clarity that the dream was not a warning.

The dream was a schedule.

"Odile," Sef whispers, in Old Tongue, because some things are too frightened to be said in any language the law allows, "they're coming for me."

Odile looks down, and in the coal-light her face does something complicated and fierce, and she puts one hand — scarred, steady, warm — on top of Sef's head, right where a blessing would go.

"I know," she says, in the same forbidden tongue, soft as snowfall. "Pack nothing. We leave by the floor."

And beneath the floorboards, Sef discovers, there is a hatch, and beneath the hatch, stairs going down into the dark — and for the first time since the fall, the dark under the stairs does not feel like where names go to die.

It feels like a road.
