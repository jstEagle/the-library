## 6. A Buyer in Ash Street

The trouble with selling luck was that it attracted the unlucky.

Odile had learned this within their first month in Corvane, when a fishwife bought a knot-charm against drowning and then, three days later, fell off the Harbormaster's Stair into six inches of harbor water and cracked her head on a bollard. She hadn't drowned—Odile had never promised she wouldn't fall—but her husband came to the stall all the same, red-faced and grief-adjacent, demanding his copper back. Odile had refunded him, replaced the charm free of charge with one woven "against falling," and spent the rest of the day wondering whether they had just committed murder-by-magic or merely fraud.

Fraud, probably. That was the comfortable answer. The charms were knots and braids and bits of river-glass tied with patient hands and no power at all, because power was outlawed in Corvane and had been for as long as Corvane had been Corvane. The customers knew it. The customers *liked* it. There was a peculiar comfort, Odile had found, in buying magic that didn't work—a way of touching the old world without letting it touch you back.

So the stall in Ash Street sold harmless things: luck-knots in boatman's blue, fever-braids for mothers with coughing children, traveler's twists that promised safe roads and delivered nothing but something to worry between the fingers on long rides. And in the back of the little shop behind the stall, under a loose floorboard beneath a trunk of unsold winter stock, wrapped in oilcloth and hidden under three years of deliberate not-thinking about it—

—the shard of black glass shaped like a spire, which had arrived at their door in the mud-caked hand of a child who spoke a dead language and left frost on windows when he dreamed.

Sef was asleep now, which was the only state in which Sef could be safely described as easy. Awake, the boy was a lit fuse. He asked questions in Old Tongue that Odile answered in Common, and the gap between them filled up with gestures, drawings in spilled flour, expressions of escalating theatrical despair. He'd learned four words of Common in two days—*bread*, *no*, *water*, and, worryingly, *hide*—and used them with the confidence of an emperor. He'd also learned where Odile kept the honey, and had opinions about the neighbor's dog, communicated through a single devastating look that Odile was beginning to suspect was genetic to all children everywhere, in every kingdom, including ones that had never officially existed.

"Right," Odile murmured, tying off a traveler's twist with more force than the knot required. "You sleep. I'll stand here being ordinary. It's what I'm best at."

The morning market was doing its best impression of normal. Fish carts, cabbage negotiations, a fiddler playing badly near the well. Odile watched the street the way they always did—not the crowd but the seams of it: who stood too still, whose boots were too clean, who looked at people instead of past them. Three years of practice had made it automatic, a second heartbeat running underneath the first.

That was how they spotted the man leaning against the cooper's awning across the lane, and it took a full ten seconds for the recognition to arrive, because the last time Odile had seen Bren of the Nine Roads, he'd had a beard down to his collarbone and a scar through his left eyebrow, and neither of those things remained. What remained was the way he held his weight on his right heel, always ready to pivot; the ink stain on the side of his right hand, dark as a bruise, from thirty years of drawing maps nobody paid fairly for; and the small, habitual gesture of tapping two fingers against his thigh—counting paces, measuring distances, a cartographer even while standing still.

Bren caught them looking. Smiled. Tapped two fingers twice—which, in the old hand-speech of Vess's companies, meant *safe, but not for long.*

Odile's heart performed a maneuver that would have gotten a lesser organ court-martialed.

They finished the sale they were in—a fever-braid to a grandmother who haggled out of pure love of the sport—and then flipped the shutter closed over the stall, hung the BE BACK BY MIDDAY board (a lie; the board said whatever got Odile through the next hour), and walked across the lane like a person with absolutely nothing in their chest but lunch plans.

"Bren."

"Fitch." Bren pushed off the awning. Fitch. The name Odile wore in Corvane, thin as tin, and hearing it in Bren's mouth made it feel even thinner. His eyes were doing the thing old comrades' eyes did—cataloguing, counting ribs, checking hands for tremor. "You look terrible."

"You look *employed*."

"Temporarily." He nodded toward the shuttered stall. "Luck-knots. You. Selling luck-knots."

"People change."

"People hide," Bren corrected mildly. "Sometimes in interesting shapes." He glanced down the lane, unhurried, a man admiring architecture. "Walk with me. There's a pie seller on Tanner's Row who commits crimes against pastry that I've been meaning to report to someone I trust."

*

Tanner's Row smelled exactly as appetizing as its name, which was why the pie seller had the corner to himself and why his mutton pies were, in defiance of all sense, transcendent. They ate standing up, out of habit, backs to the wall, watching the mouths of two alleys.

"Three years," Odile said.

"Three years, two months, and eleven days." Bren wiped grease from his chin. "I counted. Counting's what I do. You went east over the pass. Everyone assumed the Church took you at the border shrines."

"The Church didn't take anyone at the border shrines. The border shrines were empty. The whole country was—" Odile stopped. Ate pie instead. "You know how it ended."

"I know how it *officially* ended, which is that it never began. There is no map of Vessmark in any archive south of the mountains. I checked. I checked for three years." Bren's voice stayed light, but the ink-stained hand had gone still. "Do you know what happens to a cartographer who insists a country exists? He stops getting commissions. Then he stops getting *invited* to things. Then he starts drawing coastlines for smugglers, because smugglers are the only clients who want their geography accurate."

"You draw for smugglers now."

"I draw for *anyone who pays*, and lately the paying clientele has developed strong opinions about roads that aren't there." He turned and looked at Odile fully for the first time. "Which brings us to business, because I can see you bracing for sentiment and I'd rather spare us both."

"I wasn't bracing."

"Your shoulder just climbed toward your ear. You always did brace with your right side." He dug into his coat and set something on the barrel between them.

A drawing. Ink on good paper, fine-lined, precise—the work of a master. It showed a spire. Tall, crooked-topped, its door a black seam in pale stone, rendered with the loving accuracy of a man who had drawn it from memory a hundred times and finally allowed himself to put it on paper.

Odile did not move. Did not blink. Somewhere behind them a cart rattled past, and the fiddler's bad tune drifted up from the market square, and the world went on being loudly indifferent while everything in Odile's chest went silent as the god it mourned.

"Where," Odile said, when they trusted their voice, "did you get this?"

"A client. Six weeks ago. Paid triple rates for maps of the borderlands north of the Graywater—the erased country, though he never called it that. Called it 'the region.'" Bren's mouth twisted. "And when I told him there was nothing to map, he smiled like I'd said something funny and gave me this. Said, 'Draw what you remember. Someone will come looking for the original.' Then he left. No name. Boots too clean."

"Church?"

"Worse. Church robes are honest about what they are. This man dressed like money." Bren tapped the drawing. "I've been carrying it around waiting, Odile. Waiting for whoever came looking. Because whoever draws a spire like that remembers it the way I do—from the *inside*—and there are maybe forty people alive who saw it from the inside. I've found nine. Nine, in three years, scattered like seed corn from here to the coast. A ferryman in Duncastle. A woman keeping goats above Merrow Ford who swears she was a spear-dancer. Two brothers running a tannery who won't say their old names out loud even behind locked doors."

The pie turned to lead in Odile's stomach. Not from the drawing—from the arithmetic. Nine found by one man looking casually. How many others? How many had crawled out of the erasure and buried themselves in villages and harbors and goat-pastures, alive, alive, *alive*, while Odile had spent three years believing they might be the last?

"Why didn't you find me sooner?" It came out smaller than intended.

"I tried. 'Fitch' is a very stupid name and you wear it well, but you don't survive three years as a mercenary of the Silent Company without learning to leave no trail, and you left none." He shrugged. "Then a week ago, a friend of mine who sells ribbon two stalls down from a certain luck-charm merchant mentions that the charm merchant's shutters stay closed at odd hours, and that a child is living there whom nobody has seen arrive, and—" his voice dropped to almost nothing, "—that the windows of the shop frost over on warm nights. From the *inside*. In patterns."

Odile set down the pie.

"Don't," they said.

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You were about to say something with the word 'Unwritten' in it, and I'd like to preserve this exact moment, the last moment before you said it, forever."

Bren was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, the levity had drained out of him entirely, leaving something older and harder underneath—the voice he'd used before battles, giving counts of arrows and distances to walls.

"The Unwritten don't knock, Odile. I've seen what they leave. Not bodies—*absences*. Houses where everyone remembers a family until you ask for a name, and then nothing. Whole streets in Duncastle that the tax rolls skip. They erase outward from whatever they're hunting, like ink bleeding backward." He tapped the spire drawing once, softly. "And something tells me the frost-child sleeping in your back room is the loudest thing in this city to them, whether or not he makes a sound."

"He's just a boy."

"He's a boy who speaks a language that was murdered so thoroughly that the Church burned the grammar books *last*, after the people. Do you understand what that means? Language doesn't survive alone. It survives in *throats*. Living ones." Bren shook his head slowly. "There shouldn't be a child in this world speaking Old Tongue. And yet here we are, eating criminal pies."

*

They walked back along the river, where the noise of the water covered voices and the crowds thinned. Bren talked business the way he drew maps—in clean lines, no decoration.

"Here's my offer. I have a wagon, a cover story as a surveyor, papers stamped by three different magistrates, and a route north through the hill country that appears on no chart I've ever drawn, because I invented it, which means the Church can't have stolen it. I can get you and the boy out of Corvane inside two days. North, toward the Graywater. Toward—" he gestured vaguely at the space where a country used to be, "—*there*."

"And your price?"

"Ah." Bren stopped walking. He faced the river, watching a barge crawl upstream under tow. "My price isn't coin. Coin I can get. My price is this: when we get wherever we're going, if there are others—if we find the nine, or the nine find *us*—you stand with them. You don't vanish again. Three years I've been collecting ghosts, Odile. Ferryman, goatherd, tannery brothers. Good people, hollowed people, flinching at their own shadows and calling themselves by names like mine—names picked off a signpost. And every one of them says the same thing: *if only we knew if anyone else...*" He exhaled. "I need you to be the one who doesn't run. Because I'll tell you a secret about hope: it arrives disguised as a transaction. It knocks on your door wearing a ledger, and it offers you passage, and you think you're buying safety, but really you're buying *each other*. That's the price. That's all of it."

Odile looked at the water for a long time.

Everything in them wanted to say yes. Everything in them also understood that yes meant unburying the shard, waking the child, pointing both of them north at the one place in the world the Church wanted most—because Bren was right about the frost, and Odile had known it since the second night, when Sef slept and the window bloomed with white ferns and Odile had stood there with a cloth and wiped them away and felt, unmistakably, the warmth of old vows stirring under their own skin like something turning over in its sleep.

But there was another thing too, smaller and stranger: relief. The bone-deep, guilty relief of a sentry who has spent three years holding a wall alone and finally hears footsteps on the road.

"There's a shard," Odile heard themself say. "Black glass. Shaped like the spire. The child carried it here. I don't know how he got it or what it is, but the oath-mark on my arm aches when it's near, and I swore off aching three years ago."

Bren went very still. "May I see it?"

"Not here. Not today." Odile turned back toward Ash Street, already walking, because every instinct honed over a lifetime of retreats said the conversation had lasted exactly as long as it safely could. "Come tomorrow. Early, before the market fills. Bring your wagon papers. And Bren—"

"Yes?"

"If you're followed, you lose the tail before you reach my door, or you don't reach my door. Those are the terms."

"Fair terms." He fell into step beside them, and for a moment the two of them walked like soldiers again, matched pace, matched silence, two survivors of a country that had never existed heading home through a city full of strangers.

At the mouth of Ash Street they parted. Odile watched Bren amble away, whistling, ink-stained hand tapping out paces against his thigh, and felt the unfamiliar weight of the morning settle onto their shoulders.

Not dread, for once. Something worse.

Hope.

Behind the shuttered stall, upstairs, the boy slept on—and across the window glass, in letters delicate as breath, frost was spelling out a word in a language only two living throats could read.

It was not a warning.

It was a *name*—and it was not Sef's.
