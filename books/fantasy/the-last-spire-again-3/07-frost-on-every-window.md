## 7. Frost on Every Window

The knock came at dawn, and it was not a customer's knock.

Odile knew the difference the way a fisherman knows weather. Customers knocked twice, apologetically, as if apologizing to the door itself. Church men knocked like they were arresting it.

They lay still in the loft above the shop and listened. Boots on cobblestone—many boots, arranged in the particular rhythm of a cordon closing. Somewhere down Ash Street a shutter banged, then another, the sound of a neighborhood pulling its blankets over its head.

Beside them, Sef slept on, curled around the spire-shard like a cat around a warm stone. The child's breathing was slow and even, and frost feathered across the inside of the window above the bed, delicate as lace, spelling nothing today—or spelling something Odile couldn't read, which amounted to the same thing.

*Unlicensed speech*, Magistrate Halloway had said three days ago, standing in Odile's doorway with rain dripping off his hat brim, asking his careful questions. *Reports of a language that leaves marks.*

Odile had lied beautifully. They had lied about knowing Old Tongue, lied about hearing anything unusual, lied with such smooth conviction that they'd almost believed themselves. But lies were like charms: they worked until someone tested them against the truth.

The knocking came again. Not at Odile's door this time. At Marta's, two stalls down—the charm-widow who sold love-knots so weak they barely qualified as sentiment and whose only real magic was an uncanny talent for remembering everyone's debts.

"Blackwood," hissed Marta's voice through the floorboards, low and urgent. "They're sweeping the whole street. Every stall, every cellar. You've got maybe the length of a kettle boiling."

Odile was already moving. Years of hiding had taught their body to move before their mind agreed to it—boots laced one-handed, coin pouch from its hiding place inside the false bottom of the luck-knot display, Sef lifted out of sleep and onto their hip before the child could do more than make a small confused sound.

"What—" Sef began, and stopped, because the child was learning Odile's rules faster than Odile taught them: *in Corvane, we are quiet; in Corvane, we are small; in Corvane, no word falls where it can leave frost.*

But there was no time for quiet now, and smallness was a luxury of people who weren't being hunted.

"Marta," Odile whispered through the trapdoor, "the tunnels."

A pause. Marta had run her stall for thirty years and had never once acknowledged the tunnels existed, because acknowledging things was how you got hanged for them.

"The third knot on the left wall," she said finally. "Pull it and push. And Odile—" her voice caught, went gruff again, "—you owe me for the winter stock. I'm keeping the ledger."

"I'd expect nothing less."

Down in the shop, Odile did something they would remember doing for the rest of their life, however long or short that turned out to be. They took the lamp off the counter—the good lamp, brass, bought secondhand the first week—and set it deliberately beside the hearth. Then they opened the drawer where they kept the day's takings and left it open. Empty drawers told inspectors a story; locked ones told a better one.

And last, because survival meant burning what you'd grown, Odile swept every charm off the display shelf into the fire.

The luck-knots went up like small bright birds. Twelve years of patient work—knots tied with exactly the right number of turns, charmed with exactly enough warmth to ease a headache or settle a quarrel or bring a fisherman home, all of it calibrated to sit below the Church's threshold of *magic* by a hair's breadth—twelve years, curling into ash in under a minute.

It hurt more than Odile expected. That surprised them. They had thought they'd made peace with impermanence, had built a life the way you build a sandcastle, knowing the tide. But watching the little flames eat through the knots they'd tied on slow afternoons, humming, content—watching the smoke take the smell of lavender and river-water that had soaked into the walls—

"You're sad," Sef observed, in a whisper.

"I'm efficient," Odile whispered back. "Sadness is for later. It keeps."

The third knot on the left wall looked exactly like the other knots—a decorative flourish, carved oak, the kind of thing a carpenter adds because plain wood offended him. Odile pulled it and pushed, and the wall swung inward on hinges older than the city's current name, exhaling air that smelled of stone and long darkness and, faintly, of other people's escapes.

Marta's voice drifted down one last time, pitched to carry: "There's a lantern stub on the first landing. Mind the third step past the well shaft; it's been rotten since before the war nobody talks about."

Then the wall closed behind them, and Ash Street, and the shop, and the twelve years, were gone.

* * *

The tunnels under the charm-sellers' quarter were a legend nobody confirmed and everybody maintained. Smugglers had cut them during the trade wars; charm-sellers had inherited them the way you inherit anything in Corvane—quietly, without paperwork, and with a strong preference for pretending otherwise.

Odile had known about them for twelve years and used them exactly once, to smuggle in a shipment of river-stones too warm to pass inspection. That had felt like adventure. This felt like exile.

Sef rode on Odile's back, arms looped around their neck, the shard tucked inside the child's shirt where it pressed cold against both of them. The lantern stub threw a circle of greasy light that moved when they moved, so that they walked always at the center of a small trembling world.

"Is this home?" Sef asked, after a long while. The child's voice echoed strangely, coming back doubled off the wet stone.

"No."

"Will we have a new one?"

Odile considered lying. It would have been easy—*of course, little one, homes grow back like grass*—and Sef would have accepted it, because children accept what adults tell them the way soil accepts rain, without checking whether it's clean.

"We'll see," Odile said, which was the most honest lie available.

The tunnel branched, and Odile followed Marta's directions from memory: *left at the well shaft, straight past the old counting-house cellar, and if you smell tallow, you've gone too far toward the chandlers' and you should turn around before someone finds you.* The air grew warmer, which meant they were passing beneath inhabited streets. Once, directly overhead, boots crossed a grate, and Odile stood motionless in the dark for the length of ten heartbeats, feeling Sef's heart hammering against their spine, faster than their own.

Above them, muffled by stone and street, came the sound of a door being broken.

Not theirs. Someone else's. Someone whose name Odile didn't know, who had probably never done anything worse than sell a fever-charm to a desperate mother. The sound of the door was followed by voices, indistinct, and then a woman screaming—not in pain, Odile thought, but in the particular fury of a person watching strangers touch her things.

Sef's arms tightened. "Are they taking her?"

"Yes."

"Like they took the names?"

Odile hadn't told the child much about the Unwritten. There hadn't seemed any point; terror either found you or it didn't, and explaining it beforehand was like describing drowning to someone already underwater. But Sef knew. Whatever the child remembered from before—from the white robes and the walking erasure—Sef knew what happened to people the Church took.

"They won't take yours," Odile said. "I promise."

It was, they realized somewhere between the words leaving their mouth and hitting the dark, the first outright promise they had made since swearing service to Vess. Promises were expensive things. They cost you pieces of whatever future you'd planned.

They kept walking.

* * *

They came up through the counting-house cellar into the tanners' district, where the smell covered everything and even the Church preferred not to linger. Odile paid a tanner's boy three coppers to say he'd seen a tall man with a limp heading for the south gate, going the wrong direction on purpose, and then they cut west along the drainage channel with their collar up and Sef's hood down over the child's face.

By midmorning they were in the warren behind the dye-works, in the rented room of a woman named Pell who owed Odile for a fertility charm that had—through pure coincidence, obviously—worked, and who asked no questions because her own history had chapters in it she preferred closed.

Pell looked at Sef exactly once, and longer than once. "That's the child from the reports," she said quietly.

"That's my nephew," Odile said. "From the coast. He's shy."

"Uh-huh." Pell poured tea for three, which was answer enough. "Halloway himself is running the sweep. Not his usual censors—his own men. He's never cared this much about anything except tax ledgers." She set down the pot. "Whatever that kid said, somebody important heard about it."

Odile drank their tea and said nothing, and Pell, being Corvanese to the bone, respected the silence professionally.

It was in that silence, staring at the steam, that Odile let the sadness keep its appointment.

It arrived all at once, the way grief does when you've postponed it: the shop, the lamp, the knots burning like birds. The regulars—old Tam with his rheumatism, the baker's daughters with their quarrels, the widow Fen who bought a protection charm every month for a son at sea and never asked if it worked, just needed to buy something. The morning light through the front window, the specific angle of it in autumn. The way the floorboard by the counter had learned the shape of Odile's standing weight.

Twelve years. Erased in a morning, by their own hand, before the Church could do it worse.

"You're sad again," Sef said. The child had climbed into their lap without asking, the way children colonize laps, and was watching Odile's face from six inches away with unnerving directness.

"I'm resting my eyes."

"Your eyes are open."

"Resting them *open*. It's an advanced technique."

Sef considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "In the Old Tongue there's a word for being sad about a place that's gone. It sounds like water going backward." The child said the word then—three syllables, soft-edged, beautiful—and frost bloomed across the teapot, delicate fern-patterns, gone in a breath.

Odile stared at the teapot. "You have to stop doing that."

"I know." Sef picked at a loose thread on Odile's sleeve. "It comes out when I feel things big. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Just—" Odile exhaled. "When it comes, think about something flat. Something boring. Taxes."

"Taxes?"

"The flattest thing I know."

Sef nodded solemnly, filing this away among the many strange adult instructions the child had been accumulating: *be quiet, be small, think about taxes.* Somewhere in that small head, Odile suspected, a philosophy was forming that would one day be quoted back at them at the worst possible moment.

Outside, the district bells rang the hour, and beneath the bells—fainter, wrong, arriving a half-beat late like an echo that hadn't been invited—came a different bell. A clear, cold tone that Odile hadn't heard in three years and had hoped never to hear again.

A censor's assembly bell. And not one bell. Four, spaced evenly, ringing the pattern that meant a district was sealed: no one in, no one out, everyone accounted for.

Pell stood up so fast her chair fell. "That's us. That's this district. Odile—"

"I heard."

"How? How did they close the net so fast? We're in the dye-works warren, nobody sweeps here, nobody *cares* here—"

Unless, Odile thought, with the cold clarity that had kept them alive through two wars and one very bad marriage, someone had told the Church exactly where the charm-sellers' tunnels came out. Unless the sweep of Ash Street had never been about catching them at the shop at all, but about flushing them, herding them, driving them down into the dark and waiting at every exit with lanterns and white robes.

Or—less paranoid, possibly worse—unless Halloway was simply better than Odile had credited. He had struck Odile, in their one meeting, as a man who read people the way Odile read weather. Careful questions. Patient eyes. He had left the shop that rainy day with a polite nod and a card, *if anything further occurs to you*, and Odile had watched him go and thought: *that man will be back.*

"He's not trying to burn us out," Odile said slowly, working it aloud. "If he wanted us dead or chained, he'd have hit the shop with everything. Instead he's squeezing. Sealing districts one at a time, pushing us into a smaller and smaller space. He wants to talk."

"People don't send four bells because they want to talk."

"Halloway might." Odile remembered the magistrate's hands, ink-stained, folded neatly on his ledger. Remembered the moment, halfway through the interview, when Halloway had glanced at the frost on Odile's windows and something had moved behind his face—not hunger, not zeal. Recognition. "He's a strange kind of hunter. Which makes him the dangerous kind."

Sef tugged their sleeve. "What's happening?"

"We're leaving," Odile said, and stood, and felt the full weight of the day settle onto their shoulders: the burned shop, the sealed streets, the child on their hip growing heavier not in pounds but in consequence. "Pell. Is the roof route still open? Over the dye-works, across the drying lines?"

"To the river quarter, if the lines hold. They haven't held since the storm."

"Then we'll find out what 'hold' means."

* * *

They went over the rooftops the way Odile had once gone over fortress walls, which is to say badly, quickly, and with a running commentary of profanity kept strictly internal. The drying lines held. Mostly. One of them held in the sense that it deposited Odile into a pile of dyed wool rather than onto the cobbles, which Odile chose to count as a victory.

Sef, light as a bird and sure-footed as sin, crossed the same lines without incident and waited on the far ledge with an expression of polite concern. "You made a noise."

"A noise of triumph."

"It sounded like falling."

"Triumph often does."

Below, in the streets, the cordon was visible now: gray coats at the corners, checking papers, turning carts around. White robes farther back, moving unhurried, and wherever the white robes passed, Odile noticed, people stepped aside with their heads down and their mouths shut, and the frost that formed on the windowpanes they passed melted instantly, as though afraid to be seen.

They reached the river quarter by noon, and there Odile stopped in the shadow of a grain warehouse, breathing hard, Sef perched on a bollard, and took honest stock for the first time since dawn.

No shop. No stock. No street, no regulars, no life. Coin enough for perhaps two weeks of careful living. One borrowed room behind them, sealed. One city ahead of them, sealing itself district by district, closing like a hand.

And one child carrying a piece of a door that the entire machinery of the Church had decided mattered more than anything else in the world.

"Well," Odile said to Sef. "Any ideas?"

Sef reached inside their shirt and drew out the shard. In the daylight it looked worse than it ever had in the dark—not black glass anymore but something closer to a hole in the shape of glass, drinking the light. It had grown warmer. Odile could see that without touching it; the air around it shimmered faintly, like a road in summer heat.

"It wants to go home," Sef said simply.

"Everything wants to go home. Home is currently a sealed district with four bells in it."

"Not your home." The child turned the shard in their fingers, and for a moment their eyes had a depth no child's eyes should have—looking through Odile, or through the city, or through the world, at something far away and vertical. "Mine. The tall place. Where the quiet god waits." Sef blinked, and was a child again, and shrugged. "I don't know why I said that. It just came out."

Odile looked at the shard, and at the sealed city, and understood with terrible practicality that staying in Corvane was no longer an option at any price. The Church would tighten and tighten until there was nowhere left to stand. Which meant the road—which meant Bren, damn him, and his maps and his prices and his infuriating grin, the comrade Odile had buried alive in memory three years ago and who had surfaced last week in Ash Street like a body refusing to stay sunk.

*I can get you out,* Bren had said. *Out of the city, out of the country, out of the story entirely. For a price.*

*Name it,* Odile had said, assuming coin.

Bren had smiled, and it hadn't been a nice smile, and he had named a price that wasn't coin at all.

Odile had walked away from it. That was then, when walking away still meant having somewhere to walk back to.

"Sef," Odile said. "How do you feel about cartographers?"

"What's a cartographer?"

"A man who knows where roads are that don't exist."

Sef considered. "That sounds useful and also like lying."

"It's both. It's usually both." Odile shouldered their pack—one pack now, everything they owned in the world rattling inside it like bones in a cup—and took the child's hand. "Come on. We're going to see a liar about a road."

Behind them, across the rooftops, the fourth bell finished ringing, and somewhere in the sealed and shrinking city, frost bloomed on every window at once—there and gone, like a wave, like a farewell, like a country remembering its own name for one impossible instant before the sun took it back.
