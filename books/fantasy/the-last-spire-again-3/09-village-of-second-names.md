## 9. Village of Second Names

The village had no name on any map, which was the first thing I liked about it.

The second thing was the bread. We smelled it before we saw the smoke — a woodsmoke-and-rye warmth rolling down through the pines like an apology from the world for three weeks of cold rations and Bren's cooking, which consisted mostly of burning things that hadn't deserved it.

"Smoke means people," Bren said, reining the cart to a stop. He said it the way he said everything: flatly, as though reading it off a map he didn't entirely trust. "People mean questions."

"People also mean bread," I said.

Sef, wrapped in my coat in the cart bed, lifted their head at the word *bread* with the immediate, total attention of the very young and the very hungry. Three weeks of road had filled them out some, put color back in cheeks that had been mud-grey when they turned up on my doorstep during the storm. The shard of black glass rode against their chest on a cord, and when the wind shifted toward the smoke, I could have sworn it warmed against them like a held bird.

"We don't know who lives here," Bren said. "Could be Church. Could be worse."

"Who's worse than the Church?"

"Farmers," Bren said gravely. "Farmers ask about your business. They ask after your family. They want to know if you're staying." He shuddered theatrically, then clicked his tongue at the mule. "Come on, then. Let's go be interrogated."

* * *

They found us before we found them, which told me everything I needed to know about the kind of village this was.

A man stepped out of the treeline twenty yards ahead of the cart as casually as if he'd been waiting there all morning to discuss the weather. He wore a forester's greens gone grey at the elbows, a billhook over one shoulder, and the particular stillness of someone who had once stood in a shield line and never fully unlearned it. Behind him, two more shapes detached from the trees — women, one old and one young, both with axes they weren't hiding particularly well.

"That's far enough," the man said. His eyes went over us the way a customs officer goes over cargo. Cart, mule, Bren, me. Then Sef. Then me again, longer. "You're not tax men. Tax men ride the king's road and complain about their horses."

"We're not tax men," I agreed.

"You're not smugglers. Smugglers smile more." A pause. "Well. She smiles more. You look like a funeral that learned to walk."

"He's charming," I said. "Everyone says so."

The man's gaze settled on Sef, and something moved behind his face — recognition of a kind I'd learned to dread. Not recognition of a child. Recognition of a *language*, or the shape where a language used to live. Sef stared back and said nothing, because Sef had learned on this road that silence was armor. It broke my heart a little each time, watching a child learn to fold themselves up small.

"You're coming from the south," the man said. It wasn't a question. "Down the old drover's way. Nobody comes up the drover's way unless they're lost, or running, or—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Or looking."

"And if we're looking?" I asked quietly.

His hand tightened on the billhook. One of the women in the trees shifted her weight, wood creaking under her boot.

"Then you should know," he said, "that what you're looking for cost us enough already. And we buried the ones who paid."

* * *

They took our weapons at the edge of the fields — Bren surrendered his knife with the sigh of a man parting with a beloved uncle, and I handed over the short sword I'd carried eleven years without complaint — and walked us in past hedgerows and bean rows to a village that shouldn't have existed.

I say *shouldn't* because I knew this valley. Or I'd known the map of it, back when maps were honest. There was no village here. There was a burned manor and a plague pit and, according to the surveyors, bad water and nothing else. Corvane's cartographers had written the valley off a generation ago, and the valley had apparently taken the hint and hidden itself so well it had fooled even the paper.

The houses were stone and timber, mended and remended, roofs patched with different-colored thatch like quilts. Chickens argued in the lane. A boy of maybe ten was teaching a dog to sit, without much success on either side. Smoke rose from chimneys. Laundry dried on lines. Everything about it was aggressively, deliberately ordinary, the way a soldier stands at ease — ordinary as a discipline, ordinary as a disguise.

And everywhere, small things that only a certain kind of eye would catch. The way the sentry by the well tracked us without turning her head. The way the firewood was stacked to leave sightlines between the logs. The way every adult in the lane glanced at Sef twice — once as a stranger's child, and once with something older and sadder, like people looking at a photograph of someone they'd lost.

Bren leaned close. "Odile," he murmured. "These are our people."

"I know."

"No, I mean—" His voice dropped further. "*Our* people. Look at the door lintels."

I looked. Above each door, carved small and worn nearly smooth, was the same sign: a vertical line crossed by four horizontal strokes. The mark of Vess's sworn. The silent god's tally — four watches of the night, kept faithfully, answered never.

My oath-mark ached under my sleeve like a tooth.

* * *

They fed us before they questioned us, which told me these were survivors and not converts. Converts interrogate first and feed you afterward, if ever. Survivors feed you first because they remember what hunger does to a person, and because shared bread is the oldest treaty there is.

We sat at a long table in a hall that had clearly been a tithe barn in another life, among perhaps forty villagers — farmers, smiths, a woman mending nets nowhere near any net-needing water. The bread was dark and dense and better than anything I'd eaten since Corvane, and Sef ate like a small wolf, and nobody commented on it except with the fond, pained looks people give a child who has known empty days.

The forester from the treeline sat across from us. Up close he was fifty-odd, grey-shot, with hands like tree roots and a scar bisecting one eyebrow. When the bowls were cleared, he folded those hands and looked at me.

"Dace," he said. "That's my name now. Wasn't always."

"It wasn't mine either," I said. "Felix Dunmore now. Before that—" I stopped. Old habit. In Corvane you never finished that sentence.

But Dace nodded slowly, as if I had. "Before that," he agreed. "We all had a before that. Here we call them second names. First name's the one the Spire knew. Second one's the one you take so the Unwritten walk past your door." He tipped his head at the room. "Every soul here has two. Some of us have three, if the first two got too warm."

"How many?" Bren asked. "How many survived?"

Dace was quiet a moment. Around the table, the quiet spread, the way grief does in rooms where everyone shares the same wound.

"Out of the whole country? Out of the garrisons and the river towns and the capital with its hundred bells?" He shook his head. "This valley holds sixty-one. There are four other settlements we know of. After the fall, we counted ourselves the way you count coins after a robbery — over and over, hoping the number changes." His mouth twisted. "It doesn't change."

Sef had stopped eating. The child sat very still, listening with that uncanny total attention, and I saw the villagers see it — see the way Sef's lips moved faintly around sounds, shaping words in a tongue none of them had dared speak aloud in years.

An old woman across the table made a small sound, hand going to her mouth.

"They understand it," she whispered. "Gods below. The little one understands it."

"She speaks it," I said. That was as far as I got.

The room went taut as a bowstring. Forty faces turned toward Sef, and I watched hope move through them like wind through wheat — terrible, hungry hope, the kind that eats the field. I'd seen that hope before. I'd seen what it did.

Dace's hand came down flat on the table, and the crack of it pulled every eye back to him.

"No," he said. Not loudly. Firmly, the way you say *no* to a child reaching for a blade. "No. Whatever you're thinking — whoever sent you or whatever the little one is carrying — no."

"You don't know what I'm thinking," I said.

"Don't I?" Dace leaned forward. "Three weeks on the road from Corvane, up the drover's way, following roads that aren't on maps anymore, with a child who speaks the Tongue and something black glass hung round their neck. I've been fifty years old for six years now, friend, and I was young once, and young is when they get you with the thinking." His voice roughened. "Someone's told you the Spire can be opened. Someone's told you everything that was taken can be given back. Our god restored, our country un-erased, our dead unwritten. Am I close?"

I said nothing. Which was an answer, and he knew it, and the room knew it.

"We paid," he said simply. "That's what no one tells you, chasing the door. The Spire doesn't open for keys or prayers or armies. It opens for a price, and the price is a life freely given, and the last time—" His voice caught, steadied. "The last time, it was my brother who gave it. To close the thing. To seal the god away safe from the Church's knives, when sealing was the only mercy left. And do you know what we got for it? Sixty-one people in a valley that doesn't exist, eating beans and lying about our names." He looked around the table, and forty faces looked back, and every one of them had paid something. "We got to *live*. That was the price's change, and we've spent thirty years learning to call it enough."

"So you'd leave the god sealed forever," I said. "Leave the dead unwritten. Leave—" I stopped myself, but too late.

"Leave *who*?" Dace asked softly.

And there it was, the hook under my ribs. Because I couldn't say it. I'd spent three years not saying it. The names of my dead sat in my chest like stones in a wall, load-bearing, and if I pulled one out to show him —

"Leave the truth buried," I finished, instead.

"Mm," said Dace, in the tone of a man filing a lie away for later.

* * *

It was the healer who cut through the standoff, and she did it by ignoring it completely.

She came in from the rain that had started sometime during the argument — a tall woman, fortyish, with brown skin gone leathery at the knuckles from work and iron-grey threading her black hair, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone whose authority no one had thought to question in years. She took one look at Sef, hunched and hollow-eyed at the end of the table, and clicked her tongue.

"This child is half asleep and you're all shouting theology at them. Shame on the lot of you." She scooped Sef up onto her hip with the practiced ease of a woman who had raised half the village, and Sef — who flinched from strangers, who had flinched from me for the first week — went boneless against her shoulder like it was the most natural place in the world to be. "There's a bed with your name on it, little one. Well. With no name on it, which is safer."

She turned to me, and her eyes were dark and direct and missed absolutely nothing.

"You'll want to see where they sleep. Come."

"I can carry—"

"You've been carrying them for three weeks; I can see it in your shoulders. Sit down before you fall down, that's an order. I'm Maren. I'm the closest thing this valley has to a physician, which means when I tell you to rest, the alternative is me describing, in detail, exactly what will happen to your spine if you don't." A ghost of a smile. "I'm told my descriptions are very effective."

Bren, beside me, made a sound suspiciously like a suppressed laugh.

I followed her out into the rain anyway, because Sef looked back at me over Maren's shoulder with an expression I'd learned to read — not fear, not quite, but the question children can't help asking with their whole face: *are you coming?*

"Coming," I said.

Maren glanced sideways at me as we walked, rain drumming on the hood she threw over Sef's head. "Dace will have told you not to chase the Spire," she said. Conversationally. As if discussing turnips.

"He did. At length."

"And will you?"

"I haven't decided."

"Liar," she said mildly, and I nearly tripped in the mud. She didn't look at me. She just adjusted Sef's weight on her hip and kept walking. "You've got the look of the undecided who've decided. I patched a lot of soldiers, back in the before. You all wore that same face right up until the moment you did the reckless thing." At a doorway she paused, ducking under the lintel — above it, worn almost to nothing, the four-stroke mark — and set Sef down on a narrow bed in a warm room that smelled of lavender and beeswax. "Knew a sworn-brother of yours, once. Tammas Vey."

The world tilted slightly.

"Tam," I said. My voice came out strange. "Tam died at the ford at Callowmere. During the fall."

"Mm. He did. I was there." Maren pulled the blanket up over Sef, who was already sinking, eyelids heavy, one small hand finding hers and holding on. "He bought us the hour we needed to get the children out. I closed his eyes. I kept his tally-mark token." Her gaze lifted to mine, steady and unflinching. "So when I tell you that I know exactly what the Spire costs, and exactly who pays it, and exactly what kind of person volunteers to pay — you'll believe me. Won't you."

Sef was asleep. Truly asleep, deep and easy, breath slow, the shard rising and falling gently on the small chest. Outside, the rain softened the whole world to grey wool.

"Your mark," Maren said quietly. "Under your sleeve. Left forearm?"

I went cold. "How—"

"Because Tammas had one too, and in his last week it started to fail him the way a lamp fails — guttering, flaring, guttering. And yours is failing now. I saw you favor it getting off the cart." She straightened, and there was something in her face beyond competence now, something that made my chest ache in a way I hadn't budgeted for. "So you'll stay the night, Felix Dunmore, whatever your second name is. You'll eat, and sleep, and tomorrow I'm going to look at that mark. Because either it's killing you — or it's calling you. And I've seen what happens to people who answer that call without anyone standing beside them."

She paused at the door, rain silvering the air behind her.

"For what it's worth," she added, not quite looking at me, "the valley could use another pair of hands that knows how to stand in a line. And I could use someone who argues with Dace properly. He's gotten lazy — everyone just agrees with him now." The ghost-smile again, warmer this time. "Goodnight, Felix."

And I stood alone in the lamplight, next to a sleeping child who was a key, in a village that didn't exist, with my oath-mark burning under my sleeve like a coal someone had breathed on — and for the first time in three years, the burning felt less like a summons and more like a question.

Tomorrow, she'd said.

Tomorrow, I thought, watching the rain, was starting to feel like a dangerous word.
