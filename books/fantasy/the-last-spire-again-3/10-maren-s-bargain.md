## 10. Maren's Bargain

Odile had not been touched with kindness in three years.

That was the thought that arrived, uninvited, while Maren's hands worked the salve into their shoulder — hands that smelled of comfrey and woodsmoke and something older, something like the herb-smoke they used to burn in the shrine tents before a battle. The village of Second Names was loud with living: a dog barking at nothing, children arguing over a game of knucklebones on the step outside, someone hammering a loose board onto a roof as if roofs were worth fixing here, as if tomorrow were assumed.

Odile sat very still on Maren's stool and let themselves be tended, because refusing would have required an explanation, and explanations were things they'd gotten out of practice giving.

"Your oath-mark is dying," Maren said, without ceremony. "You knew that."

"I suspected."

"Don't be brave at me. I've patched braver people than you and buried most of them." She dipped a cloth in the basin, wrung it out. Her hair was iron-grey and tied back with what appeared to be a shoelace, which Odile found more disarming than any display of power could have been. "It's failing because you're too far from your god for too long, or your god is too far from everything, or both. The mark is a thread, mercenary. Threads fray when nobody holds either end."

"It aches," Odile admitted. "Some nights it burns. I thought it was the weather."

"The weather." Maren snorted. "Three years of thinking an oath burning through your skin was *the weather*. Vess chose modest servants, at least."

Odile almost laughed. It came out as a breath, which Maren pretended not to notice, which was its own small mercy.

The mark itself sat below the collarbone — a spiral like a coiled rope, once black as ink, now gone grey at the edges, flaking like old paint. When Maren pressed near it, Odile felt the ache shift, the way a tooth announces itself. And underneath the ache, fainter than memory, the warmth they'd spent a lifetime hiding under their skin in Corvane. The last proof they had ever belonged to anything.

Sef was asleep in the next room, curled on a pallet among Maren's own grandchildren's blankets — she had grandchildren, plural, alive and noisy, which still struck Odile sideways every time one of them ran past. The spire-shard lay under the child's palm even in sleep, and even from here Odile could feel it humming, faint as a fly against glass.

"You know what it is," Odile said quietly. "The shard. The Spire. You knew the moment we walked in."

Maren didn't answer right away. She set down the cloth, wiped her hands on her apron, and looked at Odile the way healers look — straight through the armor, all the way to whatever's underneath, without apology.

"I knew a sworn-brother of yours," she said instead. "Coric. Broad fellow, terrible singing voice, prayed so loudly the rest of you never had to bother."

Odile went still. "Coric Ashfall."

"He made it further than most. Made it here, in fact." Maren's voice stayed level, but her hands, gathering the salve jar, were very careful. "He carried a piece of the door too. Smaller than the child's. He'd found it in the ruins and he thought — the way soldiers think — that if he could just get it back where it belonged, everything undone would be done again. Everyone erased, rewritten. His mother. His city. All of it."

"What happened to him?"

Maren met their eyes. "The Spire told him."

* * *

She told it plainly, because she was the sort of woman who told things plainly, sitting across the table with two cups of willowbark tea going cold between them while rain started up against the shutters.

"The Spire isn't a wall with a hinge, Odile. It's a bargain that got made once and keeps getting offered. Your god sealed your country away to keep it from being unmade by the Church — that part you know, everyone who survived knows it in their bones. But a seal like that needs a price, and the price was paid then, and the door has never stopped asking for the same coin." She turned her cup slowly. "It opens for a life. Freely given. That's the whole of the law of it. No trick, no loophole, no clever third option. Coric stood at the threshold and asked it how, and it answered, and he understood that the only thing he could give was himself."

"And he did."

"He did." Maren looked at the window, at the rain. "And the door opened a crack. Wide enough for light. Wide enough that for one night, this whole valley smelled like our old country — bread, and river water, and the particular smell of the temple district after rain. People wept in the streets. Then it closed, because one life buys one crack, and his piece of the door burned to ash, and we buried what was left of him under the pear tree because he'd have hated a solemn grave." She smiled, briefly, and it hurt to look at. "We eat those pears every autumn. It's the best use anyone's made of a sacrifice in thirty years."

Odile stared into the cold tea. "One crack."

"One crack. For a whole life." Maren leaned forward. "Now look at what walked into my village. Not a man with a shard he picked up off the ground. A child *made* of the Spire, carrying the largest piece of it anyone's seen since the fall, growing stronger every mile toward home. You don't need to be a healer to do that sum. The door doesn't want a crack this time. The door wants to open all the way, and it wants Sef to be the coin."

"They're a child."

"They are a child," Maren agreed. "Which is why half this village wants you gone by morning, and the other half wants you gone by tonight, and nobody — *nobody* — wants the Spire opened. We paid its price before. Some of us paid family." Her voice roughened and she mastered it. "I'm telling you the truth because you'll hear it eventually, better from me than from a mob with pitchforks they'd never actually light because we're all too tired. The Spire opens for a freely given life. There is no version of that story where Sef comes home whole."

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere a child shrieked with laughter, and the sound went through Odile like a splinter.

"There's another option," Odile said. It came out hoarse. "There's always another—"

"No." Gentle, but final. "Soldiers always believe that. I loved a soldier; I know the disease. *There's always another way* is the prayer of people who can't stand arithmetic." She reached across the table and took Odile's wrist — the unmarked one — and held it. "So I'm going to ask you the other question. The one nobody asks soldiers. Not: how do you win? Ask instead: what are you trying to save?"

* * *

From Maren's window, past midnight, Odile watched the village sleep and tried to answer her.

What were they trying to save?

The obvious answer was the old one: the god. The people. Vess, silent three years behind a door, and a kingdom of the erased waiting to be spoken back into the world. Every mile they'd run with Sef, they'd told themselves that story. Rescue. Restoration. The great work finally finished.

But standing in a warm kitchen that smelled of pears and willowbark, listening to Sef breathe in the next room, the story wouldn't sit straight anymore.

Because the people who'd come back through Coric's crack — no one had come back. Because the survivors here had built something the old kingdom never managed: they'd lived. They'd taken second names and learned to farm bad soil and raise loud children under a Church that would erase them twice as gladly, and they'd done it without a god's protection, without oaths, without anything but each other. Coric's sacrifice had bought them one night of perfume and grief. Their ordinary mornings had bought them thirty years.

And Sef — Sef, who slept with a fist around a shard of a dead country, who said *thank you* in Old Tongue with a Corvane accent now because they'd been picking it up on the road — Sef didn't want to be a key. Odile had seen that in the child's eyes since the storm-night at the threshold, before understanding a word of it. The terror wasn't of hunters. The terror was of doors.

*You don't want to be the coin,* Odile thought at the sleeping room. *Then I won't make you one.*

Which left the arithmetic Maren had promised them. One crack per life. A whole life for a whole door. And Odile's own life currently held: one failing oath, no homeland, a burned shop, a dead profession, a face known to Church inspectors, and a bond to a god who hadn't spoken in three years except through frost on windows.

It would be easy. That was the obscene part. It would be *easy*, and it might even be enough, and no one in this kitchen would stop them except—

Except Maren had asked the wrong question, maybe. Or the right one, and Odile couldn't yet bear the answer. Because if the thing they were trying to save wasn't the god, wasn't the kingdom — if it was this: the stool, the tea, the breathing child, the woman with the shoelace in her hair who'd taken in strangers without asking a single question about their papers — then the way to save it wasn't through a door at all.

*Stay,* Maren had said, before the telling, before the tea, pressing the salve into the fraying mark. *Not forever. Just stay. Stay tonight. Stay a season. Let the mark fail on its own time instead of running it to death. Belonging isn't a bargain, Odile. Nobody's ever had to die for it.*

They'd laughed — actually laughed, a rusty sound — and said, *you clearly haven't met my life.*

*Then change it,* she'd said, and gone back to the salve, and that had been worse than any argument.

* * *

Morning came grey and clean-washed. Odile woke on the settle where they'd fallen asleep mid-vigil, neck complaining, and found the kitchen already full: Maren at the stove, two of her grandchildren setting bowls with the focused chaos of the very young, and Sef sitting on the floor showing a third child how to fold a luck-knot out of grass — badly, with tremendous authority.

The knot came apart. Sef frowned at it in Old Tongue, muttered something scolding, and the other child laughed, and Odile felt something in their chest turn over and refuse to right itself.

Maren set a bowl in front of them and sat down across the table. For a while neither spoke. The children argued about porridge. Life went on, shamelessly.

"You didn't sleep," Maren said.

"I slept some."

"You slept like a sentry. Which is not sleeping." She stirred her bowl without eating. "You've decided something. Soldiers decide in the night and then spend the morning looking brave about it. So. Tell me the stupid thing you've decided, and I'll tell you why it won't work, and then perhaps by noon we'll find something that might."

Odile looked at her. Warm, plain, immovable. The kind of person the old kingdom had been full of, once, without ever noticing — the kind that gets erased first and mourned longest.

"If I give the door my oath," Odile said quietly, "my bond — the thread itself, not just the body attached to it — maybe that's worth more than one life. Maybe a sworn life counts double. Maybe—"

"Maybe," Maren agreed, "and maybe the door takes the oath *and* you, because doors are greedy and gods are hungry and you are offering a starving thing a taste. Did you think of that?" She set down her spoon. "Or did you think of it, and decide it was acceptable?"

The silence stretched. One of the grandchildren dropped a spoon and everybody ignored it expertly.

"Maren." Odile's voice was low. "If I stay, the hunters come here. Halloway already has half the country mapped. The Unwritten don't stop, and Sef gets louder every day — the frost, the shard, the words leaving snow on windowsills. Staying isn't safety. Staying is just choosing where the fire lands."

"And opening the Spire chooses where the fire lands too," she said. "Every choice does. That's what they don't tell you about survival — it's not the absence of cost, it's the choosing of it." She reached over and, briskly, unbearably gently, tucked a stray piece of hair behind Odile's ear, the way you'd fuss at a child or someone you intended to keep. "So choose the fire that leaves somebody alive to sweep up after. Choose the one where the children are already here."

Before Odile could answer — before they even knew what their face was doing — the door banged open and one of Maren's grandsons skidded in, mud to the knees, eyes wide.

"Gran," he panted. "There's riders on the south ridge. White cloaks. And Da says—" he swallowed, "Da says they're stopping at every house. Reading names off a list."

Maren stood. The whole room changed around her standing — the children hushed, Sef rose with the shard clenched tight, and Odile felt the failing oath-mark flare hot as a coal for the first time in three years, answering something in the distance like recognizes like.

"Well," Maren said, already reaching for her shawl, calm as a woman going to check on bread. "It seems the fire's chosen its landing spot. Odile — back door's behind the pantry, and Bren's cart is still in my barn." Her eyes found theirs, steady and furious and kind all at once. "But understand me: this changes nothing about what I asked. Whatever you run toward now — *come back.* Do you hear me? Doors close. So do roads. Mine stays open."

Outside, faintly, across the wet fields, a white-robed figure raised a hand, and every bell in the village began to ring at once.
