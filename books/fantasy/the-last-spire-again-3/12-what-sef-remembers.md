## 12. What Sef Remembers

Sef knew the word for *night* in two languages now.

The new one was the language of Corvane, of Bren's maps and Maren's kettle and Odile's low voice saying *eat this, it won't kill you* about food that sometimes nearly did. It was a good language, round and warm, with words like *blanket* and *stay* that Sef collected the way other children collected river stones.

The old one was the one inside them.

They lay awake on the pallet by Maren's hearth, listening to the house breathe. Maren slept in the next room — Sef could tell by the slow rhythm of her cough, the way she turned twice and then settled, always twice, never three times. Odile did not sleep in the village at all anymore. Odile slept sitting up against the door of whatever room Sef was in, sword across their knees, which was silly, because everyone knew the Unwritten wouldn't come here, but Sef had decided not to say so. Some sillinesses were kindnesses.

The fire ticked down to embers. And in the embers, Sef saw the Spire.

That was how the remembering worked. Not like dreams. Dreams were things that happened to you. The remembering was more like — Sef chewed on it, turning the thought over the way Maren turned dough — more like something happening *in* you. Like the memory had been folded up small and tucked somewhere behind the ribs, and every night the warmth of the coals or the sound of rain or the smell of woodsmoke unfolded it a little further.

Tonight the fold came all the way open.

* * *

There was light first. That was how everything began there: light, and then the long slow hum, and then the shape of the Spire rising out of itself, black glass going up and up until "up" stopped meaning anything.

Sef had been there before they were born. That was the part nobody understood when Sef tried to say it in the new language. They had been there *before*, the way a song is in a flute before anyone plays it. The Spire had held them the way a cup holds water — patiently, completely, with no edges between.

They remembered being made. Not born. *Made.*

It had taken a hundred years. Maybe longer; time moved strangely inside the Spire, pooling in some corners and running thin in others. The Spire had gathered what it needed from the silence of the sealed god: an echo of a voice, the shape of a hand that would one day knock on a door in a city called Corvane, the particular stubbornness of a mercenary who kept swearing oaths even after the god went quiet. The Spire had known — because the Spire was the door, and doors know who is coming — that someone would come back. Someone sworn. Someone who would rather burn than break a promise.

So the Spire had made a key with legs.

Sef remembered looking down at their own hands for the first time. Small hands. New hands. They had been so *astonished* by hands. Fingers! You could hold things with them. You could press them against cold glass and feel the cold arrive. For a hundred years Sef had been a shape without edges and now suddenly there were edges everywhere, and each one was a marvel.

But along with the hands came the knowing. That was the bargain the Spire hadn't asked permission for. A vessel must understand its purpose, or it will spill. So Sef had been poured full of purpose the way they'd been poured full of self, and the purpose sat in them heavy as a swallowed stone:

*Be carried home. Be given back. Open.*

* * *

The trouble with knowing your purpose from the inside is that you can't un-know it.

Sef had tried, in the early days, walking out of the hills toward Corvane. They had tried thinking very hard about other purposes. Being a baker, perhaps. Bakers had flour and warm mornings and nobody ever gave a baker *back* to anything. Or a dog. Dogs seemed happy. But the stone in Sef's chest only grew heavier the farther they walked from anywhere, and lighter — horribly, treacherously lighter — whenever they turned toward the borderlands, toward the country that wasn't on any map, toward home.

Home. Even that word was a trick. Home was where the Spire was. Home was where Sef would stop.

They had learned words in the new language since then. *Choice.* *Freely given.* Maren said them softly, to Odile, in the next room, when she thought Sef was asleep. Sef always heard. Children hear everything; it is their single great power, greater than magic, and no adult has ever once believed this.

*Freely given,* the Spire required. That was carved into the oldest part of Sef, older than the hands. The door would not be forced. The payment could not be stolen or tricked or dragged. Someone had to walk up to the threshold and give — freely — a life.

Everyone in the village thought the giving was the hard part.

Sef knew better. Sef had spent whole nights understanding this while the others slept: the hard part wasn't the giving. The hard part was that everyone else got to decide about it. Maren decided when she begged Odile not to go. The survivors decided when they argued around the fire with their second names and their frightened eyes. The hunters in white decided when they marched. Odile decided — Odile was deciding right now, every hour, sitting against Sef's door with a sword — whether to carry Sef home like a lantern or hide Sef forever like a secret.

Nobody asked the lantern.

And that was the thing Sef couldn't say in either language, the thing that sat behind the stone: *I don't want to be a key.* Not because keys are terrible — keys open good things too, doors to kitchens and stables and rooms where people you love are waiting. But a key doesn't get to want things. A key is shaped entirely by the lock. Every part of Sef — the Old Tongue in their mouth, the frost that bloomed on windows when they spoke, the way the shard of black glass warmed when they were near it, the way grown-ups' faces went careful when they looked at Sef — every part had been shaped by the lock. Made for a door. Shaped by a door. And someday, if everyone's plans finally agreed with each other, *given to* a door.

Sef pressed their small, astonishing, edge-having hands over the stone in their chest and whispered, in the old tongue, the word for *no.*

The hearth frosted over. The embers hissed.

In the next room, Maren's breathing changed.

* * *

She came in wrapped in her shawl, hair loose, and didn't pretend she hadn't heard. She never pretended things to Sef; that was why Sef loved her, in the fierce uncomplicated way children love the people who feed them and don't lie to them.

"Bad night?" she said, and sat on the edge of the pallet without waiting for an answer, because she already knew.

Sef considered lying. Grown-ups lied constantly — *of course you're not in trouble, we're just talking, the road isn't much farther* — and maybe that was a skill worth learning. But the frost was still on the hearthstones, spelling nothing, just cold spreading in fern-patterns, and lies melt frost faster than fire does.

"The remembering came all the way open," Sef said.

Maren went still. In Sef's experience, grown-ups going still was worse than grown-ups shouting. Shouting meant they felt strong enough. Stillness meant they were holding something up that was very heavy.

"What did it show you?"

"Before." Sef pulled the blanket up under their chin. "The making. The waiting." They hesitated, then said the rest quickly, the way you pull off a bandage: "It made me out of the silence. Out of the god's last quiet and the door's own wanting. I'm not a child that the Spire found. I'm a child the Spire *made.* To be brought back."

Maren's hand found Sef's hair. Her fingers were rough from healing-work and smelled of yarrow.

"I know," she said quietly. "Sweetling. I've suspected since the ford."

"Then you know what I'm for."

"I know what you're *worth*," Maren said, "and those aren't the same thing, whatever the door thinks."

Sef wanted to believe her. Wanting to believe her felt like standing in warm shallows. But the stone knew better, and stones are honest in a way people can't afford to be.

"Odile will do it," Sef said. "If it comes to that. If there's no third path. Odile will give me back, because of the oath. Because Vess was theirs, and I'm—" the old word rose in them, the true one, and they let it out in Old Tongue because the new language had no shape for it— "*the last thing the god made.*"

The frost crept off the hearthstones and across the floorboards, delicate as lace.

"Sef." Maren's voice had gone very gentle, the voice she used for setting bones. "Listen to me. There are people in this village who paid that price before. Whole families. They gave, or they watched others give, and they built these houses on top of the grief, and they are *terrified* of you — not of you, of what you make possible again. Don't you dare start believing you're only what the Spire poured into you. You're also the child who ate six honey-buns at the market and lied about it with crumbs on your face. Doors don't eat honey-buns."

Sef laughed despite everything, and the frost stopped growing. Laughter always melted it a little. They'd noticed that early. Joy was the one thing the Spire hadn't put in them; joy they'd found on their own, out here, in the world with edges.

"Will you tell Odile?" Sef asked. "What I told you?"

"Do you want me to?"

Sef thought about it seriously, the way they thought about everything, turning it like a stone. If Odile knew, Odile would sit differently at the door tonight. Heavier. And Odile was already carrying so many heavings — the oath-mark, the memories, the guilt of surviving — that Sef sometimes felt like a pack-mule's burden had been handed to a person who was themselves a pack-mule.

"Not yet," Sef said. "Let me be a child for a few more nights. Before everyone starts deciding."

Maren bent and kissed the top of their head. "A few more nights," she promised. "And after that, we decide *with* you. All of us. That's the difference between us and the Spire, sweetling. We ask."

But as she rose to go, Sef caught her sleeve, and the question came out before they could weigh it, the true question, the one that had been unfolding in them alongside the memory:

"Maren. When the door opens — if it opens — do you think the god knows? Inside me. Do you think it can hear me wanting things?"

Maren stood in the ember-light a long moment.

"Yes," she said at last, so softly it was almost not a sound. "I think it hears everything you say. I think that's what's been keeping it alive."

She left. The house settled. Sef lay back down and stared at the dying fire, and far away — or not far away at all, but *inside*, behind the stone, curled up in the silence where a hundred years of waiting had been poured — something listened.

Something patient. Something that had waited three years and a hundred before that, and could wait a little longer.

And Sef, who was made of that patience, did the most human thing they knew.

They fell asleep planning to run.

Not far. Not yet. Just — tomorrow, when Odile walked down to the well, when Maren was grinding herbs, when no one was deciding anything — Sef would slip past the goat-pen and follow the frost-line north, the line of cold only they could see, the thread the Spire had left in them like a leash. They would find the door themselves. And they would stand in front of it, small and astonished and full of edges, and they would say *no* in two languages, loud enough that even a god would have to listen.

Or — whispered the stone — they would say *yes.*

The fire died. Outside, the first snow of the season began to fall, and every flake that touched the window above Sef's pallet melted into the same shape: a small black spire, pointing home.
