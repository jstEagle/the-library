## 15. Halloway's Offer

Odile knew they were being followed three days before Magistrate Halloway finally showed his face.

They'd learned to read the texture of a tail the way other people read weather. A Church censor moved differently than a thief—thieves watched your purse, censors watched your hands—and for three days now there had been a man in a gray coat lingering at the mouth of Ash Street with the particular stillness of someone who had been told *watch* and was taking it literally.

"He's back," Sef said, not looking up from the knot they were tying. The child had gotten good at luck-knots in the weeks since the ford. Their fingers were small and quick, and their knots came out crooked in a way that customers found charming. "The coat man. He bought bread twice so he could stand near the bakery."

"Twice?"

"Once he gave the second loaf to a dog." Sef frowned. "I don't think it's his dog."

"No," Odile said, tying off a charm-cord with more force than necessary. "I don't imagine it is."

Maren had gone ahead to the village with Bren's map-burned arm wrapped in linen and most of Bren himself wrapped in grief. Odile had meant to follow within a day. Then the gray coat appeared, and following became impossible, because a censor on your heels is a thread that leads straight home.

So they waited. Which, Odile reflected, was its own kind of skill—the same art as invisibility, just with worse pay.

He came at dusk, when the stall was half-packed and the lamplighters were making their rounds. Not in the gray coat. In plain clothes, actually, which fooled no one; Halloway walked like a man who had never once been uncertain of his right to stand wherever he stood. He stopped at the stall, considered the little rows of knots and charms and bottled blessings that couldn't bless so much as a headache, and picked up a luck-knot between two fingers like something he'd scraped off his boot.

"These are illegal," he said.

"They're decorative," Odile said. "It's cord and a bead. If you pray hard enough at a bead, magistrate, that's between you and your god."

"My god doesn't listen."

"Mine neither, lately."

Something flickered across Halloway's face—not anger. Something wearier. He set the knot down, took out a coin, and paid for it anyway, which was somehow more unsettling than an arrest would have been.

"You're a difficult woman to corner, Blackwood."

"I'm not a woman, but I'll take the compliment."

"A difficult *person* to corner, then. I've had men watching this street since the ford. Do you know what my superiors wanted me to do when I reported the crossing?"

"Arrest us."

"Burn the riverbank." He said it flatly, without drama, which made it worse. "Standard practice for Unwritten activity. There was a ferryman who saw too much. There was a fisherman." He let that sit. "I filed the report late. By the time the writ came through, you were miles gone, and the fisherman has decided he saw nothing, and the ferryman has taken work inland. I find that paperwork delays save more lives than swords do. It's the one heresy I allow myself."

Odile kept their hands busy with the cords. "You want a medal for being slow?"

"I want five minutes and a cup of whatever you're pretending isn't tea." Halloway glanced at Sef, who was watching him with the flat, unblinking assessment of a child who had been hunted long enough to know what hunters looked like. "Not in front of the little one."

"There's nothing you can say in front of them that I don't already know how to survive."

"Perhaps." Halloway's gaze didn't move from Sef. "But can they?"

* * *

Halloway's point of view began, as all his worst days did, with a ledger.

Thirty-one years in the Church of the Unwritten, and what he had learned could be written on one page: erasure is not fire. Fire leaves ash, and ash can be counted. Erasure leaves a hole shaped exactly like the thing that used to be there, and everyone walks around the hole pretending it's a doorway, and after a while even the ones who remember start to doubt.

He had a hole in his own house. His sister Alise had married a man from over the borderlands, before the borderlands stopped being anything at all. One morning she'd gone to visit, and she hadn't come back, and by winter there was no record she had ever existed—no marriage lines, no letters, and worst of all, no grief in anyone but him. His own mother had asked him why he kept setting a fourth place at supper. He'd stopped answering eventually. You can't argue with a hole. That was rather the point of one.

So when the reports came in—a mute child speaking Old Tongue in Corvane, frost on windows, a spire-shaped shard of black glass—he had done what he always did. He'd slowed the paperwork. And then he'd gone to look for himself, because thirty-one years teaches you that the people above you are managing holes, and sometimes you need to see what's standing in one.

What he'd seen was a mercenary selling beads. And a child who tied crooked knots and fed stray dogs stolen bread, and flinched at white cloth, and hummed—hummed—in a language that had been unwritten so thoroughly that Halloway's own training manuals described it as *never having existed.*

He sat across from them now in the back room of the stall, on a stool that complained under him, holding tea that tasted like hot water that had once been introduced to a leaf. The child had been sent to bed behind the curtain, though he could hear them breathing, which meant they were listening, which he respected.

"You know what happens if I take you in," he said.

"The child goes to the Choir of Quiet Names," Odile said. "And I go somewhere with fewer windows."

"And the villages. Every hidden settlement along the old roads. We've known about them longer than you'd think—we simply haven't had cause to spend the ink." He turned his cup slowly. "A new Spire-warden changes the arithmetic. My superiors believe the child is a key. They may be wrong about the theology, but they are not wrong about the danger. If the door opens—"

"It won't open."

"If it opens, everything the Church spent three centuries burying comes back through it. And the Church does not survive things coming back. Neither, incidentally, does anyone standing nearby." He leaned forward. "So here is what I am authorized to offer, and here is what I'm going to offer instead, and you may decide which is which.

"Authorized: surrender the child to the Choir, name every village, receive a full pardon and a pension. Refuse: we burn this district, the villages, the roads, the lot of it, starting Thursday, because Thursday is when the writ arrives and I cannot slow the paperwork forever."

Odile said nothing. Their face did the thing it always did—went smooth and unreadable, a shopkeeper's face—but their hands had gone still, and Halloway had interrogated enough people to know that stillness was the tell.

"And yours?" Odile said finally.

"Yours is the same offer with one difference. Give me the child, and I will lose the paperwork on the villages. All of them. Permanently. I've done it before—I lost a whole valley once, very careless of me. They live. No fire, no Choir, no questions." He set the cup down. "That's my offer. One life against thousands of people who get to keep existing quietly."

"That's not an offer. That's a bill."

"Yes," Halloway said. "Most offers are."

* * *

Odile walked him to the door because it seemed politer than throwing him out, and also because their legs needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

"One question," Odile said at the threshold. "Why? Thirty-one years. Why carry water for them?"

Halloway paused on the step. The lamplighter's flame caught the lines of his face, and for a moment the censor vanished and there was only a tired old man whose family had a hole in it.

"Her name was Alise," he said. "She liked bad poetry and she cheated at cards. She's not in any book anymore, Blackwood. Not one. When I die, no one living will know she existed—except me, and I'm the wrong witness, because grief makes unreliable testimony." He adjusted his collar. "You have a country that was erased. I understand you want to open the door and bring it back. But I've spent my career learning what comes back through doors like that. Nothing comes back. Only the debt does. The dead stay dead either way. The only question is who pays."

"Alise," Odile repeated. Just the name. Just to prove someone besides him could hold it.

Halloway's breath caught—small, quickly mastered, but Odile heard it.

"Thursday," he said. "Give me your answer before Thursday. After that, I'm only the uniform again." He walked three steps into the dark, then stopped. "For what it's worth—the knot I bought. It's rubbish. Purely decorative."

"So's most luck," Odile said. "Doesn't mean it doesn't work."

Behind them, from behind the curtain, came a small voice in the Old Tongue—soft, deliberate, unmistakably meant to be heard:

*"He keeps the fourth plate set."*

Halloway went very still.

Then, without turning around, in a voice roughened to gravel: "Tell the child—" He stopped. Started again. "Tell them thank you. And tell them the answer to the riddle they left on my window last week was correct. The frost spelling. I checked it against the archives." A pause. "There are no archives. I checked against memory. Mine."

He walked away into the dark, and Odile stood in the doorway a long time, holding a decorative knot that suddenly weighed as much as a country.

Inside, Sef was sitting up in the dark, hugging their knees.

"He'll do it," the child said. "Burn it. If I don't go."

"I know."

"But if I *do* go—" Sef's voice cracked, and for once the Old Tongue failed them and they finished in ordinary words, the way children do when the thing is too big for beautiful language. "If I go, I stop. Don't I. Like the door wants. I stop."

Odile sat down on the edge of the cot. Didn't touch them yet. Some griefs need room to breathe.

"I don't know," they said honestly. "I don't know what the door does or what Vess wants or whether there's a third road. I know what I'm not doing, and that's deciding about you while you're sitting right here listening."

Sef leaned against them anyway. Small and warm and smelling of woodsmoke and luck-knots.

"Thursday," the child whispered.

Outside, far down the dark street, a gray-coated figure who was not Halloway peeled away from the bakery wall and moved quickly toward the district offices—moving, Odile would learn soon enough and too late, with a message about routes and river fords and a healer named Maren, sold cheap to buy a brother's pardon.

Thursday was coming faster than anyone knew.
