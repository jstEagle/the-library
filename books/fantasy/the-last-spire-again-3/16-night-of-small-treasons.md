## 16. Night of Small Treasons

Maren Ashvale had delivered forty-one babies in the Village of Second Names, and she had learned something from every single one of them: people arrive screaming into this world because they already know what it costs to stay.

She was thinking about that on the evening everything went wrong, standing in her stillroom with a mortar in one hand and a sprig of feverfew going soft between her fingers, when Tam Rooker knocked at her door with the particular knock of a man who has done something he cannot take back.

Three knocks. A pause long enough to say a prayer. Two more.

Nobody in the village used that knock except for one purpose, and they hadn't needed it in nine years.

"Come in before you freeze out there," she called, though the night was mild, "and before you lose your nerve, which is the more likely danger."

Tam came in the way wet paper comes apart. He was a broad man, a farmer, a father of two girls with second names as bright as their first ones had been dark, and he stood in her doorway dripping rain and guilt in equal measure until she pointed him into a chair and pushed a cup of willowbark tea into his hands mostly so that he would have something to do besides wring them.

"Say it," she said.

"I can't."

"You walked across the village in the rain to say it. Say it while you still remember why you came."

He looked down at the tea. The tea looked back, in the way of teas brewed by Maren, who had never mastered the trick of making anything — medicine, bread, conversation — entirely comfortable.

"They found me," Tam said finally. "The white-robe. Not in the village. He found me at the ford market three days past. I'd gone for salt." He laughed, a terrible little sound. "Salt, Maren. All of this for salt."

"Who found you?"

"The magistrate. Halloway. He knew my face." Tam's hands tightened around the cup. "Not my new face. My old one. He said my name — my *first* name, the one nobody's spoken since we burned the records — and he said it kindly, Maren, that's the worst of it, he said it like a man greeting a friend across a graveyard."

Maren sat down across from him. Her knees complained. Her knees had been complaining for years; she had stopped listening to them around the same time she'd stopped listening to most things that only wanted sympathy.

"What did he want?"

"He wanted the road." Tam's voice dropped to almost nothing, as if the walls themselves might be informants. "The route Odile and the child are taking to the Spire. He said if he had it, if the Church took them quietly, without ever tracing them back here — then Corvane forgets us again. Nine more years of being no one. Nine more years of my girls growing up with names that keep."

The fire popped. Outside, someone was laughing — Odile's voice, actually, carrying from the direction of the communal hall, where Sef was apparently winning some game of stones through sheer refusal to understand the rules. The child cheated magnificently and without malice, and the village had fallen half in love with them for it.

"And you told him," Maren said.

It wasn't a question. She watched him break, nodding, tears cutting clean lines through the road-dust on his face, and she felt — not anger, not first. What she felt first was the cold, professional clarity of a healer seeing a wound she already knows how to close.

"I told him the false route," Tam whispered. "Bren's map. The one through the Hollow Fells. But Maren — the *real* route goes within a day's walk of the same ground. If they scout the fells and find nothing, they'll cast wider, and they'll find —"

"I know what they'll find."

"And if they trace it back, if anyone learns I spoke —" His voice cracked clean through. "They'll unname my girls. You know what unnaming is now. It isn't a record burned anymore. It's a *person* burned. White robes walking through the fields, and everything that loved you forgetting your face."

Maren let the silence sit for a moment, because silences were also medicine, and this one needed time to work.

Then she said: "Here is what happens now, Tam Rooker, and you will listen to every word because I will not say it twice.

"You are going home. You are going to kiss your daughters goodnight and eat your supper and complain about the barley like a man whose conscience weighs exactly what it did last week. You are not going to hang yourself from the mill beam, which I can see you considering, and which would be the stupidest thing a man with your shoulders has ever done. And you are not going to confess to anyone else in this village, ever, because a secret shared by two people is a burden, and a secret shared by a village is a bonfire."

"But you'll tell them. Odile. You'll have to warn them, and then they'll know it was me, and —"

"I will handle it," Maren said, "the way I handle everything in this village. Badly, at first, and then well enough."

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat up instead with Bren's map spread across her table — the real one, the cartographer's true route inked in that careful, cramped hand of his, all the way from the village to the shadowed spur where the Last Spire waited — and she studied it the way she studied a fever chart. Looking for the turn. Looking for the place where the sickness entered.

The map didn't answer. Maps rarely did. In her experience, most answers came from people at inconvenient hours, looking guilty.

Near dawn, she made her decision, and it was this: she would cover the betrayal. Not bury it — wounds buried fester — but dress it. She would change the route herself. Ink a false fork, a wrong turning, send any hunters following the leaked path into the Drowned Meadows where the ground swallowed horses and the fog swallowed everything else. It would buy days. Perhaps a week. Enough for Odile and Sef to reach the Spire or die trying, which were, Maren was learning, the only two options this story had on offer.

And then she would tell Odile the truth herself, because a love built on a covered lie was just a prettier kind of betrayal, and she had spent too many years becoming honest to stop now.

The ink work took her an hour. Her hand shook only a little. When she finished, the map showed a route that bent east toward the meadows, plausible, even sensible-looking, with the false fork drawn in Bren's cramped style, which she could imitate well enough to fool a stranger and not nearly well enough to fool a dead friend, which was why it mattered that Bren was dead.

"There," she told the empty room. "One small treason to hide another."

The room, being a room, declined to absolve her.

* * *

She found Odile at the woodpile behind the communal hall, splitting logs with the grim efficiency of a person who preferred problems that fought back. Sweat-dark hair, sleeves rolled, the old oath-mark visible at their wrist like a brand that had never quite healed — which, Maren supposed, it hadn't.

"You're doing that wrong," she said.

"I'm doing it fine."

"You're splitting against the grain and blaming the axe. Give it here."

Odile surrendered the axe with the wariness of a soldier handed a parcel. Maren set it down, looked at them properly — the shadows under their eyes, the way they kept touching their wrist — and felt her prepared speech rearrange itself entirely.

"Sef's asleep?" she asked.

"Dead to the world. They ate four bowls of stew and fell asleep mid-sentence, which I'm told is a family trait." A pause. "You've got your telling-off face on."

"Do I have a telling-off face?"

"You have several faces and I've catalogued them. That one says someone's about to be disappointed in me, and I should decide quickly whether to argue."

Maren laughed despite everything — a short, startled sound, like a bird flushing early. "Damn you. I rehearsed this."

"Rehearsed what?"

So there was nothing for it but the truth, laid out plain on the chopping block between them like something to be split.

"Someone sold your route," she said. "To Halloway. Three days ago, at the ford market."

She watched Odile go very still — the stillness of a person who has been ambushed before and knows that the first ten seconds decide everything. Their eyes flicked once toward the hall where Sef slept, and Maren saw the whole calculation happen: distance to the child, weight of the axe, number of exits.

"Who," Odile said.

"That's mine to carry, not yours."

"Maren."

"No." She held up a hand. "I mean that literally. It's mine. I've carried it since midnight, I've mended what could be mended, and I'm telling you the rest because you deserve a warning, not a name to hate. Hunters will move on the false route within days. I've drawn them a better wrong answer than the one they bought. Your true road holds — but you leave tomorrow, at dawn, and you don't come back this way. Ever."

Odile stared at her. Then, horribly, they began to laugh — low and bitter and not really laughter at all.

"Of course," they said. "Of course someone sold us. We're a village full of people whose entire survival strategy is pretending not to exist, and we invited a living key to the forbidden kingdom to sit in our hall and win at stones. I'm surprised it took three days. I'm surprised *I* haven't sold us yet, out of professional courtesy."

"It wasn't malice." Maren heard her own voice soften and hated how much it softened. "It was fear. The good kind. The kind that has daughters."

Odile went quiet again. And then, slowly, they looked at her — really looked, the way they'd been not-quite-looking at her for weeks, the careful sideways glances of a person relearning how to be seen — and Maren understood with sickening clarity that Odile already knew. Or suspected. Or simply knew *her*, which amounted to the same thing.

"It was Tam," Odile said quietly. "Wasn't it."

"How —"

"Because you're covering for him. Because you're standing there with your chin up and your hands steady, doing the thing you do, and the only person in this village you'd do it *this hard* for is someone whose children call you auntie." Odile exhaled. "I was a mercenary for eleven years, Maren. I know what protection looks like from the inside. It looks exactly like you right now."

The morning birds had started up. Somewhere a kettle whistled and nobody moved toward it.

"Are you angry?" Maren asked.

"Yes," Odile said. Then, after a moment: "No. Both. It's — complicated, like most weather. Ask me again when we're not standing over an axe."

"Odile. Listen to me." She stepped closer, close enough to see the exhaustion underneath the composure, the fear under the exhaustion. "What he did was betray you. What I did was help him get away with it. Those aren't the same crime, but they share a roof, and I'm not asking you to pretend otherwise. I'm asking —"

She stopped. Swallowed. Found, to her considerable irritation, that her eyes were burning.

"I'm asking you to forgive us anyway. Not because we deserve it. We don't; I've checked. I'm asking because you're about to climb a mountain to trade yourself for a child, and I cannot bear the thought of you doing it carrying this on top of everything else. Put it down. Leave it here with me. Let me be the one who carries the ugly thing so you can carry the necessary one."

For a long moment Odile said nothing. The wind moved the woodpile dust between them. Then they reached out — hesitantly, as if asking permission — and took her hand.

"Maren Ashvale," they said, "you are the most infuriating woman I have met since I stopped being paid to be insulted professionally. Forgiveness isn't a feeling. I learned that from a god who never gave me one. It's a choice you make and then make again tomorrow, and the day after, until it sticks." Their grip tightened. "So ask me again tomorrow. And the day after. I'll keep choosing yes. It's the one vow left in me that works properly."

Maren laughed and cried at the same time, which she had done at forty-one births and considered, on balance, her signature move.

"You're going to get yourself killed," she managed.

"Probably."

"Good. Then I won't have to keep forgiving you. Much less work."

But neither of them let go of the other's hand, and the kettle screamed itself hoarse in the empty kitchen, and above the eastern ridge — where the true road ran, and the false road failed, and the Spire stood waiting in its country that did not exist — the sky was turning the pale, unforgiving grey of a dawn that intended to make demands.

They would leave at first light. All of them. Even the ones who stayed behind.

Especially those.
