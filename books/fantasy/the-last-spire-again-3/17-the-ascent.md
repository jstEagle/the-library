## 17. The Ascent

The map Bren had burned into his own skin was not, strictly speaking, a map anymore. It was a memory of a map, and memories do not survive their owners gracefully.

Odile held the dead man's forearm in both hands and turned it toward the grey dawn light, the way one might turn a page too fragile to trust. The ink had gone strange since the ford — since Bren had stood hip-deep in freezing water with an Unwritten's white hand closing around his throat and laughed and said, *Watch this,* and set himself alight from the inside out. Now the lines of rivers and ridges crawled across his skin like things alive, rearranging themselves as the country beneath them changed its mind about existing.

"He always did like to have the last word," Odile said quietly, and wrapped the arm back in its linen, and did not let their hands shake where Sef could see.

They climbed.

The road — if it could be called that — began at the tree line above the ford and went up through country that had no right to still be here. Corvane's censors had unmade these lands three years ago; every official atlas ended at a blank margin labeled only *former territories*. And yet here were stone walls running along the hillside, built by no one, holding in fields that grew a crop Odile could not name but whose smell made their chest ache like a bruise. Here were fences with no gates, doors with no houses behind them, a well at the summit of a hill with no village around it, still drawing sweet water for anyone thirsty enough to climb.

"Is it real?" Sef asked, in Old Tongue, pointing at the well.

Odile had stopped trying to answer that question honestly. "It's real enough to drink," they said, which was the most truthful thing anyone said all morning.

The child drank. The child was nine, maybe ten, mud-colored hair cropped short by Odile's own knife two weeks ago when the lice got bad, and walked with the tireless rolling gait of someone who had been walking since before they could remember why. Around Sef's neck, on a leather cord, hung the shard of black glass shaped like a spire. It had been cold as riverbed stone in Corvane. It was warm now. By midday it would be warm as a sleeping bird, and Odile tried very hard not to think about what that warmth wanted.

* * *

*Sef counted the white robes.*

That was the game. Maren had taught it to them on the first night in the Village of Second Names: *when you are frightened, count things.* Counting kept the fear in a small box instead of letting it fill up the whole house of you.

Sef counted four white robes on the ridge behind them. Then the light bent wrong, the way it did sometimes, and there were six. Then the light bent again and there was one robe wearing the shapes of all the others, like a person wearing many coats.

Sef decided that counted as one.

"They're following," Sef said, because Odile had told them always to say it out loud, even when it was obvious, especially when it was obvious. Saying it out loud made it a thing in the world instead of a thing in the head.

"I know." Odile didn't look back. Odile almost never looked back, which Sef understood now was not the same as not being afraid. Odile carried fear somewhere behind the face, the way the well carried water down under the stones.

"Will they erase us?"

Odile stopped walking. For a moment they just breathed, and Sef watched the breath go in and come out and thought about how grown-ups did that — took a moment to decide which truth they could afford.

"Not you," Odile said finally. "Not while I'm breathing."

"But after?"

"After," Odile said, "I intend to be breathing for a very long time. Come on. Bren says—" they caught themselves, jaw tightening, and Sef felt the old sadness move through the air between them, the sadness that lived in the wrapped forearm and in the way Odile sometimes spoke to the dead man in the present tense. "Bren *said* the pass opens above the scree field. We make the scree by dark."

Sef looked back once, quickly, the way you look at a wound. The single coat-shaped robe was standing perfectly still on the ridge, watching, patient as frost. It raised one white hand — not threatening, not waving. *Noting.* That was what the Unwritten did. They noted you. And everything they noted long enough slowly stopped having ever happened.

Sef touched the spire-shard. It pulsed against their palm, gentle as a heartbeat, and far away — or perhaps not far away at all, perhaps underneath everything, perhaps inside Sef's own ribs — something vast and silent leaned closer to listen.

*Come home*, said the silence.

Sef put their hand in Odile's and climbed.

* * *

By the third day the country stopped pretending.

It began small. A bird crossed the sky and forgot itself halfway, becoming a smear of color, then a suggestion of color, then nothing, and the space where it had been closed up like water over a stone. Then the road itself began to flicker — solid shale underfoot one step, soft grey nothing the next, so that they learned to walk on faith and Bren's arm-map, which glowed faintly now in the dark, the lines of it brightening wherever the true path lay, dimming wherever the world had eaten the road away.

"The Unwritten aren't chasing us anymore," Odile said on the third evening, crouched over the map-arm by firelight. There was no wood up here, properly speaking; they burned a kind of pale grass that gave off light without heat, another mercy of a country that existed only out of stubbornness. "They don't need to chase. They're herding. Every path that leads anywhere but the Spire is dissolving. They want us at the door."

"That's good, isn't it?" Sef asked. "Going to the door."

Odile was quiet for a long time. Firelight moved across a face that had spent years learning to be forgettable, and failed completely at it now, because worry had carved everything back in.

"Sef," they said at last. "Do you remember being made?"

The child shook their head. "Maren says nobody remembers being born."

"You weren't born. You said — you remembered being chosen. In your dreams. What does chosen feel like?"

Sef considered this with the gravity the question deserved, chewing a strand of the pale grass, which tasted like cold air and nothing else. "Like being a glove," they said. "Like somebody's hand is already in me. But it's very careful. It doesn't wiggle the fingers much."

Odile made a sound that might have been a laugh in a better life, or a sob in a worse one. "That," they said, "is the best description of godhood I've ever heard, and I swore thirty years of service to one."

"Did it wiggle your fingers?"

"All the time." Odile looked at their own hands then, at the oath-mark on the inner wrist — the faded spiral that had ached for three years, that had answered prayers in a child's voice at the ruined shrine, that was, Odile now knew, a leash with the other end buried inside the child across the fire. They flexed the fingers slowly, deliberately, as if proving ownership. "Listen to me, Sef. Whatever happens at the Spire. Whatever it asks. Whatever I seem to agree to. You are not a payment. You are not a key. Keys get used. You are a person who likes counting white robes and complains about my cooking, and those things matter more than any door."

"Doors matter a lot," Sef said fairly. "You can't get inside without them."

"No," Odile agreed softly. "But some doors should stay shut. And some people should stay people." They fed another handful of pale grass to the lightless fire. "Sleep. Tomorrow we climb the last stretch."

* * *

*On the fourth day, Sef saw the country remember itself.*

It came in flashes, like lightning frozen mid-strike. One moment the hills were grey suggestion, half-dissolved, the valleys filled with a fog that was not weather but absence. The next — a flash, and everything was *there*: terraced orchards heavy with fruit no one had picked, a bell tower with its bell still swinging though no wind blew, a road lined with mile-markers carved in Old Tongue. Sef could read them. Sef had never been taught, but Sef could read them, the way Sef could speak without trying, because the words had been poured into Sef along with everything else when the Spire made them.

*HERE THE KINGDOM OF VESS. HERE THE LONG QUIET BEGAN.*

A flash, and the orchards were gone. Another flash, and a woman stood in one, laughing at someone out of sight, and she wore the colors of Odile's old company, and Sef opened their mouth to say so and the flash took her too.

"It's deciding," Sef said.

"What is?" Odile didn't break stride. The scree field rose ahead of them, a great grey apron of broken stone below a notch in the mountains, and beyond the notch — Sef could feel it more than see it, a pressure like the moment before thunder — the Last Spire.

"The country. Whether to exist." Sef skipped over a gap where the ground had simply given up. "It keeps looking at me to check."

Odile went very still on the mountainside. "Looking at you how?"

"Like the well looked. Like the bell tower looks." Sef hugged the shard through their shirt. "Everything here is waiting to find out if it gets to be real again. And it thinks I'm the answer."

The wind came down off the notch then, and it smelled — Odile caught it, and their whole body flinched like a struck drum — it smelled of woodsmoke and barley bread and the particular resin the temple candles of Vess had been made from, the smell of a thousand ordinary mornings in a kingdom that history had swallowed. Home, in other words. Home, weaponized.

"We keep climbing," Odile said, in a voice sanded flat by will alone. "One foot. Then the other. That's the whole plan. Don't let go of my hand."

* * *

They reached the notch at dusk.

The Unwritten were waiting — of course they were; the herding had been thorough — a crescent of white robes strung across the narrow saddle of rock, a dozen of them, motionless, faces smooth as unwritten pages. Their leader stepped forward. Where a face should have been there was a faint shimmer, like heat-haze, like text seen through frosted glass.

*ODILE BLACKWOOD,* it said, in a voice like paper turning. *SWORN OF VESS. LAST OF THE SWORN. YOU CARRY WHAT IS OURS.*

"It isn't yours," Odile said. "It never belonged to anyone. That's rather the point of it."

*THE DOOR OPENS FOR A LIFE FREELY GIVEN. THE VESSEL WAS MADE FOR THIS PURPOSE. STAND ASIDE AND WE WILL TAKE ONLY WHAT WAS ALWAYS MEANT TO BE TAKEN.*

And Odile — tired, grieving, one dead comrade's arm wrapped under their cloak and one living comrade's hand gripped tight in theirs — Odile smiled. It was not a nice smile. It was the smile they had worn across thirty years of battlefields, the one that had earned them the name Blackwood, and it said: *I have already decided what I am willing to lose, and you are not on the list.*

"You've made one error," Odile said pleasantly, drawing Sef a half-step behind them. "*Freely given.* Look it up. Whichever of you wrote the rulebook. Freely means freely. Nobody in this transaction is giving you anything today except trouble."

The shimmering face tilted. Somewhere beneath the mountain, deep in the roots of the world, something vast shifted its weight — and the shard at Sef's throat blazed suddenly hot, and every white robe took one step backward, which no Unwritten had done in recorded memory.

The leader's voice, when it came again, was quieter. Almost respectful. Almost afraid.

*THE GOD DOES NOT AGREE WITH YOU.*

"Good," said Odile Blackwood, and pulled Sef past them through the gap, because a dozen erasers had just stepped aside, and whatever that meant — whether Vess was clearing the road or clearing the board — it meant the door was open ahead, and the last climb lay straight up into the dark.

Behind them, the country flickered one final time: orchards, bell tower, laughing woman, all of it burning bright as a struck match.

Then the mountain swallowed them, and the Spire rose black against the stars, and it was waiting.
