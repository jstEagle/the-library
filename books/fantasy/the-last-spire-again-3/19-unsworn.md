## 19. Unsworn

I have stood before death more times than I can count, and every one of those times I had a sword in my hand and someone at my back. This time I had neither. I had a child's hand in mine, cold as river stones, and a door made of silence, and the whole weight of a kingdom that no longer existed pressing down on me like snow on a roof that was never built to hold it.

*Give me what was promised,* said Vess, through Sef's mouth.

The god's voice did not boom. That was the horror of it. It came out of the child the way water comes out of a cracked jug—quietly, inevitably, pooling everywhere. Sef's face stayed Sef's face: smudged, freckled, one front tooth missing since the ford. But behind the eyes something vast had turned over, like a whale surfacing under a rowboat, and I felt very small and very breakable, and also—I am ashamed to say this—*home.*

That is what they don't tell you about gods. When they finally speak to you after years of silence, some traitorous part of you purrs like a cat by a fire, no matter what they're saying.

"No," I said.

The word came out steadier than I felt. Good. Three years of selling luck-knots to people who didn't believe in luck had taught me at least that much theater.

Sef's head tilted. The whale beneath the surface shifted. *You refuse the vessel. You refuse to walk away. What remains, Blackwood?*

"My name," I said. "You can have my name."

Which was, of course, a lie, because names were the one thing I'd been hoarding all this time—mine, Sef's, the old ones I whispered into charm-knots when Corvane wasn't listening. But gods deal in lies the way merchants deal in coin. They know how to test them.

Silence. Not the ordinary kind, either. The Spire's threshold held a silence so complete I could hear my own heart stuttering through it, could hear Sef breathing beside me, quick and shallow, and underneath both, something older listening.

Then, gently—the way you'd take a splinter from a child's foot—

It began.

***

People describe pain wrong, I think, or maybe everyone's pain is different and we just use the same few words because language is lazy. Mine was not fire. Fire would have been honest. Mine was *untying*. Somewhere behind my sternum there had lived, for eleven years, a knot. Sworn-bond. Oath-mark. The thing the priestesses of Vess tied into your soul with words in the Old Tongue while you knelt on cold stone and promised yourself away.

I felt it loosen.

And everything it held came loose with it.

Do you know what an oath actually is? Not the ceremony. The thing itself. I'll tell you, because I'm the last person who will ever be able to tell anyone: an oath is a rope thrown across a gap. On one end, you. On the other, everything you've sworn to. And the rope doesn't just hold you up—it *remembers* for you. Every prayer you've ever said, every battle, every dawn watch, every comrade's laugh—it knots all of it into the fiber so you never have to carry it alone.

Vess pulled the rope in.

I went to my knees on the threshold stone. Sef cried out—my name, or the shape of my name, in that liquid Old Tongue—and reached for me, and I wanted to say *don't touch me, whatever happens, stay back*, but my mouth wouldn't cooperate, and honestly, when the child wrapped their arms around my shoulders, I stopped wanting to say anything at all.

Because here is the other thing nobody tells you: it didn't only hurt. It also *left*, and the leaving felt like spring.

Memories went first in order of age, like books taken off a shelf from the bottom. I felt the Spire's great hall go—the pillars carved with ten thousand small gods, each one a household spirit, each one somebody's grandmother's kitchen guardian. I felt the oath-day go: kneeling, the priestess Maren-of-then (no—another Maren? the same? I couldn't hold her face) tying the mark into my wrist with a thread that burned. I felt the mountains go. The real mountains, the ones with the passes named after dogs, because our people loved their dogs better than their heroes and saw no reason to pretend otherwise.

I grabbed at them. That's the undignified truth. I clawed at my own memories like a man grabbing at laundry blown off a line in a gale, and I caught one—a small one, stupid one—and held it with everything left in me:

Bren, aged nineteen, falling into a creek during a mapping exercise, standing up dripping and furious and announcing to the entire company that the creek was now officially renamed and he would see it done.

I held that one like a coal in cupped hands.

The rest poured out between my fingers.

*Let go,* said Vess—not cruelly. Never cruelly. That was the worst of it. My god had always been gentle the way winter is gentle, the way an avalanche is simply doing what gravity asked of it. *You swore yourself to me. All of you. I am only collecting what was given.*

"You're collecting a *child*," I said, through teeth I could barely feel anymore. "That was never given."

*The vessel was made for return. It agreed—*

"It was *made*." I laughed, and it came out broken, which was fitting. "Gods below—gods *below*, listen to me, there are none below anymore, I put them all in you—" I dragged air into lungs that felt full of ash. "Made isn't agreed. Made is made. Ask any slave in any country you ever failed to save."

The silence that followed had texture. Thought in it.

And Sef—Sef, who understood everything said in the Old Tongue without trying, who had been listening to god-words pour through their own mouth like everyone else listens to rain—Sef looked up at me and said, in Corvanish, plain and rough-edged and entirely their own:

"You're forgetting me too."

"I'm not," I lied again, and this time the lie had no chance at all, because even as I said it I could feel the edges of them going soft. The color of their eyes. Which ear they favored when they listened. Whether they slept curled up or starfished across every available surface. A door was closing room by room inside me and Sef was standing in one of those rooms waving.

So I did the only brave thing I have ever done, which is the same brave thing everyone does eventually: I chose the present tense.

"Take the bond instead," I said. "All of it. Eleven years of service. Every prayer, every kill, every vigil. The whole rope. Me, not the child. Not a vessel. A volunteer."

*I did not ask for—*

"You didn't ask at all! That's rather the point!" My voice cracked across the threshold and the Spire seemed to lean in around us, ancient stone attending. "You sat silent for three years while your people scattered and forgot their own alphabet, and then you sent a *child* to collect a *debt*, and now you stand there being patient at me like patience is a virtue. It isn't. Patience is what predators do." I pulled Sef closer. "So here's my counteroffer, Vess of the Quiet Hand, last god of a country you couldn't keep: take the oath. Take all of it. Burn it out root and branch. And let the child go home with nothing owed, nothing remembered, nothing *waiting*."

*If I take the bond entire,* the god said slowly, *you will lose the kingdom. All of it. Language, land, faces. You will not mourn it—you will simply never have had it. You will be a woman who sells charms in a city that forbids them, and nothing else.*

"Yes."

*You will forget why you climbed.*

"Yes."

*You may even forget the child's name. Names are the first things to go. They are only thread, and thread goes first.*

My chest did something complicated and awful.

"Then I'll teach it to the world first," I said. "Before you take it. Write it where it can't be unwritten—in people. Children remember what adults burn. Give me that much. Let me spend the name before you take the purse."

Another long silence. And then—this is true, I swear it on the one memory I had left—something that might have been laughter moved through the Spire. Dry. Rusty. Like a hinge used after years.

*Three years I waited,* said Vess, *for a soldier to offer me obedience. And in the end the one who comes is the one who argues.*

"We argued constantly. You remember. Well." My throat closed. "You remember."

*I accept,* said the god.

And finished untying the knot.

***

Pain, then. Real pain this time, fire-honest, the oath-mark on my wrist going white-hot and then—worse—going *cold*, cold as frost on windows, cold as the day the kingdom vanished, and I screamed, I have no dignity to protect on this page, I screamed loud enough that birds went up from the dead orchards around the Spire in a black startled cloud.

And then it was done.

The quiet afterward was total.

Not the Spire's silence. An ordinary silence. Wind in bare branches. Sef sniffling against my shoulder. My own ragged breathing. I knelt on the stone and waited for the grief, braced for it the way you brace for a wave—

and it didn't come.

That's the part I want written down somewhere, if anything of me lasts: the grief didn't come, because there was nothing left to grieve *with*. I looked inward and found clean shelves. Empty rooms. A woman named Odile Blackwood who sold charms, who was kneeling in the mountains for reasons she couldn't name, holding a mud-streaked child who was crying at her and calling her something in a language that sounded, to her ears, like beautiful nonsense.

I looked at my wrist. There was a pale scar there, thin as thread. I had no idea what it was from. Some old injury. Soldiers collected them like coins.

"Are you—" Sef started, in Corvanish now, hesitant, testing the words. Their Old Tongue was gone too, I realized. Whatever the god had taken from me, it had also released from the child. No more vessel. No more key. Just a kid, maybe seven years old, in a coat two sizes too big, with a missing tooth and a shard of black glass still clutched in one fist.

The shard crumbled as we watched. It went to ash, then to less than ash, then to nothing, and Sef stared at their empty palm with an expression I knew intimately: the look of someone whose purpose has just evaporated, leaving them standing in the wreckage of a plan that was never theirs.

"I don't hear him anymore," Sef whispered. Then, with the terrible directness of children: "Was he real?"

I opened my mouth to answer and discovered I had absolutely no idea. There was a hole in me shaped like an answer, and around the hole, faint warmth, already fading, like a bed someone had just gotten out of.

"I think," I said carefully, "he was real the way the wind is real. You can't see it. You can't keep it. But it moves things."

Sef considered this with the gravity of a magistrate.

"He moved you," the child said. "You walked a whole country for him and you don't even know why."

"Yes."

"That's stupid."

"Yes," I said, and laughed, and the laugh surprised us both, wobbling and wet and real. "Completely stupid. Come on. We should get down off this mountain before dark. I have a feeling someone named Bren is waiting for us, and he'll worry, though he'll pretend he was merely concerned about the cartographical implications of our route."

"Bren," Sef repeated, tasting it. "Is he the one who fell in the creek?"

I blinked. "What?"

"The creek story. It's stuck to you somehow. Like burrs." Sef patted my arm, matter-of-fact, the way you'd comfort a horse. "You're mostly empty now, but there's a creek story and a smell of woodsmoke and a feeling like—" they frowned, searching, "—like a window with frost on it. That one won't go away. I tried."

"Frost," I said.

"Frost," Sef confirmed solemnly. "With writing on it. But the writing's yours, not his. I think you put it there yourself, right at the end, before the emptying. Something you wanted kept."

We started down. The mountain path was treacherous and the light was failing and I had no memories to guide me through a country I had apparently once known like my own hands—but my feet, interestingly, knew the way. Bodies keep their own accounts. My boots found the safe stones without consulting me, and when the path forked at a cairn, my hand reached out and touched the third rock from the top, an old traveler's blessing, automatic as breathing.

*Whoever you were,* I thought to the empty rooms inside me, *you were thorough.*

Below us, the valleys opened up gold and gray in the evening, and somewhere among them—though I couldn't have said how I knew—people were waiting. A village. A healer with a familiar face. A cartographer with a temper and a burned map. A life, small and warm and utterly unremarkable, which was, I suspected, exactly the treasure I had traded a kingdom to buy.

Sef slipped their hand into mine.

"What do we do now?" the child asked. "Now that there's no god and no door and no debt?"

"Now?" I said. "Now we learn everybody's names. Starting with yours."

"Sef," said Sef. "Say it back so I know you'll keep it."

"Sef," I said. And then, because something in me insisted, insisted with a ferocity that had no history and needed none: "*Sef.* Written on frost. Kept."

The child smiled with their whole crooked, gap-toothed face, and we walked down out of the erased country together, toward the last of the light, while behind us the Last Spire settled into its long sleep—and far above its sealed summit, unnoticed by either of us, the first flakes of winter began to fall.
