## 20. The Spire Closes

The Spire was not what Sef had expected, though it was hard to say what they had expected instead. A mouth, maybe. A wound. Something with teeth in it.

But it was only tall, and quiet, and made of a stone that drank the light the way mud drank rain. It stood at the top of the hill where the road stopped pretending to be anything at all, and around its base grew grass that remembered being wheat fields and gardens and streets, all of them flickering like candleflames when you weren't looking straight at them.

Sef knew this place the way you know a word you've never said out loud.

*You came back,* said Vess, inside Sef's chest, which was where the god lived now. The god's voice was not a sound. It was more like the feeling of a hand on your shoulder when you're crying and you didn't hear anyone come into the room.

"I brought Odile," Sef said out loud, because the god liked it better when words went through the air first. "Like I promised."

*Like you were made to,* said Vess, gently, the way you'd correct a child about which hand holds the spoon.

"Both," Sef said. "I did both."

Odile stood a few steps behind them. Sef could hear their breathing — the careful kind, the kind people do when they don't want you to know they're scared. Sef had gotten very good at hearing scared breathing. They'd heard a lot of it on the road. Bren's, before the ford. Maren's, when she thought no one was listening. Halloway's, even, that day in the market, under all his white robes and important words.

Everyone breathed scared. That was how Sef knew grown-ups were mostly children who'd learned to be quieter.

"Sef," Odile said. Their voice was rough, like a door that needed oiling. "Whatever happens—"

"You'll say something brave," Sef said. "I know. You always say something brave. Can we not do it yet? I want to look at it first."

The Spire's door was right there in front of them, though *door* was a generous word for a seam of black glass running up the stone, taller than three men standing on each other's shoulders. The shard in Sef's pocket had gone hot as a hearthstone days ago, and now it pulled toward the seam the way water pulls downhill, and Sef let it pull, because there wasn't any use fighting it anymore.

The shard floated up out of their pocket on its own, slow as a soap bubble, and fitted itself into the seam with a click so small and final that Sef's whole body shivered.

The door opened.

Not outward. Not inward either. It opened the way sleep opens, the way you slide from being awake to being somewhere else without noticing the exact moment it happened. And beyond the door was—

Home.

That was the only word for it. Not Sef's home; Sef had never had one, except maybe the inside of the Spire itself, if you wanted to be technical about it, and Maren said technicalities were just lies wearing nice clothes. But it was *home*, the way the word felt when other people said it. Fields of gold grain going silver in the wind. A city of white walls and red roofs. Bells ringing from towers that had been dust for three years. People walking those streets, laughing, carrying bread and flowers and buckets, alive as anything.

They were all still in there. All of them. The whole country that the world had agreed to forget.

And standing closest to the door, waiting, was the god.

Vess looked the way silence looks if silence got a body: tall and gray-robed and faceless, but somehow kind anyway, the way an empty church is kind. The god raised one hand, palm up, patient.

*A life, freely given,* Vess said. *As promised. As owed.*

Sef looked at the hand.

Here was the thing Sef had figured out on the road, somewhere between the ford where Bren died and the shrine where the god had spoken through Sef's own mouth and frightened everyone including Sef: the god never actually lied. Gods couldn't, probably, or wouldn't. When Vess said *a life freely given*, every word was true.

It was just that nobody had asked whose life.

Nobody had asked whether *given* meant what the white robes thought it meant, or what Maren feared it meant, or what the god expected it meant. Words were doors too, Sef thought. You could walk through them different ways.

And Odile had already walked through first.

---

Because here is what happened while Sef was sleeping at the ruined shrine, which Sef only knew because Maren told them after, crying into her healer's apron like the apron had done something wrong:

Odile had knelt at the broken altar and put both hands flat on the cold stone and said, in the Old Tongue, the words Sef hadn't been able to stop hearing since. *Take mine instead. Take the oath. Take the bond. Take every year of me that belonged to you, and let the child go free.*

And the god had gone very quiet, the way a house goes quiet when someone says something true and enormous, and then Vess had answered — not in anger, not in hunger, but in something that sounded almost like relief — *You would unmake yourself for this?*

*I would unmake my debt,* Odile had said. *There's a difference.*

And then Sef had woken up with frost on their eyelashes and Odile sitting beside them looking lighter than Sef had ever seen a person look, the way a rope looks after you cut the weight off the end of it, and Odile had smiled and said, "Time to finish our errand," and refused to explain anything else, which was very annoying, but Sef had learned by then that some things have to happen in order, like stories, like bread rising, like doors opening.

So now the door was open, and the god's hand was out, and the payment was coming.

Just not the payment anyone planned.

---

Odile stepped forward.

"Don't," Sef said, grabbing their sleeve. Their fingers found the old vow-mark on Odile's wrist, the one that had ached for three years, the one that had gone strangely calm since the shrine. Under Sef's thumb it was warm. It was the warmest part of Odile, warmer than hands, warmer than breath. It had always been the warmest part. It was where the god lived in them, the way the god lived in Sef now, except older, and deeper, and paid-for.

"Sef." Odile crouched down to eye level, which they always did, which Sef loved, which made this harder. Their gray eyes were wet but steady, the way they'd been steady at the ford, at the raid, at every terrible thing. "Listen to me. I've been a sworn blade for twenty years. I've been Blackwood the charm-seller for three. Do you know which one I liked better?"

"The charms," Sef whispered.

"The charms," Odile agreed. "Terrible charms. Luck-knots that didn't work. Little glass eyes that fogged up. Best work I ever did. Because people bought them and smiled, and nobody died for them, and nobody was sealed behind a door for them. That life — the small one, the warm one — that's the one I choose. I'm choosing it again right now. I'm just paying for it properly this time."

"But it hurts," Sef said. "Maren said taking away an oath takes away everything tied to it. Your memories. Your—" Sef's throat closed. "You won't remember me."

"No," Odile said. Simple. Terrible. True, the way gods make things true. "Probably not much. Maybe not any."

"Then I won't let you—"

"Sef." Odile took both of Sef's hands. Their grip was gentle, the way it always was, like Sef was made of something worth keeping. "When I swore to Vess, I gave my sword arm and my years and my death, someday, wherever the god pointed. That was the bargain. Everyone else kept their half of it. Everyone else got sealed away while I walked around outside, alive, hiding, selling luck-knots. I've been living in arrears for three years, little one. This isn't sacrifice. This is *paying the bill.*"

"That's not fair," Sef said, and hated how young their voice sounded. "That's not fair, you can't just—"

"I'm a mercenary," Odile said, and smiled, crooked and real. "We're famously unfair."

Then they kissed the top of Sef's head, between the horns of hair that stuck up no matter what Maren did to them, and turned to the door, and walked toward the god with their chin up like it was just another job.

And Sef — who had been made from black glass and silence, who had been built to be given away, who knew exactly what it felt like to be decided about by everyone around you — understood, suddenly and completely, that there was only one thing left to do.

Sef ran after them.

---

The god watched them both come. Faceless, patient, gray as winter morning.

*One life, freely given,* Vess said. *The terms are met.*

"Yes," Odile said. "Mine. Take it. Unswear me. Take the bond, take the memory, take every thread between us — and leave the child alone forever. Those are my terms. A mercenary reads her contracts, god. You should know that by now."

*And you, little vessel?* The god turned its attention on Sef, and it was like being looked at by the whole sky. *What are your terms?*

Sef's heart was hammering. But they'd crossed a whole erased country to get here. They'd hidden under cart-canvas and eaten burnt rabbit and watched Bren burn a map into his own skin so the rest of them could run. They were allowed to ask for things. Maren had said so. Maren had held Sef's face in her hands and said, *You're allowed to want things, little one. That's the whole point of being alive.*

"One thing," Sef said. "If you take Odile's memories. If you take the old country out of their head. Leave one. Just one. Leave my name."

The silence stretched so long that Sef heard the wind moving through the flickering grass, and somewhere far below, a bell in the ghost-city rang the hour.

*Sef,* said Vess slowly. *A name in the Old Tongue. A name that belongs to a kingdom the world forgot. You ask me to break my own erasure?*

"It's not erasure," Sef said. "It's keeping. There's a difference. Odile taught me that. Keeping is a choice. You keep things on purpose, because they matter." Sef squeezed Odile's hands tighter. "You're going to take everything else. Everything. So leave the one thing that someone chose to keep. Let my name survive outside your door. Let it live in a mouth that doesn't even remember why it matters. Please. That's my price. That's my life, freely given — I give you my *asking*. Nobody's ever asked you for something small before, have they? Everybody asks you for kingdoms. I'm asking you for four sounds."

And the god — the silent god, the sealed god, the god who had waited three years behind black glass for a payment —

laughed.

It wasn't a sound. It was more like sun coming through a window onto a floor where a cat is sleeping. It moved through the whole hillside, through the flickering wheat and the ghost-bells and the seams of the world, and Sef felt it pass through their own chest and come out the other side lighter.

*Done,* said Vess. *Oh, done, little one. Twenty years I have listened to that one pray, and every prayer was a duty, and every word was a debt. You are the first soul in a hundred years to ask me for something small.* The gray robe inclined, the way a person bows. *Freely given, freely taken. Come, sworn one. We will settle accounts.*

Odile looked down at Sef one last time.

"Hey," they said, and their voice was doing the rough-door thing again. "When you get to Corvane — the new one, the real one, the one that exists — find the charm-seller's stall on Ash Street. Someone will be there. Someone with bad luck-knots and good tea. Be nice to them. They'll need a friend who talks too much."

"You'll need a friend who talks too much," Sef corrected, crying, furious, holding on.

"That too," Odile said. "That especially."

And then they let go, and stepped through, and the god closed its hand around them like a father closing his fist gently around a firefly, and the light of the vow-mark flared once through Odile's sleeve — bright, bright, bright — and began, slowly, like snow melting, to go out.

---

The door stayed open while the memories drained. Sef sat on the threshold with their knees pulled up and watched, because someone should watch, because watching was a kind of keeping.

They saw it happen in the ghost-country first. In the city beyond the door, a tower crumbled softly into glittering dust and re-formed as open meadow, and nobody minded. Somewhere a wedding procession dissolved mid-song into wildflowers, and the wildflowers swayed like they'd always been there, and it was sad and beautiful at the same time, the way most true things are.

And beside Sef, Odile changed. It happened quietly. Their shoulders settled differently — looser, less like a soldier, more like a tired shopkeeper at the end of a long day. The calluses on their sword hand stayed, but the knowledge of how they'd earned them drained out of their face. They blinked, and frowned slightly, and looked around at the black glass and the gray god and the flickering hills with the polite confusion of someone who has woken up in the wrong room.

"Oh," Odile said. Their voice had lost its roughness. It was just a voice now, ordinary, human. "Oh, I — sorry. Where am I? How did I get here?" They looked at Sef, and there was nothing in their eyes but kindness, plain and unbought. "Are you lost, little one?"

Sef's heart cracked clean down the middle.

And then — because the god keeps bargains, even strange ones — something else happened.

Frost bloomed across the black glass of the door, delicate as fern leaves, and in the frost, written in letters of shining ice, appeared four shapes from the Old Tongue.

*Sef.*

"There you are," Sef whispered.

Odile stared at the frost. Blinked. Read it the way you read a word you've never seen but somehow know. Their brow furrowed, and then smoothed, and they tried it out loud, carefully, like carrying a full cup across a room.

"*Sef,*" Odile said.

"That's me," Sef said, wiping their face with their sleeve. "That's my name. You're the first one to say it today. You say it every day from now on, okay? Even if you don't know why. Promise."

"I promise," Odile said, bewildered and warm and already forgetting why they'd promised, which somehow made the promise stronger, not weaker.

Behind them, the god drew itself up to its full height, and the whole ghost-country sighed — the wheat settling into permanent meadow, the bells falling silent not because they were silenced but because they were finished, the white walls softening into green hills that would finally, truly rest. Three years of imposed silence ended, and chosen silence began, and the difference between those two things, Sef thought, was the whole difference between a prison and a bed.

*Thank you,* said Vess, and for the first time it didn't sound like a god speaking. It sounded like a friend at a gate, going home. *Both of you. Go and be loud somewhere. I will listen.*

The door began to close — not slamming, not sealing, just easing shut the way an eyelid eases shut at the end of a long day, grateful, unhurried, done.

Sef scrambled up and grabbed Odile's hand.

Odile looked down at their joined hands, and at the frost-name still melting on the glass, and said, "Come on then, *Sef*. Let's find somewhere warm. I feel like I've forgotten something important, but I feel like it's in good hands."

"It is," Sef said. "They're mine."

And hand in hand, the key that wasn't a key anymore and the sworn blade who was no longer sworn, they walked down the hill together while behind them the Last Spire folded into the earth like a story ending well, and ahead of them lay the long road back to Corvane, to Ash Street, to a stall with bad luck-knots and good tea, to neighbors and winters and anniversaries and a name that four sounds wide would outlive them all.

Sef swung their arms as they walked, the way children do when they are happy and alive and no one is deciding anything about them ever again.

"Hurry up," Sef said. "I'm hungry."

"You're always hungry."

"So? Feed me. You promised."

"I don't remember promising."

"That's okay," said Sef, squeezing the hand that had forgotten everything except how to hold on. "I'll remember for both of us."
