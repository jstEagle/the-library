## 21. A City That Lets You Stay

The charm-sellers' quarter woke the way it always had: shutters banging open one by one like a slow, arthritic applause, someone two doors down arguing with a mule that had opinions about loading order, and the smell of frying dough drifting from Tessaly's cart at the corner of Ash and Bellringer's Lane.

Odile Blackwood stood in front of their stall—a real stall now, with legs that matched and an awning that kept off most of the rain—and arranged the morning's stock with the reverence of a general deploying troops.

Luck-knots in red thread. Wart-charms shaped like fat little frogs. A row of "genuinely blessed" buttons, which were not blessed at all but had been polished so lovingly that customers swore they could feel it. The old Odile—the one who had carried a god's warmth under her skin like a coal banked in ash—would have found the whole enterprise insulting. The new Odile found it honest. Nothing here worked. That was rather the point.

"You've put the frog charms next to the love knots again," said Sef, appearing at her elbow with the particular gravity of a nine-year-old delivering a verdict.

"And?"

"Last week Widow Corrin bought a frog thinking it was for romance."

"Widow Corrin *wanted* it to be for romance. She told me so. She said frogs are loyal."

Sef considered this with the seriousness of a scholar consulting a difficult text. "Frogs eat their babies sometimes," they said, and went to fetch the stool that was theirs, theirs alone, painted blue because blue was, according to Sef, the color of important things.

Odile watched them go and felt the thing she felt every morning now, the thing that still startled her even after months: nothing. Not emptiness—she'd known emptiness, had worn it like armor for years—but a clean absence where the vow used to sit. The oath-mark on her wrist was a pale ghost of a scar now, smooth as any old wound. Sometimes she caught herself rubbing it the way you'd touch your tongue to a missing tooth.

She couldn't remember swearing it.

That was the price, and she paid it gladly, and some mornings—like this one, with the sun coming down Ash Street all gold and forgiving—she almost managed to believe she didn't miss it.

* * *

Business was brisk by midmorning, which in the charm trade meant three sales and four long conversations. The first customer was the cooper's youngest son, who wanted a luck-knot for his apprenticeship trial and haggled Odile from two coppers down to one with the shameless fluency of the truly poor.

"One copper," Odile agreed, tying it. "And free advice: don't let Master Pellin see you measure twice. He likes a boy who looks certain even when he isn't."

"That's not advice about the knot."

"No. The knot does nothing. The advice is the product."

The boy grinned and ran, and Sef solemnly moved a pebble from the left jar to the right jar, which was how they counted sales, because Sef had decided that numbers written down could be stolen by magistrates but pebbles were safe. Odile hadn't corrected this. Some superstitions deserved encouragement.

The second customer was Maren, which was not business at all, though she insisted on buying a wart-charm every single visit out of what Odile suspected was stubbornness dressed up as courtesy.

"You have warts?" Odile asked, ringing up the fourth consecutive frog.

"I live in hope of warts." Maren set the coin down and stayed leaning on the stall's edge, her gray-streaked hair pinned back in the practical twist Odile had learned meant she intended to stay a while. "Village sent me with the autumn cart. Salt pork, dried apples, and a message: Tam says if you ever finish that shelf you promised him, he'll stop telling everyone in Second Names that you're a fraud."

"He's right. I am a fraud. It's my profession."

"He also says—" and here Maren's voice did the small careful turn it always did around these things, "—that the east wall of the shrine room is holding. New plaster took. He thought you'd want to know."

Odile's hands went still over the button tray.

They had built the shrine themselves, in the village, back before everything—before Bren's map burned true, before the ford, before the Spire closed its eye forever. A plain room with a plain stone, no god in it, no name on it. Just a place where people who had lost a country could sit and be quiet together. They'd gone back twice since, once to help with the harvest and once for no reason they'd admit to, and both times they had stood in that empty shrine room feeling absolutely nothing divine whatsoever, and both times it had felt, absurdly, like being held.

"Tell Tam I'll come do the shelf after the frost," Odile said. "Real frost. Not whatever Sef is doing to the windows."

Maren laughed, then glanced sideways at the child, who was pretending very hard to be absorbed in the pebble jars. "Still doing that?"

"Only when they're happy. Or frightened. Or thinking hard about lunch." Odile shrugged. "No power in it anymore. It's just... weather that likes them."

It wasn't quite nothing, what remained in Sef. The Unwritten had made them from the Spire itself, a vessel, a key—and when the door sealed, when Vess accepted Odile's bond in place of a life freely given, something had stayed behind in the small body like light staying in a lamp chimney after the flame's pinched out. Frost-ferns bloomed on glass when Sef laughed. Bread rose faster near them. Cats followed them through the streets in silent, reverent processions that Odile privately found deeply unsettling.

Sef had declared, with the flat certainty of childhood, that this meant they were going to be a baker when they grew up. Odile saw no reason to argue. There were worse destinies than bread.

* * *

The afternoon brought trouble, in the form of a Church inspector's gray coat turning the corner onto Ash Street, and Odile's hands performed the old dance without consulting her—every charm on the table suddenly harmless, every knot just thread, the little drawer of actually-interesting items sliding shut under her knee.

Then she recognized the walk inside the coat, and made herself stop hiding.

Magistrate Halloway came up the street flanked by nobody, which was itself unusual, and stopped at the stall with the expression of a man who had rehearsed several speeches and hated all of them.

"Mistress Blackwood."

"Magistrate Halloway." She gestured at the table like a queen presenting a kingdom. "Buttons. Frogs. Thread. All licensed, all inert, all guaranteed to do absolutely nothing except separate fools from their coppers, which is legal in every district of this fine city."

"So I've read. Repeatedly. Your file is..." He paused, choosing the word with care. "...thorough."

"I should hope so. I wrote half of it myself."

Something twitched at the corner of his mouth and was suppressed with visible effort. He looked at Sef instead, and Odile watched the look—the flicker toward the child's wrists, the quick inventory of frostless windows on a cold day—and understood that Halloway knew exactly what he was looking at, and exactly what he was choosing not to see, and had been choosing it for months.

He owed them. Everyone on this street owed someone; that was what made it a street and not a ledger. Halloway's debt was older and stranger than most—he had lost a sister to the erasure, back when the Church unmade the country, and Odile had seen his face the night he'd learned what the Spire's closing meant. Not grief. Relief. Someone, somewhere, had finally been allowed to rest, and the magistrate had walked home through the rain looking ten years lighter.

"There's been a report," Halloway said now, heavily. "Unlicensed speech. Old Tongue, specifically. In this quarter."

"Has there." Odile leaned back. "What did it sound like?"

"What?"

"The speech. What did the report say it sounded like? Because if we're going to investigate properly, Magistrate, I'll need details. Was it harsh? Melodic? Did it leave frost?" She spread her hands. "I sell knots. My knowledge of outlawed languages begins and ends with what the Church pays informants these days, which I understand is generous enough that half the reports in this city are neighbors settling scores about drainage ditches."

Halloway looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked past her, at Sef, who had abandoned all pretense of pebble-counting and was staring at him with frank, unchildlike attention.

"Frost," Halloway said quietly. "The report mentioned frost on the inside of the glass. Which is impossible. Which means either the informant is lying or mad, and neither warrants a second visit." He straightened his coat. "I'll note it as unfounded."

"You do thorough work, Magistrate."

"I try." He turned to go, then stopped, and what he said next came out rough, like a stone pulled from a boot. "The anniversary's coming. Of the—of the fall. Three years, they're saying, though I don't know how anyone counts a year that officially never happened." His jaw worked. "There are people in this city who remember anyway. Quietly. If such people were to gather somewhere—not a shrine, nothing with a name on it, maybe just a room above a bakery on Candlemaker's Row—they couldn't be prosecuted for remembering. There's no law against memory. I've checked. Twice."

Odile held his gaze. "That sounds like very thorough checking."

"It's what I'm for." And he walked away down Ash Street, gray coat among the market colors, a man carrying his own erased country the way everyone here carried theirs—carefully, silently, and close to the chest.

* * *

Evening came down soft. Tessaly's cart packed up; the mule won its argument; the lamps along Ash Street lit themselves in their usual staggered, grudging way. Odile closed the stall while Sef counted the day's pebbles with tremendous ceremony and pronounced the total "a lot," which was accurate.

They climbed the stairs to the rooms above the shop—two rooms, small, hers and Sef's, with a stove that smoked only when the wind came from the west and a window seat deep enough to sleep in, which Sef did more often than not, claiming the bed was too far from the window to hear the street.

Odile cooked. Simple food, badly seasoned, because three years of army rations had permanently broken something in her and Maren despaired of it. Sef ate with the focused devotion of the truly hungry, which never quite seemed to wear off no matter how many months of regular meals passed, and Odile never rushed them, never would.

"Tell me the story," Sef said, when the bowls were empty. "The one about the road."

"You know the story."

"Tell it anyway. You tell it different each time. Last time the river horse was enormous."

"The river horse *was* enormous. It was the size of a chapel. You were there."

"I don't remember it like that." Sef curled into the window seat, chin on knees, eyes catching the lamplight. "I remember it being small. And warm. Everything from before is small and warm, like it's far away, like I'm looking at it through the wrong end of a spyglass." A pause. "Is that what happened to you? To your memories?"

Odile sat down across from them, and the truth arrived and sat down beside her, familiar now, almost comfortable.

"Yes," she said. "Mine went through the wrong end of the spyglass too. I remember a road, and a friend with maps drawn on his arms, and a woman who smelled of herbs telling me to stay. But the middle's gone soft. I remember that I loved a god. I can't remember why, exactly, or what it felt like to be loved back. I know it cost me something enormous." She touched the pale scar on her wrist. "I know I'd pay it again. That part didn't blur. Funny, what stays sharp."

"Do you wish you had it back? The god-part?"

It was the question Sef asked perhaps once a month, always in this same careful voice, always watching her face the way children watch adults for signs of weather coming.

"No," Odile said, and it was true, and the truth of it settled in her chest like a banked fire, warm and low. "I had a place in a story, Sef. A big one. Gods and doors and kingdoms. And it was—" she searched, honestly, "—it was magnificent, and it was mine, and I would not trade having lived it. But a story isn't a life. A life is a stall that smells of thread and a boy who counts in pebbles and a woman who visits with salt pork and lies about needing frog charms." She reached over and ruffled Sef's hair, which was tolerated with dignity. "A life is a city that lets you stay. I spent years learning how to disappear. This is the first place I've practiced the opposite."

Sex nodded slowly, absorbing this, then ruined it completely: "Tessaly says you and Maren make faces at each other."

"Tessaly says many things."

"She says it's 'painful to watch.' She said she'd sell tickets if she thought either of you would notice."

"I will be discussing Tessaly's licensing status with the appropriate authorities."

Sef grinned, and outside the window, on the cold glass, frost began—just faintly, just at the corners—to bloom in delicate ferns, spreading inward like something growing toward warmth. Odile watched it happen and felt no fear at all. Only the ordinary evening miracle of it, the small leftover grace of a sealed door.

"Go to sleep," she said. "We're opening early tomorrow. Widow Corrin is coming back for another frog."

"For romance?"

"For romance."

* * *

Later, when Sef was asleep in the window seat with their breath fogging and clearing, fogging and clearing, Odile sat at the table with a stub of candle and tried, as she sometimes did, to reach backward.

Not for the god. She'd stopped reaching for the god months ago; that door was shut and she had shut it, and peace lived on this side of it. But for the small things. Her sworn-brother's face. The color of the banners. Her own mother's voice saying her name in a language that no longer officially existed.

Some nights she got fragments. A smell of pine smoke. The sensation of laughing until her ribs hurt beside someone whose name was—was—

Gone. Soft as ever. Through the wrong end of the spyglass.

She blew out the candle and accepted it, the way you accept winter: not happily, but completely.

And then, as she crossed to her bed, she glanced at the window—at Sef's window, the one above the sleeping child—and stopped.

The frost had grown while they slept. It had crept up the pane in feathers and ferns, and there, in the center, in characters Odile had not consciously read in three years but recognized the way you recognize your own heartbeat, the ice had formed a word:

*Sef.*

Just the name. Nothing else. No demand, no farewell, no god in it—or all of gods in it, depending on the hour and the heart doing the reading.

Odile stood in the dark kitchen with her breath misting and her skin prickling, and she did not wake the child, and she did not pray, because praying was a habit she had given up along with everything else. She simply looked, for a long time, at the one word the world had agreed to keep.

Tomorrow, she thought, she would teach it to the children on Candlemaker's Row. Tomorrow, and the day after, and every day after that, until it belonged to mouths that would never know what it had cost.

Tonight, she just watched the frost hold its shape, patient as anything waiting to be remembered.
