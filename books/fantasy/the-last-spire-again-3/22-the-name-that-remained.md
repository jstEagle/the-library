## 22. The Name That Remained

The frost came in the night, as frost does, without knocking.

Odile found it at first light, when they went to open the shutters the way they had opened them a thousand mornings since—the stall needed setting up, and Sef would sleep until the sun was fully up if allowed, which Odile permitted on the grounds that a child who had once been a door deserved to be lazy now and then.

But this morning the shutter stuck. Not jammed—stuck, in the way of things that are being held from the other side by something patient and cold. Odile put their shoulder into it, grumbled, and then stopped grumbling, because through the frosted pane they could see what the frost had done.

It had spelled a word.

Not a pattern. Not the fern-work that winter likes to draw when it's showing off. A word, in letters sharp and deliberate, in a script Odile hadn't been able to read in over a year—not since the Spire closed and took the old kingdom's language out of their head like a splinter drawn from flesh.

They knew it anyway. The way you know your own name. The way you know the weight of a hand in yours.

*Sef.*

Odile stood very still in the gray light with their breath fogging the glass and their heart doing something complicated behind their ribs.

It could be nothing. That was the honest answer, and Odile had made a religion of honesty in the years since they'd traded their god for a boy. Frost did strange things on old windows. Wind got under eaves. Glass held memories of every hand that had ever pressed against it, and maybe some morning it simply gave them back.

Or maybe somewhere beyond the sealed door of the world, a silent god who no longer had a sworn servant was saying goodbye the only way left to it—one word, written in cold, once a year, on the anniversary.

"Vess," Odile said quietly, testing the name. It sat oddly in their mouth now, like a coin from a country that no longer minted them. They didn't feel the old ache where the oath-mark used to live. They didn't feel anything there at all, just skin, just an ordinary arm belonging to an ordinary person who sold luck-knots to people who didn't believe in luck.

That was the bargain. That was the price paid, gladly, permanently. No powers. No god. No memory of green valleys or spired cities or the sound of ten thousand voices singing in a tongue that officially never existed.

Except one memory. Except one name.

Odile had woken on the mountainside below the closing Spire knowing exactly three things: that they were alive, that the child beside them was called Sef, and that this knowledge was a debt they intended to pay forever. Everything else had drained away like water through cupped hands—the homeland, the war, the face of their sworn-brother whose name Maren still said sometimes and waited for Odile to say back, and Odile never could, and Maren had learned to stop waiting and start pouring tea instead.

One name remained. So one name would remain.

"You're up early."

Sef stood in the doorway, nine years old now, hair a disaster, dragging the blanket around their shoulders like a small disgruntled emperor. They squinted at Odile, then at the window.

"What are you looking at?"

"Winter showing off," Odile said.

Sef padded over, stood on the stool by the sill—they were finally tall enough not to need the stool, but they liked it anyway—and looked at the frost-letters for a long moment.

"That says my name," Sef said. Not a question. They couldn't read Old Tongue either; the Spire had taken it from them too, gently, the way you take a splinter from a child so they won't remember the wound. But they knew it the same way Odile did. Bone-deep. Given, not learned.

"It might," Odile said. "Frost is unreliable."

"Frost spelled my name."

"Frost is a show-off," Odile agreed. "Put the kettle on. We've got a big day."

* * *

The big day was, in fairness, mostly the same as every other day, which was the entire point of it.

By midmorning the stall on Copper Lane was set out: luck-knots in red thread (harmless), blessing-stones from the river (extremely harmless), and the little carved doors Odile made in the evenings, which people bought to hang over their thresholds because they liked the idea of doors even if they'd never heard of the Last one. In Corvane, magic was outlawed and charms were legal, provided they did absolutely nothing, which meant Odile's stock had never moved better. There was a certain poetry in it that Bren would have appreciated. Odile tried not to think about Bren on workdays. Some prices you only counted at night.

"Morning, Blackwood." Old Tam from the chandlery next door, already arguing. "Your boy was in my shop again yesterday reading my ledgers."

"He reads everything. It's a condition."

"He corrected my arithmetic."

"Well," Odile said, "that's fatal. I'll have him put down."

Tam grinned and bought a blessing-stone he didn't believe in, which was how half of Corvane expressed affection.

Maren arrived around noon, as she did most weeks, walking down from the hidden village with her healer's satchel and her terrible opinions about city bread. She'd stayed the first month after the Spire closed, then gone home, then started coming back so regularly that everyone had stopped pretending it was temporary. She kissed Odile on the cheek in front of the whole street now, scandalizing nobody, because Copper Lane had decided years ago that the charm-seller and their boy belonged to it, and whatever else belonged to them belonged too.

"How's the mark?" she asked, low, taking Odile's wrist without asking, the way healers do.

"Gone. Still gone. Same as last week."

"Humor me." Her thumb traced the place where the oath-mark had burned itself out of existence, and found, as always, smooth unmarked skin. She frowned anyway. She always frowned anyway. "You gave up more than any of us ever asked."

"I gave up less than I almost took," Odile said. "From him." They nodded toward Sef, who was across the lane teaching Tam's granddaughter to whittle, badly, with great confidence.

Maren let it go, the way she'd learned to. Then: "The village sent something." She produced a parcel wrapped in cloth—a jar of honey, a knitted thing that was either a scarf or a mistake, and a letter in careful handwriting from the elders. *We remember you at the turning of the year. Come when the roads clear.*

"They still won't use their old names," Odile said, scanning it. Second names, all of them. Names taken to survive the erasure, worn so long they'd grown comfortable, like shoes resoled a hundred times.

"They will someday," Maren said. "Maybe not ours. Maybe our grandchildren's. That's how it works. Things come back slow or not at all."

Odile folded the letter. Someday. Once, Odile had wanted everything back at once—the country, the god, the whole drowned past hauled up dripping. Now they wanted things the way gardens want things: slowly, seasonally, with a lot of weeding.

"Sef asked me something last night," Maren said suddenly. "At supper. Asked me if I remembered their mother."

Odile went still.

"I told them the truth. Nobody knows if the vessels had mothers. And then—" Maren's voice caught, just slightly. "And then I said, but you have us. And they said, *I know. That's why I asked.*"

Odile breathed out slowly. "Nine years old."

"Terrifyingly. You should have seen the look. Like an abbot delivering a sermon on gratitude." Maren laughed, wiped her eye briskly as though it had merely watered. "Anyway. Honey. Scarf-or-mistake. I'm off to bully your neighbor about his cough."

* * *

The anniversary proper fell three days later, and the village children arrived two days before it, because children can smell a festival the way wolves smell weather.

There were six of them this winter, brought down from the hidden village by a carter owed too many favors: the ferryman's twins, the smith's daughter, two orphans the village had collected the way villages collect orphans, and a small furious girl named Pip who bit first and introduced herself after. They descended on Odile's stall like weather and were fed, as tradition demanded, hot spiced cider and too much bread.

And on the evening of the anniversary, as snow began again outside, Odile did what Odile did every year on this night.

"Sit," they said. "All of you. Yes, Pip, you too, and give Hesta back her mitten, we've talked about this."

Six children arranged themselves on the floor of the little rooms above the stall, in a ragged crescent, faces upturned. Sef sat slightly apart, as always on this night—part of the circle and somehow its center, watching with an expression older than nine.

"A long time ago," Odile began, the traditional opening, "there was a country that isn't on any map anymore. Not because it sank, and not because it burned. Because powerful people decided it had never existed, and they had a magic for making decisions stick."

"Was it nice? The country?" That was Hesta, the youngest twin, who always asked this.

"It was green," Odile said. "It had rivers that ran silver in spring. It had a god who never spoke—not because the god was cruel, but because it believed listening was holier than talking." Odile paused. That part still felt true, wherever Vess was now, behind a door that was finally, truly shut. "I lived there. I don't remember it anymore. I gave those memories away, and I'm not going to tell you it wasn't a fair price, because it was. But here's the thing about erasure. The Church erased a country. They couldn't erase everything."

"Why not?" Pip, suspicious as ever of happy endings before she'd audited them.

"Because of names." Odile held up one finger. "A name is the smallest possible story. One word, and inside it a whole person. The Church could burn books and unmake maps and make a whole nation forget its own songs. But a name, spoken aloud, taught mouth to mouth—" they leaned forward— "that lives in bodies. And bodies are stubborn."

Sef smiled, very slightly, at the floor.

"So tonight," Odile said, "I'm going to teach you a word. It's from that country. It's the only word I've got left from it, and I intend to spend it well. When I say it, you'll think I'm saying it wrong—it'll feel wrong in your mouth, all edges and breath. That's correct. It's supposed to feel like that. It comes from a place that wasn't built for comfortable mouths."

They said it. *Sef.*

The candles guttered, just slightly. Or maybe the door had opened. On a winter's night, who could say.

"Now you."

The twins butchered it magnificently. Hesta turned it into a sneeze. Pip said it perfectly on the first try and then pretended she hadn't, scowling, daring anyone to comment. The orphans whispered it like a secret and then shouted it like a battle cry. And Sef—Sef listened to each attempt with the grave patience of someone hearing their own echo come back from six directions at once, and when Odile glanced over, Sef's eyes were bright and wet and they were grinning like midsummer.

"Again," Odile said. "Worse. All of you. It has to survive being said badly, that's how words stay alive."

They said it worse. They said it until it filled the little room, edgeless now, worn round with laughter, a word becoming a toy becoming a treasure. Down on the street, a passerby paused, heard children chanting something unfamiliar through a lit window, and walked on without fear—which, in Corvane, was its own small miracle.

Later, when the children were asleep in heaps of blankets like puppies, Odile stood at the window with Sef beside them.

Outside, the frost had returned to the glass. The letters were fainter tonight, melting at the edges, giving up their shape even as Odile watched—as if whoever wrote them had only ever been able to afford one word a year, and had spent it.

"Do you think it's really him?" Sef asked softly. "Or her. Or... it. Do you think it's really Vess?"

"I honestly don't know." Odile put an arm around the boy's shoulders. "Could be a god saying goodnight. Could be winter playing favorites. Could be the window remembering. I've stopped needing to know."

"But you look every year."

"I look every year," Odile agreed. "Looking costs nothing. And if it *is* Vess—if somewhere past that door, something that loved a whole country is pressing one word against the glass of the world—" Odile's voice did something it rarely did anymore, wobbled, recovered. "Then I want it to know the word landed. That somebody read it. That the name got out, and got multiplied by seven this year, and by however many next year, and that it's going to keep going long after we're both done."

Sef was quiet a while. Snow fell on Copper Lane, on the stall with its harmless charms, on the whole lawful godless city, softening everything.

"Grandmother Maren says the village kids are teaching it to the goats," Sef said at last.

"The goats," Odile said, "are going to ruin the pronunciation."

"I think the goats are doing fine."

They stood together until the frost faded entirely, leaving only clean dark glass and two reflections leaning into each other. Then Odile banked the fire, and Sef went to bed without being told, which was rarer than frost-signs, and Odile stood a moment longer in the quiet, counting the evening's inventory the way merchants do: six children taught, one name alive in eleven throats including the goats', one jar of honey, one scarf-or-mistake, one memory, one boy, one life.

Once, Odile Blackwood had been offered everything back—all of it, the god, the country, the green and silver past—at the price of one child. And once they'd been offered peace at the price of abandoning that child to the dark.

They had refused both bargains and chosen instead this: a stall, a street, a scarred and stubborn present, a word passed hand to hand like bread.

Survival, it turned out, wasn't keeping the past alive. It was making sure something of it walked forward into tomorrow on small, loud, unimpressed feet—six of whom were currently drooling on Odile's floorboards, and every one of whom could now say a dead kingdom's last living word.

Odile blew out the candle.

Somewhere far behind a sealed door, something silent kept its silence—chosen, now, rather than imposed. And somewhere on Copper Lane, in a room full of sleeping children, a name that had outlived its country settled in for the long work of outliving everyone.

Outside, gently, it went on snowing.
