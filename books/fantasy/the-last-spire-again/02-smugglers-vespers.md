## 2. Smugglers' Vespers

The smuggler roads had their own liturgy. You didn't write it down—writing was a hanging offense on those trails—but every child who ran messages between Thornfell and Lowmark learned the hours the way choir children learned vespers. Dusk bell: light no fires above the tree line. First dark: move, and if you must speak, speak in trade-words. Second dark: sleep where the stone sleeps with you.

Ulyana had kept that liturgy for twenty years, and tonight he broke it, because tonight he needed to talk to people before they talked to each other.

He took the low path under Wolfjaw Tor, the one that smelled of pine resin and old snowmelt, and moved at dusk-bell pace when he should have been sleeping. The mountain let him pass without complaint. It usually did. Twenty years of carrying salt, letters, and other men's sins up and down its spine had earned him a kind of grudging tolerance from the stone, the way a landlord tolerates a mouse he's never quite managed to trap.

Three stops. That was the plan. Warn the three nodes of his network—the waystation at Cindermilk, the tannery cut below Greywater, and old Sefa's smokehouse in the notch—and get them all singing the same lie by morning: *no pilgrim traffic this season, passes closed for rockfall, nothing worth freezing to death for.* A good lie travels faster than a bad truth. He'd built a life on that arithmetic.

Cindermilk first.

* * *

The waystation wasn't much—a lean-to against the tor's flank, a firepit ringed with flat stones worn smooth by thirty years of illicit cooking, and a cellar dug into the hillside that appeared on no map and answered no tax assessor's questions. Its keeper was Dagny Hult, a woman built like a doorframe and about as conversational. She was boiling something that smelled like bark and regret when Ulyana came down out of the trees.

"You're off-hours," she said, not looking up.

"Off-season too." He crouched at the edge of her fire and warmed hands he didn't need warmed. "I need your voice on the roads."

"I don't sing."

"You do when I pay you." He set a folded square of oilcloth on the stones between them. Inside it: salt in quantities that would have made a bishop weep, and a letter of passage written in the careful hand of a toll-clerk Ulyana owned outright. Dagny looked at it the way other women looked at jewelry.

"What's the song?"

"Pilgrims. They're coming up the valleys in numbers nobody's seen since my predecessor's time. Zealots, penitents, the whole shuffling parade. I want word passed that the high passes are closed. Rockfall. Winter damage. Nothing up there but dead men and cold shrines."

Dagny stirred her pot. "There *are* cold shrines up there. My sister's boy went to one last week. Prayed at it." She said it flatly, the way you report weather or a neighbor's cow getting loose. "Got an answer."

Ulyana kept his face doing nothing at all. This was the discipline of gatekeeping distilled into a single skill: the face as sealed door. "Kids hear echoes. Mountain acoustics. You know that."

"He asked for rain for the barley." She finally looked up. Her eyes were pale as dishwater and just as hard to see anything through. "It rained. On his field only, near as anyone could tell. Neighboring plots dry as a sermon." She stirred again. "You want me to tell people there's nothing up there worth walking for. And I'll say it, because you pay well and pay true. But I'm telling *you* what I know, since you're the man who keeps the seals on the spire-gate, and everybody knows it even though nobody's supposed to."

"Dagny."

"I'm not asking questions. Questions are expensive." She ladled some of the bark-stew into a wooden cup and pushed it at him, which from Dagny was roughly equivalent to a mother's embrace. "But you'll drink that, and you'll hear one thing free. The shrine his answer came from? It's one of the *old* ones. Pre-dynasty stone. Nobody's tended those in two hundred years, because there's been nobody to tend them *to*." She tapped the ground with her spoon. "Somebody's home."

He drank the stew. It tasted like punishment and warmth. He told her the lie she'd agreed to carry, made sure she had the phrasing right—*rockfall, closed till thaw, turn back at the split-boulder*—and left her counting salt by firelight.

The walk down from Cindermilk was short and downhill and still felt like climbing. *Somebody's home.* He turned the phrase over the whole way, looking for the flaw in it the way a locksmith looks for the flaw in a key that shouldn't fit.

There wasn't one. That was the problem. There was never one, with the old shrines. They were older than the kingdom, older than the dynasty that built the capital spire, older than the gods whose names the priests used like borrowed coats. When Ulyana had served at the royal court—when he had held keys heavier than any he carried now—he'd once read a surveyor's report on the pre-dynastic sites. The surveyor's conclusion, in ink gone brown: *These were not built to house worship. They were built to house something that receives it.*

He had filed that away under *scholars being scholars* and thought about it exactly twice in twenty years.

He was going to be thinking about it a great deal more.

* * *

Greywater next, by second-dark, moving fast along the tannery cut where the stream ran brown and chemical and covered every sound. The tannery crew worked nights precisely because nobody respectable came near the smell, and their boss, a wide man called Odo with forearms like hams, ran more contraband through his curing sheds than the rest of Ulyana's network combined.

Odo didn't need persuading; he needed only direction. Pilgrims meant money—pilgrims always needed boots, blankets, and false papers—but Ulyana wanted the opposite of money moving. He wanted a dam.

"No pilgrim trade," he told Odo in the reek of the curing yard, by the light of a lantern shuttered down to a coin-sized hole. "Not this season. Whatever comes up the valley asking after the spire road, you sell them nothing, point them nowhere, and pass word back down-chain that the passes ate a party of twelve last month. Frozen. Whole families."

Odo scratched his jaw. "Dead pilgrims is bad luck to lie about. Ask any priest."

"Ask any priest," Ulyana agreed mildly, "what happens to liars who tell the truth about what's answering prayers up the mountain. Because that's the flood I'm damming, Odo. Not pilgrims. What the pilgrims are walking toward."

That landed. Odo was a criminal of the honest kind—the kind who believed in rules so long as he wrote them himself—and he understood floods. He'd grown up in the river towns. "What's answering?"

"Nothing. That's my official finding, and I'm the gatekeeper, and my findings are boring on purpose." He handed over the second packet: salt, a letter, and a purse heavy enough to make the point. "Twelve frozen. Say it sad. Say it with names if you can invent good ones. Names travel better than numbers."

"Vess," Odo said immediately. "Vess family. Everyone knows a Vess."

"Perfect. Vess it is." Ulyana almost smiled. This was the part of the work he'd always loved, back before love got complicated—the craft of it, the clean mechanics of a lie assembled joint by joint until it bore weight. A rumor was just a bridge built out of other people's certainty. You laid the planks and let them walk across.

He was halfway out of the yard when Odo said, behind him, casual as a dropped tool:

"One thing, keeper. My night boys found tracks yesterday. Up past the third weir. Single walker, barefoot, going uphill. In this cold." A pause. "Barefoot prints don't melt snow around them, Ulyana. These did. Snow gone to black water in a neat little lane, like something walked through summer."

"Drunk tannerman."

"My boys don't drink on shift. I flog them if they do." Odo's grin showed in the slit of lantern light, humorless. "Vess family. Sad story. Good names. Go with your gods, keeper."

*Nobody has any,* Ulyana didn't say, and walked into the dark.

* * *

Sefa's smokehouse sat in a notch between two shoulders of the mountain, a place so deliberately unremarkable that travelers had been known to walk past it while looking for it. Sefa herself was seventy, chain-smoked fish over alder wood, and had run messages for four generations of gatekeepers, including, briefly, Ulyana's predecessor, whom she remembered chiefly as "a man who cried easily and lied poorly."

"You're neither," she'd told Ulyana his first year, appraising him over a rack of trout. "So you'll last. Sit. Eat."

He sat now in her kitchen, which smelled like a century of smoke, and told her everything, because Sefa was the one node in the network he never lied to. Not from sentiment—from architecture. Every structure needs one load-bearing truth or it falls in the first storm.

"The shrines are answering," he said. "By name. With results. Pilgrims are coming in numbers. I've seeded the closure story down-chain. But I can't seed it up-chain, because up-chain is where it started, and people believe their own miracles over any rumor of mine."

Sefa turned fish on the racks with a wooden spatula worn concave by decades. "Marta Kessel came through Tuesday. Her husband prayed at the White Stone for his lung-rot to ease. It eased. Then he forgot his own wedding day." She glanced back at him. "Whole day, gone. Knows he was married, knows he loved the woman, couldn't tell you her face or the season of it. Marta thinks the rot traded places with the memory. Rot went out. Something else went in and ate."

"That's sickness."

"It's bookkeeping," Sefa said. "Everything costs. Priests say grace is free because priests aren't paying." She pulled a trout, wrapped it in cloth, put it in his pack without being asked, the way she'd done for twenty years. "You want to know why you really climbed here tonight, and it isn't the network. The network's warned. Salt's salted. So I'll ask you plain, keeper, and you can lie to me or not: what did you check, the night the pilgrims first came to your gate?"

He hadn't told anyone that. Hadn't told anyone that after turning back the zealots, after logging nothing, after standing his practiced calm up like a fence against the wind—he had climbed alone to the sealed spire-gate in the dark and put his palm against the seal-stone. And the seal-stone, which had been cold as grave-metal for twenty years, had been warm.

Warm, and faintly rhythmic. Like something breathing behind a wall.

"Sefa."

"Mm."

"How many old shrines are there between here and the capital?"

She counted with her spatula, ticking points in the air. "Tended ones, eleven. Untended—" The spatula stopped. "Untended I stopped counting last spring. Because new ones keep appearing. Little cairns, wayside stones, marks on trees. Springing up along every road like mushrooms after rain. Nobody builds them that anyone sees. Offerings just start appearing on them."

"Offerings."

"Bread. Coins. Teeth, sometimes." She said *teeth* the way she said *trout*. "And here's the part you climbed here for. On the shrine nearest my smokehouse—the one in the birch stand—I've watched the offerings three nights running. Bread goes overnight. Coins stay. Teeth stay." She turned fully to look at him then, and her old eyes were steady as a level. "Something up there eats bread and leaves silver, keeper. That's not how gods work. Gods take the precious things. This takes the humble ones and pays for them. Like a customer. Like a *buyer*."

The kitchen was very warm and very quiet. Somewhere outside, wind worried the eaves.

"Show me," Ulyana said.

* * *

The shrine in the birches stood forty minutes' climb above the smokehouse, and Sefa walked it without stopping, which shamed him slightly, so he pretended not to notice and matched her pace.

It was nothing. That was his first impression and it refused to leave. A waist-high slab of grey stone, uncarved except for a shallow bowl worn into its top—worn, or hollowed, he corrected himself, because the bowl's edges were too regular for weather. Around its base, offerings: a heel of black bread gone stiff with frost, three copper coins, a knuckle bone he chose not to identify further. Birch trunks leaned around it like a congregation at the back of a cold church.

"This it?"

"This it." Sefa hung back. She'd come as guide, apparently, and no farther.

He circled the slab. No inscription. No god-mark—not the sun-disc of the dynastic cult, not the horned crescent of the older rite, none of the symbols he'd spent half his life cataloguing for border permits and relic taxes. Just stone, bowl, offerings.

And footprints.

They started at the treeline and came straight to the shrine, and then they went away from it, uphill, toward the high country. Toward the pass. Toward the spire.

Barefoot prints. Each one rimmed with melted-and-refrozen water, a thin glaze of ice like a cast around every toe. And between the shrine and the treeline the snow was disturbed in a fan-shaped sweep, as if something had knelt there, or gathered itself, or—

"They weren't there yesterday?" he asked.

"Not pointing uphill, they weren't. Yesterday they pointed down-valley. Toward town." Sefa's voice stayed level but had gone quiet, the volume of someone in a sickroom. "Tonight they point at your gate."

Ulyana crouched beside the print closest to him. He didn't touch it. Twenty years of gatekeeping teaches you which things not to touch, and this was one, in the marrow-deep way you learn after you've touched the wrong thing once. The print was long—longer than any man's foot he'd ever measured—and the toes were wrong. Too even. Too many, possibly. He counted twice and got a different number each time and decided, firmly, to stop counting.

The offering bowl held nothing now. Clean. Wiped. But at its rim, in the frost, a residue of crumbs. Bread again. Something had eaten here, recently, and moved on.

Uphill. Toward the gate he kept. Toward the seals he checked twice a night like a man checking a wound for fever.

"Ulyana." Sefa, still at her distance. "You're the last man on this mountain who remembers what the spire was for. Before the dynasty, before the king, before all of it. Your office is older than the crown—you know that? Gatekeeper wasn't invented to keep smugglers out. It was invented to keep something *in*, or something *out*, I never learned which, because the men who knew stopped talking about it a long time ago." She pulled her shawl tighter. "So I'll ask the question you've been not-asking yourself since the warm seal. If the prayers are being answered by something that walks barefoot and eats bread and leaves silver—if it's buying its way up the mountain, meal by meal, prayer by prayer—then what's it walking toward?"

He looked at the footprints a long moment. The wind moved through the birches, and for one absurd heartbeat he thought he heard, threaded inside it, a sound like a congregation very far below the ground, humming on a single note.

"An open door," he said.

Then he straightened, brushed the snow from his knee, and did the thing that twenty years had built him for, the thing beneath all the smuggling and the lying and the salt: he made a decision and closed his hand around it.

"Sefa. New orders. The network stops carrying pilgrim traffic entirely—not just rumors, actual refusal, full penalty rates for anyone who guides them. I don't care what it costs. I'll cover the losses myself." He was already calculating routes, timing, who to wake tonight. "And send a bird to the woman at Cindermilk and the man at Greywater: whatever they've promised me, double it, because starting tomorrow I need every pair of eyes on the high trails watching for a barefoot walker going uphill."

"And if your eyes find it?"

"Then they're to come to me and no one else, and they're to say the words 'the bread is moving,' and nothing more."

Sefa studied him. "You think it can be stopped at a gate."

"I think," Ulyana said, turning downhill toward the dark and the pass and the warm seal waiting for his palm, "that gates are the only honest argument I've ever had with the world. Everything else is negotiation. A shut door is a sentence." 

Behind him, in the birches, the frost on the footprints began, very slowly, to melt—as though the mountain itself had exhaled—and far up the switchback trails, small and clear across the miles, a bell that belonged to no chapel began to ring.

He had checked the seals twice last night.

He decided, walking fast, that tonight he would check them three times.
