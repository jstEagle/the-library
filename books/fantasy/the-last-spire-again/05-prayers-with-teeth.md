## 5. Prayers With Teeth

Maren had learned to measure safety in small things. A door that latched. A fire that burned without smoke. A room where nobody asked her name. The waystation at Harrow's Rest offered none of these, but it was the last roof before the high passes, and her boots had given out two miles back, so she took what she could get and paid for it with a coin scraped of its royal stamp.

The common room was full. That was the first wrong thing.

In the years since she'd fled the capital, pilgrim traffic on the Thornfell roads had been a trickle — gray wanderers, penitents, the occasional relic-hawked fraud. Tonight the benches held forty people at least, packed shoulder to shoulder, and every one of them had the same look: not joy, exactly. Expectancy. The look of people waiting for a letter they already know is coming.

She found a corner near the kitchen door, ordered stew with her second fake name of the day — *Vess*, this time; it was easier to answer to names that started soft — and made herself small beneath a hood that smelled of tallow and other people's rain.

A woman across the room was telling anyone who'd listen about her prayer.

"I said it plain, like my mother taught me. *Hear me, Veyra of the open hand.* And I said it Tuesday, and I said it Wednesday, and on Thursday—" She stopped. Her hands were doing something in her lap, folding and unfolding, like a bird deciding whether to fly. "On Thursday something said back."

"What did it say?" someone demanded.

"It said my name." The woman's voice dropped, went reverent and slightly wrong, the way voices go when they're proud of something shameful. "Not Veyra-the-goddess. *Me.* It said, *Ilsette, you are heard. Ilsette, you are known. Ilsette, bring your sorrow to the spire and lay it down.*" She looked around the room as if daring someone to laugh. "Twenty-two years I prayed into silence. Twenty-two years. And now—"

"Now it talks," a man near the hearth said flatly.

Maren marked him without meaning to. He was a drover by his coat, big through the shoulders, weathered to the color of old rope, with the particular patience of a man who has spent his life arguing with animals and losing slowly. He was eating his stew the way she ate hers: watching the room over it.

"It *answers*," Ilsette corrected.

"Things that answer aren't always things you want talking to you." He spooned up more stew. "My father had a dog that answered. Lovely animal. Would come to any whistle, any call, any voice. You know why?"

Nobody asked. He told them anyway.

"Because it didn't care who was calling. It just wanted to be fed."

A nervous ripple of laughter went around the room and died quickly, because most of the room didn't find it funny. Most of the room had prayers of their own that had been answered, and you could see them holding those answers against their chests the way you hold a hot coal passed hand to hand — grateful, and burning, and unwilling to put it down.

Maren ate her stew and did not pray and did not laugh.

She had stopped praying eleven years ago, in a corridor smelling of beeswax and blood, when she had understood that whatever listened from behind the walls of the palace was not her husband anymore. She'd never told anyone that. There was no one left alive who would believe the details.

* * *

It began an hour past moonrise, with a cough.

A young man by the window — a mason's apprentice, by the lime dust still ghosting his sleeves — cleared his throat mid-sentence and kept going in a different voice. Not a different pitch. A different *arrangement*, as if several men were speaking through one throat and had agreed, in advance, on every word.

"The road closes," he said pleasantly, "at the snowline."

His neighbor, an old woman darning a sock, looked up. "What's that, love?"

"The road closes," the apprentice said, "at the snowline. All roads close. All roads lead. Bring what you were promised. Come as you are called." He smiled. It was a good smile, warm, entirely natural, and it did not touch any part of him above the mouth. "You are all called."

The darning needle paused. Around the room, in ones and twos, other pilgrims turned their heads toward the apprentice — not alarmed, Maren noticed. *Orienting.* Like flowers finding sun.

"Ilsette," the apprentice said.

"Yes," Ilsette breathed, standing.

"You have brought your sorrow."

"I—" Her hands flew to a bundle at her breast, wrapped in blue cloth, clutched the way other women clutched infants. "I brought it. I carried it the whole way."

"Set it on the table. Let it be seen."

She set it down with the tenderness of a woman laying down a sleeping child, unwrapped the cloth, and revealed — Maren leaned forward despite herself — a knot of hair and wax and nail-parings, worked into a crude doll no bigger than a fist, worn smooth from handling. Twenty-two years of handling. A sorrow made solid enough to carry.

The drover by the hearth had stopped eating. "That's enough of that," he said, rising. "Whatever game this is—"

"*Drover Tam Wicke,*" the apprentice said, and the whole room said it with him — thirty voices, forty, in perfect unison, a chord of flesh — "*you doubt.*"

Tam Wicke stood very still. Maren watched the blood leave his face in real time, watched a man discover that the world has a floor and the floor is not where he thought.

"That's a trick," he said. His voice came out smaller than he wanted it to. "Someone told you. Someone's paid to—"

"We do not lie," the room said. Every mouth moving together. The old woman with the sock. Ilsette. Two children huddled under a bench, saying it in their sleepwalking child-voices, eyes closed. "*We gather. We offer. We make room for what is coming home.*"

"Coming home," Tam repeated. "What's coming home?"

And here was the thing Maren would take with her from Harrow's Rest, the thing she would turn over on cold nights for the rest of her life however many rest of her lifes there were: the room went silent, all together, the way a congregation goes silent — but underneath the silence there was a sound, faint, wet, patient, the sound of many mouths smiling wider than mouths should.

The apprentice answered alone, gently, kindly, the way you speak to a child or a dying man.

"Everything you lost. Everything you gave away. Every prayer you ever sent into the dark, drover. They were not wasted." He tilted his head, and the lamplight slid along his eyes and found nothing there to catch on. "They were *saved up*."

Tam Wicke looked at the room full of pilgrims, at their folded hands and shining faces, and Maren saw the exact moment he decided to be brave instead of smart. She had watched men make that choice her whole married life. It was almost always the last decision they got to make.

"The gods don't talk like this," he said. "My grandmother served at a shrine of Veyra forty years. You know what the gods ever gave us? Silence. Silence and weather. That was the bargain, and it was a fair one, because silence can't *lie* to you." He pointed a thick finger at Ilsette's doll of hair and wax. "That thing in your hands isn't listening to your sorrow, woman. It's *eating it*. And when there's nothing left of you but appetite, it'll eat that too."

For a heartbeat, nothing. The fire popped. Snow ticked against the shutters.

Then Ilsette said, in a voice gone smooth and remote as river ice:

"He keeps his tongue."

The room rose.

There was no rush, no frenzy — that was the worst of it, Maren thought later, that and everything else. They rose the way a congregation rises for a hymn. Unhurried. Certain. Forty people turning toward one man with the serene faces of people doing a thing that has been decided for a very long time, and Tam Wicke backed away two steps and put his shoulders to the wall and understood, as she understood, watching, frozen by the kitchen door with her heart slamming, that there was nowhere for those shoulders to go.

"Friends," he said. "Friends. Listen to yourselves—"

"We listen," they said, closing in. "*We listen. We listen. We listen.*"

Maren moved before she decided to move. It was an old instinct, older than her crown, trained into her by a childhood spent among courtiers who smiled while they sharpened: *when the room turns, be water, be gone, be nobody.* But her body carried her toward the wall instead of away from it, toward the shadow beside the kitchen arch, and she hated herself for it even as she did it, and she did it anyway, because she had learned at nineteen that dead queens save no one.

They took Tam Wicke's tongue with a serving knife and two pairs of hands and no hurry at all. He screamed until there was nothing to scream with, and then the screaming stopped, and then — this was the part that broke something in her that she suspected would not mend — the room knelt. All of them, around the spreading blood, kneeling in the rushes, and Ilsette lifted the tongue on the flat of the knife the way a priest lifts bread, and said:

"An offering. Freely given. Make room. Make room. What is coming home is *hungry*, and we will have room ready."

"Amen," whispered the old woman with the sock, and the word went around the kneeling circle like a spark down a fuse, and Maren turned and walked into the kitchen on legs made of someone else's courage.

* * *

The kitchen was dark and smelled of onions and old fat. A boy crouched behind the flour barrels.

He was maybe eight, thin as a winter branch, in a servant's smock two sizes too big, and he had both hands pressed hard over his own mouth. His eyes found hers in the dark and she saw the arithmetic happening in them — *adult, stranger, danger, help?* — the same arithmetic she'd been running since the palace gates closed behind her.

She crouched. Kept her voice below the fire's crackle.

"Did they see you?"

He shook his head. Then, muffled behind his fingers: "I ran when they stood up. Cook's boy. Nobody looks at cook's boys."

Smart child. She filed that away — *nobody looks at servants, nobody looks at queens in hoods, invisibility is a craft and it can be taught* — and put out her hand.

"Maren," she said, and immediately wished the name back, and covered: "Marren. Like the bird. What's yours?"

"Pell." He didn't take her hand. He was looking past her shoulder at the kitchen doorway, and his face, in the dark, was doing something no eight-year-old's face should know how to do. "Lady. Lady, they're singing now. They sing after. Cook said they sing after, at the other stations too, all down the road. Everyone sings after."

From the common room, low and sweet and perfectly harmonized, forty voices began a hymn she knew. That was the final horror of it, the detail that made her stomach drop through the floorboards and keep dropping: it was *the Evening Canticle of Veyra*, the lullaby-liturgy every child in the nine valleys learned before they learned letters, the song her own nurse had sung her, the song she had once sung — 

No. She would not finish that thought. Some doors stayed shut because you kept them shut.

But the words were wrong. She couldn't have said how at first, and then she could: the hymn was being sung *backward*, phrase by phrase, ending to beginning, an unmaking in harmony, a blessing run in reverse like water climbing a hill. *Lay us down, lay us down, gather what we scattered, carry what we cannot carry* — sung from the far end, arriving at its first line like a key arriving at a lock.

*Open, open, we are coming home.*

Pell grabbed her sleeve. Small fingers, iron grip. "We have to go now," he whispered. "When they finish singing, they count everyone. Cook said. They count, and anyone who isn't singing gets asked to sing, and if you can't—" He swallowed. His hands were shaking. So were hers. "Cook can't sing anymore. Cook's outside in the woodpile. Don't look at the woodpile."

She didn't look at the woodpile.

* * *

The back door opened onto the stable yard and the yard opened onto the night, and the night was doing something impossible.

Snow was falling upward.

Just above the station, just in the lee of the mountain's black shoulder, flakes hung suspended — thousands of them, motionless, glittering in the moonlight like a ceiling of stars nailed into place — and beyond them, up-slope, Maren could see the treeline bending. Not breaking. *Bending*, inward, every tree leaning toward the summit the way wheat leans toward sun, and at the top of everything, half-lost in cloud, a red glow pulsed slow as a heartbeat. Too far to be a fire. Too steady to be anything else.

The spire. Or the direction of it. The place every answered prayer on these roads bent toward, whether the prayers knew it or not.

"They're all looking at it," Pell whispered. Through the station's front window, visible past the kneeling singers, the pilgrims' faces had lifted — all of them, mid-hymn, eyes open and fixed on the same point of sky, tears freezing on cheeks that wore expressions of unbearable homesickness. As if something up there belonged to them. As if they'd only just remembered.

Maren pulled the boy back from the window and made herself think like a smuggler, because thinking like a queen would get them counted and sung and offered. Fences, she thought. Ditches. The drovers' cut below the hay barn — she'd seen it from the road, a drainage gap under the fence-line wide enough for a body if the body was willing to be cold and dirty. Ulyana Whitlock would know three better ways off this mountain by now, and the thought of him arrived unbidden and unwelcome and *useful*, which was the most dangerous kind of thought, because it meant relying on a man whose loyalty was a locked room she'd been circling for three days.

*The gatekeeper of Thornfell,* the border clerk had murmured, when she'd shown him her third false seal. *Man doesn't log so much as a stray goat. If the gods are answering prayers, he'll know. If anyone knows it isn't the gods —*

Behind them the hymn reached its true first line at last, and the whole building seemed to inhale.

"*Open, open,*" sang Harrow's Rest, forty throats tender with joy, "*we are coming home.*"

The suspended snow let go all at once, falling upward no longer, and the red pulse on the mountain beat once, twice —

and went dark, like an eye deciding to wait.

Maren took Pell's hand. "Run," she said, "and whatever you hear behind us — whatever calls your name in your mother's voice, whatever promises you anything —"

"I know," the boy said grimly, eight years old and already fluent in the grammar of monsters. "Don't answer."

They went out through the drovers' cut, into the snow, into the dark that at least had the decency to stay silent — and behind them, faint and sweet and following, something began to hum the canticle forward again, from the beginning, patiently, like a lesson that would be repeated until learned.
