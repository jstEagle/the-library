## 6. The Hollow Crown

You wake the way a man surfaces from deep water—too slowly, with too much pressure on his chest, and no memory of having gone under.

The first thing you see is your own hand.

It is moving. That is the wrong part. A hand should be still when you are not commanding it, and this one is moving across parchment in a long, confident script that is not yours. The letters are elegant. Your hand has always been clumsy; your tutors said so when you were nine, and your father said worse, and Maren once laughed and kissed the ink off your fingers and told you that clumsy hands were honest hands.

These hands are not honest. These hands are beautiful now, and they are writing something terrible.

*By order of His Majesty: let the tithe of prayer begin at the autumn equinox. Let all shrines within the realm report directly to the palace. Let none refuse.*

You do not know what the tithe of prayer is. You want to say so. You open your mouth to say so, and what comes out is:

"The seal."

Your voice sounds like your voice. That is somehow the worst thing of all. You could forgive it if it came out of you sounding like someone else—a stranger's voice, a borrowed voice—but no. It is exactly your voice, warm and low, the voice Maren used to fall asleep against, and it says *the seal* as if it has been saying nothing else for years, and the chamberlain hurries forward with wax and gold, and your beautiful dishonest hand presses down, and the decree becomes true.

That is how it works here. You press, and things become true.

* * *

You have tried to count the lucid hours. You keep the count somewhere the other thing cannot look, which is to say you keep it nowhere at all—you keep it the way a man keeps his mother's face, in the part of himself that has no walls for anyone to search. Eleven hours this year, by the nowhere-count. Eleven hours in which you have been Aldric instead of the shape that wears Aldric.

Eleven hours. You used to have eleven hours before lunch.

Here is what you understand about your situation, assembled slowly across those eleven hours like a man piecing together a shattered window from the shards he can reach:

Something lives in you. It came up through the spire's foundations, or down out of the sky, or out of the space between prayers—it does not matter where it came from; what matters is that it is old and hungry and patient, and it found the throne room of a kingdom whose god had been silent for three hundred years and thought: *here is an empty house with good bones.*

It did not kill you. You wish it had killed you. Killing you would have been mercy, and whatever this thing is, it does not deal in mercy. It deals in use. It kept you the way a farmer keeps a seed—buried, dark, alive enough. Because the fire at the heart of the capital spire, the last dragon-fire in the world, will not burn for the thing itself. It will only burn for a king. Something in its ancient covenant requires a crowned head, a consecrated body, a man anointed before gods who are mostly dead. So it keeps you. It keeps you breathing and beating and warm, and when it needs the fire fed or a decree signed or a crowd blessed, it puts you on like a coat and walks you out into the light.

And you watch. That is your whole life now. You watch.

* * *

Sometimes you get minutes, not hours. Minutes are worse, in their way, because there is no time to do anything with them except feel.

Today is a minutes-day. You surface in the middle of an audience, standing—standing? you never stand for audiences anymore—and looking down at a line of petitioners who are all weeping, and you understand with cold clarity that they are weeping because they believe a miracle just happened, and you understand further that no miracle happened, that the warmth they felt was your passenger reaching out through your skin and touching them, tasting them, taking its fee.

A woman holds up her infant. "He slept," she says. "Three years he didn't sleep, and last night after I prayed at the new shrine, he slept. Bless you, Majesty. Bless you forever."

*Take him back,* you want to scream. *Whatever it gave him, take it back, ask it what it ate to pay for that sleep.* But your mouth is smiling the smile it always smiles, gracious and slightly tired, kingly, and your hand extends over the child's small head in blessing, and beneath the smile you are doing arithmetic you cannot stop doing: every answered prayer costs a memory. You learned that in another lucid hour, watching a courtier who prayed for his brother's recovery and then, three days later, asked his wife why there were two chairs set at their table. Every miracle is paid for. Nothing here is free. The thing wearing you is not generous; it is *shopping*.

The audience ends. The doors close. And in the gap between the closing doors and the arrival of the next obligation—the gap it leaves you, the way a driver leaves a horse watered but bridled—you get forty seconds alone with yourself.

You spend them walking to the window.

The capital spreads below, gray-gold in the afternoon, chimney smoke going up straight because there is no wind, and beyond the rooftops the spire rises, black stone drinking the light, and at its crown the brazier burns. Even from here you can see it guttering. It used to burn steady as a fixed star. Now it stutters. Now it flickers like a candle in a doorway, and every time it dips, something in your chest dips with it, some tether pulling taut, and you understand—you understood it months ago, in hour six, maybe seven—that the fire is running out, and that when it goes out, the thing inside you will need a new fire, and there is only one left in all the world, and it is the one burning in you. In your blood. In whatever is left of the boy who was anointed at fourteen and thought being chosen was the same as being loved.

You put your palm flat against the glass. Cold. Real. You hold onto realness the way a drowning man holds onto a plank—there is not much of it, but it floats.

"Aldric."

You turn. It is using your own face to speak to you now. It does that sometimes, in the mirror-hours, standing where you stand, wearing what you wear. It has your face and it is smiling at you with perfect kindness, which is how you know it is lying.

"You are agitated," it says. "Agitation is bad for the vessel. Rest. I will carry us for a while."

"Let me write a letter," you say.

It tilts your head, curious, indulgent. This is a game you play sometimes. You ask for small impossible things, and it refuses them in small ways, and you learn the shape of its rules. "A letter?"

"To the man at the gate." Your throat is tight. "There was a gatekeeper at Thornfell, at the mountain passes. Before. When I still—" You cannot finish. Saying *when I still ruled myself* feels like handing it a map to the last locked room in you. "I owe him a letter. Let me write it and I will rest. Truly rest. You can have everything else."

It considers you. Behind your own eyes there is something vast turning over, patient as geology, and you feel it weigh the request—not the words, but the want underneath them, the way a merchant weighs a coin to hear if it rings true.

"No," it says gently. "But thank you for telling me about the gatekeeper."

And it takes you under.

* * *

Under is not sleep. Under is a room with no doors, and you have furnished it over the years, God help you, the way prisoners furnish cells—with memories arranged just so, with games played against yourself, with a floor plan of the palace drawn from memory on a floor that isn't there, in case, in case, in case.

In case of what? You have no answer that survives daylight. Escape is not a word that applies anymore. You are not locked in a place; you are locked in a *self*, and the key was swallowed by the lock.

But today, under, in the doorless room, you find that it has left something behind. An oversight, or a taunt, or a splinter of the morning's lucidity it could not fully digest: the gatekeeper's face.

Ulyana.

The name comes back to you the way blood comes back to a numb limb—painful, flooding, real. Ulyana Whitlock, who stood the night watch at the royal spire when you were young and couldn't sleep, who taught you which stars were liars and which roads out of the city didn't have tolls, who held the keys to everything important and treated them like they were nothing, like keys were just metal and the real locking was done by trust. Twenty years ago. Twenty-two. He vanished the night you changed. Everyone said he betrayed you, fled with the royal seals, sold the kingdom's secrets on the smuggler roads. You believed it, in the early years, when believing things was still something you got to do.

Now, floating in the dark, you remember differently—or you remember *something*, a fragment with edges sharp enough to cut: your own voice, hoarse, in the spire's underchamber, the fire roaring behind you and something already inside your mouth shaping your words. Remember Ulyana's face lit red by dragon-fire, terrified, loyal, doing the hardest thing anyone ever did for you.

Remember the order. *Your* order. Given through lips that were already only half yours: *Seal me in. Whatever I say after this, whatever I beg—seal it shut and don't look back.*

And then the guilt, his guilt, enormous as the mountain, and the sound of him running, and you—what was left of you—alone in the dark with the thing that wore your crown, sealed in by the best man you knew, who has spent twenty years believing he was a traitor.

He wasn't a traitor. He was the only honest man in the kingdom, and you let him carry the blame because carrying it himself would have required a self, and you were already being eaten.

The grief arrives without a body to shake, which is its own special horror. You grieve in the doorless room, silently, weightlessly, and when it finally exhausts you, what remains is purpose, thin and bright as wire.

*The man at the gate.*

If Ulyana is alive—if he kept the Thornfell passes all these years, if he is anywhere between here and the mountains—then there is one person in the world who knows what you ordered, one person who heard it from your own mouth before the mouth stopped belonging to you. One person who might believe that the thing signing decrees is not the king, and that the king is still, in the nowhere-place where you keep such things, alive.

And one more thing, which you assemble slowly, turning it over like a coin found in the street: the fire is guttering. The thing is hungry and getting hungrier, and it is reaching farther now—out past the capital, into the provinces, answering prayers by name in villages it shouldn't be able to touch yet. Reaching for something. Reaching for *someone*. You felt it strain toward the north this morning, toward the passes, and you felt, for one instant before it caught itself, a flicker of something almost like fear.

It is afraid of the man at the gate.

* * *

You surface again near midnight, and this time you are ready.

Lucidity comes like a knife slipped between ribs—sudden, cold, total—and you do not waste a second on confusion or despair. Your body sits alone at the writing desk in the privy chamber, candles burned to stubs, the day's decrees signed and stacked and gone. The thing is deep in its feeding; you can feel it distantly, savoring some harvest of answered prayers, glutted and slow. Slow is what you need. Slow buys minutes.

There is a hymnal in the drawer. There has been a hymnal in the drawer for twenty years; Maren's, from the chapel at Kestrel Ford, the only thing of hers the regents didn't confiscate. You take it out. Your hands shake—they are shaking *because of you*, and the joy of that nearly undoes you; even trembling is sweet when it is yours.

Paper. Ink. Write.

*To the man at the gate.*

The pen drags. Your hand wants to write beautifully again and you fight it, force it clumsy, force it honest. Clumsy hands, honest hands. Somewhere below, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the thing shifts in its feast, and you write faster.

*I don't know if these words can reach you. I don't know anything anymore except what I am about to tell you, so I will tell it plainly. I ordered my own sealing. You did not betray me. Whatever you have carried for twenty years, put it down. It was never yours.*

*The fire is dying. When it dies, the thing in me must feed the last fire in the world into itself, and I am the wick. Do not let it complete. Whatever it offers you—and it will offer, it knows what men want, it has had my wanting to study—refuse it. Refuse it even if it offers you back every year you lost. Especially then.*

*Come to the spire. Bring the queen if she lives, though she will hate what I have become and she will be right to. Come to the crown-chamber and unbind me. It will kill me. Understand that clearly: I am asking you to kill me, and I am asking you to hurry.*

*You were the best of them all, and I made you carry the worst of it. Forgive an old friend his last command.*

*A., who was king.*

The ink is barely dry when the temperature of the room changes.

It is not wind. It is not cold. It is *attention*—the specific, dreadful attention of something very large noticing something very small, and you know with absolute certainty that you have run out of time. It felt the flare of purpose in you. It always feels the flares; usually it arrives before the second sentence.

You have perhaps ten seconds. Maybe fewer.

Think. Where does a thing not look? Not in the desk—it searches desks. Not under the mattress, not in the wall, not in any hiding place, because it does not search places, it searches *minds*, and it will pull the hymnal's location out of you the moment it arrives unless—

Unless someone else holds it.

Footsteps in the corridor. The night page, making rounds, a boy of twelve with a choirboy's clear voice and a habit of humming while he walks, a boy who has served the hollow king for a year and never once suspected, a boy whose mind is quick and light and utterly unaware of what he carries past every night.

Ten seconds. Five.

You wrap the hymnal in the day's discarded blotting paper, and you cross the room, and you open the door just as Tam comes around the corner mid-hum, and you put the book into his hands with the full weight of your remaining royalty, your remaining humanity, your remaining everything pressed into four whispered words:

"Hold this. Don't read it. Keep it hidden, and someday—someday soon—a man will come asking at the gates, a gray man from the mountains with honest hands. Give it to him and no one else. Swear it."

Tam stares. Boys stare at kings. Then, because he is twelve and loyal and has no idea he has just become the most important person in a dying kingdom, he swears.

Behind your eyes, the dark begins to turn.

"Run along," you say aloud, in the warm, tired, gracious voice, while underneath it you scream. "And Tam—thank you."

The door closes. The dark takes you. The last thing you hold onto, as the doorless room swallows you whole, is not hope exactly—it is smaller and harder than hope, more like a splinter, more like a nail gripped in a fist: *a gray man from the mountains, with honest hands.*

Somewhere north, in the snowline, the passes are filling with pilgrims.

Somewhere north, prayers are starting to get answered by name.

Hold on, you tell the darkness, tell the wire-thin spark of yourself. Hold on. Help is coming down the mountain road, and he doesn't know it yet, but he is coming.
