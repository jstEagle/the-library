## 8. The Choir Below

The mine-shrines began an hour after we left the snowline, and they were wrong in a way I couldn't name until Ulyana named it for me.

"Look at the wax," he said, crouching at the first one. It sat in a niche where the tunnel widened—a shelf of black stone, a stub of candle, a bowl of copper gone green with age. "Old shrines, these. Miners used to light them before a shift. Ask the deep gods to hold the mountain's temper."

"And?"

"And nobody's lit this candle." He touched the wick, rubbed the ash between his fingers. "But somebody's been praying here. Look at the floor."

I looked. Kneeling prints in the dust—dozens of them, overlapping, some fresh enough that the edges hadn't crumbled. People had come down into an abandoned mine in ones and twos, in the dark, without light enough to leave soot on the walls, and knelt at a cold shrine.

"That's not devotion," I said. "That's summons."

Ulyana glanced back at me, and for a moment I saw past the gatekeeper's flat calm to something quicker underneath—the animal attention of a man who had survived twenty years by noticing things. "You've seen it before," he said. Not a question.

"I saw a court do it." I kept my voice level. "My husband's court. When the prayers started being answered, half the lords went to their knees inside a month. They called it grace. It looked more like hunger to me." I turned the candlestub over in my hands. Cold wax, but soft, as if something warm had been held against it. "They'd kneel anywhere. Corridors. Stables. Once, three of them in the middle of the tiltyard while a match was going on around them, singing under their breath."

"Singing what?"

"I didn't stay to learn."

He nodded slowly and did something I was beginning to recognize as his version of agreement: he took out his little ledger book and wrote nothing in it. Just held the pencil over the page for a moment, then put it away.

"You log nothing," I said.

"Nothing that can be read." He shouldered his pack. "Come on. There's a cut-through through the old Veyran workings. Two days' walk saves us nine above ground, and nine days of pilgrim roads is nine days of being recognized. You're still the most wanted woman in the kingdom, Majesty."

"Don't call me that."

"Then stop standing like that."

I laughed before I could stop myself—a short, cracked sound that bounced off the tunnel walls and came back sounding like someone else's voice. Ulyana didn't smile, but something at the corner of his mouth shifted, and we walked on into the dark together, two liars with a lamp between us.

* * *

The second shrine was lit.

That was how I knew, before I heard anything, before I felt anything, that the day had turned. The first shrine had been cold and empty; this one, three hours deeper along the Veyran cut, burned with a fat yellow flame that cast our shadows long up the tunnel—and kneeling before it were four people who did not turn when our light touched them.

"Ho," Ulyana said softly. His hand found my shoulder and pressed me back against the wall, out of the shine. "Don't."

"They haven't seen us."

"They don't need to see us." He set the lamp down behind a fallen timber and came forward alone, low, quiet as water finding its way downhill. I watched him study them the way he'd studied the seals on his gate: methodically, looking for the flaw.

They were ordinary pilgrims. That was the horror of it. A broad woman in a dyer's smock, hands stained violet to the elbow. An old man with a woodcutter's shoulders and a boy's face of simple peace. Two others whose clothes said farmers, road-worn, boots split at the seams. Any of a thousand souls tramping toward the capital since the miracles started. Except—

Except they weren't praying. Their lips moved, but no sound came out. And their chests rose and fell in unison. Four strangers, breathing as one body, mouths shaping words I could almost—but not quite—read.

"The voice-taken," I whispered.

Ulyana came back to me without ever turning his back on them. "You've heard the term?"

"At court. In the last dispatches before I ran. Pilgrims who stopped speaking in their own voices. The priests called it possession by grace." I watched the dyer-woman's lips move, move, move. "What would you call it?"

"Borrowed." He crouched, taking a twist of cloth from his pack. "A thing that lends you a voice wants something back. Question is what." He unfolded the cloth: coarse grey salt, and beneath it, a strip of dried meat, a bent iron nail, a scrap of ribbon faded pink. "Smuggler's kit. Old roads, old rules. Salt for silence, iron for doors, and—" he tapped the ribbon, embarrassed, almost— "a little borrowed faith. Something loved, carried close. It reads as belief to anything that's listening for belief."

"Does it work?"

"It's worked twice." He handed me the nail and half the salt without asking whether I believed in any of it. Maybe he saw in my face that I'd stopped believing in much of anything except my own survival. "Rules. From here to the far side of the workings, you don't speak. Not a word, not a whisper. You don't sing, you don't hum, you don't clear your throat. If your body makes a sound you didn't choose—stomach, joints, breath—you let it happen and you don't apologize to me afterward."

"And if I need to tell you something?"

"Touch my shoulder once for stop, twice for go. Squeeze for danger." He looked at me then, fully, and there was nothing performative in it. "Maren. This part matters. What's down there isn't hunting by sight or scent. It listens for the shape of a voice the way a spider reads a web. Every sound you make tells it what you are. So for two hours, you are no one. Understood?"

I thought of my daughter's name and how it felt in my mouth, and I nodded, and I did not say it aloud. First lesson learned before the descent even properly began.

We went down.

* * *

The mountain swallowed the daylight in stages. First the grey behind us thinned, then the quality of the dark changed—from absence to presence, from something we moved through to something that moved around us, attentive. The air grew warm and mineral, tasting of iron and old smoke, and the tunnel walls changed from cut stone to raw seam-rock veined with something pale that caught our single lamp and gave it back grudgingly.

Ulyana navigated like a man reading a letter he'd written himself. Forks that looked identical he passed without slowing; once he stopped dead at a junction, listened, and chose the branch with a broken ladder rather than the clean one. "Clean means used," he breathed, barely voicing it. "Used means known."

I counted the shrines as we passed them because counting kept the fear at a manageable size. Four. Seven. Eleven. All lit now, every one, small flames burning without flickering in air that should have stirred them. At the ninth, the offering bowls were full—not coins, not bread. Buttons. Teeth. A wedding ring. Little pieces of people, surrendered.

At the twelfth shrine, we heard the first voice.

It came from below and ahead, a single thread of sound rising through the rock like water through chalk. Not words yet. A hum. But a *shaped* hum, melodic, patient, with the structure of a hymn worn down to its bones.

Ulyana stopped. Put out two fingers against the wall. Listened the way I imagined he listened at his gate—for what was on the other side of it.

Then he did something that frightened me more than the humming did. He took off his boots. Set them beside the track, toes pointing onward, and gestured for me to do the same. Barefoot on freezing stone, we went on, and now I understood the shape of his caution completely: he wasn't trying to be silent. Silence was impossible. He was trying to be *unrecognizable*—to make our sounds small, formless, animal. Footsteps could be read. Bare skin on rock was just weather.

The hum became voices as we descended. Plural. Dozens.

And then the tunnel opened, and I saw them, and I have no language adequate to what I saw, so I will give you the plain inventory and let you build the cathedral of it yourself.

An old working, vast as a buried church, pillars of raw stone holding up a ceiling lost in dark. Tiered ledges where ore-carts had once run, now crowded with people. Hundreds. Sitting, standing, kneeling, packed into the ledges like swallows' nests, their clothes a hundred different trades and seasons—dyers and miners, a woman in fine kid slippers ruined to rags, a man still wearing a town watchman's cloak with the badge torn off. Every face wore the same expression: gentle, emptied, *pleased*. And from all those throats, in perfect unison, came the hymn.

It was beautiful. That is the thing I cannot make anyone believe who hasn't heard it. It was the most beautiful sound I have ever heard, and it was made of stolen mouths. The harmony folded over itself in patterns no human choir could sustain, because human choirs breathe; this did not breathe. The unison breathing I'd seen at the shrine—that had been rehearsal. Here, the song itself did the breathing, drawing air through hundreds of chests in one long tide, in, and out, and in.

And the mountain was answering.

I felt it before I understood it—a vibration under my bare feet, deeper than sound, a slow pulse rising through the rock like a heartbeat remembering itself. Where the pale veins in the walls caught the shrine-light, they seemed brighter than they'd been above. Warmer. As the hymn swelled, a vein directly ahead of us pulsed once, softly, like flesh under skin.

They were singing it awake. Whatever slept under the capital, whatever had been wearing my husband's crown and answering prayers with hungry generosity—they were singing the mountain's own deep blood into motion, and the mountain was turning over in its sleep, and every vein of it was leaning toward the spire.

I grabbed Ulyana's arm. Twice. *Stop.* He was already stopped. He stood very still in the shadow of a pillar, and I watched him take in the congregation with that ledger-keeper's eye, cataloguing exits, and I saw him arrive at the same arithmetic I had: the tunnel we'd come by curved within sight of the lowest ledge. The way onward ran along the chamber's edge, thirty feet of open ground between the last pillar and the far passage. Thirty feet in full view—or near view—of several hundred people who did not see, because seeing was not among the senses they were using anymore.

He pulled me back behind the pillar. In the dark, his hands spoke: he pointed at the salt, at the ribbon, at the far passage, then held up fingers. Three things. We would need three things.

He worked fast and quiet. He poured a line of salt across the tunnel mouth behind us—an old smuggler's seal, he'd told me once: not magic, exactly, but a boundary anything listening would hesitate to cross, the way a dog hesitates at a threshold. Then he tore the dried meat in half and pressed a piece into my hand. *Eat,* the gesture said. *Your stomach will growl either way. Feed it so the sound is small.*

Last, the ribbon. He tied it around my wrist, then his own—faded pink silk, soft with handling, someone's keepsake carried against the skin for years. Borrowed faith. It felt absurd. It felt like the only honest thing in the mountain.

He looked at me. I looked at him. And in that look, in the lamplight we'd shuttered to a coin-sized glow, something passed between us that I want to set down accurately because it mattered: trust. Not the negotiated, conditional arrangement we'd been calling trust since the snare in the snowline. The other kind. The kind you extend across open ground in a haunted mine because there is no alternative and because, God help me, I believed he would get me to the other side.

His hand closed over mine once—squeeze, danger, and also something else—and we stepped out onto the open ledge.

* * *

Thirty feet has never been longer.

The hymn poured over us like warm water. Up close it had texture—I could hear individual voices braided inside the unison now, a man's low drone, a girl's bright thread, an old woman's cracked note held steady by the whole—and each of those voices had belonged to someone, and none of them belonged to themselves anymore. Walking through that sound was like walking through a crowd of sleepers who might wake.

At fifteen feet, my foot came down on a loose flake of stone.

It didn't clatter. It *clicked*—a small, dry sound, less than a coin dropped, less than a cough. In the roaring unison it should have been nothing. A grain of sand in a river.

The hymn stuttered.

Not stopped—*stuttered*, the way a heart stutters, one beat misplaced, and in that gap I heard the mountain's pulse skip too, and every head on the nearest ledge turned. Not toward us. Toward the *sound*, faces tilting with terrible synchronized grace, eyes still closed, mouths still smiling, listening with their whole bodies.

Ulyana's hand closed on my wrist over the ribbon. Don't run. Running has a rhythm; running is a sentence the thing could read. We stood. We became stones among stones. My heart was loud enough that I was certain it would betray us both, certain the whole congregation could hear my blood, and the turned faces hung there, drinking the silence where the click had been—

And then the dyer-woman from the first shrine, the one with violet hands, rose from the lowest ledge and walked toward us.

Toward *me*. Eyes shut. Smiling. She came to a stop an arm's length away, close enough that I could smell her—woodsmoke, dye, sweat, humanity—close enough to see that her lips were moving, shaping words without voice, and this time I could read them.

*All are welcome. All are welcome. All are welcome.*

Her hand came up. I did not move. I could not have moved; terror had nailed me to the stone. Her fingers, stained violet to the elbow, hovered over my chest—over my heart—and I felt a warmth come off her palm like the warmth off the candle at the cold shrine, something held against wax, something testing softness.

The ribbon on my wrist warmed.

Just that. A faded strip of silk, some dead or forgotten person's small beloved thing, and it warmed against my skin like a held hand, and the dyer-woman's palm paused, and her head cocked—reading, reading—and whatever she found there, she found *belief*. Small, tattered, borrowed, but present. Faith-shaped. Welcome-shaped.

Her hand withdrew. She bowed—deeply, graciously, the bow of an usher at a wedding—and returned to her ledge, and sat, and folded her hands, and the hymn resumed its tide as though it had never stumbled.

Ulyana's grip on my wrist loosened finger by finger. We crossed the remaining fifteen feet at the speed of growing moss. We reached the far passage. We went down it fifty paces, a hundred, until the hymn was a rumor and the mountain's pulse a faint drumming in the soles of our feet, and then, only then, he let me stop.

I put my back against the wall and slid down it and discovered I was shaking so hard my teeth chattered, and I let them chatter, because noise was allowed again and I have never been so grateful to make an ugly sound in my life.

Ulyana crouched in front of me. He untied the ribbon from my wrist with surprising gentleness, retied it, checked the knot. Then he spoke, voice rusty from disuse, pitched low anyway.

"She read you."

"What did she find?"

"A prayer." He met my eyes. "You were praying. Down there. I saw your lips."

Of course I had been. I'd been praying without deciding to, the way a drowning man grabs without deciding to. I thought about telling him what the prayer was. I decided he already knew. Everyone fleeing something prays the same prayer: *let me not be found, let me not be found, let me not be found.*

"And it answered," I said. "The ribbon answered. Your smuggler superstition answered."

"It's not superstition." He was quiet a moment, rolling the ribbon between his fingers. "Faith is real, Maren. That's the whole problem. It's a currency, and something down here has learned to mint it. Everything that answered those pilgrims' prayers—it wasn't kindness. It was a purchase. I've spent twenty years keeping goods off roads where the toll gets paid in ways you don't see till later." He pocketed the ribbon and stood. "Lowmark's a day ahead, through the flooded galleries. There's a man there—a priest, defrocked, drinks too much and hears too well. Brannic. If anyone can tell us what that hymn was *for*, and what happens when the mountain finishes waking up, it's him."

"You trust him?"

"No." Ulyana shouldered his pack and gave me the ghost of the gatekeeper's flat calm, but underneath it, for the first time since the snowline, something that might have been fear, honestly worn. "That's why it'll work. Trusting men is how you get caught. Distrusting them correctly is how you survive." He lifted the lamp, and the light reached down the flooded gallery ahead, where black water lay mirror-still, reflecting a darkness that had no business having a reflection.

"Maren," he said. "Look at the water."

I looked. Far out in the middle of the flooded gallery, where nothing stood and nothing could stand, the reflection showed a light. A tall, thin, golden light, like a candleflame in a high window.

There was no window. There was no candle. The water was showing us the spire, eighty leagues away, burning in a city we had not yet reached—and the light in the reflection was guttering.

Low, and lower, and lower.

"Come on," Ulyana said, and started walking on top of the dark water's edge, faster now. "We're out of time."
