## 9. An Honest Thief

Lowmark smelled like a town that had decided rules were for other people.

Ulyana had always liked that about it. The free-town sat in a fold of the hills where three borders met and none of them mattered, a sprawl of timber halls and leaning market stalls and smoke that smelled of tallow, tar, and roasting onions. No lord's banner flew here. No tax collector dared. The town's only law was the Ledger — the long list of debts and favors kept by whoever happened to hold the Ledger-keeper's chair, which changed hands roughly as often as the weather.

He came down off the smuggler roads at dusk, Maren hooded beside him, and felt his shoulders drop an inch for the first time since the snowline. Lowmark didn't care who you'd been. It cared what you owed.

"You've been here before," Maren said. Not a question. She'd learned to read him by now, or thought she had — the way he walked lighter on familiar ground, the way his eyes went to rooftops first and faces second.

"I wintered here once. Long time ago."

"Doing what?"

"Staying warm."

She snorted, but let it go. She was getting better at that, too. Two weeks on the road together had taught them both a grudging grammar of silence: when to push, when to pocket a question for later. Ulyana suspected she kept a whole ledger of her own, questions banked against him like coin.

The streets were fuller than they should have been. That was the wrongness he'd been chewing since the pass. Pilgrims. Dozens of them, then scores — thin travelers with frostbitten knuckles and prayer-knots tied round their wrists, moving through Lowmark not *toward* anywhere but *through* it, all flowing one direction like water finding a crack.

"Capital road," Ulyana murmured. "They're all pointed at the capital."

"Like the ones at the waystation," Maren said quietly.

Neither of them said the rest of it aloud. Neither needed to. The waystation was two hundred miles behind them now, but Ulyana still woke some nights hearing the chorus — forty pilgrims mid-sentence, all turning their heads at once like weathercocks answering the same wind.

They took a room at the Sign of the Split Kettle, because Ulyana knew its keeper owed him from a smuggling run gone bad eight years back, and because the Kettle was one of maybe three buildings in Lowmark without a shrine-corner in it. He paid double and asked no questions about why the price had tripled since his last visit.

"Pilgrims," the keeper said anyway, sliding the key across. "They pay in anything. Buttons. Teeth. One of them tried to settle his bill with a memory." He laughed, then stopped laughing when neither of them joined in. "You want food?"

"Food. And information. Is there still a priest working out of the old tithe-house? The one they—"

"Defrocked. Yes. Brannic." The keeper's mouth went sour. "Half the town thinks he's mad. Other half thinks he's the only honest man left. He sits in the tithe-house every evening and listens to prayers."

"People pray at him?"

"No." The keeper leaned in, dropping his voice as if the walls might be taking notes. "He listens to *prayers*. All of them. Says he can hear them the way you'd hear flies against glass — every prayer said within ten miles, buzzing past his skull. And he can tell you which ones get answered." He tapped the counter twice. "That's why they defrocked him. He told a congregation their beloved saint's miracle was bought with a dead girl's memories. In front of the bishop."

Maren went very still beside Ulyana.

"And can he?" Ulyana asked. "Tell which ones get answered?"

"He's never been wrong." The keeper straightened. "Food's mutton. It's always mutton."

* * *

The old tithe-house sat at the edge of the market square, a stone building with a collapsed roof over its eastern half, open to the sky like a broken tooth. Candles guttered inside — real wax, Ulyana noted, not prayer-tallow — and among the pews sat perhaps thirty people, heads bowed, lips moving.

And in the ruined chancel, sitting backward on a chair with his arms folded across its spine, sat a large man built like a grain sack, bald as an egg, wearing vestments so old they'd gone gray, with holes patched in three different fabrics.

Brannic did not look up when they entered. His eyes were closed. His lips moved faintly, tracking something.

"—and the miller's wife prays her son comes home from the coast," he was saying, to no one, in the tone of a man reading a shipping manifest. "Unanswered. The cooper prays his wife's cough improves. Unanswered. The tanner prays for rain. Unanswered. The woman in the green shawl prays her husband forgets the name of the woman he—" Brannic paused. "Answered. Oh, that's grim. That one cost him his wedding day, I'd wager. There it goes."

The woman in the green shawl lifted her head, blinking, faintly puzzled, already reaching for something just out of reach behind her own eyes.

Maren made a small sound. Ulyana put a hand on her arm.

Brannic opened his eyes. They were pale, watery blue, and utterly tired. "If you're here to pray," he said, "I regret to inform you the management is a fraud and the service is listening-only. If you're here to have me arrested, the bishop's men gave up years ago. If you're here for either of those things, you're standing in the wrong building, because I'm the other thing."

"What other thing?" Maren asked.

"The thing that tells you what your gods are actually doing." He unfolded himself from the chair with the grace of a collapsing barn. He was taller than he'd looked seated, and broader, and moved like a man who'd once carried a cathedral door under each arm. "You're not locals. You're not pilgrims — pilgrims don't look disgusted, they look hungry. Which makes you either robbers or trouble, and robbers would have taken my candlesticks by now."

"They're brass," Ulyana said.

"They're *sentimental* brass. State your business."

Ulyana looked at Maren. Maren looked at Ulyana. Some negotiation passed between them without a word — another entry in the ledger they kept on each other — and then Maren stepped forward and pulled back her hood.

Brannic studied her face for a long moment. Then he sighed, deeply, the sigh of a man whose evening had just gotten much worse.

"Oh good," he said. "A queen. I defrocked myself rather than deal with kings, and now the kings send queens. Sit down, Your Majesty. Mind the third pew; it bites."

"We need to know where the answered prayers are coming from," Ulyana said.

Brannic's eyebrows went up. "Do you, now. And why would a gatekeeper from Thornfell and a runaway queen care about the direction of miracles?"

Ulyana didn't answer. Brannic watched him for a moment, then nodded slowly, like a man confirming a diagnosis.

"That bad, is it. You've seen one. An answered prayer close-up." He wasn't asking. "You have the look. Pilgrims get it too, after — the ones who pray and win and can't remember what they won *for*. You've got the look of a man who watched someone lose something and couldn't name what."

"Can you trace them?" Maren pressed. "The answers. Can you tell where they come from?"

Brannic held up one enormous finger. "I can tell you what I tell everyone, which is the truth, which nobody wants: a prayer isn't a letter. It's a sound. When it gets answered, the answer comes back along the same sound, like an echo. Most echoes are local. Little shrines, little spirits, little bargains struck by little dead things. Those I ignore." He began walking down the aisle, gesturing for them to follow. "But lately there's an echo that isn't local. Lately there's an echo like a bell hung in the deep of the world, and everything — *everything* — is bending toward it."

He reached the far wall of the tithe-house, where the plaster had fallen away to expose old stone, and pressed his palm flat against it. He closed his eyes.

"There's a farmer in Harrow Vale praying his blight clears. Answered this morning. The answer came from..." His brow furrowed. "East-northeast, and *down*, the way sound goes down a well. There's a widow in Millbrook praying for bread. Answered yesterday. East-northeast and down. There's a soldier in the garrison at Vail praying to forget a massacre he took part in." Brannic's jaw tightened. "Answered. Fully. East-northeast and down."

He turned around. "Every line bends the same direction. Every answer, for three months now, comes from the same place. I've been mapping it." He tapped the wall. "It's the capital. And beneath the capital, if the old maps are honest, there's a spire with something at the bottom of it that used to be fire."

Maren sat down hard on the biting pew. It bit her. She didn't notice.

"The dragon-fire," she whispered. "The king."

"The king," Brannic agreed cheerfully, "is a wick. And somebody or something has been lighting candles all over the country with the wax of people's lives. Every answered prayer costs the asker a memory — you knew that part, yes? No? Well. Now you do. The gods aren't granting wishes, friends. They're *eating*, and using the bones to build themselves bodies. And every scrap they eat gets carried home to the same table."

Silence, except for a candle sputtering.

"So," Brannic said, clapping his hands together with a report-like boom. "You're going to the capital. Both of you. I can see it in your posture — that particular grim set of the shoulders people get when they've decided to do something stupid and noble. I've spent twenty years watching that posture walk into my tithe-house, and it never ends well."

"We haven't decided yet," Maren said.

"You decided somewhere around the snowline. I'm coming with you."

Ulyana blinked. "You're what?"

"I'm coming with you." Brannic began gathering things: a worn hymnal, a sack of candles, a coil of salt-stiff rope. "Because I am the only person in this country who can hear which prayers are being answered and from how far away, which means I am the only early-warning system you have. Because you'll be walking into a city where the air itself will be thick with petition, and you'll need someone who can tell you which voices in the choir are the god's and which are just frightened people. And because—" he paused, hymnal in hand, and for a moment the cheerful bulk of him seemed very heavy indeed— "because I told a congregation the truth once and it cost me everything, and I swore I'd never again watch a lie walk out the door while I stood still. So. I'm coming."

"It's dangerous," Maren said.

"Majesty, I listen to prayers for a living. Danger would be a promotion."

"It's suicide," Ulyana said.

"Also a promotion." Brannic slung the sack over his shoulder. "Besides, you need a priest. Whatever you're planning to do at the bottom of that spire — and don't tell me, I don't want to know, deniability keeps my sleep — you'll want someone who knows the old rites well enough to break them. Defrocked, remember. I know exactly where the seams are."

Ulyana looked at Maren. Maren, to his surprise, was looking at him back — waiting. Asking. Somewhere in the last two weeks the question of who led this had quietly stopped being hers alone to answer, and neither of them had remarked on it.

"He snores," Ulyana said at last.

"Like a landslide," Brannic agreed.

"He eats like a siege."

"A compliment. Thank you."

"He'll want payment."

"I take payment in truths," Brannic said. "One truth per day, from each of you, spoken aloud, however small. That's my fee. A priest on the road needs sermons, and I'm fresh out of my own."

Maren laughed — short, surprised, the first laugh Ulyana had heard from her that hadn't had blood in it. "An honest thief," she said. "Stealing our stories instead of our silver."

"Madam, I was a thief of souls' secrets professionally for fifteen years. Stories are a step up in honesty."

Ulyana rubbed his eyes. This was a mistake. This was absolutely a mistake, adding a loud defrocked oracle with a taste for dramatic exits to a party of two liars carrying enough buried history to fill a mass grave. Everything in him that had survived twenty years at Thornfell said *stay small, stay quiet, stay unknown.*

But the keeper's words were still in his ears. *He's never been wrong.*

And the lines all bent east-northeast and down. Toward the spire. Toward the king. Toward whatever Ulyana had sealed shut with his own hands on the worst night of a life full of contenders.

"Fine," he said. "Dawn. North gate. If you slow us down, we leave you."

"Naturally." Brannic blew out the candles one by one, saving the last. "Though I'll note, for the record, that nobody has ever successfully left me anywhere. I'm like a debt."

They walked out into the Lowmark night. The market square had emptied of pilgrims hours ago — all of them flowing east, all of them pointed at the capital, prayer-knots swinging on their wrists like little hanged men.

At the edge of the square, Brannic stopped. He tilted his head, the way he'd tilted it in the tithe-house, listening to something far off in the dark.

"Huh," he said.

"What?" Maren asked.

"Someone just prayed, not fifty yards from here. Small prayer. Child's voice. Praying for her mother to remember her." Brannic's face, lit by the last candle, had gone carefully blank. "*Answered.*"

He looked at the other two, and the candlelight made his pale eyes look like holes in the world.

"The answer came from the capital," he said. "Which means the capital is listening *here*. Which means, friends, we are already on somebody's route home."

* * *

They made the north gate before dawn, and Ulyana spent the cold hours doing what he did best: checking knots, counting supplies, memorizing the road ahead. Brannic slept sitting up against the gatepost, snoring like a landslide, exactly as advertised.

Maren stood beside Ulyana at the wall, looking east, where the sky was going the color of old scars.

"Your priest," she said. "He said the answered prayers cost memories. He said it like it was arithmetic. Like everyone knew."

"Did you know?" Ulyana asked.

She was quiet a long moment.

"I think," Maren said slowly, "that I need to check something. Something I traded. On the road. Before I met you."

Something in her voice made Ulyana turn and look at her properly. Her face was composed, the queen's face, the one she wore like armor. But her hands, laced tight on the wall in front of her, were shaking.

"Maren," he said. "What did you trade?"

She didn't answer. Behind them, Brannic stirred, woke, and squinted at the lightening sky with the expression of a man preparing to collect his fee.

"Well," he said cheerfully. "Day one. Who's paying first?"
