## 10. The Price Already Paid

Brannic had a rule about revelations: never give them away for free. Truth was the only currency left to a defrocked priest, and he'd learned in Lowmark's gutters that people paid better for truth when they were afraid.

He wasn't planning to charge these two, though. That was the strange part. The queen with her frostbitten hands and her too-careful face, and the gatekeeper who watched everything and admitted nothing—they'd fed him stew, given him dry socks, and hadn't once asked him to prove his usefulness. Twenty years since anyone had done that.

So on the third night out of Lowmark, huddled under an overhang while sleet ticked against the tarp, Brannic decided to tell them what he'd spent four years and every scrap of his credibility learning.

But first, the stew.

"You're stalling," Ulyana said.

"I'm seasoning my words." Brannic ladled another portion. "There's a difference. Stalling is cowardice dressed as patience. Seasoning is craft."

Maren laughed—a short, startled sound, like she'd forgotten she could. She sat close to the fire with her boots off, rubbing feeling back into her toes, and Brannic watched the gatekeeper watch her, and thought: *these two have a history, and neither of them will say so.* He'd heard enough confessions to know what unsaid looked like. It looked exactly like this—two people arranging themselves at careful angles around a hole in the conversation.

"All right," Brannic said. "Here's how I lost my collar."

Ulyana's eyebrows rose a fraction. "I assumed it was drinking."

"Also that. But first the doctrine." He turned his bowl slowly, buying one more moment of ordinary time. "The church teaches that prayer is a petition. You ask; the gods weigh; they answer or they don't. Grace, unearned. A gift." He looked up. "Four years ago I started keeping records. Actual ledgers—who prayed for what, whether it came true, and when. I had a parish in the Vail hills, nothing important, but I'm a fussy man and fussiness is just devotion with poor posture. And I noticed something."

"Spare us the suspense," Ulyana said, but he'd stopped eating. He was leaning in without meaning to. Men like him always leaned in when the thing being described touched their own dread.

"Every answered prayer cost the petitioner something," Brannic said. "Not coin. Not blood. Memory."

The sleet filled the silence for a moment.

"A woman in my parish prayed for rain during the drought," he went on. "It rained—three days, out of a clear sky, only over our valley. Miracle. Everyone said so. Two weeks later her husband died, and at the funeral she asked me why she couldn't remember his voice. Not grief—grief she had plenty of. She remembered loving him. She remembered the wedding. But his *voice* was gone. A hole where a sound used to be. And she said—" Brannic's own voice caught, the way it still did, "—she said, 'Father, did God take my payment?'"

"And you told her yes," Maren said quietly.

"I didn't know yet. I told her grief does strange things. Then it happened again. A farmer prayed his sick son well—the boy recovered, and the father couldn't remember his own mother's face. A girl prayed to find her lost ring and forgot the name of the river she nearly drowned in as a child. Small things first. Then bigger. I started writing it down: miracle granted, memory taken. Forty-one cases before the bishop told me to stop." He smiled without humor. "Then sixty. Then he told me to stop again. Then a pilgrim came through whose whole childhood had been eaten by a single answered prayer—one prayer, asking that his village be spared the fever—and I stood up at the harvest homily and told three hundred people that their gods weren't listening to them."

"What did you tell them instead?" Ulyana asked.

"That something was *listening*. Something hungry. That the old voices answering prayers weren't gods granting gifts—they were merchants running a tab. Every wish granted is a life nibbled. They're not coming back from wherever they went." He set the bowl down. "They're being rebuilt. Out of us. Stitched together out of other people's memories, other people's loves, until they have enough substance to walk around in. We're not their faithful. We're their larder."

Maren had gone very still. Firelight moved on her face and she didn't blink with it.

"The king," she said.

"Is either their finest work or their next coat," Brannic said. "I don't know which. But you've seen what's happening in the lowlands. Pilgrims by the thousand, all of them praying harder than ever, all of them getting answers now—the gods are hungry, and hunger makes them generous. Generosity is the trap. The more they grant, the more they can take, and nobody notices because the taking happens inside your own skull. Who audits their own mind? You notice a stolen purse. Nobody notices a stolen afternoon."

Ulyana was staring into the fire. When he spoke, his voice was flat, gatekeeper-flat. "You traced it. In Lowmark. You said the answers bend toward the capital."

"They do. Every line. I can hear which prayers get answered—it's a residue thing, a taste in the ear after, like copper. And when I follow the taste, it doesn't scatter toward shrines or holy places. It funnels. Northeast. Toward the spire. Whatever is eating these memories is dragging them all home to one hearth." He shrugged. "A body builds itself around a heart. Or a fire."

"And the memories themselves?" Maren asked. Her voice was controlled, but Brannic had spent years hearing what people hid beneath control. Under hers was terror. "What happens to them? Are they destroyed?"

"No." He hated saying the next part. "Worse. They're *used*. Somewhere in the capital there may be a woman who remembers a dead husband's voice, and it isn't hers anymore. Your stolen life gets worn by something else. It doesn't even get the mercy of ending."

Maren stood. Not abruptly—carefully, the way a person stands when standing is a decision made joint by joint. "I need to walk."

"It's sleet," Ulyana said.

"I need to walk anyway."

She went out past the edge of the tarp-light, into the gray murk, and Ulyana half-rose to follow, and Brannic said, "Let her."

"She's not dressed for it."

"Neither am I, and I'm staying put because some conversations are private even alone." Brannic poked the fire. "Let me guess. You two met before. Before she was fleeing anything."

The gatekeeper sat back down slowly. "Eat your stew."

"That's a yes."

"That's a shut door."

"I hear a lot of those." Brannic watched the flames. "In my line, doors shutting was the whole job. People come to a priest to open the one door they can't. Now I'm defrocked and the doors are all opening at once and I miss them shut, honestly. There was a dignity to locked doors."

Outside, the queen's footsteps crunched away and faded. The sleet kept ticking.

* * *

She was gone longer than a walk should take. Long enough that Ulyana got up twice and Brannic talked him down twice, the second time with more difficulty. When she finally came back into the light, her shoulders were white with ice and her face was wet in a way that sleet didn't explain.

She sat down. She held her hands to the fire. And then, without preamble, she said:

"My daughter's birthday."

Brannic kept very still.

"She'll be nine this spring. Is. She *is* nine this spring." Maren's voice was level, which meant she'd rehearsed the sentence outside in the dark. "And tonight I tried to count backward from now to the day she was born, and I could do it—all the dates, all the winters, I'm good with numbers—but between the counting and the remembering there was—" She stopped. Started again. "When I picture her at seven. At six. At any age. It's like looking at a portrait painted by someone who'd only had her described to them. Right shape. Wrong everything else. I know her name the way you know a word in a language you don't speak."

"Maren." Ulyana leaned forward.

"Don't." She raised a hand—not cruelly, just firmly, a border drawn. "Don't comfort me yet. Let me finish, because if I stop I won't start again." She turned to Brannic. "On the road north, at the shrine below the snowline. The one where offerings vanish. I prayed. I was desperate and frozen and I prayed for safe passage through the pass, and the pass opened for me—roads clear, weather turning, a smuggler's snare catching me gently instead of breaking my leg. I thought I was lucky. I thought someone up there liked me." Her laugh was terrible. "I paid for it. I've been paying for weeks and I didn't know the price because the merchant never shows you the ledger."

"How much is missing?" Brannic asked. It was the clinical question and he hated himself for asking it first, but someone had to, and better him than the gatekeeper, whose jaw was doing something complicated.

"I don't know. That's the horror of it, isn't it? You can't count holes you can't see. My mother's voice—I think. A house we lived in when I was small. Songs. Maybe songs." She wrapped her arms around herself. "How do you fight something that steals the reasons you'd want to fight it? Take enough and I'll kneel willingly. Take enough and I'll love them for it."

"Not you," Ulyana said. It came out rough, certain, and both of them looked at him, and he seemed surprised by his own certainty. "People forget things all the time. What they are doesn't go with the memories. You'd still—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "You crossed a mountain range alone in winter rather than kneel. That's not stored in a memory. That's load-bearing."

Brannic felt something twist in his chest that he refused to examine. *Load-bearing.* Twenty years of theology and no one had ever put it that well. He'd have stolen it for a homily if he ever gave one again.

"Pretty speech," Maren said softly. "But here's what I keep circling." She looked at Brannic. "Your forty-one cases. Sixty. However many now. All little people. Parish farmers, pilgrims, a village spared a fever. Why does a god eat minnows when it could eat whales?"

"Because whales are hard to catch," Brannic said. "Because a queen praying openly draws questions. Because—" He paused, because the real answer arrived as he said it, the way truth sometimes does, sideways. "No. That's not it. It's because minnows don't check the ledger. A powerful person loses a memory and they have secretaries and physicians and enemies who'd notice. A farmer loses his mother's face and everyone says grief. The small are eaten first because they're *safe* to eat. It's not strategy, it's habit. It's how everything feeds."

"Until it isn't small anymore," Maren said.

"Until it isn't small anymore." Brannic nodded slowly. "And that's the arithmetic that should frighten you more than anything I've said tonight. There are thousands of answered prayers now. Tens of thousands, if the roads are as full as your gatekeeper says. Every one a mouthful. Do you understand what's being assembled out of all those mouthfuls? It's not a god getting its strength back. It's a *composite*. A creature stitched from ten thousand strangers' best moments—first kisses, dead fathers' voices, children's faces, the taste of bread in a summer that ended thirty years ago. It will know us intimately. Better than we know ourselves. Because it will literally be *made* of us." He fed a stick to the fire. "And the worst part, the part that got me defrocked twice over: it won't be lying when it calls itself divine. What do you think a god ever was?"

Nobody answered. The sleet ticked. Somewhere far off, thunder rolled under the mountains, though the sky held no storm.

"So what do we do?" Ulyana asked at last. Practical man. Give him a problem shaped like a door and he'd find the hinges. "Stop praying? Tell people?"

"Telling people hasn't worked. They hear 'your miracles cost memories' the way they hear 'wine causes sorrow.' The wish comes true and the bill arrives invisibly, months later, to an audience of one. You can't protest a theft you can't see." Brannic spread his hands. "No. There's exactly one way to starve a thing that eats prayers, and it's not persuasion. It's cutting off the source. Whatever sits at the top of that funnel, wearing whatever it wears—if it dies, the answering stops. The ledger closes. Everything already taken stays taken, and I won't pretend otherwise, and I'm sorry for it. But the eating stops."

"And the source is the king," Maren said.

"Or the fire under him. Or the throne itself. I only hear directions, not architecture." He looked from her to the gatekeeper and back. "Which is why I'm following a fugitive queen and a man who won't say her name toward a city where every road bends northeast. In case either of you wondered why a coward like me volunteered."

"I didn't wonder," Ulyana said.

"I wondered," Maren said, and almost smiled.

* * *

Later, when the others slept, Brannic stayed awake with the fire and did what he always did with unbearable information: he organized it. Ledgers of the mind. He listed what they knew—the funnel, the composite, the price. What they suspected—the king, the fire. What they didn't—how to kill a thing made of everyone's best days.

His thoughts drifted, as they did at night, to the case that had ended him. Not the harvest homily. Before that. His sister, Illa, praying at his own altar for her sick boy, and the boy waking whole the next morning, and Illa weeping with gratitude, and Brannic—knowing, already knowing, ledger in hand—saying nothing. Letting her pay. Because the alternative was telling her the cure would cost her, and watching her choose, and living with whichever choice she made.

He'd let her pay. The boy was eleven now and healthy and Illa couldn't remember teaching him to walk.

That was the confession he'd never made, the reason "defrocked" fit him like a tailored coat. Not the heresy. The silence.

The fire settled. Across the camp, Maren slept badly, murmuring names—her daughter's, he guessed, testing them against the dark the way you test a loose tooth. Ulyana slept like a man guarding a door even unconscious, knife-hand curled near the fire-iron.

And Brannic sat between them and understood, finally, why he'd told them for free.

Because the ledger had to be read aloud somewhere. Because a price paid in secret stays paid forever, but a price named aloud becomes a debt, and debts can be collected. These two were going to walk into the capital and confront a thing built out of ten thousand stolen lives, and they needed to walk in knowing exactly what the thief had taken from them—so that whatever they chose to do there, they chose it with the account open in front of them.

He just hoped, watching the queen flinch in her sleep, that she'd finished counting her losses.

Because in the morning, he was fairly sure, she was going to ask him to help her find out precisely what was gone. And he'd seen what happened to people who went digging through their own holes.

Sometimes the hole had teeth.
