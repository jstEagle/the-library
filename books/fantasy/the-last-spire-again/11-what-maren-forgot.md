## 11. What Maren Forgot

She knew something was wrong before she knew what it was wrong about.

They had stopped for the night in a shepherd's bothy above the Grey Linn, a stone hut with a smoke-black roof and a floor that smelled of lanolin and old rain. Brannic had gone to sleep muttering calculations—prayer-lines bending, always bending, toward the capital—and Ulyana had taken first watch without being asked, which was his way of apologizing for things he never said out loud.

Maren sat by the fire with her pack open and was looking at a locket.

It was silver. It had been silver once; now it was black with road-grime, and she rubbed it with her thumb the way you'd rub a bruise, absent, habitual. Inside there should have been a portrait. She knew this the way she knew her own name—not because she could see it, but because the shape of the knowing was intact. A locket like this held a face. Her hands remembered opening it a thousand times. Her thumbs remembered the little catch that stuck, the one you had to press twice.

The portrait was gone. Not stolen, not scratched out. Gone the way a word goes when it sits on the tip of your tongue and then the whole sentence walks away and doesn't come back.

She opened the locket. The catch stuck. She pressed it twice, out of pure animal habit, and the two halves swung apart on an empty oval of faded velvet, and Maren, Queen of Vellmarin, fugitive from her husband's court, began to scream.

She didn't mean to. It came out of her like water through a cracked dam, low and continuous, and she clamped both hands over her own mouth and it kept coming anyway, muffled, awful, the sound of a body discovering a wound the mind hadn't reported yet.

Ulyana was through the door in three heartbeats, knife drawn, scanning the dark for whatever had gotten into the hut. Then he saw. He put the knife away. He stood in the doorway with snow melting off his shoulders and did absolutely nothing, which was, she would later understand, the most honest thing anyone had done for her in years.

* * *

"Her name is Ilse."

Maren said it aloud, testing it, the way you'd test ice. They were sitting across the fire now. Ulyana had made tea, badly—it was mostly hot water with ambition—and pushed a tin cup into her hands. Brannic slept on, or pretended to, curled against the far wall like a man who'd learned in seminary that some griefs are not for witnesses.

"Ilse," she said again. "Ilse, Ilse." The name fit her mouth. But behind it, where a face should have been, there was a courtyard in summer, a fountain, a nursemaid's apron, a small hand in hers—and the hand had no owner. She had built the memory back around the absence like a house around a missing room. "I can tell you everything about her except what she looked like. She was born in the spring. She was afraid of geese. She had—I know she had—" Her voice snagged. "I don't know if she had my hair or his."

"You traded it," Ulyana said. Not cruelly. Just laying the fact on the table between them like a coin he couldn't spend.

"At the ford shrines. The one under the waterfall, and the one in the mine-shaft, and the one—the little one, the clay one, outside Lowmark." She counted them on her fingers and hated how easily the fingers moved. "Three times we were trapped. Three times I prayed. And three times the mountain opened a road that shouldn't exist—a bridge where the rope-bridge had rotted, a pass cleared of snowfall overnight, a fog that lifted exactly when we needed it gone. I thought I was clever. I thought I was being *practical.*" She laughed, and the laugh was the worst sound she'd made yet. "Do you know what they took? Do you know how it works?"

Brannic's voice came from the dark, quiet and precise: "You pay in memory. The oldest ones first, usually. Whatever's closest to the fire." He didn't get up. He spoke to the ceiling, or to no one. "I told you. I told you both, the night I joined you. Miracles aren't gifts. They're purchases. Something down there is hungry, and prayer is how it eats."

"You said memories," Maren said. "You said it like losing a birthday. Like forgetting a name at court."

"I said it like a debt," Brannic said. "Debts are always smaller until they're yours."

Silence. The fire ticked. Somewhere up-slope a bird made a sound it had no business making at night, and all three of them ignored it together, a small conspiracy of denial.

"My daughter," Maren said, very evenly, because evenness was the only thing holding her upright. "I paid for safe passage with my daughter's face. And I would do it again. That's the part that—" Her hands shook around the tin cup. "That's the part I can't forgive. Not the gods. Me. Put me at that shrine tomorrow with Ilse dying in front of me and I'd trade every face I own."

"Yes," Ulyana said.

She looked at him sharply. She had expected comfort. Some platitude about how no mother could be blamed, how any woman would have done the same. What she got instead was a gaunt man watching the fire with the expression of someone reading a ledger he already knows is balanced.

"Yes?" she repeated.

"You'd do it again. Anyone would. That's why it works." He turned the cup in his hands. "That's the whole design of it, Your Majesty. They don't take things you don't value. They wait until you're drowning and they only charge you for what you love. Nobody prays harder than a desperate person, and nobody remembers sweeter than a mother, so that's where they feed." He paused. "I'm not saying this well."

"No," Maren said. "You're saying it terribly."

"Good." Something almost like relief crossed his face. "Then you'll believe me. Everyone lies beautifully to me these days. Pilgrims, priests, queens. You say terrible things honestly, at least."

And because he had said it terribly and therefore truly, because he had not wrapped the truth in wool or handed her a single easy forgiveness, Maren found that the scream inside her settled into something colder and more useful. Grief is a flood. Fury is a river, and rivers can be aimed.

* * *

She slept, or lay still pretending to, and in the grey hour before dawn she got up quietly and went outside, because there was a thing she needed to do and she needed to do it alone.

The frost had come down hard. Every stone wore a rime of white, and her breath hung in front of her like a small ghost refusing to move on. She walked twenty paces from the bothy, knelt in the snow, and folded her hands.

She was not going to pray. She wanted that understood, by whatever was listening. She pressed her palms flat against the frozen ground instead, the way her grandmother used to press a hand to a fevered child's forehead—to feel how deep the sickness ran.

"Can you hear me?" she whispered. "Good. Then hear this."

The mountain said nothing. The wind moved through the pines below, indifferent, ordinary. But she had spent eleven years in a court full of men who listened without answering, and she knew the texture of attention when it settled on her. It was faint. It was interested. It was patient as a debt.

"Ilse," she said. "Her name is Ilse. She's four years old. She's hiding somewhere in the capital with a woman named Sera Ostrow, who was her nurse, who is loyal to me and terrified of you, and she thinks her queen abandoned her." The words came steadily. She had rehearsed them against the dark of her eyelids all night. "You took her face out of my head. Fine. Keep it. I don't need her face to find her. I need her alive, and I will get her alive, and here is what you should carry back to whatever wears my husband's crown—"

She leaned closer to the ground, close enough that her lips nearly touched the frost.

"—I am coming to end the bargain. Not renegotiate it. End it. Whatever fire you're feeding on down there, I'm going to watch it gutter out, and when you unravel, I want you to unravel remembering that a mother whose face you stole is the one who closed your mouth. Take that as payment. Consider us even when you're dead."

The attention lifted. Or she stopped feeling it, which amounted to the same thing. Either way the mountain went back to being a mountain, and Maren stayed kneeling in the snow a moment longer, shaking, emptied, strangely clean—like after vomiting, like after confession, like after anything the body does to survive its own poison.

When she turned around, Ulyana was standing by the bothy door. He had a blanket over one arm and an expression of profound discomfort, the look of a man who has accidentally witnessed something sacred and would like very much to be excused.

"How much did you hear?" she asked.

"Enough to know you'll want this." He held out the blanket. "The ground takes heat out of you faster than the air does. People kneel in frost and freeze their knees and then wonder why they can't walk the next day. Can't have that."

She took the blanket. Wrapped it around her shoulders. Stood there looking at him, this gatekeeper who had spent twenty years perfecting the art of being nobody, and felt something crack open in her chest that wasn't grief, wasn't fury—was maybe the first honest warmth she'd allowed herself since the flight from the capital began.

"Why do you do that?" she asked.

"Do what."

"Comfort people like a man describing weather to someone standing in a storm. 'The ground takes heat faster than the air.' My daughter's face is gone, Silas. Say something useless and kind for once. Lie to me. Everyone lies to grieving women; it's practically law."

He considered this with the gravity other men gave to matters of state. Snow gathered on his shoulders. When he finally spoke, it was slow, each word selected like a smuggler picks a path—one tested foothold at a time.

"Ilse," he said. "Four years old. Afraid of geese." A pause. "Born in the spring."

"That's right," Maren whispered.

"I don't know what she looks like either. But now I know those three things, and I'll keep them. However this ends—however bad the roads get, however much they take from either of us—I'll keep them. So you won't be the only one carrying her." His jaw worked, embarrassed. "It's not comfort. It's redundancy."

Maren started laughing. It hurt, and it helped, and it scared the birds out of the pines below, and she didn't stop it. She laughed until she had to lean on him, and he endured it the way he endured everything—stoically, awkwardly, present.

* * *

They broke camp at first light. Brannic emerged from the bothy rubbing his eyes, saw their faces, and wisely asked for tea instead of explanations.

"We go faster today," Maren said, kicking snow over the fire. "No more shrines. No more shortcuts. If the mountain offers us a miracle, we walk around it, even if walking around costs a week. I've paid enough. We all have." She looked at Ulyana. "How long to the capital on honest roads?"

"Honest roads, winter, avoiding Ossen's checkpoints?" He chewed on it. "Nine days. Ten if the passes argue."

"And the dishonest way?"

"Three days. Through the Undercroft mines, past the choir." His eyes flicked to hers, and she saw he understood exactly what he was offering and what it would cost her to walk that road—the congregation of the voice-taken, singing the mountain awake, the place where faith had teeth. "Your call, Majesty."

Maren pulled the empty locket from her neck. She looked at the blank velvet oval where her daughter's face used to live, and she closed it, and she pressed the stubborn catch twice.

"The dishonest way," she said.

Brannic groaned. "Of course it's the dishonest way. It's always the dishonest way. Why do I travel with monarchs?"

But when they set out, descending toward the black mouths of the old mine-shrines, it was Brannic who walked first, head tilted, listening for prayers the way other men listen for wolves. And it was Ulyana who paused at the treeline and looked back up the slope—at the bothy, at the frost, at something in the pattern of the snow.

"Majesty," he said slowly. "Did you carve anything into the ground last night? Mark the snow? Anything like that?"

"I knelt. That's all. Why?"

He pointed. Where she had knelt, the frost had melted in a ring, neat as a coin. And leading away from the ring, down the mountainside toward the mines, the snow itself had softened along one line—a path, thin as a scar, pointing the way she had sworn to go.

Not toward the honest roads. Toward the deep ones.

"It heard you," Ulyana said quietly. "And I think it just agreed with you. That's what frightens me. Things that agree with you are things that want something."

Maren looked down the melted path, straight into the dark of the Undercroft, and felt no fear at all—which frightened her more.

"Then let's find out what it wants," she said, and led them down.
