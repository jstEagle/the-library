## 12. Roads That Remember

The Kestrel Pass closed on the third morning after Lowmark.

Ulyana knew it before he saw it. He woke with his teeth aching, the way he always did when the mountain shifted its weight, and by the time the three of them reached the pass mouth there was nothing left to argue about. The defile that had carried smugglers, exiles, and one very memorable shipment of illicit candles for thirty years was gone. Not blocked. *Gone.* The rock walls had leaned together like old men sharing a secret, and where the road had run there was now forty feet of unbroken stone, smooth as poured wax, still faintly warm under his palm when he pressed it.

"Stone doesn't do that," Brannic said.

"Stone does whatever it's told," Ulyana said, "if you know who to ask." He said it flat and certain, because he was the one who was supposed to know, and because Maren was watching him the way she watched everything now—hungrily, like a woman relearning what hunger was. "There are eleven ways over the Thornfells from here to the capital. We'll take the Widow's Stair."

He had named eleven roads in his head the night before, lying awake, and had already crossed off four of them. He didn't mention that part.

The Widow's Stair was two days south: a goat track that switchbacked up a cliff face no honest army would climb and no dishonest one had ever needed to. It was ugly, exposed, and it worked. That was the whole religion of the smuggler roads—ugly, exposed, working—and Ulyana had spent twenty years being their priest.

They reached the base of the cliff at dusk and found the stair full of snow.

Not snowed upon. *Full.* Packed into every switchback like mortar between bricks, ten feet of it, blue-white and dense as iron, though the sky above them was clear and had been clear for a week and the temperature hadn't dropped below freezing since they'd left Lowmark. Ulyana climbed the first thirty feet anyway, drove his axe in twice, and came back down with frostbite blooming on his knuckles and nothing else.

"The mountain's rerouting us," Maren said.

It wasn't a question. She'd been saying versions of it for two days, ever since the stream they'd camped beside had reversed direction overnight, running uphill in polite defiance of everything Ulyana understood about water. He had told her then that streams did strange things near old shrines, which was even true, once.

"Yes," he said now, because lying to her had started to cost more than it paid.

"To the capital."

"To the spire," he corrected. "The capital just happens to be sitting on top of it. There's a difference. Roads want destinations, not cities."

"And what does this road want?"

Ulyana looked at the packed snow, at the warm stone behind it, at the whole patient geometry of a mountain rearranging itself around three small travelers. He thought about the shrine wall in the pass below Lowmark, where someone—or something—had scratched his name between two prayers, with his face rendered above it in lines so fine they might have been done with a needle. *Answered,* the pilgrim mark beneath it had read. His name. Answered. He had spent twenty years making himself a man nobody prayed to, nobody remembered, nobody *wanted*, and something in the deep places of the world had put him on a list.

"It wants us walking," he said. "So let's disappoint it. There's a mine-road through the Graywackes—the Hollow Way. No shrines on it. Nothing holy within fifty miles. If the mountain wants to herd us, we go where there's no flock."

Brannic made a sound that was half laugh, half prayer, which was how most of his sentences came out. "You're proposing we out-stubborn a god using geography."

"I'm proposing," Ulyana said, shouldering his pack, "that I've been bribing, cheating, and bullshitting this mountain for twenty years, and I'm not going to let it win on my watch."

* * *

The Hollow Way had been cut by miners four generations dead, and it showed. Timber props furred with white fungus. Rails rusted to red lace. Water somewhere in the dark, keeping its own counsel. Ulyana liked it immediately, the way he liked all places too poor and too bitter to attract worship. There were no niches cut for offerings, no candle-scorch on the walls, none of the little black mouths of roadside altars that pocked every other tunnel in the range. Just work. Dead men's work, holding up the sky's underside.

For a day and a half it held. They walked. Brannic complained with genuine artistry. Maren learned to sleep standing up against a prop, a court skill she claimed she'd learned from ambassadors, delivered so dryly that Ulyana almost laughed for the first time since the shrine wall.

Then, on the second night, the map changed.

He'd spread it across his knees at their fire—it wasn't much of a fire, just enough heat to keep their hands working—a sheet of cured hide sixty years old, inked by a smuggler named Vetch who had died the way most mapmakers don't: peacefully, rich, and full of wine. Ulyana had inherited it along with the network, and he had amended it faithfully, in his own hand, every season. Every pass, every cache, every safehouse, every grave that mattered. It was the closest thing he had to a diary, and he had never once written anything true on it.

He noticed the ink moving the way you notice a spider on your arm—not seeing it first, feeling it.

A line was crawling. One of Vetch's fine old contour marks, marking a ridge that had stood for six decades, was dragging itself across the hide like an earthworm on a hot flagstone, and as it moved the paper remembered other shapes. A pass Ulyana knew intimately drew shut, the ink pooling into a solid black bar. A valley he'd never used opened, fresh lines branching off the main route like veins surfacing under skin. And every new line, every single one, bent the same direction—south and east, south and east, converging toward the little symbol Vetch had drawn at the center of the range without ever labeling it. A tower. A line through it. *Do not knock,* Vetch had written underneath, and Ulyana had always assumed it was a joke.

"Maren," he said quietly. "Come look at this."

She looked. Brannic looked. The ink kept moving while they watched, unhurried, the way water is unhurried, the way roots are unhurried. The hide was bleeding its old truths out and writing new ones, and the new ones were not suggestions.

"That's your hand amending the margins," Brannic said. "Is that also yours?" He pointed at the newest line, still wet, still glistening, snaking down toward the spire-mark.

"No."

"Then who—"

"Doesn't matter." Ulyana rolled the map up before any of them could read anything else into it, which was a lie of a different kind, because all three of them had already seen the shape the roads were making. A funnel. A throat. Everything flowing one direction, the way pilgrims flowed, the way prayers flowed, the way the answered ones had walked in their neat choruses toward the capital with their eyes full of borrowed light.

The mountain wasn't closing doors at random. It was building a corridor.

* * *

They came up out of the Hollow Way at dawn into a country Ulyana did not recognize, and recognizing country was the one thing he had never failed at.

The Graywacke foothills were supposed to fall away westward into moorland, boggy, treeless, useless—the kind of ground you crossed fast and cursed slowly. Instead they stood on a ridgeline looking down into a forested vale that went on for miles, pine and birch, a river glinting through it, and at the far end, half-hidden in haze, the pale vertical scar of a road cut climbing toward the high passes.

"There's no vale here," Ulyana said.

"You're sure?"

"I set a snare line through this ground when I was nineteen. There's no vale here." He crouched, pulled a handful of soil, crumbled it. Rich, black, wrong. The fungus smell of the mineshaft clung to it. "This grew overnight. Or it grew somewhere else and got moved."

"Roads that remember," Brannic murmured. He'd taken to muttering scripture again, the defrocked habit of reaching for authority he no longer had. "The liturgy has a verse about it. The old gods' creation hymn—the one nobody sings anymore. *The world is the god's memory, and where the memory walks, the world follows.*"

"I thought you were defrocked."

"I'm defrocked, not lobotomized. The point is—" Brannic turned his palms up, helpless, which was unlike him. "If something is rebuilding the old gods out of stolen memories, then it isn't just eating lives. It's spending what it eats. Every miracle costs a petitioner a memory, yes—but the coin gets *spent*. Spent on this." He gestured at the impossible vale, the river that had no source, the road climbing toward the spire like a rope thrown down a well. "It's paving the way to its own resurrection with other people's pasts. Somewhere out there, thousands of people can't remember their mothers, and their mothers are *this*. Ground. Gravel. A convenient roadbed."

Maren had gone very still. Since the waystation—since Brannic had told them what the answered prayers cost, and she had gone quiet in a way that meant she was checking her own shelves—she had a new habit of touching her temple when she thought no one saw, like a woman confirming a bruise. Ulyana saw. He didn't say anything. There was nothing he could say that wouldn't be either cruel or useless, and he'd had twenty years of practice knowing the difference.

"We go around," he said instead. "West, off the ridgeline, cross-country. It'll cost us four days."

"The mountain will cost us more if we fight it."

"Then it'll cost me more gladly than it costs me obediently." He was already repacking the map, and stopped, because his fingers had found something on the hide that hadn't been there when he rolled it up. Fresh ink, on the outside of the roll. Small, precise, in a hand he almost recognized—fine-lined, needle-fine, like the face on the shrine wall.

One word, over the western route he'd chosen:

*Colder.*

* * *

They went west anyway. Ulyana was many kinds of stubborn but he drew the line at taking directions from cartographic graffiti, even when—as became obvious by noon—even when the warning was correct. The moorland beyond the false vale was cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather. Frost formed ahead of them, always exactly ahead, retreating down their path like a tide pulling back from walkers it disapproved of. By afternoon their breath hung in the air long after they'd exhaled, hanging and drifting backward, trailing behind them like the wake of boats.

Maren stumbled once on the broken ground and Ulyana caught her elbow, and she said, "You're limping."

"I'm favoring my left knee. There's a difference."

"When did you hurt it?"

"A long time ago."

She looked at him with that new attention of hers, the attention of a woman who had learned that people were made of things that could be taken away. "You limp on cold days," she said. "You've limped on cold days since I met you at the snare line. You hold your right hand differently when you think no one's watching, like it remembers holding something heavy. You hum when you're nervous—four notes, always the same four notes, and you don't know you're doing it." She paused. "I've lost my daughter's face, Ulyana. I know exactly what it means to carry someone in your hands instead of your head. So don't tell me there's a difference."

The wind moved the moor grass. Somewhere behind them, very far away, the mountain groaned—a deep structural complaint, stone settling onto stone, the sound of a house changing its mind.

"What are you asking me?" Ulyana said.

"Whether it's started taking things from you yet. Whether the mountain knows your name the way the shrine wall did. Whether every mile it herds us is costing you something you won't miss until it's gone." Her voice stayed level. Queens' voices did that; he'd forgotten that about her, or perhaps he'd never known it, or perhaps—he filed the thought away where he filed all thoughts about the years before Thornfell—perhaps he'd known it very well once. "Because I watched the pilgrims at the waystation give themselves away piece by piece and smile while they did it, and I watched you calculate our route like a man counting coins he can't afford to spend. So. Has it started?"

"No," he said.

And then, because she deserved better than the reflex, because two liars trusting each other only worked if the lies got thinner as they went: "I don't think so. I checked. Last night I recited the network—all the names, all the caches, all the debts owed and owing. Forty-one names. They're all still there. My mother's face, my—people. They're all there." He flexed his right hand, aware he was doing it, hating that she'd seen it. "Whatever it's spending on these roads, it isn't spending me. Yet."

"Why not?"

"Because I'm the gatekeeper," Ulyana said, and heard how it sounded, the old title coming out of him like a key turning. "Twenty years I've kept the spire-gate shut. Whatever's waking up down there needs a gatekeeper who forgets why he's holding the door. I'm not useful to it with my memory intact. So it herds me and spends others and leaves me whole—" he looked south, where the pale thread of that impossible road climbed toward the passes, toward the capital, toward everything, "—until it doesn't need me whole anymore."

"Or," Brannic offered from behind them, cheerfully doomed, "until it gets you exactly where it wants you, and then the bill arrives."

"Thank you, Brannic."

"I'm a priest. Comforting the afflicted and afflicting the comfortable. You looked comfortable."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a tor that Ulyana's map insisted did not exist, and he had begun to accept that the map was now a historical document rather than a tool. He laid it out one last time anyway, an act of respect for the dead—Vetch, and the version of the mountains Vetch had believed in—and that was when he saw what the ink had become.

Every line he'd ever drawn was gone. Forty years of smuggler knowledge, burned away, the hide washed clean as an egg. In its place, in that needle-fine hand, was a single route: from the tor where they sat, south and east, through passes he had never known and could now see drawn in perfect detail, switchbacks and water sources and campsites marked, generous, hospitable, *considerate*—a road built for them specifically, the way you build a chute for livestock.

And at the top of the page, where Vetch's spire-mark had been, the ink had finished its work. The tower. The line through it. But the words beneath had changed. *Do not knock* was gone.

In its place, small and patient and unmistakably addressed:

*KNOCK.*

Ulyana sat with the map across his knees for a long time while the fire burned down and his companions slept, and he thought about gates. About the fact that every gate in the world had two sides, and that he had spent twenty years guarding one side of one gate and calling it a life. About the letter-shaped absence in his memory where the years before Thornfell should have been—smooth, painless, suspiciously well-healed, like a wound he'd been told was a birthmark.

The four notes escaped him before he could stop them. He was humming. He always hummed when he was nervous. Four notes, the same four, and tonight, alone in a country that had grown overnight, he finally heard them clearly enough to realize they weren't nerves.

They were the opening phrase of the old creation hymn. The one nobody sang anymore.

*Where the memory walks, the world follows.*

South of them, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of the world, something began—very softly, very sweetly—to sing back.
