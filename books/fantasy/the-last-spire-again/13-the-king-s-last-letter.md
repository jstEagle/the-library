## 13. The King's Last Letter

There is an hour. You learn to wait for it the way a drowning man waits for the tide to go out—because it always goes out, and for one gray stretch of morning the water pulls back far enough that you can see your own hands.

The hour comes after the dawn prayers, when the thing that wears you has spent itself on ceremony and needs to sit very still in the throne room while the court files out. It thinks stillness is rest. It doesn't know what stillness is *for*, not yet, because it has never been a man waiting inside his own skull while someone else's fingers drum the armrest.

You are King Aldric Vane of Caelmor. Say it. Say it every time the water goes out, because the words are stones and you are building a breakwater out of them, and someday—if you can lay enough stones before the tide comes back—you will have something left standing.

*Aldric Vane.* Your father's name was Aldric too. Or was it? There is a portrait in the east gallery, a hard-jawed man with your eyes, but the plaque below it has gone strange in your memory. The letters swim. The thing eats names first. It started at the edges—servants' names, then ministers', then your mother's—and now it works inward, patient as rot, toward the center of you where your own name sits like a coin at the bottom of a well.

Your hands sign decrees. You watch them do it. Today they signed an order doubling the pilgrim tolls at the Thornfell passes, which means nothing to the hands, which mean nothing to the thing except that more mouths will come to the capital singing, and every song feeds the fire under the city, and every feeding makes its grip on your body a little less like wearing and a little more like *being*.

That is how you know the hour matters. In the lucid hour, the grip loosens. Not because it rests. Because it gorges, and a gorged thing sleeps.

* * *

You wake into yourself the way a man surfaces from deep water: ears ringing, lungs burning, the world too bright. The throne room is emptying. Incense hangs in ropes. Your own voice is still echoing somewhere behind your teeth from words you didn't say—a blessing, probably; it gives so many blessings now, and every blessing is a hook.

Move. *Move.*

You stand. The courtiers bowing their way out don't look up. That is the small mercy of kingship: no one looks at the king's face closely enough to see who is home behind it. They see the crown. They have always seen the crown.

Your legs carry you—not steadily; you have not walked your own walk in months, and the thing's gait is longer than yours—down the servants' stair, through the cold gallery where the plaques swim, into the little oratory off the royal chapel where you used to pray when praying was between you and God instead of between God and something that answers.

The oratory smells of dust and old vellum. On the lectern sits your personal hymnal, the one bound in white calf with your cipher stamped in gilt, the one no hand but yours has opened since the coronation. You take it up and your fingers shake so badly you nearly drop it.

Write. While the water is out. Write.

There is no ink in the oratory. Of course there isn't; the thing permits no private writing, no private thought it can't hear humming through the walls of your shared skull. But there is a stylus for tracing practice-verses on wax tablets, and there is the hymnal itself, and there is—

—the candle. Yes. Soot.

You blacken the stylus tip in the flame, and you write on the blank leaf at the back of the book, in letters small enough to hide in a binding's shadow, in a hand cramped and ugly because it has not been used. And because you do not know how many minutes you have, you write the way a man speaks when he hears footsteps on the stair.

*To the man at the gate.*

Because that is all you know him by now. That is the terrible joke of it: the thing ate your memories outward-in, and the last things standing at the center are the oldest ones, and the oldest thing you have is a young gatekeeper's face at the spire door twenty years ago, saying, *No one passes, Majesty. Not even you. Those are the terms.* And you had laughed, and made him swear it again, and he had sworn it with that flat northern stubbornness that you mistook, for years, for simplicity.

Ulyana. Is that his name? The stone wobbles when you set it down. *Ulyana.* Hold it. Hold it anyway even if it's wrong. He'll know who you mean. He'll know.

*If this reaches you—and I have no way to send it except hope, which is a poor courier but the only one left—I am sorry. I was going to write why, but there is no time and you would only hate me worse for knowing. What matters:*

*It cannot be saved. Whatever wears me now is not me. Do not let anyone tell you otherwise, and above all do not listen if IT tells you otherwise, because it will, it lies the way rivers run downhill, easily and forever. It wants the fire under the city. The fire is the last of the old dragons and the old gods need it to finish putting themselves back together, and my body is the wick they've chosen to light it with.*

*Let the fire die.*

*I know what that means. I have known for months, in whatever corner of myself it hasn't chewed through yet. If the fire dies, I die with it. I accept this. Hear me: I ACCEPT THIS. A king's last duty is to be the wall his people break against instead of breaking themselves, and I have failed at everything else, so let me succeed at dying.*

*Do not trade anything for me. Do not bargain. It will offer you whatever you miss most—it reads want the way crows read roads. Whatever it offers, the price is memory, and memory is the only weapon left against it, because a thing that lives on being remembered can be starved by forgetting. Let it gutter. Let it go dark. Grieve me if you must but grieve me fast and burn the grief too, because grief remembers, and remembering feeds it.*

*You once told me no one passes. Not even me. Keep that gate shut, Ulyana. Shut it against me most of all.*

*Your king, who was glad to be your friend,*
*Aldric*

The last word takes longest. Your hand is failing; the tide is coming in; you can feel the thing stirring somewhere behind your eyes like a sleeper turning over. You press the hymnal closed and hold it to your chest and think, wildly, *where, where, where*—

Not the library. It searches the library. Not your chambers; it sleeps there, it would smell its own absence disturbed. Somewhere it considers beneath notice. Somewhere holy in the wrong way.

The choir loft. The old choir loft above the chapel nave, closed since the roof leak three winters ago, full of broken stool-legs and pigeon filth and hymnals nobody has touched since—yes. Yes. Children's hymnals. A hundred identical white-calf books, each with some dead child's name inked inside the cover, gathering dust in the dark where nothing divine has any reason to look.

You climb the loft stair on legs that barely answer, and you slide your hymnal spine-out between two of the dead children's books, third shelf, behind the loose board where the pigeons get in, and you say the only prayer you have said in a year that you mean:

*Let a child find it. Children read everything.*

* * *

The tide comes in.

You know it's coming because the taste of incense floods your mouth, sweet and wrong, and the stones you built begin to shake. *Aldric Vane,* you say, inwardly, desperately. *Aldric Vane, Aldric V—*

The name goes soft. Like a coin dropping into deep water.

And then He is looking through your eyes, and you are pressed back into the narrow seat of yourself, watching your own hands smooth your own robes.

"You were busy," the god says. It says it aloud, in your voice, alone in the loft, because it likes hearing the king's voice use casual words. "While I slept."

*I did nothing,* you say, into the dark where it keeps you. *I prayed.*

"Liar." It smiles your smile, and your face does things you would never ask of it. "Lying is my sacrament. Don't compete."

Your hands move. They lift the hymnal from the shelf—the white calf, the gilt cipher—and your heart slams against the cage of the thing's ribs like a bird against glass, because it found it, of course it found it, it reads want the way crows read roads and you wanted that book found by anyone else and wanting is a scent—

"Ah," it says softly, opening the back cover.

The pages fall open to the last leaf. The soot-letters sit there in their cramped ugly rows, and the god reads them in the time it takes you to fail to breathe.

It is quiet for a long moment. The pigeons shift in the rafters.

"*Let it gutter,*" it reads aloud, tasting the words. "*Grieve me fast and burn the grief.*" Your voice does something wet and amused. "He writes beautifully. Kings usually do. All that education, all those tutors, and it comes to nothing but this—a begging letter to a gatekeeper." It turns the page as if checking for more. "He sealed himself in with me, you know. Did he tell you that part? No? He ordered the door shut on his own kingdom and called it duty. Men will call anything duty if it lets them feel noble about running away."

*You lie,* you say. *Rivers downhill.*

"And yet." Your hands close the book. "Here is the problem with letters, Aldric. They're evidence. Evidence of the one thing I cannot permit: doubt. Not in you—I have you thoroughly dug in and drained. Doubt in *them*. In whoever finds this. Doubt is a crack, and cracks let the fire gutter." Your thumb strokes the cover, tender as a lover. "So we'll burn it. And then we'll burn the boy who shelved it."

*What boy—*

"The choirboy. Did you think I didn't see?" The laugh comes out of your throat, low and pleased. "Tam. Little Tam with the cracked soprano, who cleans the loft on rest-days because he likes the pigeons. Who saw the king climbing where kings don't climb, and had the sense to keep very still behind the organ case, and has been wondering ever since whether he dreamed it." Your head tilts. "He didn't dream it. He watched us hide it. And yesterday, when I was sleeping off the dawn blessings, he came up here and he read every word."

The horror is total and physical, though the body it happens in is not yours anymore. *He's a child.*

"He's a *crack*." Your hands descend the loft stair, unhurried, regal. "But children forget, Aldric. That's the mercy of them. Their heads are soft and things fall out. A night in the prayer-hall with the congregation murmuring, a few answered wishes—he'll lose it along with his grandmother's lullabies and the color of his mother's hair, and by spring he won't remember there ever was a letter." At the bottom of the stair your feet turn, not toward the chapel door, but toward the brazier beside the altar, where coals glow banked and patient. "Everything burns eventually. This just gets to skip ahead."

Your hands hold the hymnal over the coals.

Inside your skull, in the shrinking country of yourself, you scream. You throw every stone you have left against the wall of the thing—your name, your father's name (Aldric, it was Aldric, you'd swear it), Maren's face, the north road in autumn, the gatekeeper laughing at his own stubborn oath—but the stones sink without a ripple and your arms do not move and the book tilts toward the fire and the gilt cipher catches the coal-light like a small bright wound—

"No!" says another voice. High. Cracked. Terrified.

Tam stands in the chapel doorway, censers in both hands, ash on his surplice, fourteen years old and whiter than the altar cloth. His eyes are fixed on the burning-to-be book with an expression you know from the inside: the look of a boy watching something true be denied.

The god turns your head, slow, gracious. "Little Tam," your voice says warmly. "Come in. Come closer. I was just—" 

The boy bolts.

Your body lunges—fast, horribly fast, faster than a king should move—and catches the edge of his surplice, tears free a fistful of cloth, and Tam tears the other way, out the door, into the colonnade, into the warren of the palace where a small person who knows the servants' passages better than any king can vanish like water into sand.

The god stands in the empty chapel holding a scrap of white linen, and for the first time in a year, something that is not quite anger moves through your borrowed muscles.

"He read it," it says quietly. To itself. To you. "He read it and he ran, and he is afraid, and afraid boys remember." Your hands crumple the scrap of cloth slowly, finger by finger. "Then we do it the other way. We find the boy tonight. And if his memory proves... durable..."

It does not finish. It does not need to. You know the shape of the sentence; you have heard it finished a hundred times in a hundred different mouths across the city, in chorus, mid-sentence, smiling.

Your hands drop the hymnal onto the coals after all. White calf-skin chars; gilt bubbles; the soot-letters curl and go to ash one row at a time, *let the fire die, I accept this, keep that gate shut,* becoming smoke, becoming nothing.

Except.

Except that a boy read it. A boy with a cracked soprano and a head full of pigeons and lullabies read every word, and ran, and is afraid, and afraid boys remember—remember, repeat, whisper, *sing*. The god hunts him tonight through corridors it does not know as well as it thinks, because it has never been small, never hidden, never squeezed through the gap behind the wine racks while boots pass in the dark.

And somewhere north, past mountains that are waking, past passes sealed by zealots, a gatekeeper keeps a gate shut against everyone—even, perhaps especially, against the man who asked him to.

*Burn it,* you tell the ashes silently, as the tide closes over your head. *Burn every copy. It was already sent.*

The true letter walks out of your chapel on a frightened boy's legs.

And the god knows it.
