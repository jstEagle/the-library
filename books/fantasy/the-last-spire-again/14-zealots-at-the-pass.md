## 14. Zealots at the Pass

The first sign that the passes were closing was the smell of pitch.

Ulyana smelled it from the ridge above Coldwether Saddle, half a day before he saw the fires—pitch and wet wool and the sour tang of a crowd that had been marching too long on too little faith. He had spent twenty years reading mountains by their smells. Snowmelt meant the high road was passable. Pine resin meant shelter within an hour. And pitch meant someone was building something they intended to burn.

He crouched behind a shelf of granite and looked down at the saddle.

They had come in the night. White-robed figures moved among the boulders with the efficiency of soldiers, stacking brush against the two narrow defiles that funneled traffic through Coldwether—the smuggler's way and the honest one alike. Torches guttered at intervals along the rock face. And at the center of it all, standing on a flat stone like a man addressing a congregation instead of a checkpoint, was a tall figure in grey, arms open to the dawn.

Even at this distance, Ulyana knew him. Everyone in Thornfell knew him now. Prelate Ossen, whose voice carried without shouting, whose smile made you feel forgiven before you'd confessed anything.

"Brothers! Sisters!" The wind did Ossen's work for him, carrying the words up the slope in pieces. "The old roads are roads of confusion. The mountain has many mouths, and lies come out of all of them. But there is one road to the Spire, and one voice upon it, and we will walk it together or not at all!"

Below, scattered through the rocks, several hundred pilgrims murmured their assent. Ulyana counted watchfires, counted guards, counted the brushpiles stacked against each defile. Six men at the western mouth. Eight at the eastern. Archers on the two overlooks. And between them, the whole of Coldwether Saddle sealed like a jar.

His map lived in his head—every goat track, every drainage culvert, every seam in the stone that a body could squeeze through if the body didn't mind bleeding a little. There were eleven ways through Coldwether that no honest cartographer had ever recorded. By morning, he guessed, Ossen's people would know about ten of them.

*They've been watching my roads.* The thought settled in his chest like swallowed river-stone. *Not following pilgrims. Watching the ground.*

Maren crouched beside him, breath fogging. Three weeks on the mountain had taught her to move quietly; it had not yet taught her patience. She opened her mouth, and he put two fingers gently against her sleeve before she could speak.

"Not here," he breathed. "Sound carries off the granite."

She nodded, and they backed away down the scree on their heels, the way he'd taught her, slow as dripping water, until the ridge stood between them and the prelate's congregation.

Only then did she speak, low and furious. "He's sealing the passes."

"He's herding." Ulyana kept moving, angling north through the pines. "A shepherd doesn't block every path. He blocks every path but one. Every pilgrim in Thornfell is about to be funneled down the King's Road to the capital, in plain view, in good order, singing."

"And us?"

"We were never going down the King's Road."

"No," she said. "But your safehouse at Harrow Fen is. Or near enough."

He stopped walking.

She watched him with those level eyes that missed nothing, the eyes of a woman who had spent years reading courtiers' faces and had learned exactly how much weight a small silence could carry.

"Harrow Fen," she said again. "The drying shed above the tarn. Your network's western anchor. If the King's Road runs through the valley—and it does—then anyone who wants to slip past Ossen's net has to cross Harrow Fen land. Including us."

"You've been studying my maps."

"I've been studying *you*." She said it without apology. "You have rules, Ulyana. I've watched you keep them. Never burn a contact. Never spend a safehouse. Never take a road you can't walk back. I'm asking which of them breaks first."

He started walking again, faster than he needed to, because if he stood still any longer she would see the answer arrive in his face.

*All of them,* he thought. *One at a time, starting soon.*

* * *

Harrow Fen was a shallow bowl of black water ringed with reeds, and above it, on stilts over the marsh, stood the drying shed where his people had smoked fish, cured hides, cached goods, and occasionally hidden fugitives for longer than Ulyana had been alive. It belonged to Old Wren, who had inherited it from her mother, who had held it for Ulyana's predecessor, who had held it for no one, because before Ulyana there had only ever been the mountain and the people clever enough to use it.

Wren met them at the boardwalk with a crossbow that she lowered the moment she recognized his stride.

"You brought the queen," she said. Not a question. News traveled on the smuggler roads faster than light traveled anywhere else.

"I brought a traveler," Ulyana said.

"Mm." Wren spat over the rail into the reeds. "And I keep a pet duck. Come inside before the fen sees you."

Inside, the shed smelled of peat smoke and dried lingonberries, and it was full—fuller than it should have been. A dozen faces turned toward them from the shadows: runners, guides, two families with children asleep against their packs. Pilgrims who had chosen the wrong road and been pulled out of Ossen's current by Wren's people, one body at a time.

"Thirty-one since the prelate sealed the saddle," Wren said, following his eyes. "We're eating through winter stores. And it gets worse." She led him to the far wall, where a strip of birchbark hung covered in charcoal marks—a tally, and beneath the tally, a crude sketch of the valley. "They posted riders on the King's Road three days ago. Not pilgrims. Riders. In grey. They stop every party, they ask names, and they carry this." She unrolled a scrap of vellum.

On it, rendered in careful ink, was Ulyana's face.

It was a good likeness. Too good. The heavy brows, the crooked nose broken in some year he couldn't quite summon anymore, the scar through the left eyebrow. Beneath it, in a clerk's neat hand: *THE GATEKEEPER OF THORNFELL. SEEN HIM? SPEAK, AND BE BLESSED.*

"They're calling him the False Keeper," Wren said. "Says he locked up the gods' house and threw away the key. Says every miracle since is the gods knocking to be let out." She rolled the vellum shut with something between disgust and grudging respect. "Whoever writes his sermons understands the roads, Ulyana. They haven't just closed the passes. They've closed the *story*. Now every soul on this mountain believes the fastest way to salvation runs through the capital, and anyone who says otherwise is working for you."

Maren stood by the door, very still. "Ossen knows what Ulyana looks like," she said slowly. "But how does he know the gatekeeper matters? That story died twenty years ago. I was there when it died."

"The choirboy," Ulyana said quietly.

They had collected Tam at Lowmark, the boy who carried the king's letter in his skull, word-perfect, too frightened to stay in the capital and too marked to stay anywhere else. Word had spread from Lowmark outward, as everything did. Somewhere in the spreading, the letter had become rumor, the rumor had become scripture, and scripture had put a price on the head of the man at the gate.

"He's not hunting you to kill you," Maren said. "Think. If Ossen wanted you dead, he wouldn't need a likeness. He needs you *found*. You're the last key the story needs. The keeper who shut the gate can be the one to open it—publicly, at the spire, before ten thousand witnesses. The god doesn't just want fire and a king. It wants a *ceremony*."

Wren looked between them. "Somebody want to tell me what's actually in that spire?"

"Later," Ulyana said. "Maybe never." He turned to the birchbark map. "How many of Ossen's riders between here and the valley fork?"

"Four patrols. Two on the road, two walking the shore. And they have dogs now." Wren's jaw tightened. "They bought the hounds off the tannery at Millbrook. Bought them with blessings, which tells you what blessings are worth, but dogs don't care about theology."

Ulyana studied the map for a long time. The others waited. Outside, the fen made its small wet noises, patient as ever.

Every route he knew crossed Harrow Fen land. Every route required the shed—its caches, its boats, its blind channels through the reeds that only Wren's family poling for four generations could navigate in the dark. He could take Maren and Brannic and Tam around the north rim, over bare fell, exposed to sky and rider and dog for two full days. Maybe one in three of them arrived.

Or—

"There's another option," Wren said, watching his face. She always had known how his thoughts moved; she'd been reading his silences since he was young and stupid. "The shed burns clean, Ulyana. Peat smoke, reed thatch. By morning there'd be nothing but a stain and a story: zealots burned a smuggler den, righteous wrath, terrible tragedy, everyone dead. The riders would ride to look at ashes instead of patrolling the shore. The whole valley opens for a night."

"No," Ulyana said.

"It's wood and reeds."

"It's *thirty-one people* and four generations of—" He caught himself, lowered his voice. The sleeping children shifted against their mothers. "No."

"Then say the third thing," Wren said. "Say it out loud, because I think you've already thought it, and I'd rather hear it ugly than watch you chew it."

He looked at the map. At the shed around him. At the families breathing slow in the dark.

"Burn it empty," he said. "Tonight. Move everyone through the blind channels to the mill caves—we'll need two polesmen and the moonless window. Leave the caches sealed, leave nothing worth digging for. Then torch the shed ourselves, from the shore, so the burn points the riders east while we go west." His voice came out level, which cost more than shouting would have. "And when they ask Wren's neighbors what happened, the story will be that the False Keeper's people torched it with everyone inside. Martyrs. Ossen will preach it for a month. The valley will be so busy grieving that nobody watches the west shore."

Silence. Even the fen seemed to hold still.

"That's my mother's roof," Wren said at last, and her voice was not angry, which was worse. "That's the smell of my childhood. And you're asking me to strike the match."

"I'm asking you to choose which lie keeps your people alive. I know what I'm asking. I've spent twenty years not asking it."

"Why now?" Maren asked softly. "You've lied your whole life, Ulyana. This is just a bigger one."

"Because until now, every lie I told kept something *closed*." He heard the words come out of himself and recognized their truth the way you recognize your own handwriting. "Keep the gate shut, keep the passes quiet, keep my name buried. Every lie was a lock. This one opens something. And I find—" He stopped. Started again. "I find I'm better at locks."

Wren was quiet a long moment. Then she laughed, short and hard, without much humor in it.

"My mother used to say the mountain takes tolls. Coin, blood, memory, roofs—it doesn't care which, so long as you pay." She pushed off the wall. "Two hours to load the boats. Get your people to the shore. And Ulyana—" she paused at the door, backlit by the fen's grey light, "—when this is done, whatever's done, you owe my mother's ghost a new roof. Stone, next time. Stone doesn't burn."

"Stone," he agreed. "Whatever it costs."

"Tolls," she said, and went to wake the sleepers.

* * *

They fired Harrow Fen an hour before dawn, when the mist lay thick on the tarn and the world was reduced to sound and suggestion.

Ulyana struck the match himself. He would not ask anyone else to carry that, not even shared. The peat caught with a soft whump, and the reed thatch went up bright and fast, and the flames stood up out of the mist like a second sunrise, red where the real one would be gold.

From the shore, thirty-one people and three conspirators watched the stilted silhouette collapse into sparks. One of the children woke and cried, thinking the sun was falling, and her mother held her and whispered that it was only a bad dream burning up, which was true in a way Ulyana hadn't planned and couldn't have engineered if he'd tried.

By the time Ossen's riders reached the shore, drawn by the glow, the boats were deep in the blind channels and the mist was closing over the water like a book shutting.

Ulyana knelt in the shallows at the stern, one hand trailing, guiding them by feel through channels he knew blind, and let himself feel it fully for exactly as long as the burning took: the shed, the caches, the tally-marks, four generations of quiet competence given to the flame so that a queen and a defrocked priest and a boy with a dead man's letter in his head might slip past a holy man's net.

*Justice outside the law,* Maren had called it once, watching him work the roads. He was beginning to understand what the phrase actually cost. It wasn't lawlessness. It was worse. It was becoming your own legislature, sentencing yourself case by case, and living with every verdict.

The eastern sky went pale. Behind them, thin across the water, he could hear the riders' voices, and then—carried clear over the fen by some trick of the cold air—another voice, deeper, resonant, unmistakable even at distance. Prelate Ossen himself had come to see the fire.

And Ossen was not raging. Ossen was preaching. Even now, even here, turning ash into scripture, pointing his followers east toward the capital, his voice rolling out over the water like a benediction:

"—see how the False Keeper burns what he cannot defend! See how fear consumes him! But the gods are patient, friends, and the road is open before you, and the Spire is waiting—"

Ulyana poled on, westward, into the mist.

Beside him, Maren watched the preacher's distant silhouette shrink. "He gave us the valley," she murmured. "Just like you wanted. So why do I feel like we handed him something too?"

"Because we did." Ulyana fixed his eyes ahead, where the fells rose dark against the lightening sky, and beyond them, faint as a bruise on the horizon, the shape of the capital and the single needle of its spire. "He wanted the passes empty and everyone walking one road. We just cleared the road for him." He dipped the pole, pushed, felt the boat glide forward into the grey. "Now we race him to the door—and pray we get there before ten thousand faithful do."

Behind them, Harrow Fen burned down to embers. Ahead, the mountain waited, and it was no longer pretending to be indifferent.
