## 15. Ossen's Offer

They took her at the ford below Greywater, in the hour when the river ran silver and the sentries' eyes ran dull.

Maren should have seen it. She had been a queen for eleven years; she had been hunted for six months; she knew the shape of an ambush the way other women knew the shape of a lullaby. But she was tired down to the marrow, and Brannic had gone ahead to buy bread, and Ulyana was a quarter mile behind with the mule, and the men who stepped out of the alder scrub wore no livery at all—just grey wool, just pilgrim's knots, just the small white stitches of the Answered on their collars.

Six of them. No swords drawn. That was the courtesy that told her everything.

"Lady," said the one in front, and bowed. He had a soft face, a farmer's face, and his voice came out level and warm as banked coals. "The Prelate asks for you by name."

"Many people ask for me by name," Maren said. "Most of them want me dead."

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly. "But only he knows why you aren't."

* * *

They did not bind her hands. That, too, was a message. They walked her upstream through the alders to a waystation she recognized from better years—a drover's rest with a long low hall and a turf roof, back when drovers still drove anything but prayers. Now its yard was full of the faithful: hundreds of them, kneeling in rows in the frost-rimed mud, faces upturned, mouths moving without sound. A congregation of the quiet kind. The kind that scared her worse than the chorus-kind.

In the doorway of the hall stood a man in white.

She had heard of Ossen before Thornfell. Every fugitive had heard of Ossen. He'd been a canon of the Cathedral of the Nine Voices once, they said, until he started answering questions the Nine had never answered, and the Cathedral had stripped him and cast him out—and he had walked out of the capital with nothing but a staff and a smile, and come back two years later with forty thousand people walking behind him.

He was smaller than the stories. Thin as a winter branch, grey-bearded, with ink-stained fingers and the mild, patient eyes of a schoolmaster. He looked like a man who had spent his life being underestimated and had learned to farm the underestimation like a crop.

"Your Majesty." He bowed exactly deep enough. "You'll forgive the theater. Roads are dangerous, and my people worry."

"I'm not a queen," Maren said. "Ask anyone I've left behind."

"Ah." He straightened, and smiled, and gestured her inside. "Yes. Let's talk about what you've left behind."

* * *

The hall was warm. There was a fire, real wood, and a table laid with bread and hard cheese and a jug of wine—not the sour pilgrim swill but good red wine, southern wine, wine she knew because she had drunk it in another life at another table with people whose names were starting to fray at the edges.

She did not touch any of it.

Ossen sat across from her, poured himself a cup, and waited the way patient men wait. Outside, through the smoke-hole, she could hear the congregation breathing. Hundreds of lungs, in rhythm. It sounded like tide.

"You've lost something," he said at last. Not unkindly.

"I've lost a great many things. You'll have to be specific."

"Her name began with—" He stopped, tilted his head, watching her the way a surgeon watches a wound. "No. You wouldn't thank me for guessing. The face goes first, doesn't it? Names last. Faces first. You wake some mornings and you know you loved someone more than your own heartbeat, and there is nothing where her face should be. Just a shape. A warm hole in the world."

Maren kept her hands flat on the table. Steady. She had sat across from ambassadors who lied for a living and generals who lied for sport and a husband who lied so beautifully he sometimes believed it himself. None of them had frightened her the way this thin man's gentleness frightened her.

"How do you know that?" she asked.

"Because I keep the ledgers." He said it simply, as a clerk might say it. "Every prayer that gets answered, I write down. What was asked. What was given. And what was taken. Your Majesty, do you know how miracles work? Truly? Because the Church never taught it, and the old gods never said it, and the truth is so simple it would break every altar in the nine kingdoms if it were preached from one."

"Tell me anyway."

"The gods don't give. They trade." He folded his ink-stained fingers. "A miracle is a purchase, and the coin is memory. Every answered prayer eats something out of the one who prayed. Small prayers, small bites—a face here, a birthday there. Great miracles, great meals. And the gods are very hungry right now, Majesty, because they are not quite themselves yet. They are rebuilding. And they need a great deal of stone."

Outside, the breathing of the congregation rose and fell.

"And you help them," Maren said. "You feed people to them and call it grace."

"I give people hope, and they pay with their own coin, freely given, and no one lies to anyone." His eyes were terribly clear. "Can your smuggler friend say the same? Can your defrocked priest? Can the man who locked a living king inside a spire and ran?"

The fire popped. Maren discovered she had stopped breathing and made herself start again.

"You know about Ulyana."

"I know everything that walks toward the spire, Majesty. The mountain tells me. The faithful tell me. And now you're going to tell me the rest." He leaned forward, and here it was—the thing she had been waiting for since the alder scrub, the reason for the courtesy and the wine and the unbound hands. "Or," he said softly, "I can tell you something instead."

* * *

He reached into his robe and brought out a ledger. Small, bound in grey leather, its pages soft as cloth from handling. He set it on the table between them but did not open it.

"Do you know what happens to a memory when the gods eat it?" he asked. "Everyone assumes it dies. Burned like a page. But I have read the accounts of those who gave much, Majesty, and I can tell you: it isn't burning. Burning leaves ash. This leaves a *hole*. And a hole has edges. And edges can be mapped."

"You're saying you could bring them back."

"I'm saying the gods could." He turned the ledger around and opened it, and there, in his neat clerk's hand, was a column of entries—and beside each entry, in different ink, a notation she didn't understand until she understood it all at once and her whole body went cold.

*Held.*

"Held in escrow," Ossen said. "The gods don't digest what they take. They store it. Why would they destroy the only currency worth having? Memory is the mortar of souls, Majesty—it's what makes a self stick together long enough to be worn." He smiled again, gently. "And they intend to wear a king."

"The king is already worn."

"The king is a wick. A wick holds the flame; it does not hold the god. For that they need a vessel with weight. With history. With *love*—because love is what lets a god grip a soul without it sliding off." He closed the ledger and rested his hand upon it. "There is a woman in these pages who traded eleven years of passage-miracles for safe roads. Eleven entries. Faces, mostly. One very large face, near the top."

"Don't," Maren said.

"—and every scrap of her," Ossen went on, merciless as spring water finding a crack, "every single bite they took, is held. Intact. Waiting. All it requires is collection." He slid the ledger aside and looked at her with those schoolmaster's eyes, and for one moment she saw past the mildness to the thing underneath, which was not madness at all, which was worse: it was certainty, clean and polished and total. "Deliver Ulyana Whitlock to the spire. Walk him through the gate yourself—he trusts you, or he will, or he must. Give him to the choir. And walk out of that spire whole. Her face first. Then her laugh. Then her name. Everything. Restored, entire, yours again."

The hall was silent except for the fire and the tide-breathing outside.

Maren looked at the ledger. At the neat grey binding. At the hand resting on it, ink-stained, patient, offering.

And the temptation landed.

Not as a blow—as a warmth. That was the obscenity of it. It arrived the way dawn arrives, gradually and everywhere at once, filling the room before she noticed the light. Somewhere in her chest there was that warm hole, the shape of a girl she had carried through six countries and could no longer see, and she felt the hole *reach*, the way a root reaches for water. She thought: *I could see her. I could look at her face again and know it, know it the way I knew it when she was new, when she slept on my chest and I memorized her like scripture.* She thought: *It's one man. One guilty man who sealed a king in a tomb and ran. Who am I to weigh him against her?* She thought, worst of all, in a voice that sounded almost reasonable: *he would probably even agree, if I asked him honestly. He carries his guilt like a pack he can't put down. Maybe he'd be glad.*

The warmth spread. She let it. She sat with it, honestly, for three breaths, four, five—let it fill her the way flood fills a cellar, because she had promised herself long ago that she would never lie to herself about what she wanted. That was the one discipline her mother had left her. *Know what you want, Maren, or the world will sell it to you at interest.*

Five breaths.

Then she raised her head and looked at Prelate Ossen, and said:

"No."

* * *

Something flickered across his face. Disappointment, yes—but beneath it, briefly, respect, and beneath *that*, hunger, sharp and sudden as a pike rising in a still pond. She marked it. She filed it away. Whatever else Ossen was, he was not a believer in his own god; he was a clerk of it, and clerks could be audited.

"No?" he said.

"No." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Here's what you've miscounted, Prelate. You think love is a debt I'd pay anything to settle. But love is also the thing that tells me what I won't do. If I feed that man to your gods to buy back my daughter's face, then the woman who gets her back won't be me. She'll be someone wearing my memories over a rotten beam. And when my daughter looks into that face—if she ever does—she'll see it. Children always see it. The place where the mother used to be."

"She would have a mother again."

"She would have a *costume*." Maren stood. Her legs shook; she let them. "You keep ledgers, so let me teach you an entry you've never recorded. Some things cannot be bought back, because buying them destroys the buyer. My daughter's face is worth more than my having it. Do you understand? It's worth more to the world that I go on wanting it than that I get it. That's the whole of it. That's the arithmetic you people never learned."

Ossen regarded her for a long moment. Then he sighed, and it was almost genuine, almost sad.

"I hoped you'd say yes," he admitted. "But I planned for no." He raised his voice slightly, to the doorway. "Brother Aldous. The lady will be escorted to the east cell. Gently. We are not monsters—we are stewards. She may reconsider. They usually do, by the third day. Hunger is persuasive, and grief is hungrier."

Men came in behind her. She heard them. She did not turn.

"One more thing, Prelate," she said, still facing the fire. "That ledger. The one with the held memories. Where is the master copy kept?"

His silence lasted half a beat too long.

"That," he said, "is above your station."

"Mm." She nodded slowly, as if defeated, and let them take her arm. "Above my station. Of course. Everything important always is."

* * *

The east cell was a drover's storeroom: stone floor, straw pallet, a slit window too narrow for her shoulders and a door with a lock old enough to have opinions. They took her knife and her boots, which told her they feared her competence more than her rank. Good. Fear was information.

They gave her a blanket and a candle stub and left her alone with the breathing of the congregation, which continued through the night without pause, rising and falling, rising and falling, hundreds of sleeping throats singing in their sleep.

Maren sat against the wall and did not sleep. She took stock.

Ossen had planned for refusal, but he had also shown her the ledger, and men only show what they believe is safely theirs. Which meant either he was certain she would never leave this cell—or the master ledger was close enough that he hadn't minded her knowing it existed.

And there was this: they had searched her for knives and boots, but they had not searched her hair, because she was a lady, and ladies' hair was not a place where weapons lived, in the theology of men like Brother Aldous.

A pin. One long steel pin, the kind that held a crown-braid together, the kind she had worn every day for eleven years and through six countries, because her mother had taught her two disciplines and the second one was: *a lady's ornaments are load-bearing.*

She worked it free, slow as growing. Outside, the breathing rose and fell. Somewhere beyond the yard, a horse stamped. Somewhere far north, a man with a mule and too many ghosts was presumably discovering she was missing, and she found—to her own surprise—that the thought of him coming after her warmed her more than Ossen's whole ledger had. That was worth noting. That was worth keeping.

The lock was old. The pin was patient.

By the time the candle burned to a puddle, the door stood open on darkness, and Maren Whitlock—no, Maren of the House of Vail, queen of nothing yet, keeper of one warm hole where a face should be—stood barefoot in the straw, listening.

Two things she knew now, and both of them pointed the same direction, south, toward the capital, toward the spire, toward the fire under the city that Brannic swore was guttering like a dying lamp.

One: the gods kept what they stole, and stolen things could be stolen back.

Two: Ossen needed a vessel delivered to the spire—and if it couldn't be Ulyana, then the gods would have to make do with whatever walked through that gate carrying enough love to be gripped.

She meant to make sure it was Ossen himself.

She slipped out into the frost, barefoot and silent, and ran south.
