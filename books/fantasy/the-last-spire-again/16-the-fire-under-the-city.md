## 16. The Fire Under the City

The stair down had nine hundred steps. Brannic counted them the way he'd once counted psalms—because counting was a kind of prayer, and because if he stopped counting he would have to think about what was at the bottom.

"Four hundred twelve," he said aloud. "Four hundred thirteen."

"You don't have to narrate," Ulyana said behind him. The smuggler's voice was flat as slate in the dark, but Brannic had learned to hear the tension underneath it—the sound of a man walking toward a place he'd sworn never to see again.

"It helps," Brannic said. "In the seminary they taught us that repetition quiets the soul. Then they taught us that quiet souls are easier to manage. I left before the third lesson."

Maren's breath came short above them both. She hadn't spoken since the mine-shrines, since Lowmark, since she'd stood in the dark testing her own memory like a woman counting coins and finding one missing. She carried a lantern now instead of a torch—Brannic had insisted. Fire answered fire. He didn't want to find out what that meant until they understood more.

"Tell me again what we're listening for," Maren said.

"Direction," Brannic said. "Every prayer that gets answered bends somewhere. I told you that in Lowmark. The trick is that most people never notice, because the answer comes back along the same channel it went out. But if you stand in the right place—geometrically, spiritually, pick your word—you can feel the seam where the answer enters the world." He paused on a step. "We're standing in it."

"And?"

"And it's getting wider."

* * *

They had come into the capital through the underways, because the gates above were choked with pilgrims three days deep. Brannic had walked among them for an hour before they descended, hood up, listening, and what he'd heard had frightened him worse than the choir below the mountain.

Not frenzy. That was the thing no one understood. Not screaming ecstasy, not the wild-eyed mania of the converted. The pilgrims in the streets of the capital were *calm*. They knelt in the snow in orderly rows. They prayed in low, contented voices, like farmers watching rain fall on their own fields. A woman had taken Brannic's hand as he passed and said, "He answers, brother. He answers everyone who asks," and her eyes had been clear and kind and utterly empty, and when she smiled he'd seen that two of her teeth were new—not grown, *made*, smooth as river stones—and he'd known she had paid for something with pieces of herself and couldn't remember what.

The city was praying itself empty. Not all at once. A memory at a time. A name at a time. And every prayer, every small surrendered piece of a life, drained downward like rain finding a cellar.

Nine hundred steps. He was at six-forty when the air changed.

It wasn't temperature, though the warmth surprised him—this far under the palace foundations, in tunnels older than the dynasty, he'd expected cold. It was the quality of the silence. Above them, the city made noise even when it prayed: wind, bells, the shuffling of ten thousand knees on stone. Down here the silence had weight and grain. It pressed against the ears like deep water.

And underneath the silence—so faint he almost missed it—a sound he knew from his ordination year, when he'd spent a season tending the great censers: the sound of a very large fire burning very low.

"Guttering," he said. "That's the word. Not roaring. Guttering."

Ulyana's hand found his shoulder in the dark. "How far?"

"Close. Close enough that I can hear the flame think."

"Fires don't think."

"This one does." Brannic started down again. "Seven hundred. Seven hundred one. Keep counting with me if you want to keep your soul quiet."

* * *

The last hundred steps opened into a cavern that had not been built so much as *hollowed*—the rock around them curved and blackened in ways no chisel made, and Brannic understood, with a lurch, that this chamber predated the city, the kingdom, possibly the mountain. It was a throat. They were standing inside the throat of something that had once been alive.

The brazier sat at the center: a bowl of black metal wide as a millpond, held aloft on chains thick as trees, and in it burned the fire.

He had expected grandeur. Every hymn about the dragon-fire described grandeur—the sun imprisoned, the first light caged, the wrath of heaven banked to warm mortal kings. What he saw instead was a fire dying. It burned low and blue-white at its heart, and its edges kept going out and relighting, out and relighting, like a lamp in a drafty room, and each time it guttered the whole cavern flinched.

Around the brazier, arranged in concentric rings on the floor, lay hundreds of people.

"They're asleep," Maren whispered.

"No," Brannic said. His stomach had gone cold despite the heat. "Look at their lips."

Their lips moved. All of them, all at once, in perfect unison, though no sound came—or rather, a sound came, but not through the air. Brannic felt it arrive directly behind his eyes, the way you feel someone say your name in a dream. The sleepers were praying. In their sleep, without knowing it, without wanting it, the people of the capital were lying here beneath their own city and giving themselves away, mouthful by mouthful, and the fire drank what they poured.

"The wick," Ulyana said quietly.

Brannic followed his gaze. Beyond the rings of sleepers, at the cavern's far end, a second stair rose toward the palace above—and on the landing halfway up, framed in the glow, stood a figure.

Even at this distance, even in the bad light, Brannic knew the posture of kings. He'd been raised on portraits of them. This one stood too still. Kings fidget; power is restless. This figure stood the way a coat stands on its hook.

"That's him," Maren breathed. "That's Aldric." Her voice did something complicated on the name—grief and hatred sharing a single syllable—and Brannic pretended not to hear, because some things are not for priests.

"He can see us?" Ulyana asked.

"I don't know," Brannic said honestly. "Watch."

For a long moment nothing moved. Then the king's head turned—slowly, joint by joint, like a door on rusted hinges—and though Brannic could not see the face clearly, he felt the attention land on them the way you feel weather change. And in that instant the fire flared. Once. Bright enough to throw all their shadows huge against the cavern wall, bright enough to show him the faces of the sleepers, and what he saw there made him step back:

They were smiling. Every sleeper, smiling, mouths still moving, tears running freely down faces gone serene as saints'.

Then the flare died, and the king turned away, and climbed the rest of the stair out of sight, and the fire settled back into its guttering rhythm, and Brannic discovered he'd been holding his breath long enough to hurt.

"It knows we're here," he said.

"It let us be here," Ulyana corrected. "There's a difference."

"There is," Brannic agreed. "The difference is a trap."

* * *

They withdrew to a side gallery—an old priest's cell, by the look of it, a niche carved with faded blessings, where watchmen of some forgotten order had once kept vigil over the flame. There was a bench, a broken lamp-niche, and a slot in the wall for receiving written prayers, which Brannic examined with professional interest and personal revulsion.

"Prayer-mail," he muttered. "Direct line to the divine. No wonder the thing set up shop down here. This whole cavern is a funnel. Everything anyone ever sent up—" he tapped the slot, "—came down."

Maren sat with her back against the stone, lantern between her feet, hands wrapped around her own arms. She had barely slept since the waystation. Since the child. Brannic had watched her carry that child half a mountain and then give her over to a temple nurse in Lowmark with a gentleness that had cost her more than the carrying.

"How long," she said. "That's what I need to know. How long before the fire dies or the god finishes or whatever the ending is."

Brannic lowered himself onto the bench and closed his eyes, and did the thing he was actually good at—the only thing left of his vocation worth keeping. He listened.

The flame's rhythm was irregular but not random. Flare, gutter, feed. Flare, gutter, feed. He timed it against his pulse, then against Maren's breathing, then against the drip of water somewhere in the dark, building the pattern the way he'd once built the cadence of a litany. The intervals were shrinking. Each cycle came faster than the last, and each flare was smaller, and each feeding—

Each feeding was bigger. More sleepers arriving. He could hear them being brought, somewhere above, the soft shuffle of the willing and the unwilling alike, funneled down by Ossen's faithful into the rings around the bowl.

"Three days," he said finally. "Maybe four. The fire's nearly out. It needs the king fully hollowed to burn through what's left of him, and it needs—" he gestured vaguely at everything, "—more of *that*." The sleepers. The prayers. The slow harvest of a city forgetting itself.

"And when it burns through him?"

"Then the god has a body with a crown attached, and a fire hot enough to make more bodies. Lots more." He opened his eyes. "I've read the old texts, Your Majesty. The ones the seminary kept in the locked case. Resurrection isn't coming back from the dead. It's coming back with *staff*. An embodiment needs vessels. One vessel per worshipper, roughly. Do the arithmetic on how many people are kneeling in the snow up there."

Maren did the arithmetic. He watched her do it, watched her face go still as she arrived at the number.

"The whole city," she said.

"The whole city," Brannic confirmed, "and then the faith spreads. It always does. Miracles travel faster than plagues."

Silence in the cell. Below, the fire fed, and guttered, and fed.

* * *

Ulyana had gone quiet in the corner—that particular smuggler's quiet that Brannic was learning meant calculation, not absence. When he finally spoke, it was to the wall more than to either of them.

"The king climbed the stair. Up, not down. Whatever's wearing him spends its time up there, in the palace. The fire stays here. Which means the binding between them runs through that stairway, and through those rings of sleepers, and through—" he paused, "—through whoever tends the flame."

"Prelate Ossen," Maren said.

"Prelate Ossen," Ulyana agreed. "Which means somebody has to get close enough to him to learn the rite. The actual words. Because I've sealed a lot of doors in my life, priest, and every seal has an unsealing, and nobody seals anything without leaving the key somewhere."

Brannic laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "You want the key. To a god."

"I want the lock," Ulyana said. "Keys are your department. You used to talk to heaven for a living. Talk to it again."

"Heaven's occupied."

"Then talk to whoever's squatting in it. Learn its terms. Everything that bargains has terms—I've never met an exception, divine or otherwise." Ulyana pushed off the wall. "Meanwhile I'm going to count guards, count stairs, and count exits. Three things I'm good at."

He moved toward the gallery mouth, then stopped. For a moment, in the firelight leaking around the corner, Brannic saw the gatekeeper's face do something unguarded—something old and afraid.

"I knew this room," Ulyana said quietly. "Before I saw it tonight. The lamp niche is on the left. I knew it would be on the left."

Then he was gone into the dark, footsteps measured and silent, and Brannic looked at Maren, and Maren looked at Brannic, and neither of them said what they were both thinking: that the man who kept every gate in Thornfell had once kept one much closer to this fire than he'd admitted, and that the past was following him down nine hundred steps like a debt collector.

* * *

Left alone with the queen, Brannic did what he always did when fear got large: he worked.

He unpacked his satchel by lantern-light—salt, chalk, a cracked bell with the clapper removed, the vestments of a defrocked man folded around tools of a trade the church would have called blasphemy and Brannic called due diligence. He laid out the counter-choir materials. Three nights ago, below the mountain, singing against the congregation of the voice-taken, he had learned something important: the god's chorus could not abide dissonance. Harmony it ate. Discord stuck in its throat.

If it came to a contest down here—if the fire flared and the sleepers woke and the thing wearing the king descended—he would need a choir of one, loud and ugly and sincere.

"You pray," Maren said suddenly. She was watching him work. "You're defrocked, excommunicated, I assume anathema in at least three dioceses. And you still pray."

Brannic considered lying. Decided, not for the first time, that the queen had earned better.

"I pray the way a sailor swears," he said. "Habitually, irreverently, and mostly in emergencies. But yes. I pray." He wound the bell's cord around his fist. "Here's the joke of it, Majesty. I can hear which prayers get answered. Do you know what I've never heard? A prayer answered *kindly*. Not once. Not in twenty years of listening. Every miracle I've ever traced had teeth marks in it." He nodded toward the cavern mouth, toward the guttering blue-white heart of the world's oldest fire. "Whatever's down there didn't corrupt the gods' voices. It just copied them accurately. The hunger was always in the original."

Maren was quiet a long time. Then she said, "My daughter used to ask why God needed our prayers. She thought it was greedy. She was seven." Her voice didn't break. It had gone somewhere past breaking, into a flat country where grief became fuel. "She was right, wasn't she? About the needing."

"Yes," Brannic said. "I think the needing is the whole architecture of heaven. They build us because they're hungry. We pray because they taught us to. And the ones who refuse—" he thought of the skeptic at the waystation, of his tongue, offered, "—get eaten anyway."

"Then we stop feeding it."

"We stop feeding it," Brannic agreed. "Starting with figuring out what happens if the fire goes out *before* the god is ready. Because a resurrection interrupted mid-meal—" he smiled, thin and joyless, "—well. Even gods choke."

Below them, the flame flared. Held. Guttered.

Faster than the last time. He marked the interval against his pulse and did not like the number.

* * *

Ulyana returned near dawn—or near what passed for dawn, forty fathoms under a snowbound city, which was to say the moment Brannic's exhaustion stopped being deniable. The smuggler crouched at the gallery mouth, and the look on his face rearranged the priorities of the night.

"Change of timetable," Ulyana said. "Ossen's moving the coronation forward. I heard the guards talking at the upper landing—they're calling it the Second Advent. Three days is wrong, priest. We have one."

"One day," Maren repeated.

"One day, and the stairway up is crawling with faithful, and the stairway down—" Ulyana tilted his head toward the cavern, listening, and his expression curdled, "—the stairway down is filling up. They're bringing more sleepers. Hundreds. Whole parishes."

Brannic got to his feet, joints protesting, and slung the satchel across his chest. Somewhere in the last hour the fear had finished its work and settled into something harder and more useful. Resolve, the seminary had called it. Brannic had always preferred *spite*.

"One day," he said. "Then we'd better decide which of us talks to the god, and which of us starts the ugliest choir in history."

Below, in its ancient bowl, the last dragon-fire guttered—and this time, when it relit, it did not settle. It began, very slowly, to grow.

Brannic heard it change key.

So, he was certain, did the thing upstairs wearing the crown.
