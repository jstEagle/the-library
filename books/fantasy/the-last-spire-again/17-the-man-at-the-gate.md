## 17. The Man at the Gate

The spire looked smaller than Ulyana remembered.

That was the first lie the mountain told him in twenty years that he almost believed. He had carried it in his head as a mountain carries a wound—vast, black, bleeding light at the crown—and here it was, only stone after all, rising out of the capital's rooftops like a needle someone had driven through the heart of the city and forgotten to pull out. Its windows were lit. They should not have been lit. No one had lived in the royal spire since the night he sealed it, and yet every window burned with a soft gold glow, patient as banked coals.

"You keep staring at it," Maren said.

"I'm counting windows."

"How many are there?"

"Sixty-three." He didn't look away. "There used to be sixty-two."

Brannic, walking behind them with his collar up against the ash-fall, snorted. "A god moved in and added a window. That's the most bureaucratic miracle I've ever heard of."

"It's not a window," Ulyana said. "It's an eye. And it's been watching the gate since we crossed the river."

They came into the city the way water comes into a cellar—not through the doors but through every crack at once. Ulyana had planned three routes and abandoned two by noon; the streets rearranged themselves around processions of pilgrims who walked with their faces tilted up and their mouths moving in unison, all of them speaking different prayers at the same rhythm, like a thousand clocks ticking out of time together. The city smelled of tallow and incense and, underneath it, faintly, of hot iron—the smell of the dragon-fire, leaking up through the stones the way a fever leaks through skin.

Maren wore a servant's grey cloak and walked like a queen anyway. Twenty years of smuggler craft and Ulyana still couldn't have taught her to walk small. She kept her hood low and her chin level, and the pilgrims parted around her without knowing why.

"The choirboy meets us at the Tanner's Stair," she said for the fourth time. "Sundown."

"I heard you."

"You haven't listened to me once since the pass."

He had no answer to that that wasn't true, and the true answer was that since Prelate Ossen's zealots had sealed the high road and Ulyana had burned his own safehouse to cut them a way through, some part of him had already left the conversation and gone ahead to the spire, where it stood waiting in the dark with its hand on a door he'd sworn never to touch again.

*You'll go back,* Maren had told him on the snowline, half-frozen, furious, alive. *Men like you always go back. You just want company on the road.*

The Tanner's Stair was a smuggler relic from another century—a switchback path cut into the spire's western flank so tanners could haul carcasses up to the old rendering sheds without passing the palace gates. The sheds were ruins now. The stair was still sound. Ulyana had helped cut it himself, in another life, when he was a young man with royal keys at his belt and a king who trusted him more than he trusted his own brothers.

He climbed it now with his palms sweating inside his gloves.

The choirboy was waiting at the third landing, exactly where he said he would be, which told Ulyana more about the boy than any introduction could. A child who kept appointments in a city being eaten by a god was either very brave or very frightened, and usually both.

He was perhaps twelve. He wore a chorister's robe gone grey with ash, and he held a book against his chest the way soldiers hold shields—with both arms, knuckles white, expecting it to be taken from him.

"Tam," Maren said gently. "This is the man I told you about."

The boy looked up at Ulyana, and something crossed his face—recognition, or the ghost of it—and Ulyana felt the ground shift under him the way it does before an avalanche, when nothing has moved yet but everything has already decided to fall.

"You're the man at the gate," Tam said.

"I'm a smuggler," Ulyana said.

"No." The boy hugged the book tighter. "You're *the* man at the gate. He wrote to you. He's been writing to you for years."

The wind came up the stair then, off the burning windows above, and it was warm, which was worse than cold. Warm wind from a sealed spire meant the fire below was waking. Brannic made a small sound in his throat, a priest's reflexive syllable, and bit it off.

"Inside," Ulyana said. "All of you. Now."

* * *

The rendering shed still had its roof, mostly, and its walls, and a door that Ulyana barred with a knot he could tie blind and drunk and dying. Inside, among the rusted hooks and crusted floors of his old trade's ugliest cousin, the choirboy opened the hymnal.

It was a palace edition—vellum, gilt edges, the kind of book that gets locked away and dusted and never sung from. Someone had hollowed the back pages out with a knife, carefully, lovingly, and fitted letters inside: folded sheets, many of them, packed tight as seed grain.

"He started hiding them the second year," Tam said. "After the thing learned to read what he wrote. It burns anything he leaves out in the open. So he puts them in the hymnals, because the hymnals go down to the chapel for the services, and the services are the only time it—" the boy's voice caught, steadied— "the only time he sleeps. When they sing. It lets him sleep during singing. I think it likes the singing."

Ulyana took the book. His hands were steady. He noted this distantly, the way you note the behavior of someone else's hands—that they did not shake, that they turned the pages, that they lifted out the topmost letter with the competence of a man opening forty years of correspondence.

The handwriting was his king's.

He knew it the way he knew the weight of a gate key. Aldric had written like a man building something—each letter set square, each line ruled straight without a rule, the hand of a mason who'd been taught penmanship as a punishment. The first letter was dated nineteen years ago.

*To the man at the gate.*

Ulyana did not sit down. Sitting would have meant admitting his legs wanted to.

*I don't know if you're alive. I don't know if you ran or died or if my brother's men found you at the ford. I know what they will have told you happened here, and I know it isn't what happened, because you were there, and if you were there then you already know, and if you already know then this letter is only for the parts you couldn't have seen.*

*So. The parts you couldn't have seen.*

*When you shut the door, I was still me. I need you to believe that. Whatever they say I became, whatever they found in the morning—the thing that signed the orders, the thing that burned the west wing—I was still me when the last bolt went home. You looked at me through the grate and I nodded and you did what I asked. I have spent nineteen years holding onto that nod. It is the only proof I have that I existed.*

Ulyana stopped reading.

Not because he wanted to. Because his eyes had filled and the page had become a smear of gilt and ink and firelight, and Maren was saying his name, and Brannic had put a hand on his shoulder with the wordless tact of a man who had once administered last rites and knew when silence was the sacrament.

"I sealed it," Ulyana said. His voice sounded borrowed. "I sealed him in and I ran. That's the whole story. That's what everyone—"

"What everyone says," Maren finished quietly, "is that the king went mad and his gatekeeper betrayed him and fled with the keys. I've heard it in four kingdoms. It's a good story. Good stories are always wrong in the same places."

Ulyana looked at her.

"Read the rest," she said.

He read the rest.

*You will hate yourself for the nod. Don't. I ordered it. Hear me: I ORDERED IT. The fire was taking me—it had been since the gods' priests fed the brazier too deep, since the old rites came back into fashion, since I let them convince me a king could carry a dragon's death in his chest and stay a king. By the end I was surfacing in my own body like a drowning man, minutes at a time. I knew what was coming. I knew that one morning soon I would wake up wearing myself and not be in there anymore.*

*So I gave you the order while I still owned my mouth. Seal the door. Keep the key. Tell no one. Let them call you traitor—traitor is a survivable name. If you had stayed, they would have made you open it again. They would have found a way to use your love the way they use everything. Running was the last loyal act available to you, and you took it, and I have thanked you for it every lucid hour I have clawed back in nineteen years.*

*Which brings me to the request. There is always a request. Forgive me.*

*The fire is guttering. I can feel it the way you feel a candle near its end—shorter flames, longer darks. The thing that wears me is afraid. Understand that. All its power, all its answered prayers, all its hungry choirs, and it is afraid of the same thing I am: that when the fire goes out, we both do. But I am ready. Nineteen years is enough. My sons are dead or grown, my kingdom is a story other people tell, and I am tired of watching my hands do its work.*

*Let it die, Ulyana. Come to the crown-chamber and speak the unbinding, even though it kills me. Even though it takes everything you remember and everything you love, because it will—it takes payment from the speaker, that's how the old binding works, that's why no one has dared. Pay it. Pay it gladly. A man with no past and an honest future is worth ten kings in gilded cages.*

*And if you cannot—if the price is truly past bearing—then live. Live hidden and whole and far from here, and hate me a little, and let the fire gut itself out on its own schedule, slow and starving, and may the god starve with it.*

*But if you come: I will know your step on the stairs. I have known it for twenty years. It is the only music left in this place.*

*A., the man in the spire*

The shed was silent except for the warm wind worrying the roof.

Ulyana became aware, slowly, that he was on his knees. He had no memory of going down. The letter was still in his hands, held with the care owed to something living, and the other letters—dozens of them, years of them—waited in the hymnal like a congregation.

"There's one for every season," Tam said softly. "Spring ones are hopeful. Winter ones aren't. This year's winter one is short. It just says *soon.*"

"Nineteen years." Ulyana heard his own voice from far away. "Nineteen years he's been writing to a man he thought might be dead."

"He never thought you were dead," Maren said. "Dead men don't get letters. He thought you were *hiding*, and he was right, and he loved you for it, and he waited." She crouched in front of him, and for once there was no queen in her face at all—only a woman who had lost her daughter's face to the same hunger and knew precisely the size of the hole it left. "I know what you're feeling. It feels like guilt. Look closer. It isn't."

"It feels like I ran from a crime I never committed," Ulyana said, "and built twenty years of walls on the foundation of it, and now—"

"Now you stop running." She took the letter from his hands, gently, and set it on the hymnal. "That's all. Not redemption. Not forgiveness. Just: you stop. The running is over. Whatever happens at the crown-chamber tonight, you walk in as the man who kept the door, not the man who broke it. Those are different men, Ulyana. I've met both. One of them is worth following."

Brannic cleared his throat. "For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much, I was defrocked for drinking the communion wine and telling the truth about the bishop—I've traced the prayer-lines for six days now. Every answered prayer in this country bends toward that spire. Every single one. It's not collecting worship. It's collecting *fuel*. The fire's nearly out and it knows it, and it's eating a kingdom's memories to blow on the coals." He tapped the hymnal. "Your king's been right about everything for nineteen years. The fire dies tonight or the god finishes dressing itself in him and walks out that door wearing a crown."

Outside, across the ruined yard, the sixty-third window flared.

Not gold anymore. The light coming from the crown-chamber had gone the color of the inside of a forge, and under it, thin and clear and carrying impossibly across the ash-dark city, they could hear singing. Hundreds of voices. Thousands. The pilgrims in the streets below, the voice-taken in the mines, the hollow-eyed faithful kneeling on the ice of the river—all of them singing one note, one long unbroken note, a sound like a drawn breath that refused to end.

Tam stood up. "That's new," he whispered. "It's never called them to sing at night."

"It's not calling them to sing," Ulyana said.

He rose. His knees ached. His hands, he noticed, were steady again, and he understood at last why: they had finally been given work worthy of them. Twenty years of knots and gates and small kindnesses, all of it training for one door.

"It's tuning them," he said. "Tonight it finishes the resurrection. Tonight it burns through the last of the fire and the last of the king, and steps outside."

Maren pulled her hood up. Under it, her eyes were dry and hard and lit from within. "Then we're done reading letters."

Ulyana looked once more at the spire—at the door at its base that only he had ever closed, that only he could open, that had waited nineteen years for his step on the stairs. Somewhere at the top of it, a drowned man was surfacing in his own body for the last time, listening.

"Yeah," Ulyana said, and picked up his keys. "We're done."

He tied the shed door behind them with a knot that would take a god an hour to untie, and they started up the Tanner's Stair, into the forge-light, toward the man in the spire—who had heard, all the way from the crown-chamber, footsteps beginning to climb.
