## 18. Unbinding

**MAREN**

The throne room had not been built for what Maren intended to do in it.

It had been built for awe. Vaulted ceiling ribbed like the inside of a whale, pillars of black granite veined with gold, a floor of polished stone so deep and dark that courtiers once joked you could drown in it. At the far end, beneath banners that hadn't been changed in twenty years because no one dared touch them, stood the throne—and on the throne, the king.

She walked down the center of the hall with her chin up and her hands empty, which was the hardest thing she had ever done with her hands. Behind her, at the great doors, Brannic was already singing.

His voice filled the vault like water filling a cup. It wasn't beautiful, exactly—it was cracked and untrained and it caught on the high notes—but it was *his*, every note paid for out of his own chest, and that was the point. The choir below the city sang with borrowed throats. Brannic sang with his own.

The court turned to look. Hundreds of them—lords and ladies, clerks and guards, all of them with the faintly glazed patience of people who have been waiting for something for a very long time without knowing what. Their eyes followed her. Their mouths stayed closed.

*They're listening*, she thought. *Underneath everything, they're still listening.*

"Your majesty," she said, and stopped at the foot of the dais, and bowed—the full bow, the one she'd been taught at nine years old by a woman who told her a queen's spine should bend only twice in her life: once to marry, once to bury.

The king looked at her.

Or rather, something behind the king's eyes looked at her, the way light looks at you through a dirty window. His face was still handsome in the way a portrait is handsome—correct, composed, finished. But there were cracks now. She could see them from here. Fine lines around the mouth where nothing human lived anymore, a slackness in the left hand resting on the armrest, fingers curled like a dead bird's feet.

"You are not summoned," the king said. The voice was his voice. It had always been his voice. That was the cruelest part.

"No," Maren said. "I'm not."

"Then you are trespassing." The king rose. The whole hall seemed to rise with him, a thousand chests drawing breath in unison, and Maren felt Brannic's song falter behind her before it steadied. "Trespass against the gods' anointed is—"

"I know what it is," she said. "I married into the liturgy. I sat through eleven years of it."

Something flickered across the king's face—not expression, but the *memory* of expression, like wind moving over wheat without bending it.

"Maren," he said.

"Yes."

"My queen."

"Not for six years."

"The crown does not recognize—" He stopped. The thing inside him ground against the shape of its own sentence, a cart wheel jammed in a rut. When it spoke again the words came slower, dragged up from somewhere deeper. "*You fled.*"

"I fled," she agreed. "And I came back. That's the difference between us tonight."

Behind her, Brannic's voice climbed. She didn't look back. She'd heard him practice it on the road, the counter-choir he'd reconstructed from a hymnal fragment and half a memory of his mother singing in a kitchen, and he'd told her the theory of it while sharpening his knife: *the god needs agreement, your highness. Every answered prayer is a contract. A voice raised against it, freely, in the same room—that's a breach of terms.*

So she talked. That was her work tonight. She held the court's attention while Ulyana climbed, while Brannic sang, while the thing on the throne tried to conduct three conversations at once and found, for the first time in twenty years, that it did not have enough of itself to go around.

"What do you want?" the king asked.

"The same thing you do," Maren said. "To be done."

* * *

**BRANNIC**

Brannic had sung funerals for thirty years and weddings for five, and he knew in his bones what a congregation sounded like when it wanted to kill someone. This was worse than that. This was a congregation trying to decide whether it was a congregation at all.

He sang louder.

The hymnal fragment was pinned inside his coat, over his heart, where the king's letter had been carried for two hundred miles. Fourteen lines of music in an old notation, transcribed by a choirboy with ink-stained fingers and a look in his eyes like a boy who has carried something heavy through a burning city and finally set it down. Brannic had harmonized the rest himself, working backward from the way the counter-choir made his teeth ache when he tested it near the mine-shrines.

Below them, under forty feet of stone, the congregation of the voice-taken had gone quiet. He could feel their silence like pressure on the eardrums. Listening.

Good. Let them listen. Let them hear one man singing badly and freely and let some buried scrap of each of them remember that they used to make sounds like this too, sounds nobody asked for, sounds that belonged to no god.

The court began to move. Not all of them—a few dozen, drifting toward him along the walls with the smooth unhurried glide of sleepwalkers, their mouths opening on syllables he couldn't quite hear. The god was splitting its attention. Maren's voice from the dais; Brannic's from the doors; and somewhere above, in the crown-chamber, footsteps on stairs that hadn't been climbed in twenty years.

Brannic watched the nearest walker come within arm's reach. An old woman in good wool. Her eyes were fixed on the middle distance. Her lips moved.

"—*answered,*" she breathed. "*It answers. It answered me, my son's fever, it answered—*"

"It ate your answer, mother," Brannic said, without breaking the melody. "You paid for that fever out of your own skull. Ask it what your son's name was."

The old woman stopped walking. Her brow creased. Her lips kept moving, but the syllables lost their shape, and after a moment she stood swaying in the middle of the usurping hymn like a woman who has stepped off a stair that isn't there.

Then she sat down. Right there on the floor, among the lords, and put her face in her hands.

Brannic's throat tightened. He kept singing. One. There would be more, or there wouldn't. It wasn't his to count. His job was fourteen lines and his own cracked voice, offered freely, in breach of terms, until his lungs gave out or the mountain did.

Above him, faint through the vaulting, he heard the crown-chamber door groan open.

**ULYANA**

Twenty years ago he had climbed these stairs every morning, two at a time, carrying the king's breakfast and the day's correspondence and whatever gossip he'd harvested from the gate guards, because Aldric had liked to start the day laughing and Ulyana had always known where the laughter was.

Now he climbed them one at a time, with a cold lantern and a knife he didn't expect to use and the king's letter folded against his chest, and the stairs remembered him anyway. His boots found the worn places. His hand found the iron rail at exactly the height his hand had always found it. The tower had kept his shape the way a glove keeps a hand's shape long after the hand is gone.

The door at the top was sealed with the same sigil-work he himself had laid, twenty years ago, kneeling on cold stone with wax and salt and the king's own blood on his fingers, following instructions spoken in a voice that was already going strange at the edges.

*Aldric's order*, he reminded himself. *Aldric's words. You obeyed. You sealed him in because he begged you to seal him in.*

*And then you ran*, said the other voice, the one that had ridden him across two decades and a border range. *You ran and you buried it and you called the burial duty.*

He pressed his palm to the sigil. The wax had gone amber with age. Under his hand, something pulsed—slow, vast, patient as geology.

"You remember me," he said quietly.

The sigil flared white.

The door opened inward onto heat.

The crown-chamber had been a library once—he remembered the shelves, the maps, the globe with the southern continents painted wrong. Now the shelves were ash-drifts. The windows had been bricked. And in the center of the room, in a brazier of black iron older than the kingdom, the last dragon-fire burned low and blue, guttering, starving—and around it, drawn up through the stones and the air and the seams of the world, coiled the shape of the thing that wore the king downstairs.

It didn't have a body. It had *attention*. It gathered around the fire like fog gathering around a lamp, dense and slow, and where its attention touched the walls the ash stirred, and where it touched Ulyana the ash rose off the floor in a slow spiral and hung there, turning.

"GATEKEEPER."

The voice came from everywhere. From the fire, from the walls, from his own ribs.

"You kept the small gates," it said. "Passes. Doors. Toll-roads. All your life, keeping things shut. And all your life, things knocked." The ash spiraled closer. "We are what knocks."

"I know what you are," Ulyana said. "You're a hunger with good manners. You wear voices because no one opens a door for silence."

"And yet." The attention tightened, and he felt it press against his memories like a thumb testing fruit. "Yet here you stand, keeper of the last lock, and we find you... interesting. You carry a letter you have not read past its first line. You carry guilt like a coin you refuse to spend. We could spend it for you."

The fire flared.

Blue light flooded the chamber, and in the light the god showed him—

Kestrel Ford, springtime, the king laughing so hard he had to hold the fence rail. Aldric's wedding, the whole court drunk, Ulyana catching the bride's thrown wreath by accident and going red to the ears. The night watch on the spire wall, two men and a jug of cider, Aldric saying *you'll keep it, won't you, whatever happens, you'll keep the gate* — and Ulyana saying *to my last breath* —

Every memory, whole and warm and lit from inside.

"Walk away," the god said gently. "Descend the stairs. Sing nothing, speak nothing, unbind nothing. And take them with you. All of them. We will restore what you buried. We will give you back the friend, the ford, the cider, the vow kept instead of broken. Twenty years of exile, refunded in full. This is our offer. This is our *answer*."

The ash settled. The fire guttered. Somewhere far below, muffled by stone, Brannic's voice wavered and held.

Ulyana stood in the doorway of the room where he had imprisoned his best friend, holding the letter he'd been afraid to finish reading, and understood at last why he'd never opened it all the way.

Because the letter was Aldric's answer to the same offer. Because the letter ended, he knew now, he'd known since the choirboy recited it in the ruined chapel with tears running into his collar, with the words: *let the fire die, even if the fire is me, even if remembering me is the price — let it gutter.*

The god had offered him a life.

Aldric had asked him for a death.

"That's the difference between us," Ulyana said, and drew the letter from his coat, and unfolded it, and stepped toward the brazier with the firelight climbing his face.

The god screamed with a hundred thousand stolen voices, and the tower began to shake, and below him Maren's clear voice rose through the cracking stone:

*"Do it!"*

Ulyana spoke the first word of the unbinding.

* * *

**THE KING**

—for the first time in twenty years, the body was *quiet*.

The king surfaced inside his own skull like a swimmer breaking into air. Below him the throne room roared—or perhaps it was only blood in the ears of a dying machine. A woman's voice, familiar, furious, alive. A man singing badly. Stone dust falling through shafts of light.

And overhead, through the bones of the tower, a word. The first word of the unbinding, spoken in a voice he knew better than his own.

*Aldric*, he thought, with the last strength of a man who had been a vessel for two decades. *Aldric, you came back.*

*I'm sorry*, he thought, toward the voice, toward the fire, toward whoever might still be listening in the wreckage of heaven. *I asked too much of you. I asked you to keep the door and I never told you how heavy the door was—*

The fire flared. The god convulsed through his limbs, and the king felt his own hands rise, clawing, reaching for a throat that wasn't there anymore, and he laughed—actually laughed, a rusty, ruined, human sound that startled even him—

*Let it gutter*, he thought. *Let it gutter, let it gutter—*

And in the crown-chamber, Ulyana Whitlock spoke the second word.
