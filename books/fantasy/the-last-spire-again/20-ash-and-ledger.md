## 20. Ash and Ledger

The morning after the fire died, the capital woke up missing itself.

Maren stood on the balcony of the palace her husband had hollowed and watched them come out of the great temple in ones and twos, blinking, like people surfacing from deep water. Ossen's faithful. Thousands of them, kneeling for days around the spire's base while their god unraveled above them, and now standing up with the particular lostness of those who cannot remember why they knelt.

They looked at their hands. They looked at each other. A woman near the temple steps turned to the man beside her and said, loudly enough to carry across the square:

"Do you know my name?"

And the man said, "No. Do you know mine?"

They didn't. Maren watched them stand there in the ash-fall—fine grey drifts of it still sifting down from the spire, settling on shoulders and ledges like a season that had arrived out of turn—and she understood what freedom had cost these people. The god had eaten its way through them for months, taking memories as toll for every answered prayer, and when it died it had not given anything back. It never gave anything back. That was the whole architecture of the thing.

But they were free. Hollow, grieving without knowing what they grieved, some of them weeping into their hands for losses they couldn't name—but free.

A boy of maybe ten came out last, alone, and stopped at the bottom of the steps and looked up at the spire until his eyes found the high dark window where the crown-chamber was. Then he bowed to it. Just once, small and formal, the way you bow to a grave.

Maren knew him. Tam. The choirboy who had carried a dead king's letter through a city gone mad with prayer, who had memorized eleven lines of a plea to a gatekeeper he'd never met because the paper version burned. She had promised herself she would find him after, and here he was, finding himself instead, in a square full of strangers who used to be his congregation.

She went down to him.

* * *

The palace smelled of cold hearths and old incense and, beneath it, faintly, of scorched feather—the residue of a god made of borrowed voices. Maren walked its halls with a candle in one hand and no guard, because there was no one left to guard against and no one loyal enough to guard her. The servants who hadn't been taken by the chorus had fled during the final days. The ones who had been taken were out in the city, learning their own names again from documents they didn't remember signing.

In the council chamber she found the ledgers.

There were forty-one of them, bound in white leather with gilt spines, arranged on a long table by year. The Prayer-Ledgers of the Restoration, Ossen had called them, in his beautiful carrying voice. Every petition recorded. Every petitioner named. Every miracle granted, logged in a clerk's neat hand beside the price paid—a memory surrendered, itemized like a debt.

*Ilse Varn, laundress. Petition: healing for her son's fever. Granted. Price: the memory of her own wedding day.*

*Tomas Reeve, cooper. Petition: rain for his fields. Granted. Price: the name of the village where he was born.*

Page after page. Year after year. Ten thousand small surrenders, all of them voluntary, all of them signed, because Ossen had made the bargain sound like devotion and the devouring sound like grace.

Maren sat down at the table and read until the candle guttered low, and somewhere past midnight she found the entries she had been both dreading and hunting since the moment she'd understood what Brannic had told them on the road.

Her own name. Her own hand. Dates from her flight south, when she'd been desperate and frozen and alone, when a waystation priest had offered her passage-miracles and she had paid without reading the fine print of her own soul.

*Petition: safe passage over the Kestrel pass in winter. Granted.*

*Price:* and then the clerk's hand, recording what the god took, and Maren read the entry four times before the words would sit still.

Not her daughter's face. That loss she already knew; she had discovered it weeks ago on the road, reaching for Liss's face the way you reach for a stair in the dark and finding nothing, and Ulyana—Silas, whatever his true name was—had comforted her badly and honestly, which is the only kind of comfort worth having.

This entry was different. This entry was newer. From the descent through the mine-shrines, from the choir below, from the moment she had quietly tested Brannic's claim on herself, trading one more memory for one more miracle of silence and shadow.

*Price: the sound of your daughter's voice.*

She had known the face was gone. She had not known she'd also sold the voice. Somewhere in her there had been a last reserve—a lullaby Liss used to demand, sung wrong on purpose, with a growl in the second verse—and she had spent it without knowing, buying darkness for a few hours in a tunnel full of singing things.

Maren closed the ledger very gently, the way you close a coffin.

Then she carried all forty-one volumes down to the courtyard, stacked them in the fountain basin, and burned them.

It took oil from the kitchens and most of an hour and she did it alone, feeding the pages in one at a time so the names caught fully, watching ten thousand debts go up in a column of black smoke. A clerk's ghost of an instinct made her want to keep copies, make restitution, build some apparatus of repair. But you could not give back a wedding day. You could not re-teach a woman the name of her village. There was no restitution for this, only an ending, and endings should be visible.

The smoke rose straight in the windless dawn, and people began to gather at the courtyard gates to watch it, and no one asked her what she was burning. Some of them, she thought, recognized the smell of their own names.

* * *

The council of whatever remained convened on the third day, in the same chamber where she'd found the ledgers, and it was less a government than a triage station.

Brannic came, defrocked and unrepentant, wearing his stolen vestments inside out as a joke only he appreciated. He had spent two days walking the temple district with his ruined ear cocked toward the crowd—not listening for answered prayers anymore; there were none, the lines all dead, the directionless quiet he called "the first honest silence in years"—but listening for grief, cataloguing it, telling people which of their holes were wounds and which were just absence. "You'll mourn the face you can't picture," he told one woman, "and that's real mourning. But you're not sick. Sick is fixable. Absent is just absent." He was terrible at comfort and tireless at it anyway.

Tam came too, because Maren had appointed him something between page and ward, mostly so he'd have a bed and meals and a reason not to wander back to the spire. He sat through the whole council with his hands folded, memorizing everything, and afterward recited the proceedings back to her word-perfect, including her own pauses.

The question before them was simple and enormous: who ruled?

The king was dead—had been dead for twenty years, in every way that mattered, and finally, mercifully, in the way that counted. There was no heir. Liss was three hundred miles south behind walls Maren had fled, and even if Maren could have reached her, even if her daughter's guardians honored a mother they believed deposed or worse, a child could not hold a country whose gods had just been revealed as predators. There was no parliament worth the name; the old king had dissolved it when he'd begun to change. There was Ossen's apparatus, and Ossen himself had been found at dawn on the temple steps, alive, awake, and hollow—his charisma intact, his purpose gone, sitting amid his flock like a man in a house where all the furniture had been repossessed. He remembered nothing of his sermons. He asked Maren, politely, who she was.

"He's more honest empty than he ever was full," Brannic observed.

The council looked at her. She had been dreading this look since the fire died. It was the look of people who wanted someone else to be responsible, and she knew it intimately, because she'd worn it herself across a border in the snow, and because she'd seen it once more recently, on a man standing in a crown-chamber choosing to forget everything rather than keep a guilt he hadn't earned.

"I don't want it," she said.

"Nobody wants it," Brannic said. "That's the qualification."

She thought about refusing. She had a whole speech prepared about being a fugitive queen, about legitimacy, about how she had run from one crown and wouldn't crawl under another. But Tam was looking at her with his hands folded, and outside the window the smoke from the ledger-fire had long since dispersed but everyone in the city knew who had lit it, and there was a regency-shaped hole in the world and she was the person standing nearest to it.

"A regency," she said finally. "Interim. Written mandate, public terms, a fixed term of years, and the first act is a census of what was taken—not to restore it, that's impossible, but so that no one has to wonder alone what's missing from them. And the second act—" she heard herself say it before she decided to— "is reopening the roads. All of them. No tolls, no pilgrim levies, no gates that answer prayers. Gates that answer nothing but travelers."

"Thornfell," Brannic said softly.

"The passes are open," she said. "By order. Effective today."

* * *

She looked for him among the survivors, though she knew better.

She knew, in the part of her that had held his hand in the choir-dark and watched him walk up into the fire, exactly what the unbinding cost. She had felt the ritual's edge pass through the chamber like a scythe through wheat, and she had understood that the payment was being collected in the only currency the mountain respected. When he'd come down afterward—or rather, when a stranger with his body and his careful hands had come down, polite and unmoored, asking who they all were—she had stood in front of him with her whole history in her mouth and swallowed it.

He had thanked her for the soup. That was the worst of it. He had eaten the soup she made him in the ruins of the gate-house and thanked her the way you thank a stranger, kindly, completely, with nothing underneath.

So she knew better. But knowing better is not the same as not looking, and for six days she looked. She walked the temple district where the hollowed faithful were learning to be people again, scanning faces. She sent inquiries down every road out of the capital. She checked the infirmaries, the caravanserais, the lists of the dead and the departed that clerks were compiling in three different offices with three different systems because no one had ever needed such lists before.

On the sixth day, Tam found her in the map room, staring at the wall of routes with a charcoal circle around Thornfell that she did not remember drawing.

"There's a courier," Tam said. "From the border. He says the crossing at Miller's Ford reopened last week—new keeper, new name on the post, but the old-timers say he ties knots like nobody they've ever seen, and he turned away a slaver caravan yesterday without raising his voice, and—" Tam hesitated, because he was eleven and learning discretion faster than any child should have to. "He keeps a spare room. For travelers with nowhere to go. And he doesn't ask their names unless they offer them."

Maren looked at the map. Three hundred miles of road between the charcoal circle and the capital, and spring coming on, and a regency with a fixed term and a census to build and a country full of people learning their own names again, none of which she could leave, not for months, not yet.

But roads ran both ways. That was the first law of them—Ulyana had taught her that, or Silas had, the smuggler's catechism: *every road you open lets something come home.*

"Courier," she said. "Is he staying the night?"

"Yes, Majesty."

"Then I'll write a letter tonight. Not a summons." She touched the charcoal circle once, lightly, the way you touch a scar. "An invitation. Spring is a good season for learning a new trade. And if he doesn't remember me—" her voice did something she allowed it to do only because Tam was the only one listening, "—then he gets to meet me honestly. Everyone deserves one relationship in their life with no past attached. Maybe this time it's ours."

Tam nodded gravely and went to see the courier fed, and Maren stood a while longer in the map room, in the smell of ink and cold stone and the ghost of burning ledgers, grieving and building in the same breath, because that was the work now—that was all the work there was.

Three hundred miles north, at a crossing with a new name carved on the post, a man with no past was teaching knots to anyone who wanted to learn.

She had time. She had a road.

She had, at last, an honest future to offer him—because she was going to build it first.
