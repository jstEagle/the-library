## 21. A Keeper With No Keys

The ferryman's rope was fraying again.

Tam watched the old man at the crossing work it without looking at it—hands moving through the knots like they were remembering something the rest of him had never learned. A bowline first, then a second loop doubled back on itself, then something Tam had no name for, a hitch that seemed to argue with the rope until the rope agreed. The frayed section ended up wrapped and bound and bearing weight, all in the time it took to say a sentence.

"Where'd you learn that?" Tam asked.

The man looked up. He had gray coming in at his temples and a face weathered like a fence post left out too many winters, and when he smiled it was small and crooked and real. "Don't know," he said. "Hands do it. I just watch."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got." He tested the knot with a hard pull, nodded once, satisfied, and went back to his ledger—a cheap thing, grease-spotted, where he recorded every crossing in a hand so careful it looked printed. *One cart, two horses, toll paid in copper. One woman walking, toll waived — she had a child asleep on her shoulder and I don't charge sleeping children's mothers.*

Tam had been apprenticed to the border crossing for six weeks now, and he still didn't know the keeper's name. Not his real one. The papers in the customs box said *Corren Vale*, but Tam had once caught the old man staring at that name on the page like he was waiting for it to stare back.

"Corren," Tam said now. "There's riders coming down the north road. Three of them. Court colors."

The keeper's hands stopped.

It was a small thing—the pause of a man deciding whether he'd left the stove lit—but Tam had learned to watch for those pauses. They came at odd times. A hymn sung by travelers on the road. The word *Thornfell*. Once, inexplicably, the smell of woodsmoke and tallow, which made the keeper sit down heavily on the ferry bench for a full minute, hands flat on his knees, breathing like a man who had surfaced from deep water.

"Court colors," the keeper repeated. He wiped his palms on his trousers, an old habit from some other life, and stood. "Well. Gates don't open themselves."

* * *

Maren had rehearsed this.

She had rehearsed it in the palace at night, pacing the regency rooms she still thought of as someone else's; on the road south, in inns where nobody knew her face under a merchant's hood; standing at the edge of the Thornfell high country last autumn, where the passes had opened like a fist unclenching and the air smelled of pine and snowmelt and nothing else—no god in it, no hunger, just weather.

She had rehearsed saying his name.

And now she sat a horse ten yards from the border crossing, watching a stranger mend a rope, and every word of the rehearsal turned to ash in her mouth.

Because it wasn't only the face. Faces changed; twenty years had changed hers past recognition by anyone who hadn't loved her. It was the hands. She would have known those hands blindfolded, at the bottom of the sea. Hands that had once taken her elbow at a palace stair because she was six months pregnant and proud and refusing help, and had said nothing about either fact. Hands that had sealed a door on a dying king's order and carried the guilt of it like a stone in the chest for twenty years, and then set the stone down, at the end, so a whole world could keep breathing.

He didn't know her. Of course he didn't. That had been the price, and he had known the price going up the spire stairs, and he had climbed anyway.

"Riders," said the boy beside the keeper—young, sharp-eyed, wearing an apprentice's tabard with the crossing's new mark stitched on it. A simple gate, open, with a road going through. Someone had designed it who understood what gates were for.

"I see them," the keeper said.

Then he looked up, and across ten yards of muddy spring ground, Maren watched him see her.

Nothing happened. That was the terrible part. No flicker, no shadow of memory behind his eyes. He saw a woman in traveling leathers on a good horse with two guards hanging back, and he did what a border keeper does: he came to meet her, ledger under his arm, courteous and unhurried, a man with nothing hidden and nothing found.

"Morning, my lady. Business or passage?"

*Ulyana.* She could have said it. Just the name, and watched whether anything moved in him. Brannic had warned her against it—"You'd be handing a man a sword with no hilt, Majesty. He'd know what it cost him and not why"—and she had argued, and lost, and argued again, and here she was anyway, ten yards away, with his name loaded behind her teeth like a crossbow bolt.

"Passage," she heard herself say. "And information. I'm told the Thornfell road is open again."

"Open since thaw." He said it easily, and then, because he was who he was even with no memory of being anyone: "You've come far. You ride like the horse is borrowed."

That startled a laugh out of her, quick and involuntary, and she saw him register it—not the laugh itself, but something underneath, some tide moving in water too deep to see. His brow creased. Just slightly. Then smoothed.

"Borrowed?" she managed.

"People ride differently on their own horses. Looser." He shrugged. "Bad habit of mine, reading riders. Don't know where I got it."

*You got it smuggling fugitives over the Thornfell passes for twenty years,* she did not say. *You got it reading me, the winter I crossed your line half-frozen in a stolen cloak, and you pretended not to know me then too, and we both let the lie stand because it kept us alive.*

"Toll?" she asked instead.

He consulted the ledger out of habit, though there was nothing to consult. "Two coppers a rider. Waived if you're carrying letters for anyone on the far side—I do that free. Keeps the post honest." A pause, and that small crooked smile. "And I don't charge queens."

Her blood went cold and hot in the same instant. "I beg your pardon?"

"You're sitting like the crown's still on," he said mildly. "Even without it. Like I said—bad habit." He flipped the ledger closed. "Two coppers, my lady, whatever you are."

* * *

She paid the two coppers. Her hands were steady doing it, and she hated how much effort steadiness took.

She should ride on. That had been the whole plan, the plan she'd built out of mercy and cowardice in equal measure and couldn't tell apart anymore: come south, find him, confirm he was alive, confirm he was—well, not *happy*, no one was happy after paying what he'd paid, but *whole*, unhaunted, eating regular meals and sleeping through nights. Confirm it, and leave him to the life he'd bought at the price of everyone in it. That was the deal she'd made with herself on the palace steps the morning the prayer-ledgers burned. *He gave us his past. We owe him a future we stay out of.*

But he was right there. Close enough to touch. And he had called her a queen with a joke in his mouth, and the sound of his voice was doing something to the scar tissue over the place where her daughter's face used to be, and Maren Whitlock—Queen Maren, Regent Maren, the woman who had refused a god's offer of her own child's memory back because accepting it would have fed the thing that ate memories—Maren stood at a border crossing with two coppers' change in her palm and discovered that mercy was much harder at close range.

"The inn here," she said. "Is it decent?"

"Decent's generous. It's dry, and Marta's cooking won't poison you." He hesitated. "I eat there most nights, if you want company that knows which dishes to avoid."

Every rehearsal, burned. Every plan, ash. She heard herself say: "Yes. Thank you. I'd like that."

* * *

They ate barley soup and brown bread at a table by the window, and he talked the way water runs—easily, finding its level. About the crossing, and the flood of ordinary traffic since the passes opened. About the boy, Tam, who was hopeless with accounts and gifted with animals. About the strange peace of the roads these days: *no pilgrims burning offerings, no zealots checking eyes for the shine, people just... going places, like they used to.*

"Do you ever wonder," Maren said, carefully, "where you were? Before this?"

He stirred his soup. Outside the window, Tam was arguing with a mule and losing.

"Sometimes," he said. "Mostly I wonder why I don't wonder more. You'd think it'd gnaw at a man. Twenty-odd years gone, just—blank." He looked up. "But it doesn't. It's like a room with the door painted on the wall. My hands remember things. Knots, gates, how to read a liar, how to splint a leg. But the remembering stops at the wrist, like. There's no *me* back there. Or there is, and he decided I was better off not knocking."

"What makes you think he decided?"

"Because whoever I was, he was thorough." The crooked smile again, but quieter now. "No letters. No scars with stories. Nothing buried where a man buries things when he wants them found later. Either there was nothing worth keeping—" he paused, and Maren watched his hand flatten slowly against the tabletop, the way it had flattened against his knees that day at the smell of woodsmoke— "or everything was worth keeping, and he couldn't carry it."

The soup went cold between them.

"There was a fire," Maren said. "At the end. In the capital. Did you hear of it?"

"Everyone heard of it. Gods died, or whatever you call what happened to them. Half the city woke up hollow and the other half woke up crying." He frowned faintly. "I don't remember hearing of it. Which is strange, isn't it? I remember the year before and the year after. There's just a shape missing, like a tooth gone. When I press on it, I feel—" He shook his head. "Warmth. That's all. Warmth and a lot of names falling."

*Let it gutter.* The words he had spoken while the fire took him apart name by name, and the king had died human in his arms saying *thank you*, and the god had unraveled mid-offer with its bribe still on its lips—*your old life back, all of it, just walk away*—

"You were there," Maren said. It wasn't a question, and she hadn't meant to say it aloud.

His eyes came up. For one moment—one held breath—something moved behind them, vast and drowned, a body surfacing and sinking again. His hand rose halfway toward her across the table, toward some gesture his muscles remembered and his mind did not, and then it stopped, hovering, and settled back around his spoon.

"I dream about a door sometimes," he said quietly. "A big one, black iron, seals all down it. And in the dream I know exactly how to lock it and exactly how to open it, and both knowings hurt." He laughed, short and embarrassed. "Listen to me. You ask a civil question about the capital and I give you dreams. Marta's soup does that. Fills a man's mouth up with things his head doesn't want."

"Corren." His false name in her mouth tasted like coin metal. "If someone could tell you who you were. If they were sitting across from you right now and could give it all back—every name, every year—would you want them to?"

And here was the hinge. Here was the whole chapter of it, balanced on a soup spoon and a border keeper's answer.

He was quiet a long time. Through the window, Tam finally won his argument with the mule, or lost it gracefully; either way, the animal plodded onto the ferry and the boy scratched its ears with real affection.

"No," the keeper said at last.

Maren's breath left her.

"Not because I wouldn't want to know them—them, whoever they were. I'd give a lot for that." He turned the spoon over, studying it. "But whoever I was, he spent himself buying something. I don't know what. But men like the one my hands say I was—they don't spend like that on nothing. And if I take the payment back out of the till, maybe the purchase comes undone." He looked at her then, straight on, and his eyes were clear and kind and utterly empty of her, and it broke her heart cleanly, like ice breaking off a roof in thaw. "So if you're that person, my lady—if you're sitting there holding twenty years of me in your pocket—do me one kindness. Let it stay bought."

* * *

She walked out to the crossing at dusk to pay her respects properly before riding north in the morning. He was showing Tam the evening ritual: chains checked, ferry tied down against wind, the day's ledger squared. Teaching without teaching, the way he must have taught once, at another gate, to another life.

"Safe road north," he said when he saw her. "You'll make Thornfell country in four days if the ford behaves."

"I'm not going to Thornfell." She heard the lie come out smooth and wondered at herself. "I'm going home."

"Well." He inclined his head, courtier-correct, and neither of them remarked on it. "For what it's worth—whatever you're carrying, put it down somewhere warm tonight. Some loads aren't meant for the whole journey."

She almost laughed. Almost wept. He was giving her, out of nothing, out of pure reflexive kindness with no memory behind it, the exact counsel he'd given her on a frozen pass twenty years ago: *some weights you carry, and some you set down at the border and let the mountain hold them awhile.*

"Keeper," she said. "One last question. That gate you dream about. In the dream—when you open it—what's on the other side?"

He considered it honestly, head tilted, the dusk gathering in the lines of his face.

"A friend," he said finally, and sounded surprised by his own answer. "I think there's a friend on the other side, waiting for me to do my job." He smiled, that small crooked realness. "Good thing dreams don't keep records. I'd hate to owe a debt I can't count."

Maren mounted. She rode ten paces, stopped, and looked back once—at the lamplit crossing, the keeper and his apprentice, the gate standing open on an ordinary road where nothing answered prayers anymore except the weather—and she rode on north without telling him, keeping the mercy, carrying the weight, letting the purchase stay bought.

Behind her, in the customs box at the border station, the keeper logged the day's final entry in his careful printed hand:

*One woman riding north, toll paid, refused nothing. Looked at me like she'd known me all her life. Strange. I hope whoever she lost, she finds him again.*

And below it, in the margin, without knowing why, his pen added a single line:

*Spring is coming to Thornfell. Open the gate early.*
