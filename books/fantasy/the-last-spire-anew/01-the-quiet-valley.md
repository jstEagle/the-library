## 1. The Quiet Valley

There are three things every visitor to the Hollow Vale notices within an hour of crossing the mist-line, and none of them is the thing that actually matters.

First: the quiet. Not the absence of sound—the vale is full of sound, of sheep complaining and brooks muttering and old Bram Tuller's dog announcing the arrival of every cloud with the urgency of a man reporting a fire—but the absence of *pressure*. Beyond the mist, the world hums. Here it simply sits.

Second: the bones. Every house in the vale keeps its dead close, in the form of carved memorial bones hung by the door or set above the hearth. A femur etched with lilies for a grandmother. A rib scrolled with the shape of a river for a boy who drowned in one. I make my living from this. I am, as far as anyone knows, the only bone-carver between here and the coast who does not need to be told what to carve.

Third—and this is the one they never notice, because it's mine alone to carry—is that I am a liar.

I have been lying for twenty-two years, which is to say, since Maren pulled me out of the mist at age seven, half-drowned in grief for parents I couldn't remember clearly enough to mourn properly, and asked me if I was hungry. I said yes. It was the first lie I told her, though she'd have disputed the word. She always said a boy who'd walked out of the fog alone had earned the right to decide what he was walking toward.

Here is the truth of it, then, laid out plain the way I lay out tools on my workbench each morning, because I find that starting the day with an honest sentence makes the rest of the dishonesty easier to bear:

I can hear the dead. And in the Hollow Vale, I cannot hear them at all.

* * *

The gift—Maren hated that word; she called it "your ear," as if it were something you might get pierced or cover with a wool cap—works like this. Everywhere else in the world, the dead leave echoes in their remains. Bone holds voice the way wax holds a seal. Press your thumb to a skull that once belonged to someone, and listen long enough, and something listens back. Most people can't do it. The ones who can are called bone-speakers, or worse, depending on the decade and the mood of whatever crown is currently pretending magic doesn't exist while quietly being terrified of it.

In the Hollow Vale, nothing works. That is the entire point of the place. Magic cannot follow past the mist-line. Wards fail. Charms go dull. Gifts fall silent like birds landing on a frozen lake. My ancestors—or somebody's ancestors, anyway, the founders—built the valley as a refuge, a pocket of ordinary where anything touched by the old covenant could live unremarked and unharmed. You give up wonder when you cross the line. In exchange, nobody burns you.

For most of my life I thought that was a fair trade.

I want to be clear about the lying, because I've thought about it a great deal, usually at night, usually staring at the ceiling beams of the workshop Maren built me behind her house. When a grieving widow brings me her husband's knucklebones and asks, "Can you tell me what he wants me to know?"—I don't hear anything. There is no husband in those bones. There is just bone, clean and dumb as driftwood. But I look at the widow, and I see twenty years of marriage in the way she holds them too tightly, and I say, "He wants you to plant the roses he kept meaning to plant," because there were rose thorns scarred into his fingers and a widow who let the garden go, and I have learned to read the living as fluently as I once read the dead.

It works. Gods help me, it works. People weep with gratitude. They leave eggs at my door. They tell me I have been given a holy gift, and I thank them, and inside, something small and honest curls up tighter and says: *you have been given a talent for theft.*

Because that's what it is, isn't it? I take their grief, which belongs to them, and I hand back a story, which belongs to me, and everyone goes home feeling heard. Except nobody has been heard. Not really. Not by me. The dead in the Hollow Vale are as silent as stones, and the living are heard only well enough to be deceived kindly.

Maren figured it out before I did. Mothers do.

"You're not hearing them," she said to me once, when I was maybe sixteen, home from delivering a memorial piece up-valley, glowing with praise. "You're watching us."

"I—" I started, and stopped, because what was there to say?

She was kneading bread. She didn't even look up. "Your grandfather could hear the dead proper. Terrible curse, he called it. Never had a moment's peace outside the mist." Her hands worked the dough like it owed her money. "You came through that fog mute as the rest of us, and I thanked every saint whose name I knew. Then I watched you grow up listening to people the way he listened to bones, and I thought: well. The boy found a quieter church." She finally looked at me. Flour on her cheek. Eyes that missed nothing, ever, which is the occupational hazard of having raised a liar. "Just don't ever start believing your own sermons, Gid. That's how good men turn into frauds."

"I'm already a fraud, Ma."

"No." She went back to the dough. "A fraud takes and gives nothing. You give them the truth about themselves. You just let them think it came from somewhere holier than a boy who pays attention." A pause. "Still. Watch yourself."

That's Maren. She will forgive absolutely everything except self-pity, which she treats the way other people treat spilled lamp oil—as an immediate fire hazard requiring swift action.

* * *

The morning this story properly begins—if stories can be said to begin anywhere, which I doubt, since mine began in the mist twenty-two years ago and has been unwinding slowly ever since—started with rain, as most mornings in the vale do, and with a knock at my workshop door that was too polite to be Bram Tuller and too early to be anyone sensible.

"Come in before you drown," I called.

Widow Corren came in, dripping, holding a cloth bundle against her chest the way you hold something that used to be part of a person. She was perhaps sixty, built like a fence post, face weathered down to essentials. Her husband Hal had died in the autumn, slow, of his lungs, and she'd waited until now—which meant she'd spent the winter rehearsing.

"Bone-carver." Everyone calls me that. Nobody uses my name except Maren, and children I've scolded. "I brought him."

"Set him down where you like." I cleared space on the bench. "Tea?"

"No." She sat anyway, which told me this would take a while. "I don't want tea. I want—I want to know what to do with the rest of it."

"The rest of it?"

"The grief." She said it flatly, like naming a farm animal. "Everyone tells you it gets smaller. It hasn't got smaller. It's just changed shape to fit wherever I put it. So I thought—Hal always said you could talk to the dead, that your carvings weren't decoration. He believed it. Half the vale believes it. So I'm asking you straight, carver, and I'll know if you're soft-soaping me: is it true?"

And there it was. Twenty-two years of careful architecture, and one widow with rain in her hair asking the load-bearing question.

I looked at her husband's bundle. A knucklebone, two finger joints, a small piece of skull—she'd chosen well, whoever advised her. Good material. If we'd been anywhere else on this green and suffering earth, I'd have pressed my thumb to that skull fragment and waited, and something would have come: a smell, a phrase, an irritation, a love. The dead are rarely eloquent. They're mostly fragments themselves—Hal Corren would probably have been mostly smoke and the memory of tobacco and one strong image, maybe his wife's hands, maybe his garden.

Here, I pressed my thumb to the bone and got cold bone, and silence so total it rang.

"He was a patient man," I said, "but he hated being fussed over."

Corren's chin came up. "How could you possibly—"

"Your hands. You keep almost reaching for the bundle and stopping yourself. You've been hovering over his remains for months, haven't you? Talking to them?" I smiled. "He'd have told you to stop mollycoddling him and eat something hot."

Her face did three things in quick succession—offense, recognition, and the collapse of both into grief—and then she was crying, hard, the ugly relieved kind, and I poured her tea after all, because she wasn't going anywhere now.

What I gave her, over the next hour, was a reading of her own heart dressed in her husband's clothes. The roses Hal kept meaning to plant—no wait, that was the other widow; Hal's was the bees, he'd wanted bees for thirty years and Corren had vetoed them twice a year since the wedding. "He wants the bees," I told her. "He wants you to stop being frightened of things that sting. That's all he ever wanted."

"That's not all he wanted," she said, wiping her nose. "But it'll do for a start."

She left with her bones carved—latticework, honeycomb pattern, a queen bee at the crown—and left eggs, and left me standing in my doorway feeling like a man who has just picked a stranger's pocket and handed the wallet back with a bow.

Maren found me that evening sitting on the workshop step, whittling nothing in particular.

"Corren's bees," she said, sitting down beside me without asking, as she owned the step and half the valley besides. "Half the village is talking about it. Old Tam swears you spoke to Hal himself."

"Old Tam swears a lot of things."

"Mm." She was quiet a moment. The rain had stopped; the vale was doing its evening trick of turning gold and green at once, sheep going soft-edged in the light. It is, I want to state for the record, a beautiful place. I have made myself believe that completely, most days. "Does it ever bother you, Gid? Doing it that way?"

"You've asked me that every year since I was sixteen."

"And you've given me a different answer every year. Which tells me you still haven't got one."

I turned the blank wood in my hands. "It bothers me less than the alternative."

"Which is?"

"Which is leaving." I said it lightly, and it didn't come out lightly. "Ma, everything I am is built on this. On being useful here, on the lie holding, on nobody ever asking the wrong question at the wrong time. If the vale stops working—if I lose this place—I'm just a fraud with nowhere to practice."

"You're not a fraud."

"You said—"

"I said watch yourself. Different thing." She took the blank wood out of my hands, inspected it, and handed it back, which is how she hugs people. "You're a boy who walked out of the fog and made himself a life in the one place on earth where his curse can't reach him. That's not fraud. That's sense. Your grandfather would have traded a leg for it." She stood, knees cracking like green branches. "Supper's in an hour. Wash the blood off your hands, you've been carving again without gloves."

"I have not—"

"You always say that. You always have blood on your cuffs." She paused at the door, silhouetted against the last gold light, and for a moment she looked old, older than she ever lets herself look indoors. "Gid. Whatever happens—whatever comes—we're fine, you and I. Remember that. Whatever comes."

It struck me as an odd thing to say. But Maren said odd things; she'd earned the right decades ago, hauling a strange child out of the mist and deciding, against all evidence, that he was worth keeping.

* * *

I want to record the ordinary things now, before I forget how ordinary felt. This may be the last chance I get to describe the Hollow Vale as it was, and I find I want to get it right.

Spring in the vale smells like wet earth and lamb dung and Maren's chives. The elders meet in the meeting-house on the first Tuesday of the month and argue about drainage with a passion most nations reserve for war. Bram Tuller's dog has opinions about the moon. Children dare each other to touch the mist-line, and every adult pretends not to notice, and every adult also remembers doing it, and knows the rule: you can stand at the edge and feel the hum of the wider world like heat off a forge, but you do not cross, because the world beyond remembers what you are.

I deliver carvings on Thursdays, up and down the valley, and people wave, and I wave back, and it is a small life. I have told myself for twenty-two years that small is safe and safe is enough, and for twenty-two years I have mostly believed it, in the way you believe a roof holds—not by proof, but by not looking up.

On the Thursday after Widow Corren's bees, I delivered a memorial rib to the Fenwick family and stopped, on the walk home, at the top of the ridge where the mist-line runs along the valley's eastern lip like a held breath. I do this sometimes. Everyone does, eventually. You stand there and you feel it—that faint pressure, that hum, the sense of an enormous room just beyond a thin wall. The whole world, humming with everything the vale was built to shut out. Dragons dying somewhere far off—I'd heard travelers' rumors of that, guttering fires, empty skies. Crowns hunting people like me. Dead things whispering in their bones, waiting for ears.

Waiting for my ears.

I stood there longer than usual that day. Long enough that my fingers went cold. And I remember thinking, with a clarity that should have frightened me more than it did: *one day, something is going to come through that mist. One day the wall won't hold, or I won't want it to.*

Then I walked home, washed the blood off my cuffs, and ate supper with the woman who saved me, and told her about the Fenwicks' rib, and laughed at Bram Tuller's dog, and went to bed in the silence I had built my whole self around, brick by patient brick.

Three nights later, a traveler crossed the mist-line carrying a keepsake in her pack.

And for the first time in twenty-two years, in the middle of the Hollow Vale, in the one place on earth where my gift could not follow—

I heard the dead speak.
