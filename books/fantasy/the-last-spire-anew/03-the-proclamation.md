## 3. The Proclamation

I have lived in the Hollow Vale for fifty-one years, and in that time I have learned exactly three things worth teaching anyone.

First: bread rises poorly in the mist, so you knead it angrier than usual and let the anger do the work.

Second: never trust a man who says he has nothing against your neighbors right before he lists what he has against your neighbors.

Third: peace is not a thing you find. Peace is a thing people sell each other, usually with someone else paying the price.

I was thinking about all three of them the morning the crown riders came down the mist-line road, because I'd been baking, and my loaves were rising badly, and I am an old woman who takes her lessons where she finds them.

* * *

They came at midmorning, six of them on grey horses that looked as though they had been bred specifically to disappoint children. Their armor was black lacquer over steel, unornamented except for the crown's sigil — a tower within a circle, which I have always thought was a strange thing to be proud of. A tower is where you lock things up. A circle is how you keep them from getting out. Put together, it's less a heraldry than a confession.

The village square filled up the way water fills a cellar — slowly, then all at once, and everyone pretending they just happened to be passing through. Old Hesk with his walking stick. The Aldery twins, who hadn't spoken to each other since the incident with the goat, standing shoulder to shoulder now because fear makes better glue than blood ever did. Young Pellia with her baby, who was too small yet to know he should cry.

I stood near the bakehouse door with flour still on my apron, because I am not the sort of woman who runs inside when trouble arrives. Trouble has a way of following you indoors anyway, and I'd rather meet it in daylight with witnesses.

Their captain dismounted. Not Vane — I didn't learn that name until later, and by then I would have given a great deal to forget it. This one was called Brannock, a broad man with a voice like gravel poured into a bucket, and he carried a scroll with more wax seals on it than sense.

"By order of the Crown Regent," he read, "and pursuant to the Edict of Containment, all persons found to bear the old covenant's taint — the hearing of dead voices, the sight of distant things, the mending of flesh by will alone, or any other gift of the sundered age — shall be surrendered to the Crown's hunt within thirty days of this decree."

He said it the way men read tax notices. That was the worst part. Not cruelty. Boredom.

"Persons so surrendered shall be conveyed to Ironhold for assessment." He paused, and here he did look up, and I will grant him this much: he knew what he was doing when he chose his next words. "Assessment is not execution. Cooperating communities will be left in peace. Communities that shelter the gifted—" he rolled the scroll shut like a man closing a coffin lid "—will not."

Silence in the square. The baby finally cried, one thin complaint, and Pellia hushed it, and somehow that small sound broke something open in the crowd, because everyone started talking at once, low and fast, the way people talk when they're trying not to hear themselves.

Hesk raised his stick. "What does 'assessment' mean?"

Brannock smiled. It was a well-practiced smile, the kind that goes nowhere near the eyes. "It means we determine whether the gift is active or dormant. Dormant cases are released back to their communities."

"And if it's active?" someone asked — Dara Quill, the farrier's widow, who lost her husband to a mine collapse and has been angry at the world ever since in a way I privately respect.

Brannock's smile stayed perfectly in place. "Active cases are retained for study."

Nobody asked what *retained* meant. That's the trick of it, see. They don't lie to you. They just choose words soft enough that lying isn't necessary, and then they let you do the rest yourself, because people will always build themselves the gentlest possible meaning out of whatever terrible materials are offered. We stood there in our square and told ourselves *retained* probably meant something survivable, and every single one of us knew better, and we did it anyway.

Brannock nailed the decree to the notice post beside the well — three nails, unhurried, taking his time the way men do when they want you to watch — mounted his disappointing horse, and rode back up toward the mist-line without another word.

The square emptied slowly after that, everyone drifting off in pairs, murmuring. And I stood by my bakehouse door and thought: thirty days. Thirty days before this valley decides what it is.

I gave us three.

* * *

The elders convened that evening in the meeting hall, and I attended, because I've sat on that council twice myself and I know how its mind works. The hall smelled of tallow smoke and the particular staleness of rooms where difficult decisions get made regularly enough that the difficulty has worn grooves into the furniture.

There are nine elders. I'll not name them all; some of them were decent people making an indecent choice, and I try to hold those two truths apart, though they kept sliding together like wet hands.

Elder Coram spoke first. He's the miller, a careful man with careful hands and a face that always looks like it's apologizing for something it hasn't done yet. "We have no gift-bearers among us," he said. "That's the whole point of the Vale. Magic doesn't follow across the mist-line. Nothing touched by the old ways survives here."

"That's not quite true," said Elder Sef, who keeps the records, "is it, Coram? We all know it's not quite true."

And there it was. The room went very quiet, the way rooms do when everyone suddenly becomes aware of the same person sitting among them.

You should understand something about the Hollow Vale, and about me. Forty years ago, I came here carrying a boy who wasn't mine — a bone-carver's apprentice, half-starved, whose gift had made him a target even as a child. The valley took us in. That was the promise of this place: magic cannot follow past the mist-line, and neither can the fear of it. Here, Gideon Halloran could carve memorial bones for grieving families with his own two hands and hear nothing, be nothing, want nothing but bread and quiet. Here, his silence was safety.

We built a whole life on that promise. I baked. He carved. He pretended, for the sake of the families who needed comfort, that he could hear their dead — and I let him, because grief is a hungry thing and kindness sometimes has to wear a mask. But the truth was that in the Vale his gift fell mute, and he had made his home inside that muteness, and I had helped him build it, stone by stone, year by year.

So yes. We all knew.

"The traveler's keepsake," Sef said into the silence. "Last month. The bone token that crossed the border with the peddler from Harrow Fen. You remember the fuss. The boy went white as chalk and couldn't finish the carving."

"He was ill," Coram said quickly. Too quickly.

"He heard something," Sef said. "In the Vale. Where nothing can be heard."

Another elder — Tammas, the smith, big hands folded on the table — said quietly, "If word gets out the Vale is harboring a gifted man, we lose the protection of compliance. Brannock's riders come back. And they won't come back six strong."

"They might not come back at all," said Elder Wren, the herbwife, oldest of us and least impressed by anything. She said it flatly, without hope, the way you state the weather. "Or they might. The edict says *surrendered or hunted.* Those are the choices on offer. Neither one includes leaving him be."

"We could warn him," someone said — young Elder Cass, barely forty, who had been a girl when Gideon first arrived. "Give him time to run. Tell no one else."

"Warn him and we're sheltering him," Tammas said. "A warning is a kindness, but it's also complicity. If the crown learns we knew and let him go—"

"Then let the crown learn," Wren snapped. "Forty years that boy has carved memorials for our dead. For yours, Tammas. Your wife's marker has his work on it. Half the graves on the hillside do. And now we weigh his life against our comfort and call it governance?"

"It isn't comfort," Coram said, and his careful face finally cracked enough to show the misery underneath. "It's the whole vale. Two hundred souls. Children. If we hide him and they find out, we burn for it — maybe literally. I've read what the hunt does to sheltering villages. Have any of you? Because I have." His hands shook slightly. "I won't feed two hundred people into that fire to save one man. God forgive me, but I won't."

And that was the vote, more or less. Comply. Send word to the riders that the Vale holds no gifted persons — technically true, if you squinted, since the Vale's whole nature was supposed to make such a claim honest — and accept whatever inspection followed. No one said the word *surrender* aloud. No one had to. Compliance meant opening our doors, letting the hunt walk our lanes, and letting them find whatever they found.

Seven votes for. Cass voted to warn him. Wren voted for nothing, saying only, "I'll not put my hand to either sin," which is the most useful thing cowardice ever said.

I walked home in the dark with my lantern held steady and my heart anything but, and I thought about the third lesson again. Peace is a thing people sell each other. Tonight my neighbors sold theirs cheaply — for the price of a signature, a rider's nod, the chance that the wolf would eat someone else first.

I wasn't angry. That surprised me most of all. I had expected fury and found only a cold, clear sadness, like looking down a well. Because I understood them, you see. That was the unforgivable part. Given their fear and their arithmetic, I understood every vote in that room except my own conscience's.

* * *

Gideon was working when I got home. He always worked evenings, the lamp turned low, his knives moving over the pale lengths of bone with the patience of a man who has made friends with time itself. He was finishing a memorial piece for the Ferrow family — their daughter, taken by fever last spring — and I stood in the doorway watching him and felt something in my chest go tight and strange.

He looked up. He always knows when I'm in a room; forty years will do that. "Bread rise badly again?"

"Terribly. I kneaded it furious, all the same."

"Then it'll be good bread." He smiled, set down his knife, and studied me the way he does — that long, level look, as though I were a piece of bone and he could read the grain of me. "Maren. What happened tonight?"

Straight to it, as always. The boy has never once let me soften a hard thing for him, which I have resented and admired in equal measure for four decades.

"There was a decree posted today," I said. "Crown riders. An edict of containment. All persons bearing the old gifts are to be surrendered to the hunt within thirty days."

His hand went flat on the table, very still. "Here?"

"Here. In the Vale. Where magic can't follow." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Apparently the crown's reach is longer than the mist."

"What did the elders say?"

And there it was — the question under the question, because Gideon Halloran is many things but he is not a fool, and he had already done the arithmetic I had done. I could have lied. Heaven knows I considered it. But I watched his hands on that table, hands that had given this valley forty years of beautiful, patient work, and I could not insult them with a lie.

"They voted to comply," I said. "To declare the Vale clean and admit the hunt for inspection. Seven to two." I swallowed. "No one said your name. But everyone in that room was thinking it."

He nodded slowly. Not surprise — I think that was the hardest thing to watch. He had expected it. Somewhere in the years of his contentment, he had known it was rented, not owned.

"Well," he said, after a while. "It's a fair bargain, from where they sit. Two hundred lives against one carver." His mouth twisted. "I'd have made the same trade. Probably. Maybe."

"You would not."

"I'd like to think I wouldn't." He picked his knife back up and turned it over, not carving anything, just holding it. "Thirty days."

"Yes."

"That's generous of them."

"Gideon—"

"Maren." He looked at me, and his eyes were calm, and that calm frightened me more than panic would have. "It's all right. I've had a good life. Better than I had any right to expect."

"No," I said, and my voice came out harder than I intended, hard enough that it startled us both. "No, you listen to me, Gideon Halloran. I did not carry you across half the country on my back when you were eleven years old and skin over bone, I did not bury three husbands and outlast two floods and one fire to raise you into a man worth ten of any of those elders — I did not do all that so you could sit in this kitchen and negotiate the terms of your own surrender like it's a fair market price. You will pack. Tonight. Quietly, while the valley sleeps, and you will go up through the north pastures before dawn, and you will cross the mist-line if you have to, God help us both."

He stared at me. "Cross the line? Maren, out there my gift comes back. Everything I buried—"

"Better a loud world than a short one."

For a moment he said nothing, and the lamp guttered between us, and outside the wind moved through the pines the way it always does, indifferent and ancient. Then he set down the knife, carefully, the way he sets everything down, as if even tools deserve gentleness.

"All right," he said softly. "All right, Mother."

I went to fetch the satchel while he doused the lamp.

* * *

Packing a life into a leather bag is a brutal kind of arithmetic. I took the wool coat, the good one. The whetstone. His father's — the carver's, I mean, the man who trained him — folding knife, because a boy should carry something of the hands that made him. Bread, cheese, dried apples wrapped in cloth. The little tin of salt I bless every autumn, out of habit rather than faith; I'm not sure I believe in blessings anymore, but I believe in salt, which has never once lied to me.

At the bottom of his chest I found the thing I both hoped for and dreaded: the unfinished keepsake. The traveler's bone token, the one from Harrow Fen, the one that had made him go white as chalk in front of half the village. A woman's name carved along its edge. He'd hidden it instead of burning it, which told me everything I needed to know about the state of my son's heart. He'd heard something. Out loud, in the Vale, where the dead are supposed to sleep — and instead of running from it, he'd tucked it away in the dark like a candle he was afraid to admit he wanted lit.

I held it a long moment. Then I wrapped it in flannel and put it deep in the satchel, because whatever frightened him about it, it was also the first thing in twenty years that had made him look alive in a way his quiet valley never quite managed. Some fires you don't put out. Some fires you hand a coat and point them at the road.

I added the small carving of Maren-and-boy — no, forgive me, I mean the carving *he'd* made, of a woman and a boy on a doorstep, done when he was fourteen and still clumsy with faces. It's ugly and I love it beyond reason. Into the satchel it went, wrapped in my winter shawl, because if he must carry nothing else, he'll carry proof he was loved by somebody with no arithmetic in her at all.

By the time I finished, the house had gone very quiet, and I realized I could hear hooves on the road.

Not many. Four, perhaps five, riding hard, no lanterns. Coming down from the mist-line at an hour when no honest traveler rides.

Thirty days, the decree said. I had given the elders three days before the valley's resolve cracked. I had assumed the crown would honor its own paperwork.

I stood at the window with the satchel heavy in my hand and watched the dark shapes of riders crest the hill above the north pasture — *his* road, the escape road, the one I'd planned around all evening — and I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that someone in the valley had not waited for the vote to be counted before sending word.

The community that sheltered him had sold him. And someone had hurried ahead to collect payment early.

I blew out the lamp, slung the satchel across my body, and went to wake my son.
