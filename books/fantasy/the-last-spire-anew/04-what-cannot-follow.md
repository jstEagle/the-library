## 4. What Cannot Follow

The trouble with living in a valley where nothing magical can survive is that you start to believe the world will be polite about it. That the border holds. That the mist-line is a wall with a door on your side only.

I had spent thirty-one years making that mistake. It took Serah Vane one morning to correct it.

* * *

I want to say I saw it coming. I want to tell you I woke that dawn with my gift humming a warning through the floorboards, some spectral alarm bell tolling. But the Hollow Vale doesn't work that way. In the Vale, I am just a man. A carver of memorial bones with good hands and a habit of talking to himself, which the neighbors find charming because they don't know he's rehearsing conversations he'll never have with voices that can't reach him here.

So no warning. Just Maren banging around the kitchen before sunrise, burning porridge the way she always did, insisting it was "caramelized" the way she always did, and me sitting at the table sharpening my chisels because a bone-carver's hands go strange if they're idle too long.

"You'll take the Corrin order out today," she said. "Old woman Corrin wants her husband's name on the femur piece, and she'll stand over your shoulder and correct your lettering if you let her."

"She has every right. I spelled 'beloved' wrong last time."

"You did not. You spelled it the old way and she pretended not to know there was an old way." Maren set a bowl in front of me. Porridge, caramelized. She was a broad woman gone soft at the edges, gray coming in at her temples like frost on a window, and she had raised me from a foundling nobody wanted and never once—not once—made me feel like a debt being paid down. "Gideon. Look at me."

I looked at her. That should have been my warning. Maren only asked you to look at her when what she had to say needed a face to land on.

"Riders came through Millbrook two nights ago. Posting proclamations. The elders voted yesterday while you were at Brannoc's farm." She said it plainly, the way she said everything, as though plainness could make a thing less terrible. "They've decided to comply. Names surrendered. Anyone touched by the covenant goes to the crown."

The chisel stopped in my hand. "Touched by the covenant," I repeated. "That's a phrase for other people. For hedge-witches in stories."

"It's a phrase for you." She sat down across from me and took both my hands in hers, chisels and all, which hurt, and she didn't care. "You hear the dead outside this valley. You told me when you were nine years old, crying under the workbench because a dead man wouldn't stop asking you to carry a message to his wife in Lowmarsh. Do you think I forgot? Do you think I ever once thought less of you for it?"

"I hoped," I said. My voice came out smaller than I meant it to. "It's been quiet so long. I thought maybe the valley had cured me."

"The valley didn't cure you, love. The valley deafened them. There's a difference." She squeezed my hands. "The riders are coming here next. Elders gave them our names yesterday evening. Yours was third on the list, behind the Tarrow sisters, who I'm told are only guilty of knowing which herbs stop bleeding, which by the crown's arithmetic makes them witches, and by mine makes them the reason half this valley still has all its children."

"When do they come?"

"That's the thing." Her jaw set. "Millbrook's riders were due here tomorrow. But there's a hunt-captain attached to this sweep now. Vane, they call her. And word from the pass is she doesn't wait for schedules."

I remember laughing. I'm not proud of it. It was the laugh of a man whose body understood danger before his mind agreed to, a short ugly sound, and Maren didn't laugh with me. She stood, went to the corner chest, and began pulling things out: bread wrapped in cloth, cheese, a flint, my father's—her husband's, my foster-father's—old knife, dry socks.

"Maren."

"Pack your satchel."

"There's nowhere to *go*. Outside the line, the whole world's hunting people like me, and inside the line—" I gestured at the walls that had held me safe my whole life, and heard how hollow the gesture sounded even as I made it.

"Outside the line, you're alive," she said. "That's the entire argument. I've considered alternatives and found none worth the ink."

* * *

We might have made it. I want you to understand that. If Vane had kept her schedule, if the elders' messenger had ridden faster or slower, if any one of a dozen small mercies had landed differently—we'd have walked out through the north pasture before first light and this chapter of my life would be about walking, not running.

Instead, hoofbeats on the lane at midmorning. Not many. Four horses, moving unhurried, which frightened me more than a gallop would have. Hurried men doubt themselves. Unhurried men know exactly what they've come for.

Maren looked out the window, and whatever she saw in her face, I saw reflected: four riders, three in crown black, and a woman in front on a gray mare with a sword worn like it had grown there.

"The back gate," Maren said. "Now. Through the hazel scrub, down to the mist-line. Don't stop for anything. Don't look back for anyone."

"I'm not leaving you to answer their questions alone."

"Gideon." She caught my sleeve, and her grip was iron, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady at the same time, which is a trick I have never learned and suspect requires either sainthood or motherhood. "You are the only thing in this house they cannot replace. Go."

There's a version of this story where I argue longer. Where heroism looks like standing on the doorstep refusing. I've thought about that version a great deal since, and I've come to believe it's a coward's fantasy dressed in brave clothes—the fantasy that staying would have saved her, when everything about the morning said otherwise. Four armed riders don't leave empty-handed because one carver makes a speech. Staying would have bought nothing except two names for the list instead of one.

So I went out the back gate like she told me. And I hate myself a little every day for how easily my legs obeyed.

* * *

The hazel scrub runs down behind the tannery to where the pasture gives way to the mist-line—a seam of pale fog maybe twenty paces wide that never burns off, never freezes, never moves. Children dare each other to touch it. Farmers plow up to its edge and no further, the way you'd respect a sleeping animal. Beyond it lies the rest of the world, the loud world, the one I'd only ever known secondhand.

Behind me, the front door of my mother's house opened.

I was forty paces into the scrub. I could see the mist ahead, gray-white and patient. And I did the thing she told me not to do. I looked back.

Vane stood in the doorway. Tall, dark-haired, armor dulled to kill the shine, and she was looking past Maren—at the kitchen, at the chest hanging open, at the evidence of a hasty packing—and then her eyes found me through the hazel branches, and I watched something move across her face. Recognition, maybe. Or arithmetic.

"Halloran," she called. Not shouted. Carried, the way a voice carries when it's used to rooms full of men going quiet. "Bone-carver. Stop there and this stays civil."

Maren stepped into the doorway between us. All five foot two of her, apron still dusted with flour, planting herself like she was a wall instead of a woman.

"He's not here," Maren said.

I have replayed the next minute more times than I've slept soundly since. Vane dismounted. She walked toward the doorway. She said something I couldn't hear—I was already moving again, crashing downslope, thorns raking my arms—and then Maren's voice rose, clear across the pasture, pitched deliberately high so I couldn't miss it:

"RUN, BOY! DON'T YOU DARE STOP FOR ME!"

And then a sound I won't write down. Not because it's unspeakable, but because writing it would make it small, and it wasn't small. It was the largest thing that has ever happened inside my skull.

I ran. The mist took me at a dead sprint, cold as well-water, thick as wool, and for three strides I was blind and drowning in white—

—and then the world's dam broke.

* * *

Here is what I cannot make anyone understand who hasn't lived it. The Hollow Vale had not cured me. It had been holding a finger against a wound for thirty-one years, and the moment I crossed the line, the finger lifted.

They came in like a flood through a burst levee. Hundreds. Thousands. Voices without bodies pressing in from every direction at once—an old man sobbing a child's name over and over; a soldier reciting his regiment like a prayer; a drowned girl singing a lullaby in a key that does not exist; ten, fifty, a hundred murmurs braiding into a roar that had no air to travel through and therefore traveled directly into the marrow of me.

I fell. Of course I fell. I went down into the wet grass on the far side of the mist-line with my hands over my ears, which was useless, which was like holding your hands over your ears underwater to keep out the sea, and I screamed, and the dead screamed with me, or near me, or through me, and somewhere in the middle of it I understood that I was hearing the truth about the world for the first time: that it is crowded. That the ground everywhere is standing room only. That every road I would ever walk was paved over the restless.

*Get up.*

Not one of theirs. Mine—or hers. Maren's voice, remembered, arriving through the noise like a coal carried cupped in bare hands.

*Run, boy. Don't you dare stop for me.*

So I got up. I ran until the scrub ended and the pine forest began, ran until the roar resolved from chaos into currents I could almost navigate, ran until dark, and then I lay in the roots of a lightning-split fir with my satchel for a pillow and shook for a long time.

The dead talked all night. Some begged. Some raged. One, very close, very patient, simply repeated: *"Tell the warden. Tell the warden. Tell the warden."* I didn't know who the warden was. I didn't know anything yet. I knew exactly one thing, and I held it the way you hold a coal in winter—carefully, painfully, because letting go means losing the only warmth there is.

She died saying my name. Not the crown's law. Not surrender. My name, and the word *run*, and the word *dare*, stitched together into a sentence that was, I understood finally, lying there in the dark with a thousand strangers' ghosts murmuring over me, the last lesson she had left to teach: that some things matter more than survival, and she had spent her life teaching me which ones, and she would be furious—furious—if I wasted her purchase on grief.

So I made her a promise, there in the roots, in the dark, in the roar. I promised her I would find whoever killed the peace she'd given her life to buy. I promised her I'd find out why the crown hunts its own people, why the dead are so loud, why the dragons are dying—because even in the Vale we'd heard that rumor, dragon-fire guttering like a candle in wind, and now, outside the line, I could *feel* it, a wrongness in the world's bones, a silence where a great warmth should be.

And I promised her I would not stop. Not for soldiers, not for fear, not for the thousand voices telling me I was too small.

The coal burned in my chest all night. By morning it had hardened into something with edges. Something you could build a purpose on.

I got up, and I walked south, because south felt like away, and the dead came with me whether I wanted them or not—murmuring, mourning, and one of them, patient above all the others, still saying:

*"Tell the warden. Tell the warden."*

"All right," I said aloud, to the empty pines, to the crowd no one else could see. "All right. Who in the hells is the warden?"

The forest, naturally, declined to answer.

But behind me, far off on the road I'd left, I heard—or imagined I heard—hoofbeats. One horse. Unhurried. Following.
