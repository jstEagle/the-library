## 5. The Huntress Who Quit

There are three things every witch-hunter learns in their first year of service, and none of them are written down anywhere.

First: the gifted rarely look like monsters. They look like grandmothers and shepherds and the girl who sells you bread, which is inconvenient, because it's much easier to burn a monster.

Second: the paperwork is worse than the killing. Every capture requires a Manifestation Report, a Lineage Inquiry, a Confession Ledger entry, and three signatures from men who have never once left Ironhold. I have filled out ledgers describing women who could coax milk from dry goats and boys who dreamed true dreams twice a season. The Crown calls this threat assessment. I call it what it is: arithmetic done by cowards.

Third — and this is the one they never say aloud, though every hunter over the age of thirty knows it in their bones like an old fracture — sooner or later, you will be ordered to do something that you will spend the rest of your life explaining to yourself at four in the morning.

Mine came on the ninth day of Harvestmonth, in a village called Ashenbrook, which before that morning had been called simply Brook.

* * *

I want to be precise about Ashenbrook, because precision is all I have left, and because the official report — filed under my name, Serah Vane, Captain Third Rank — contains eleven lies and one omission, and the omission is me.

The intelligence said a hedge-coven operated out of the millhouse. The intelligence was wrong; the "coven" was seven families who had paid a wandering bone-charmer to bless their wells against rot. The charmer himself had left two weeks prior. There were no manifestations. There was no magic. There was a millhouse full of people who had made the catastrophic decision to be home when Lieutenant Dross's company arrived with torches and an order my commander had signed without reading past the village name.

I gave the order to stand down. Dross countermanded it, citing Directive Nine, which permits immediate destruction when "resistance to inquiry is encountered." The resistance we encountered was a miller asking if he could fetch his wife from the loft first.

I stood in the yard and watched the fire take the roof, and I did not draw my sword, and I did not weep, and I did not stop it. All three failures live in me now like swallowed stones. Forty-one dead. Two escaped into the tree-line — a man and a boy, running north toward the Hollow Vale.

Afterward, Dross clapped me on the shoulder and said, "Hard duty, Captain. But the law is the law." And I looked at this man — competent, obedient, empty as a drum — and understood that the law was whatever the loudest coward in the room said it was, and that I had spent nineteen years sharpening myself into its instrument.

That night I took my horse, my kit, and one unauthorized item — the confession ledger page for Ashenbrook, which I did not sign — and I rode west instead of east.

Desertion, formally. Though I prefer the term the old hunters used, back when the hunt still pretended to have a soul: *going quiet.* You stop hunting. You start looking.

* * *

Here is what I know about Gideon Halloran, bone-carver of the Hollow Vale, which by then was the most wanted name on the Crown's rolls outside of actual dragons:

He had fled the valley nine days after the proclamation, in the company — or so the trail suggested — of no one. His adoptive mother, Maren Okonkwo, was dead at the mist-line, killed delaying Hunt-Captain Voss's riders. That detail mattered to me more than I admitted even to myself. Voss's report described her death as "regrettable but necessary." Reports always find a way to make necessity sound like weather.

The man himself was traveling with a hollow boy — one of the branded exiles, maybe twelve years old — moving north by night, sleeping in root cellars and shrine ruins. He wasn't running well. He was running like a man who had never needed to run before, which meant he'd spent his whole life inside the one place the hunt couldn't follow, and now the whole world had teeth.

And there was the other thing. The thing that had gotten my superiors excited in a way I found obscene.

Every hunter knows the stories about the vale-born. Magic doesn't cross the mist-line; it can't. Whatever covenant the old world made, the Hollow Vale sits outside its reach entirely — a pocket of silence where gifts go mute and the Crown's engines read nothing. Which meant Halloran had lived forty-odd years without ever using his gift, or perhaps without knowing he had one. And now he was out in the living world, where magic worked, carrying whatever it was the Crown feared enough to spend companies of riders on.

The dispatches called him "a vessel of unquantified capability."

I called him a man whose mother had died buying him ninety seconds, and I intended to find out what was worth ninety seconds of Maren Okonkwo.

* * *

I caught up to them on the eleventh day, at a ruined wayshrine below the Grey Fells — one of those old covenant-shrines the Church pretends predate the Church, all mossy arches and carvings worn to suggestion. He'd chosen it badly, or desperately; there was only one approach, and I'd been reading tracks since before Dross could ride.

I came in at dusk, alone, sword sheathed, hands visible. This confused him immediately, which told me something. A guilty man expects a blade. He looked at my empty hands like they were the trick.

"Don't," he said. His voice was low and roughened, a carver's voice. He put himself between me and the boy, which cost him something — I could see the cost in how carefully he moved. "There's nothing here worth your warrant."

"There's a great deal here worth my warrant," I said. "That's rather the problem."

Behind him, the boy — Tam, I'd learn later, the brand still raw on his cheekbone, picked at by anxious fingers — held a carving knife in a grip that would get him disarmed and worse in any real fight. I ignored him, professionally. Mostly.

"Captain Vane," I said. "Third Rank, Crown Hunt. Formerly." I let the word sit there. "You're Gideon Halloran."

"I'm a bone-carver."

"You're both. That's why I'm here." I sat down on a fallen lintel, deliberately, the way you sit when you want a frightened man to remember that sitting is a thing bodies do. "I'm not going to draw. I'm not going to call for my company, because I don't have a company anymore. I burned my last bridge in Ashenbrook, and if you don't know what happened at Ashenbrook, I'll tell you, because you deserve to hear it from someone who stood in the yard and let it happen."

So I told him. Forty-one dead. No coven, no craft, blessed wells. I told him about the miller and his wife in the loft. I kept my voice level because that was the only penance available to me in that moment: to deliver the truth without flinching from it, like handing someone a hot iron and saying *hold this, it's yours too now.*

Halloran listened the way stone listens. But his eyes — dark, watchful, tired past his years — never left mine, and I saw the moment my report landed somewhere behind them. Not horror. Recognition.

"My mother died the same week," he said quietly. "Same kind of order. 'Regrettable but necessary.'"

"I've written that phrase," I said. "It means nothing. It's what we write when the truth would take too many pages."

* * *

Here is what I had actually come to ask, and it took me until the fire was lit — I built it small, and I asked permission first, which startled him again — before I could get it out:

"What are you?"

Because the reports said *unquantified,* and quantification was supposed to be my trade. Nineteen years of assessing manifestations, and I had learned to smell the truth of a gift the way farriers smell lameness in a horse. Most of the gifted, when cornered, either performed — little sparks, little charms, desperate proof of their own smallness — or denied everything and made you dig. Both told you what you needed.

Halloran did neither. He considered the question honestly, which unnerved me more than any performance could have.

"I carve memorial bones," he said finally. "For the grieving. Names, dates, sometimes a likeness, into sheep-bone or ox-bone. It's honest work. In the vale, it's just work." A pause. His hand went to his satchel — a reflex, protective — and stopped. "Out here it isn't. Out here I can hear them."

"Hear whom?"

"The dead." He said it plainly, without drama, and the plainness was what convinced me, because liars decorate. "In the valley, nothing. Silence. I thought — I told myself the gift was gone, or that it had never really been anything. A carver's fancy. Then a traveler's keepsake crossed the border last spring and a woman's voice came out of it begging me to warn someone called the warden, and I haven't slept properly since." He laughed, short and bitter. "Since I crossed the mist-line, it's been — there are so many of them, Captain. Everywhere. Roads are *loud.* Battlefields are unbearable. I heard a soldier who died eighty years ago complaining about his boots for six miles."

The boy, Tam, spoke up from the shadows: "He talks to 'em polite, though. Even the boots one."

"Tam."

"It's true."

I looked at Halloran for a long moment. Everything in my training said: *this is a Prime Manifestation, this is exactly what Directive One exists for, this man should be in irons in Ironhold before dawn.* Everything in my training also said Ashenbrook was a coven, and I had watched that particular lie burn forty-one people alive.

"Prove it," I said.

His jaw tightened. "I don't perform."

"No. I'm not asking for a show." I reached into my coat — slowly, telegraphing every inch, because I knew what sudden movement meant to a hunted man — and drew out a flat leather packet. Inside was a splinter of bone, yellowed, cracked lengthwise. I'd carried it since Ashenbrook. I hadn't known why until this moment, or rather, I had known and refused the knowledge. "This was pulled from the millhouse rubble. It's got a name carved on it. Half a name. I couldn't bring myself to finish reading it."

He took it in both hands, careful as a man handling a live coal, and closed his eyes.

I watched his face change. It wasn't theatrical. It was the change you see in someone opening a door onto wind — the slight widening, the involuntary breath. His thumb traced the crack in the bone.

"Her name is Aldith," he said softly. "Aldith Merrow. She carved the rest herself — her own marker — because she knew what was coming. She says—" His voice caught, steadied. "She says the fire was slow. She says she forgives the ones who ran. She does not forgive the ones who watched." His eyes opened and found mine, and there was no cruelty in them, which was somehow worse than cruelty. "She's talking about you, Captain Vane. She saw you standing in the yard. She wants to know why you didn't stop it."

The night went very quiet. Tam had gone still. My hands, resting on my knees, had begun to shake, and I let them, because hiding them felt like one more dishonesty in a life suddenly crowded with them.

"I couldn't," I said. "There were thirty men and one of me and the order was legal—"

"That's what she thought you'd say," Halloran murmured. His head tilted slightly, listening to a frequency I couldn't reach. "She says legality is the fire's favorite wood."

I am not a woman who cries easily. I didn't cry then, either. But something broke in me along a line I hadn't known was load-bearing, and I understood, sitting in that moss-eaten shrine with a dead woman's verdict in my ears secondhand, that there was no version of Serah Vane that went back. Not to Ironhold, not to the uniform, not to the arithmetic. The instrument had developed a conscience, and instruments with consciences are scrap metal.

"She's right," I said. "Tell her I said so. Tell her I'll carry it."

Halloran blinked. "Carry what?"

"The name. Aldith Merrow. All of them." I stood, and my knees ached like an old woman's, and I didn't care. "I rode out here to interrogate you, Halloran. To quantify the vessel. Instead you've quantified me, and the findings aren't flattering." I unbuckled my sword belt — Crown issue, nineteen years of wear grooved into the leather — and laid it on the lintel between us like an offering or an accusation; I honestly couldn't tell which. "I'm deserting. Formally. Informally, I'm going quiet."

"You don't have to—"

"I know exactly what I have to do. Nothing. That's the revelation. Nobody's making me do anything ever again." I managed something that was probably a smile. "But if you'll have an extra pair of hands that knows how Crown patrols think, how they track, where they'll look — I'd rather walk with you than ahead of you."

* * *

We left the shrine before dawn, the three of us: a deserter, a carver, and a branded boy who fell asleep walking and trusted us enough to do it, which shamed and steadied me in equal measure.

Halloran walked beside me through the grey light, and just before the road bent north he said, quietly, without looking at me:

"She answered you. Before we left. Aldith." A pause. "She said: *Then carry it forward, not heavy.*"

I laughed — a rough, unexpected sound, the first in weeks — because it was good advice and because it was delivered posthumously and because my life had become the kind of story where the dead give marching orders.

"Not heavy," I agreed. "Forward."

We walked north. Behind us, somewhere south, the hunt was already learning my name had gone into the deserters' ledger, and I knew what that meant: by week's end there would be notices with my face beside Halloran's, and Dross or someone like him would be given my trail and told to close it.

I found I didn't mind. For nineteen years I had hunted the gifted for the Crown.

Now I would learn, at last, what it was we'd actually been afraid of.
