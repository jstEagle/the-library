## 6. Road of Small Ghosts

I had made a rule about trusting witch-hunters, and the rule was simple: don't.

Serah Vane broke that rule every time she handed me back my satchel without searching it, which was roughly twice a day. She walked three paces ahead of me on the north road with her hood up and her sword slung low, moving like a woman who expected the road itself to report her whereabouts to somebody. Maybe it would. I'd stopped expecting the world to make sense around the time I started walking beside a hunt-captain who kept apologizing with her posture.

"You're staring," she said, without turning around.

"I'm wondering what you get out of this."

"Out of what?"

"Walking me to the Spire. Or wherever we're going. You burned my village's name off your own orders. There's a price on my head now, and you're paying for my bread."

She considered that for longer than most people would have. That was the thing about Serah—she treated questions like wounds, something to be examined before touched.

"Bread's cheap," she said finally. "Guilt's expensive. One balances the other."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed, and walked on.

The dead, for their part, were delighted by all of it. They murmured along the roadside like sparrows—*he's going the wrong way, she's lying about something, they'll both die badly, isn't it lovely to have company.* The dead are terrible gossips and worse prophets, and after four days on the road I had learned to let them talk over each other the way you learn to sleep near a river.

We were heading northeast, toward the coast ranges, because the drowned woman in the traveler's keepsake had said *warn the warden*, and because Anselm—I'm getting ahead of myself; there was no Anselm yet, only a rumor of one, a defrocked chaplain whose trail Serah swore she could pick up out of habit—was supposed to be somewhere in the hill country. I told myself I was chasing a ghost. I was. That's the honest version. A ghost had spoken to me through bone for the first time in eleven years, and I had discovered that the silence I'd built my whole life inside of had been a kind of death, and now that the door was open I couldn't shut it again even if I wanted to.

Maren's last words rode in my chest like a coal. *Run, love. Don't look back until you're past the mist.* I looked back plenty, actually. She'd have forgiven me. She forgave everything except wasted bread.

* * *

The first hollow village announced itself by smell—woodsmoke and wet ash and something sour underneath, like milk left out in a warm room. Then by sound, or rather the absence of it. No dogs. No children. Just wind moving through a street of houses with their doors standing open, as if everyone had stepped out mid-meal and meant to come back.

"They emptied it?" I asked.

"Three weeks ago." Serah's voice had gone flat, the way it did when she was handling something carefully so it wouldn't cut her. "Order came down from Ironhold. Every child within forty miles tested against the covenant rolls. Anyone who doesn't spark gets marked."

"Marked how?"

She didn't answer. She didn't have to, because the door of the nearest house had been left open, and inside, chalked onto the hearthstone in a clerk's careful hand, was a circle with a slash through it. Underneath, someone—smaller letters, a shaking hand—had written *she is seven.*

"The hollow ones," Serah said. "Born without the gift. No spark, no resonance, nothing for the covenant to catch hold of." She said it like a catechism, which told me she'd been taught it young. "The crown calls them nulls. The priests call them unlit. The villages—" She stopped.

"The villages call them cursed," I finished. "And the crown calls them useful, because a hunt needs something to hunt when the gifted run out."

Serah looked at me then, really looked, and I saw her do the arithmetic she'd been doing since the mist-line: that I wasn't a fugitive who happened to be right, but a man whose whole life had been spent listening to the machinery of cruelty from just outside its walls. In the Hollow Vale we had no magic, no gifts, no sparks. By the covenant rolls, every soul I'd ever loved was hollow. The elders had voted to hand me over anyway, to keep the peace. I understood now that the vote hadn't been cowardice. It had been curriculum. The crown teaches this lesson everywhere it can reach, and the valley had learned it perfectly.

"Where do they send them?" I asked.

"There's a place. North. Old fortress, decommissioned." Her jaw worked. "It's called the Foundling Yard on the manifests."

"And off the manifests?"

"Off the manifests it's called nothing. Nobody comes back from it to give it a name."

The dead in that village were quiet—not absent, but hushed, the way people go quiet at a funeral. As we walked the length of the street I felt them press close, not speaking, just *witnessing*, and it was worse than any haunting. A doll lay face-down in a puddle. A pair of small boots stood neatly by a doorstep, toes pointed at the door, waiting for feet. I picked up the boots. I don't know why. They fit in my satchel like they'd always had a place there.

"We should burn the mark," I said.

"It won't help them."

"It'll help the next family that walks through and reads it."

Serah watched me scrape the chalk circle off the hearthstone with my thumbnail, all seven years of her, and something in her face shifted—the same shift I'd seen at the mist-line, the moment an instrument of the law notices it has edges that can cut inward. She took the other end of the house and scrubbed the second mark herself. We worked down the street in silence, erasing a village's condemnation one hearth at a time, and if the dead had opinions about two strangers tidying up after an empire, they kept them to themselves.

* * *

The second village still had people in it, which was worse.

They were the ones who'd stayed—grandparents mostly, and a couple of farm families too stubborn or too poor to run—and they looked at us the way you look at weather coming over a ridge. Resigned. Already wet.

An old woman named Bess fed us barley soup because Serah paid for it, and talked while we ate, because talking was the only crop still growing there.

"Took the Marchand girl first," she said. "Sweet thing, eight years old, couldn't light a candle to save her life—which is the test, did you know that? Hand them a candle and speak the old words, and see if the flame leans. Hers didn't lean. So they branded her." She turned her wrist over, showing a scar of her own, old and white. "Farmers used to be marked too, generations back, when the harvest-blessings failed. My grandmother wore one. Said it taught her who her real neighbors were." She smiled without any joy in it whatsoever. "You find out quick. The ones who bring you bread after, them's your neighbors. The rest were never neighbors at all, just people whose luck hadn't turned yet."

"Where's the girl now?" Serah asked.

Bess looked at her—at the sword, at the hunter's bearing she couldn't quite shed—and answered anyway, which told me something about Bess. "Yard. Same as the others. Wagon come through on a Tuesday, men in gray, paperwork all proper. Mother fought them." A pause. "Mother's in the churchyard now. Father's gone north to stand outside the wall of that place and be a nuisance. God keep him. He'll freeze before spring."

"How many children?" I asked.

"From here? Four. From the valley towns beyond?" She shrugged, vast and tired. "Enough to fill a wagon. Enough to fill a yard."

That night, camped in Bess's barn, I lay awake listening to the dead of the village murmur around the eaves, and for once they weren't gossiping. They were counting. I could feel it—an old man's ghost tallying names the way he'd tallied sacks of grain his whole life, four from here, nine from the next town over, fourteen, twenty-two—like if he kept count long enough the number would turn into something that could be fought.

I understood him completely. I'd been doing the same arithmetic since the first empty village, and the sum kept hardening in my chest into a shape I recognized from Maren's kitchen, from the night the riders came: not grief exactly. Purpose. Grief is weather. This was masonry.

* * *

We found the boy on the fourth day out of Bess's village, and I want to be precise about how, because precision matters when you're deciding whether a thing is providence or coincidence or just the world being cruel in an organized way.

There's a shrine road that climbs through the hills—old waymarkers, pre-covenant, carved with spirals nobody remembers the meaning of. The dead like it. Whatever the spirals meant, the dead treat those stones like milestones home, and they gather along the route in loose congregations, chattering. So when the chatter changed pitch—a sudden hush rippling down the line of stones like wind through wheat—I noticed.

Then I heard what they were looking at. Not saying. Looking at.

"*Small one,*" they said, in the tone people use for a stray dog. "*Small one, cold, alone, walking wrong.*"

He was maybe ten. Thin as a lath, wrapped in a blanket that had been a good blanket for somebody else once, sitting in the lee of a waymarker with his knees drawn up, watching us come with the flat, rationed attention of a creature that has learned not to spend hope carelessly. His left forearm was wrapped in dirty bandages, and under the wrapping, when he moved, I saw the shape of a brand healing angry.

Hollow. Exiled. Walking *wrong*—the dead meant directionless, I think, though they might also have meant limping.

Serah's hand went to her sword out of pure reflex, and I put my own hand over hers, and we stood there like idiots in the middle of the road while a starving child decided whether we were wolves.

"I'm Gideon," I said. "This is Serah. We're not with the wagons."

"I know you're not with the wagons." His voice was hoarse, scraped thin. "Wagons don't stop."

Fair point. We stopped.

"What's your name?"

A long calculation behind his eyes. Then: "Tam."

"Tam. Where are you walking to?"

"Nowhere. Everywhere's got the test in it now." He said it the way you'd report the weather. "I figured I'd walk till winter, and then winter would sort it."

The dead went very quiet around us, and I realized they were watching to see what I'd do. All of them. The whole congregation of small ghosts along the shrine road, gathered at their milestone, waiting on a bone-carver from a silent valley to decide whether another small ghost got made today.

Here is what I knew: taking him was dangerous. A branded boy on the road with a wanted man and a deserter was a signature, a story, a confession. Here is what else I knew: Maren had found a frightened boy with a dangerous gift at her door once, and the elders had voted to shelter him, and it had cost her everything, and I would trade none of it—not one day of her, not one carved bone, not one lie I told grieving families about hearing their dead—not one single piece of it, because she had decided I was worth the price, and the only way to repay a decision like that is to keep making it.

I crouched down to his level. Up close he smelled of smoke and river water and the particular sourness of fear worn for weeks.

"Those boots yours?" I asked.

He looked at his feet—cracked leather two sizes too big. "Found 'em."

"I've got a pair in my bag. Somebody left them behind in a hurry and I've been carrying them like an idiot ever since. They'd probably fit you." I pulled them out. They did fit, nearly. Tam stared at them the way men stare at miracles, which is to say suspiciously, waiting for the bill.

"What's wrong with you?" he asked finally. "Why're you being—people don't—"

"I know," I said. "It's a bad habit. I caught it from my mother. It's fatal, usually takes decades."

Something happened at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. The ground where a smile would be built someday, cleared and graded.

"He's coming with us," I told Serah.

"Gideon—"

"Yes. I heard all the reasons. I gave most of them to myself on the walk over." I shouldered the satchel. "He's coming."

Serah looked at the boy, at the bandaged arm, at the waymarker behind him where the dead had begun, very softly, to sing—some wordless rising thing, like a congregation getting its voice back. Then she looked at me with the expression of a woman losing an argument with her own conscience and knowing it had reinforcements.

"He slows us down."

"He does."

"He's a liability."

"He is."

"If the hunt finds us with him—"

"Then the hunt finds us having done one thing right," I said, "which will confuse them terribly."

She blew out a breath. Held out her hand to the boy, formal as a treaty. "Serah Vane. I used to be the enemy. Working on it."

Tam shook her hand with grave suspicion, the way you'd shake hands with a dog that had bitten you before but seemed sorry. Then he fell into step between us, small and sharp-eyed, and the dead along the shrine road sang us three miles like a choir that had been waiting years for a reason.

* * *

That night, in the ruins of a shepherd's bothy, Tam slept like the dead themselves—asleep the instant the fire was lit, one fist curled around a heel of bread he refused to eat until Serah promised no one would take it. Children learn hunger's lessons permanently. I sat across the flames from Serah and listened to the ghosts murmur at the edge of the light, and I told her about the counting. The old man tallying names like grain.

"It's systemic," I said. "That's what I couldn't see from inside the valley. I thought the cruelty was weather—bad luck, local, passing. It's not weather. It's architecture. Someone drew these plans a century ago and we've all been living in the building without reading the blueprints." I turned my mother's coal over in my chest. "Children branded for lacking a gift. Villages emptied to fill a fortress nobody returns from. And a hunt with a budget that never runs dry. You don't fund that forever unless it's buying you something."

Serah fed the fire a stick. "You sound like a man building toward a conclusion."

"I sound like a man who's done pretending the road is random." I nodded toward the sleeping boy. "Every small ghost on that road was put there by the same hand. I intend to find out whose."

For a long moment Serah said nothing. Then, quietly, in the voice of a woman confessing to a priest she hadn't chosen:

"When I burned Marrow Fen—it was orders. Containment, they called it. I told myself containment." The fire cracked. "But I counted too, afterward. Forty-one souls. I've been counting ever since. I just didn't have anywhere to put the number."

"Put it here," I said. "It stacks."

And that was when Tam, who was supposedly asleep, said from his blanket, in a voice thick with drowsiness and absolutely certain conviction:

"The gray men have a ledger. I seen it. On the wagon. Names and marks and numbers, and at the front a stamp like—" he made a shape in the air with one finger, already half under again, "—like a tower with a broken top."

Serah and I looked at each other across the fire.

A tower with a broken top. I'd seen that sigil once before, pressed into wax on the proclamation nailed to my mother's door, and I had assumed it was just the crown's seal, meaningless ornament.

Nothing was ornament. Everything was blueprint.

"Sleep, lad," I said, and pulled his blanket higher, and sat back down slowly, because my legs had gone strange. "Tomorrow we follow the ledger."

The dead at the edge of the firelight had stopped murmuring. All of them, every voice, were leaning in toward the boy like flowers toward sun, and for the first time since I'd crossed the mist-line, they weren't gossiping or singing or counting.

They were waiting to see what we'd do with what he'd given us.

So was I.
