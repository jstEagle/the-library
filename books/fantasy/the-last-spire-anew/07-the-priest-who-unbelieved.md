## 7. The Priest Who Unbelieved

I had preached eleven hundred sermons in the Cathedral of the Radiant Chain, and I want it noted for posterity that not one of them was true.

Not all of them were lies, either. That is the peculiar cruelty of a chaplain's life: you spend decades saying mostly true things—be kind, forgive your brother, the dead deserve rest—and then one afternoon you find the document that reveals the entire scaffolding was built over a murder, and every kindness you ever preached becomes a brick in the wall that hides the body.

My name is Anselm Corvane, formerly Chaplain-General to the Crown of Vhalderan, currently a man with lice, and this is how I came to be following rumors through the wet end of autumn like a dog after a cart of fish.

* * *

The discovery happened on a Tuesday. This matters to me because Tuesdays were when I catalogued the Undercroft, and cataloguing was the only hour of my week when nobody wanted anything from me except alphabetical order.

The Undercroft of the Radiant Chain holds everything the Church has decided to keep and nothing the Church has decided to remember. Ledgers of indulgences. Correspondence with heretics we'd later canonized. The tax rolls of dioceses that no longer exist because the crown burned them, which we recorded, in our tidy way, as *consolidations*.

I was re-shelving a crate from the Archive at Kel Doreth—a monastery, incidentally, which is now ash; hold that thought—when my hand found a folio bound in wyrm-hide. Not calfskin stamped to look like wyrm-hide, which is what the reliquaries used. Actual hide, taken lawfully or otherwise from an actual dragon, and it made my skin crawl before I'd read a word, the way you flinch from a stove before you know it's hot.

The folio was a warden's journal. Dragon-warden, third rank, name redacted by someone with a very steady pen and, I now understand, a very clean conscience about none of it. She had been stationed at the Last Spire—the Spire itself, mind you, the one from the children's rhymes—in the last year before the Sundering.

I have read that journal perhaps two hundred times since. I could recite it. But the first reading, standing alone in the cold undercroft with a tallow candle guttering, I remember chiefly the sensation of the floor tilting. Not literally. Literally would have been easier.

She wrote about the covenant the way a mason writes about a wall she has inspected personally. It held. It was *holding*. The realms were knit through the Spire and its sister-seals, dragon-fire feeding the hinge, magic distributed across the world like rain instead of hoarded like grain. And then, in the final entries, in handwriting gone small and hurried:

*They have asked me to open the seal "for maintenance." There is no maintenance rite. There has never been a maintenance rite. I have written to the Archprelate and received no reply. I have written to the Crown and been thanked.*

*If I am found dead in my bed, know that I was not ill.*

The next entry was three lines long and dated nine days later. Different hand. A clerk's hand, neat and untroubled: *Warden relieved of post. Seal works proceeding. Praise the Chain.*

*Seal works proceeding.* Four words that described the deliberate breaking of the covenant between realms—the severing that ended magic's free passage, that began the slow strangling of dragon-fire, that created, in time, the Hollow Vales and the hunts and the whole architecture of fear I had spent my life blessing.

I sat down on the crate. I believe I sat there for some hours. At one point a deacon looked in and asked if I was praying, and I said yes, and God forgive me, that was the last fully honest conversation I had in that building.

Because here is what I did next. Here is the sin I will carry past my death into whatever accounting waits, if anything waits.

I put the folio back on the shelf.

I told myself a hundred stories that night, kneeling in my cell. *One man cannot fight the Church. The truth would cause schism, war. The faithful would be shattered. Surely the Archprelate doesn't know; surely this is some administrative horror committed by men long dead; surely—*

Surely. The favorite word of cowards in every century.

For four months I did nothing. I performed Mass. I heard confessions. I absolved a tax-farmer whose ledgers showed he had starved two villages, and I spoke the words of absolution with a straight face while knowing—I *knew*, down in the wyrm-hide cold of me—that the institution granting me authority to absolve anyone was founded atop a crime so large it had become geography. The world without free magic, the dead pressing against fraying borders, the gifted hunted like wolves: all of it built on that Tuesday folio.

What finally moved me was smaller than any grand moral crisis. It was a boy.

He came to confession during Lent, maybe fourteen years old, born in one of the border parishes where the old blood still surfaced now and then despite everything. He could hear his dead grandmother. Just her, just sometimes, telling him where his mother had hidden the rent money before she died. He wasn't frightened of the gift. He was frightened of *us*. Someone had told him the priests would know what to do, and he had correctly understood that "what to do" meant the hunt, meant the wagon, meant the white hood, and he confessed anyway because he had been taught that hiding a thing from the Church was the graver sin.

I gave him three Hail Chains and told him the voices would pass.

Then I went back to my cell and did not sleep, because I understood that I had just done exactly what my institution exists to do: taken a living connection to the truth and smothered it gently, with kindness, in the name of order.

The hunt took the boy in May. I checked. Of course I checked. What else is guilt for, if not to make you check?

* * *

I left the Cathedral on the night of the Feast of the Sundered Veil—which the Church celebrates, I ask you to savor this, with lanterns commemorating the day magic was locked away from the wicked and the righteous were made safe. I left through the crypts, carrying the wyrm-hide folio, a satchel of documents I'd spent those four months quietly copying, forty-one years of tithes I refused to feel guilty about taking, and a conviction so heavy it has functioned ever since as a second spine.

You will notice I do not describe my departure as brave. It was not. Bravery would have been staying, climbing the pulpit on the Feast itself, and reading the warden's journal aloud to ten thousand worshippers. I calculated the odds of surviving that sermon and chose the road instead. A priest who calculates is called a pragmatist right up until you need him, and then he is called something else.

But here is the thing I have learned on the road, sleeping in ditches and bartering Latin lessons for soup: flight can ripen into purpose if you feed it. Mine ripened the night I met the bone-carver's work.

* * *

It was in a market town called Ferrow's Cross, in the territory where the hunt's writ runs thickest, where people speak in the low voices of the permanently careful. I had fallen in with a carter hauling salt, and in the town square, while the carter haggled, I drifted—as I always drift, as a moth drifts—to the stall of a traveling carver.

Bone-carvers are common enough along the old borders. Grieving families commission memorial pieces, little carved keepsakes, on the theory—leftover from the covenant days—that bone remembers. The Church officially holds this to be superstition. The Church also, I can now confirm from the archives, maintains a secret office that collects such carvings and studies them, but superstition is only heresy when it works.

The stall's pieces were good. Better than good. I picked up a small shard, no longer than my thumb, carved with a spiral pattern, and felt—felt, physically, the way you feel a draft from a door you thought was shut—a hum against my palm.

Now, I should be clear: I have no gift. I am the most spiritually deaf man in any room I enter, a fact my seminary instructors noted with the gentle disappointment of men describing a horse born without legs. Whatever I felt, it was not the dead speaking. It was more like holding a struck bell that someone else, somewhere far away, was still hearing.

"Fine piece," the carver said. He was not the maker—he was selling on commission, a lean woman with frostbitten knuckles. "Came out of the Vale country. Fellow there does work like nothing else. They say—" she lowered her voice, the automatic lowering of everyone in that territory—"they say he's got the touch. Hears them. Carves what the dead ask for."

"And this spiral?"

"Don't know. Dead woman's design. Her daughter commissioned it, brought the pattern on a scrap." She shrugged. "Old pattern, though. My gran wore one like it, and she said her gran said it was a warden's mark."

I bought the shard with tithe-money. I held it under my cloak the rest of the day like a coal.

Because I knew that mark. I had spent four months sleeping with a wyrm-hide journal under my head like a pillow stuffed with dynamite, and I knew that mark. The warden's own seal, drawn in the margins of her last entries. The sign of the third rank, keeper of the hinge.

A dead woman in the Hollow Vale had asked a bone-carver to carve the murdered warden's sigil.

Or—no. Sit with this, the way I sat with it in a rented room above a tannery, candle burning to a puddle. Consider what it means. The warden died nine days after writing *if I am found dead in my bed, know that I was not ill*. And a century later, her mark surfaces in a carving commissioned by a grieving daughter, in a pattern the dead woman herself specified.

Either coincidence—and I have seen the odds table the Church keeps for explaining away miracles, and even it would blush—or the warden's ghost had been trying for a hundred years to get a message out, and had finally found a man who could hear it.

And the crown's riders, according to the carter, were riding toward the Vale country even now. Proclamation posted. All gifted persons to be surrendered. The hunt coming, at last, to the one man who might be carrying the most dangerous object in the world without knowing it: a message from the murdered witness, carved in living bone.

I packed my satchel that night. I did not sleep, which was fine; I hadn't really slept in eight months. In the morning I set out for the Hollow Vale, a fifty-four-year-old fugitive with bad knees, following the trail of a ghost who had waited a century for someone to listen.

I have reflected, on the long wet miles since, on why I am doing this, and I will be honest, since honesty is the one currency I have left: it is not only redemption. Redemption implies the possibility of being cleansed, and I have read the ledger of my complicity too carefully to expect that. Four months of silence. A boy given Hail Chains and handed to the wagons. Eleven hundred sermons.

No. What drives me is simpler and less flattering. I know where the body is buried. I have carried the shovel's location in my satchel across half a kingdom, and every mile I travel without using it, the boy in the confessional gets heavier in my arms. Truth-telling is the only penance the Church never taught me, because it is the one penance that costs the Church.

So: I will find the bone-carver. I will show him the journal. I will tell him that the voice he hears is the voice of a woman the crown murdered to break the world on purpose, and that his little carved shard matches an artifact sealed in the burned Archive at Kel Doreth—one of the few copies of the seal-records to survive the fire that was supposed to erase them.

And I will pray—not the polished prayers of the Radiant Chain, but the raw ones, the ones I invented in ditches—that whatever the dead woman wants him to do, it does not require more courage than either of us possesses.

Though privately, holding the shard at night, feeling it hum against my palm like a held breath—

Privately, I think it will.

* * *

I reached the edge of the Vale country twelve days later, and I knew it before any map confirmed it, because the world went quiet in a way that had weight. Travelers say the mist-line looks like weather. It is not weather. It is a held breath a hundred miles wide, and crossing toward it feels like walking into the pause before someone speaks.

I stopped at the last inn outside the line, a low-roofed place full of farmers not looking at each other, and bought stew I couldn't eat and information I paid too much for.

The news was this: the proclamation had come and gone. The elders had voted compliance—of course they had; safety is the argument that always wins, I had watched it win from a pulpit for forty years. Names had been given. And three nights ago, hunt-captain Serah Vane herself had ridden up the valley road, and there had been a death at the mist-line—an old woman, a carver's mother, they said—and the carver had run.

Run *out*, mark you. Out through the line, into the loud world, alone, carrying grief like a lit fuse.

"He'll not get far," the innkeep said, wiping the same spot on the bar for the tenth time. "Vane's hounds are the best in the hunt. Man hears voices? They'll have him inside a fortnight."

Perhaps. But I had spent a career watching powerful institutions underestimate precisely the people they had taught to be quiet, and I put my coin on the carver.

I walked to the mist-line at dawn. Up close it was worse than I'd imagined—a wall of grey nothing, and beyond it, faintly, the valley lying flat and silent as a held tongue. Somewhere on the other side of that veil was a man who had grown up deaf to the dead and was now, at this moment, drowning in them, alone, hunted, carrying a shard of bone he didn't know was a key.

I had a journal, a satchel of stolen truth, and a plan with the structural integrity of wet paper: find him before the hunt does, tell him everything, and follow him wherever the dead woman's message leads.

Behind me, on the road, I heard horses.

I turned, and my heart performed a maneuver I had previously only read about. Riders—six of them, hunt-grey, and at their head a woman whose face I knew from the wanted-notices I had lately begun to appear on myself, though these riders were not looking for me. Captain Serah Vane, they called her. The best hound the hunt had.

She was riding away from the valley. Riding hard, riding *wrong*—not sweeping the roads the way a hunter sweeps, but cutting cross-country like a woman late for an appointment she regretted.

And tied to her saddle, wrapped in oilcloth but unmistakable to a man who had spent months studying every drawing of it, rode the warden's mark in carved bone.

She had it. Or he had given it to her. Or—my mind raced down corridors I didn't like—something had happened at that mist-line that no one in that inn yet understood.

I stood at the edge of the silence with my satchel of ashes and my paper-thin plan, and I made the choice that has defined every day since.

I followed the huntress.
