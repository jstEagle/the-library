## 8. Archive of Ashes

I had burned a monastery once.

Not this one — this one the crown had done for me, years before I ever stood in its shell with ash sifting down like gray snow that refused to melt. The one I burned had been in the eastern marches, a squat stone place full of monks who, according to my orders, were harboring gifted children. They weren't. I knew they weren't by the time the roof came down. I've had eleven years to make peace with that knowledge and I can report that peace is not available. What is available is the ability to keep walking, which I did, and do.

This monastery was called Saint Verreth's, or had been, before someone decided it was better as a chimney. We came to it at dusk on the ninth day out of the hollow villages, Tam riding double behind Gideon because the boy's legs gave out every fourth mile and he'd sooner bite his tongue through than admit it. Anselm led us off the road with the confidence of a man who has either been somewhere many times or is about to be very embarrassed.

"Through here," he said, ducking under a fallen lintel blackened to the color of old teeth.

"If you're leading us into an ambush," I said, "I want you to know I'll be professionally disappointed. Ambushes are supposed to be a surprise."

"No ambush." He glanced back at me, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen on him before. Not fear exactly. Shame's cousin, maybe — the look of a man about to show you the room where he keeps everything he's failed to throw away. "Only paperwork."

* * *

The nave was open to the sky. Fire had taken the roof and most of the east wall, but the fire had been selective — surgical, even — and it took me longer than I'd like to admit to understand why that was strange. Fires are not selective. Fires are the most honest thing the crown ever fielded. But here the scriptorium wing stood scorched while the chapel beside it was rubble, here a shelf of ledgers sat unburned in a room whose wooden floorboards were charred around it like a frame drawn in the negative.

"They wanted the books gone," Anselm said, watching me see it. "They didn't much care about the monks."

"You were one of the monks."

"I was the archivist." He said it flatly, the way men say things they've said to themselves ten thousand times in the dark. "Royal Chaplaincy, Third Archive. Which means that when the order came down to burn Saint Verreth's, I was the man who signed the inventory of what we were destroying."

"And what were you destroying?"

He held up a lantern — a real flame, small and stubborn, the first I'd allowed since leaving Ironhold's patrol routes behind us — and the light caught rows of shelves rising into the dark like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down here to die.

"Everything," he said.

* * *

What follows, I set down as plainly as I can, because plainness was the only mercy the truth had left to offer us that night.

Anselm worked for two hours. Tam slept against Gideon's shoulder, the boy's breath going slow and whistling. Gideon stood among the shelves with his head tilted, listening to whatever he listens to — the dead of this place, I suppose, though the dead of Saint Verreth's had been quiet for thirty years, and I wondered if they'd gotten quieter still when the books went up. He didn't say. He mostly doesn't, unless the saying is unavoidable.

And I read.

I read until the lantern oil ran low and Anselm fed it another flask from his endless supply, and what I read was this:

A hundred and six years ago, the realms were bound together by a covenant sealed in dragon-fire and living bone — seven anchor-points, seven spires, each one tended by a warden of the old blood. The covenant was not a wall. That's the word everyone uses, the word I grew up using: the Veil, the Wall, the Boundary between the quick and the dead, between magic and its absence. A wall implies a builder with a benign purpose. It implies protection.

It was a hinge. A door deliberately ajar, so that power could move between realms without drowning either one. The Hollow Vale wasn't a refuge God forgot; it was a pressure valve, a place where the door stood widest, where the giftless could live without being ground between realms that pulled at them from both sides. And the dragons —

The dragons were the lock.

Seven dragons, one per spire, their fire feeding the seal the way a heart feeds the body. Slow-burning. Centuries-deep. Nobody was supposed to need them for anything except to simply keep burning, generation after generation, the way mountains keep standing.

Except somebody decided a hinge was a terrible idea if you were the only one holding the handle.

"The crown," I said, because Anselm had stopped talking and was looking at me with the particular patience of a teacher waiting for the student to arrive at the answer she already dreaded. "The crown broke it."

"The crown severed it. Deliberately. At all seven spires, in a single night, a hundred and six years ago last midwinter." He drew a ledger from the shelf and opened it to a page marked with a ribbon gone brown as jerky. "This is the requisition record. Seven companies of wardens' guards, reassigned. Seven shipments of 'quarry salt' — which is what they called the anti-magic compound in those days, before anyone thought to disguise it. Seven payments drawn on the privy purse, authorized under the personal seal of King Aldric the Second." He turned the page toward me. "Your witch-hunt, Captain Vane. Your hunt that you served for eleven years. It was founded nine days later."

I looked at the numbers. I am good with numbers; you have to be, to provision a company across winter roads. And the numbers said what Anselm said they said, in ink a century old, in a hand that had never imagined anyone would read it by lantern-light in a ruin with a deserter and a bone-hearer and a branded child sleeping three feet away.

"Why?" My voice came out wrong. Smaller than I meant it. "Why break it? What does the crown gain?"

"Monopoly." Anselm closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin. "Think it through, Serah. You've lived it; you just never had the whole shape of it. When the covenant held, magic belonged to everyone and no one — it moved like weather. After the severance, it pooled. It gathered wherever the crown built its engines, its vaults, its garrisons. Every drop of power in the world now flows through crown hands first. And the price—"

"The price is everything else." I heard myself say it and hated how steady I sounded. "The Vale goes silent. The hollow children get born — the ones like Tam, born with nothing, because the valve is closed and there's nothing left to flow into them. The dragons starve, because their fire was feeding a seal nobody tends anymore. And the dead—"

"The boundary frays," Anselm said. "That's the part they didn't plan for. You can't cut a hinge cleanly. The wound leaks. The dead press against a torn edge, and every year the tear widens, and every year the voices get louder, and every year the crown needs more witch-hunters to convince everyone the loudness is the problem rather than the symptom." He smiled, and it was the ugliest smile I have ever seen on a gentle face. "You weren't hunting sorcery, Captain. You were pest control for a cover-up. The hunt exists to make sure no one gifted lives long enough to hear the dead clearly enough to ask why they're so angry."

Tam woke up somewhere in there. I don't know how much he heard. He didn't say anything, just sat up slowly in the ash-dark with the brand on his cheek catching the lantern-light — the mark we put on children for the crime of being born downstream of a king's greed — and looked at the ledger the way you look at a snake you've finally managed to see the whole length of.

Gideon spoke for the first time in hours.

"The woman in the bone." His voice was rough, like a door long unlatched. "The traveler's keepsake. She told me to warn the warden. She said the seal was bleeding." He looked at Anselm. "How long has she known? How long have any of them known? The dead must remember. They were alive when—"

"Some of them were." Anselm's voice softened. "And yes. I believe some of the dead have been trying to tell the living for a hundred years. But the living who could hear them kept getting arrested, or worse." He nodded at me without malice, which was somehow worse than malice would have been. "The system works, Captain. That's the horror of it. It works beautifully. I administered it for twenty years before I found this inventory in a box I wasn't supposed to open, and I want you to understand that my first instinct was to re-shelve it. That's what obedience does. It makes complicity feel like tidiness."

* * *

We argued after that. Of course we argued. Gideon wanted to know why the crown hadn't simply killed the dragons outright, and Anselm explained — with archival relish, the man cannot help himself — that killing a seal-dragon breaks the anchor entirely and lets the realms slam shut, which would mean no magic anywhere for anyone including the crown, and probably a cataclysm besides. Better to let them gutter. A dying lock still locks. For a while.

"So there's a clock," I said. "When the last dragon dies, the anchors fail, the realms come apart at the seams, and—"

"Or the opposite," Gideon said quietly.

We all looked at him. He was standing with his hand flat against a charred shelf, and I realized he'd been doing that for some time — resting his palm on the burnt wood the way you'd rest it on a fevered brow.

"The dead aren't only angry," he said. "Some of them are working. There's a warden — the one whose grave the voice in the bone wanted me to find. She's been pulling at me for days, since we crossed into the hill country. She knows where her tomb is. She wants—" He stopped, frowned, listened to something none of us could hear, and when he spoke again his voice had changed, gone careful, the way it does when he's translating rather than reporting. "She says the covenant can be mended. Reforged. If someone carries a key to the Last Spire before the last fire gutters."

"Which is when?" I asked.

Anselm answered instead, because Anselm always had the document for everything, and I watched his face as he consulted whatever archive lived behind his eyes and found the entry.

"Midwinter," he said. "The requisition records show the dragon-fires measured annually. Eyrrith's flame — the Last Spire's dragon, the seventh, the final one — has declined every year for a century. The projections put embers at—" he did the arithmetic, and I watched him wish he didn't have to, "—midwinter. This coming midwinter. Perhaps a month past it, if the wind is kind."

"Then we have a season," I said.

"We have a season," Anselm agreed. "To find a murdered woman's grave, learn what a covenant-key is and whether our carver happens to be carrying one, cross half the realm ahead of the most efficient killing apparatus in its history, and reach a mountain fortress guarded by the last dragon alive — who will have every reason to eat us on principle."

"Put that way," Tam said, around a yawn that fooled no one, "it sounds almost doable."

I cuffed the back of his head, gently, because that's the whole of my tenderness and he's learned to bank it.

* * *

Near dawn, while the others slept, I climbed to what remained of the bell tower and looked east, where the sky was doing its slow gray work. Somewhere out there was Ironhold, and Verrick, my former lieutenant, running my former hunt with my former authority, hunting a deserter whose description had surely reached every garrison by now. Somewhere out there was the Hollow Vale, where Maren Halloran had died buying her son forty minutes, and where the elders were presumably telling themselves the story that made their vote bearable. Somewhere out there, a dragon I had never thought of as anything but a beast and a hazard was dying alone atop a spire, keeping faith with a covenant her keepers had betrayed a hundred years before I was born.

Eleven years. Eleven years of warrants and pyres and the clean language of containment, and it had all been a janitor's broom sweeping up the evidence of a theft.

I thought about the monks I'd burned in the eastern marches. I thought about whether any of them had known. Whether any of them had died trying to shout the truth through my smoke.

Below me, faintly, I heard Gideon stirring — and then I heard something else, something that raised every hair I own. He was talking to someone. Low, urgent, half-pleading. But no one else was awake, and when I leaned over the tower rail I saw him alone in the ruined nave, lantern-light pooling around his boots, his hand pressed flat against the air in front of him as if steadying a shoulder I couldn't see.

And the cold morning air around him was moving. Just slightly. Just enough.

Like breathing.

He lifted his head and looked straight up at me through the broken tower floor, and his face told me that whatever had come calling in the night had brought news — and that we had less time than Anselm's ledgers said.

"Gideon," I called down. "Who's there?"

"The warden," he said. "She says we're already too late to walk. She says we ride for her grave today — and Serah—" he paused, listening to words I'll spend the rest of my life wondering at, "—she says your old commander's riders crossed the river at dawn. Three days behind us. Maybe less."

I looked east again, at the thinning dark, and made the arithmetic I was good at, and for the first time in eleven years the numbers terrified me.

"Then we ride," I said.
