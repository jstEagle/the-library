## 9. The Warden's Grave

The dead do not give directions. I want that recorded somewhere, in ink, in stone, preferably on a signpost at the edge of every road in the realm: *the dead do not give directions.* What they give is urgency without geography, grief without grammar. For six days we had followed a voice that said only *find her, find her, the warden, find her,* and it had led us up a goat track into mountains that seemed personally offended by our presence.

"Your ghost," Serah said, breathing hard beside me, "is either lost or lying."

"She's dead," Anselm offered from behind, "so technically she can be both."

Tam laughed until I glared at him, which only made him laugh harder. The boy had developed an alarming talent for finding humor in precisely the moments I least wanted any. I'd have corrected him if I weren't quietly grateful for it. Fear travels light when you're laughing; it's when you're silent that you feel how heavy your pack really is.

We crested the ridge as the sun was going down, and there it was.

I had expected a tomb to look like a tomb—stone, somber, squatting in the earth like something ashamed of itself. This was not that. It stood alone on the shoulder of the mountain like a finger pointing at the sky: a spire of white stone no taller than a man, carved all over with script I couldn't read and figures I could—dragons, dozens of them, coiling down the length of it in shallow relief, each one smaller than the last, as though the carver had been running out of mountain, or time, or hope.

And the voices.

They hit me before I'd taken three steps toward it. Not the single pleading voice that had ridden in the traveler's keepsake across the valley border, not the scattered whispers of the roadside ghosts I'd learned to bear like weather. This was a chorus, hundreds strong, and they were all singing the same word in different languages, different centuries, some of them so old their speech had worn smooth as river stones:

*Warden. Warden. Warden.*

"They know we're here," I said. My own voice sounded thin against them.

"I gathered," Serah said, hand already on her knife. Old habits. "Anything with teeth?"

"No." I closed my eyes, which is a stupid thing to do among strangers on a cliff, but listening is easier blind. "No teeth. Just—" I searched for the word and hated the one I found. "Grief. Organized grief."

Anselm came up beside me, and for once the ex-chaplain had nothing clever queued. He was staring at the little spire with an expression I recognized from churchyards—the face of a man meeting proof of everything he'd stopped daring to believe. "This matches the archive drawings," he murmured. "Warden-markers. There used to be hundreds. One at every place a dragon-warden fell."

"And nobody buried her here?" Tam asked. "It's just... standing out in the wind?"

"The wardens aren't buried," Anselm said slowly, working it through as he spoke, the way he did when truth was arriving faster than his theology. "They're *stationed*. Even in death. A tomb keeps a body. This keeps a duty."

Serah looked at him. "That's the most unsettling sentence I've heard all month, priest, and I've been traveling with Gideon."

*

We made camp in the lee of the marker because none of us suggested anywhere else, and none of us would admit why. The wind came off the peaks carrying snow-dust that glittered like ground glass, and the fire burned strangely steady, as though the air itself were holding still to listen.

I sat closest. Of course I sat closest. Six days of *find her* had worn a channel in me, and water always finds its level.

When the dead woman finally came, she didn't arrive the way the others did—not a pressure at the edge of hearing, not a cold spot, not the awful slow leak of someone else's dying. She simply *was*, suddenly, sitting across the fire where no one sat, and every other voice in the world went quiet to make room for her. Hundreds of ghosts, hushed by one.

She had been old when she died. I could tell that much even through the veil—old and weathered and built like the mountain she guarded, with hands that had done decades of work and been proud of every scar. Her warden's cloak was rendered in the memory-fabric the dead wear, patched a dozen times, dragon-scale clasps long since tarnished. And through her chest, where a spear or sword or something crueler had gone in, the wound showed as a darkness that didn't flicker with the firelight. Dead wounds don't. They're the one honest thing about the dead; they never heal over into prettiness.

"You're the bone-carver," she said.

Not a question. Word travels, apparently, even among the dead. Especially among the dead. They'd been passing my name hand to hand—or whatever the equivalent gesture is—since Ironhold, since the fragment I freed went whispering through the spirit-world like sparks through dry grass. *The carver who hears. The carver who listens back.*

"Yes," I said aloud, because Serah and Tam were watching me stare at empty air, and I've learned it's kinder to narrate than to let them wonder.

"Good," the warden said. "Then you can carry it properly. The last three who tried just carried it."

"Carry what?"

She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, patient and sad, the smile of someone who has waited a very long time for a very bad piece of news to finish arriving. "Show me what you took from the traveler's bundle. The shard."

My hand was in my satchel before I'd decided to move it. That's the thing about the gift they never warn you about: your body learns obedience to the dead faster than your mind learns caution. I drew out the shard of living bone—the one that had first spoken to me across the valley line, the one I'd nearly burned, the one Anselm had gone pale over in the ash-archive because its carving matched a diagram in forbidden records—and held it over the fire.

The moment it cleared the leather, the whole mountain changed key.

The chorus surged. The wind dropped dead. The fire leaned toward the shard like a crowd leaning toward a speaker. And the shard itself—it had always been warm, faintly, the way a stone stays warm after an animal leaves it—*blazed*. Light moved under its surface, slow and deep, amber-gold, like embers seen through skin.

Every ghost within a hundred miles felt it. I knew that the way you know a storm has noticed you.

"Covenant-key," the warden said, and the words landed in me like nails driven home. "One of seven cut from the First Covenant when it was whole. The crown thought they'd destroyed them all a century ago. They missed this one because it was inside a living person—a warden carries her key in her own body, grown into the bone of her forearm, so no thief can take it twice." She lifted her ghostly arm, and beneath the memory-flesh I saw it: a seam of gold light, long-healed around a shape that was no longer there. "I gave mine away the night I died. To a farmer's boy who was running the wrong direction. Told him it was a saint's relic. He believed me. Farmers' boys always believe old women."

"That boy," Anselm breathed. "The traveler. His grandmother kept it—"

"His granddaughter. Time moves quickly downhill, priest." The warden's eyes hadn't left me. "And now it's come to you, which means the covenant is failing faster than even I feared, because keys don't wander. Keys are *drawn*. Something called it, and you answered before you knew you were answering. That's how it worked for all of us. Nobody chooses the wardening. You just wake up one day already carrying it."

I looked down at the shard glowing in my palm, and for the first time since the valley, since Maren, since the mist-line—I wanted to put it down. Wanted it with my whole body, the way you want to set down a weight you've forgotten isn't supposed to be yours.

"What does it open?" I asked, though I already knew. Some part of me had known since the archive, since Anselm read those scorched pages aloud and my bones went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

"The Last Spire." She said it plainly, mercifully, without theater. "Where the First Covenant was forged. Where it must be reforged, or the realms stay severed and the dragons go out like candles and the dead—" she gestured at herself, at the dark wound, at the whole hushed choir pressed against the edge of the firelight, "—the dead keep getting louder, and angrier, and more numerous, because the boundary frays and nothing holds them and nothing releases them. Your crown broke the covenant to hoard magic behind walls. Every year the seal weakens. Every year the hunt gets more desperate, because frightened kings burn louder than wise ones."

"How long?" Serah asked. She'd moved closer without seeming to, the huntress reading threat-assessment out of a conversation with a ghost. Practical to the marrow. I was starting to love her for it, in the specific way you love a blade that has decided to stop cutting you.

The warden turned to her. "Midwinter. When the last dragon-fire gutters, the seal dies with it. After that, reforging becomes impossible—not difficult, not costly. Impossible. The Spire accepts a new covenant only while the old flame lives." She paused, and her terrible patient smile returned. "You have until the longest night. Perhaps forty days. Perhaps fewer. Eyrrith is very old and very tired, and the crown's engines drink the air itself."

"Eyrrith," Tam whispered. "The last dragon."

"The last of anything is always named," the warden said gently. "It's how you know the end is near—everything left has a proper name and a story and someone who will grieve it individually."

Nobody said anything for a while. The fire cracked. Somewhere below us, far off, a wolf sang one note and thought better of continuing.

Then I asked the question I'd been holding behind my teeth like a coal. "Why me? You had wardens. You had an order. You had a hundred years to train a successor. Why does a bone-carver who spent his life hiding in a valley where he couldn't hear anything get handed the end of the world?"

The warden considered me for a long moment. The fire reflected off her the way moonlight reflects off water—there, but not touching.

"Because the valley was the training," she said. "Do you understand? Every warden before you learned to listen among the dead, and it warped us, sweetling. We grew fluent in grief and illiterate in peace. We heard so many voices we forgot what silence was *for*." She leaned forward. "You lived thirty years in silence. You learned to sit with people who couldn't speak and honor them anyway. You carved their loved ones' names into bone with your hands and your heart and no help from any gift. That's the whole office. That's the entire wardening. Anyone can hear the dead. Almost no one can honor them."

Her voice gentled further, and somehow that was worse.

"A stranger's death became your charge the day you crossed the mist-line, carver. Mine became yours the moment you picked up that shard. I'm sorry. I truly am. I waited a century hoping to hand this to someone who'd have an easier road, and the world sent me a fugitive with a bounty on his head and a found family he can't afford to lose." She rose—or the memory of rising; the dead don't stand so much as *conclude*—and the chorus began to stir around us, hundreds of voices taking up a low sustained tone like monks at vigil.

"Midwinter," she said. "The Last Spire. Before the fire fails." She looked at each of them in turn—Serah, Tam, Anselm—as if filing their faces somewhere permanent. "Keep him alive. Not for the realm. The realm can find another fool. Keep him alive because he'll try to spend himself the moment things grow desperate, and the covenant doesn't need a martyr, it needs a *witness*. Someone who heard everyone. Do you understand me?"

"We understand," Serah said, and her voice had that flat iron quality I'd learned meant she meant it absolutely.

"Good." The warden began to thin, the firelight coming through her now instead of bouncing off. "One more kindness, carver, since you've carried my plea so far: the voice that called you—the one in the keepsake. She was my daughter. She's been trying to reach a warden for sixty years. You're the first who answered." The smile again, and this time it wasn't terrible at all. It was grateful, and gratitude from the dead is the heaviest coin in circulation. "Tell no one I thanked you. It ruins our reputation."

And she was gone, and the chorus sighed down into silence like a congregation sitting, and the four of us sat around a small fire on a cold mountain with the largest task in history resting in my satchel like a lump of ordinary bone.

*

Tam fell asleep first, curled against Serah's side the way he still did sometimes when the night got big enough. Anselm sat up late scratching notes by firelight—dates, distances, the arithmetic of forty days—and muttering calculations that grew less hopeful with every page.

I stayed awake. The shard lay against my chest under my shirt, warm as a sleeping bird, and I listened to it the way I'd once listened to Maren's breathing through the wall of our house—proof of life, borrowed, temporary.

Forty days to the end of the world. Forty days to become something I'd spent my whole life refusing to be.

I was still doing that arithmetic when Serah settled beside me, wrapped in her cloak, and said quietly, "The warden told us to keep you alive."

"She told you not to let me spend myself."

"Same thing, from where I'm standing." She poked the fire. "So let's establish terms now, carver, while the priest's too tired to argue theology and the boy's too asleep to volunteer for anything heroic. Whatever happens at that Spire—we come out together. All of us. I've buried enough people who died being noble. I won't bury you."

"You might not get a vote."

"I'm a former captain of the royal hunt, Halloran. Votes are what I *take*." But she said it softly, and after a moment she added, "Maren bought you thirty years of silence so you'd live. Don't make her purchase worthless."

I didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wasn't either a lie or a promise I didn't yet know how to keep.

Above us, past the little white marker and its patient stone dragons, the stars were coming out hard and cold, and somewhere beyond them—south, weeks away, at the top of the world—the last dragon was burning down toward midwinter like a candle in a drafty church.

I touched the shard through my shirt. It pulsed once against my palm, gentle as a heartbeat, and in the space between one pulse and the next I heard—faint, far, threaded through a thousand sleeping voices—

Eyrrith.

Not words. Just awareness. The vast, weary attention of something enormous turning over in its sleep and noticing, for the first time in a hundred years, that someone was coming.

The dead had given me a charge. Now the living fire had given me a deadline.

Forty days.
