## 10. Company of Strays

The first thing you should know about me is that I was branded on my left forearm at age six, in front of the whole village, by a man who smelled like onions and self-righteousness. The brand says *HOLLOW* in letters so ugly they look like they were carved drunk. The second thing you should know is that I have been practicing fighting with a stick for three weeks now, and I still lose to a stick.

"Again," Serah said.

I picked up the stick. My arms had opinions about this, all of them negative.

"You're telegraphing," she said. She was sitting on her heels across from me in the frost-killed grass, eating an apple, which felt like adding insult to injury. "You swing from your shoulder. Swing from your hip. Your shoulder is where you tell people what you're about to do. Your hip is where you keep secrets."

"I don't have any secrets," I said. "I'm twelve."

"Everyone has secrets. The hollow ones especially." She took another bite of apple, watching me with those gray eyes that made you feel like she'd already read the last page of your life and wasn't impressed. "Again."

I swung from the hip, or something near it, and the stick actually whistled, and for one glorious half-second I thought I'd done something right — and then her boot caught my ankle and the sky and the ground traded places and I was flat on my back looking at a very indifferent autumn sky.

"Better," she said.

"That's lying."

"It's encouragement with structure." She stood and offered me a hand up, which she only did when I bled, so I checked my elbow. Yep. "You'll live. Go wash. Anselm wants you at the fire for letters."

"Letters," I said. "Thrilling."

"You want to spend your whole life not being able to read the notices that say they're hunting you?"

She had a point, the way she always did, which was frankly rude of her.

* * *

Anselm taught letters the way he did everything: carefully, apologetically, as if he were constantly asking forgiveness of the alphabet itself. He was a big soft man who had once worn fine robes and now wore a coat with a burnt shoulder, and his hands shook when he held a quill, though never when he held a book. Books, I think, were the one thing in the world that steadied him.

"This is an *a*," he said, drawing it in the dirt beside the fire. "It's a small house with a roof over a little man. See him? There."

"He looks cold," I said.

"He's *inside*, Tam. That's the point of houses."

We worked through the letters while Gideon sat on the other side of the fire carving. He always carved. That night it was a piece of antler, some small shape taking form under his knife, curls of bone falling into the flames and flashing bright before they died. Every so often he'd glance at us, and I got the feeling he wasn't really listening to Anselm's lesson but to something else entirely — something under the wind, under the crackle of the fire, under everything.

Gideon heard things. I didn't know exactly what, then. I knew Serah knew, because she'd gone quiet and strange after whatever happened between them on the road before me, and I knew Anselm suspected, because Anselm suspected everything and apologized for most of it.

"What are you making?" I asked him, mostly to give my hand a break from copying an *s* that looked like a dead snake.

Gideon turned the antler toward the light. It was a bird. A small wren, wings folded, beak tipped up like it was listening for dawn.

"For you," he said. "When it's done. You hold it at night. It helps."

"Helps what?"

"The listening." His eyes came back to mine, and there was something in them — kindness, but also a kind of tiredness so old it looked like geology. "You hear things too, don't you. At night. In the quiet places."

I went hot all over. Because yes. Yes, I did. Not voices, nothing so grand — but feelings. Pressures. On certain roads, near certain stones, a sense of standing in a room where someone had just left, except the someone had been gone a hundred years. The village priest had said that was how the devil found hollow children, through the cracks in us. That was why we got branded. Cracks let things in.

"I don't hear anything," I said.

"No," Gideon agreed mildly, and went back to his wren, and somehow that was worse and better at the same time.

Anselm cleared his throat and drew another letter in the dirt. "*B*. Two bellies. A well-fed letter."

I laughed, and Gideon smiled down at his carving, and Serah came back from checking the treeline and dropped onto a log with a groan like a felled tree, and just like that — without anyone saying it out loud, because nobody ever did — we were a family. A ridiculous one. A huntress who'd quit hunting, a priest who'd quit believing, a carver who heard the dead, and a hollow boy with a stick and a dead snake alphabet.

I would've fought the whole crown for that fire. Which turned out to be roughly the correct estimate of the situation.

* * *

The bounty notices started in a market town called Ferren's Cross, three days later.

I saw them first because I was the one sent in to buy bread — the logic being that nobody posts guards to watch for a branded boy, since hollow children aren't worth guarding against, only throwing stones at. There's an advantage to being despised: invisibility. I recommend it to no one.

There were five notices nailed to the market board. Four of them were the usual — runaway laborers, a tax-dodger, a woman accused of hedge-craft whose crime, per the notice, was "unlicensed drying of herbs," which made me want to burn the whole board down on principle alone.

The fifth one stopped my feet like a wall.

It was a drawing. Good, too — whoever drew it had seen him properly. The long face, the careful hands, the eyes that looked at you slightly past yourself. Underneath, in letters I could now, thanks to Anselm, actually read:

**GIDEON HALLORAN. BONE-CARVER. TOUCHED BY THE OLD COVENANT. SURRENDER ALIVE TO THE CROWN'S HUNT — REWARD OF FIFTY GOLD CROWNS. HE IS TO BE TAKEN UNMARKED. FAILURE TO REPORT HIM IS TREASON.**

*Treason.* For not handing over a man who carved birds out of antler and taught hollow boys to listen without fear.

Fifty crowns. I stood there doing arithmetic with my heart hammering. Fifty crowns was a farm. Fifty crowns was never being hungry again, never being branded, never being invisible. Fifty crowns was what I was worth to every stranger in that square if I just walked to the guardhouse and pointed.

I bought the bread. My hands shook doing it. Then I walked out of Ferren's Cross as fast as a boy can walk without running, which is a specific and miserable speed, and I didn't stop shaking until the trees closed behind me.

They were waiting at the campsite, and I must have looked wrong, because Serah was on her feet before I was through the trees, and Gideon set down his knife.

"There's a picture of you," I said. "In town. On the boards. Fifty crowns, alive, unmarked." I thrust the bread at nobody in particular. "Treason, if you don't report it."

Silence. Serah took the notice question out of the air between us just by looking at me — she didn't need the paper; her face did the reading.

"How good a likeness?" she asked.

"They got your eyes," I told Gideon. "Sorry. They got everything else wrong, but the eyes are right."

Gideon nodded slowly, the way he did, as if bad news were a stone he was turning over to see what lived underneath. "Fifty crowns alive," he said. "That's new. The hunt doesn't usually pay for alive."

"That's what I thought," Serah said grimly. "Alive means they want to question him. Alive means somebody important knows what he can do." She looked at Gideon, and something passed between them that I wasn't invited into — some history from before me, from the road, from whatever had made her quit. "We move tonight. Deeper into the hills. Away from markets for a while."

"I can go back," I heard myself say. Everyone looked at me. "For supplies. Nobody watches a hollow boy. I'm — I'm useful that way. Invisible."

Serah crouched down to my level, which she almost never did, and studied me with those eyes. "You walked past fifty crowns with your name nowhere on it," she said, "and came back to warn the man instead. Do you know how many grown soldiers I served with who couldn't have done that?"

"Some?" I guessed.

"All of them," she said. "Every single one." She flicked my ear, gently, which from Serah was roughly equivalent to an embrace from anyone else. "You're not invisible to us, Tam. Get used to it."

I got used to it slowly, the way you get used to warm water, flinching at first.

* * *

We moved into the high country, and the days took on a shape I came to love so much it frightened me, because I already knew how the world treated things I loved.

Mornings: Serah and the stick, and gradually the stick began to lose, and then one gray morning my practice blade — a real one, short, wooden-hilted, terrifyingly heavy — actually rang off hers twice before she put me down. She laughed. Actually laughed, a short rusty sound like a gate opening after years shut. I thought about that laugh for a week.

Afternoons: Anselm and letters, which became words, which became sentences, which became — one unforgettable evening — me reading aloud from one of his salvaged books while he wept quietly into his beard, apparently at the sound of his own life's work being received by an audience of one. The book was a history of the covenant, dry as August, and I loved it anyway because it was mine, the reading of it, the first thing anyone had ever given me that no one could take back by branding me.

Evenings: Gideon.

He taught me to listen. Not to voices — he was careful around that subject, careful the way you're careful near a frozen river — but to listening itself. How to sit still until the forest stopped performing for you and started just being. How to hear the difference between wind moving and wind being moved. How to close your eyes and feel where the fire was without looking, feel where each of us sat, the whole camp drawn in warmth and breath.

"Now," he said one night, when I'd gotten halfway decent at it, "listen further."

"Further than what?"

"Just further."

So I listened. And out there past the firelight, past the treeline, in the cold dark — there was something. Not a voice. A pressure, like the memory of a hand on my shoulder. Like standing in a doorway someone kind had recently stood in.

My whole body went rigid. Old lessons, onion-smelling lessons, rose up: *cracks let things in, hollow boy.*

"Breathe," Gideon said quietly. "Whatever it is — it isn't reaching for you. Things only reach when you run. Listen without fear. Fear is loud. It drowns out everything gentler."

"What if the gentle thing is worse?"

"Then you'll hear it honestly," he said, "instead of hearing your fear about it. That's all any of us can do." A pause. His knife whispered along the antler. "That's more than most ever learn."

I breathed. The pressure out there in the dark didn't leave, but it changed — softened, the way a stray dog softens when you stop throwing stones at it. I sat with it until my legs went numb, and when I finally opened my eyes, Gideon was watching me with an expression I didn't have a word for yet. Later I learned the word. It was *grief*, and underneath it, *hope*, and I think he was grieving the hope, or hoping against the grief, or both.

He gave me the wren that night. Finished. Its wings folded, its beak lifted toward a dawn that hadn't come yet.

"Hold it when it gets loud," he said.

"What gets loud?"

He just smiled, tired and warm, and patted the ground beside him, and I sat there holding a bird carved from bone while the fire burned down, and for the first time in my branded, thrown-stones-at, invisible life, I fell asleep feeling watched over instead of watched.

* * *

Of course, that's when it got dangerous. Belonging always arrives with the bill attached. I understand that now. I understood it then too, the morning Serah came back from scouting the pass with a bounty notice crumpled in her fist and her face like a closed door.

They'd updated it. The drawing was better — it had the hands now, the careful hands — and the reward was higher, and there was a new line at the bottom in fresh ink:

**KNOWN ASSOCIATES TO BE DETAINED FOR QUESTIONING.**

Under it, a second, smaller notice, newer still, paper barely dry:

**JUVENILE, BRANDED, APPROX. TWELVE YEARS. LAST SEEN FERREN'S CROSS. BELIEVED TRAVELING WITH THE ABOVE.**

They had a description of me. Of *me*. Somebody in Ferren's Cross had reported a hollow boy buying bread with shaking hands, and now I was on a board somewhere too, worth however many crowns a hollow boy goes for, which I promise you is not fifty.

"Well," I said, when Serah showed me, aiming for wry and landing somewhere nearer to sick, "at least I'm wanted."

Nobody laughed. Gideon put his hand on my head, briefly, the way you'd steady a cup.

"We split the risk differently," Serah was saying, pacing. "Tam walks point with me — a branded kid and a woman reads as mother and son, nobody looks twice. Anselm and Gideon hang back—"

"No," Gideon said.

It was the first time I'd ever heard him interrupt anyone. Serah stopped pacing. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"They'll be watching the roads for pairs like that," Gideon said. "A woman and a branded child, heading for the hills — that's a story every guard in the realm has been trained to pull apart. But an old priest walking alone is nothing. A carver traveling to work is nothing." He looked around our fire, at each of us, and I realized his eyes were wet and his voice was perfectly steady. "But four strays together draw every eye in the valley. So we stay together, and we stop pretending we're anything but what we are. A company. Of strays." A ghost of a smile. "Strays bite, Serah. Ask any dog-catcher in the realm."

Serah stared at him for a long moment. Then she snorted — the closest thing to surrender I'd ever gotten out of her — and sat down, and started dividing our food with more force than the bread deserved.

That night I lay with the wren against my chest and listened past the firelight, past the treeline, into the dark. Something was out there. More than one something. Pressures, movements, the feeling of doors standing open where doors shouldn't be — and beneath it, faint as a pulse under snow, a sound I couldn't name, coming from the north, from the high passes, from wherever the world ended.

Gideon sat up suddenly. I felt him listening too, the whole camp holding its breath around him.

"What is it?" Anselm whispered.

Gideon was quiet a long time. When he answered, his voice had that geological tiredness in it, deeper than I'd ever heard.

"She's louder now," he said. "The warden. Whoever she is — she's been trying to reach me for weeks. And she's getting desperate." He looked north, into the black. "She keeps saying the same thing. *The fire is guttering. Come before midwinter, carver, or there will be nothing left to reforge.*"

Above us, far to the north, above the mountains, the clouds glowed faintly red. Like a hearth going out.

Like the last ember of something enormous, dying.
