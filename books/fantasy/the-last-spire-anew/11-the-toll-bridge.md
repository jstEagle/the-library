## 11. The Toll Bridge

There are three kinds of bridges in this world: the kind you burn behind you, the kind you cross and never think of again, and the kind you know is going to kill you.

The Toll Bridge at Marrow's Crossing was the third kind. I knew it the way a soldier knows a bad piece of ground—by the ache in my teeth, by the way my hand kept drifting to a sword I no longer carried, by the simple arithmetic of it. One bridge, narrow enough for two riders abreast or four people on foot. Two approaches. And on the far side, sitting in the tollhouse like he'd been expecting us for days—which, knowing Dain Corvath, he had—my former lieutenant.

"Tell me again why we can't go around," Tam said.

"Because around means three days through the fens," Anselm said, "and the fens mean fever, and fever means we arrive at the Spire too late to matter."

"Because," Gideon said quietly, "the dead told her to stop."

I hadn't told him that. Not in those words. But Gideon Halloran had a way of hearing things you said without saying them, which was the whole problem with Gideon Halloran, and also the reason I'd deserted an army for him, so I suppose it balanced out.

We crouched in the alder scrub above the river and looked at the bridge like it might look back. It did, more or less. The tollhouse lamp burned. A figure stood on the span itself, arms crossed, watching our side of the water with the patience of a man being paid by the day.

"That's Corvath," I said.

"You've mentioned," Gideon said. "Twice. You mentioned he's good. You mentioned he doesn't miss."

"He doesn't." I settled deeper into the wet brush. "Dain Corvath. Twelve years in the hunt with me. He pulled me out of the Ashfen fire when I was nineteen. He held the line at Coldmarket when half the company ran. He's not cruel, Gideon. That's what makes him dangerous. Cruel men break. Dain just *obeys*, and obedience doesn't crack, it only bends until something snaps it."

"And what snapped him?"

"Nothing yet. That's the trouble." I watched the figure shift. Even at this distance I could read his stance the way other people read letters. Weight forward. Sword loose. Not bored. Waiting for exactly what he'd been promised. "He knows we're coming because the bounty notices reached Marrow's Crossing before we did. Your face is worth eight hundred crowns now. Mine's worth five hundred and a court-martial. The priest's worth nothing, which he'll find insulting. The boy—"

"They don't know about the boy," Tam said. "I'm not on any notice."

"No," I said. "You're not. Remember that."

Because here was the shape of the thing I'd been turning over since dawn, the shape I hadn't shown anyone, the way you keep a knife under your coat and smile at customs men. Corvath had come for us with a tollhouse and a squad—I counted six shadows moving behind the lamp, maybe eight—and he had come early, which meant he'd been told where we'd be, which meant someone upstream had sold us, which meant every road ahead of this one was already bought and paid for. The bridge wasn't a trap. The bridge was the *only* door, and Dain was standing in it politely, the way the hunt always did, letting you believe you had a choice.

But traps have architecture, and architecture has assumptions, and every assumption Dain made rested on one fact: that Serah Vane would run.

* * *

We argued about it in the reeds while the light went from grey to gold to the bruised purple that meant we were running out of time to be clever.

"We go at dark," Anselm said. "I've read of crossings under cover of—"

"There's no cover. The lamp burns all night, the water's loud but shallow, and Dain posts pickets up and down the bank. I taught him how." I said it flatly, because flattery was wasted on the situation. "We go at dark, we die at dark. We go now, we die tired. Neither option appeals."

"Then we swim," Tam said. "The boy who grew up in a fen town says we swim."

"The river's snowmelt. You'd be dead of cold before the far bank, and if you weren't, you'd be too stiff to climb out." I looked at Gideon. He was doing his listening thing—head tilted, eyes somewhere else, hands resting on the satchel where the bone shard rode against his ribs. "And you?"

"Maren's voice has been quiet today," he said. "That usually means she wants me to work it out myself." A pause. "She used to say the valley taught patience because everything worth keeping takes time. She didn't finish the lesson before—" His jaw tightened. "What are you not telling us, Serah?"

I should have lied. I'm good at lying; twelve years in the hunt will do that. But there's a limit to how many lies you can stack on top of a man who hears the dead, so I told the truth, and the truth came out sounding like what it was: a plan I'd built alone, the way I built everything, because building alone meant breaking alone.

"Corvath wants me," I said. "Not primarily—he wants you, carver, you're the prize—but I'm the desertion. I'm the stain on the hunt's honor, and my old commander wants me brought back breathing so she can make an example of me properly. Which means Dain's orders are probably *take Vane alive*. And orders like that make a man careful. Careful men watch the prize, not the bait."

Nobody said anything. The river said a great deal. Then Anselm, slowly, with the tone of a man reading scripture aloud at his own funeral: "You're offering yourself."

"I'm offering to walk out onto that bridge with my hands empty and give Dain exactly what his orders say he wants. While he's securing me—and he'll be thorough, because I trained him to be thorough—you three go over the tollhouse roof, through the loft window, down the far stair. It's a two-count gap, maybe three. Enough."

"It's not enough," Gideon said.

"It's what exists."

"It's not *enough*, Serah." His voice cracked on it, and for once I saw the bone-carver plain underneath the quiet—the man who'd spent his whole life pretending not to hear grief, holding all of ours now like stones. "You walk onto that bridge and they take you to Ironhold, and Ironhold is where they put the people they want to forget. I've heard what comes out of that city. The dead there don't even sound like people anymore."

"I know what Ironhold is."

"Do you? Because I hear it every night. Thousands of them. Pressed into vaults like grain in a silo. If you go in there—"

"If I stay here, we all go in there, because the alternative routes are closed and the Spire doesn't wait for midwinter out of courtesy." I stood, brushing mud off my knees, because it's easier to be hard on your feet. "Listen to me. All of you. I burned a village last spring. I stood in the square at Hollowmere and watched a house full of gifted children go up because my commander said the order was lawful. I have been the hunt's instrument for twelve years, and the only thing I've ever done right was the day I walked away from it. This—" I gestured at the bridge, the lamp, the patient shadow of Dain Corvath, "—this is the payment. I'd rather pay it than owe it."

"That's not courage," Gideon said. "That's arithmetic. Maren taught me the difference."

"Maren sounds like she was smarter than both of us."

"She was." He rose too, and for a moment I thought he was going to argue more, and I braced for it, because Gideon arguing is like water arguing—it finds every seam. Instead he just looked at me with those grey eyes that had listened to the dead for thirty years and said, "If you do this, I'm coming back for you."

"Don't."

"I'm coming back for you. So whatever you tell Dain, whatever story you build to keep him off our trail—build it knowing it won't hold. Build it to buy hours, not weeks. Because I don't leave family on bridges."

Family. He said it the way Tam had started saying it—casually, like it cost nothing, like it was a word you could just *have*. My throat did something inconvenient. I turned away before it showed.

"Anselm," I said. "Roof route. Confirm it."

"The loft window is barred," Anselm said miserably. "I checked this morning while you slept. Someone barred it within the last year."

Of course they had. Dain Corvath, thorough. I laughed—a short, ugly sound—and the plan rearranged itself in my head with the grim efficiency of a battlefield dressing. No roof. Fine.

Then the bait would have to be better bait.

* * *

I walked onto the bridge at dusk with my hands open and my old insignia torn off, and I made sure Dain saw me coming from fifty yards, because surprise is for ambushes and this was a surrender.

"Well," Dain Corvath said. He'd gone silver at the temples since Coldmarket. Otherwise unchanged—same broad frame, same scar splitting his left eyebrow, same voice like a millstone. "Serah Vane. They said you'd gone soft. They didn't say you'd gone stupid."

"Both. It's been a productive year." I stopped ten feet from him, well outside sword range, and let him look at me. Let him count my empty scabbard, my road-worn coat, the exhaustion I wasn't bothering to hide. "Eight men?"

"Ten. You're slipping. Used to be you'd have said nine."

"You always hid one extra in the trees. Old habit." I nodded past him, toward the tollhouse. "They crossed yesterday. The carver and the priest. Took the north road toward Highmoor. You're a day behind them."

It was a good lie. I'd built it all afternoon—specific, verifiable-sounding, aimed exactly at the place where ambition meets impatience. Dain wanted the capture credited to himself, not shared with the garrisons downstream. Every hour he chased north was an hour we bought.

Dain studied me for a long moment. Then he smiled, and it was the saddest thing I'd seen all year.

"You're lying," he said. "Badly. You bite your lip on the left side when you lie. You've done it since you were nineteen and I pulled you out of the fire."

The river roared. Behind me, somewhere in the alders, I knew Gideon was listening and hating every second.

"Dain—"

"They're in the reeds, aren't they. Right now." He stepped closer, and I saw the ten shadows resolve out of the dusk along both banks, closing like a hand. "You're standing on my bridge as bait, Serah, and the worst part is you're doing it for free. There's a bounty on you. Five hundred crowns. You could've at least negotiated."

"Let them go," I said. "The carver's done nothing. The priest is a madman with a satchel of papers. The boy is nobody. Take me. My commander wants me alive and making speeches in chains—that's worth more to her career than a bone-carver's head. You know how she counts."

"I do know how she counts." Something moved behind Dain's eyes—not cruelty, never cruelty, just that terrible obedient sorrow. "That's why I can't let them go, Serah. The order changed last month. It's not containment anymore. I've seen the sealed writ." He said it quietly, the way men say things they wish weren't true. "Extermination. Everyone touched by the covenant, everyone sheltering them, before midwinter. Before the Spire. I don't know what happens at the Spire. I know I'm ordered to make sure nothing does."

The bottom dropped out of my stomach. Extermination. Not the hunt I'd served, then—something worse wearing its coat. And Dain had just handed me the truth for free, because some part of him still needed me to know what he was carrying.

"So take me in," I said. "Take me to her. I'll tell her what I've seen out here—the hollow children, the villages. Maybe I can't change her mind. But you'll have done the one decent—"

"Hands," Dain said, almost gently. Behind me, boots in the mud, many boots. "I'm sorry, Serah. Truly. But you taught me that sentiment is what gets soldiers killed, and I'd very much like neither of us to die tonight."

They bound my wrists with cord, not iron—considerate, or cautious; with Dain it was always both—and walked me toward the tollhouse while the lamps came up along the banks and men waded into the alders after my family.

* * *

Here is what I learned about being captured: it is mostly waiting.

They put me in the tollhouse cellar, which smelled of tallow and old fish, and posted two guards on the stairs, and left me alone with the sound of the river and the sound of my own failure doing laps in my skull. I'd bought them nothing. Dain had seen through the lie in four seconds. The others would be taken within the hour, and by morning we'd all be cargo on the Ironhold road, and the sealed writ would grind us all fine.

Except.

Except that Gideon Halloran had said *I'm coming back for you*, in that flat valley voice, the way he says things that are simply true—true like stone, true like weather—and I lay in the dark of that cellar and realized I believed him, and realized further that believing him was the most frightening thing that had happened all day, more frightening than the cord on my wrists.

Because rescuing me meant walking into the trap I'd built to save them. Every guard positioned for a rescue attempt, every approach watched, their faces on bounty notices nailed to every post between here and the mountains. Loyalty, I thought, staring at the ceiling beams. What a stupid, magnificent, ruinous thing. I'd spent twelve years learning to rely on no one, and these three—the deserter's stray, the runaway priest, the branded boy—had gone and made reliance feel like the only honest currency left.

Footsteps on the stairs. Not the guards' rhythm—lighter, uneven, favoring one side. The door opened on a slice of lamplight and a familiar silhouette, and my heart did something complicated and painful.

"You absolute idiot," I whispered.

"Gideon," Tam breathed, sliding inside, pulling the door nearly shut. Blood on his sleeve—his own, I'd bet. "There were only two on the back wall. Serah, the writ—did he tell you—"

"Extermination. Yes." I sat up, cords and all. "How did you get past the pickets?"

"The dead walked them," Gideon said simply, from the doorway, where he stood pale as paper with the shard glinting at his throat. "Not—moved them. *Spoke* to them. There are dead in this river, Serah. Drowned toll-keepers, a soldier from the border wars. They've been waiting a long time for someone to ask. The pickets heard footsteps in the fog and went to investigate ghosts." His mouth twisted. "I'm learning that my gift is more useful than it is comfortable. Maren would be appalled. Or proud. Possibly both."

"You shouldn't be here."

"Probably not." He knelt and began working at my bonds with a bone knife, quick and precise, a carver's hands. Close up I could see what the crossing had cost him—the tremor in his fingers, the voices pressing behind his eyes like a crowd at a gate. "Anselm's watching the road. We have perhaps twenty minutes before Dain counts his men and notices the cellar guard changed shifts. So. Can you fight?"

"My wrists are raw and I've been lying on a fish-smelling floor. I can fight."

"Good." He cut the last cord and looked up at me, and there was no wryness in him at all, none of the gentle irony he wore like armor. Just that grave, listening certainty. "Because Dain's orders changed an hour ago. New riders came from the north. I heard the guard talking about it downstairs." He swallowed. "They're not taking you to Ironhold, Serah. They're taking everyone to the Spire. The whole hunt. To bury the truth under the mountain with the dragon."

Outside, across the water, a horn sounded—one long note rolling up the valley, answered by another from the north road. Lanterns bloomed along the bank like a string of orange flowers, dozens of them, converging.

Gideon stood and offered me his hand.

"So," he said. "Coming back for you worked. Now we just have to get out."
