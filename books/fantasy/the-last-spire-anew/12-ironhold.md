## 12. Ironhold

There is a particular sound a city makes when it is built on top of a secret. I had never heard it before Ironhold, because the Hollow Vale kept no secrets louder than whose turn it was to mend the mill fence. But crossing the causeway at dusk, pressed into a line of charcoal-carts with Serah's hand fisted in my collar like I was a dog she'd bought off a drunk, I heard it: a hum beneath the wheels, beneath the cobbles, beneath the river. A sound like a held breath that has been held for so long the lungs have forgotten how to empty.

The dead were down there. Thousands of them.

I want to say I knew it immediately, the way heroes in ballads know things—clean and certain, with trumpets. The truth is uglier. What I knew first was that my teeth hurt. Then that the bone shard against my sternum had gone cold enough to burn through three layers of wool. And only then, when we passed the first of the black iron grates set flush into the road, breathing out a thin gray vapor that wasn't smoke and wasn't fog, did I understand what I was hearing.

Not voices. Not yet. A pressure. Like standing in a cellar full of people who have all been gagged.

"Stop staring," Serah muttered. She'd cut her hair to her jaw and dyed it with walnut ink, which made her look less like the most decorated hunt-captain in the realm's service and more like a woman who sold fish and regretted it. "Grates are normal here. Everyone stops staring eventually."

"What are they?"

"You already know what they are." Her voice didn't change at all, which is how I knew it cost her. "You've been flinching since the bridge."

* * *

We lodged in a chandler's loft that smelled of tallow and mouse, and Anselm laid out his maps by candle stub like a general, except his army was four people and one of them was me, holding a stolen laundry list upside-down until Tam turned it around without comment.

"The soul-vaults run under the Foundry Quarter," Anselm said, tapping a smudge that might have been a tower or might have been his thumb. "The archives called them reliquaries, officially. Places where the crown 'tends the blessed dead.' Unofficially—" he hesitated. Anselm always hesitated before the parts that had driven him from his church. It was a kind of penance, I think, making himself say it slowly. "Unofficially, they are engines. The dead are pressed—compacted, bound into lattice-work—and their passage generates stillness. Anti-magic. The same quality as your valley, distilled and weaponized."

"That's what the mist-line is," Tam said. He'd been quiet all day, turning his brand-scarred wrist over and over, a habit he thought none of us noticed. "It's not natural. It's built."

"It's fed," Anselm corrected gently. "And the feedstock is people."

I looked at the map. I listened past it, past the creak of the loft, past Serah sharpening a knife with the patience of someone who found sharpening soothing and knives incidental. Down there, under everything, the held breath went on holding.

"How many?" I asked.

Anselm's candle guttered. "The archive gave numbers up to forty years ago. Eleven thousand in Ironhold alone. There are nine other cities."

Eleven thousand. My valley had four hundred souls, and I had carved memorial bones for perhaps sixty of them over fifteen years, and every single name had mattered to me individually, the way a carver knows grain. Eleven thousand was not a number. It was a weather system.

"And the hunt runs on them," Serah said flatly. "Every witch-finder's compass, every suppression net, every cage they put the gifted in. All of it powered by—" She stopped. Put the knife down. Picked it back up. "I signed requisitions for those compasses. I counted them like spoons."

Nobody said anything comforting, because there wasn't any to be said, and we were past the stage of our acquaintance where we lied to each other politely. That was the thing about the company we'd become: we had burned through courtesy somewhere on the road of small ghosts, and what remained underneath was truer and much harder to live with.

"We need to see them," I said.

Everyone looked at me.

"Not free them. Not yet. *See.* If I'm going to carry a covenant-key to the Last Spire, I should know exactly what the crown does with the dead it catches. I should carry that too." I touched the shard through my shirt. It pulsed once against my palm, cold, attentive. "Besides. They're already talking to me. They've been trying since the bridge. It sounds like shouting through a wall, and I would very much like to take the wall down."

"Gideon." Serah rubbed her eyes. "If you get caught, you don't get a cell. You go *in* the vaults. You'd be fuel."

"Yes," I said. "That's rather the point of seeing them first."

* * *

Anselm got us in the way he got us everywhere, which is to say he wore his old chaplain's ring and radiated an air of bureaucratic grievance until doors opened. We went down through the Foundry Quarter's inspection galleries—Serah in a borrowed warden's coat that fit her like an apology, Tam carrying the document satchel and looking, in his oversized cap, like a clerk's child pressed into service—and then down further, past where inspection galleries have any business going, down stairs worn hollow in the center by feet that had been climbing out of something for a century.

The wall of silence hit me on the last landing.

I don't mean quiet. I've known quiet; I lived inside it for half my life, the Vale's mute hush where my gift went deaf. This was the opposite of quiet. This was eleven thousand voices compressed into a space meant for hundreds, packed so tight they couldn't even shape words anymore—just *pressure*, just the raw fact of them, pressing outward against whatever held them in. Standing at the bottom of those stairs felt like standing against a dam with my ear pressed to the stone.

"Oh," I said. It came out very small. "Oh, you poor—"

The gallery opened onto the vault floor.

I had imagined cages. Cells, maybe. Chains. Some human shape to the cruelty, something with bars I could hate. What I saw instead was worse, because it was *beautiful*, in the way that machines are beautiful—the way a loom is beautiful if you forget about the wool.

The vaults were vast glass-and-iron cylinders, tall as trees, ranked in avenues under a ceiling of ribbed stone. Inside each cylinder hung a lattice—a three-dimensional weave of fine silver wire—and pressed into that weave, layer upon layer upon layer, were the dead.

They looked like frost-flowers. That was my first obscene thought: they looked like the ferns of ice Maren used to scrape off the kitchen window, delicate white fractals blooming along the silver. It took me a long moment to understand that each frond of frost was a person, or the part of a person that doesn't stop when breath does—that what I was looking at was thousands upon thousands of souls flattened into ornament, arranged by some engineer's aesthetic sense into pleasing spirals, and that the spirals *turned*. Slowly. Generating, as they turned, that valley-hush, that stillness, feeding it up through conduits in the ceiling to power compasses and nets and cages and the great mist-lines that kept magic out and truth in.

And they were singing.

Not on purpose. No one could sing like that on purpose. But press enough voices close enough together and they resonate, the way a string hums when its neighbor is struck. The whole vault floor thrummed with a chord too vast and too slow to be music—more like geology given grief. Every few minutes the chord shifted, a new note surfacing, and I realized with a lurch that the shifts weren't random. They were *responses*. Something down in that frost was listening to the others. Even pressed into lattice, even harvested, even made into machinery—the dead were still reaching for each other.

"They can't hear each other properly," I whispered. "Look. Look how they lean."

"Don't look," Serah said hoarsely. She was looking anyway. Her face had gone the color of the silver wire. "Saints' teeth, I escorted shipments to this place. I stood on that landing. I drank wine with the vault-wardens."

Tam had gone very still beside the nearest cylinder, his forehead almost touching the glass, and I saw his lips moving. Reading, maybe. Or praying. Or both—he'd learned his letters from a defrocked priest, after all; the two activities were probably fused in him forever.

A warden came down the avenue on his rounds, lantern swinging, keys chiming, and we became furniture: clerks checking seals, a captain inspecting her charge, a boy waiting on his elders. The warden barely glanced at us. Why would he? Who comes down here to grieve?

Who indeed.

Because that's what I was doing, I realized, standing among the engines with a forged seal in my pocket. Not infiltrating. Grieving. Fifteen years in the Valley pretending I couldn't hear the dead, carving bones blind, and here was the proof of what happens everywhere else—here was the harvest my silence had been sheltering me from. The crown hadn't just broken a covenant. It had taken the one thing death guarantees everyone—a continuing, however faint, however strange—and turned it into lamp oil.

The shard against my chest had stopped being cold. It was *ringing*, a frequency below hearing, and I understood suddenly why it had led us here, or at least hadn't objected. Bone remembers bone. And this place was full of it.

* * *

It was Tam who heard the fragment.

We were nearly out—up the hollowed stairs, almost to the landing—when his hand shot out and grabbed my sleeve with a strength that surprised us both. "Gideon. There's one. Off to the side. It's—it's *wrong*, the others are all in the big ones, but there's a small jar behind the pillar and something's in it."

A small jar. Behind a pillar. I should have walked past. Every sensible instinct I owned, every survival lesson Serah had beaten into me on the road, voted loudly for walking past.

Instead I followed Tam back down six steps and around a pillar of ribbed stone, and there it sat: a reliquary the size of a honey pot, glass gone cloudy, holding what looked at first like nothing at all. Just a curl of vapor. Just a breath.

But it was a breath that moved when I moved.

I knelt. And the wall between us—thin already, thinner here, thinnest of all for a man with a covenant-key in his shirt—took one more blow and came down.

*—cold, cold, they took me at the ferry, I only wanted to cross the ferry, my sister is waiting, my sister has been waiting sixty years, is she dead yet, is she—*

One voice. One small, frayed, ordinary voice. Not a warden's prize, not an engine's component—a fragment, a scrap left over when they'd pressed someone into the lattice and missed a piece, swept into a jar like a spilled coin. Someone's sister-waiter. Someone's ferry-crossing. Sixty years in a jar behind a pillar while the empire ran its lamps.

"I hear you," I whispered.

The vapor flared. The whole vault-floor chord shivered, bent toward us like grass toward wind, and I felt it happen—the attention of eleven thousand dead swinging around and fixing on the one living man kneeling in their prison who could hear them.

*You hear,* said the fragment, wonder cracking through the fraying. *You HEAR.*

"Hush," I breathed. "Hush, yes. I'm going to open the jar."

Serah's hand closed on my shoulder. "Gideon. If that thing screams—"

"It won't scream." I didn't know how I knew. I knew. "It's been quiet sixty years. It's forgotten how to make noise. It only knows how to *wait*, and it's done waiting." I worked the glass lid loose with my thumbnail, hands steadier than they had any right to be. "I can't free the others tonight. I know that. I know it, and it's the worst thing I'll ever know. But I'm not leaving *this one* behind a pillar."

The lid came off.

The fragment rose out of the jar like a thread of smoke drawn upward by love, coiling once around my wrist—cold, cold, but a clean cold now, a running-water cold—and I felt it pass through the shard, felt the key drink it in, felt it *go*. Where, I couldn't say. Outward. Upward. Into the spaces between realms that had been nailed shut for a century. Free.

And the chord of eleven thousand dead broke.

Not loud—nothing in that place could afford to be loud—but every voice in every lattice leaned at once, and the resonance that powered the anti-magic engines stuttered, hiccupped, dropped for three heartbeats into something ragged and human and *awake*. Somewhere above us a compass needle spun. Somewhere a witch-finder swore at his instruments. Somewhere a mist-line flickered like a candle in a draft.

Then the chord smoothed itself, the machinery swallowed the stumble, and the vault was orderly again. Frost-flowers turning. Lamps burning on the pressed dead.

But the wardens had noticed the flicker. Boots on the upper landings. Keys chiming faster.

"Up," Serah said. "Now. Walk, don't run."

We climbed. At the top of the stairs I allowed myself one look back down the avenue of glass trees, and I let myself be heard—not aloud, not in any way the wardens' instruments could catch, but the way I had once spoken to bones across a shop counter in a valley that couldn't hear me.

*I'm coming back,* I told them. *All of you. Hold on. Hold on to each other, if you can't hold anything else. I'm coming back with fire.*

And from eleven thousand throats pressed into silver wire, wordless, vast, patient as geology and grieving as people, the answer rose through my boots and my bones and the ringing shard:

*We know your name now, bone-carver. We will keep it for you.*

I have carried a lot of things since I fled the Hollow Vale—a ghost's plea, a murdered warden's charge, my mother's last words like a coal in my chest. None of it weighed anything next to eleven thousand voices knowing my name.

Behind us, the alarm bells of Ironhold began to ring.
