## 13. What the Dead Ask

The dead do not knock. I learned that much in the first night after Ironhold, when the fragment I'd freed came pouring through the walls of my skull like water through a cracked dam, and every voice in it wanted something.

I had expected gratitude, maybe. Or peace—the quiet that the warden's ghost had promised would follow the dead once they were loosed from the soul-vaults. Instead what I got was a thousand strangers arguing in a language made of grief.

They gathered in the ruin of an old shepherd's hut three miles east of the fortress walls, where Serah had stashed us while Tam's ankle healed and Anselm's cough ran its course. The hut was a good choice, or would have been, if "good" were a word that applied to anything we did anymore. Stone walls thick enough to muffle a scream, a roof half-collapsed but holding, and not another living soul for miles. The dead didn't need the door. They came through me.

It started as a hum at the edge of hearing—familiar, almost comfortable, the way a fever can feel familiar on its third day. By midnight it was a choir. By dawn it was a mob.

*You opened the vault,* they said, all of them at once, in a hundred different accents, some so old the words themselves had worn smooth like river stones. *You put your hand into the crown's machinery and pulled one thread free, and now the whole weave knows your name.*

"I didn't mean to," I said out loud, to an empty room, which is the kind of thing that gets you branded gifted even in a valley full of people pretending you aren't. "I was trying to find Maren."

*We know,* said the fragment—that was what they called themselves, the Fragment, though there was nothing fragmentary about them anymore. Word of my little act of vandalism had spread through whatever passes for gossip among the harvested dead, and spirits had come drifting in from all across the realm, drawn to the one living man who could hear them. *We know what you seek. We have her.*

The room went very still. So did I.

"She's dead," I said. "I watched her die."

*Dead is not gone. Not here. Not with the boundary fraying.* The voices layered over each other, urgent, insistent. *She crossed at the mist-line with her last breath caught between worlds. She is close, Gideon Halloran. Closer than any dead woman has been in a century. And she is angry.*

Of course she was angry. Maren had died buying time for a boy who wasn't even hers by blood, shot down by a hunt-captain's crossbow in her own doorway, and if there was one thing Maren Okonkwo could not abide, it was unfinished work.

"What do you want?" I asked.

*What we have always wanted. What the crown took from us when they pressed our souls into their engines.* The voices rose, and the hut's remaining window rattled in its frame. *Justice. Vengeance, if justice is too slow. We have been prisoners for a hundred years, carver. We have felt our own deaths used as fuel to hunt our children's children. And now the man who freed a piece of us stands within reach of the machine itself.*

"You want me to destroy Ironhold."

*We want you to unmake it. Every vault, every engine, every chain. And we will give you the power to do it. All of us. Every spirit within calling distance, poured through one vessel—*

"No," I said.

*—through you.*

"I said no."

The voices went quiet, which was somehow worse than the shouting. In the silence I could hear Tam breathing in his sleep in the corner, the small hitching rhythm of a boy who had learned too young that sleep was something you had to defend. Anselm murmured scripture in his dreams—not because he believed any of it anymore, but because old habits are the last things to starve.

*You refuse,* said the Fragment, and there was no question in it. Only weight.

"I refuse."

*Explain.*

I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged at the edges. "You want me to explain why I won't let ten thousand ghosts wear me like a coat and march on a fortress city? Fine. Here's your explanation." I stood up, because sitting felt like surrender, and paced the length of the hut, stepping over Tam's blanket, avoiding the hole in the floor where the sheep had apparently made its opinions known before the shepherds abandoned the place. "Ironhold is a horror. I know. I walked its halls. I heard what's in those vaults—you don't have to describe it to me, I was *there*. And yes, I want it burned. I want it burned down to the bedrock and the bedrock salted and the salt cursed."

*Then let us—*

"But not like that." I stopped at the window, looking east toward the fortress, a smudge of smoke against the grey morning sky. "You're asking me to become exactly what the crown became. A vessel stuffed full of stolen souls, pointed at people, unleashed. That's all their engines are. That's all their *hunt* is. Souls in a box, doing violence on command." I pressed my palm flat against the cold stone. "If I say yes, then the difference between me and Witch-Hunter Vane's old commander is just a matter of whose ghosts are angrier."

*The difference is that ours are justified.*

"So were theirs, once. Everyone's ghosts are justified. That's what makes this so damned dangerous." I turned back to the empty room, to the presence that filled it like smoke fills a chimney. "Maren taught me better. She used to say—"

My throat closed. I hadn't said her name out loud since the mist-line. Saying it felt like opening a wound to check if it had healed, knowing it hadn't.

*Say it,* the Fragment urged, gentler now, and that gentleness was the cruelest weapon they had. *She is right here. One word from you, one thread of consent, and you can hear her again. Not the memory. Not the echo we carry. Her. She has waited months to tell you—*

"To tell me what?" My voice cracked like green wood. "That she's proud of me? That she forgives the elders who voted to hand me over? That dying for me was worth it?" I shook my head. "Or maybe she wants to tell me to do exactly what you're asking. Maybe she's leading this chorus. Maybe that's the bait."

Silence again. But it was a different silence—a considering silence, and underneath it, faint as a candle behind a wall, something warm.

*Would it change your answer?*

"No."

*Liar,* said a thousand voices, almost fondly. *But an honest liar. That is rarer than you think.*

I sat back down on the floor, my back against the wall, because my legs had decided the conversation was over whether the rest of me agreed or not. Outside, dawn was doing its slow, reluctant work on the mountains, turning the snow pink and gold like something worth saving.

"Here's what I can offer instead," I said. "I'm going to the Last Spire. I'm going to reforge the covenant, and when the realms knit back together, the boundary that lets the crown harvest souls is going to fail forever. No more vaults. No more engines. Every prisoner in Ironhold goes free—not through my anger, but through the world healing itself. It's slower. It's worse. It might get us all killed on that mountain. But it's *right*, and right matters more than fast."

*And if the Spire fails? If the dragon dies before midwinter? If the crown reaches you first?* The voices hardened again, frost creeping back in. *Then all our waiting, all your refusing, will have bought nothing, and Ironhold will still be standing, and we will still be fuel.*

"Then I'll have lost honestly," I said. "Which is more than the crown can claim."

For a long moment, nothing. Then the warmth behind the wall flickered—and moved. Came closer. And a single voice separated itself from the chorus, low and dry and familiar as bread, and said:

*"Stubborn as your father. Worse, actually. He knew when to quit."*

I stopped breathing.

*Maren speaks for herself now,* said the Fragment, and there was something almost bitter in it. *She does not ask what we ask. She never does.*

"Maren." Her name came out of me like a splinter leaving flesh. "Maren, please—"

*We part in anger, carver,* the Fragment said, drowning her out, the chorus closing over her voice like water over a stone. *Remember that you chose this. Remember, when the Spire breaks you, that we offered you everything and you asked for nothing but permission to suffer alone. When the crown comes—and it is coming—we will not come to help. The dead keep accounts, Gideon Halloran. Yours is now open.*

And then they were gone.

Not faded. Gone—the way a candle isn't just blown out but snatched from the room, the dark rushing in to fill the space where the light had been. For the first time since crossing the mist-line, I heard nothing. The silence of the Hollow Vale, the silence I had built my whole life inside of, delivered to me now like a punishment.

Except—

At the very edge of it, so faint I might have imagined it, something warm. Something that smelled, absurdly, of woodsmoke and juniper tea and the particular soap Maren rendered from goat tallow every autumn.

*"Silly boy,"* it said, or seemed to say, or perhaps only felt like saying. *"You did right. Now finish it."*

Then even that was gone, and I sat alone in the ruined hut while the sun climbed, listening to two sleeping men breathe, and understood that I had just refused the only easy road left to me.

* * *

Serah found me there an hour later, sitting in the same spot, whittling a piece of scrap bone into nothing in particular. She looked at the shavings around my feet, then at my face, and did the thing she did best, which was assess a situation without asking a single unnecessary question.

"The dead came," she said.

"They came."

"And?"

"And I told them no." I set the knife down before I could carve a groove into my own thumb. "They offered me everything, Serah. Ten thousand souls, all aimed at Ironhold. One word from me and the whole fortress comes down."

Her jaw tightened. He was thinking about the village. The one she'd burned before she quit, the one that lived behind her eyes like a debt she couldn't pay. "And you said no."

"I said no."

She lowered herself onto the bench across from me, armor creaking, and studied me for a long moment with those grey eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. "Why?"

"Because someone had to."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got." I leaned my head back against the stone. "Their plan would've worked, Serah. That's the worst part. Ten minutes, maybe less, and Ironhold is rubble and every soul in those vaults walks free. Fast, clean, righteous." I closed my eyes. "And then what? The realm learns that a bone-carver and an army of ghosts can topple a fortress whenever the mood strikes. The next person with my gift hears about it. And the one after that. And eventually somebody says yes who shouldn't have, and we've invented a new kind of hunt, except this time the hunters are the dead."

Serah was quiet for a while. Then: "Vane's commander would've taken the offer."

"Vane's commander built the offer. Somebody just handed him a different army."

A ghost of a smile crossed her face—there and gone, like most things with Serah. "Anselm's going to be insufferable about this. He's going to call it a moral triumph and quote something dreary at you."

"Let him. It'll be the happiest he's been since the monastery."

From the corner, Tam stirred, blinked awake, and looked between us with the sharp, wary intelligence that had kept him alive through branding and exile and a winter on the road. "Did the ghosts leave?" he asked. "It got quiet. Quiet like the Vale."

"Yeah," I said. "They left."

"Was it bad? What they asked?"

I thought about lying to him. I thought about how much of his childhood I'd already spent, how many rooms of it I'd already emptied out. But Maren hadn't raised me to lie to children about hard things—she'd raised me to hand them the truth gently and stand next to them while they held it.

"They asked me to let them use me as a weapon," I said. "And I said no, because weapons don't get to choose what they're pointed at. And now they're angry, and they might not help us when things get bad."

Tam considered this with the gravity of a judge. "Good," he said finally. "Being a weapon sounds awful. You'd never get to carve anything."

Despite everything—despite the cold silence in my skull and the war ahead and the dragon guttering out atop the Spire—I laughed. Really laughed, until my ribs hurt, until Serah was looking at me with an expression that on anyone else would have been fondness.

* * *

We broke camp at midmorning. Anselm emerged from his blankets looking like a scholar who'd lost an argument with a broom, listened to my account of the night's bargaining, and pronounced it—as predicted—a moral triumph, quoting something dreary from the Book of Lamentations that I politely declined to hear twice.

But as we packed the sledge and checked the bindings on our boots, he lingered beside me, and his voice dropped to the register he saved for things that frightened him.

"Gideon. The Fragment said they would not help. Do you believe them?"

"I believe they believe it."

"That is not the same as safe."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

He fussed with a strap, not meeting my eye. "There is a passage in the archives. Sealed material, from the century after the Breaking. Accounts of... other vessels. Others who let the dead ride them. The crown called them *hollow men*, and hunted them alongside the gifted, because a vessel filled with enough grief becomes something that cannot stop grieving. Cannot stop *taking*." He finally looked up. "You may have refused the easy road. But understand what waits on the hard one. The Spire will ask something of you that makes tonight's bargain look generous. And when it does, there will be no one left to say no on your behalf."

"I know," I said.

"Do you?" His eyes were wet, and furious about being wet. "Because I have read what happens to men who carry too many voices, Gideon. I have read it in the crown's own handwriting, gloating. And I am telling you that somewhere between here and that mountain, you must decide which of these voices you will keep and which you will lay down, because no vessel holds everything."

He walked away before I could answer, which was wise of him, because I didn't have one.

We turned north that afternoon, toward the pass, toward the snow, toward the mountain where a dying dragon kept the last fire in the world. Behind us, Ironhold smoked on the horizon, patient as a spider. Ahead of us, the road climbed into country where even the maps gave up.

And in my skull, where a chorus had shouted and begged and threatened, there was only silence now—except, at the bottom of it, banked like a coal carried against the wind, one voice's warmth that I could not hear and could not lose.

I walked north, and I carried it.
