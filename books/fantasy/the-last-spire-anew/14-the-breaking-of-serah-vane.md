## 14. The Breaking of Serah Vane

They took my boots.

That's how I knew it was serious. In eleven years with the Hunt, I'd seen prisoners dragged in bloody, bound, half-dead—and always allowed to keep their boots. Boots were practical. Boots said *you'll be walking again, eventually*. A prisoner without boots was a prisoner the crown expected to bury.

So when they hauled me down into Ironhold's lowest level and left me standing barefoot on cold stone, I did what any sensible woman would do. I counted the exits.

One exit. One door, iron-banded, no window. Two guards outside it, judging by the way their voices overlapped through the stone—one bored, one nervous. The bored one was a problem. Nervous men are easy; boredom is a discipline you can't break with a knife or a smile.

I sat on the cot—there was even a cot, which was either courtesy or theater—and waited for my old commander to make his entrance. Dain Corvath had never once in his life entered a room without making an entrance. He'd have rehearsed this one. He probably had a speech.

The door opened at the fourth bell, which meant he'd timed it to feel like I'd been forgotten. Clever. Cruel. Very Dain.

"Serah." He stood just inside the doorway, hands clasped behind his back, wearing his captain's blacks like they'd been poured onto him that morning. Not a wrinkle. Not a hair out of place. Eleven years I'd served under him and I don't think I ever saw him sweat.

"Commander." I didn't stand. Let him chew on that.

He pulled up the room's single stool—of course there was only one, of course he got to sit—and settled onto it with the patience of a man who owned the clock as well as the room. "You've caused quite a lot of paperwork."

"That's a shame. You were always more of a verbal man."

"Three dead at the toll bridge," he went on, as if I hadn't spoken. "A burned patrol post. And now this." He gestured vaguely upward, toward the whole edifice of Ironhold above us, its vaults full of screaming souls, though of course he wouldn't call them that. "Desertion. Aiding a fugitive. Treason, if we're feeling generous with the word."

"We're not feeling generous, though. Are we?"

Something flickered across his face—not anger. Something worse. Pity. "No, Serah. We're not."

* * *

Here is what I need you to understand about Dain Corvath: I loved him. Not romantically—I'd have sooner married a crossbow—but the way you love a wall between you and the storm. He taught me everything worth knowing about the hunt. How to track a gifted child through snow. How to keep your face still when you burn a house. How to sleep after.

He also taught me, without ever saying it aloud, that the hunt had rules. That containment was not slaughter. That we were a dam against a flood, ugly work but necessary, and that the difference between us and butchers was the difference between a scalpel and a cleaver.

I had watched that belief die slowly over the years—every village that turned out to hold nothing but frightened grandmothers, every brand seared into hollow children's faces for the crime of being born ordinary. But some part of me had kept a scrap of it alive, folded away like a letter from someone who might write again someday. Some part of me needed Dain Corvath to still believe it too.

"Tell me why you ran," he said. "Not the official version. Mine. You owe me that much."

"I owed the people I burned more than that, and nobody collected."

"Serah—"

"The boy," I said. "The Halloran carver. His gift is real. I've heard it myself. And every order I ever followed told me he was a threat to be put down, and he's spent his life trying to *help the dead*, Dain. Help them. Do you know what we do? We harvest them." I'd seen the vaults' edges by then, felt the wrongness humming off Ironhold's walls. "We put them in boxes and burn them for fuel."

His expression didn't change. That should have been my first warning. A good man accused falsely gets angry. Dain just waited, patient as frost.

"You sound like the priest," he said at last. "Anselm Voss. Yes, we know all about him. Stole documents from the royal archive, fled south, gathered himself a little procession of strays." He leaned forward. "Do you know what those documents actually say, Serah? Because I do. I've read them. All of them."

My heart did something complicated. "Then you know the covenant was broken deliberately. By the crown. A century ago."

"I know the covenant was *renegotiated*," he said smoothly. "By men facing a choice no history book will ever fairly judge. Magic doesn't share, Serah. It never has. Left free, it pools—it pools in bloodlines, in tyrants, in things that come out of the deep places and wear the faces of kings. The old realms tore each other apart for centuries before the covenant. The founders broke it open because a monopoly was safer than a war. One power, held responsibly."

"Held by whom?"

"Does it matter?" For the first time something warmed in his voice—an evangelist's heat. "It has been held. A hundred years of the longest peace the realms have known. And now a bone-carver with a ghost in his pocket wants to unmake it because a murdered warden told him to. You've met the dragons, haven't you? Eyrrith, guttering on her Spire? You know what dragon-fire is, Serah?" He let the silence stretch. "It's the seal. It's the lock on the cage. And when it fails—if it fails—the covenant reforges itself around whatever key walks through the Spire's heart, and every power the crown has contained for a century goes back into circulation. Every mad king's heir. Every thing in the deep places. Everything."

"The dead aren't a cage, Dain. They're people."

"The dead are *pressure*," he snapped, and for one moment the mask slipped and I saw the man underneath—not cruel, exactly, but small, and terrified, and holding his terror like a torch. "You want to hear something true, since truth is your new religion? Here it is." He stood. Walked to the door. Turned back.

"The standing order is not containment. It hasn't been for two years. When the Spire falls—or rather, before it can be used—we kill Halloran, we kill the priest, we kill the boy, and we kill every gifted soul in the realms who could serve as an alternative key. All of them. Systematically. The hunt stops being a hunt and becomes what it should have been from the start." He straightened his cuffs. "You weren't deserting an institution that lost its way, Serah. You were deserting an institution that finally told you what it was."

And there it was. The scrap of paper unfolded at last, and the handwriting was my own commander's, and it said *we were always going to burn them*.

I made my face very still. He'd taught me that.

"Why tell me?" I asked quietly. "Why give the traitor the full confession?"

"Because I'm offering you a way back." He crouched to my eye level, and his eyes were genuinely, terribly kind. "You were the best I ever trained. The council will want your testimony—*against* Voss's documents, confirming they're forgery or heresy or both. Your name clears. Your boots come back. And when the last dragon gutters out at midwinter, you'll stand on the right side of the wall when the world stays quiet." He paused. "Or you die here, barefoot, in a hole, for a carver who won't remember your name in ten years."

I looked at him for a long moment. Then I laughed. I couldn't help it—a short, ugly laugh with no joy in it anywhere.

"Dain. You absolute fool. I remember *everyone's* names. That's the whole problem."

* * *

They left me alone after that. I took it as a bad sign. Men like Dain don't leave offers on the table; they collect answers, and unanswered offers get answered for you.

I worked the cot frame loose where it bolted to the wall—third hour of prying with a splintered slat while my fingertips bled—and got nowhere useful, because the door was iron and the guards were awake and I had no boots, and I want it noted for posterity that I am not a fool even when I'm desperate.

Which is why, when the lamp outside the grate guttered and the nervous guard's voice said, "*Oi—did you hear that? Something's in the walls*," I pressed my ear to the stone.

And heard whispering.

Faint. Rhythmic. Words in no language I knew, and yet I understood them the way you understand a lullaby—by feel, not translation. And beneath the words, threading through them like wire through wax, another voice. Thin, dry, unmistakably human, muttering Latin conjugations under its breath the way Anselm Voss did whenever he was frightened and pretending not to be.

"*Anselm,*" I breathed. "You magnificent, suicidal old fraud. What are you doing in my walls?"

"*Praying,*" came the dry reply, muffled through three feet of masonry. "*Badly. There's a service duct behind the reliquary niche—Ironhold was built on monastery foundations, they kept the bones of the old abbey in the lower courses, and the dead here are—"*

"*Loud,*" I finished. "*I know.*"

"*They want to help. Which frightens me considerably, because things that want to help from inside walls generally want something. But there's a gap behind the east vault-bracing, twenty feet of crawl, and it opens into the crypt stair. Can you reach the bracing from your cell?*"

I looked at my cot. At the loose bolts. At the iron door with its two guards.

"No," I said. "But I can reach the *guards*."

Here's what eleven years of the hunt teaches you that no academy admits: the bored guard is the dangerous one, but the nervous guard is the useful one. Nervous men look at sounds. So when the whispering swelled—when the dead of Ironhold pressed their voices against the stones until the walls themselves seemed to murmur like a congregation shuffling in pews—the nervous guard did exactly what nervous men do. He left his post to look.

The bored guard stayed. Of course he stayed. Boredom is a discipline.

So I gave him something better than discipline. I screamed.

Not a woman-in-distress scream. A hunt-trained scream—the kind we used in training yards to simulate a comrade going down, pitched to hit a soldier's spine like a hook. He came through that door fast and stupid, sword drawn, expecting an assassin, and found instead a barefoot woman sitting calmly on a cot with her hands visible and her ankles crossed.

"Sergeant," I said pleasantly. "Close the door. This stays between us, and I'd rather it stayed quiet."

He hesitated. Hesitation was the opening; I'd built my whole escape plan around the half-second of hesitation between *orders say stay* and *curiosity says look*. He stepped in. I stepped past him—not fighting, just leaving, sliding through the doorway as his sword arm came up—and then the whispering in the walls rose to a chorus and the nervous guard shrieked and dropped his lamp, and the corridor went dark except for the flame crawling up spilled oil, and I was running barefoot on cold stone with my bleeding fingers hooked into the dark like claws.

The gap behind the east bracing was exactly where Anselm said. So was the twenty feet of crawl, which I have decided never to speak of again, involving as it did tight stone, old abbey bones packed in the mortar like raisins in bread, and voices pressing close enough that I could feel breath on my neck that had no source in living air.

One voice, clearer than the rest, said something I chose not to examine. It sounded almost like Maren Halloran's cadence—Gideon had described it to me once, around a fire, homesick. It said: *go left at the fork, girl. You're bleeding on the ancestors.*

I went left at the fork.

* * *

The crypt stair opened into the undercroft of Ironhold's chapel, which was empty at fifth bell, lit by one votive lamp, and stocked with the crown's idea of mercy: a rack of confiscated pilgrim cloaks, among them—gods are vain, occasionally—a pair of soft worn boots two sizes too big.

Anselm was waiting at the foot of the stairs, gray-faced, oil-lamp shaking in his fist, dressed in a novice's habit that fit him like a sack on a fencepost.

"You're bleeding," he said.

"You're wearing theft," I said. "We match."

"Serah." He caught my arm as I limped past. His grip was stronger than it looked; grief does that to a man's hands. "What did Corvath tell you? Before I came. What did you learn?"

I stopped. Under the votive lamp, in a chapel built by monks whose bones now braced their conquerors' walls, I made myself say it plainly, because plainness was the only penance I had left.

"There is no containment anymore. There hasn't been for two years. The standing order is extermination. Every gifted soul in the realms—before midwinter, before the Spire can be used. Gideon isn't a fugitive to them, Anselm. He's a *loose end*. And so is Tam, and so are you, and so was every branded child in every village I ever walked through, and I helped carry the list." My voice cracked on the last word, and I hated it, and I let it crack anyway, because a woman who can't feel her own breaking can't testify honestly to anyone else's. "I thought we were a dam. We were the flood. We were always the flood."

Anselm was quiet a long moment. Then he did the most priestly thing I ever saw him do: he took off the stolen novice's hood, folded it, set it on the altar like an offering, and said, "Then we run faster than guilt. Guilt is heavy. Leave yours here if you must—but take the truth. Truth is lighter and travels farther."

"That's a terrible sermon."

"It's the only one I have left."

We went up through the chapel, past the sleeping acolytes, out through the gate where the dead of Ironhold—hundreds of them, thousands, pressed into their vaults below—did something I still cannot explain. The sentries stumbled. The watch-lamps guttered blue. And every dog in the fortress began to howl at nothing, at once, in harmony, like a choir given sudden and terrible instruction.

The gates were unbarred. No one knew why. The guards on duty swore afterward they'd never left their posts, and they were telling the truth; the posts had simply, briefly, been elsewhere.

We walked out through the middle of a fortress that had swallowed me whole, wearing stolen clothes and borrowed silence, and the dead carried us past every eye like a secret passed hand to hand.

* * *

We made the tree line by dawn, and I looked back exactly once. Ironhold sat on its hill with its banners hanging limp in the windless cold, and from this distance it looked almost peaceful—a gray crown on a white skull. Somewhere under it, Dain Corvath was waking to an empty cell and a report he wouldn't believe, and somewhere deeper than that, the vaults were humming with the harvested dead who had, for one night, remembered they were people.

"Serah," Anselm said. He'd gone gray around the mouth. "There's something you should know. Why I really came back for you, instead of going north to meet the others."

"Tell me on the road."

"You won't walk if I tell you here. Or you'll walk faster. I genuinely don't know which." He steadied himself against a birch. "The archive fragment I recovered—the one I couldn't read before, the sealed cipher—the dead helped me with it, in the walls. They wanted out, and knowledge is their only currency." He swallowed. "It's a schedule, Serah. The crown's not waiting for midwinter to see if the Spire fails. They march on the Last Spire in nine days. Full force. To kill Eyrrith and bury the reforge forever—and they expect to arrive *before* Gideon does."

The cold went through me like a spear through wet cloth. Nine days. Bare feet, stolen cloaks, a wounded woman and a frightened priest, and nine days between us and the end of the world.

I looked north, where the mountains were already wearing their winter white.

"Then we'd better hope," I said, "that my deserter's instincts are as good as my hunting ones. Move, priest. We're going to make a liar out of the crown."

And we ran.
