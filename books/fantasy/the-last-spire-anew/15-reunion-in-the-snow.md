## 15. Reunion in the Snow

The thing nobody tells you about being a lookout is that it's ninety percent boredom and ten percent terror, and nobody warns you which ten percent is coming.

I'd been posted at the mouth of the Greywater Pass for two days. Two days of sitting on a frozen rock behind a screen of wind-bent pines, watching the road below for signs of the company, the crown, or anything else worth screaming about. Anselm had given me a brass whistle for signaling—one blast for friends, two for trouble—and then he'd given me a lecture about how the whistle was a last resort because sound carried strangely in the mountains, and then Serah had given me a shorter lecture about how if I blew it wrong she'd personally rebrand my other arm, and then Gideon had said nothing at all, which from Gideon was its own kind of blessing.

So there I sat. Watching snow fall on nothing. Chewing the last of a smoked fish that had gone leathery enough to qualify as armor. Practicing being brave in my head, which is easy, because in your head you're always brave and never cold.

I'm Tam, by the way. Thirteen years old, formerly of the village of Marrow's Ford, formerly branded *hollow* across my forearm for the crime of being born without magic in a world that had decided magic was contraband. The brand had faded to a pinkish scar that ached in the cold, which felt about right. Everything I owned ached in the cold. I'd made peace with it.

The plan—such as it was—was simple. We'd scattered after Ironhold, when the whole mountain range lit up with bounty notices and crown riders. Split up, cross the pass separately, regroup at the shepherd's hut above the Greywater. Serah's idea. "A company of five traveling together is a procession," she'd said. "A company of five arriving one at a time is just weather." I'd drawn the lookout post because I was smallest, quickest, and—her words—"the only one of us who can fit through a bramble without announcing it to the county."

I'd taken it as a compliment. Mostly.

* * *

The first one up the pass wasn't supposed to be first.

I heard him before I saw him—the particular rhythm of a man walking who is trying not to walk like a fugitive and failing. Anselm came around the bend below me with his priest's coat buttoned wrong and his satchel clutched like a shield, and when I stepped out of the pines he made a sound like a kettle being startled and nearly fell off the mountain.

"Saints' bones, boy. You're supposed to whistle."

"You told me one blast for friends."

"I did."

"You didn't whistle either."

He blinked at me, snow gathering in his eyebrows, and then his face did the thing it did sometimes—all the guilt and grief rearranging itself into something almost like warmth. "No," he admitted. "I suppose I was hoping you'd be here so I wouldn't have to be alone with my own thoughts. They've gotten very poor company since Ironhold." He looked past me at the empty road. "The others?"

"Not yet."

"Then we wait." He settled onto my rock, produced a stub of pencil and a scrap of vellum from his satchel, and began writing in that cramped hand of his, the one that looked like a spider drowning in ink. After a while he said, without looking up: "You've kept the fire small. Good. And you've cleared the sight-lines. Good. You've been counting the riders that passed below?"

"Eleven. All heading east. None stopped."

"Good." He wrote something down. "You know, in another life you'd have made a fine clerk."

"In another life I'd have made a fine *person* instead of a hollow one."

Anselm's pencil stopped. He set it down and looked at me with that terrible earnestness priests are trained in before they lose their faith and keep using anyway. "Tam. The brand says what the crown thinks of you. It has never once said what you are. Do you understand the difference? Because I have read every archive this kingdom didn't burn, and I can tell you the men who invented that brand were cowards frightened of children. That is the entire history of it. Cowards, frightened of children."

I didn't say anything. But I sat up straighter on my rock, and I think he noticed, because priests always notice.

We waited. The snow thickened. Somewhere below us the wind found the pass and began playing it like a flute, which made listening hard and imagining things easy. Every snap of ice became hoofbeats. Every shadow between the pines became a rider. I'd learned in Marrow's Ford that fear is mostly imagination with bad aim—it fires at everything and hits nothing until, once in a while, it doesn't.

Gideon came an hour before dusk.

I knew him the way I always did—not by sight but by the way the birds went quiet and then, oddly, went *loud* again, as if something had passed through them and left them reassured. He came up the trail slow, favoring his left leg where the Ironhold vault had burned him, his hood crusted white, and when he saw me his whole face changed. Not a smile exactly. More like a door opening in a house everyone thought was empty.

"Tam."

"Gideon." Then, because I'd rehearsed it for two days and couldn't help myself: "You're late."

"The dead are chatty in the mountains." He crouched beside me, and I saw the shadows under his eyes had gotten worse, deep as thumbprints. Since Ironhold he'd been like this—listening to something none of the rest of us could hear, carrying conversations we weren't part of. Sometimes at night I'd see his lips moving, answering people who weren't there, and I'd feel the old familiar sting of it: *everyone gets a voice except the hollow.* But then he'd catch me looking, and he'd say, "They're saying thank you, most of them," and somehow that helped. Being useful to ghosts was more than I'd ever been to anyone alive.

"Serah?" he asked.

"Not yet."

His jaw tightened. Of all of us, he worried about her loudest, though he'd deny it with his dying breath. She'd walked into the toll bridge trap on purpose—walked backward into it, smiling, telling us to run—and the fact that we'd doubled back and pulled her out hadn't erased the four days she'd spent in a crown cell, or the limp she now pretended wasn't there.

"She'll come," I said. "She's too mean to die."

That got the ghost of a smile. "Yes. Mean survives."

* * *

Night came down like a lid. We risked a fist-sized fire in the lee of the rocks, and Anselm heated water while Gideon sat staring into the flames with the look of a man reading a book no one else could see. I took the first watch, back at my pine screen, because looking out for people was the one job I'd ever been given that I intended to keep.

It was full dark when I saw the torches.

Not one. Not three. A dozen, maybe more, strung along the eastern approach to the pass like a necklace of orange teeth, and beneath them, moving slow and deliberate, riders. Crown riders. And not a patrol—a column. Wagons. Banners. I pressed myself flat against the frozen ground and counted through the gap in my fingers, and my stomach did what stomachs do when the arithmetic comes out wrong.

Forty riders. Two supply wagons. And at the head of them, on a gray mare, a figure in black-and-silver whose bearing even at distance said *commander.*

They were making camp. Here. In the pass. Directly astride the only route Serah could take to reach us.

I scrambled back to the fire, low and fast, the way Serah taught me—heel to toe, weight forward, like the ground owed me money—and the two of them looked up, and whatever they saw in my face killed the conversation before I opened my mouth.

"Riders," I whispered. "East approach. Forty, maybe fifty. They're camping across the road." I swallowed. "And Gideon—they've got wagons. Big ones. Covered."

"Wagons," Anselm repeated, and I watched the meaning land on him like frost. "Engines. They're hauling the anti-magic engines up the mountain." He was already digging through his satchel, pulling out maps, his hands shaking. "Why would they bring engines to a mountain pass unless—"

"Unless they're going somewhere engines are needed," Gideon said quietly.

We all arrived at it together. I watched it happen in their faces before it happened in mine.

The Last Spire. The final dragon. Midwinter.

"They know," I said. "They've always known. They're not hunting us anymore—they're going to beat us there. Kill Eyrrith, bury the truth, and—" My voice cracked on the last part and I hated it, thirteen years old and my voice still picked its moments to betray me.

Gideon put a hand on my shoulder. His grip was warm. It was always warm; I'd decided long ago it was all the voices keeping him heated, like coals in a basket.

"One problem at a time," he said. "Right now the problem is Serah, walking up that road in the dark, into a camp of forty men."

* * *

Here is what I want to write down, because someday someone might ask and I want to answer honestly: I was afraid. Not storybook afraid, not trembling-heroically-in-the-snow afraid. The real kind, the kind that lives in your knees and your bladder and makes your own heartbeat loud enough to give you away. Forty armed men. A whistle meant to carry. A friend walking blind toward them.

Anselm wanted to go down himself. Gideon talked him out of it in four sentences, flat and final, the way he did when the dead had already shown him how it would end. "They'd take you. You'd talk. Not because you're weak—because you were built honest, and honesty breaks under iron." Which was, I thought, the kindest way anyone had ever described a man's worst feature.

Which left me.

"Signal positions," Gideon said, crouching over the map Anselm had scratched in the dirt, the firelight making his scars move. "If Serah comes up the western fork tonight, she'll walk directly into their picket line. Someone has to warn her off. Someone small, quick, quiet, who knows these rocks now better than any of us."

"And if the warning fails," Anselm said, "whoever gives it dies."

Nobody looked at me. That was the thing—I could feel them both *not* looking at me, the silence bending around the shape of the question neither would ask. And underneath the fear, in some cellar of myself I hadn't known was there, something stood up.

"I'll do it," I said.

They both turned. Gideon's eyes were steady and sad and proud all at once, which seemed like too many things for one pair of eyes to hold.

"Two blasts means trouble," I went on, before the fear could climb up out of its cellar and drag me back down. "But two blasts also tells them exactly where I am, so I'll do it different. Serah taught me the rock-codes. Knock-stones. One knock, pause, two knocks—*danger, go wide.* Three fast—*run.* I'll get to the fork above the picket line and I'll knock it out on stone. Sound carries down the valley at night; she'll hear it before they will, because she'll be listening for it and they won't."

"And if they hear it too?" Anselm asked gently.

"Then I'll be very fast," I said, "and very sorry."

Gideon held out his hand, and I took it, and for a moment he just held on. "Maren used to say," he murmured, half to himself, "that courage isn't the absence of running. It's choosing, each time, which direction to run in." He squeezed my fingers. "Choose well, Tam."

Then he gave me the whistle after all. "Last resort," he said. "One blast. We come for you. Whatever it costs."

I nodded, and I didn't believe him—nobody sensible believes *we'll come for you whatever it costs*, because cost is the whole point—but I took the whistle, and its little brass weight in my pocket turned out to be heavier than any brand.

* * *

Getting down to the fork took everything I had. The snow had a crust on it that broke like sugar glass, announcing every step to the county, so I went along the streambed where the water sang loud enough to cover me, thigh-deep in freezing slush, teeth clenched so hard I thought I'd crack them. Twice I froze flat against stones as a picket patrol passed close enough that I could smell the horses, hear a soldier complaining about his boots. I lay in the shallows with the current pushing at my back and counted my heartbeats and thought about Marrow's Ford, about the day they'd held my arm against the branding iron, about how I'd screamed and been told it was for my own good, that a marked child was an *honest* child.

Well. Here I was, honest as sin, lying in a frozen creek to save a woman who'd once threatened to rebrand me. If that wasn't family, I didn't know what was.

The fork was a Y of stone where the western path split from the main road, and above it a shelf of granite jutted out like a shelf in a giant's pantry. I climbed to it, numb past feeling, and I waited.

The moon came out from behind cloud and showed me the camp below—tents, picket lines, wagon beds, the engines lashed down under oilcloth like sleeping animals. And on the western path, far below, a single figure moving through the trees.

Her. Even limping, even alone, even in the dark—there was no mistaking the way Serah Vane moved, like the ground was an argument she was winning. She was maybe six hundred yards from the outermost picket. Maybe less. And she hadn't seen them, because they'd banked their fires low and the snow ate the sound of forty men the same way it ate everything.

My hands shook so badly I could barely find two stones the right size. This was the moment, I understood dimly, that would decide everything—not the Spire, not the dragon, not the covenant, but whether one branded boy on a rock in the dark could do the one small terrifying thing in front of him. Hiding was safe. Hiding was *smart*. Hiding was what the hollow were supposed to do, what I'd done my whole life: make yourself small, make yourself nothing, let the world pass over you like weather.

I knocked the stones. Once. Pause. Twice.

The sound went down the valley clean as a bell. Below me, Serah stopped. Turned her head. Stood absolutely still for three heartbeats—and then she was moving, silent and swift, off the path, up the slope, wide of the pickets, flowing through the trees like smoke.

And then a voice below me barked, "There! On the rocks! *Light!*"

A torch flared. A horn sounded. The camp boiled awake, and I ran.

I ran the way fear teaches you to run, better than any drillmaster—down the back slope, through the brambles that opened for me because I was small and quick and had spent my whole life fitting through places the world didn't watch. Arrows hissed past and buried themselves in snow with sounds like spitting. A shout: *"Hollow runner! Take the little wretch alive!"* Alive. How generous.

I blew the whistle then—one long blast, the last resort, the promise I hadn't believed—and I swear on Maren Halloran's memory that before the note finished echoing off the peaks, shapes came boiling out of the darkness above me: Gideon with a dead man's fury on his face, Anselm swinging a quarterstaff with the wild incompetence of a scholar, and Serah Vane materializing out of the treeline with two stolen knives and an expression that made grown soldiers reconsider their careers.

What happened next happened fast and ugly and mostly in the dark, and I did my part, which was to stay low and trip a sergeant who really should have watched where he was going. When it was over, three crown men lay groaning among the pines, disarmed but breathing, because Gideon had said *no killing* and Serah—limping, bleeding from a cut on her brow, her old commander's seal torn from her collar—had actually listened.

We ran. All five of us, up into the high passes, until the horns faded and the stars came out fierce and close, and then we collapsed in the lee of a boulder, gasping, laughing in that cracked hysterical way people laugh when they've been certain they were about to die.

Serah found me first. She crouched down, took my chin in her rough hand, and studied my face like she was reading a map.

"You knocked," she said.

"Twice."

"On the shelf above the fork. Six hundred yards out, in the dark, with the whole camp below you." Her voice did something strange on the last word. She let go of my chin and gripped my shoulder instead, hard, the way soldiers grip. "I heard it and I thought, *that's my boy.*" Then, gruffly, before the moment could get comfortable: "Don't let it go to your head. Your rock-codes are still sloppy."

But her eyes were shining, and Gideon was smiling his open-door smile, and Anselm was saying something breathless about how the boy had displayed remarkable tactical instinct, and I stood there in the snow with my soaked boots and my hammering heart and understood, finally, what a family was. Not blood. Not a village that votes. It's whoever hears your knock in the dark and knows it's you.

* * *

We made camp at dawn in a hollow above the tree line, exhausted, and it was Anselm who spread out his maps with shaking hands and laid out the arithmetic we'd all been avoiding.

"The camp in the pass wasn't chasing us," he said. "It was staging. Supply lines, engines, a forward base. The crown isn't sending a patrol to the Spire, my friends. It's sending an army—and they'll march within days. Midwinter is nine days off. Eyrrith's fire is down to embers." He looked around our circle, at each of us, lingering last on Gideon. "Whatever we're going to do, we do it now. Ahead of them. Through country crawling with hunters. Or not at all."

Silence. Wind. Far to the north, past the shoulders of the mountains, a faint smudge on the horizon that might have been cloud and might have been smoke.

Gideon rose slowly to his feet, listening to something none of us could hear, and when he spoke, his voice was quiet and certain and terribly tired.

"Then we'd better start climbing," he said. "Because the dead tell me the dragon hasn't much longer than we do."
