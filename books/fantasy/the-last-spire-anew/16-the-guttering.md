## 16. The Guttering

The Spire did not look like the end of the world. It looked like a finger bone.

That was my professional opinion, and I stand by it. Twenty years of carving memorial bones teaches you the anatomy of endings: the taper, the hollow, the way a structure narrows toward its purpose. The Last Spire rose out of the mountain's crown like something a god had snapped off and forgotten to discard—pale, pitted, taller than anything built by hands that worried about falling. Snow smoked off its flanks in long gray banners. And at its summit, where the wind screamed loud enough to drown even my dead, something glowed.

Ember-colored. Faint. The way a hearth glows an hour after the fire dies, when you keep staring at it anyway because looking away means admitting it's gone.

"There," Anselm said, and his voice did something I'd never heard it do. It cracked. The priest who had unbelieved his whole church, who could recite three centuries of institutional atrocity without blinking—he cracked on a single word, like a man pointing at his own funeral.

Tam squinted against the wind. "Is that... is she still burning?"

"She's guttering," Serah said. She said it flatly, the way soldiers say casualty numbers, but her hand went to the sword hilt she hadn't worn since Ironhold, out of habit for a weapon she'd sworn off. "I've seen fires die before. That's what dying looks like."

We climbed.

I want to tell you the climb was heroic. It wasn't. It was four exhausted people hauling themselves up switchbacks cut into ice, with Tam half-carrying Anselm, whose archive-haunted lungs had finally filed a formal complaint about altitude. My satchel knocked against my hip with every step, and inside it the shard of living bone knocked back—the warden's key, warm as a held hand, heavier than it had ever been. Or maybe that was just me getting heavier. Grief does that. It accumulates. By the time we crested the last ridge, I felt like a man carrying a second skeleton under his skin.

And then the wind stopped.

Not died down. *Stopped*, all at once, the way a crowd goes quiet when someone important enters the room. Snow hung in the air around us like a held breath. And I heard—not the dead this time, though they were there, thousands of them murmuring beneath everything like a river under ice—I heard *her*.

*You brought it.*

The voice came from everywhere. From the stone. From the ember-light bleeding over the ridge. It was old the way mountains are old, and tired the way mountains are not allowed to be, and underneath both of those things, curious.

*Little listener,* she said. *You brought my sister's work home.*

My knees nearly quit. Serah caught my elbow—an instinct from the bridge, from Ironhold, from all the places we'd learned each other—and didn't let go until I nodded that I could stand.

"Eyrrith?" Anselm whispered. He had read her name in ashes. Reading a name and standing in front of what it belongs to are different professions entirely.

*Come up,* the dragon said. *Mind the ice on the north stair. I have not been tidy.*

* * *

She lay coiled around the Spire's crown the way a mother coils around a child she can no longer lift: protectively, uselessly, beautifully. Eyrrith was vast beyond sense—her wing alone would have shaded the Hollow Vale—but she was *thin*. That was the horror of it. Scales that had once been armor-bright now showed the dark of the stone beneath them, like a forest going bald. Her fire showed through the gaps in her chest, a slow pulse of ember-light, each beat fainter than the last.

Her eye found us as we picked our way across the summit. One eye. Amber, deep as a well, and in it I saw reflected four small figures and one small satchel, and I understood suddenly how we must look to her: ants bringing a grain of sand to a collapsing cathedral.

"You're the warden's ghost," I said. My voice sounded absurdly small. "The one who begged me to warn—"

*I know what I asked.* Her breath moved across the summit like a warm tide. *And I know what you carried instead. Show me.*

I knelt. My joints complained; my dead murmured approval, which was new and unsettling. When I drew the shard from the satchel, Eyrrith's whole body shuddered—a tectonic event, snow avalanching off her flanks in sheets—and the ember-light in her chest flared once, bright, hopeful, before sinking again.

*Yes,* she breathed. *Yes. There it is. A hundred years I have burned low, holding the seal together with stubbornness and spite, waiting for someone to bring the key home. Do you know what I have watched while I waited?*

"No," I said honestly.

*Everything ending slowly.* She lowered her great head until her eye was level with me, close enough that I could see myself in it, small and wind-scoured and terrified. *Now. Give it here, little listener. Set it in the heart below, speak the covenant as your warden taught her daughters to speak it, and I will do the rest. Simple as a birth. Painful as one, too.*

Hope is a dangerous thing to hand a tired man. I felt it go through me like brandy—warmth first, then dizziness, then the beginning of a very bad idea. I looked back at my companions. Tam was grinning. Anselm had his eyes closed, mouthing something that was either prayer or bibliography. Serah was watching me with the expression she wore when she already knew something I didn't and was deciding whether saying it would kill me.

"Then let's do it," I said. "Today. Before Vane's lot—" 

*There is a cost.*

"—arrives." I stopped. "Say that again."

*There is a cost,* Eyrrith repeated, with the gentleness of someone who has delivered this kind of news before and hated it every time. *The covenant is not a wall, little listener. It is a hinge. Two realms, one door between them, and the door must be *held*. Not by magic—magic cannot hold its own boundary, that is the whole disease of the age. By a will. By a living mind that stands in the doorway and chooses, every moment, to hold it shut against the flood and open it only for passage.*

"Someone holds it," Serah said slowly. "Someone alive."

*A soul that hears the dead,* Eyrrith said. *Only such a one can stand in that doorway and hear both sides of it. Only such a one can know when to open, when to bar. The covenant must be reforged *through* a listener, and the listener who does the forging becomes the hinge. Forever.*

The word landed on the summit like a dropped anvil.

"Forever," I repeated.

*The dead you hear now—you would hear always, but never answer. Never speak. You would feel every passing soul in the realm, every grief, every crossing, and you would hold the door steady through all of it. And no voice of yours would ever reach anyone again. Not your friends. Not the valley. Not even the dead themselves.* Her ember-heart pulsed, dimmed, pulsed. *You would become the first of the silent. The guardian no one can call. Only, perhaps, someday, a warmth at the edge of a fire. A feeling of being watched over. That is the whole of what remains after.*

The wind came back then, all at once, screaming, as if it had been eavesdropping and couldn't bear the silence either.

I sat down. I don't remember deciding to. My legs simply resigned their commission, and there I was, on the frozen crown of the world, holding a shard of living bone that had traveled two thousand miles to tell me how I was going to die.

Not die. Worse. *Continue.*

* * *

They gave me a few minutes. They're good people that way. I heard them withdraw down the stair a little ways—Tam arguing in a furious whisper, Anselm shushing him, Serah saying nothing in that way she has that is louder than most people's shouting.

The dead gathered around me. Not the way they gather in cities—pressed, hungry, clamoring. Here, on the Spire, they were quieter. Older. The warden's ghost settled near my shoulder like a woman taking a seat beside a sickbed. Maren's memory—the shape of her, not her voice, she'd crossed over clean at the mist-line—stood a little farther off, the way she used to stand in the doorway of the workshop when I worked late, watching, not interrupting, just making sure I knew I wasn't alone.

"The whole way here," I said aloud, to no one, to everyone, "every ghost, every omen, every burned village and branded child—it was all leading here. To a door. And the door needs a keeper, and I'm apparently the only fool in creation shaped like a hinge."

The warden's ghost said nothing. Dead rarely comfort you directly; they mostly witness. It's more useful and much less comforting.

I turned the shard over in my hands. Living bone, warm, patient. I'd carried it from a grave to the top of the sky because a murdered woman asked me to and because, somewhere under all the fear, I wanted to matter. I wanted the gift I'd spent thirty years burying in the Hollow Vale's blessed silence to have meant something. Well. Congratulations, Gideon. It meant exactly this.

A boy climbed back up the stair.

Tam plopped down next to me in the snow without asking, which was how he did everything. For a while he just looked at the dragon, mouth open, doing the arithmetic of awe. Then he looked at me.

"Serah says you're thinking about not doing it."

"Serah talks too much."

"She says you're thinking about not doing it," Tam repeated, "and she's wrong, isn't she? Because you've got the face. The one you get when you've decided something and you hate it." He hugged his knees. "Anselm says heroes are just people who run out of better options. But I don't think that's true. I think you've got lots of options. You're just picking the one where everyone else gets to keep existing."

"When did you get wise?"

"I've been hanging around priests and deserters and ghosts. Something was bound to stick." He bumped his shoulder against mine, careful of the satchel. "I just want you to know I'm keeping the unfinished carvings. All of them. The heron especially. You left the heron half-done and I've decided that's a message."

"It's a bird, Tam."

"It's a *message*," he insisted, "and the message is you expect me to finish things. So." He stood, wiped his nose on his sleeve in a manner that would have horrified Anselm, and went back down the stairs, leaving me alone with the largest dying creature in the world and the smallest finished thought I owned.

* * *

Eyrrith spoke again when I approached her, and this time her voice had folded itself down smaller, gentler, the way you talk to someone at a bedside.

*You should know what it will feel like,* she said. *It is not oblivion. Oblivion would be a mercy. You will remain. You will see the spring come to the vale. You will watch your strange little family grow old and argue and build things, and you will not be able to tell them so much as good morning. Some nights the weight of every soul in the realm will pass through you, and you will hold, because there is no alternative to holding. That is the office. That is what my sister did, before they cut her down for it.*

"And if I refuse?"

She was silent a long moment. Ember-light pulsed in her ruined chest. Once. Twice.

*Then I die within the season. The seal fails within the year. The realms tear apart along hundred-year-old seams, and the dead pour through unheld, and the crown's engines burn whatever is left, and your friend Serah's sacrifice at the bridge, and Anselm's burned archives, and Tam's brave signal in the pass—all of it becomes a story with no ending, because no one was willing to stand in the door.* She inclined her head, and there was no reproach in it, which somehow made it worse. *I do not say this to bind you, little listener. Free souls forge free covenants. I say it because you deserve to choose with your eyes open. That is more courtesy than the world has shown either of us lately.*

Below us, far down the mountain's flank, a light appeared. Then another. Then a line of them, snaking up the switchbacks like a burning rosary—lanterns, torches, the campfires of an army. The hunt had come. Of course it had. Truth travels slower than fear, but the crown had been chasing this particular truth for a century and had finally caught up with it at the top of the world.

Serah reached the summit at a run. She looked at the lights below, then at Eyrrith, then at me, and I watched her do in three seconds the math she'd been refusing to do since the bridge.

"How long do you need?"

"For the reforge? Minutes." I looked down at the torchline. "For the army to reach us? Less."

"Then we stall," she said, and started unbuckling the pack with her old uniform rolled inside—the one she'd sworn she'd never wear again. "Anselm! Get up here! We need every word of that covenant written down and legible!"

I turned to Eyrrith. Her ember-heart was failing faster now, as if the approach of hunters had hurried it, as if even dragons lose hope by inches.

"One question," I said. "When I set the shard and speak the words—will I hear her? Maren? Even once?"

The great amber eye regarded me, and something ancient and sorrowful moved behind it.

*Every voice you have ever heard,* she said, *will pass through you on the way to the sealing. Every one. Once.*

Below us, the torches climbed. Above us, the last fire in the world guttered. And in my satchel, the shard pulsed warm against my ribs like a second heartbeat, patient as a door, waiting to be opened.
