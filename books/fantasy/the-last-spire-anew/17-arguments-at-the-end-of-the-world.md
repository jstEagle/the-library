## 17. Arguments at the End of the World

I had spent thirty years arguing with God, so I suppose it was only fitting that my last great argument would be with a bone-carver who was better at listening than I had ever been.

The wind on the Spire's shoulder did not howl. That was the worst of it. I had expected, at the roof of the world, some operatic gale, thunder, drama worthy of a hymn. Instead there was only a thin, patient cold, and beneath it a silence so complete I could hear my own heart conducting its nervous little business. The dragon above us—Eyrrith, vast as a cathedral and dying like one, her fire banked to a scatter of embers along her ribs—breathed once an hour. Each breath moved the snow on the ledges. Each breath was one fewer.

And Gideon Halloran sat at the mouth of the Spire's heart-chamber, sharpening his carving knife, as calm as a man mending a fence.

"We're not doing this," Serah said.

"We are, in fact, doing this," I said, "because we have run out of other things to do."

"Then we'll find more."

"Serah." I kept my voice gentle, which took effort; gentleness has never been my native tongue. "I have read every text that survived the burnings. Every fragment, every marginal note, every heresy the Church thought dangerous enough to destroy and incompetent enough to miss. There is no alternative."

"You found the truth about the covenant," she snapped. "You found it in ashes."

"The truth was *written down* because the crown wanted to keep it secret, not because they feared someone would act on it. The reforge—" I stopped. Took a breath. The cold bit my lungs. "The reforge requires a hinge. A living soul who can hear the dead, set into the boundary between realms, permanently. Not killed. Worse than killed. *Silenced.* Made into a door that no one will ever hear knock again."

Tam, sitting with his back against Eyrrith's foreleg where the residual warmth pooled, spoke up. His voice cracked in the middle, which he'd have hated me mentioning. "There has to be another way. There always is. In stories there always is."

"In stories," I said, "the archivist finds the hidden page. I know the story, boy. I *am* the archivist. And I am telling you the page is not hidden. It was never written."

Gideon looked up from his knife then. He had that quality I'd noticed on the road—the stillness of men who spend their lives listening. Even his eyes listened.

"Anselm," he said. "Tell them about precedent."

"*Don't* tell them about precedent," Serah said.

"He asked."

"Gideon—"

"I did ask," Gideon said mildly. "It seems rude not to answer."

So I told them. Because it was true, and because I have never in my life been able to leave a true thing unsaid for longer than decency strictly required—a failing that cost me my collar, my library, and very nearly my neck, and which I recommend to no one.

"There were wardens before," I said. "Not just the one whose grave we robbed—forgive me, *visited*. The covenant is old, older than the crown, older than the Church, and it has frayed before. Twice, by the records. Both times it was reforged by a listener. Warden-chronicles call them *hinges*. The first was a woman named Sefa of the Ashlands, three hundred years ago. She heard the dead from childhood. When the boundary tore over the Weeping Marches, she walked into it singing, and the Marches closed behind her, and she has not been heard from since. The second—"

"Don't," Tam said.

"The second was a man called Ordo. He left a journal. It survives in fragments." I recited it, because I had made myself memorize it on the road, because I believed then—and believe now—that Gideon deserved to know exactly what he was choosing, unvarnished, unsweetened. "*'The voices grow quieter as I go up. This should frighten me. It does not. For the first time since I was six years old, I am beginning to understand what silence feels like to everyone else. If this is the price, let it be noted that I pay it gladly.'*" I paused. "The entry after that is a single line: *'It is warm here.'* Then nothing."

The wind did not howl. The snow did not fall. Eyrrith breathed, long and slow, and the embers along her ribs dimmed a fraction further, and nobody said anything for a while.

Then Serah stood up, and I remembered why half the hunt had feared her and the other half had loved her, and both halves had been right.

"No," she said. "No. You don't get to dress it up in a dead man's diary and call it fate. Anselm, you've been looking for a way to buy back your own guilt since the monastery. Don't you dare hand him a precedent and call it absolution."

"That is unfair and also completely accurate," I said. "Both things can be true, Serah. I spent years reading scripture I didn't believe to comfort people I couldn't help. I know what it is to mistake duty for penance. But knowing my motives are rotten doesn't make the archives wrong."

"Find another way."

"With what? We have two days. Midwinter comes, the last ember goes out, and everything your crown built its lie upon collapses into a world where the dead scream forever and magic belongs to whoever holds the biggest vault. You've seen Ironhold. You know what's coming if we fail."

"Then *we* fail together," she said, "and we die fighting, and it means something—"

"It means nothing!" I surprised myself with the volume. My voice came back down the mountain at me, thin and strange. "Forgive me. Truly. But it means nothing, Serah. Heroic deaths are a luxury of people who expect someone else to fix the world afterward. There is no one else. There is a carver, a key, and a door, and the door is closing."

Tam was crying. He wasn't hiding it, either, which at fourteen is either weakness or enormous courage, and I have come to believe it is the latter. "He taught me to listen," Tam said. "Who teaches me when he's gone?"

"Everyone you ever meet," I said, before I could stop myself. "That's what teachers do, boy. They make themselves unnecessary. Most of them have the decency to do it slowly."

I went through the archive of my memory that night, all of it, page by page, the way I had gone through the burned monastery's cellars with a lamp and bleeding hands. It is a peculiar torture, being the only person in a company who knows the scholarship. They look at you with such hope. Hope is heavy. I would rather have carried the satchel.

The covenant, as near as the oldest fragments render it, was a spoken thing. Not written—*spoken*, jointly, by every voice that could hear across the veil, binding realms the way a keystone binds an arch. When the crown severed it a century ago they did not break the stone; they pulled the keystone and locked it in a vault. Everything since—the guttering dragons, the loudening dead, the hollow children, the hunt itself—was architecture settling toward collapse.

To reforge it, the keystone must be living speech. A voice that hears both sides of the wall. Set into the boundary, speaking forever.

Forever. I turned the word over the way Gideon turns bone in his hands, looking for the flaw, the grain, the place where it might split differently than it looks. I found precedents instead. Sefa. Ordo. A third name in a margin, half-erased: *Ysolt*, and beside it, in a different hand, *she chose it, God forgive us for letting her*. Even the medieval annotators had argued. Even they had lost.

There was a passage—I remember it with terrible clarity—in the *Concordia Fragments*, the oldest text we had recovered. A dialogue between an unnamed listener and an unnamed priest. The priest demands: *Is there no other way?* And the listener answers:

*You are asking whether the door can open without a hand. Ask instead whether the hand regrets the door.*

I had underlined that passage in charcoal, in the monastery, weeks ago, thinking it poetry. Sitting in the snow with my collarless throat bare to the wind, I understood it was not poetry. It was an answer. The hand does not regret the door. The hand has been waiting its whole life for a door worth opening.

I hated it. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever archive survives me: I hated it, and it was true anyway. Truth has no obligation to be likable. I spent thirty years telling grieving widows that God's plan was mysterious, and the whole time the mystery was this plain: sometimes the only honest ending is the one nobody wants, taken gladly, by the one person brave enough to take it.

God, if You are reading this, I would like my collar back.

*

I found Gideon at dawn, or what passes for dawn at the top of the world—a bruised grey light leaking over the cloud-sea below us. He was carving. Of course he was carving. The man whittles when other men pray, and I suspect for much the same reasons.

"What is it?" he asked, without looking up.

"A question. From the Concordia. Two thousand years old." I sat down beside him, joints protesting, and held my hands out over the little brazier Tam had coaxed from damp wood. "A listener was asked whether there was another way, and gave an answer I've been chewing all night. *Ask whether the hand regrets the door.*"

Gideon's knife paused.

"Maren used to say something like that," he said.

My chest did something complicated. Maren Halloran had died buying him nine minutes in the mist-line, and I had never met her, and yet she was the most present member of our company. Ghosts, I have learned, need not be dead. Some of them are simply mothers.

"She said—" He turned the bone over in his fingers. A memorial piece, unfinished; I recognized the work now, the way you recognize handwriting. "When I was young, before the Vale, I used to beg her to take me over the border. Just to the edge. Just to see if I could hear anything, anything at all. And she'd say, *'Gideon, love, the gift isn't yours to use. It's yours to carry. Carrying is the whole job.'*" He smiled, small and crooked. "I thought she meant I should hide it. Bury it. Keep quiet in the quiet valley."

"And now?"

"Now I think she meant it exactly as she said it. Carry it. All the way." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet and perfectly steady, which is a combination I have only ever seen in saints and soldiers, and he was neither. "Anselm. Is there truly nothing?"

I could have lied. Understand that. I could have invented a ritual, a loophole, a conditional clause in some half-burned folio. He might even have believed me—he trusted scholars, poor lamb, which tells you how badly the crown's education served him. One lie, and I could have bought us all another week of hope, and hope, on a mountain, is warm.

But I watched him listen—not to me, to something behind me, some frequency I have never been able to tune—and I knew that a lie would be the cruelest thing I ever did. He would hear the truth eventually, in the voices. He would hear *me*, afterward, in the silence, carrying my falsehood into eternity with him.

"No," I said. "Nothing. Only precedent. And precedent says it can be borne gladly."

He nodded, slow, the way men nod at verdicts they expected.

"Then that's that," he said. "Thank you for looking anyway. Thank you for hating it properly. Someone ought to hate it on my behalf, and Serah's too angry and Tam's too young, so I'm glad it's you."

"You are insufferable," I told him. "Do you know that? You sit there accepting apocalypse like a man accepting soup."

"My mother raised me to be polite to guests."

"I am not a guest, I am a chaplain without a church, which makes me a professional mourner, and I decline to mourn you in advance. I reserve the right to be furious at your funeral. If funerals require a body, which yours conspicuously will not—"

"Anselm." He put his hand over mine. His hands were scarred all over, carver's scars, a lifetime of small cuts from making beautiful things for other people's grief. "Listen to me. I have heard the dead my whole life. Every regret, every unfinished sentence, every *I wish I had said*. Do you know what none of them say? None of them say *I wish I had kept my gift*. They say *I wish I had given it*. Spent it. On someone." He squeezed my hand once and let go. "Maren spent hers on nine minutes. I intend to be slightly more efficient."

Above us, Eyrrith breathed. Below us, the world waited. Somewhere down the mountain, I knew—Serah had seen the smoke-signals at dusk—the crown was climbing, a thousand strong, with orders I had read in Anselm-the-chaplain's old world and understood in Anselm-the-fugitive's new one: kill the dragon, bury the truth, salt the earth behind it.

We had perhaps a day.

I went to tell Serah. It was the worst conversation of my life, and I once told a duchess her son was damned, so the bar was high. She argued until her voice broke, and then she argued silently, which was worse, and then she went and stood at the Spire's edge for a long time with her old uniform coat folded over her arm like a flag.

And Gideon stayed where he was, carving, unhurried, finishing a stranger's memorial as though the world had time—which, I realized, watching him, was the entire point. He was not spending his last day preparing to die.

He was spending it working. Carrying. Right up to the door.

The first horn sounded from the valley below just as the light failed.
