## 18. The Crown Arrives

I had worn the hunt's uniform for eleven years, and I had burned it once, in a ditch outside a village called Marrow's End, and I had thought that was the end of it.

It turns out a thing you burn can still fit you. That's the trick nobody warns you about. You can shed a skin, walk away from it, build a whole new self out of desertion and doubt and a boy who needed feeding—and then the day comes when the only armor left to you is the shape of what you used to be, and you put it back on, and it fits like a confession.

Anselm found it for me. Of course he did. The man has an archive where his conscience should be, and somewhere in the wreck of the monastery he'd carried out of Ironhold's fires there was a campaign chest, and in the campaign chest, folded with a precision I found frankly insulting, was a captain's coat. Hunt-grey. Silver braid gone green at the edges. My old insignia stitched at the collar—Vane, S., Captain, Third Company—which meant either the crown kept excellent records or Anselm had stolen my file on his way out the door, which would have been the most criminal act of his entire priesthood and therefore the one I respected most.

"You don't have to wear it," he said, holding it out to me like a man offering a snake by the tail.

"Nobody *has* to do anything," I said. "That's rather the point of everything we've been arguing about since the bridge."

He didn't smile. Anselm rarely smiled when he was frightened, and he was frightened now, and honest enough not to pretend otherwise. "Serah. If they see you in that and you fail—"

"If they see me in this," I said, taking the coat, "they'll hesitate. Half a heartbeat. Maybe two. Hesitation is a currency up here, and I intend to spend every coin I have."

Above us, through the wound in the Spire's flank where Gideon had gone in with his shard and his ghosts and his terrible quiet certainty, dragon-fire pulsed like a heart remembering how to beat. Below us, coming up the mountain road in columns of grey and steel, came three thousand reasons why it wouldn't matter if I hesitated too long.

Three thousand. I counted them from the ridge while Tam watched the far passes with the spyglass and pretended his hands weren't shaking. I'd trained most of them. That's the arithmetic nobody prepares you for: not that you'll face enemies, but that you'll face your own work walking around without you, wearing your drills, singing your cadences. Lieutenant Ossler's banner at the center—I could pick his standard-bearer's stride out of a crowd of thousands, because I'd set that stride myself, years ago, on a parade ground in the rain. Colonel Brask's black lancers on the flanks. And behind them, at the rear where commanders always station themselves, close enough to take credit and far enough to dodge blame, the supply wagons that were not carrying supplies.

I knew what was in those wagons. Anselm's documents had told us. Soul-vaults, portable ones, harvested dead pressed into iron jars to feed the anti-magic engines—the same engines that had hummed outside Ironhold's walls, the same theft wearing a different road. The crown hadn't come to fight a battle. It had come to perform a burial. Kill the last dragon, seal the Spire with its corpse, salt the truth with fire, and go home to a kingdom where magic stayed safely caged and the only voices the living ever heard were the ones the crown approved.

And all that stood between them and forever was a bone-carver with no talent for fighting, a priest with no faith left to lose, a hollow boy with a spyglass and more courage than sense, and me.

Well. Me, plus whatever Gideon had managed to wake up in the world's bones.

"They'll reach the lower switchbacks by midmorning," I said. "The ice field slows them after that. If Gideon needs until dusk—"

"He won't say," Tam said. He'd been relaying messages up and down the Spire's throat all night, small and quick as a ferret in the broken stairwells. "He just says, tell Serah: *do what you're good at.*"

"What am I good at, exactly?"

Tam looked at me with those brand-scarred eyes of his, and for a moment I saw Gideon in him—not the features but the habit, that way of looking at a person like they were something unfinished and worth finishing. "Stalling," he said. "You're really good at stalling. You stalled Ossler at the toll bridge for almost an hour and he *liked* you."

There was a compliment buried in there somewhere. I chose to dig for it later.

* * *

I dressed for war the way you dress for a funeral—slowly, alone, with attention to detail. The coat first. Then the sword, because a captain without a sword is a curiosity, and curiosities get cut down out of hand. Then the insignia, polished. Then I stood in front of the ice-glazed wall of the camp and looked at my reflection, and there she was: Captain Serah Vane, Third Company, loyal hound of a crown I had personally watched murder a valley full of innocents.

My hands remembered the weight of authority before my heart did. That was the worst part. Eleven years leaves grooves in you, and my spine found the old posture on its own, shoulders back, chin level, the whole architecture of command settling over me like snowfall. I hated how natural it felt. I hated that some part of me stood straighter *because* of what the coat meant, even now, even knowing what I knew.

Gideon found me there. He came down from the Spire's wound pale as the ice, dusted with white bone-powder, moving like a man who had been listening to something enormous and wasn't sure yet whether it was music or a landslide.

"That's a cruel joke," he said, nodding at the uniform.

"It's a tool."

"So are hammers." He studied me a moment. Gideon's silences were never empty; they were full of other people's words, and I'd learned to read the weather of them. This silence had Maren in it, I thought. It usually did, before hard things. "You could come inside. Eyrrith says the heart-chamber will hold. She says—" he stopped, and something moved across his face that I had no name for. "She says she remembers the last time the crown came to the Spire. She says they brought wagons then, too."

"I know what they brought."

"I know you know." He touched my shoulder—briefly, the way you touch a door you're not sure you want to open. "Come back through, Serah. Whatever happens out there. Come back through."

"Captain Vane doesn't know how to do anything else," I said. "That's rather her problem."

He held my eyes a moment longer. Then he went back up into the mountain, toward the fire and the shard and the ending or beginning of everything, and I turned downhill toward three thousand men and women I had once loved in the way you love a machine that works.

* * *

They saw me at the second switchback.

I walked out onto the road ahead of the vanguard with my hands empty and my collar bright, and I heard it move through the ranks like wind through wheat: *that's Vane. Vane. They said she deserted. That's Captain Vane.* A hundred yards of frozen road between me and four hundred drawn blades, and every one of those blades suddenly uncertain, and I thought, *good,* and I thought, *God forgive me, this feels like home.*

Ossler came forward himself. Of course he did; I'd taught him that too—you meet trouble at the front or you teach your whole company that trouble flows downhill to them. He'd put on weight since the bridge and lost nothing else. His sword was drawn, which was protocol, and lowered, which was sentiment.

"Serah Vane." He said it the way you'd name a disease. "You have a great deal of nerve."

"I have a great deal of experience," I said. "Nerve is what you're showing, bringing soul-wagons up a holy mountain."

His jaw tightened. So they *hadn't* told the rank and file what the wagons carried. Good. Ignorance is a wall, and I intended to climb it loudly. "By order of the Crown—"

"Oh, spare me the order. I wrote half your orders' phrasing, Ossler, I know the stock phrases better than the clerks. Let's talk instead about what's actually behind us on this road." I raised my voice—not shouting, never shouting; a captain's trick is to pitch your voice so the third row leans in—and I let it carry. "Wagons of iron jars. Ask your quartermaster what's in them. Go on. Ask him why the vaults need escorting past a battlefield. Ask him why the dead of Ironhold were counted as *cargo.*"

"Vane—"

"Ask him why children born plain are branded and exiled! Ask him what the hunt is actually hunting, because I'll tell you what I found, lieutenant. I found archives. Burned monastery, ash and vellum, and the truth sitting in the middle of it like a stone in a loaf: the covenant wasn't broken by dragons or witches or any enemy at all. The crown broke it. A century ago. To keep magic for itself. Every fire we ever set, every village, every family—we weren't containing a threat. We were burying a *theft.*"

The ranks had gone very quiet. Snow-quiet. Somewhere in the third row a soldier—a girl I'd promoted myself, two winters ago, for steadiness under fire—was staring at the supply train as if it might confess.

Ossler stepped closer, voice dropping to a knife's whisper. "Whatever you think you found, you're standing between this company and its lawful duty."

"Yes," I said. "I am. That's the job today."

"You'll be arrested."

"Probably."

"You'll be *executed.*"

"After the arrest, presumably, yes. Ossler." I softened, because there was a time softness worked on him, and because underneath the hatred he was afraid, and afraid men make poor decisions unless someone gives them a better one. "Look at the mountain. Look at the Spire. When did the sky last look like that? When did the ground last hum? The engines are dying, whether we kill that dragon or not. The covenant is reknitting itself with or without our permission. The only question the crown hasn't thought to ask is: which side of history do you want frostbite on?"

For a moment—one long, crystalline moment—I thought it might work. I thought I could talk three thousand people off a cliff their careers had pushed them toward.

Then the horns sounded from the ridge above the ice field, and Colonel Brask's lancers came down at the trot, and the moment shattered like everything else does when cavalry arrives.

* * *

Brask didn't negotiate. Brask had never negotiated in his life; conversation, to Brask, was a prelude to positioning. His lancers swept around my standoff at the switchback in a wheel of grey riders, and Ossler, given the gift of a superior officer to defer to, took it with both hands.

"Take her," Brask said. Two troopers dismounted. I didn't resist, partly because resistance would cost lives I wanted spent elsewhere, and partly because being arrested bought time, and time was the entire economy of my morning.

They bound my wrists in front—someone's small mercy, or perhaps someone's memory of me—and marched me down the column past faces I knew and faces I'd trained and one boy, hardly shaving, who whispered, "Is it true? About the wagons?" as I passed. I met his eyes and gave him the smallest nod I could manage, and watched the nod travel down the line like a spark down a fuse.

Good. Let it smolder. I only needed the mountain to hold until dusk.

They put me in front of Brask's command tent on the ice field itself, kneeling in the snow, which was theater—commanders execute people standing—but Brask liked his theater cold. He circled me once, twice, the way he'd circle a position he meant to take.

"Eleven years, Vane. Eleven years building the Third into what it is. And you throw it away for a carver and a defrocked priest and a branded child."

"Not thrown away," I said. "Spent. There's a difference. You'd know it if you'd ever owned anything worth spending."

He struck me then, open-handed, a commander's slap, more insult than injury. I tasted blood and grinned at him, which was unwise, and worth it.

"The dragon dies tonight," Brask said. "The Spire gets sealed. Whatever witch-song that carver is piping in there dies with it. And you'll watch, Vane. You'll kneel right there and watch, and then you'll be tried properly, in the capital, so everyone can see what becomes of captains who forget whose coat they wear."

I looked past him, up the mountain, to where the Spire stood black against a sky that was doing something skies should not do—shivering, faintly, like the surface of a struck bell.

"Whose coat," I said slowly, "do you think I'm wearing right now, colonel? Because it isn't yours anymore. And it isn't the crown's." I raised my voice again, deliberately, letting it carry to the nearest ranks, to the girl I'd promoted, to the boy with the whisper, to every ear the wind would favor. "It's theirs. Everyone the hunt ever burned. Everyone in the wagons. I'm wearing *their* colors today, and I have never been prouder of a posting in my life."

Brask signaled, and a gag was produced, which I considered the highest compliment available to a staller: they had finally decided I was more dangerous talking than kneeling.

But the afternoon was dying. The light on the ice went gold, then copper, then the bruised violet of a sky holding its breath. The horns massed at the base of the Spire. Ladders and grapnels. Torches kindling in the blue dark like a constellation falling uphill.

And then—first as a tremor in the snow beneath my knees, then as a sound, a vast slow exhalation like the world turning over in its sleep—the mountain began to answer.

Light poured from the Spire's wound. Not torchlight. Not engine-light. Something older, redder, alive. The ice field glowed. The dead in the wagons, I swear to you, began to sing—or the jars did, a thin harmonic keening that made horses scream and veterans cross themselves—and Brask shouted orders that dissolved into the noise, and every eye on that mountainside turned upward to the burning heart of the Last Spire.

Where, in the doorway of light, small against the roar, a figure appeared. Bone-dust white. Arms raised. Speaking—I couldn't hear the words, but I felt them, in my teeth, in the scar tissue of my old life—in a voice made of ten thousand voices.

And beside him, wings unfolding like dawn deciding to happen, the last dragon opened her eyes.

I knelt there in the snow in a dead woman's uniform, gagged, bound, condemned, grinning like a fool with blood in my mouth.

Hold the line, Gideon. I've got the door.
