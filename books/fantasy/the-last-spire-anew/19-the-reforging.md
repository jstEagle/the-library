## 19. The Reforging

I have climbed a great many stairs in my life. I carved the banister of Maren's staircase from a single length of ash when I was fourteen, and I once hauled myself up the bell-tower of the Hollow Vale's chapel on a dare and a bad decision. None of it prepared me for the Last Spire.

The stairs inside it were not built. That was my first thought, and it was a carver's thought: whoever made this did not build, they *shaped*, the way you shape bone—slowly, with intent, each step grown rather than laid. The stone was warm under my palms. It had a pulse. Of course it did. Everything at the top of the world had a pulse now that we were close enough to hear it, and most of those pulses were failing.

"Keep moving," Serah said behind me, which was her way of saying *don't look down, don't look back, don't think about the army climbing toward us*. She had put on her old uniform for this—the hunt's grey-and-silver, the one she'd sworn she'd burn—and she wore it like armor worn ironically, like a joke only she was allowed to laugh at. "Anselm's counting the banners below. Eleven thousand."

"That's a lot of people to disappoint," Anselm said from somewhere above me. He had gone up first because he refused to let me die before he finished arguing, and because his legs were longer than his courage, though only slightly.

"They're not here for you," Serah said. "They're here for the dragon. And for him." A pause. "Mostly for him."

I kept climbing. The shard of living bone sat against my chest in its wrapping, and it had been getting warmer by the mile for three days, until now it felt less like an object and more like a small animal pressed to my sternum, breathing. Every dead voice I had ever heard could feel where I was going. They were quiet—not silent, never silent anymore—but quiet, the way a congregation is quiet. Waiting for the sermon. Hoping it's a good one.

The voices of the dead do not hope. That's what I told people, back when I lied for a living in the valley. Dead things don't hope; they remember, they ache, they repeat. But climbing those shaped stairs with eleven thousand soldiers coming up the mountainside behind us and a dying dragon coiled around the summit like a candle burning down to its last inch, I learned I had been wrong about that too. The dead hoped. They hoped through me, apparently, whether I liked it or not.

* * *

Eyrrith lay along the crown of the Spire like a river of embers deciding whether to become ash.

She was smaller than I'd imagined and larger than I could hold in my eyes at once, both at the same time, which is a thing dragons do to you. Her scales had gone the color of cooling charcoal, grey shot through with lines of dull red, and where her fire should have been there was only a slow breath, in and out, each exhale carrying a thread of smoke and no flame at all. When I stepped onto the summit platform she opened one eye—an eye like a well with something patient at the bottom of it—and looked at me for a long time.

"So," she said. Her voice didn't come through the air so much as through the ground, up through my boots, through the bones of my legs. Dragons speak to bone. I suppose that's why the wardens were wardens, and why I had been chosen without being asked. "The bone-carver. You are smaller than the last one."

"He said something similar about you, probably."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. It might have been a smile. With a dragon it might also have been indigestion. "You joke. Good. The last warden joked too, at the end. It is how the brave ones cover the sound of their own fear." The eye closed again. "You know what is required."

"I know what's required."

"Gideon—" Anselm started, and I held up a hand.

"We've done the argument," I said. "We argued at the end of the world, literally, on a mountain pass, in the snow, for two days. You lost honestly. I'm not doing overtime."

"There may be precedent," he said, and his voice cracked on the word, the scholar's word, the word he'd been hiding behind since the archive of ashes. "There may be—"

"Anselm." I turned and looked at him properly. He had ink on his fingers even now, even here, at the top of everything. He had run from a church, burned a life, walked half a realm on a guilty conscience to hand a bone-carver the truth, and none of it had bought him absolution yet. Maybe this would. Maybe nothing would. "When you find the alternative in your archives—and you will keep looking, I know you—you can tell me about it afterward. I'll be very nearby."

He stared at me. Then he took off his spectacles, polished them on his robe with tremendous concentration, and put them back on. "Afterward," he repeated, in the tone of a man agreeing to terms he intends to renegotiate with God directly.

Serah didn't argue at all. She just came forward and gripped my forearm, huntress-style, the grip she'd taught Tam, the grip that means *I see you and I'm not letting go until I choose to.* Her hand was shaking. Her voice wasn't. "Vane," she said. "Say it back."

"Serah Vane. Hunt-captain, retired, twice. Conscience over orders. Worst soldier the crown ever produced."

"And?"

"And the best friend a dead man ever had."

She let go fast, like the words had heat in them, and turned to the stairhead, where the sound of the army was beginning to arrive—a sound like rain on iron, thousands of boots finding the same rhythm. She rolled her shoulders. Straightened the uniform she hated. "Then I've got a door to hold," she said, "and you've got a world to fix. Try not to make it weird."

"I'm speaking to every dead person who ever lived while a dragon watches," I said. "It's going to be extremely weird."

* * *

The heart of the Spire was a socket, and the socket was empty.

That was the whole horror of it, once I stood in front of it. Not grand, not terrible, not some vast engine of covenant-magic. Just a hollow in the summit stone, shaped exactly like the shard against my chest, worn smooth by centuries of holding something that wasn't there anymore. A century of emptiness. A century of dragons guttering and dead souls pressing against a fraying boundary and a crown building soul-vaults to harvest the leak. All of it because someone had pulled a key out of a lock and put it in a vault and called it policy.

I unwrapped the shard.

Living bone doesn't look like much. It looks like what it is: something carved by someone who loved the work. My own tool marks, from years ago, from a keepsake that crossed a valley border and woke me up. Along its length the dead woman's plea still lived—*warn the warden*—though the warden had been dead a year by then, murdered for knowing too much, and the warning had become a job description instead.

"You knew," I said to it. "All along. From the first moment you spoke."

The shard pulsed. Warmth spread through my palm, up my wrist, into the old scar where Maren's kettle had scalded me when I was nine. I set it into the socket.

It fit the way a word fits at the end of a sentence.

And the Spire *woke*.

Light ran up through the shaped stone like sap through a branch, and the pulse I'd been feeling in the walls became a drumbeat, became a heartbeat, became *my* heartbeat, because the Spire reached into me the way I had spent my whole life reaching into other people's grief, and it found the gift, and it said: *there you are. We've been waiting.*

Every voice I had ever heard arrived at once.

Not the flood—that had been crossing the mist-line, drowning, terrifying. This was different. This was ordered. This was a choir finding its note. The traveler's wife whose keepsake started all of this. The drowned boy from the river village. The branded children's grandmother, the hollow ones' forgotten dead, the thousands upon thousands pressed into Ironhold's soul-vaults whose fragment I had freed, whose anger I had refused, who had parted from me cold and come back anyway, because dead things forgive what the living cannot afford to. The murdered warden. The old kings. The no-name dead, the unnumbered dead, the ones history never bothered carving a bone for.

They came up through my arm into the Spire, and the Spire gave them back to me as light.

And underneath all of it, briefly—briefly, the way a hand rests briefly on your shoulder in passing—

"Maren."

Her voice. Not remembered. *Her.* The particular dryness of it, the way she'd say my name when she caught me lying about hearing the dead, which was always, the way she'd said it the last morning, standing in the doorway with my satchel already packed, saying *run, love, run now*—

"You packed light," I said, and my face was wet, and I didn't care, there was no one left to pretend for. "You always told me to pack extra socks."

*Listen,* she said—or I heard her say, or the whole chorus said with her voice, it hardly mattered—*you listen better than anyone I ever knew. Time to use it.*

So I used it.

I spoke the covenant.

I don't know the words. That sounds like a lie from a man who has just reforged the binding of realms, but it's true—I don't know them, I have never known them, they passed through me the way a river passes through a channel it didn't dig. The dead knew. The Spire knew. Eyrrith knew, and lifted her head, and breathed, and her breath met mine somewhere in the middle, and between the three of us—the living carver, the dying dragon, the numberless dead—we said what needed saying:

*The realms are one. What was sundered is knit. What was hoarded is returned. Magic follows no crown. No crown holds magic. The boundary is not a wall but a door, and the door stands open, and the key turns forever.*

Eleven thousand men were climbing the mountain. The anti-magic engines at their backs—the great iron hearts fed on harvested souls, the machines that made the Hollow Vales everywhere, that made silence a weapon—those engines died. Not broke. *Died*, the way a lie dies when the truth walks into the room: instantly, completely, almost politely. I felt them go out across the whole realm, one after another, a hundred candles pinched between the same wet fingers, and with each one a stolen soul went home.

Dragon-fire roared back.

I mean that literally. Eyrrith inhaled once, a sound like the world drawing breath before it speaks, and then flame—true flame, gold-shot, the color the archives swore dragons used to be—erupted from her throat and lit the entire sky, and the army on the mountainside stopped climbing, and stopped shouting, and stood in the sudden daylight of her burning and understood, all of them at once, the way armies understand things, that the war was over and they had been on the wrong side of it for a hundred years.

The realms knit together. I felt it happen in my bones, because of course I did; I was the needle, and this was the seam. Magic poured back into the places it had been locked out of. Somewhere south, the Hollow Vale's mist-line thinned to nothing, and I thought, absurdly, *Maren's garden is finally going to get real rain.*

And I began to fade.

* * *

Fading is not what I expected. Nothing about this day was what I expected, so fair's fair.

I expected pain. There wasn't any. I expected fear, and there was a little, the way there's a little cold water at the edge of a warm bath. Mostly what I felt was *lighter*. The gift—which had been a weight, always, even mute in the valley, even pretending—it was pouring out of me and becoming the world's instead, and every drop of it that left made the world more whole and me less solid.

The Spire hummed. The dead sang. Eyrrith circled the summit once, airborne for the first time in a century, trailing fire like a banner, and her shadow swept over me and I waved at it, because if you can't wave at a resurrected dragon's shadow at your own dissolution, when can you.

Below, at the stairhead, Serah stood alone in her hated uniform with her sword planted in the stone, and the front rank of the army knelt to her—one by one by one, like wheat going down before wind—and I laughed, or the idea of me laughed, and she couldn't hear it, and that was fine. That was going to be the arrangement from now on. Fine.

Anselm was crying and taking notes at the same time, which was the most Anselm thing I had ever witnessed, and I resolved to haunt him specifically and often.

Tam—where was Tam? Down the mountain, safe, with the mules and the unfinished carvings, because we'd agreed, because I'd insisted. I sent what was left of my voice down the bond, not words, just warmth. *Watch the high passes, kid. Learn the grain of the bone before you cut. Don't be afraid of the quiet.*

The light took my hands first. I watched my fingers go translucent against the Spire's stone and found, to my surprise, that I minded far less than I should have. A carver gives his hands to the work. That's the whole trade. You give and you give and someday the work takes the rest.

The last of me that stayed was listening. Of course it was. That had always been the truest part of me, the part I buried in the valley, the part that crossed a border and woke up, the part Maren had believed in before I did.

The realms healed around me like bone knitting. The dead went quiet—not silenced, not exiled, just *resting*, the way you rest when someone reliable is keeping watch. And I settled into the Spire and the passes and the mist-lines and the high quiet places, everywhere and thin and warm, and began the long work of being heard by no one and felt by everyone.

Somewhere below, Serah looked up at the summit—at the empty air where I had been standing—and frowned, and shivered, and pulled her cloak tighter, and said, quietly, to nobody:

"Make it weird, then."

Oh, Serah. You have no idea.
