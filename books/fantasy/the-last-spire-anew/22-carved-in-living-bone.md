## 22. Carved in Living Bone

The last piece Gideon ever carved was a sparrow.

Not a grand thing. Not a covenant-key or a memorial for some lordling's grandmother. A sparrow, no bigger than my fist, wings folded, head turned as if listening to something just past the edge of hearing—which, knowing Gideon, was probably the joke. He'd been working on it the winter before the Spire, back when we were still a company of strays pretending we weren't walking toward his death. He'd whittle at it by firelight while Serah drilled me on how to hold a knife without stabbing myself, and Anselm read aloud from his heretical little archive, and Gideon would say things like "the wing needs to look like it's about to decide," which made no sense until you looked at the wing and saw that he was right, it did need that, and you had no idea how to give it what it needed.

He never finished it. The left wing was still a suggestion of a wing, more wood than bird. I found it in his satchel after everything, wrapped in oilcloth with his good chisels, like he'd known someone would come looking and wanted them to find the tools along with the unfinished thing. Which, knowing Gideon, was also probably the point.

I finished it. It took me two years, because I am not Gideon Halloran and never will be, and because every time I picked up a chisel I could hear him in my head saying *the wing needs to look like it's about to decide*, and mostly what I wanted was to throw the whole sparrow into the river and take up honest work, like banditry.

But here's the thing nobody tells you about grief: it doesn't teach you anything new. It just makes you better at what you already knew. And what I knew—what I'd learned from a bone-carver who heard dead women begging him to warn wardens, from a huntress who quit the only life she'd known because her conscience finally got loud enough, from a priest who unbelieved his way into the truth—was that you finish the work. You don't get to leave the wing half-decided. That's not how any of this goes.

So I finished it. And now I'm carrying it up the Last Spire, which is exactly as pleasant as it sounds, and I'd like a word with whoever decided that monuments to sacrifice should be located at altitudes where your ears bleed.

* * *

Let me back up, because Serah says my stories start in the middle and wander backward like a drunk looking for his house.

My name is Tam. It was the only thing I owned for most of my childhood: a name, given to me by a mother I don't remember, kept through four villages that took one look at my bare wrist—the smooth, unbranded wrist, which is its own kind of brand if you think about it—and decided I was hollow. Empty. A cup with nothing in it. They said it like an insult. Hollow. Like there was supposed to be magic in me and someone had scooped it out, and the absence was my fault, personally, some failing of mine committed before I could walk.

Here's what I learned later, from Anselm's burned-archive truth: there was never anything missing. The crown stole it before any of us were born, hoarded it behind their mist-lines and their soul-vaults, and then branded the children who came up short so we'd blame ourselves instead of them. Clever, really. Cruelty that recruits its victims as enforcers. I spent ten years being ashamed of a theft I didn't witness.

And then one day on the Road of Small Ghosts—a road named by someone who clearly hated hope—a bone-carver with tired eyes and a satchel full of other people's dead stopped at the edge of the village stockade where they'd tied me out overnight like a goat, and he crouched down, and he looked at my wrist, and he said:

"They've got it backwards, you know."

I said, "Got what backwards."

"The mark. Everyone acts like the gifted are marked and the rest are blank." He turned my hand over, gentle, like he was reading it. "But a blank page is where everything gets written. I've spent my whole life wishing I were blank."

I didn't believe him. I want to be clear about that, for honesty's sake. I thought he was either mad or setting me up for something worse. But he cut me loose anyway, and fed me anyway, and when the village reeve came shouting he stood up slow and calm and let his gift do the talking—he could hear the reeve's dead mother, apparently, and she had opinions about how her son treated runaways—and by nightfall I was following three strangers up a mountain pass because one of them had looked at the thing I'd been told was my worst feature and called it a doorway.

That's the whole story of how I got adopted, if you're wondering. No ceremony. No blood-oath. Just a man who was too busy carrying the weight of the world to notice he'd picked up a boy along the way.

* * *

The Spire hasn't changed much since the reforging, except in all the ways that matter.

It used to be dead stone, cold as a tax collector's heart, with a dying dragon coiled at its summit like the world's saddest hearth-fire. Now it hums. That's the only word for it. You feel it in your teeth from a mile out, a low warm thrumming, like the whole tower is purring. The dragon-fire came back the night Gideon set the shard in the heart-stone—we all felt it, even down in the battle-churn below, a heat that went through the world like a struck bell goes through air—and it hasn't guttered since. Eyrrith lives. She's old and cranky and she pretends not to notice pilgrims, which is a lie, because I've seen her open one eye and track every single person who climbs past her ledge with the expression of a cat pretending not to care about the moth.

She lets me climb past. She always lets me climb past. I choose to believe this is respect and not pity.

The stair is carved with names now. That started as an accident—some grieving pilgrim scratched a loved one's name into the stone two years back, and nobody removed it, and then somebody else added one, and now the whole ascent is a ribbon of names winding up into the sky. Mothers, fathers, soldiers, hollow-born children who died in exile and got carried home by friends who refused to bury them anywhere but here. Anselm says it's the largest act of collective memory in recorded history, and then he cries a little, because Anselm has been crying a little for six years and shows no sign of stopping, and honestly at this point it's part of his charm.

I add names as I go. It's part of why I make this climb every year. My hands do the carving now—that's my trade, my calling, the thing the blank page turned out to be for. Carved in living bone, Gideon used to say about his best work, though he worked wood and horn and antler more often than actual bone, because actual bone required asking permission from the dead, and he was careful about that. Polite. He talked to the dead like they were neighbors whose fence he might've leaned on. I think about that a lot, when people bring me their keepsakes—a father's pipe, a daughter's hairpin, a soldier's bent buckle—and ask me to carve the memory into something they can hold. I'm not Gideon. I can't hear anybody. But I've decided that's its own kind of gift. Everything I carve carries only what the living remember, and the living, it turns out, remember beautifully when you give them somewhere to put it.

* * *

Summit. Wind. The heart-stone chamber, open to the sky, where the shard of living bone sits in its cradle of dragon-glass and glows like a coal that refuses to die.

I kneel. I always kneel, not because anyone requires it but because the room asks something of you and kneeling is my answer.

"Hey, Gideon," I say. Same as every year. "Brought it."

I unwrap the sparrow.

Here's the strange part, the part I've never told Serah or Anselm because they'd either worry about me or write a theological treatise about me, and I don't have the patience for either. When I set the sparrow on the shelf beside the heart-stone—there are shelves now, pilgrims leave offerings, the church that once hunted people like us built them at our request, which is a sentence that would have gotten us all burned seven years ago—I feel him.

Not hear him. Feel him. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point of everything.

Warmth. Watchfulness. The unmistakable sensation of being *noticed* by something that wishes you well. Travelers report it all through the high passes now—shepherds who swear something steered them away from a cornice crack, lost children who walked out of whiteouts because they felt, and I quote the official inquiry, "a hand on the shoulder that wasn't there." The provisional council has a name for it in the new liturgies: the Silent Guardian. Serah testified before that council herself, in full view of the crown remnant she once served, and told them exactly what waits at the boundary between realms. A voice that cannot be heard. Only felt. The first of the silent dead, and—if the warden-ghost's old prophecies hold—the doorkeeper of all the others.

Because here's what the reforging bought, what it cost him: the dead rest quiet now. All across the realm, the restless voices, the hungry ghosts, the souls pressed into vaults—they're settled. Held. Tucked in, like Maren used to tuck Gideon, I imagine, back in the valley where he made silence his home. And the one who holds the door for them is the one who can't come through it anymore. Who gave up hearing to become the hearing itself.

I talk to him anyway. Every year. I tell him about the shop in the rebuilt vale—yes, the valley opened its borders, Maren's people voted again and this time chose differently, and they asked me to set up my bench in Gideon's old workshop, which nearly killed me with irony and I accepted anyway. I tell him Serah is teaching now, training a new kind of hunter whose job is protecting the gifted instead of gutting them, and that she still cheats at cards, and that she visited last month and critiqued my joinery and I wouldn't trade her criticism for anyone's praise. I tell him Anselm's archive became the founding text of the Open Covenant schools, and that children learn the true history before they learn to fear it, and that Anselm cried during his lecture again, obviously.

"And the sparrow's done," I tell him. "Finally. Left wing decided. Took me two years, and it's not as good as yours would've been, but—" I stop, because my throat does the thing it does. "But it's about to fly. So. There."

The heart-stone pulses. Once. Slow. Warmth spreads through the chamber like a hand laid flat between my shoulder blades.

I know, I know—it's dragon-fire resonance, thermal mass, atmospheric pressure, pick your explanation. Anselm has provided several. Serah has provided none, because Serah just puts her hand on the stone every year and stands there with her eyes shut for a long time, longer than she'd ever admit to, and dares anyone to comment.

But I'm the one who finishes his carvings. Let me keep this one belief. It costs nothing, and it pays out warmth every time.

* * *

On the way down, past the names, past Eyrrith's ledge (one eye opens; I bow; she closes it with tremendous dignity), I stop at the shelf where the sparrow sits and look at it one last time against the sky.

Wings folded, head turned, listening. About to decide.

That's when I see it—or think I see it, and I've made peace with never being sure. The left wing. The one I carved, the one that took two years and three thrown chisels. It looks different in this light. Not finished-looking. *Deciding*-looking. Lifted, just slightly, just enough, like the bird caught the exact moment before flight and froze there forever, poised on the edge of going.

I stand in the wind a long while.

Then I laugh—it comes out wet, but it's a real laugh—and I wipe my face with my sleeve like a professional, and I start down the stairs, past the thousand names, toward the vale, toward the shop, toward the living who wait and the work that continues.

Behind me, the Spire hums. Somewhere above, a warmth watches me go, wordless, patient, unmistakably pleased.

The wing, I decided, was right.

It's about to fly.
