## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley had one rule, and the rule was simple: nothing followed you in.

Edmund Ravensworth had learned it her first winter, when she'd come down the eastern pass half-frozen with a stolen cloak and a name she'd already started shedding. She'd crossed the ridgeline at dusk, and somewhere between one stone and the next — between the last pine on the slope and the first crooked fencepost of Hollowmere — the world had gone quiet. Not silent. Silent was just an absence. This was a *quiet*, thick and total, like sinking into a feather bed that pressed gently against your ears.

The magic died at the ridgeline.

She hadn't believed it at first. She'd spent six years with Mistress Yarrow learning exactly how thin the skin of the world was, how easily you could slip a finger through and feel what moved underneath — the listening dark under hearthstones, the slow green attention of old trees, the small hungry things that answered if you called them wrong. She knew the texture of that other country the way a fisherman knows water. And crossing into the valley was like being pulled out of it, gasping, onto dry land.

The pots didn't sing here. The shadows kept to their proper shapes. When she dreamed, she dreamed only what she'd seen, which after years of hedge-witch dreams was its own kind of mercy.

So she stayed. Seven years now. She mended pots.

It was honest work, or close enough to honest. She'd set up in a lean-to behind Widow Crake's house on the low street, with a bucket of rivets and a tub of flux and a sign she'd painted herself: MENDING DONE, FAIR RATES, NO QUESTIONS. The no-questions part wasn't for the customers. It was for her.

Hollowmere was a valley of people who'd come there to be forgotten, though nobody said so out loud. The miller had hands too soft for milling and a way of flinching at hoofbeats. The schoolteacher drank on feast days and wept at songs about mothers. Old Tam, who fished the mere, never once said where he'd been before, and everyone agreed this was correct behavior. They were all of them boats run aground, hulls scraped clean, riding quietly in shallow water. You didn't ask another boat about the sea.

Edmund liked them. She even loved a few of them, carefully, the way you love something you might have to put down. But she never let herself forget that the quiet was a wall, not a home, and walls are for keeping things out as much as keeping yourself in.

For seven years, it had held.

* * *

The morning the stranger came, Edmund was fixing a kettle for the schoolteacher, who had burned through the bottom of it by forgetting it on the fire during a lesson about the Aldermish kings. Edmund found this funny in a private, bitter way. There were three kings on the schoolroom chart — Aldous the Third, crowned in gold leaf gone brown; his father; his grandfather — and beneath them, in smaller print, the long gray list of regents and stewards who'd actually done the ruling while the crown passed from soft hand to softer hand.

She was hammering a patch flat when the dogs started.

That was the first wrong thing. Hollowmere's dogs didn't bark much. Nothing came up the passes that the valley didn't see coming for hours, and the few travelers who did come were met at the ridgeline by whoever drew the short straw that week, walking them in politely, feeding them, and walking them back out before they could notice that their prayers felt heavier here than anywhere else in the world. The dogs barked at foxes, occasionally, and once at a cloud shaped like a wolf. They did not bark like this — high, frantic, a whole chorus of them, every dog in the village at once.

Edmund set down the kettle. Her hands had gone still in that particular way they went still when the hair on her arms lifted, and she hated her own body for remembering what that meant.

*Mistress Yarrow used to say,* a voice in her head supplied, treacherous and familiar, *when your skin knows before you do, believe your skin.*

She hadn't let herself think about Yarrow in four years. She made a note of that, too: seven years in the valley, and the first crack in it, and look what crawled through. Not soldiers. Not memories of the road. Her teacher's voice, warm as a hearth and cold as a well, saying *believe your skin.*

She went out into the street anyway.

* * *

They came down the low street together — half the village, trailing dogs, keeping a loose circle around the thing that had staggered out of the western pass.

Edmund worked her way to the front, because being at the front meant seeing and seeing meant surviving, and because some part of her that she despised wanted to know.

It was a knight. Or it had been. The armor told that story: good steel once, dented now, the surcoat beneath it bleached almost white by weather so that the device on the chest was a ghost of itself — some bird, maybe, wings spread, or just a stain. One gauntlet was missing entirely, and the bare hand beneath it was pressed hard against the rider's side, and the hand was red to the wrist.

The stranger had made it as far as the well before collapsing against its stones, one arm hooked over the lip like a swimmer clinging to a wreck. Under the helm — pushed back, showing a face gray with pain, jaw stubbled, a woman maybe forty with a scar cutting through one eyebrow — under all of it, the stranger was holding something against her chest with her other arm, curled around it, protective, the way you hold a child.

"Water," the stranger rasped. "Please. Water."

Widow Crake looked at Edmund. Everyone looked at Edmund, which was unfair and also accurate; somehow over seven years she'd become the person the valley consulted about anything unusual, precisely because she was the least unusual person in it, the steadiest, the one who never flinched. The irony of that could have filled a book.

"Get her water," Edmund said. "And someone fetch Tam — he's got the strongest cart. We'll want her out of the sun."

"You know the rule," said Miller Ansel quietly, beside her. His voice had that careful flatness they all used when the rule came up. "Nothing follows us in."

"She's dying," Edmund said. "Dying doesn't count as following."

But even as she knelt by the well and took the cup Widow Crake handed down, Edmund felt the wrongness pressing against her like weather. The stranger's armor stank of outside — of road dust and horse sweat and blood and something else, something faint and sweet and rotten underneath, like flowers left too long in a sealed room. Edmund knew that smell. She had spent six years learning smells like that, and she had crossed a mountain range to forget them.

Then the stranger shifted, uncurling the arm around her chest, and Edmund saw what she'd been holding.

It was a stone the size of a fist. Pale gray, veined with white, smooth as a river pebble — except that it was glowing. Faintly, softly, the way a banked coal glows, a light that lived inside it and breathed. Light like that did not exist in the valley. Light like that was impossible in the valley, the same way singing pots and moving shadows were impossible, the same way everything Edmund had buried was impossible.

Her hands stopped working. The cup nearly slipped.

"That's a prayer-stone," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded very far away. "Where did you get a lit prayer-stone?"

The stranger's eyes found hers. They were brown, ordinary, and full of a desperation so complete it looked almost like calm.

"So you know what it is," the woman said. "Thank every god left standing." She tried to laugh, and it turned into a cough that bent her double, and fresh blood seeped between her fingers where they pressed her side. "I've ridden nine days looking for someone who'd know. Nine days. Every shrine I passed, every wise-woman, every hedge-priest — either they couldn't help me or they were too scared to try." Her grip tightened on the stone. "They said go to the valley. They said the valley forgets. They said if anyone can make it stop hearing me, it's the dead ground past the ridge."

"Make *what* stop hearing you?" Edmund asked. And then, because the question arrived in her mouth fully formed, without her permission, in a voice that was almost but not quite her own: "Or make you stop hearing *it?*"

The stranger stared at her. Around them the villagers had gone very quiet, in the way of people realizing they've wandered into a conversation in a language they don't speak.

"I don't—" the knight began.

But it was too late. Because Edmund, kneeling there in the dust of the low street with a mended kettle cooling behind her and seven safe years stacked up like sandbags around her heart, had heard it too. Just for a second. Just a thread of it, threading through the glow of the stone like smoke through a keyhole:

A voice, calling a name.

Not *Edmund.* Not *the pot-mender.* Not any of the names the valley knew her by.

It called the name she'd buried. The name from before. Her true name, spoken the way Mistress Yarrow had taught her it must be spoken — with the breath, and the weight, and the little silence after it, the silence where a god would listen.

The light in the stone pulsed once, like a heartbeat.

Edmund stood up so fast the world tilted.

"Tam," she said. "Cart. Now. Get her off the street."

* * *

They laid the stranger in Widow Crake's front room, on the good bed, and Edmund cleaned the wound while the widow hovered and the rest of the village pretended not to crowd the doorway.

It was a sword cut, long and deep along the ribs, but it was the edges of it that made Edmund's stomach drop. Wounds told stories if you knew how to read them, and this one read clearly: *made by a blade that had been blessed, and recently, and not kindly.* The flesh around the cut was gray-white, refusing to bleed properly, refusing to knit. She'd seen marks like it once before, years ago, on the flank of a horse Yarrow had cursed as a lesson. The wound hadn't closed until the curse was lifted.

"This wasn't bandits," Edmund said.

"No," the stranger agreed. Her name was Ser Maren, she'd finally said — Ser Maren Vale, formerly of the King's Own, formerly of several other things she didn't specify. She lay propped on pillows, gray-faced, holding the prayer-stone in both hands now like a talisman, which Edmund supposed it was. "Bandits don't ride royal colors."

"Whose colors?"

Maren's jaw worked. Outside, dusk was settling over the valley, and the light through the window went amber and then gray. "You know what a prayer-stone is," she said instead. "So you know how they work. Shrine-stone takes a prayer, holds it, carries it to whatever god it's addressed to. Old system. Older than the crown. Every chapel in Aldermarch has a shelf of them."

"They're never lit in the valley," Edmund said. "I've seen dead ones. They're just rocks."

"Right. Because the gods have to *answer,* see, for the light to catch." Maren laughed, briefly, horribly. "Eight months ago, they started answering again. All of them. Everywhere. Prayers that had sat unanswered on shelves for a hundred years — suddenly answered. The priests were weeping in the streets. The end of the Long Silence, they called it. The gods returning to their people." Her hands tightened around the stone until the knuckles went white. "Except."

"Except," Edmund said.

"Except the answers are wrong." Maren turned the stone over, and in the dim room its glow caught the planes of her face and made her look carved, monumental, like a figure on a tomb. "A woman prays for rain for her crops, and gets a flood that takes her barn. A man prays his sick daughter lives, and she lives — wakes the next morning healthy and hollow, staring at walls, not knowing her own name. A village prays for protection from raiders, and the raiders die to a man, every one, torn apart in the night by something no one sees. Answered. All of it answered. Cruelly, perfectly answered." She looked up. "Every prayer in Aldermarch is being answered, mistress mender. And every answer is a knife."

The room was silent. In the doorway, Widow Crake crossed herself with the old sign, the one that predated the Church of the Twin Rivers, the one you weren't supposed to use anymore.

"And this one?" Edmund nodded at the stone. "What does yours ask?"

Maren was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had dropped, gone rough, and Edmund understood suddenly that this was the real reason the woman had ridden nine days bleeding, why she'd come to a valley famous for nothing at all.

"It's my son's," she said. "He's seven. He prayed at the shrine in our village for — for his mother to come home safe. He prayed it in June. I was garrisoned at Caer Duloc; I'd be home by harvest." She swallowed. "The stone lit. The priests rejoiced. And then the answering came, and I — I've been running from it ever since. Because whatever heard him, it took the prayer literally. *Come home safe.* So it's been keeping me safe. From everything. Everything that comes near me dies, Edmund. Wolves, brigands, the king's own riders who were sent to collect me — the moment anything threatens me, it dies, horribly, and I can't stop it, and I can't get *home,* because home isn't safe, don't you see, I'm what isn't safe—"

Her voice broke. The stone pulsed in her hands, gentle, patient, monstrous.

"It won't let me stop," she whispered. "It won't let me *die.* It keeps me safe forever. And my son prayed for his mother, and he got *this,* and he's seven years old, and I need someone to make it stop hearing me."

Nobody moved. Somewhere outside a dog whined and fell silent.

Edmund stood in the amber lamplight with her sleeves rolled up and her hands still smelling of flux and vinegar, and she thought about walls. About boats run aground. About seven years of quiet bought at the price of everything she was, and how the price had seemed fair right up until this exact moment.

Because she knew voices. She knew what it meant when a god spoke a name with the breath and the weight and the listening silence after. And she knew — had known since the moment the stone lit and the thread of sound slipped through it — that the voice calling that buried name had not been addressing Ser Maren Vale at all.

It had been addressing *her.*

"I can't help you," Edmund said.

Maren looked at her. There was no anger in it. Only a tired, bottomless recognition, the look of one drowning person watching another stand safely on shore.

"No," Maren agreed. "I didn't think so. Nobody can. That's rather the point." She settled back against the pillows, gray and bleeding and unbearably polite. "But you'll want to know, before you decide. What's hunting me will be here within two days. Royal riders, and worse than riders. They want the stone. They want me. And now they'll want you, because I watched your face when it sang, mistress mender, and so did the thing that sings through it."

The lamp flickered. The stone breathed its slow coal-light against Maren's chest.

And from somewhere far away — beyond the ridgeline, beyond the wall of quiet, out in the wide listening world where magic ran like blood in old veins — Edmund felt something turn its attention toward the valley. Toward her. Patient as winter, curious as a child pulling the legs off a fly.

The valley's silence cracked a little further.

*Say it again,* the voice seemed to say, from everywhere and nowhere, kind and vast and terribly attentive. *Say your name again, little one. We've missed you.*

Edmund stood very still, and did not answer, and knew — with the absolute clarity of a woman watching her seven years of safety burn down around her like a paper house — that the past had found her, and that it had brought friends.
