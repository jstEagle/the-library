## 2. A Prayer Answered Wrong

Edmund did not sleep. She sat by the hearth through the small hours, mending a kettle handle that didn't need mending, and watched Ser Maren breathe.

The knight had not improved. Whatever wound she'd carried over the ridge had gone past bleeding into something else — the skin around it gray as old tallow, the fever burning her from the inside like a lamp behind parchment. Edmund had cleaned it, packed it with comfrey, done everything Tam had taught her for a hurt that wanted to kill. None of it worked. The wound wasn't behaving like a wound. It behaved like a mouth.

And on Maren's chest, wrapped in oilcloth and cord, lay the prayer-stone. It still glowed. Even now, in the dark of Edmund's cottage, with the fire burned down to coals, the stone's light seeped through the cloth in thin pulses, slow and steady, like a heartbeat that belonged to someone who was not in the room.

Magic died at the ridgeline. That was the whole reason this valley existed, the whole reason Edmund had found it seven years ago half-starved and followed the road down until the ache behind her eyes — the constant, low-grade headache of hearing things other people couldn't — simply stopped. The gods' voices cut off at the top of the pass like a door closing. Down here, nothing answered when you called. Nothing noticed you at all. She had spent seven years being gloriously, blessedly unnoticed: Edmund the pot-mender, Edmund the one to fetch the midwife, Edmund who kept to herself and asked no questions and had no history worth mentioning.

The stone glowed anyway.

That was the thing she kept circling back to, turning over like a coin she suspected was clipped. A prayer-stone was made to hold a prayer — carry it, keep it warm, deliver it somewhere. It should have been as dead as everything else down here. Instead it pulsed against Maren's chest, patient as a debt collector, and every pulse made the gray around Maren's wound spread a fraction wider.

Near dawn, Maren woke.

Not gently. She came up out of sleep like a drowning woman breaking surface, hand already reaching for a sword that wasn't there, and Edmund caught her wrist before the thrashing reopened anything. For a moment the knight stared at the rafters without recognition, chest heaving, and then her gaze found Edmund's face and something in it settled.

"You're still here," Maren rasped.

"Where would I go?"

"Away." Maren tried to laugh; it turned into coughing. "Everyone sensible goes away."

"I'm not sensible. I'm a pot-mender." Edmund pressed a cup of willow-bark tea into her hands. "Drink. And then you're going to tell me what that stone is doing, because it's killing you."

Maren looked down at her own chest as if she'd forgotten the stone was there. Then, slowly, she began unwinding the cord.

"It's not killing me," she said. "What killed me happened three days ago. This is just—" She stopped, jaw working. "This is just the proof."

The oilcloth fell open.

Up close, the prayer-stone was uglier than it had looked by firelight. It was the size of a fist, river-smooth once, but now cracked in a web across its surface, and the light inside those cracks wasn't gold or white or any color Edmund associated with prayer. It was a deep bruised red, and it moved — not pulsing now but crawling, slow worms of brightness chasing each other through the stone's veins.

"This is a courier-stone," Edmund said flatly. "My mistress used them. You pray into it, it holds your words until they reach—" She stopped. Carefully: "Until they reach whatever they were addressed to."

"And if what they were addressed to is listening," Maren said, "it answers."

* * *

She told it badly, the way people tell stories they've told themselves too many times and still don't believe. Eight months ago, King Aldous of Aldermarch stopped speaking. Not died — stopped. Sat through council meetings with his eyes open and his hands folded and said nothing at all, and the queen regent, Lady Vessia, began ruling through him, reading out his decisions to assembled courts while he sat silent beside her. And around that same time, across all of Aldermarch, prayers started getting answered.

"That doesn't sound so terrible," Edmund said, before she could stop herself.

"No," Maren agreed. "It sounded wonderful. That was how it worked. A farmer prays for rain, and rain comes — two valleys over, where it drowns his neighbor's lambs. A woman prays her son recovers from fever, and the fever leaves him and jumps to the boy next door. A village prays for an end to bandits, and the bandits are found dead — along with the miller who'd sold them bread once, because the thing that answered couldn't tell the difference between a prayer and a wish and a curse, or maybe it could, and didn't care." Her voice had gone very quiet. "It answers everything, pot-mender. Everything, instantly, exactly literally enough to ruin you. People learned fast to stop praying. But you can't stop needing. And it hears the need."

Edmund sat very still. Outside, the first birds had started up, ordinary and stupid, and she hated them a little for it.

"Why are you telling me this? Why did you come here?"

"Because the valley is the only place in Aldermarch it can't hear." Maren pushed herself upright against the wall, and Edmund saw what it cost her and let her do it anyway. "I carried this stone from the capital. There are others like me — we were trying to map which shrines still answer true and which answer wrong. Mine went wrong. I prayed at a wayshrine for safe passage, and the stone took my prayer and held it, and the thing on the throne heard it, and it sent riders." She touched the gray flesh at her side, lightly, the way you touch a scar you haven't accepted yet. "This isn't from a sword."

Edmund understood then, all at once, and wished she didn't. "You led them here."

"I led them to a valley they can't enter," Maren said. "There's a difference. But they'll wait at the ridgeline. They're patient. Things like that are always patient. Sooner or later someone walks out of a quiet place." She looked up, and her eyes were clear and fever-bright and terribly direct. "So I'm asking. Take it back over the ridge. Carry it somewhere it can be studied, broken, unmade — there are people who'd know how, god-speakers, scholars—"

"No."

The word came out of Edmund faster than thought, flat and final, and she stood up because standing felt safer than sitting. "No. I mend pots. I don't carry cursed stones over mountains for knights who bleed wrong. Find someone else."

"There is no someone else. That's what I've been trying to tell you." Maren's voice stayed level, which was somehow worse than shouting. "I've walked four hundred miles looking for someone else. Every hedge-witch is dead or fled or bought. Every shrine that answers true has gone quiet. Do you know why I stopped at this valley, Edmund? Because the moment I crossed the ridge, the stone stopped whispering."

Edmund's hands had gone cold. "Whispering."

"All the way down the pass. Like a man muttering in his sleep. Prayers, hundreds of them, layered on top of each other, all of them still live, all of them still— " Maren closed her eyes. "Addressed. And when I crossed the ridge, silence. Which means the stone knows where magic ends. Which means it's awake. And which means—" she opened her eyes again— "that whatever sleeps in this valley that keeps the magic out might also be keeping the stone quiet. Or the stone might be waiting for something on the other side of that silence. I don't know. But you do."

"I don't—"

"You flinched," Maren said simply, "when I said god-speaker."

* * *

Edmund spent the morning not thinking about it, which meant she spent the morning thinking about nothing else.

She went through the motions. Fed the chickens. Drew water. Took a neighbor's cracked crock in for mending and sat with it in her lap, her thumbs moving over the crack in the old patterns Tam had drilled into her — find the break, honor the break, the break tells you what the thing used to be — and all the while the name sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Because that was the truth of it, wasn't it. That was the thing she'd buried deeper than the valley buried magic. Not that she could hear the gods. That she'd *liked* it. Seven years old, kneeling in Tam's kitchen, and the first time a hearth-god spoke to her — a small brown voice like a banked fire, asking her name — she hadn't been frightened. She'd been *found*. And the name she'd given it, the name she'd carried like a lit coal through her whole apprenticeship, was the name her mother gave her, and when she ran, she left it at the border of the valley like an unwanted coat, and she had never once, not in seven years, said it aloud.

*Maren is dying,* said the pragmatic part of her mind. *The stone is killing her and soldiers are waiting at the pass and you could fix at least one of those problems.*

*I mend pots,* she told it.

*You mend breaks,* it answered, in Tam's voice, cruelly. *The break tells you what the thing used to be.*

In the end it was curiosity that undid her — or maybe it was only that she'd run out of ways to not-touch the stone, and her body decided for her. Late afternoon, with Maren sleeping a gray-lipped sleep that Edmund genuinely feared she'd wake from, Edmund knelt beside the pallet and reached out and laid two fingers on the oilcloth over the stone.

The world went red and vast.

Not a vision. A *crowd*. Hundreds of voices, all of them mid-prayer, all of them tangled together like roots under a forest floor — a woman begging for her daughter's life, a man begging for forgiveness he hadn't earned, a child begging for the dog to stop being dead — and underneath them, beneath and behind and threaded through every one, something enormous turning over in its sleep and *listening*, the way a mountain listens to footsteps—

And then one voice came free of the crowd. Small. Brown, if a voice could be brown, like a banked fire, like a kitchen.

*"Edra."*

Her knees nearly went.

*"Edra, little god-speaker, can you hear us? Can you hear us still? They told us you were dead. Your mother told us you were dead, to keep them from looking — Edra, please, if it's you, the doors are closing, one by one, we are starving, we are going out like candles—"*

Edmund snatched her hand back and sat down hard on the floorboards.

The cottage was ordinary. The coals ticked. Maren breathed, shallow and gray. Somewhere outside a chicken complained about nothing. The world was exactly as it had been, except that a voice had said her name — her real name, the buried one, the one nobody living knew — and had known her mother, and had begged.

*They told us you were dead.*

Tam had told the gods she was dead. Tam, whom she had hated for seven years with a clean, simple hatred that required no examination — Tam had lied to monsters to keep them off her trail, and Edmund had repaid her by running and never once wondering why.

"No," Edmund said aloud, to the cottage, to the stone, to the small brown voice that was probably starving somewhere beyond the ridge. "No. I'm not her. She's dead. I'm a pot-mender."

The stone pulsed once, red and patient, and said nothing, and somehow that was worse.

* * *

She was still on the floor when the knock came.

Three raps, polite, measured. The kind of knock a stranger gives when he wants you to know he could give a different kind.

Edmund rose without deciding to. Through the window she could see the lane, and the lane was wrong — hens scattered, old Widow Hale standing frozen at her gate with a basket of onions tipping slowly from her arm, staring up the track toward the ridge. Two figures stood at the edge of Edmund's yard. Not soldiers yet — no livery, no drawn steel. Just men, travel-stained, one large and one thin, both wearing the particular stillness of people who are very good at violence and are enjoying the pause before it.

"We're looking for a wounded woman," the large one called, pleasant as a tax collector. "Came over the pass yesterday. Gray cloak. Might be carrying something that doesn't belong to her."

Behind Edmund, from the pallet, came a sound. Not words. Just breath changing — the breath of a woman waking up and understanding, all at once, exactly where she was and what was about to happen.

Edmund looked at the window. At the ridge beyond it, blue and distant and utterly silent. At the crock in her hands, cracked clean through, waiting for someone to decide what it used to be.

"Edra," she said, very quietly, testing the weight of it after seven years. It fit her mouth better than it had any right to.

Then she set the crock down, unbroken, and went to get her boots.
