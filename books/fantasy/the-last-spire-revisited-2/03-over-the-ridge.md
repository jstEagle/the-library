## 3. Over the Ridge

Edmund had a system for leaving places. She'd learned it young and refined it over seven years of practice: pack nothing you can't carry at a run, take no object that carries memory with it, and never — *never* — let yourself look back at the door.

She broke the third rule within the first minute, standing in her cottage with a bundle of dry bread and her good mending needle, staring at the door as if it might explain itself. Behind it, somewhere down the dark lane, Ser Maren was bleeding slowly into a poultice Edmund had made from comfrey and spite, and the prayer-stone sat on the table between them like an egg that hadn't decided whether to hatch.

The stone still glowed. That was the whole problem. Nothing in this valley was supposed to glow.

"Stop looking at the door," Maren said from her chair by the hearth. Her voice was steadier than her color warranted. "It won't apologize."

"I wasn't—"

"You were. You've been looking at it for two hours." The knight shifted, winced, and pressed her palm flatter against her bandaged side. "I know the look. I wore it myself for three years after I left the king's guard. Every door becomes a question you're afraid to answer."

Edmund turned away from the door and busied herself with the kettle instead, which gave her hands something to lie about. "I haven't agreed to anything."

"No."

"I told you I'd think about it."

"You did."

"And thinking takes time."

"It's been two days," Maren said. "In two more days, whoever cut me open on the ridge will have followed my blood down into your valley, or they'll have given up. In my experience, Edmund Ravensworth—" she said the name carefully, tasting each syllable, watching Edmund flinch at all four of them, "—the people who hunt prayers do not give up."

"That's not my name."

"It's the name the stone called you."

"The stone is a rock."

"The stone," said a small voice from the window seat, "is humming."

Pip had been there for three days too, which was how long it took Edmund to notice that the orphan girl who sometimes slept in her woodshed had moved herself indoors without anyone granting permission. Pip was perhaps ten, thin as a winter branch, with a gap where her front teeth should be and a habit of talking to empty air that the village had long ago decided to ignore rather than address. She sat cross-legged now, chin in her hands, staring at the table.

"Humming what?" Edmund asked, because she hated herself for asking but asked anyway.

"A song. A sad one." Pip tilted her head. "Like the miller's dog when they chained it up. It wants to go home, but home is far, and something is sitting on home."

Maren and Edmund exchanged a look over the kettle's steam. It had become, in two days, an uncomfortably familiar exchange — the knight's flat gray eyes asking *you heard that too?* and Edmund's silence answering *yes, and I hate it.*

Because here was the thing Edmund had not said aloud in seven years: the valley was quiet. Not peaceful-quiet. Quiet like a held breath. Magic died at the ridgeline; she'd known that when she came, half-fleeing and half-following a rumor, and she had loved it with a desperation she'd never admitted even to herself. No voices in hedgerows. No gods leaning close to whisper their hungers. Just pots to mend and herbs to dry and neighbors who thought she was a plain woman with steady hands.

But a quiet room isn't empty, her old mistress used to say. It's just waiting.

And for two days the valley had been holding its breath around a glowing stone, and Edmund could feel the silence straining like ice before thaw.

"They'll come tonight," Maren said quietly. "Not tomorrow. Tonight. There's no moon until late, and riders move best in the dark. I would."

"You don't know that."

"I was the queen regent's shield for nine years. I know how she moves." A pause. "How *it* moves. Whatever sits behind her eyes now."

Edmund poured water nobody had asked for. "Even if — *if* — I agreed. Even if I carried your glowing rock back over the ridge. Then what? Prayers are being answered wrong, you said. Twisted. What does a mender of pots do about that?"

"Nothing," Maren said. "You. Whatever you are, whatever blood runs in you — the stone answered to you, Edmund. It hasn't glowed for anyone else in eight months. Eight months of priests and hedge-witches and god-touched fools, and it waited in my pack like a dead coal until you picked it up." She leaned forward despite the wound. "Things wait for people like you. And things like what wears the crown wait for people like you too. The only question is which one finds you first."

The kettle began to sing. Edmund didn't move to lift it. She stood there listening to it scream and thought: *seven years.* Seven years of anonymity, of being no one, of the beautiful dead silence where nothing knew her name. And in two days a dying stranger had cracked all of it open like an eggshell, and underneath, embarrassingly, horribly, some part of her was *relieved.*

That was the worst part. Not fear. Relief.

"I need to check the goats," she said, which was a lie, because she owned no goats, and walked out the back door into the cold.

* * *

The valley at night was a bowl of stars. Edmund stood in her yard breathing white breath and tried to reason with herself the way she reasoned with frightened animals: slow movements, low voice, no sudden demands.

Here were the facts, laid out like tools on a bench. One: she had run from her mistress because the woman wanted to sell her gift — her *hearing*, her awful inherited knack for hearing things that spoke — to agents of the crown. Two: those agents had come once before, when Edmund was sixteen, and her mistress had hidden her in a root cellar and lied beautifully, and Edmund had repaid her by running three counties west and never writing. Three: the crown was now, according to Maren, hollowed out and wearing a devouring thing, and it fed on stolen prayers, and it had been hunting the one stone in Aldermarch that still answered honestly.

Four: the stone knew her name.

She looked up at the ridgeline, a black serration against the stars, and felt the old familiar comfort of it. Beyond that line was everything she'd fled. On this side was safety, if safety could be called a life spent mending other people's pots while pretending she had no ears for anything but human voices.

"Penny for them."

Pip materialized at her elbow the way she always did, silent as spilled flour. Edmund startled. "Gods' teeth, child, make noise sometime."

"They say you shouldn't," Pip said seriously, meaning the empty air beside her, and nodded at whatever it said back. Then, to Edmund: "They want to know why you smell afraid."

"I'm not afraid."

"You smell like the goose before market day." Pip considered her with unsettling directness. "The lady knight says we're leaving tonight. She told the walls, so I heard."

"She told the *walls?*"

"People tell walls everything. Walls are very good listeners." Pip shrugged. "Are we going over the ridge?"

"I haven't decided."

"You have. You decided when you made her the poultice. You only make the strong comfrey for people you're keeping alive on purpose." Pip said it simply, without triumph, the way children state weather. "Besides. If we stay, the bad thing finds us. If we go, maybe it doesn't. Those aren't hard sums."

Edmund stared at her. "How old are you?"

"Dunno. Old enough for sums." Pip scuffed her boot in the frost. Then quieter: "Can I come? When you go. If you go." For the first time the girl looked less than certain, and Edmund saw under the strange talk to the actual child beneath — the one who'd been passed hand to hand through the valley since her parents died, tolerated everywhere, kept nowhere. "Nobody takes me anywhere. They feed me and they put up with the talking, but nobody *takes* me."

And there it was. The hook, sunk clean, in the exact soft place Edmund had spent seven years protecting.

"If we go," Edmund said, hearing her own doom in the words and saying them anyway, "it's dangerous. People are hunting us. You'd be walking toward the thing you should walk away from."

"I talk to things that aren't there," Pip said. "Dangerous is where my friends live."

* * *

They left at moonset, three figures and one glowing stone wrapped in three layers of wool, moving up the shepherd's track along the western flank of the valley. Edmund had chosen the route the way she chose everything — carefully, cowardly, she told herself — a path that avoided the village entirely, though she found herself memorizing rooftops anyway. Tilda's chimney. The mill. The widow Anthea's crooked fence. Seven years of small kindnesses traded back and forth, and she was leaving in the night without a word, and the shame of it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"You could wake them," Maren offered, reading her silence as easily as everyone in this cursed story seemed to read her. "Say goodbye properly."

"And tell them what? 'Sorry, Anthea, the crown's been eaten by a devil and I'm the reason prayers work again, mind the hens.'" Edmund climbed on. "People sleep better not knowing."

"Some do," Maren said. "Some just sleep worse alone and call it not knowing."

The track narrowed. Frost glazed the stones. Pip ranged ahead like a creature native to darkness, chattering softly to her invisible companions, occasionally relaying intelligence Edmund couldn't verify and therefore chose to trust: *"Bramble says the wind shifts at the notch. He's a wind spirit, he knows wind."*

"There are spirits *here?*" Edmund said sharply. "In the valley?"

"Not here-here." Pip waved a vague hand. "They wait up top. Past the line. They've been waiting since you came down, they said. Well—" she glanced sideways, gap-toothed and guileless, "—since before you came down. But they got interested lately. Interested in you."

Edmund's mouth went dry. She climbed faster.

An hour before dawn they reached the notch — a saddle in the ridgeline where the mountain had, ages ago, shrugged off a shoulder of stone, leaving a pass barely wide enough for two. The valley lay below them, silvered and sleeping. Beyond the notch, the land fell away into the wide dark of Aldermarch: forests, roads, villages, a capital, a throne with something hungry sitting on it.

Maren stopped, one hand pressed to her bandage, breathing hard. "Rest here. Once we cross, I can't promise another safe pause."

Edmund stood at the line. She could *see* it — not with eyes, exactly, but with the sense she'd spent seven years strangling. The valley's silence ended at the notch like a shoreline. Beyond it, the world was loud. Not sound-loud. Presence-loud. A vast murmuring crowd pressing up against a door.

Her door.

"Last chance," she said, mostly to herself. "Walk back down. Mend pots. Live to eighty."

"You'd die at forty of boredom and we both know it," Maren said.

"The stone's humming louder," Pip reported helpfully.

Edmund closed her eyes. Thought of her mother, whom she barely remembered. Thought of her mistress, whom she remembered too well — the kind hands and the calculating eyes, and the day Edmund realized love and price tags could live in the same house. Thought of seven years of deliberate smallness, a life shrunk to fit inside a silence.

Then she stepped over the line.

Magic hit her like cold water.

There was no gentler word for it. It poured in — through her skin, her ears, the place behind her sternum where she'd kept a door shut so long she'd grown scar tissue over the hinges — and it was *everywhere*, and it was *loud*. Every hedge woke. Every stream muttered. The stones under her feet hummed with deep slow thoughts, and the wind arrived full of names, and above the pass something enormous and patient and *old* turned its attention toward her the way a lighthouse turns, slow and inevitable, a beam sweeping across the dark and finding —

Finding *her.*

"Oh," Edmund gasped, and staggered, and Maren caught her arm. "Oh, that's — they're *loud* —"

"You crossed before," Maren said, holding her upright. "When you came to the valley. Was it like this?"

"No." Edmund laughed, a wild unsteady sound she didn't recognize as her own voice. "Before I was small. I made myself small, I folded everything down and crept past like a thief. This time I just — walked in. Like I own the door." Her heart was slamming. Terror, yes, terror absolutely, every nerve screaming *visible, visible, VISIBLE* — but threaded through it, shamefully, gorgeously, that same relief. Seven years of holding her breath and now the breath tore out of her and she was *breathing*. "They're noticing me. All of them. The little ones first, then — oh, there's something big, something really old, it's —"

"*Interested,*" Pip finished, delighted. "Told you."

The prayer-stone, unwrapped now in Edmund's grip, blazed like a captured star. And out of its light came a voice — not the twisted, honeyed cruelty Maren had described from the far side of Aldermarch, but something raw and young and desperate, a voice like the miller's dog:

**Edmund Ravensworth. God-speaker. Daughter of the ledger-keeper. We heard you were dead.**

"I'm not dead," Edmund said aloud, to a stone, on a mountaintop, in front of witnesses, and discovered she meant it more than she had in years.

**No,** the voice agreed, and there was something in it that might have been hope, or hunger, or both. **But something has been eating our prayers, and eating our small kin, and wearing the king like a coat. And you are the first god-speaker to cross this line in eleven years. So we are going to ask you — politely, while we still remember politeness —**

The light in the stone guttered. The voice frayed like cloth tearing.

**— to hurry.**

Below them, far off in the dark of the valley they'd just abandoned, a dog began to bark. Then another. Then torchlight, moving along the lane toward Edmund's cottage — several torches, riding fast, arriving at the home of a woman who had packed nothing she couldn't carry at a run precisely because she'd always known this morning would come.

Maren drew her sword with her good arm. "Riders. Four, maybe five. They'll find the cottage empty and read the trail by sunup."

"By sunup," Edmund repeated. Her hands were shaking. Her blood was singing. She was terrified down to the marrow and she had never in her life felt more awake. She looked at the stone, at the road beyond the notch winding down into a kingdom that ate prayers and kings alike, at Pip's upturned face and Maren's grim one.

"Well," she said, stepping forward onto the far slope, into the loud bright terrifying world. "We'd better hurry, then."

Behind them, unnoticed by any of the three, the notch itself seemed to exhale — and high on the ridgeline, something vast settled in to watch them descend, the way the old things always watched a Ravensworth walking toward trouble.
