## 4. The Hollow King's Road

Ser Maren had sworn oaths to two kings in her life, and she had broken both, and she had learned that the road was the only confessor that never lied back to you. She rode at the front of their little procession — three horses, one of them stolen, all of them tired — and she did not look back at the ridgeline where the valley sat folded into its own forgetting.

The girl Edmund rode behind her like a woman being dragged to her own execution. Which, Maren thought grimly, wasn't far from the truth of it. She'd seen that look before, on recruits the morning before their first battle: the particular stillness of someone cataloguing everything they might lose.

"You could turn around," Maren said, not turning herself. "No one's holding a knife to your back. I told you what waits over that ridge. You chose."

"I know what I chose." Edmund's voice was flat as slate. "I'm deciding whether to regret it yet. Give me a few miles."

Behind them, Pip laughed from atop the packhorse — a bright, unguarded sound that made both older women flinch, though for different reasons. The child had been laughing most of the morning. She'd also spent half the night talking to the empty air beside the fire, and Maren had lain awake listening to it and telling herself the cold had gotten into her ears.

"Brann says you're going too fast," Pip announced.

Maren reined in despite herself. "Who is Brann?"

"My friend." Pip kicked her heels against the packhorse's barrel, an instruction the horse ignored with dignity. "He walks. He can't ride. He says horses are too big to trust."

"There is no one named Brann," Maren said.

"He's right there." Pip pointed at the space beside Maren's stirrup. Empty frost-browned grass. A crow lifted off a fencepost some twenty yards off, unbothered.

Edmund had gone very quiet. When Maren glanced back, she saw the hedge-witch's apprentice — runaway, whatever she was — staring at that empty patch of ground with an expression Maren couldn't name. Not fear. Something closer to recognition, or grief.

*She sees something,* Maren realized. *Gods below, she hears them too.*

* * *

They stopped at midday in the lee of a fallen boundary wall, old work, pre-kingdom maybe, the stones furred with moss that should have been dead by this season. Maren built no fire — smoke drew eyes on the king's road — and shared out hard bread and harder cheese, and watched Edmund feed strips of bacon rind to nothing.

"To Brann," Edmund said quietly, dropping the last piece onto the grass.

Pip beamed. The rind vanished. Maren decided, with the weariness of a woman who had already lost everything worth losing, not to ask how.

"All right," she said. She wiped her knife on her knee and sheathed it. "You carried a glowing stone across a mountain because a stranger asked nicely. Before we go another mile, you're owed the whole of it. And after I tell you, if either of you wants to walk away — back to the valley, down to the coast, anywhere — I won't stop you. I'll probably thank you."

"Story," Pip said, settling cross-legged with her chin in her hands. "Brann likes stories."

"Then Brann can listen too," Maren said, and was rewarded with a small silence in which she felt — absurdly — observed.

She looked out at the road, brown and rutted, running east toward everything.

"The king's name is Aldous," she began. "Third of that name. I held his shield at his coronation when I was nineteen years old. I stood in the chapel of the Spire while they poured the oil, and I heard him swear to Aldermarch with his hand on the old stones. He was not a great man. He was vain, and slow to anger in the way of men who have never needed to be quick. But he was *present*. Do you understand? He was there, inside himself. When he looked at you, someone was looking back."

Edmund had stopped chewing.

"Eight months ago," Maren said, "the king stopped speaking."

"Not died?" Edmund asked carefully.

"Not died. That's the horror of it. He eats. He sleeps. He rides through the capital in state twice a week, and he sits his horse beautifully, and when the crowds cheer him, he turns his head toward the sound like a man listening to weather. His physicians call it a wasting of the spirit. Grief, they say — his son died the spring before, the fever took him, and the king's tongue went with his heart. A pious story. Convenient." Her jaw tightened. "I served in the inner guard until four months ago. I saw him up close, every day. And I will tell you what no physician will print: there is nothing behind his eyes. No grief. No sleep. No man. Whatever wears King Aldous now, it came in through his mouth and hollowed him out like a gourd, and it has been wearing him ever since."

Pip had gone still too, chin still in her hands, but her eyes were wide and fixed on Maren with terrible attention.

"And the regent?" Edmund asked.

"Lady Vessia. The queen dowager's sister, appointed when the king's grief made him unfit — which, mark this, was *before* his tongue failed. She rules through him. Every decree passes his lips in that voice that doesn't breathe properly. And here's the part I need you to hear, because it's why I crossed mountains to find a valley full of people who want nothing to do with any of us." Maren leaned forward. "The prayers are being answered."

"What prayers?"

"All of them. Across Aldermarch. Farmers pray for rain and get rain — too much, rivers out of their banks, fields drowned. Villages pray for an end to bandits and the bandits are found dead in ditches, every one, throats opened, including two boys of fourteen riding with them under duress. A town prays for a good harvest and gets a harvest so heavy the granaries burst their doors and half of it rots in the streets because there aren't carts enough in the county to move it." She let that sit. "Every prayer answered. Quickly. Efficiently. Precisely as asked and never as meant. Do you see? That's not a god answering. Gods are careless the way parents are careless; they answer with love and poor aim. This thing answers like a ledger. Like a machine built to consume requests and spit out results."

"It's eating the prayers," Edmund said softly. It wasn't a question.

"Eating them, or feeding on them — I don't know the mechanics and I don't want to. What I know is the shape of it. The kingdom prayed for order after the plague years. Vessia promised order. And something answered that promise by putting on a crown." Maren looked at her hands. They were steady. She was proud of them for that. "I reported what I saw to my captain. My captain vanished within the week. Two other guards who spoke of it — gone. I ran before I joined them. I've been running since, gathering what I could, looking for anyone who might know what could answer prayers wrong and how to make it stop. And every wise woman and hedge-priest I found said the same word before I even finished asking."

Edmund's face had drained of color. "God-speakers."

"God-speakers." Maren nodded slowly. "A bloodline that could hear the gods plainly and speak back. Hunted near to nothing a generation ago — burned, drowned, driven into hiding. Your mistress, I think, was one of the last who kept the old knowledge, even watered down. And you—" she gestured at Edmund with her chin, "—you heard the stone calling a name you buried. Don't insult me by denying it. I've watched you listen to things on this road since dawn. The wind changes and you tilt your head like a hound."

For a long moment Edmund said nothing. Then, quietly: "If I'm what you need, then whoever hunts Ser Maren will hunt me next. You knew that when you came over the ridge."

"Yes."

"And you brought me anyway."

"Yes." Maren met her eyes without apology. "Because the alternative was watching a kingdom eat itself, and because I am a coward about exactly one thing, which is dying knowing I didn't try. I'd rather be honest about my sins than pretend they're virtues."

"That's not honesty," Edmund said. "That's just a different kind of selfishness."

"Probably." Maren almost smiled. "Welcome to the company of the damned. We travel light."

* * *

They made nine more miles before dusk caught them in a stretch of country where the hedgerows grew wild and tall and the farms had been abandoned long enough that the orchards had gone feral. Maren chose a ruined tithe-barn off the road, walls mostly standing, roof half-collapsed into a nest of rooks. She set wards — chalk marks, salt, the small superstitious geometry of a soldier rather than a witch — and got a fire going small enough to hide in the barn's deep corner.

It was Pip, again, who broke the day open.

She'd been quiet through the afternoon ride — quieter than Maren liked; the child's chatter had been the only proof the world hadn't entirely curdled — and now she sat close to the fire with her knees pulled up, watching the flames, and said, without preamble:

"They're starving."

Maren looked up. "Who?"

"My friends." Pip's voice was different. Older. Careful, the way a child is careful when they've finally understood that adults might not believe them and have decided to try anyway. "Brann. And Sefa, who smells like bread. And the lady with the lantern who only comes when it rains. There used to be lots of them. Everywhere we walked, there were always some. In every village, every shrine, every little house with the shelf above the door." She hugged her knees tighter. "Now I can't hardly find any. And the ones left are so thin. Brann says he hasn't eaten in — he doesn't count time the way we do, but he says it's been a very long hunger. Since the king went quiet, he thinks."

Edmund had gone white to the lips. "Pip," she said, very gently, "when you say they're thin—"

"I can see through Sefa now," Pip said simply. "I couldn't before. Before, she was solid, like a person, just smaller and warm. Now when she holds my hand I can see the fire through her fingers." Her chin trembled once and steadied, because Pip was the sort of child who had learned early that trembling cost you. "Brann says the crown-man is eating them. All of them. All the little ones first, because the little ones live closest to people, in the doorways and the hearths, and the crown-man reaches into everywhere people pray."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the roof, the rooks muttered.

"They're not imaginary," Edmund said. It was barely a whisper. She turned to Maren, and her eyes were wet and furious at once. "They're not — she's not mad. She never was. They're *real*, they're the minor gods, the hearth-gods, the threshold-wardens, the ones nobody's prayed to properly in a hundred years because everyone prays to the crown and the Spire now — and this thing on the throne is eating what's left of them."

"I know," Maren said.

"You *know*?" Edmund's voice cracked upward. "You've known this whole time and you let her talk to empty air and—"

"I let her talk to her friends," Maren said steadily, "because a child who talks to gods needs one person who doesn't flinch. Yes, I suspected. From the first night. The way she'd pause, like she was waiting for an answer. The way the fire leaned toward her when she laughed." She shrugged. "I've been alone with this truth for eight months, Edmund. Watching a child confirm it broke something loose in me I haven't sorted yet. Forgive me for wanting one evening where somebody at this fire was just a girl."

Edmund stared at her. Then she laughed — a short, cracked, startled sound — and put her face in her hands. "Gods' teeth. I fled a valley where nothing magical could reach me so I wouldn't have to feel like this ever again, and three days later I'm sitting in a barn arguing about theology."

"Welcome to the company of the damned," Maren repeated.

Pip looked between them, sensing the shift without understanding it, and did what children do with such moments: she drove straight into the center of it. "Will Brann die?" she asked. "Like Sefa's sister died? She winked out in autumn and Brann cried, only gods don't cry like us, it sounded like wind in a chimney."

And there it was — the question Maren had been carrying like a stone in her boot since the valley, since the prayer-stone, since the ridge. She looked at Edmund, because Edmund was the god-speaker, Edmund was the bloodline, Edmund was the one whose gift had been hunted and sold and buried, and Maren was only a broken knight with a sword and a story.

But Edmund surprised her. She scooted across the straw until she sat beside Pip, shoulder to shoulder, and took the child's hand.

"I don't know," Edmund said honestly. "But I know someone who might. Tomorrow, on this road, there's a shrine. A roadside hearth-shrine — my mistress's notes mentioned it, one of the last tended ones in the marchlands. If any of your friends are left between here and the capital, they'll shelter somewhere like that." She squeezed Pip's hand. "And if the crown-thing is eating the small gods, then the small gods have reason to help us kill it. We're not walking to Aldermarch to save a king, Pip. We're walking to save your friends. The king's just where the eating happens."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she nodded, once, decisively, and lay down against the pack-saddle with the boneless ease of the young, and was asleep within minutes.

Maren banked the fire and sat watching the girl sleep and the woman watch over her, and felt the unfamiliar weight of something she'd refused to carry for four months.

Trust. Or the first raw edge of it. Three broken travelers and a ruined kingdom, and she had just handed her truth to two strangers and watched them hold it without dropping it. It terrified her more than the riders on their trail. Riders, you could fight.

"Get some sleep," she told Edmund. "I'll take first watch."

Edmund didn't move at once. She was looking east, toward the dark beyond the broken doorway, where far off — impossibly far off — the lights of the capital would sit like a ring of swallowed stars. When she spoke, her voice was so low Maren nearly missed it.

"Maren. On the road today. When Pip pointed at Brann." She hesitated. "There was a moment — just a breath — where I could have *seen* him. Felt him, like warmth off a stone. It's coming back. Being over the ridge. It's coming back faster than I want it to."

"Good," Maren said. "We'll need it."

"No," Edmund said. "You don't understand. Things notice you when the gift wakes. My mistress told me — the louder you are to the gods, the louder you are to everything that hunts them." She finally looked at Maren, and in the firelight her eyes were old and young at once. "Whatever's wearing the king. If it eats god-speakers' voices — if it took the throne because someone like me said yes to it once—"

Maren went cold. "Why would you think that?"

"Because the prayer-stone called me by name," Edmund said. "Not my valley-name. My true one. The one my mother gave me. And it said—" she swallowed, "—it said *welcome home*."

Outside, the wind rose along the hedgerows, and in the roof the rooks went suddenly, completely silent — every bird at once, as though something enormous had paused to listen.
