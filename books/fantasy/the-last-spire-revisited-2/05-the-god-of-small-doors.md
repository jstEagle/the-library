## 5. The God of Small Doors

The shrine appeared where no shrine should be.

They had walked two days since the ridge — two days of Maren setting a pace that treated Edmund's legs like a personal insult, Pip riding on the knight's shoulders because her own were too small for the road, and the world humming with a magic Edmund was still learning to ignore. She had spent six years in silence. Now every hedgerow whispered, every stream sang a little off-key, and twice she'd caught herself answering a crow before remembering that crows did not usually mean what they said.

So when the road bent around a hillside of grey stone and she saw the little door set into the rock — knee-high, painted blue, its paint flaking like old skin — she stopped so abruptly that Maren nearly walked through her.

"What is it?" Maren's hand went to her sword on reflex. Everything made the knight reach for that sword now. It was becoming a habit Edmund found both exhausting and oddly comforting.

"That." Edmund pointed.

Maren squinted. "It's a rabbit hole."

"It's a *door*."

"A very small door."

"For a very small god," Pip said, sliding down from Maren's shoulders with the boneless ease of a cat. The girl crouched in front of the blue door, cocking her head the way she did when listening to things that weren't there — except Edmund knew better now, God help her, they were there. "He's shy," Pip reported. "But he says we look frozen and stupid and we can come in if we don't step on his floor."

"It's a hole in a hill," Maren said flatly.

"He's very proud of the floor."

Edmund knelt beside Pip. Up close, the door resolved itself: not a hole but a proper little door with a hinge of green copper and a latch shaped like an acorn, and above it, carved shallow into the stone, a mark she almost recognized — a spiral, or a hearth flame drawn by someone who'd only ever seen fire described. Old work. Older than the road, older than the kingdom probably.

"Hello?" she said, feeling ridiculous, and then not ridiculous at all when something behind the door exhaled — warm air, smelling of woodsmoke and bread crusts and clean ash, the smell of every kitchen she'd ever loved.

*In,* said a voice like a banked fire settling. Not heard so much as felt, low in the chest. *Before your teeth start chattering. It's undignified, watching travelers shake.*

Edmund looked at Maren. Maren looked at Edmund. Pip was already crawling toward the door, which stretched — Edmund's eyes insisted otherwise, but the door stretched, widening as the girl approached until it swallowed her whole, girl and all, without disturbing one pebble of the hillside.

"I hate this country," Maren announced to no one, and crawled in after her.

* * *

Inside was impossible, and Edmund had stopped apologizing to herself for impossibles somewhere around the second night after the ridge.

The hill's interior opened into a single round room the size of a modest cottage, though from outside the hill couldn't have held a goat. A hearth occupied the entire far wall, burning low and orange, and the firelight did strange courteous things — it reached every corner without casting shadows, warmed every surface without scorching the rushes on the floor. There was a table. Three mismatched stools. A kettle already singing.

And the god himself, sitting on the hearthstone with his feet in the coals like a man soaking tired feet in cool water: a small figure, child-sized, made mostly of suggestion. Grey hair like cinders. An apron gone soft with use. A face you forgot the moment you looked away, the way you forget the exact arrangement of your own kitchen — except that Edmund didn't forget, and she suspected that was the point.

"You see me clearly," the hearth-god said. His voice came from everywhere and nowhere and mostly from the fire. "That's rare, these days. Sit down, god-daughter."

"I'm not—" Edmund stopped. Her hands had gone cold despite the warmth. "What did you call me?"

"Sit," he repeated, gently, "and I'll explain why I know that word, and why it frightens us both."

She sat. Maren sat too, sword across her knees, watching the god the way she watched everything — like it might be a trap laid specifically for her. Pip curled up against the hearth-god's side as naturally as if she'd known him all her life, and he put one small arm around her without looking, the way a parent does a hundred times a day without thinking.

"Tea?" he asked.

"Yes please," said Pip.

"Please," said Edmund, because her mother had raised her right, whoever her mother had turned out to be.

* * *

His name, he said, was Osk-with-the-Door. He had been, in the long ago, the god of thresholds and back entrances and the particular safety of a room where someone expected you. Every farmhouse had kept a bit of his fire. Every servant girl slipping home late through the scullery had walked under his protection without knowing his name. He had never been grand. Grand gods got temples; he got butter rubbed on a lintel and a crumb swept into the corner at year's end, and he had been content with that for four thousand years.

"And then eight months ago," he said, poking the coals with a poker taller than he was, "the prayers started going wrong."

Maren leaned forward. "Wrong how?"

"You've seen it. Everyone's seen it. A woman prays for rain for her crops and gets a flood that drowns her oxen. A man prays his feverish son will sleep and the boy doesn't wake. Prayers used to be *requests*, knight. You set them at a god's door like a note, and the god weighed them against ten thousand other notes and answered what it could, how it could, slowly and imperfectly, like everything honest. Now something answers them *immediately*. Perfectly. Literally." He spat into the fire, and the fire flinched. "Nothing that grants wishes literally is good. Any god will tell you that. Wishes are made by frightened people, and frightened people wish carelessly."

"The thing on the throne," Edmund said quietly. "Ser Maren told me. Something wearing the king."

"Wearing him? Oh, worse than wearing." Osk's coal-grey eyes fixed on her. "It *eats*. That's what it does, girl. It eats prayer, and prayer is just worship with the manners worn off, and worship is just — " he waved a hand — "attention. Love, aimed. And it has grown fat on a kingdom's worth of love aimed at a king who cannot even blink anymore. But prayer alone is thin broth. It wants richer meat."

"What kind of meat?"

"Gods."

The fire cracked. Outside, wind moved along the hilltop, and Edmund thought of the whispering hedges, the off-key streams, and wondered suddenly how many voices she'd passed in two days that had been voices, actual voices, calling out to anyone who might listen.

"The little ones go first," Osk said. "We're easier to catch. A river god needs her river; corner her at the source and she's done. A field god starves when the field freezes. Me—" he patted his hill fondly — "I'm stubborn and small and my door opens onto a road nobody travels since the ferry bridge washed out. So far, I'm overlooked. But I've watched three shrines on this stretch go dark since midsummer. Just — dark. No dying, no fading. *Taken.* And whatever takes them is getting closer to the valley, Edmund Ravensworth."

Her name in his mouth landed like a dropped stone.

"How do you know my name?"

"Because I knew your grandmother," said the god of small doors. "Slightly. We shared a few roads. And because your mother prayed here once, at this exact hearth, when she was younger than you are now, and left an offering, and I remember everyone who leaves an offering in my house for as long as the house stands." He leaned forward, firelight pooling in the hollows of his face. "Wren Ravensworth. God-speaker of the old line. Do you want to tell me why her daughter has been hiding in the silent valley pretending to be a pot-mender named Edith?"

* * *

Edmund discovered she was gripping her stool's edge hard enough to hurt her fingers.

"I don't talk about my mother."

"No," Osk agreed. "You don't. You don't talk about the gift either, though you've been hearing us since the ridgeline and doing an admirable job of pretending each voice was the wind. Here's what I'll give you instead, free, because you're freezing and your friend looks ready to fight me and the child is asleep on my shoulder, and those are the three kinds of guests I like best." He counted them off on coal-dark fingers. "One. Your bloodline isn't a curse and it isn't a talent. It's a *door*, same as mine. God-speakers are people born with the door standing open — the gods can speak to you uninvited, and you can speak back. There were once thousands of you. Priestesses of the river-kings, hearth-tenders, oath-makers. Kingdoms were built on your family's ability to say a god's name correctly."

"And then?" Maren asked, though her voice suggested she already dreaded the answer.

"And then people realized that a person who can open a door for a god," Osk said softly, "can also open a door for something that only *looks* like a god. Or close a door on one. Or sell the key." His eyes came back to Edmund. "Your line was hunted, girl. Not by mobs — mobs are honest. By courts. By kings who wanted god-speakers leashed, and later by kings who wanted them extinct, because an extinct gift can't contradict a crown. The last few dozen scattered. Some ran. Some married into silence and let their children grow up deaf. And some—"

"Some were taken," Edmund whispered. "For the crown."

"For the crown," Osk agreed. "Which brings me to two. The thing on the throne knows exactly what your blood is worth. It ate the king's court first because a court prays loudest. But what it truly hungers for — what it has been *searching* for these eight months, sending its soldiers down every road — is a god-speaker. Because there's one thing it cannot do alone. One lock it cannot pick."

Edmund's mouth had gone dry. "Which is?"

"A bargain freely given can only be unmade freely given. Whatever binding sits on that poor dead-eyed king, someone consented to it once — consented in the old words, in the old way, and consent given by a true god-speaker binds tighter than iron and lasts longer than kingdoms. The crown-thing can't undo its own leash-magic. Only the blood that made it can." He paused. "Or the blood that inherits it."

The kettle screamed. Nobody moved to take it off the fire.

"My mother," Edmund said. Each word cost something. "You're saying my mother—"

"I'm saying," Osk said, gentle as banked embers, "that the thing on the throne knows the name Wren Ravensworth. Says it sometimes, they tell me, to its servants. Like a man saying the name of a meal he remembers fondly. And I am telling you, god-daughter, with four thousand years of hospitality behind me and no reason left to lie: it is not looking for your mother. Your mother is twelve years dead and beyond anyone's hunger." He reached over and, with great tenderness, lifted the screaming kettle off the flames. "It is looking for what your mother left behind."

* * *

Pip woke halfway through the tea and demanded to know everything, so Osk told her a softened version involving rabbits, and she accepted it with the serene skepticism of children everywhere and fell asleep again.

Later, when the fire had burned to a thoughtful red and Maren stood watch by the impossible door out of pure habit, Osk caught Edmund's sleeve as she gathered her pack.

"A last thing. Free advice from a small god, which is the only kind that's ever worth anything." His grip was surprisingly strong, warm as a stove-top. "You're thinking of running back to the valley. Don't. The silence there protects you from us, girl, but it won't protect you from *it* — the crown-thing's reach crosses ridgelines fine. Soldiers follow roads, and roads follow food, and winter is coming early this year. Wrong-early. When the snow starts falling out of season, every hungry soul in Aldermarch will be moving, and some of them will be carrying orders with your description on them."

"Then what am I supposed to do?"

"Keep walking east. Toward the capital, God help you — and I would help you if helping were in my power, but I'm a hearth, not a sword." He hesitated, and for a moment the ancient small god looked simply old, and tired, and afraid. "There's an outlaw company in the Ashwood, two days on. Brannoc's people. They took in a deserter or two of mine — well. Of my *priesthood*, if a woman who leaves bread on a windowsill counts as clergy, and I say it does. Find them. A door that stands open is safest when it's surrounded by friends."

"Why are you helping me?" Edmund asked. "You don't know me."

Osk smiled, and for one instant Edmund saw him as he must have looked four thousand years ago: bright, and young, and the warm center of ten thousand kitchens.

"I knew your mother," he said. "She left an offering and a promise, and I keep both as long as my fire burns. Now go, before dawn. And Edmund — " the door was already stretching open onto grey morning light — "when you get where you're going, ask the big ones what happened to the god-speakers' covenant. Ask them what your family *promised*. They'll pretend they've forgotten. Make them remember."

She stepped out into the cold. Behind her, the hillside showed nothing but stone and lichen and a rabbit-sized hole, and the wind coming down off the northern hills smelled — wrong, faintly, underneath the clean frost — of smoke, and of snow arriving months before its time.

Two days to the Ashwood. Edmund pulled her collar up and started walking, and did not look back, and did not manage, no matter how she tried, to stop hearing the word *covenant* echoing in the open door of her skull.
