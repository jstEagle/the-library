## 6. Outlaws of the Ashwood

Pip counted her gods the way other children counted coins. There was Wren-of-the-Windowsill, who lived in every drafty gap and smelled like rain on dust. There was Old Tally, a road-god with no feet who traveled by leaning — Pip had seen him lean all the way from the valley gate to the first milestone without once touching ground. And there was the small nameless one she'd found in Ser Maren's saddlebag, curled around the prayer-stone like a cat around a warm loaf, humming when Pip hummed.

That one didn't have a name yet. Pip was working on it.

"You're doing it again," Edmund said.

"Doing what?"

"Talking to nothing."

"He's not nothing," Pip said, offended on behalf of everything. "He's shy. You'd be shy too if everyone kept calling you nothing."

Edmund gave her the look that wasn't really cross, just tired in a deep-down way, like someone carrying water up a very long stair. She'd been wearing that look since the ridge. Pip understood why, sort of. Crossing over had changed something in Edmund — you could see it in how she flinched from shadows now, and how the shadows flinched back.

Maren rode ahead, half-turned in the saddle, watching the road behind them the way a hare watches a hawk. Three days since the soldiers came to the valley. Two days since they'd burned the ferry behind them and cut across country that no sensible person crossed in autumn. The Ashwood rose ahead of them now, black-branched and fog-wrapped, and even Pip's gods got quiet near its edge.

Except the little one. He pressed close against her collarbone, cold as a coin left out overnight.

"They're scared," she told the others. "The trees are eating the prayers."

"Trees don't eat prayers," Edmund said.

"These do. These eat *everything*." Pip said it cheerfully, because saying scary things cheerfully made them smaller. "That's why nobody lives here. It's not bandits. Well — it is bandits. But the bandits picked this wood because the wood already did the scaring for them."

Maren's hand went to her sword. "How far to the far side?"

"A day," Pip said. "Maybe less if we're eaten quickly."

Nobody laughed. They never laughed at her good ones.

* * *

The arrows came an hour past noon, when the fog had thickened until Pip couldn't see Maren ten feet ahead, only hear the creak of her armor and the little god's frightened hum against her chest.

"Halt." The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, which Pip respected enormously. "Three riders. One armed. Drop what you carry and state your business."

"We carry a prayer-stone," Maren said, and Pip felt the whole forest go still around them, the way a room goes still when someone says a dead man's name.

Rope dropped out of the fog. Hands pulled them down. Pip bit one of the hands, mostly on principle, and got shaken gently by the scruff like a kitten, which she resented more than the rope.

They were marched — dragged, in Edmund's case, who fought like a wet cat the entire way — into a clearing where the fog thinned and firelight showed her the outlaws of the Ashwood.

There were more than she expected. Thirty, maybe forty, camped among the roots of a tree so old and wide it had its own weather. Lean-tos of hide and canvas. A cookfire big enough to roast a pig. Children — actual children, two of them, chasing each other between the tents — and an old woman mending a shirt, and men with swords who watched the newcomers the way wolves watch weather.

And at the center, sharpening a knife with terrible patience, sat the biggest man Pip had ever seen. Not tall so much as *built*, like something carved rather than grown, with a beard going gray at the edges and a scar that split his eyebrow and kept going, as though whoever made it ran out of face halfway through.

"That's Brannoc," whispered one of the children, appearing at Pip's elbow as if summoned. "He killed eleven men in the king's own hall."

"He did not," said the old woman, not looking up from her shirt. "He broke three arms and apologized to two of them."

Brannoc stood. Up close he was worse — or better, depending. He looked at Maren first, and his eyes did a strange thing: recognition, then grief, then something carefully blank.

"Ser Maren Vale," he said. "Sworn shield to Lady Vessia. I carried your standard at Coldford."

"I don't carry standards anymore," Maren said.

"No. I heard you fell."

"I fell a long way."

Brannoc huffed — almost a laugh, almost not. Then he looked at Edmund, and Pip saw him do the thing adults did, cataloguing her like goods at market. Hedge-witch. Runaway. Scared. Then his gaze snagged on the prayer-stone's outline under Edmund's cloak, and the careful blankness cracked straight down the middle.

"Where did you get that?"

"It was given to me," Edmund said. "By a dying man who said prayers were being answered wrong."

The clearing went quiet. Even the fire seemed to listen.

"Wrong how?" Brannoc asked.

"People pray for rain and get floods. Pray for their sick mother to be eased and she dies smiling before morning. Pray for a good harvest and the grain comes in heavy while the farmers drop dead in the fields." Edmund's voice shook, but she kept it level. "Every prayer in Aldermarch is being answered. Cruelly. Efficiently. Like something reading them for instructions."

Brannoc sat back down slowly, the knife forgotten. Around the fire, the outlaws had stopped pretending not to listen.

"The king stopped speaking eight months ago," he said. "And everything started getting *answered*. I thought it was coincidence. I thought—" He stopped. Rubbed his face with a hand like a shovel. "Right. You three are either the most dangerous people in Aldermarch or the most doomed, and either way I want no part of—"

"Pip can talk to gods," Pip announced.

"*Pip*," said Edmund, mortified.

"What? You were taking too long."

Brannoc looked at her. Pip looked back, and smiled the smile that had gotten her fed in four counties and thrown out of two.

"How many?" he asked quietly.

"Just the small ones," Pip said. "The big ones sleep. Or they're hiding. Mostly hiding lately. This one—" she fished the little god out of her collar, held him cupped in her palms where he glowed faintly, a warmth like a candle seen through a curtain, "—came with us from the valley. He doesn't have a name yet. He likes bread crusts and being hummed at."

The old woman set down her mending. The children crept closer. And one by one, the outlaws of the Ashwood leaned toward the light in a child's cupped hands the way they'd lean toward a fire in winter — hungry, hopeful, and afraid to hope.

"Well," Brannoc said at last, to nobody, to everybody. "Damn me. It's been a long time since anything prayed-to looked back."

* * *

They stayed the night, because the fog wouldn't lift and the forest ate stragglers, and because Brannoc said the word *stay* in a voice that made it sound like a question he badly wanted answered yes.

Pip worked the camp like a market square. She learned names fast — Dodger, who was called Dodger because he'd once dodged a tax collector for a full year; Marta-the-Mender, whose shirts were the best-mended in three shires and who let Pip help, which nobody ever did; twins named Fern and Fox who taught her the camp's whistle-language in under an hour because Pip collected languages the way magpies collected shine.

She learned the shape of the place too. How the sentries walked the perimeter in pairs, never alone. How the children knew which root-cellar was the bolt-hole. How everyone glanced at the sky when they spoke the word *king*, as though the word itself might be listening.

Because it might be. That was the thing nobody said plainly, but Pip heard it anyway, in the gaps between sentences. Something wore the crown now. Something listened through it.

At supper she sat herself down next to Brannoc, who was pretending not to be watching Edmund across the fire.

"Why did you leave?" Pip asked. Direct questions worked better than polite ones. Adults hid things behind politeness like squirrels hiding nuts.

Brannoc's jaw worked. For a moment she thought he wouldn't answer.

"There was a night," he said finally, "in the castle chapel. I was on door duty. I heard singing that wasn't singing, and then the king screamed, and then he stopped screaming, and then there was silence like the inside of a bell. And the queen regent walked out smiling. Smiling like a woman who'd paid off a debt." His hands closed around nothing. "I asked questions. Men who asked questions started disappearing. So I disappeared first. Took anyone who'd come with me."

"Do you miss it? Guarding?"

"I miss believing the thing I guarded was worth guarding." He looked at her sidelong. "You ask sharp questions for a small person."

"I'm not small," Pip said. "I'm concentrated."

That got a real laugh out of him, short and rusty, like a gate opening after years shut. Across the fire, Edmund glanced over — and Pip saw her friend see it. See Brannoc laughing. See the children asleep in a pile by Marta's knee. See the sentries nodding to each other, the mended shirts, the shared bread. See the shape of a family, drawn in exile and firelight.

And Pip saw Edmund flinch from it.

Not obviously. Just a tightening around the eyes, a shift of weight, the look of a woman measuring a doorway for escape. Edmund did that whenever kindness came near her — leaned toward it like a plant toward sun, then jerked back like the sun had teeth.

Later, walking to the stream for water, Pip caught her alone.

"You liked it here," Pip said. "Don't lie. I can tell. Your shoulders came down."

"My shoulders did not come down."

"They came down *this* much." Pip held her fingers a generous inch apart. "You almost smiled at the twins. I saw it. It was mostly teeth but it counted."

"Pip." Edmund crouched to her level, and her face was doing the careful thing again, the wall-building thing. "These people are kind. That's exactly why we can't stay. Everything that gathers around that stone gets hurt. The soldiers, the regent's hunters — whatever's answering prayers wrong knows we have it. Every hour we sit in this camp, I'm painting targets on forty backs."

"You painted them anyway. When you saved me. When you took Maren in."

"That was different."

"Why?"

"Because—" Edmund stopped. Her mouth opened and closed. Somewhere in the dark, an owl asked its question twice.

"Because you hadn't decided to love us yet," Pip said, not unkindly. "It's easier to spend strangers than family. But you've spent us now. Sorry. No refunds."

Edmund stared at her. "You are eight years old."

"And wise beyond my concentratedness."

"*Concentration.*" But Edmund was almost smiling, and her shoulders had come down again, and she didn't pull away when Pip hooked a hand into hers on the walk back.

Halfway to the fire, Edmund stopped. "If it comes to it — if hunters come — you run. You and the stone go different directions and you don't look back."

"Nope."

"Pip—"

"I'll run," Pip allowed. "But I'm taking the god, and the god likes you, and he'll drag us both back to you if we get separated. Small gods are stubborn about their people. It's their whole job."

Edmund looked at her for a long moment, firelight moving on her face, something wrestling behind her eyes — hope against habit, wanting against fearing.

"Fine," she said at last. "One more night."

Which was, Pip reflected, exactly how families started. Not with a yes. With a *one more night* that kept becoming another.

* * *

Pip woke near dawn because the little god woke, shivering hard enough to rattle her ribs where he'd nestled against her for warmth.

The fire had burned low. The sentries' whistles had gone quiet — not softly quiet, *wrongly* quiet, the silence of held breath. She sat up and saw Brannoc standing at the camp's edge, staring north through the thinning fog, and saw Marta wake the children without a word, folding blankets with hands that moved too fast.

Maren was already saddling the horses.

"What is it?" Edmund asked, coming awake the way she always did — instantly, completely, like a snare springing.

"Smoke," Brannoc said. "North road. Rider signal-fires, three of them in a line. That's the regent's pattern." He turned, and his face was grim as winter. "Someone sold our position. And they're not coming for us."

He looked at Edmund. At the stone beneath her cloak.

"They're coming for what you carry. We ride south in ten minutes — there's a crossing at the Ashwater the fog will cover till midmorning. After that, company, you're on your own." His jaw tightened. "I'd say I'm sorry. I'd say the safety of my people comes first. And it does."

"I know," Edmund said. "I'd do the same."

But Pip, pulling on her boots, saw the little god press himself flat and cold against her skin, and heard him whisper in the voice like wind through keyholes:

*"They're faster than the fog. Ask him about the river path. Ask him about the river path, small speaker — the drowned chapel knows your mother's name."*

Pip froze with one boot half on.

"Um," she said. "Before we go. Anyone want to hear something about a chapel?"
