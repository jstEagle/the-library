## 7. What Wears the Crown

Brannoc had killed men for less than what he was about to say, and he had buried men for saying less than he was about to show.

He waited until the fire burned down to coals. Pip was asleep against Maren's shoulder, the girl's fingers still curled around a stick she'd been using to draw shapes in the dirt — shapes that sometimes answered back, if you watched long enough. Edmund sat across the flames with her arms wrapped around her knees, wearing the look she wore every night now: like a woman listening to a room she couldn't see.

Good. He needed her listening.

"You want to know why I deserted," Brannoc said. "You've been circling it for three days. You and your questions dressed up as politeness."

"I haven't asked," Edmund said.

"No. That's worse. That means you've been guessing, and I'd rather you stopped guessing wrong." He turned his hands toward the coals. They were big hands, sword-callused, scarred at the knuckles from a lifetime of holding things together — walls, lines of battle, other men's nerve. "I'll tell it once. After this I don't say it again."

Edmund said nothing. She just nodded, once, and something in her stillness told him she understood the shape of what he was offering. People who'd run from their pasts recognized each other. It was one of the few honest things left in Aldermarch.

"The king changed on the ninth night of Harvestmoon," Brannoc said. "Eight months ago. I know because it was my daughter's nameday, and I'd traded three days' leave to be there for it. So when the summons came — *all household guard to the chapel, full kit* — I remember being angry about that first. Angry. That's how stupid we all were. The end of everything we knew, and my first feeling was annoyance at missing a cake."

Maren shifted but said nothing. The knight had heard pieces of this before, Brannoc suspected, back when they'd both still worn the crown's colors. But not all of it. Nobody had all of it.

"The chapel of the inner keep hadn't been used in years. Old King's Chapel, they called it — pre-Unity stonework, older than the dynasty. There was dust on the pews thick enough to write in. We were posted inside and out, thirty of us, and nobody would say why. Lady Vessia arrived last, in grey silk, no mourning colors even though the court was still in half-mourning for the plague dead. She smiled at us on her way in. I want you to understand that detail, because everyone who tells this story leaves it out or remembers it wrong. *She smiled.* Not a queen's smile. A woman watching a pot come to boil."

* * *

"There were six people in the ritual circle. Five I could name: Vessia; her chaplain, a soft man called Ferren who'd never struck me as brave enough for heresy; three scholars from the Collegium with ink still on their fingers. And the sixth was a woman I didn't know. Middle-aged, travel-worn, dressed plainly like a hedge-witch or a village cunning-woman. Her wrists were bound with white cord, which should have been the first alarm — you don't bind an accomplice.

"She wasn't struggling, though. That was the thing that kept me standing there instead of doing my oath's work. She looked — " Brannoc searched for it. "Resigned. Like someone who'd agreed to something and was trying to believe she'd chosen right. And she was crying without making any noise about it. Silent crying, steady as rain down a window.

"King Aldous came in carried, not walking. That was the second thing. The whole court knew he'd taken a fever in the spring, but we'd been told he was mending. He wasn't mending. He was dying. I'd stood honor guard over enough deathbeds to know the color of a man three days out from the grave. His eyes were open and they weren't seeing anything.

"Vessia put him on the altar stone herself. Refused help. Laid him out like she was arranging a body for viewing, except she tucked a blanket under his chin first, gentle as anything, and that gentleness frightened me more than the blood did.

"Because there was blood. The scholars had chalked a circle on the floor and cut channels into it, and there was blood in the channels, and the woman in the circle — the bound woman — had her sleeve rolled up and a basin beside her. But it wasn't only hers. There was a bowl of it, dark, old, and when the chanting started I felt it in my teeth before I heard it in my ears.

"I've stood in shield walls. I know what fear feels like coming off two hundred men. This was different. This was the chapel itself being afraid. The stones were afraid. The air went thin and the candles bent sideways though there was no draft, and the woman began to speak words that weren't in any tongue I knew, and the words had weight. You could hear the weight. Like someone stacking iron on the far side of a wall.

"And Vessia stood at the king's head with her hand on his forehead, smiling. All through it. Smiling.

"The woman faltered near the end. I saw it. She looked at the king's face and she stopped speaking, and for one heartbeat I thought — thank the gods, it breaks, whatever it is, it breaks — and then Vessia leaned down and said something to her. Quiet. Just for her. And the woman closed her eyes and finished the words, and I have spent eight months trying to decide whether that makes Vessia merciful or the cruelest creature I ever served.

"The light went out of the candles all at once. Not blown — *taken*, like a debt collected. In the dark there was a sound I still can't describe right. Closest I can get is a door opening somewhere very large, and wind going the wrong way through it. Inward. Everything pulling inward, toward the king on the stone.

"When the candles relit themselves, the king was sitting up.

"Sitting up. Color in his cheeks. Eyes clear. He got off the altar unassisted, thanked the guards at the door by name — my name among them, and I'd never told him it — and walked out on legs that had been failing him an hour before. The court rejoiced. The plague was ending, the harvest came in heavy, the border war wound down inside a season. Miracles. Every prayer in Aldermarch getting answered like coins falling from a torn purse.

"And King Aldous never spoke another word."

* * *

The coals ticked as they cooled. Somewhere out in the dark, one of the Ashwood sentries coughed.

"He talks now," Pip said sleepily, without opening her eyes. "Not with his mouth. You have to listen underneath."

Brannoc looked at her. The child had a gift for saying the floor out from under a conversation. "Aye, maybe. What I'm telling you is what I knew then." He dragged a breath in. "The part I don't tell is what came after.

"We were sworn to silence — official story was a private rite of thanksgiving, the king's recovery, nothing more. Fine. Armies run on not asking. But three of us had seen the bound woman, and seen her face, and one of them — Tam Wicke, a good man, stupid-honest — couldn't hold it. Started drinking and talking. Said the rite smelled of blood-oath, said the woman hadn't been willing, said somebody ought to ask the Collegium what words get spoken over a dying king with his eyes shut.

"Tam vanished eleven days later. Not fled — vanished. His kit was in his quarters, his wages uncollected, his horse in its stall. Then Ser Danelle, who'd stood next to me at the chapel door. Gone. Then the chaplain, Ferren, who'd gone pale and quiet ever since — gone, and his family moved out of the city the same week, paid well, according to the gossip, to forget where they'd lived.

"I did the arithmetic every soldier does. I counted the dead-and-disappeared and I counted my own daughter asleep upstairs, and I resigned my post the same month and took her out of the city so fast we traveled by night. She didn't live long enough for any of it to matter. Fever, two winters back. But I stayed gone, because by then running was the only true thing I did."

He reached into his shirt then, and Edmund stiffened — every runaway does when a big man reaches toward his own chest — and drew out a scrap of leather, soft with handling, wrapped around something flat. He unwrapped it on his knee.

A page. Torn from a book, ragged at one edge, browned with age and damp. It was covered edge to edge in fine, cramped script in faded brown-black ink, and threaded through the text ran diagrams — circles within circles, names in columns, a figure lying prone at the center with lines radiating out of it like a caught sun.

"This came out of the chapel with me," Brannoc said. "After. When I cleared out my quarters, I went back in one last time. The Old King's Chapel had been sealed — wax on the doors, guards on it, the whole weight of the regency saying *nothing happened here*. But I'd been a guard there eight years. I knew the sally-passage behind the vestry that the masons never finished sealing. I went in looking for proof. Anything. Something to carry to someone who mattered.

"The altar had been scoured. The circle chiseled out of the floor — they'd taken a hammer to it, which tells you they were afraid of it too. But the scholars' working papers had been burned in a brazier, badly, and one page had caught the draught and gone under the pew. Under the pew where I'd been standing that night."

Edmund had gone very still. Stillness was her weapon; he'd learned that much about her in three days on the road. But her stillness now had a different grain to it. Recognition, maybe. Dread, certainly.

"I can't read most of it," Brannoc said. "It's not letters, mostly — it's godscript, the old kind, the kind the Collegium says died out with the Unity. But the margins—" He turned the page toward the firelight and tapped a line of annotations in a different hand, smaller, quicker, written in the common alphabet in a script that slanted hard to the right, impatient, brilliant. "These are notes. Corrections, almost. Whoever wrote them knew the ritual better than the scholars did. Was fixing their mistakes. And here—" his finger dropped to a single word in the lower margin, underlined twice, "—is the only word in the whole page I can read plain."

Edmund read it aloud, barely above breath:

"*Consent.*

"And beneath it, smaller, as if the writer had argued with herself: *it must be given freely or the door won't hold. Gods forgive me. It holds either way.*

"Maren made a sound low in her throat. Pip sat up, suddenly, entirely awake, staring at the page like it was humming.

But Brannoc watched Edmund. Watched her take the page with hands that weren't quite steady. Watched her thumb hover over the margin notes the way you hover over a wound you're afraid to touch. Watched her lips move slightly, reading ahead of herself, and watched the color go out of her face one degree at a time.

"You know the hand," he said quietly. It wasn't a question. He'd known since the shrine, since the hearth-god had said *your mother's name* and Edmund had flinched like a struck bell. "Whose is it?"

For a long moment she didn't answer. The fire popped. Out in the dark, the Ashwood breathed around them, ten thousand trees keeping secrets better than any of them could.

"My mother taught me my letters," Edmund finally said. Her voice came out scraped clean, no ornament on it at all. "She wrote shopping lists like this. Slanted, fast, always in a hurry, always smarter than the paper deserved." She turned the page over, and over again, as if the reverse might hold an escape. "She disappeared when I was nine. Everyone said she abandoned us. My father believed it. I believed it. I built my whole life on believing it."

"And now?"

"Now I find out she was in the king's chapel with her wrists tied in white cord, bleeding into a basin, correcting the grammar of whatever they were doing to him." Edmund's voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "And then she was gone, and the king went silent, and everyone who asked questions vanished, and I spent twenty years being ashamed of a woman who was probably—"

She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes.

"Probably what?" Brannoc asked, though he knew. He wanted to hear her say it, because he needed to hear someone else say it too, needed it out loud in the world where it could be fought.

"Probably dead for it," Edmund whispered. "Or worse. Probably the reason the door opened at all. Probably the consent." Her hand dropped and her eyes when they found his were wet and furious and lit from underneath with something he recognized from mirrors, from bad nights, from the months after Tam Wicke disappeared. "They used her. Whatever wears the crown right now, whatever is eating prayers out of my people's mouths — it came through a door my mother opened because someone she trusted lied to her. And I have spent my whole life hiding from a gift I hated, and it turns out the only thing that matters about me is that I inherited *her*. Her blood. Her ears. Maybe her debt."

"That's not certain," Maren said carefully. "We don't know the terms of—"

"Don't." Edmund held up a hand. "Don't soften it. I've had twenty years of soft." She looked down at the page again, and Brannoc saw the moment the grief folded itself away and the fury sat down in its place, neat and cold and permanent. It was a soldier's transition. He'd seen it on the faces of men the instant before they chose a side.

"Then here's what we do with it," Brannoc said. He took the page back gently, wrapped it, and pressed it into her hands instead of his own. "You keep it. I've carried it eight months and it's heavier every mile. It was never mine to carry — I just didn't know whose it was."

She stared at it. "Why give it to me? You've known me three days."

"Because I watched a woman walk into that chapel knowing it would cost her, and I watched a girl walk out of a valley knowing the same, and I'm tired of proof sitting in the wrong hands." He shrugged, the old armor-weight settling back onto his shoulders even bare. "Besides. If I'm wrong about you, you'll sell it and disappear, and good luck to you. If I'm right—" he nodded at the page, at her mother's slanted, hurrying, brilliant hand, "—you'll read every word of it. And then nobody in that pretty rotten court sleeps easy again."

Pip crawled over and wedged herself against Edmund's side, the way children do when they've decided something is settled. "Your mama fixed their mistakes," she said. "That means she was winning, some. Right up till she wasn't."

Edmund laughed — a broken, startled sound, half sob — and pulled the girl close, and Brannoc looked away to give them the privacy of it, and found Maren watching him with an expression he couldn't afford to examine.

"One more thing," he said, because the fire was nearly dead and the hour was ugly and it was time for all of it to be said. "The page. The main script, the godscript. There are four names in the columns there. Three have been scratched out, scored through hard enough to tear the paper. One hasn't."

Edmund went rigid.

"It's not your mother's," Brannoc said. "Her name isn't on it at all — she was the instrument, not the party. The fourth name, the one still standing, the one they didn't need to scratch out because it's still *true*—" he met her eyes across the embers. "It's a god's name. And I've been carrying it eight months waiting for someone who could tell me whether crossing it out matters."

Edmund looked down at the page in her lap, at the one unstruck name gleaming faintly in the guttering light — and Brannoc swore afterward that the letters seemed to lean toward her, the way a hungry thing leans toward warmth.

"Ysolde," she read, and the wind in the Ashwood went utterly still, every tree at once, like a forest holding its breath. "Ysolde of the Long Night."

The fire died between one heartbeat and the next, and in the sudden dark, from everywhere and nowhere, something ancient sighed — and answered.
