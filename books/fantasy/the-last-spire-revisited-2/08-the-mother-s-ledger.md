## 8. The Mother's Ledger

The scrap of paper sat in the middle of Brannoc's bedroll like a snake someone had brought indoors.

Edmund hadn't touched it since he'd handed it over two nights ago. She'd looked at it plenty. Looked at it by firelight, by grey dawn, by the flat light of a snowed-in morning that made everything in the Ashwood camp seem underwater and slow. It was a rectangle torn from a larger page, perhaps a handspan across, and on it were written eleven lines of cramped script in an ink that had gone brown with age — or with something else; Edmund didn't let herself speculate about what else.

She knew the handwriting.

That was the whole problem. That was the knife of it, turned slow. She could have looked at a hundred strangers' hands and felt nothing, but this was her mother's writing the way her own heartbeat was her own: she would have known it blind, in the dark, at the bottom of a well. The way the ascenders on the *d*s leaned east. The little hook on the final stroke that made every line look like it was reaching for something just past its edge.

Her mother had reached for something just past her edge, too. And this was what she'd grabbed.

"You're going to stare a hole through it," Maren said. The disgraced knight sat across the fire, working a whetstone down her blade in long even strokes. She'd been doing that for an hour, which meant she was thinking, which meant she was worried. "Either read it or burn it."

"I can't do either."

"You can read. You told me you can."

"I can read *it*." Edmund nodded at the scrap. "I mean I know what it says. Mostly. It's not the words I can't —" She stopped. Pressed her palms into her eyes until sparks bloomed. "It's what the words are *in*, Maren. This isn't common script. It's god-script. Ledger-script. The old notation hedge-witches used to keep accounts with the small gods. Offerings given, debts owed, favors banked. My mistress had books full of it." A breath. "My mother taught it to me when I was nine. Before she sent me away."

Pip looked up from where she lay sprawled against Brannoc's saddle, feet in the air, having a silent conversation with something hovering near the cook-tent that only she could see. "Your mum taught you secret writing?"

"She taught me how to talk to things that don't want to be talked to. The writing came with it." Edmund heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for prices at market. "Most mothers teach their daughters bread. Mine taught me bargains."

* * *

Brannoc found her after midday, sitting alone at the edge of camp where the trees thinned toward the frozen creek. He didn't say anything at first. That was one of the reasons she'd half-let herself trust him: he understood silence, being a man who'd walked out of a king's guard with nothing but his sword and his shame and had been living inside both ever since.

He lowered himself onto a fallen log with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions, and set the scrap of paper between them on the bark, weighted down with a stone.

"My wife kept ledgers," he said.

Edmund blinked at him. In three weeks of traveling together, Brannoc had mentioned his wife exactly once, and then only as a name attached to a grave he hadn't been able to visit.

"Tallow merchant, out of Correnvale. Every candle in the house had a column. What it cost, what it burned for, who it lit." He turned his hands over, studying the old calluses. "When the king changed — when I ran — I took nothing. Couldn't tell you why. But on the road I kept catching myself doing sums. What I'd spent. What I owed. Who I'd failed to light." His jaw worked. "A man keeps accounts whether he means to or not. Question is only what book they're written in."

"And hers," Edmund said quietly, nodding at the scrap, "is written in the gods' book."

"That's what I'm hoping you'll tell me. Because I've had it memorized since the night I tore it out of that chapel wall, and it reads to me like madness. Words that repeat. Numbers that aren't numbers. And one line at the bottom that I've wished a hundred times I couldn't read." He recited it without looking down, his voice gone rough: "*Consent given freely, under witness, unto the saving of the realm.*"

The cold went through Edmund like the creek water through her boots.

Freely given. She knew those words. Every child raised anywhere near the old ways knew them, the way children near a river knew about drowning. Consent freely given was the load-bearing wall of all covenant magic. No binding held without it. No bargain bit without it. A god could be tricked, starved, caged — but never *bound* outright. There always had to be a door, and the door always had to be opened from the inside by a willing hand.

Someone had opened the door for the thing wearing King Aldous's crown.

And the handwriting on the invitation was her mother's.

* * *

That night she took the scrap, a stub of charcoal, and the camp's one lamp into the lean-to Brannoc used as a scriptorium, armory, and argument pit, and she did the thing she'd been circling for three days.

She read it properly.

Ledger-script wasn't hard, once you'd learned to stop reading it like language. It wasn't meant to carry meaning the way words did; it was meant to carry *weight*. Each glyph was a value and a direction. Give, take, hold, release. Underneath the surface nonsense of Brannoc's recitation there was a second text, arithmetic dressed as poetry, and once Edmund let her eyes slide into the old habit of it — the way you'd slip into a river current — the eleven lines unfolded themselves.

Line one: a designation. Not the crown-thing's name — bound things had no true names, that was rather the point of them — but its *account number*, if you could stomach thinking of a devouring spirit that way. A serial in the great ledger of the world, filed under winter, filed under hunger, filed under *held*.

Lines two through seven: terms. She read them twice, three times, her lips moving. Sustenance by prayer, drawn through the seat of kingship. Duration: indefinite, contingent upon continued consent. Scope: the realm of Aldermarch entire. And threaded through every clause, in the notation that meant *irrevocable while standing*: the word for *freely*.

Line eight stopped her cold.

It was a date.

Not in years — the old script didn't use years, it used reigns and famines and comets, the way farmers used weather — but Edmund could read the reference. It pointed back past Vessia's regency, past the plague winters, back twenty-six years, give or take a bad harvest. Twenty-six years ago.

She sat very still and did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do since the Ashwood.

Twenty-six years ago, Edmund Ravensworth had been four years old. Twenty-six years ago, her mother had still been alive — alive for another two years, in fact, before the fever year took her, before the letter came, before a hedge-witch named Osla took in a six-year-old girl as apprentice and collateral both. Twenty-six years ago, in some chapel Edmund had never seen, in front of witnesses she'd never meet, her mother had stood in the sight of whatever passed for gods in that room and given her consent.

*Freely.*

"No," Edmund said aloud, to the lamp, to the empty lean-to, to nobody. "No, she wouldn't have. She *hated* —" 

Hated what? she asked herself, and hated herself for the question. Hated the court. Hated the crown's appetite for anything it couldn't tax. Hated the way the great houses treated god-speakers — her people, the few scattered families left — like tools with legs. Her mother had said so a thousand times, laughing, bitter, braiding Edmund's hair by the peat-fire. *They'll come for us someday, little wren. Folk always come for what hears them. Best we're worth more running than staying.*

So how did that woman end up with her hand on the door?

Edmund bent back over the scrap because the alternative was screaming, and lines nine through eleven waited like teeth.

Line nine: witnesses. Three sigils. One she recognized — Ysolde's mark, the long night, the veiled moon. The last great god, Maren called her, though Edmund now understood that *last* meant *last one big enough to matter*, not *last one alive*. Whatever Ysolde had witnessed here, she had witnessed it standing on the side of the binding. Gods didn't witness lightly. Gods especially didn't witness *against their own kind* lightly.

Unless they believed it was mercy.

Line ten was the one that undid her.

It was a clause. A single conditional, tucked at the bottom like a footnote, written smaller than the rest, in the notation for *understanding acknowledged*. And it read:

*This binding shall hold until such time as the consenter, or the consenter's blood, stands in the place of striking and takes the consent back.*

Edmund read it four times.

Then she put the lamp down very carefully on the table, because her hands had begun to shake, and she pressed both palms flat against the cold boards and breathed, the way Osla had taught her to breathe before any working, in through the nose, out through the mouth, until the shaking was only her body's and not her soul's.

There it was. There it had been all along, sitting in the fine print of her mother's hand for twenty-six years: a door within the door. A key cut into the lock itself. Whoever gave consent had built the unbinding into the giving — had *insisted* on it, from the notation, from the small defiant size of the letters, the way a person insists on something they know the other party doesn't want to hear.

Her mother hadn't just opened the door. She'd left the latch on her side of it.

Or her daughter's side of it.

*The consenter's blood.*

Me, thought Edmund. She means me. She always meant me. Even at the end — even in the thing that hollowed out a kingdom — she was leaving me a way out of the burning house.

And then the other thought arrived, the one underneath, the one she'd been carrying since she was six years old and had never once set down, because setting it down meant looking at it.

Her mother had done this. Her mother had helped bind a spirit to the throne of Aldermarch. Everything since — the stolen prayers, the starving small gods Pip mourned each night, Ser Maren's ruined honor, the winter coming down the valleys early like a lid closing — all of it traced back, through clauses and conditions and twenty-six years of consequence, to the freely given consent of Ilsette Ravensworth, god-speaker, braid-maker, laugher-by-the-fire.

Edmund had built her whole life on top of that stone. She'd run from Osla because Osla wanted to sell her gift to the crown, yes, but underneath the running there had always been the older flight, the original one, the one with no road: the knowledge of what her bloodline had *done*. God-speakers didn't get hunted out of history for nothing. Somewhere back down the family line, somebody had always paid for the hearing. She'd assumed her share of the debt was simply owed. Inherited guilt, like inherited eyes.

But the ledger didn't read like guilt.

She looked at it again, forcing herself, line by line. Line ten's defiant smallness. Line eight's date — twenty-six years ago, which meant, which *had* to mean, a kingdom in crisis; Maren had said the old king had died badly around then, a wasting sickness, a succession in doubt, the realm one bad winter from tearing itself apart. A dying king. A hungry realm. A woman who could hear the gods being asked — begged — by powers bigger than she was, to save the kingdom, please, whatever it costs, surely it won't cost what you fear —

And a clause slipped in small at the bottom, because the woman signing it was not a fool and did not trust anyone in the room, and wanted her daughter to have a key.

Her mother hadn't failed.

Her mother had been *used*. Deceived about what would wear the opening — that was the only reading that fit. You didn't build a self-destruct clause into a deal you knew would birth a devourer. You built it into a deal you believed was rescue. Someone had shown her mother a drowning kingdom and a rope, and hidden what was holding the other end of the rope, and her mother had grabbed it with both hands and signed her name and her blood into the bargain, believing — *believing* — she was saving everyone.

Twenty-six years of Edmund's shame, and it had never been hers to carry. It was theft. It was fraud, committed against a grieving woman by people who wore crowns and smiles, and it had compounded interest ever since, paid out in prayers and gods and winters, and the debt-collectors were still riding.

The lamp guttered. Outside, Pip was singing to the dark in her tuneless, unembarrassed way, some song about a fox and a door, and one of her invisible friends was — by the sound of Pip's giggling — objecting to the lyrics.

Edmund picked up the scrap of her mother's handwriting and held it over the lamp flame out of pure reflex, the old fugitive instinct — destroy the evidence, stay small, stay gone.

Then she pulled it back.

No. Not anymore. This wasn't evidence of her crime to hide. It was evidence of a crime done *to her*, and to her mother, and to a kingdom — and evidence was only useful in daylight, in front of witnesses, in the company of people who'd help you carry it.

She tucked it inside her shirt, over her heart, where the paper warmed against her skin like something almost alive.

* * *

Maren found her at dawn, as the camp stirred and the frost smoked off the woodpile. Edmund was already packed, already waiting, the ledger-scrap's words arranged in her head in marching order.

"You've read it," Maren said. Not a question. The knight had a way of arriving at conclusions ahead of the conversation.

"I've read it." Edmund stood. Her breath hung white between them. "The binding needs consent. Freely given. My mother gave it — twenty-six years ago, when the old king was dying, when someone convinced her it was salvation." She watched Maren's face do its careful soldier-blankening and kept going anyway. "But she wrote the way out into the terms. Unbinding requires a god-speaker to stand where the bargain was struck and take the consent back. The consenter — or the consenter's blood."

Maren went very still. "Edmund."

"It's my mother's hand, Maren. It was always going to be my blood. I'm not telling you because I've decided anything." Which was a lie, and they both probably knew it, but it was a polite lie, the kind travelers grant each other. "I'm telling you because I need to know where the bargain was struck."

Something moved behind Maren's eyes — recognition, dread, and underneath both, the grim relief of a woman who has finally been given a task shaped like her guilt. "I know the chapel," she said quietly. "Everyone who served in the palace knows the chapel. Sealed since the night the king changed. Brannoc tore your scrap from its wall." She paused. "You understand what you're saying. To stand in that place and take back a god-binding — that's not a key you turn and walk away from. Gifts like yours don't survive their own revocation."

"Then it's fortunate," Edmund said, "that I spent six years in a valley where my gift was worthless, learning exactly who I am without it."

From the cook-tent, Pip's voice rang out across the frost, bright and certain: "Edmund! Edmund, the fox-god says there's riders on the south road! Lots of riders! And they're *not* being quiet about it!"

Edmund closed her hand around the scrap of paper over her heart, and turned toward the sound of hoofbeats beginning to rise out of the trees.
