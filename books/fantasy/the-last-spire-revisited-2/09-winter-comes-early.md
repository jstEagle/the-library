## 9. Winter Comes Early

The first snow came on the ninth day out of the Ashwood, and Maren knew before she opened her eyes that it was wrong.

She'd spent twenty years as a knight in Aldermarch's service, most of them on the northern marches where winter was a neighbor you learned to read. Real snow announced itself. It came with a pressure in the joints, a smell like iron and coming dark, a hush in the birds. What woke her now was none of those things. It was the silence — a silence with weight to it, pressing down through the canvas of the wagon like a hand.

She climbed out into a world gone white overnight. Not dusted. Buried. The road they'd followed yesterday, rutted brown mud to the axle, had vanished under two feet of snow so dry it squeaked, and the sky above was the color of old pewter, low and close, snow still sifting down without wind, without pause, like ash from a fire no one had put out.

"It shouldn't be here." Brannoc stood at the wagon's tongue, arms crossed, breath steaming. He said it the way he said everything now, flat and careful, a man rationing his anger. "Harvest's not done. There's barley standing in the fields back at the last village. You don't get snow like this in the Leafmonth. Not even up north. Not once in my life."

"The king stopped speaking eight months ago," Maren said. She pulled her cloak tighter. "You want me to be surprised the seasons broke too?"

"I want you to tell me what it means."

Maren looked down the road, south and east, toward the capital they were creeping toward. Somewhere ahead of them sat a thing wearing Aldous's face and crown, drinking prayers meant for heaven and answering them wrong. She thought about what answered prayers did when they came from something hungry. Rain in the wrong valley. Crops rotting in the barns while the granaries of loyal lords stayed full. And now this.

"It means," she said, "that someone is taking."

* * *

They found the first frozen village by its smoke.

Or rather — by the absence of it. Maren spotted the rooftops below the road an hour past midday, a scatter of thatch around a church spire, and there should have been chimney smoke, dozens of threads of it, because whatever else happened in a village, hearths burned. Instead the whole settlement lay under its snow like something asleep, and not one thread rose.

Brannoc wanted to ride past. "We can't feed them," he said quietly, when Edmund pointed. "There are eleven of us and half a wagon of grain."

"We're going down," Edmund said.

It wasn't a request, and Maren watched Brannoc decide not to fight it. Something had changed in him since the ledger night, since Edmund had held her mother's notes up to the firelight and read aloud what her mother had written in a hand that shook. He looked at Edmund differently now. Like a banner worth rallying to.

The village was called Harrowmere. Maren learned that later, from a signpost mostly buried. What she learned first was the cold inside it — a cold that didn't belong to weather. It sat in the streets like a presence. Their horses refused the lane and had to be led on foot.

The people were alive. That was the horror of it. Alive, and slow, moving through their houses like figures in honey, wrapped in every blanket they owned beside fires that burned low and gave no heat. A woman opened a door to Edmund's knock and stared at them with eyes that took too long to focus.

"You've come from outside," the woman said. Her voice was flat as slate. "Outside is warm?"

"Outside is autumn," Maren said. "Barely."

The woman nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something she'd suspected. "It started with the praying," she said. "Priest told us to pray harder. Hard times, he said, pray harder and the crown will see us right. So we prayed. Whole village, every night, kneeling in the church till our knees bled." She smiled, and it was the worst thing Maren had seen all year. "And then the warm went out of everything. Not the fire. The *warm*. Like something reached in and took it."

Edmund had gone very still. Maren saw her looking at the woman's hands — chapped, blue at the nails — and then at Pip, who stood at Edmund's shoulder with an expression Maren had never seen on the child's face before. Fear. Not of the village. For it.

"They're emptying," Pip whispered. "The little ones. They're getting *emptied*."

"What little ones?" the woman asked, but Pip had already pushed past her into the house, into the dark, and Maren followed.

Inside, in the corner behind the cold hearth, Pip knelt and cupped her hands around something invisible and spoke to it the way you'd speak to a wounded bird. "I know, I know, I'm here, I've got you—" She looked up at Edmund, and her face was wet. "It's a door-god. A tiny one. Just for this house. It used to live in the latch. It says the big cold came and it couldn't hold the door against it and now it's leaking, it's leaking out, and it doesn't think it'll last the week."

Maren heard nothing. Felt nothing. She never had; the gods spoke only to god-speakers, and she was merely a woman with a sword and a debt. But she believed Pip completely, the way you believe someone reporting a wound.

"How many?" Edmund asked softly. "How many are dying like this?"

Pip's jaw worked. "All of them? Everywhere? They lived off the praying, Edmund. Little jobs, little offerings, crumbs off the table. And everyone's praying harder than ever — but it's not going to *them* anymore. It's going to the throne, and the throne takes it all, and the little ones are starving in a famine of plenty." Her voice cracked. "Hearth-gods and door-gods and well-gods and the lady of the barley-field, and nobody even knows they exist except me, and they're *dying*, and—"

She stopped. Pressed both fists against her mouth. Edmund crouched and gathered her in, and over the child's head she met Maren's eyes, and Maren saw there what she'd been refusing to see since the ridge: this wasn't politics anymore. Politics killed soldiers and taxed farmers and sometimes hanged the wrong lord. This was something older being murdered, root and branch, and the villages freezing around it were just the sound the murder made.

"We stay tonight," Edmund said. "We burn half the wagon's wood. Tomorrow we figure out how to move them."

Brannoc, in the doorway, closed his eyes briefly — a man doing arithmetic he already knew would come out short — and said, "Aye."

* * *

They got forty-one people moving the next morning, which Maren would remember as the hardest arithmetic of her life.

Forty-one, out of perhaps three hundred. The rest couldn't or wouldn't go. The old priest refused to leave his church — "my flock prays here" — though his flock could barely kneel anymore. A farmer wouldn't abandon his cattle, though the cattle were already dying standing up, frost in their nostrils. An old man simply said, "My wife's under the yew tree, and I'm not leaving her," and there was nothing anyone on earth could say to that.

So they took the ones who could walk and loaded the ones who couldn't onto the wagon and the cart they'd bought from the farmer — bought with nearly the last of Brannoc's coin, over his own objection, another small war she'd watched Edmund win — and they moved them toward the Ashwood, where the Company had begun digging shelters into the earthworks of an old hill-fort, because Brannoc had sent a rider ahead with one sentence: *Winter's coming wrong. Make room.*

The column crawled. Snow kept falling, dry and endless, and the temperature fell with every mile, and Maren rode rear guard watching the stragglers and understanding, with a soldier's clarity, exactly what she was looking at. This was a retreat. This was what a retreat looked like when there was no enemy line anywhere — just cold, patient, everywhere.

On the second day a child died. A boy, maybe four, asleep in his mother's arms on the cart, who simply didn't wake up. No wound, no sickness that Maren could find. He'd just gone out, like a lamp, somewhere in the night.

His mother didn't scream. That was the part Maren carried afterward, the part that woke her in the nights that followed. The woman just held him and rocked him and said thank you to Edmund when they helped her dig, in a voice like the woman at the door in Harrowmere, flat as slate, and Maren understood that the cold had already taken her too, taken whatever part of a person screams.

That evening, camped in the lee of a ruined mill, Pip found Maren sitting apart on watch and sat down next to her without asking, which was Pip's way.

"He went to one of them," Pip said. "The little gods. The boy. The door-god from his house carried him, after. It couldn't save his body but it took the rest of him somewhere warm." She wiped her nose. "I thought you'd want to know. People always want to know, when it's children."

"Do they," Maren said. It wasn't a question and wasn't bitter either, just tired.

"You were a knight." Pip picked at the ice on the millstone between them. "Knights are supposed to stop things like this."

"Yes."

"You couldn't stop this one either."

"No."

Pip considered that with the terrible seriousness of children, then leaned sideways until her shoulder rested against Maren's arm, a small point of warmth in all that cold, and said, "The door-god says you keep good watch. It says most watchers look *at* the dark. You look *for* people." A pause. "It likes that."

Maren found, to her considerable annoyance, that she had to look away at the snowfield for a moment before she could speak.

"Tell it," she managed finally, "thank you."

"It heard you. They always hear." Pip stood, brushing off snow. "That's the whole problem, isn't it."

* * *

The little gods began winking out on the fourth day.

Pip felt each one. Maren watched it happen — watched the child stop mid-step, mid-sentence, mid-bite, and go rigid, hand flying to her chest as if catching a falling thing, and then the long exhale and the name she'd say, always the same ritual: *"Gone. That was Neth, the well-lady of Marleford. She held the rope and bucket for two hundred years."* And then she'd stand a little smaller, and they'd walk on.

By the sixth day she'd stopped eating much. By the seventh, Edmund made her eat anyway, sat across from her at the fire and refused to accept refusal, and Pip ate with tears running down her face and said, "It feels like celebrating at a funeral, every meal, because every meal I'm stronger and they're weaker and it's my *fault* I'm still here," and Edmund said, quietly, "Eat," and Pip ate.

Maren took Edmund aside after, into the dark beyond the firelight, where the snow came down between them and the stars.

"She won't last if they all die," Maren said. "She's tied to them. You can see it. Every one that goes out takes a piece of her light with it."

"I know."

"And we're walking her toward the capital, where the thing that's killing them sits on a throne."

"I *know*, Maren."

Edmund's voice had an edge Maren hadn't heard before, and in the firelight spilling faintly across the snow Maren studied her — this runaway apprentice turned reluctant prophet, this woman who'd wanted nothing but a quiet valley and had been dragged out of it by a glowing stone and a dying knight's stubbornness. Who had lost her mother twice over now: once to distance, once to the truth of what her mother's consent had built.

"The hearth-god," Maren said. "The one at the shrine. It told you the thing on the throne eats gods, and it knows your mother's name."

"It did."

"Then you understand what I'm about to say better than I do." Maren breathed the cold air, and it hurt, and she said it anyway. "We may not be able to save them. Any of them. The gods, the villages, possibly the king. We may be walking into a mouth. And if we get to choose — at the end, when it comes down to it — we have to know beforehand what we're choosing. Because Pip will ask us to spend her for them. She's seven. She'll *ask*."

Edmund was silent a long moment. Snow settled on her shoulders and in her hair and she didn't brush it away.

"When I was in the valley," she said at last, "I mended pots. That was my whole ambition. Keep the fire lit, mend the pots, don't be noticed." She looked up, and her eyes in the dimness were steady and unhappy. "I ran from my gift because everyone who ever wanted it wanted to *use* it. My mistress. The court. Whoever wrote my mother's name into that ritual. And I swore I would never become someone who used people." A breath. "So no, Maren. We don't spend her. We don't spend any of them. If the choice ever comes to that, we've already failed, and we should say so out loud rather than dress it up as strategy."

"That's a hard promise to keep in a famine."

"It's the only promise worth keeping in one."

From the fire behind them, Pip's voice floated thin through the cold: "*Gone.* That was Old Tallyman, the god of the crossing-stones at Ferrow Ford. He was *older than the road*."

Neither of them spoke. Then Edmund said, "Come on. She shouldn't say the names alone."

They walked back to the fire together, and Maren thought about prayers — all those thousands of kneeling villagers, praying harder and harder into a hunger that swallowed everything and gave back only cold. Somewhere ahead a hollow king sat wearing stolen warmth, and somewhere behind them the dead boy traveled with a door-god toward somewhere warm, and between the two of those facts stretched a road, a company, a child losing her oldest friends one name at a time, and a woman who mended pots deciding, meal by meal and mile by mile, what she was willing to be.

Two days later, a rider found them — one of Brannoc's outriders, horse lathered despite the cold, face gray beneath the snow-dust.

"Company's safe," he gasped. "But you need to see the ford at Wetherlow. The river's frozen solid, top to bottom, mid-autumn. And there's—" he swallowed— "there's people standing on the ice, ser. Hundreds. Walking out of the east. Refugees, from the whole breadth of the march. And behind them, riding easy through the storm like it's a garden party—" his voice dropped, and Maren felt the cold go deeper than the weather— "riders in royal livery. The queen regent's own. They're following the refugees. Like wolves follow the herd."

Brannoc was already reaching for his sword. Edmund had gone white, her hand pressed flat over her breastbone where the prayer-stone used to hang, as if she could feel, through skin and bone, some vast attention turning toward them across the snow.

And Pip, small and pale at the fire, said the thing none of the rest of them had the courage to say:

"It knows we're coming. It's *rolling out the carpet*."
