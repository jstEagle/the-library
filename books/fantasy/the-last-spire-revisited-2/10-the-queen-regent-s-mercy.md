## 10. The Queen Regent's Mercy

They did not beat her.

That was the first wrong thing. Edmund had spent the whole ride from the ambush site — wrists bound in silk cord, of all the absurd cruelties, silk, like they were afraid of leaving a mark — rehearsing what came next. The cell. The fists. The questions asked between blows by men who'd been told she was nobody and could therefore be treated as nothing.

Instead they gave her water. Warm water, with honey in it.

The soldiers who took her were not Vessia's riders, as it turned out. They wore no livery at all, grey cloaks over grey mail, faces ordinary as bread. Their captain had apologized twice: once when he bound her hands, once when Pip screamed her name from Brannoc's arms until Maren clapped a hand over the girl's mouth to keep her from getting herself taken too. Edmund had looked back only once. She saw Maren holding Pip down, saw Brannoc on his knees in the snow with blood on his side and fury on his face, and then a bend in the road took them away, and that was that.

Two days' travel. A coach, not a cart. Cushions. A woman attendant who changed the dressing on Edmund's rope-burned palms without being asked and never spoke a word.

And now this: a room in a hunting lodge deep in the royal forest, with a fire lit and a bath drawn and a dress laid out on the bed in her size, which meant someone had known her measurements before she was ever caught, which meant someone had been expecting her for longer than she wanted to think about.

"You'll want the blue one," the silent attendant said suddenly, breaking her own silence at the door. "She prefers people not dressed in mourning colors. It makes conversations difficult."

"Conversations," Edmund repeated.

The woman almost smiled. "You'll see." Then she left, and Edmund stood alone in a warm room full of soft things, and understood that the trap was not the cell. The trap was the comfort.

* * *

They came for her at dusk. Four guards, unhurried, courteous, walking her down a corridor hung with tapestries so old their dyes had gone the color of weak tea. At the far end, double doors opened onto a study that smelled of woodsmoke and ink and, underneath both, something faintly sweet and wrong, like fruit just past its prime.

Lady Vessia stood at the window with her back to the door.

Edmund had prepared herself for a monster. Everyone in the Ashwood described her like one: the regent who ruled through a hollow king, whose riders burned villages whose prayers ran dry, whose smile the survivors couldn't describe because none of them ever got a good look at it before the killing started. Brannoc talked about her the way you talk about weather that killed your family. Flat voice. Careful words.

The woman at the window was small. Grey-streaked hair pinned simply. A wool dress the color of slate, no crown, no jewels, hands folded behind her back like a farmer surveying a field. When she turned, her face was tired in the way Edmund's mother's face had been tired — the tiredness of someone carrying something heavy for a very long time and having made peace with the weight.

"Edmund Ravensworth," Vessia said. "Or should I say the other name? Your mother's name?"

"You don't get to say either."

"No," Vessia agreed mildly. "I suppose I don't." She gestured to a chair by the fire. "Please sit. I've had supper sent up; I imagine you haven't eaten properly since the Ashwood. Prisoners are always fed badly, and it makes them stupid."

"I'm not hungry."

"You will be. Hunger always wins eventually. That's rather the theme of everything I have to say to you." She sat herself, across the small table, and poured two cups of wine without asking, sliding one across. "Drink or don't. But sit. Standing over someone is a way of lying to them, and I've promised myself I wouldn't lie to you tonight. You're owed better than lies. You especially."

Edmund sat. Not because she was told to. Because her legs had been shaking since the coach and sitting let her hide it.

"Where are my friends?"

"Alive. Unharmed. Moving south under watch, which is not the same as free, but is considerably better than dead. I want that understood from the first minute, because everything else depends on it." Vessia folded her hands. "Your outlaw friends shot three of my men at the ambush. Two will live. One won't see spring. And still I've ordered no reprisals. Do you know why?"

"Because you need me."

"Because I need everyone," Vessia said, "and killing is such an inefficient way of getting them." She leaned back. "Ask your real question, girl. The one sitting behind your teeth."

Edmund's jaw ached from clenching it. "Why am I here? Why the silk and the honey-water and the — " she gestured at the room, the fire, the dress upstairs — "all this? If you know what I am, you know what I can do. You know I heard your little god-thing's leash jingle the moment they brought me inside the wardline. Why not put me in a hole in the ground?"

"Because a hole in the ground is where your mother ended up," Vessia said, "and look how well that served any of us."

The words went into Edmund like a blade finding the gap in armor. She kept her face still. It cost her.

"You knew my mother."

"I employed your mother. I trusted her. And in the end, I buried her in an unmarked grave because even that much honesty was more than the kingdom could afford." Vessia's voice didn't rise. It never rose, Edmund realized. It just kept coming, level as a millrace. "So yes. Courtesy. Because the last time I handled a god-speaker badly, I lost the war before I knew there was one."

"There isn't a war."

"There has been one every year of my regency," Vessia said, "except you weren't in the valley long enough to notice, were you? Mending pots. Very peaceful work. Let me tell you about the world while you were mending pots."

She counted on her fingers. It was obscene, how neat it looked.

"The Sweating Fever, eight years ago. Three hundred thousand dead in eighteen months, and the healers' guild selling cures they knew were sugar and chalk. It stopped. Overnight. No one knows why. I know why. Six years ago, the Border Lords marched on the capital with forty thousand men, and the histories will tell you our armies met them at Redford Bridge. There was no battle at Redford Bridge. Their army woke up one morning having all dreamed the same dream, and they went home, and forty thousand men who would have died in the mud went home to their farms instead. Five years ago, the blight year — do you remember the blight year? Of course you don't. You were hiding. The grain failed from coast to coast, and not one person starved. Not one. Grain appeared where it was needed. Silos that had been empty were found full. I signed the requisition orders, and I will tell you honestly, girl, half the time I signed orders for food that did not exist, and the food arrived anyway."

She let the silence sit. Outside, wind moved in the forest, and somewhere below a log shifted in a hearth.

"Aldous was dying," Vessia said quietly. "The plague had him. His father before him, same wasting sickness, and his grandfather. Your mother came to me with a solution. Something old. Something from before the Aldermarch kings, back when the land itself was asked rather than merely claimed. She said she could save his life. She believed — she genuinely believed — that she was binding a guardian to the throne. A shepherd spirit. Something that would hold the king's body steady while the fever passed and then step back."

"And it didn't step back."

"It never intended to." For the first time, something moved under Vessia's composure — not guilt, Edmund thought. Irritation. The irritation of an engineer describing a flaw in someone else's design. "What came through the opening your mother made ate its way into Aldous and settled there, and it discovered, very quickly, that a king who answers every prayer is a king no one questions. It ended the fever. It turned the Border Lords. It filled the granaries. And it fed, because nothing does anything for free — it fed on prayer, on devotion, on the small daily faith of four million people who never learned what they were tithing. And when whole regions stopped believing fast enough, it grew impatient, and it took winter as a whip." Her eyes flicked toward the window, toward the early dark. "This cold is mine as much as its. I permitted it. Villages that hoard grain while neighbors starve learn quickly when the snow comes in harvest month. Order, Edmund. That's all I have ever been buying. Order at any price, because the alternative — " she spread her small hands — "you saw the alternative. You lived in it. A valley full of runaways and broken people pretending the world outside wasn't drowning."

"That's not order. That's a leash on four million necks."

"Yes," Vessia said simply. "It is. And I have held the other end of it for six years, and I sleep soundly, and I will tell you why." She stood, walked to the fire, stood looking into it. "Because I remember the fever pits. I was twenty-two. I carried my sister's body to a lime trench because there was no one left to help me carry it, and the king we had then — the human king, the free king, the beloved king — issued a proclamation of mourning and did nothing else. Human kings fail slowly, Edmund, and beautifully, and with excellent speeches. This thing on the throne fails never. Choose."

* * *

The supper came, and Edmund ate, because hunger always wins eventually and Vessia had known it would, and hating yourself for it was part of the lesson too. Roast fowl. Bread that hadn't gone hard. Pears in syrup, sweet past the edge of rot — that smell again, that faint wrongness under everything, and Edmund wondered if it came off the crown-thing like smoke off a coat, if everything Vessia owned had been standing too close to it too long.

Vessia ate little. She watched Edmund eat, and there was something almost gentle in it, and the gentleness was worse than cruelty would have been.

"Your mother," Vessia said, when the plates were cleared, "sat where you're sitting. In this room. Eight months before the ritual. She'd come to ask permission — she was scrupulous that way, she wanted consent from the crown before she touched anything crown-shaped. We talked until dawn. She told me about her daughter." A pause, delicate as a surgeon's probe. "The runaway. The one who'd fled her apprenticeship. She cried, telling me. She thought you hated her. She never understood that what you hated was the gift, and she blamed herself for giving it to you."

"Stop."

"She asked me for one thing. Not money, not protection. She asked that if anything ever happened to her, I would find you and leave you alone. That was the whole of her request. Find my Edmund and let her be." Vessia turned from the fire. "I intend to break that promise tonight, and I want you to understand that I know exactly what it costs, because I liked your mother more than I have liked most living people, and I have spent six years regretting the shape of what I let her build."

"What do you want?" Edmund said. Her voice came out low and steady and surprised her.

"Six months ago, the crown-thing began to change. It has grown — greedy is the wrong word, greedy implies appetite, and this is closer to gravity. It pulls harder each season. The small gods are going out like candles — you know this, your little friend talks to them. The winter lengthens. And I am discovering, six years too late, that I built a machine I cannot steer, operated by something I cannot see, powered by people who cannot consent." She drew a folded paper from her sleeve and laid it on the table between them. Unsealed. Edmund didn't touch it. "Aldous has not spoken in eight months. I believe it has finished eating him, or nearly. Which means soon there will be nothing on the throne but the thing itself, wearing him like a coat, and no regency to pretend otherwise. I need a god-speaker. Not to destroy it — destruction would take the granaries and the peace and the border and plunge us into the fever pits again within the year, and I will not do that to four million people to satisfy anyone's sense of justice. I need a speaker who can talk to it. Steer it. Rein it. Your mother's work, done properly, with the consent she was cheated out of giving."

"And the place you're offering."

"Her place." Vessia said it without flinching. "Court Speaker. Rooms in the palace. Your friends released, pardoned, the outlaw company left alone — Brannoc's desertion struck from the record, the girl given schooling, the knight restored to honor if she wants it. Everything you've been running from, undone. And all it asks is that you stand where your mother stood, and finish what she started, knowingly this time."

The fire popped. Edmund looked at the paper, at the seal she could feel wanting her eyes, and thought about the valley. Thought about eight years of mended pots and lowered eyes and the particular loneliness of a person practicing being no one. Nobody had ever offered her a place in the world. They had offered her a cage as an apprentice and silence as a fugitive, and now here was a third thing: a chair with her mother's shape worn into it, and warmth, and Pip safe, and all she had to do was hold the leash a monster dangled from and call it steering.

That was the horror of it, Edmund thought. Not that Vessia lied. That she might not be lying. That the arithmetic might be right. That some cold reasonable part of her own mind was already doing the sums — the granaries against the candles going out, the border lords home in their beds against the small gods starving — and finding them uncomfortably close.

"You really think I'd say yes," Edmund said.

"I think," Vessia said, "that you're the daughter of a woman who said yes to save one man's life, and I think you loved her, whatever you tell yourself. Take the night. Read the terms. The paper is honest; I wrote it myself." She rose, smoothed her slate-grey skirt, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame — and for one moment the tiredness fell off her face like a mask slipping, and what was underneath was older and colder and looking at Edmund the way a locksmith looks at a lock.

"One thing more. A courtesy, since I've promised you honesty." Vessia did not turn around. "The crown-thing knows you're in the house. It has known since you crossed the wardline. It has been very patient, because I asked it to be. But it remembers your mother, Edmund, and it remembers the taste of her gift, and patience is not the same as obedience — not even to me. So whatever you decide, decide by morning."

The door closed behind her.

Edmund sat alone with the fire and the sealed paper and the sweet-rot smell that seemed, now, to be getting stronger, seeping up through the floorboards of the great house like something rising from a cellar. Out in the dark, far off beyond the forest, she felt it again — the vast slow attention she'd felt crossing the ridge, the old gods noticing her — except this attention was not old, and it was not noticing.

It was hungry. And it had already begun, gently, patiently, to pull.

* * *

She didn't read the terms. She sat by the fire until it burned down to coals, turning her mother's ghost over in her hands — the crying, the request, find my Edmund and let her be — and somewhere past midnight she understood the shape of the trap completely, and laughed once, bitterly, at the elegance of it.

Vessia hadn't offered her a choice between freedom and service.

She'd offered her a choice between her mother's failure and her mother's chair, and made sure Edmund knew the failure had gotten her an unmarked grave.

Edmund pressed her palm flat against the cooling paper without reading a word, and in the silence of the locked room, faint as a voice through water, something beneath the floor answered her touch — pleased, attentive, and beginning, at last, to speak her buried name.
