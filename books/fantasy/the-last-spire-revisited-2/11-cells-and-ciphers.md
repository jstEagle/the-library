## 11. Cells and Ciphers

Pip counted the stones in her cell because the small gods had gone quiet and counting was better than listening to the silence.

Forty-one across the long wall. Thirty-one up the short one. Seven in the ceiling, and one loose near the floor that she could wiggle with a fingernail if she ever got desperate enough to try. She had been desperate enough twice. Both times she'd stopped, because wiggling it made a sound like a mouse dying, and somewhere down the corridor something wearing boots always turned around.

The cell smelled of wet stone and old straw and, faintly, of the kitchens — bread and onions drifting through gaps in the masonry from some warm place far above. Pip's stomach had given up complaining an hour ago. Now it just sat there like a fist.

"They'll come for us," she said to the wall, in case the wall was listening. Walls sometimes listened even when gods didn't. "Edmund's clever. Maren's got all those knives they didn't find. Brannoc's probably already eaten half the guard."

The wall said nothing. The wall was very good at its job.

She hugged her knees and thought about the last thing she'd seen before they took her: Edmund going rigid as the guards closed in, not fighting, *choosing* not to fight, her eyes finding Pip's over the pikes and saying with them, plain as speech, *don't run.* So Pip hadn't run. It had felt like swallowing a hot coal, standing still while hands grabbed her arms, but Edmund had looked at her like that meant something, so Pip decided it meant something.

The small gods were still gone. That was the worst part. Since the moment they'd passed under the palace walls — since the moment the gates swallowed the cart they'd been thrown into — every whispering presence at the edge of Pip's hearing had winked out like candles pinched by a draft. Even Wren, who never left her, who'd chattered in her ear through every bad night of her life. Gone. Just gone.

"It eats gods," the hearth-god had said, back at the shrine, in its voice like banked coals. Pip pressed her face against her knees.

It didn't eat *all* of them. It couldn't have. Because late on the second night — or maybe the third; there was no window, only a slot where a guard shoved bread — Pip heard something new.

Not a god. Smaller than a god. A sound like a coin dropped down a well, way below the floor.

She put her ear to the stone.

*Tap.*

Pause.

*Tap-tap.*

Pip sat up so fast she cracked her head on the ceiling stone and saw stars, which was fine, because stars were friends.

* * *

The tapping came from below. Not the next cell — below, through the floor, through whatever darkness the palace was built on top of. Tap, pause, tap-tap. Then again. Then again. Patient as rain.

Pip knocked back. One knock, then two, then — because she didn't know the rules and rules had never once saved her — three, four, five, a whole rude drumroll.

The answering pattern stopped. A long silence. Then, slowly, deliberately: tap. Tap-tap. Tap-tap-tap. Like someone teaching.

"Oh," Pip whispered. "Oh, you're doing *numbers.*"

Edmund would know what to do with numbers. Edmund knew letters too, real ones, the kind that lived in books. But Edmund was two cells down — Pip had heard her voice once, hoarse, calling "Pip? Are you hurt?" before boots came and the voice stopped — and the space between cells was full of stone and men with keys who didn't like talking.

So Pip did what Pip did best. She talked to the thing under the floor without knowing what it was.

"I'm Pip," she said, pressing her mouth to the cold stone. "I'm eight and a half. I can whistle through my thumbs. I had six friends but I think you ate them, no offense, or maybe the crown-thing ate them, also no offense, I don't actually know whose fault this is."

Tap-tap. Faster. Almost — amused?

"Right." She rubbed her palms together. "Teach me your numbers, then. Slowly. I'm clever but I'm also starving, so go gentle."

By the time the bread slot rattled open, she had learned eleven taps meant yes and two meant no and seven meant *stop talking, boots.* She used seven twice that night. The second time, the boots paused outside her door for a long, long moment, and Pip held her breath and stared at the ceiling and thought about soup until they moved on.

*Yes,* said the floor, when the boots were gone. *Good.*

"You're welcome," Pip whispered, grinning in the dark like an idiot, because being spoken to by anything at all, even a floor, was the first kindness this place had shown her.

* * *

On the fourth day they took her out.

Two guards, one big and bored, one young with a twitchy eye who kept looking at her like she might bite. They marched her down corridors that got warmer and brighter and busier, past servants carrying linens and logs, past a window where winter lay flat and white and wrong to the horizon — snow with no hills in it anywhere, snow like a lid on a pot.

Then down again. Deeper. Colder. Into a part of the palace where the torches burned blue-green and nobody spoke above a murmur, and Pip realized with a lurch why: the small gods were here after all. Or their ghosts were. This deep, this close to whatever the crown-thing was, they didn't dare make a sound, but she could feel them pressed against the edges of the dark like children hiding in a cupboard, dozens of them, hundreds, holding their breath.

One of them brushed her mind as she passed. Not words. Just a feeling, fierce and fast, like a hand squeezing hers:

*Little speaker. Little speaker, you came, you came.*

"I came," Pip breathed, and the twitchy-eyed guard yanked her arm and said "move," but he couldn't hear what she heard, and for one step, one single step, she wasn't afraid at all.

They brought her to a room with a grate in the floor — iron bars, wide-spaced, set into a channel where warm air rose from the furnaces below. The room was otherwise empty except for a bench and a brazier, and a servant woman in grey with a face like folded laundry, who said, "You'll wait here. Don't touch anything."

"Why am I waiting?"

The woman's eyes flicked to the grate, then away, quick as a fish turning. "You'll wait," she said again, softer, "here."

Pip waited. The woman left. The door didn't lock — Pip checked, casual as anything, leaning against it, and it swung an inch under her hand before she pushed it shut again and pretended to examine the brazier.

She sat on the bench. Above the grate, the rising air was warm on her ankles. She dangled her feet over it and let the warmth soak in, and that's when she noticed the sounds coming up out of the channel. Not furnace-sounds. Footsteps. Slow, dragging, bare feet on stone. And breathing — ragged, wet, like someone breathing around something stuck in their chest.

And underneath the footsteps, faint as memory: tapping. Numbers. Her numbers. Eleven, eleven, eleven. *Yes, yes, yes.* Over and over, urgent now, like a hand waving from a drowning boat.

Pip slid off the bench onto her belly, put her eye to the grate, and looked down into the dark.

A passage ran beneath, lit every few yards by a blue-green lamp. Bare feet crossed it, paused directly under the grate, and stood there. She could see legs — thin, gray-skinned, scarred with old rope-marks at the ankle. Chains dragged from them, heavy links pooling on the stone. Above the legs, too high for her angle, a body she couldn't see. But the breathing was right below her now, and the tapping had stopped, and the whole passage seemed to hold its breath along with him.

"Hello?" Pip whispered.

The chains shifted. A head tilted up into the lamplight.

It was an old man's face, or a starved man's, or both — hollow cheeks, white stubble, lips cracked and split. His eyes were the worst and the best thing about them: brown, ordinary, human, and *burning*, burning with a look Pip had seen only once before, on Edmund's face, the night she'd touched the prayer-stone and heard her own name called out of it.

His mouth opened. No sound came out. He tried again, working his jaw like a man remembering how doors worked, and nothing came — his voice was gone, cut out of him or worn away, and Pip watched him understand that and almost break, and then not break.

Instead he raised one hand — trembling, filthy, nails cracked — and began, very slowly, very carefully, to shape words with his lips. Silent. Exaggerated. The way you talk to someone across a loud river.

Pip read lips. Everyone assumed she couldn't, because everyone assumed everything about her except what she could do. She'd learned it years ago watching merchants argue in market squares, eavesdropping on the whole world through her eyes because her ears caught things ears weren't supposed to catch.

The old man's lips said: *Can you read me?*

"Yes," Pip breathed. "Yes, I can read you."

Something shuddered through the body below — relief, huge, nearly knocking him off his feet. He gripped the wall. Then, deliberately, he began again, each word shaped slow and clean as coins laid in a row:

*Who sent you?*

"A knight," Pip said. "Ser Maren. And Edmund Ravensworth. And Brannoc of the Ashwood Company. We came to fix the king."

Below her, the man went absolutely still.

Then his shoulders started shaking, and for a horrible moment Pip thought he was sobbing, and maybe he was, a little, but when his hand came up to his mouth it wasn't to weep behind — it was because he was laughing, silent, soundless laughter wracking his whole ruined body, and when he got control of himself his eyes were streaming and he looked ten years younger and a hundred years more tired.

He pointed at himself. Tapped his own chest. Once. Twice. Three times, hard.

Then he mouthed, with the slow enormous care of a man saying the most important thing he will ever say:

*I am the king.*

Pip's mouth went dry. "No," she whispered. "No, the king's upstairs, the king's on the throne, everybody says—"

The king shook his head. He crouched beneath the grate so his whole face showed, gaunt and gray and alive, and he cupped his hands around his mouth as though sound could be poured into them, and mouthed, word by word, each syllable a stone laid in a wall:

*Not. Dead. Buried.*

"Buried," Pip repeated. Her voice cracked on it. "Buried how? Who buried you?"

But he wasn't listening anymore. His gaze had gone strange, unfocused, fixed on her — no, *through* her, at something over her shoulder that Pip couldn't see but suddenly, desperately wanted to. His cracked lips kept moving, faster now, and the words tumbled out of him in a rush he clearly couldn't stop, and Pip caught most of them:

*Tell the girl. The god-speaker girl. Tell her the bargain has a seam. Tell her my mother's ring — the chapel — under the third—*

Boots. Far off down the passage, iron on stone, two sets, unhurried.

The king's head snapped toward the sound. In one motion he was up, chains clattering softly, shuffling away from the grate — but he turned back at the edge of the lamplight, and his eyes found hers one last time, and his mouth made three final words with such force that Pip felt them land in her chest like struck bells:

*Find. My. Daughter.*

Then he was gone into the dark, dragging his chains, and the boots came and went beneath the grate without pausing, and Pip lay flat on the warm iron bars with her heart slamming and the taste of copper in her throat.

* * *

They brought her back to her cell and locked the door properly this time, which told Pip the folded-laundry woman had reported the unlocked one, which told Pip the woman had wanted it unlocked in the first place. Allies. The palace had allies in it, hiding in plain sight like the gods hid in the deep places. Servants who left doors ajar. A guard with twitchy eyes who'd slipped an extra heel of bread through the slot and wouldn't meet her own. Somewhere, a woman named Vessia ruled a kingdom through a hollow puppet, and beneath her feet, in a blue-lit tunnel, the real king walked in circles and tapped numbers through the floor and waited for someone who could read him.

That night — Pip chose to believe it was night; she'd decided the bread-slot light meant evening and stuck to it — she knelt by the wall she shared with Edmund's cell and knocked, soft and careful, the pattern she'd spent five days inventing for exactly this purpose: three taps, pause, three taps, pause, one long scrape of a fingernail. *Wake up. Wake up. Listen.*

For a long time, nothing. Long enough that doubt started crawling up her spine.

Then, muffled, groggy, furious with hope: "*Pip?*"

"It's me." She pitched her voice low and pushed it through the gap where the loose stone met the floor. "Edmund. Edmund, listen. Listen to me, this is important, don't interrupt, you're bad at not interrupting—"

"Pip, are you hurt? Did they—"

"*Listen.*" She took a breath that shook, steadied it. "The king's alive. He's under the palace, in the passages under the furnaces. He's thin and chained and they cut his voice out but he's *alive*, and he talks with his mouth like writing in the air, and I can read it, Edmund. I can read every word."

Silence from the other side. Then Edmund's voice, very quiet, stripped down to its bones: "Say that again."

"The king is alive. He is *buried*, not dead. Those were his words. Not dead. Buried."

"And—" A pause. Pip could hear her thinking, could practically see her pressing her palm flat against the wall the way she did when the world got too big. "Did he say anything else?"

"He said tell the god-speaker girl the bargain has a *seam*." Pip frowned, replaying it, hunting the fragments. "And something about a ring — his mother's ring — and the chapel, and *under the third* something, and then the guards came and he ran. He said find his daughter, Edmund. Does the king have a daughter? Edmund, does the king have a *daughter*?"

The silence that followed was different from all the others. It was the silence of a woman standing at the edge of something and realizing the drop goes much further down than anyone thought.

When Edmund finally answered, her voice had changed — steadier, harder, lit from underneath like coals.

"Pip," she said, "tell me everything he said. Every word, in order. Because tomorrow morning they're moving us — I heard the guards — and before they do, we're going to need a plan. And I think," and here her voice did something Pip had never heard it do, something bright and terrible and glad, "I think the king just handed us the knife."
