## 12. Escape by Small Doors

The hearth-god came to her cell on the third night, when the bread had gone stale enough that Edmund was rationing crumbs like a miser.

She heard him before she saw him — a faint crackle in the dark, like a fire just caught. Then warmth bloomed against the far wall where no brazier hung, and out of that warmth stepped a shape no taller than her knee: a stooped figure of ember-light and soot, wearing what might once have been an apron, its edges fraying like cloth burned thin.

"You're smaller than I remember," Edmund whispered.

"Famine," the god said simply. Its voice was the sound of a kettle settling, a log collapsing into good coals. "You fed me at the roadside shrine. Bread and salt and a kind word. Do you know how long it's been since anyone gave me a kind word?" It tilted its head, embers shifting beneath its skin. "Three hundred years. Give or take."

"I didn't do it for payment."

"No," the god agreed. "That's why it counts."

It crossed the cell in three steps and pressed one small hot hand flat against the stone wall. Where it touched, the rock softened — not melting, but remembering, the way dough remembers it was once wheat. An archway opened like a mouth.

"The palace has more doors than it has masters," the god said. "Servants built these passages when servants were allowed to have secrets. Then the masters forgot them, because masters always forget the people who carry their water." It looked up at her, and though it had no eyes she could read, she felt the weight of its regard. "But I remember. Remembering is most of what I am."

Edmund woke Maren first, then Pip, then went to the bar of Brannoc's door two cells down and put her mouth to the gap.

"Brannoc. Wake up. We're leaving."

A long silence. Then, low and rough with sleep: "Leaving *how*? The last count put eight guards on this corridor alone."

"Through a wall."

Another silence. "I'm too old for walls."

The god melted Brannoc's lock the same way it had made the archway, and when he stepped out — big as he was, gray-bearded, moving like a man who expected the floor to betray him — he stopped dead at the sight of the little ember figure and crossed himself with a hand that shook only slightly.

"That's a god," he said.

"That's our way out," Edmund said. "Try to keep up."

* * *

The passages were exactly what the god had promised: narrow, dust-thick, and utterly unmapped by anything living. They crawled single file through spaces built for scullery children and coal carriers, the god leading with its own glow, which it kept banked low — a dull red shimmer, like coals raked under ash.

Edmund went second-to-last, with Pip ahead of her and Maren behind. She could feel Maren's breath on her neck, quick and controlled, the breathing of a soldier counting her way through fear.

"Where does it come out?" Maren asked the dark ahead of them.

"The laundry yard," the god answered. "Then the old well gallery. Then—" It paused. Its glow guttered. "Then we are outside the thing's reach. Mostly."

"*Mostly,*" Brannoc repeated from the rear, his voice flattened by stone. "I do love a plan with a mostly."

"It eats prayer," the god said. "Prayer is attention given a name. In the deep places, where no one attends, it is nearly blind. That is why it never sealed these passages. It cannot see what it does not attend to." A dry crackle that might have been laughter. "Kings forget the small doors. It is how small gods survive kings."

They moved on. Hours, maybe — time went strange in the dark, measured in elbows scraped raw and knees gone numb. Once they passed a junction where warm air rose from below, carrying the smell of bread, and the god stopped and stood very still for the length of ten heartbeats, listening.

"There are ovens working," it said at last, wonderingly. "Someone down there still says grace over the loaves. Old words. Their grandmother's words." The glow around it brightened a shade, just a shade. "I am less hungry than I was."

Pip, wedged between Edmund and the wall, reached out and patted the god's sooty shoulder. "You can come with us," she said. "After. You could live in our fire."

The god said nothing. But the passage got warmer for the next half mile, and Edmund chose to believe that meant yes.

* * *

The laundry yard was a square of flagstones sunk between two wings of the palace, steaming in the pre-dawn cold, empty except for a single drowsy washwoman who never saw them cross it — the god walked ahead and poured itself over the ground like spilled warmth, and where it passed, eyes slid away. Edmund felt the effect like a hand cupped over her face: she could see the washwoman clearly, and knew the woman could not see her back. Being unattended. It was the whole of her life before the valley, and she had missed it desperately, and now it made her skin crawl. She couldn't have explained that to anyone. She barely understood it herself.

The well gallery was worse. It ran along the base of the great keep, open to the sky in slits, and halfway down its length the air changed — went thick and sweetish, like the moment before a fever breaks.

The god stopped so abruptly that Pip bumped into it.

"What," Edmund breathed.

"It attended here." The god's voice had gone very quiet. "Recently. Look at the stones."

Edmund looked. At first the wall seemed ordinary — pale granite, mortar, damp. Then she saw it: frost. Not the honest frost of a winter night, but a lacework pattern spreading across the granite in spirals, delicate and wrong, each spiral curling inward toward a center that wasn't there. She had seen frost like this once before, in her mistress's warning-book, in a chapter headed *Do Not Answer What Knocks Twice.*

"It marked the path," Maren whispered. "It knows something moves through here."

"It knows something *cared* through here," the god corrected softly. "Care leaves a trace it can taste. Your kindness to the refugees. Your feeding of me. Care is the richest food there is, and it has been starving for real care since the day it took the throne." The little god turned its ember face up to her. "It will be watching for you now. Not your name. Your *warmth.*"

Behind them, Brannoc made a sound — short, bitten off.

Everyone turned. He was leaning against the well-gallery wall, one hand pressed flat to his left side, just below the ribs. When he lifted the hand, the palm came away dark.

"When did that happen?" Maren was already beside him, tearing at his coat.

"The ambush," Brannoc managed. "Ashwood. Thought it was closed. It's—" He exhaled through his teeth. "It's been weeping since."

Edmund pushed in close and saw it and wished she hadn't. The wound was a puncture, small as a coin, and it should have scabbed days ago. Instead the flesh around it was white as candle wax, and from the wound itself crept the same spiral frost she'd seen on the stones — tiny, fine as embroidery, lacing outward across his skin a finger's width at a time.

The crown-thing's touch. Lingering. Growing.

"We have to move," Maren said, and her voice was the flat voice Edmund had learned meant terror held under military discipline. "Now. If it can track him—"

"It's not tracking him," Edmund said slowly. She touched the frost-lace at the wound's edge and felt it — a faint pull, like a hook set in flesh, drawing gently toward the keep. Toward the throne above the keep. "It's *reeling* him. Slowly. It found something that cares and it's winding him in." Her stomach turned. "Like a fish that bit too late."

"Then cut the line," Brannoc said. He'd straightened. His color was bad but his voice was steady, and Edmund thought, absurdly, *this is what he was, before. This is what they broke.* "Whatever you have to burn out of me, burn it. I've had worse."

"You haven't," Maren said quietly.

"Well," Brannoc said. "There's a first time for everything. Move."

* * *

They almost made the outer gate.

The god led them up through a final passage that smelled of rain and horse, past a door whose hinges it greased with a touch, into the covered walk along the guardhouse wall — and there the world ended in torchlight.

Six riders in the courtyard beyond, saddled. And walking the line of them, unhurried, wrapped in gray fur, a woman Edmund recognized even from the shadows: Lady Vessia's chief huntsman, the one they called Master Corvane, who smiled like a man reading a pleasant letter. He stopped at the courtyard's center and did not look at the covered walk. He looked at the ground.

"He feels the wound," Edmund breathed. "He's following it."

"Back," Maren hissed. "Back to the passages, find another—"

Corvane raised one hand, lazily, and pointed — not at the walk, but at the well gallery they'd just left. Frost bloomed visibly across the cobbles between the riders, spiraling inward, and where it bloomed, shapes formed: men made of packed snow and stolen prayer, featureless except for the places where faces should be, which were smooth and attentive as eggs.

"They're not alive," Pip said, in the small wondering voice she used for things that weren't there. "They're *leftovers.* Prayers it didn't finish eating."

"Fall back," Brannoc growled, already turning, one hand clamped to his side. "All of you. I'll hold the—"

"You'll do nothing of the kind," Maren snapped.

And then the hearth-god stepped past all of them, out onto the open stones of the covered walk, and let itself blaze.

Edmund had seen it banked and dim for three days. She was not prepared for what it was when it spent everything: a pillar of gold-white fire no taller than a child, and yet the whole walk lit like noon, and the snow-things in the courtyard flinched as one, recoiling from warmth the way drowned men recoil from air.

"Small doors!" the god roared, and its voice was every oven in Aldermarch, every forge, every kitchen hearth from here to the coast. "*SMALL DOORS!*"

And the doors answered.

Every hinge in the guardhouse wall screamed open at once. Every latch flipped. Doors Edmund hadn't known were doors — a woodbin's false back, a chimney flue wide enough for a man, a gate in the stable wall that had bricked-over written all over it and was suddenly, impossibly, not bricked at all — swung wide in a ragged row, and behind each one yawned darkness that smelled of different centuries.

"It can't hold them all open long," the god gasped, its light flickering, guttering, spending itself like a fortune. "Choose! CHOOSE!"

Edmund grabbed Pip's collar. Maren hauled Brannoc bodily toward the nearest opening — the stable gate, wide enough for the big man, breathing cold night air and the smell of straw. They plunged through, and the world behind them became noise: riders shouting, Corvane's voice rising in command, the shriek of the god spending its last coin —

—and then the door slammed shut behind them on nothing at all, and they were standing in a hay barn that existed nowhere on any map, and the hearth-god was a scatter of dying embers on the straw, and it was laughing.

"Told you," it crackled, fainter than a matchstrike. "Masters forget. *We remember.*"

Then it was out, or near enough, a warmth in the straw and a smell of woodsmoke and nothing else, and Edmund knelt in the dark feeling the shape of its absence the way you feel a tooth pulled.

"He'll come back," Pip said confidently, patting the straw. "Fire comes back. That's the whole point of fire."

* * *

They rode hard through the rest of that night on horses the stable had generously not mentioned missing, and by grey morning the capital was a smudge of smoke behind them and the road bent north, toward the high country, toward the last great shrine Maren had spoken of only in whispers: Ysolde of the Long Night.

Brannoc lasted until noon before he fell out of the saddle.

They got him down and Maren stripped the bandage and Edmund looked and looked away and looked again, because there was no denying it anymore: the frost-lace had crossed his whole side overnight, spirals upon spirals, white as bone, and at the outermost edge the skin had begun to go *quiet* — not dead, exactly. Unattended. As if that patch of Brannoc had stopped being somewhere the world bothered to look.

"How long?" Maren asked Edmund, because apparently Edmund was the one who answered questions like this now. She hated it. She answered anyway.

"A week. Maybe less. Whatever it's reeling him toward, it's patient — it thinks it has us. It'll spend him slow." She swallowed. "Unless we get him to a place where its reach ends. The shrine. If any place in Aldermarch is outside its grasp—"

"Ysolde's shrine is a week's ride in good weather," Maren said. "And the weather stopped being good eight months ago."

Above the northern ridgeline, clouds were gathering that had no business gathering in autumn — vast, blue-black, organized. Snow-smell rode the wind down to them, clean and hungry.

Pip tugged Edmund's sleeve. Her invisible friends were out in force tonight; Edmund could tell by the way she kept glancing at empty air, listening.

"Pip?"

"The little ones say the big one's awake early," Pip said. "Ysolde. The winter one." Her eyes went wide, reflecting the coming storm. "She says — she says if we're coming, we'd better come now, because she wants to meet the girl who carries her mother's hands."

Edmund's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

"My mother's *what?*"

Pip just looked at her, and behind her the snow began to fall.
