## 13. The Dying God's Bargain

They found the door to Ysolde's house on the third day of the climb, when the snow stopped falling and started *waiting*.

Maren had climbed mountains before. She'd garrisoned the White Spur for two winters in her service to Lady Vessia, and she knew how snow behaved — how it drifted, how it slid, how it killed the careless. This was different. The snow hung on the branches without weight, and the wind held its breath, and the whole mountain seemed to be listening. Pip kept stopping to wave at things none of them could see, and Edmund had gone quiet in a way that Maren was learning to read as *listening* too.

"It knows we're coming," Pip said, skipping ahead through drifts that should have swallowed her to the hip but didn't. "She sent the snow down to meet us. It's like a carpet."

"Gods don't send carpets," Brannoc grunted. He was walking better than he had any right to, given that his ribs were strapped with linen and his face was still the color of old tallow from the fever. He walked anyway, because Brannoc did not know how to do anything else.

"This one might," Edmund said softly. She hadn't looked away from the summit in an hour.

The door was set into the mountainside itself — no hall, no shrine, nothing built by human hands. Just an arch of blue ice older than any stone Maren could name, tall enough to ride a warhorse through, and it stood alone on the shoulder of the mountain like the last tooth in an old jaw. Through it, Maren could see stars. It was midday.

"She was winter," Edmund said. "Before the crown-thing took the season. She *was* it — not its keeper. Its body." Her voice did something strange on the last word. "That's what my mother's notes meant. The great gods aren't priests of their domains. They're the domains, wearing names so small things like us can talk to them."

"And this one is dying," Maren said. Not a question. She'd felt it since the treeline — a cold that wasn't cold, a hush that wasn't peace. A vastness running out.

"Yes," said the archway, in a voice like a glacier calving somewhere very far away, gentle only by virtue of its distance. "I am. Come in out of the waiting-snow, god-speaker. You'll want to sit. What I have to tell you takes longer than I have left, and you will want every word of it."

* * *

Inside there was no inside. Or rather, there was: a hall of dark ice lit by no fire, floored with no stone, and at its far end a figure on a bench of packed snow — a woman so old her age had stopped meaning anything, wrapped in furs that might have been clouds or might have been the grey light before dawn. Frost feathered across her lips. Her eyes were the white of deep ice, and they were kind, and they were tired beyond anything Maren had ever seen on a living face.

Pip ran straight to her. Of course she did. The child climbed onto the bench beside the dying god as if she'd known her all her life, and Ysolde put one enormous hand — a hand with frost for fingernails — on the girl's head like a benediction.

"You're the one who talks," Pip said. "The little ones talk about you. They say you used to be so loud."

"I was loud once." Ysolde smiled, and ice cracked along a seam of the wall, far off, like distant thunder. "Now sit, all of you. Even you, deserter. Yes, I know what you are. I know what all of you are. Winter keeps accounts."

Maren sat. Brannoc sat, which cost him visibly. Edmund remained standing, arms crossed tight over her chest, the way she stood whenever someone got too close to the truth of her.

"Tell me how to save the king," Edmund said. "You called us here. Your snow met us on the road. Say what you brought me up this mountain to say."

"Direct." Ysolde looked at her a long moment. "Your mother was direct too. It got her killed, though not the way you think." Edmund flinched as if struck. The god went on without cruelty. "Very well. Here is what winter remembers, since no one else alive does.

"Eight months ago, a thing older than your kingdom came to Aldermarch wearing hunger. Not a demon. Not a god. A *gap* — a hole where something was torn out long ago, and holes like that are always hungry. Your queen regent found it, or it found her, and they struck a bargain in the chapel under the keep: the gap would wear the king, and answer the prayers of the realm, and end the plague and the famine and the border war. And it has. You've seen it. Every prayer answered within days. Every crisis solved. Every solution feeding it more prayer, and every mouthful making it stronger and the people weaker, until now it must take the winter itself to keep its strength through the lean months. That is why the snow falls wrong. That is why my little cousins are winking out like candles. It is eating the gods, child by child, prayer by prayer, and when it finishes with the small ones it will come for me, and then there will be nothing left in this country between the people and the dark but a crown that answers."

Brannoc's jaw worked. "And the king? Aldous. Is he dead?"

"No." For the first time, something moved behind the glacier-eyes that Maren, with a soldier's instinct, recognized as fury. "He is *folded*. Tucked into a corner of himself so small he can barely remember his own name. But he is alive, and he hears everything the thing wearing him does, and that, deserter, is the cruelest mercy in this entire affair."

Edmund's knuckles were white. "How do we undo it?"

"The bargain was struck with consent." Ysolde folded her hands, and the temperature of the hall dropped, and Maren understood suddenly that they were being told the one thing the god most hated to tell. "All bargains with things like the gap require consent. Freely given. That is the oldest law, older than me, older than the dark. The consent came from a god-speaker — one of the last — who believed she was saving a dying man's life. She spoke the binding words in the chapel, and the gap stepped through the door she opened, and it has been eating ever since."

"My mother," Edmund said. Flat. Final.

"Your mother. Who was deceived about what would come through. Who died knowing, I think, though I cannot see where or how — the gap eats even my sight near the capital." Ysolde turned those terrible kind eyes fully on Edmund, and Maren saw the girl brace herself like a shieldwall bracing for cavalry. "But hear the rest, because the rest is the price.

"A bargain made by consent can be unmade by taking the consent back. But consent, once given, lives where it was given. Someone must stand in the place of striking — the chapel, under the keep, in the heart of the usurper's court, during some hour when the veil is thin — and speak the unbinding. And not just anyone. It must be spoken by blood and gift. A god-speaker must stand where your mother stood, and take her consent back into her own mouth, and mean it."

"Then we do it," Maren said. "We get Edmund into the chapel. We've gotten in and out of worse—"

"You haven't heard the price," Ysolde said, and the hall went so silent Maren could hear her own heartbeat. "To take back a consent spoken in another's voice, a god-speaker must pour her own gift into the words. All of it. The unbinding is spoken *through* the speaker, and the speaking burns the channel out forever. Whatever she uses, she loses. Whatever she is, in that moment, ends."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, the waiting-snow shifted.

"What does that mean?" Pip asked, small. "What happens to Edmund?"

"It means," Ysolde said gently, "that she will never hear us again. Never feel a prayer arrive. Never speak to anything that isn't flesh. The valley she hid in, where magic dies at the ridgeline — imagine that valley, and then imagine it inside your own skull, forever. She will spend the rest of her life deaf to us. And she will do it knowing exactly what she's giving up, because unlike her mother, she will not be deceived."

* * *

They camped that night in the archway's lee, because Ysolde refused to let them descend before dawn — "the waiting-snow is honest, but it is not gentle" — and the god's people, if you could call them that, brought food: bread that tasted of nothing Maren could name but filled her like a feast, and snowmelt that tasted of being a child again. Small mercies from a dying power.

Edmund didn't eat. She sat apart, at the edge of the firelight, looking down at the world below the mountain — the smoke of villages, the frozen river, the whole suffering breadth of Aldermarch laid out like a map.

Maren went and sat beside her. She didn't say anything for a while. She'd learned that from Edmund, actually — that silence could be a gift instead of a weapon.

"They'll ask you to do it," Maren said finally. "Everyone. Me included. I'm asking right now, just by sitting here."

"I know."

"And I need you to know that I know what it costs. I served Vessia for eleven years. I gave her my sword, my oath, my name. And when I finally walked away, I discovered I'd given so much there was almost nothing left to walk away *with*." Maren turned her hands over, palm-up, empty. "So I'm not going to dress it up. It's a terrible price. It's maybe too high a price. And I am going to ask you to pay it anyway, because I watched what that thing's mercy looks like in the villages, and I would rather lose you than lose to it."

Edmund laughed — a short, broken sound with no humor in it. "There it is."

"There it is."

"You know what's funny?" Edmund said, staring at the fire. "Part of me wants to say yes. Part of me has wanted, since the day I touched that prayer-stone, to be rid of it — the hearing, the voices, the *knowing*. I spent six years building a life where none of it could reach me. And now the universe offers me exactly that, permanently, as a reward for doing the one brave thing of my life." She shook her head. "It feels like a trap. Like the price is shaped too perfectly to fit me."

"Or," Maren said, "it's shaped like every real price. Real prices always fit."

Below them, the waiting-snow began to fall — soft, unhurried, beautiful. Somewhere in it, Maren thought she heard singing, very faint. Pip's friends, perhaps. The small ones, gathering around their dying elder.

"Ask her," Edmund said suddenly. "Ysolde. Ask her if there's another way. Ask her properly, not the polite way she'll want to answer. Because if I'm going to burn out my whole self in a chapel full of enemies, I want to know it was the *only* door, not just the first one offered."

Maren rose, knees cracking, and walked back up to the archway. Inside, Ysolde sat exactly where she'd been, watching the night with eyes going dimmer by the hour. Pip was asleep against her fur-wrapped side, and the god had not moved so much as a finger to disturb her.

"Lady Ysolde," Maren said. "Is there another way?"

The dying god was quiet for a long time.

"When the gap first came," she said at last, "there were nine great gods in Aldermarch. Now there is me, and I am a memory of myself keeping a door open. There is no army. There is no ritual. There is one law, one chapel, one girl, and one price. If there were another way, god-speaker's friend, I would be moving heaven and earth to build it. Instead I am spending my last strength holding open a mountain." She looked down at the sleeping child. "Take the consent back. Take the king out of that thing's teeth. And then—" the glacier-voice cracked, actual ice fracturing somewhere in the walls, "—then let winter go. Let it be weather again. Let it belong to no one. That is my unbinding, and I give it freely, and I would thank you to remember that freedom is the only inheritance worth leaving."

Maren bowed, because there was nothing else to do.

At the fire, Edmund had not moved. But when Maren sat down beside her, the god-speaker spoke without turning, in a voice scraped raw and steady all at once:

"The Feast of First Snow. That's when the veil thins over the capital. That's our window." She finally looked at Maren, and her eyes in the firelight were dry and terribly calm. "Get me into that chapel, Ser Maren. And when I come out the other side deaf to every god in creation — make sure I don't regret it."

Above them, the waiting-snow stopped falling altogether.

And far to the north, over the capital, the sky began to darken hours before it should have — early, unnatural, hungry — as if something wearing a crown had felt them climbing, and was already setting the table for winter's feast.
