## 14. Ashwood Reckoning

Brannoc had learned to read snow the way other men read ledgers, and the ledger was lying.

The tracks were wrong. Not wrong like a man walking backward or dragging a body — he'd seen that kind of wrong and knew its grammar. Wrong like a page copied twice. The same set of boot prints appearing where they shouldn't, at intervals too neat, as if someone wanted the trail found and had grown bored of making it convincing. He crouched at the edge of the treeline with his breath smoking and let the cold climb into his knees, and he thought about who in his company could copy a page.

Nine days out from Ysolde's shrine, six from the ritual site in the high pass, and the company moved like a family that didn't know it was one yet: Maren walking point with her sword arm stiff and her pride stiffer; Edmund quiet as ever but listening now, always listening, to things Brannoc couldn't hear and had stopped pretending not to believe in; Pip riding double on Wren's packhorse because Pip rode anywhere she pleased and the horses had decided to allow it.

And Dell.

Dell had been with him four years. Dell had held the line at Marrow Ford when half the company broke. Dell laughed at the right moments and drank exactly his share and asked about the route only once — which was once more than anyone else had needed to.

Brannoc stood up slowly, joints announcing themselves, and did not look back toward the camp. You never looked at the thing you'd just understood. You looked at everything else and gave your face nothing to read.

* * *

He told Edmund first, because Edmund would tell no one.

They walked apart from the firelight, far enough that their voices wouldn't carry even to god-touched ears — though he suspected by now that distance meant little to whatever lived in her blood. She came without asking why. That was the thing about her that had unsettled him most, back in the beginning, and the thing he trusted most now. Ask her to move and she moved. Ask her why later and she'd wait for later.

"The tracks," he said. "East fork, half a mile back. Someone's been marking our line for anyone following."

"Who?"

"I don't know yet." He turned his hands to the fire's distant warmth. "That's the problem. I know everyone in this company down to what they dream about. And I can't make it be any of them."

Edmund was silent a moment. Snow ticked through the branches above them, soft as ash falling.

"You're looking for a reason," she said. "There might not be one. People sell each other for smaller reasons than we'd like."

"Vessia's riders are close enough to buy, then."

"Close enough." She looked east, where the pass waited, where the chapel stood sealed under Feast-day ice, where her mother's bargain hung over all of them like weather. "Two days. If someone sold us, they sold us before we left the Ashwood. Which means the ambush won't come when we're ready for it. It'll come when we're tired."

Brannoc grunted. "So we're never tired."

"We're always tired," Edmund said, and there was something almost gentle in it. "We just decide which tiredness matters."

He wanted to argue. He was an old soldier; arguing was how he loved things. But she'd said it plainly, the way she said true things, and he had learned — deserting a king teaches you this, if nothing else does — that some truths arrive early and you either make room for them or die denying them.

"Say nothing," he said. "Watch. If I'm wrong, I've insulted no one. If I'm right—"

"If you're right," Edmund said, "then whoever it is has already watched us long enough to know we'd handle it quietly. So handle it quietly."

She turned back toward camp, small and steady against the snow-dark trees, and Brannoc thought: *the king should have had people like this around him.* Then he thought: *the king had me, and I ran.* The two thoughts sat together in him the way they always did, uncomfortable bedfellows, and he followed her in.

* * *

It happened at dusk the next day, in the white bowl of a frozen river valley, and it happened the way she'd promised: when they were tired.

The riders came down both slopes at once — Vessia's grey-and-silver, thirty strong, maybe forty, lances couched, hooves throwing up sheets of powder. Brannoc heard Pip scream a warning before he heard the hoofbeats, because the girl heard things arriving before they arrived, and then the world was noise and white.

"Company!" he roared, and the Ashwood answered. Four years of outlaws do not become soldiers again, but they remember. They remembered. Trees became walls; sledges tipped into barricades; Maren put herself between the charge and the children with the flat calm of a woman doing arithmetic she hated. Brannoc fought the way he'd taught himself to fight since the night he deserted — angry, careful, alive on purpose.

And through all of it, one thread pulled tight in his chest:

The pass. The chapel. The site stood open — Edmund had said so at dawn, had gone still and strange and said *the door is unbarred today, it won't be tomorrow*. Two days of open window in a winter that was closing like a fist, and here was Vessia spending forty riders to shut it.

Which meant Vessia knew the window mattered. Which meant—

Which meant every minute here was a minute the traitor had bought precisely, and somewhere behind them the route to the chapel lay empty, and the mission — the *reason*, the king buried alive, the mother's name written in a dead woman's hand — sat waiting for them like a debt accruing interest.

"Fall through!" he shouted. "Break east! We go around!"

But Edmund wasn't moving east.

* * *

Later — much later, in a barn that smelled of hay and old smoke — Brannoc would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail, because memory is a coward in battle and edits for mercy. What he kept, what stayed in him like a splinter, was this:

Pip, knocked off the packhorse, small and bright and alone on the ice in the middle of the killing ground, a rider bearing down on her with his lance level, and the child not screaming now but standing very still, talking — *talking*, lips moving fast, to something no one else could see.

And Edmund, twenty feet away in the other direction, at the mouth of the escape route, with the whole company funneling past her toward survival and the open road to the chapel beyond — Edmund turning.

Not hesitating. Turning. The way water turns. The way a decision looks when it was made years ago and is only now arriving.

She went back onto the ice. Into the charge lane. Toward the child.

Brannoc saw the rider see her. Saw him adjust — a professional's adjustment, a half-turn of the lance, choosing the easier kill. And Brannoc was too far, thirty feet of churned snow between him and anything useful, and he did the only thing a commander can do in the moment he cannot command anything.

He watched.

Edmund reached Pip, got an arm under her, and did something Brannoc had no craft for and no words for. She *spoke* — one word, one syllable of whatever language lived in her blood — and the ice spoke back. It heaved. Not dramatically; not like a story. Just a ridge of river-ice rising between the girl and the horse like a wall being raised by something patient underneath the world, and the rider's lance shattered against it and the horse screamed and swerved and the moment passed, and Edmund was running again with Pip against her shoulder, and Brannoc's heart restarted in his chest like a struck flint.

Then the company swallowed them, and the valley, and the snow closed over everything like a page being turned.

* * *

They lost three. Harl, and the twins' cousin Osla, and young Tam who'd joined them in autumn and whose real name nobody had ever learned. They burned no bodies — the ground wouldn't take a spade — but they said the words over the mounds of snow, the old ones and the new ones both, because Brannoc believed you buried people with whichever gods would listen, and lately, more of them were listening.

The traitor they found at midnight, sitting by the remnant fire with his hood back, waiting. Dell. Hands empty. Face tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"My sister," Dell said, before Brannoc could speak. "In the capital. Vessia's people have her. Have had her since autumn. They sent proof she was alive with the offer, and proof she'd stop being alive with the reminder." He looked up, and his eyes were clear, and Brannoc hated him a little for making it hard to hate him. "I told myself I'd sell you the road and nothing else. That no one would die. I told myself a lot of things."

Brannoc stood over him a long time. Around them the company watched, and he felt their watching like weight — not waiting for justice, exactly. Waiting to learn what they were.

Maren's hand rested on her hilt. Edmund's hand rested on Maren's wrist, gently, and stayed there.

"He sold the road," Maren said quietly. "People died on it."

"Yes," Edmund said. "And he was trying to save someone. Both things are true. That's what makes it hard instead of easy."

"Easy keeps the company alive," Maren said.

"So does being worth staying loyal to," Edmund said. "Ask yourself which one Vessia's court has. Nobody there stays loyal because they're loved. They stay because they're afraid. Are we going to build the same thing in the woods?"

Nobody answered. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, faint as a thought, Pip was singing to something.

Brannoc looked at Dell for a long time. Then he crouched, so they were eye to eye, the way he'd crouched before a hundred men he'd judged in the king's name, back when judging was his job.

"Here's what happens," he said. "You're exiled from the company. Tonight. You keep your weapons and your share and your life, because you fought beside me four years and because Edmund vouched for your soul, and I trust her judgment more than my own these days. You go south. You get your sister. And if you get her out—" his voice roughened, "—you send word. Because there might be a place in this world after all this is done for two people who chose the hard true thing too late."

Dell wept. Silently, the way soldiers do, like a pipe leaking. He stood, bowed — actually bowed, the old formal bow of the royal guard, which none of them used anymore except Brannoc and now, apparently, Dell — and walked into the dark toward the capital, toward his sister, toward whatever came.

Brannoc watched him go until the snow took him, and then he stood there longer than he needed to, feeling the shape of the choice he'd made, testing it for cracks. It held. Barely. He suspected it would hold differently in ten years, and he suspected that was what a real decision felt like — not settled, just *carried*.

* * *

Edmund found him at the watch fire near dawn. She sat down without asking, handed him tea that tasted like pine needles and forgiveness, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"You went back," he said finally. "For Pip. With the chapel open."

"I know what it cost."

"Do you?" He turned to look at her. Her face was chapped raw with cold, and there was frost in her lashes, and she looked, for the first time since he'd known her, tired all the way down. "Two days, you said. The door unbarred one day and not the next. We spent our day on ice and funerals, and Vessia knows exactly what she bought. Every hour we sit here licking wounds, that thing on the throne gets another hour of stolen prayers, another village frozen, another god snuffed out like Pip's friends. I should be furious at you. A good commander would be furious at you."

"But?"

"But I watched you turn around," Brannoc said. "And I thought: there it is. There's the thing I deserted over. Everyone in the palace talked about the greater good until the words wore smooth as river stones, and not one of them — not Vessia, not the regent's council, not me — not one of us would have crossed twenty feet of open ice for one child. We'd have calculated. We'd have weighed the kingdom against the girl and called the weighing wisdom." He shook his head slowly. "You didn't weigh. That's not a flaw in you, Edmund. That might be the only unbroken thing any of us has left."

Edmund looked into her tea. When she spoke, her voice was low.

"She'd have died," Edmund said. "Or worse. Vessia's people know what she is — what she hears. They'd have taken her apart to find out how she works." A pause. "The chapel will open again. Doors like that don't stay shut forever; my mother's notes say the binding thins at each feast. But Pip doesn't come back. Nothing comes back, once it's spent. I've spent enough things I can't get back. I wasn't going to spend her."

Brannoc nodded, and found his throat too tight for a moment, and covered it with tea.

"There's something else," Edmund said. She reached into her coat and drew out a scrap of paper, creased and thawed and refrozen, edges browned. "One of the riders dropped it. Or was meant to drop it — I haven't decided which, and neither has Maren." She smoothed it against her knee. In the firelight Brannoc could see the writing, fine and precise, and his stomach turned over, because he knew that hand. Everyone in this company who'd seen the ritual script knew that hand.

"It's her handwriting again," Brannoc said. "Your mother's."

"It's a summons," Edmund said. "Written in her hand, in Vessia's words. 'To the last god-speaker: the chapel stands open through First Snow. Come alone, and the child goes free. Come with armies, and we will show you what we did to the king.'" Edmund looked up, and her eyes in the firelight were the color of the ice on the river, clear and cold and lit from beneath. "It's a trap. Of course it's a trap."

"Of course," Brannoc agreed.

"And the chapel opens again tonight," Edmund said, "and we're two days away, and she knows we know it's a trap, which means the trap is the only honest thing left on the table." She folded the paper along its old creases, carefully, the way you fold something that belongs to a dead person. "Get the company ready, Brannoc. We ride for the capital at first light — through the trap, if we have to."

Brannoc looked at her a long moment. Small, exhausted, magicless-in-a-week-maybe, terrified — he could see all of it, now that he'd learned to read her — and underneath all of it, steady as a keel.

"Aye," he said. "First light."

Out past the firelight, the snow kept falling on the graves and the road and the frozen river, and far to the north, in a sealed chapel where a bargain waited to be unmade, a door that had been shut for eight months stood — for one more night — open.
