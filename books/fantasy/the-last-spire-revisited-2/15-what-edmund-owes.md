## 15. What Edmund Owes

The barn smelled of hay and tallow and the sour-sweet stink of too many bodies healing in one room. Edmund had been in it four days, flat on her back on a straw tick, listening to the wind worry at the shuttered window and counting the cracks in the rafters because counting was better than thinking.

There were eleven cracks. She'd checked twice.

Her ribs had been cracked too — two of them, on the left, where a Vessian rider's horse had shouldered her into a fence post. The bones were knitting. The bruise across her hip was going through its colors like a slow sunset. Her body was doing what bodies did, patiently, without asking anyone's permission.

It was everything else that wouldn't knit.

She could hear Pip outside, talking to someone. That was nothing new; Pip talked to someone always. But this morning there was a different note in it, a smallness, and when Edmund turned her head she could see through the gap in the door: the girl crouched by the well with her hands cupped around something invisible, feeding it crumbs from her breakfast as though a starving thing could eat bread.

*They're winking out,* Maren had said last night, in the dark. *The little gods. Ysolde said they'd be first to go.*

Edmund closed her eyes. She owed them. That was the thought that kept circling, patient as a fly. She owed Maren a truth, and Pip a truth, and she had been paying out small coin instead — fragments, deflections, the story of a hedge-witch named Alditha who taught herbs and mending and nothing else, which was true the way a coin clipped round the edges is still money.

Four days of convalescence was enough. Four days of Pip watching her with those too-old eyes, saying nothing, which from Pip was louder than shouting.

She heard the girl come in, and then Maren's boots behind her, heavier, favoring the left knee that ached before every storm.

"Brannoc says the fever's broken," Pip reported, hauling herself up to sit on the edge of Edmund's tick. She smelled of woodsmoke and cold air and, faintly, of something older — incense, maybe, or rain on old stone. "He also says if you die he gets your blanket."

"He can have it," Edmund said. "It smells like him anyway."

Maren lowered herself onto the stool by the hearth-end with a grunt. The knight looked worse than Edmund felt, and Edmund knew what she herself looked like. There was a grayness under Maren's skin that sleep didn't touch anymore.

"You've been composing something," Maren said. "For four days. I've watched you do it. You compose like you mend pots — very carefully, so no one sees the join."

"I don't—"

"You do." Maren folded her hands. "Whenever you're about to lie, you look at people's left ears. You've been looking at my left ear for four days."

Pip giggled. Edmund felt the laugh die somewhere in her chest, and what rose in its place was not anger but a kind of exhaustion so complete it felt almost like relief. *Now,* said the exhaustion. *Or admit you never meant to.*

She sat up, hissed at her ribs, and stayed sitting.

"All right," she said. "You want the join? Here's the join."

* * *

"Alditha wasn't kind," Edmund began, "but she was careful, and for a long time I mistook one for the other."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the loft, a refugee child coughed.

"She took me when I was nine. My mother had been dead three years by then, though I didn't know she was my mother — Alditha called her 'the woman who left you,' and I believed whatever Alditha told me, because that's what children do with the person who feeds them. She taught me letters. She taught me which mushrooms killed and which cured. And she taught me—" Edmund stopped. Started again. "She taught me to listen."

"To the gods?" Pip asked softly.

"To everything. To the way a house holds its history in its walls. To the prayers people mutter into their sleeves, thinking no one hears. Most people's prayers just... evaporate. Mine didn't. When I listened to a prayer, it landed. It stuck to me like burrs." She looked at her own hands, the healer's hands, the mender's hands. "Alditha found out what I was before I did. And for six years that was fine. We were useful. Farmers paid us in eggs to bless their wells. A woman walked nine miles so I could listen to her son's prayer from the war front and tell her if he still wanted to come home."

"And did he?" Pip whispered.

"Yes. He prayed mostly about a girl named Sela and whether sheep got lonely. He came home. They have four children now, probably." Something in Edmund's face almost became a smile and didn't. "That part was good. I want to be honest about that. Six years, most of it was good."

"What changed?" Maren asked. But her voice said *I know what changed.* Maren had been a knight of Aldermarch. Maren knew what crowns did with things that were valuable.

"The king stopped speaking," Edmund said, "eight months ago. And before that — before that, the letters started coming. Not to me. To her. Alditha would read them and burn them, but I could feel the wax seals through the floorboards upstairs. Royal wax. And she got careful in a new way. Careful the way you're careful with a lamb you intend to sell at market — extra feed, extra soft words, checking its teeth."

Pip made a small sound.

"One night I woke up and she was standing over my bed with a candle, just... looking at me. Measuring. And she said, 'You'll be leaving soon, girl. There's a place for you at court. God-speakers are rare, and the crown pays for rare things.'" Edmund's voice went flat and even, the voice she used for prices and weather. "'Pays' meaning it buys. Meaning I'd be inventory. A tool in a box in a chapel somewhere, taken out when the throne needed a prayer answered and put away wet afterward."

"So you ran," Maren said.

"So I ran. In the night, with nothing, because the alternative was being owned. And here's the part I've never said out loud, the part that's been sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone for six years:" Edmund's hands had closed into fists in her lap without her noticing. She made herself open them. "When I crossed that ridge into the valley and the magic died at the ridgeline — when the silence came down and I couldn't hear a single prayer in all the world — I was *glad*. Not relieved. Glad. Because I thought, this is what I deserve. I took the gift my mother bled to give me, and instead of anything holy I let it be sold, or almost sold, and running was the only brave thing I ever did with it, and hiding was all I deserved after. Six years I fed myself that. Silence as punishment. Anonymity as penance. I built a whole life out of it — mending pots, keeping my head down, being no one — and I told myself it was safety." She laughed, short and bitter. "It wasn't safety. It was a cell with a nice view."

The fire cracked. Nobody moved.

"That's why I refused the stone," Edmund went on, quieter now. "That first night, when Ser Maren staggered in dying. It wasn't only fear. Fear I know how to carry. It was that touching it again meant admitting the silence had never actually fixed anything — that I'd spent six years punishing myself for a crime I hadn't committed yet. Alditha never sold me. I ran before she could. And I've been serving the sentence anyway."

Pip had gone very still beside her, in the way she went still when her invisible friends were close and saying something.

"They want me to tell you something," Pip said. "One of them. The small one. The one that lives in hinges." She tilted her head, listening. Then she reached out and took Edmund's fist — the one that had clenched again — and unfolded the fingers one at a time, gently, like opening a stubborn jar lid. In the open palm she placed nothing at all. "She says you held the door shut for six years and you think that makes you the jailer. She says jailers get paid."

Edmund stared at her empty palm.

Something hot slid down her cheek. Then another. She hadn't cried since the ridge — since the flood of magic and terror and the old gods turning their attention toward her like a field of eyes — and she'd sworn she wouldn't cry in front of these two, because crying meant needing, and needing meant staying, and staying meant—

"Oh, Edmund," Pip said, with the terrible gentleness of eight-year-olds. "You're allowed to be loved anyway."

The words went in clean, the way an arrow goes in — no resistance until it's already deep.

"I ran," Edmund managed. "I left. People who leave don't get—"

"You left a woman who was selling you," Maren said. Her voice was rough. "I deserted a queen I'd sworn my life to. Brannoc broke an oath in front of thirty men. Pip's parents—" She stopped. "Every soul in this company is a leaver, girl. That's rather the qualification. We're not the people who stayed and were good. We're the people who left and are trying."

"But the gift—" Edmund pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "Everything it costs. Everything it's already cost. Pip's gods are dying, Brannoc's carrying a wound from that thing, Maren's gray as ash — and half of it traces back to blood like mine, to what my mother consented to, to what I'm apparently *for*. Sometimes I think the kindest thing I could do for everyone is stop being the thing the story needs."

"No," Pip said.

"Pip—"

"*No.*" Eight years old and absolute as winter. "You don't get to decide what you're worth by what you cost. That's Alditha's arithmetic. Selling-things arithmetic." She climbed fully onto the tick and wrapped her arms around Edmund's shoulders, fierce and bony. "You mended my pot the second day we met. Before you knew I mattered. That's not what a tool does."

Edmund held on. Her ribs protested. She held on anyway.

Maren watched them from the stool, and for once the knight's face wasn't guarded — it was simply tired, and fond, and old in a way that had nothing to do with years. "For what it's worth," Maren said, "I've served under a king who answers every prayer perfectly, and I'd take one flawed human being who shows up anyway over that hollow perfection till I die. Which, at current pace, may be soon, so consider the endorsement weighty."

"Don't joke about that," Edmund said.

"I'm not joking. I'm motivating." Maren levered herself up, joints cracking like green firewood. "Eat something today. All of it. Grief goes down easier with bread." At the door she paused. "And Edmund. Thank you. For telling us the join."

Then she was gone, and it was just the two of them, and the fire, and the eleven cracks in the rafters, which Edmund found she could no longer be bothered to count.

* * *

Afternoon came down gold through the shutter slats. Edmund slept without deciding to, and woke to find a bowl of barley soup cooling at her elbow and Pip cross-legged at the foot of the tick, deeply occupied with teaching a card game to three entities who required no cards.

"You're smiling," Pip observed, without looking up. "In your sleep, you were smiling. Hinge-god says it's the first time."

"It is not."

"It might be." Pip laid down a card nobody could see and declared herself the winner, to visible divine protest. Then she looked up, and her face did the thing it sometimes did — shed eight years entirely, briefly, like a snake skin. "The small gods want you to know something else. About what's coming."

Edmund sat up slowly. "What's coming?"

"They've been whispering to each other along the hedges and the hearths and the undersides of bridges. News travels fast among small things." Pip's voice dropped. "Ysolde has opened the way. The bargain-stone stands unguarded tonight — tonight only, while the Feast of First Snow pulls every guard in the capital to the feast-hall. After tonight, Vessia seals the chapel for the winter. Maybe forever."

Edmund's heart began to beat somewhere high and strange. "How far?"

"Three days' ride. Two if we push." Pip gathered up her invisible cards. "Brannoc's already saddling. Maren's arguing with him about whether you're well enough. They sent me to ask you." A pause, and the ghost of a grin. "They figured you'd want to choose yourself this time. Instead of being sold, or running, or being carried."

Edmund looked at her hands — healer's hands, god-speaker's hands, the hands that would stand at the bargain-stone tomorrow night and give back a consent given in blood, knowing what it would cost her.

Three days ago she'd have said she was choosing duty. This morning she understood it differently.

She was choosing them. All of them — the hollow king buried alive in his own body, the starving hinge-god in Pip's cupped palms, the knight going gray, the outlaw with the wound that wouldn't heal, the girl who'd told her the one true thing.

"I'm well enough," Edmund said, and swung her legs off the tick, and stood, and the world held. "Fetch my boots, love. And tell them both—" she smiled, really smiled, and it felt strange on her face, like a door long painted shut finally swinging open— "tell them I'm done owing secrets. From here on, I pay in full."
