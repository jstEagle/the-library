## 16. The Chapel Where It Was Struck

The Feast of First Snow had turned Aldermarch City into a lantern.

Edmund saw it from the crest of the cart road, where Brannoc's hay wagon creaked along among a hundred others hauling festival timber and tallow into the capital. Every window in the lower city burned gold. Paper snowflakes hung from balconies. Children ran the gutters with candles in jars, and somewhere a fiddle was being played very badly by someone very drunk.

"They love him," Pip said softly. She sat between Edmund and Maren on the hay, wearing a borrowed coat two sizes too big and an expression far too old for her face.

"They love what he used to be," Maren said. "They're toasting a memory."

"Memories are still people," Pip said. "Sort of."

Edmund said nothing. She was watching the palace on its hill above the city, black against the darkening sky, one tower crowned with light — the chapel spire. Somewhere under that light was a sealed door, and behind the sealed door was the bargain that had eaten her mother alive, piece by piece, year by year, until there'd been nothing left to send letters home with.

*Tonight,* she thought. *It ends tonight, or I die not knowing.*

Brannoc clicked his tongue at the horses. "Guards at the palace gate are doubled," he rumbled, "but the feast means half of them are drinking. The servants' postern takes flour deliveries all night for tomorrow's bread. After that, it's the undercroft, the old gallery, and the chapel door." He glanced back at them. "And after that, I've no idea. I've never been past the door. Nobody has. Not since they sealed it."

"Then we'll be the first," Maren said.

Edmund felt Pip's small cold hand slide into hers and hold on.

* * *

The flour postern smelled of yeast and wet stone. A servant girl with flour in her eyebrows — paid in Ashwood silver and a promise from Brannoc that no reprisal would ever reach her — let them through and pointed wordlessly down a stair.

The undercrofts beneath the palace were older than the palace itself. Edmund could feel it: the stones remembered being part of something else, some older hall or holy place, and the little gods of thresholds and corners still clung here like moss in shade. Pip walked with her head turning constantly, whispering greetings to empty air, and twice Edmund heard — faint as breath on a winter window — something whisper back.

"You hear them again?" Maren murmured.

"Bits," Edmund said. "Like hearing a song through a wall."

Ahead, Brannoc held up a fist. They stopped. From somewhere above came music — strings, hundreds of guests, the regent's court glittering away the first night of winter directly over their heads.

"The gallery," Brannoc breathed. "Stairs at the end. The chapel's above the old crypt level. One corridor, one door."

One corridor. One door. Eight months of horror behind a single leaf of iron-banded oak.

They climbed.

* * *

The corridor to the sealed chapel was lined with niches, and every niche held a god that wasn't there anymore.

Edmund felt them the way you feel missing teeth. Empty alcoves where household gods had stood for centuries — swept out or eaten, she couldn't tell, but the hollows where their presence had lived were still shaped like them. She trailed her fingers along the stone as she passed, an apology she didn't know how to say aloud.

Pip stopped at one niche and pressed her palm flat against it. "She's still in there," she whispered. "A little bit. Like ash in a grate."

"Come away, love," Maren said gently.

But Edmund stopped too. She looked into the empty alcove and felt something stir — not a voice, nothing so kind. Just a pressure, like the valley's silence pressing in reverse. The crown-thing knew she was here. She was certain of it the way you're certain of weather coming.

It wanted her to come. That was the worst part. Every step down this corridor felt like walking into an open mouth.

"Edmund," Brannoc said quietly. "We can turn around."

"No," she said. "No. My mother walked this floor. Let's finish what she started."

The door was exactly as Brannoc had described it: oak and iron, sealed with lead poured into the hinges' seams, stamped with the royal seal gone green with age — except it wasn't age. Eight months. Lead didn't green in eight months. Something else had done that.

Maren drew her sword and worked the pommel — a knight's trick, a hidden pry-bar in the grip — and set it to the seam. "On three."

The lead gave with a sound like a tooth coming out. Cold air rolled through the gap, air that hadn't moved since spring, and it smelled of candle smoke and dried flowers and underneath it something sweetish and wrong, like fruit going bad in a closed room.

They went in. Brannoc barred the door behind them.

* * *

The chapel was small. That was the first shock of it. Edmund had imagined a cathedral; instead it was a private royal chapel, thirty feet long, windows of plain glass now frosted white with the early winter outside. An altar. Rows of pews. And on the altar —

The bargain-stone.

It was the size of a loaf of bread, a rough-hewn slab of grey rock that might have come from any field wall in Aldermarch, except that it glowed. Faintly, sullenly, like a coal buried in ash. And around it, carved into the altar itself, was writing.

Edmund approached it the way you approach a sleeping animal. The glow brightened as she neared — recognizing her, or tasting her — and words began to surface in her mind without sound, the way prayers do when they're answered:

***Who comes to the stone?***

"A god-speaker," Edmund said aloud, because lying seemed suddenly obscene in this room. "Daughter of Elowen Ravensworth."

The glow steadied. Almost — almost — like relief.

"She stood here too," Edmund said. Her voice cracked on the name. She knelt, laid both hands flat on the altar stone, and read.

* * *

Her mother's hand was unmistakable. Edmund had learned her letters copying it, tracing looped E's and fierce downstrokes while Elowen cooked or cursed or prayed. Now those loops marched across the altar in rows, a contract in the old formal script, and Edmund read it in the cold chapel light while three people who loved her stood watch at the door.

*Let the record stand before gods and men.*

*I, Elowen Ravensworth, god-speaker of the old line, being of sound mind and grieving heart, do give my consent freely.*

*The king is dying. The healers have failed. The priests have failed. I am told — and I believe — that there is a spirit of order, old and hungry, that can wear a willing body and hold it together, and that it asks only consent, freely given, to enter where death waits. It promises: the king will live. The realm will know no famine, no plague, no war while he wears the crown. In exchange it will keep its hunger leashed, take only what is offered, and depart when the body fails naturally.*

*I do not fully trust the ones who brought me this offer. But I trust less the alternative, which is watching a good man die and a bad succession bleed the country white.*

*I consent freely. I ask forgiveness of whatever gods remain if I am wrong.*

Edmund read it twice. Then a third time, because her eyes kept blurring.

*Save the dying king's life.* That was all. That was the whole of it. No summoning, no ambition, no hunger for power. Her mother — sharp-tongued, impatient, secretly tender Elowen, who had once spent a week's wages on medicine for a stranger's cow — had stood in this room and sold herself to save a man she'd never met, because she believed it was the only mercy left.

And then the thing had worn the opening wider. Taken the consent meant for one dying body and stretched it over the whole kingdom. Leashed? It had never been leashed. Departing? The king's body hadn't failed, so it simply stayed, and fed, and grew, and ate gods in niches and prayers in villages and finally ate the woman whose voice had opened the door.

"I ran from her," Edmund said. Her voice sounded very far away. "For six years I thought — she wanted to sell me. That's why I ran. Because I thought I was just... stock. Another thing to trade."

"You weren't wrong to run," Maren said quietly. "You couldn't have known."

"But I was wrong about her." Edmund pressed her forehead against the cold altar. "She did this to save someone. She wrote *I ask forgiveness if I am wrong* and she signed it anyway. She was so afraid, and she did it anyway, and I—" Her breath hitched. "I hated her for six years for being exactly the kind of person who would hate herself afterward."

Pip crossed the chapel and put her arms around Edmund's shoulders, small and fierce. "You forgive her now?" she asked, blunt as children are.

"Yes," Edmund said, and found it was true, and found that saying it loosened something in her chest that had been clenched since the ridgeline. "Yes. Gods help me. Yes."

The stone flared.

* * *

Light poured off the bargain-stone like water off a tipped bucket. The sweetness in the air thickened. Frost crawled up the inside of the windows in fern-patterns, and every candle flame in the chapel bent toward the altar though there was no wind.

***DAUGHTER OF ELOWEN.*** The voice filled the stone walls, filled her teeth. ***YOU HAVE READ THE BARGAIN.***

"All of it," Edmund said. She made herself straighten, made her voice level. "Every word. Including the parts your keeper left out — the leash, the departure, the limits. She consents to save one life. You took a kingdom."

***I TOOK WHAT WAS OFFERED AND KEPT WHAT WAS PROMISED. THE KING LIVES. THERE IS NO PLAGUE IN ALDERMARCH. NO WAR. NO—***

"There's a famine," Edmund said. "You're making it. You're eating the prayers of villages you froze on purpose." She stepped forward, between the stone and her friends. "My mother gave you a dying man's body. You'll give back everything else tonight."

A pause. Then, horribly, the voice softened.

***SHE SAID MUCH THE SAME, NEAR THE END. STANDING WHERE YOU STAND. SHE ALSO CAME ALONE, WHEN SHE COULD HAVE BROUGHT ARMY OR GOD. THEY ALWAYS COME ALONE, AT THE END. WHY IS THAT, DO YOU SUPPOSE?***

Edmund didn't answer. Behind her, she heard Maren's sword clear its scabbard, and Brannoc's low warning rumble, and Pip saying something in a language that had no words.

***BECAUSE IT IS SHAME THAT WALKS ALONE,*** the thing said, gentle as frost forming. ***AND YOU ARE MORE ASHAMED THAN SHE EVER WAS. I CAN TASTE IT FROM HERE. COME. SIT AT MY ALTAR. BE MY SPEAKER, AS SHE WAS. YOUR MOTHER'S PLACE HAS BEEN EMPTY EIGHT MONTHS. I HAVE KEPT IT WARM.***

Above them, faintly, the feast music changed — a fanfare, trumpets. Somewhere upstairs, Vessia was toasting the first snow.

Edmund looked at the glowing stone, and thought about the empty niches in the corridor, and the ash in the grate, and her mother kneeling where she now stood.

"Not alone," she said.

"What?" said Maren.

Edmund turned and smiled at them — shaky, real. "She came alone because she was deceived into thinking she had to. I'm not deceived. I've got an outlaw, a knight, and the loudest orphan in Aldermarch." She looked back at the stone. "And we're leaving now. But I'll be back, crown-eater. When the whole court's watching. When your regent has to answer for what she fed. That's a promise."

The light guttered, furious. The frost on the windows cracked.

And from beyond the chapel door, muffled through eight inches of oak and iron, came footsteps. Many of them. Descending the gallery stairs at parade pace, unhurried, certain — and a voice Edmund recognized from a gilded audience chamber, courteous as ever, calling through the wood:

"Ravensworth. We really must stop meeting like this. Open the door, dear. Or shall I let myself in?"

Lady Vessia had come to her own chapel.
