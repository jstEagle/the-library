## 17. Vessia's Winter Court

Maren had knelt before Lady Vessia a thousand times. She knew the exact angle of the woman's chin when she was pleased, the small crease between her brows when she was calculating, the way her hands folded themselves in her lap like two patient birds. For eleven years those hands had signed Maren's orders, pinned the shield to her arm, and once — Maren had never told anyone this — brushed a stray hair from Maren's cheek and said, *You look tired, Ser. The kingdom sees it, even if no one thanks you.*

She had loved her. That was the rot in it. You could not renounce someone you had merely served. Renunciation required love, or something close enough that the difference didn't matter.

Now she stood in the winter court with frost climbing the great windows of the Aldermarch throne room like ivy, and Vessia sat below the dais — not on the throne, never on the throne, she'd always been careful about that — in a plain chair set one step down from it, as if to say *I am only keeping it warm.* Behind her, on the throne itself, King Aldous sat upright and silent, his eyes open, his face smooth as poured wax.

Maren had spent eight months telling herself the king was ill. Grieving, perhaps. Mad, at worst. Standing here now, close enough to see that his chest rose and fell in perfect even intervals, too perfect, like a bellows worked by machinery — she understood that she had been lying to herself the way a soldier lies about a wound, pressing cloth against it and calling it a scratch.

"You came through my riders' cordon," Vessia said. "Impressive. I assume you killed some of my men."

"Two," Maren said. "They'll be buried properly. I saw to it."

"I would expect nothing less." Vessia's voice was calm, unhurried. That was the thing people mistook for mercy. It wasn't mercy. It was the confidence of someone who had already decided how everything would end. "You're thinner, Ser Maren. Desertion doesn't suit you."

"Desertion." Maren tasted the word. "You want to call it that."

"What else would you call walking away from your sworn charge?"

"The king stopped speaking. The court stopped asking why. Guards who asked were reassigned to posts they never reached. I stayed four months longer than my gut told me to, because I thought loyalty meant not trusting my own gut." She looked up at the throne, at the hollow king with his candlelit stillness. "Then I watched you answer a border raid in an afternoon. No council. No debate. Orders issued before the smoke cleared. And I understood what we were serving now, and it wasn't a king."

Something moved behind Vessia's eyes — not guilt. Recognition. The look of a chess player watching an opponent finally see the whole board.

"Yes," Vessia said simply. "That."

Behind Maren, Edmund made a small sound. Not a gasp. More like someone setting down a weight they'd carried so long they'd forgotten its shape.

* * *

The confession, when it came, did not come like a villain's boast. It came like a ledger being opened.

"You all want me to say I was deceived," Vessia said. "That I made a bargain I didn't understand, that the thing wearing his crown slipped its leash. It would be cleaner that way, wouldn't it? Easier to hate. A monster is simple." She unfolded her hands. "I fed it. Deliberately. Every crisis since the night it took him — every plague scare, every grain shortage, every raid at the marches — I brought it the problem, and it answered before dawn. No committees. No bribed ministers. No war fought over pride because some lord's honor needed stroking. Aldermarch has not lost a single soul to famine since midsummer. Do you know what the last three kings cost us? Do you know how many villages burned because a human king took three weeks to decide?"

"Aldous decided things," Maren said quietly. "Slowly. Wrongly, sometimes. He argued with his council until midnight and changed his mind by morning. He was *human*, Vessia."

"He was slow, and slowness kills." For the first time her composure flickered — not into anger but into something wearier, older. "Do you know what I did the first month? I sat outside his door every night waiting for him to wake up. Waiting for my husband's—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "Waiting for the king to return. And every day the realm bled a little while I waited, and the thing inside him grew stronger, and one morning I realized I had a choice. I could mourn a man who might already be gone. Or I could use what remained and keep two hundred thousand people alive through the worst harvest in living memory." Her gaze moved across them: Brannoc grey-faced with his bandaged wound, Pip clutching Edmund's sleeve, Edmund herself standing like someone who'd walked into a story she'd been written into without consent. "I chose. Someone has to choose. That is the entire office of regency, and every one of you would have chosen as I did, given the same weight on the scale."

"No," Edmund said.

It was the first time Maren had heard the girl raise her voice in the capital. It cracked a little in the middle, and she pushed through the crack.

"You keep saying *the choice*, like there was one choice. There wasn't. You didn't get offered *save everyone* or *save no one*. You got offered a machine that eats gods and answers prayers wrong, and you said yes because it made you fast." Edmund's hands were shaking. Maren noticed she didn't try to hide it. "My mother stood where you're standing. She was told the same beautiful lie. The difference is she believed she was saving him. You know exactly what he is, and you feed him anyway, and you dress it up as arithmetic."

Vessia regarded her for a long moment.

"You have her hands," she said softly. "Lirien's hands. They shook the same way when she was certain."

"Don't," Edmund whispered. "Don't you say her name like you knew her kindly."

"She came to me," Vessia said, "with the ritual in her own writing, and tears on her face, and more courage than anyone I've known before or since. Whatever else happened in that chapel, remember that."

* * *

It was Brannoc who broke the silence, which surprised Maren; she'd expected him to stay quiet, gray and hurting, letting others do the talking. But he stepped forward, boots loud on the frozen flagstones.

"The guards who spoke of the chapel night," he said. "Tomas. Wren. The twins out of Harrowgate. Where are they?"

"Gone," Vessia said.

"That's not an answer."

"It is the truest answer I have. The crown does not permit loose threads. I permitted myself one blind eye — yours, Ser Brannoc, because deserters who run toward trouble are useful — but I could not permit theirs." She said it flatly, without apology, and Maren felt the old sick familiarity of it: this was how Vessia confessed to everything. As arithmetic. "I am not going to perform remorse for you. It would be wasted breath. I will tell you what I am: a woman who found the kingdom dying by inches and fed it to a faster death to stop the bleeding. Judge that however your consciences require."

Pip tugged Edmund's sleeve and whispered something Maren couldn't hear. Edmund went very pale, then nodded, and Pip stepped forward — a child of maybe ten, snow in her hair, facing down the most powerful woman in Aldermarch.

"The little gods say you used to pray," Pip said. "Before. To the hearth-lady at your country house. Every night, real prayers, not court prayers." Pip tilted her head, listening to something. "They say the crown-thing ate her first. Your hearth-lady. It saved your favorite prayer for last."

For the first time since Maren had known her, Vessia's face did something unplanned. It was brief — a flinch, quickly mastered — but Maren caught it, and she understood suddenly that she was looking at the crack in the whole architecture. Not doubt of the arithmetic. Doubt of whether the arithmetic had been worth what it had eaten.

"My lady," Maren said, and her voice came out steadier than she expected, "step aside from the chair."

Vessia raised an eyebrow.

"Not forever. Not into chains, not yet — that judgment belongs to the king, if we can wake him, and to whatever justice is older than law if we can't. Just step aside. Right now. One step back from the regency, one hour, and let us pass to the chapel. If you believe in your arithmetic so much, let it stand on its own without your hand on the scale."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we take the long way around, and more people freeze before spring, and their deaths land on your ledger too. You count everything, my lady. Count that."

The silence stretched. Frost ticked against the windows. On the throne, the hollow king breathed his perfect mechanical breaths, and Maren thought she saw — or wanted to see — one finger twitch.

Vessia looked at the throne. Then at Maren. And something passed across her face that Maren had never seen there in eleven years of service: the expression of a woman looking at the total of her own sum and finding it did not balance.

"You were the best shield I ever had," Vessia said quietly. "Not because you were strong. Because you always knew what you were protecting." She rose from the chair. "Go to your chapel, then. All of you. I won't summon the guard until the feast fires burn low — I find I'm curious what a god-speaker does with an honest altar." Her eyes found Edmund's, and held. "But understand me, Edmund Ravensworth. If you unbind him and the realm burns for it, I will spend my last coin making certain history records whose choice that was."

"It will," Edmund said. "Mine."

* * *

They walked out through corridors hung with banners stiff with cold. At the great doors, Maren stopped, turned back, and found Vessia still standing beside the empty chair, one hand resting on its back like a mourner at a graveside.

She had rehearsed this speech for months, in the Ashwood, on the road, in the dark before sleep. Formal words. Sworn words. Words a knight says when she returns her shield.

What came out was simpler.

"Lady Vessia. Eleven years ago I swore my shield to the crown of Aldermarch, and you stood witness. I held that oath through the plague year. Through the marcher wars. Through every order you gave that I hated and obeyed, because the oath was bigger than my comfort." She drew a breath. "But the oath was never to *you*. It was to the crown, and the crown has been eaten, and the woman who fed it to the eater has no claim left on any sword I carry. So hear it spoken, outside any courtroom, under whatever gods still listen: I, Maren of the Vale, renounce my service to Lady Vessia, regent of Aldermarch. My shield is forfeit to her. My sword is not — it goes home to the crown, when there's a crown worth carrying it for. May you answer for Tomas and Wren and the twins from Harrowgate. May you answer for the hearth-lady of your own childhood, most of all."

She unbuckled the shield from her arm — the queen regent's colors, blue and silver — and set it on the frozen flagstones between them. It rang against the stone like a struck bell.

Vessia looked down at it. When she spoke, her voice was almost gentle, which was somehow worse than anything cruel she might have said.

"I know exactly what I'll answer for, Ser Maren. I've kept the list myself. It's very thorough." She straightened. "Run along to your chapel. And Maren — when it's done, whatever happens — I hope you find the crown worth carrying that sword for again. I genuinely do. It's the one prayer I have left, and I suspect no one is listening."

Maren left the shield where it lay and walked out into the falling snow, and did not look back.

Behind them, faint through the closing doors, they heard Vessia give one order to her steward, quiet as a confession:

"Open the feast halls. Let them eat. All of them. Tonight, let the whole city eat."

Edmund fell into step beside Maren, snow gathering on her shoulders. "Was that enough?" the girl asked. "Renouncing her. Will it matter?"

Maren watched her breath cloud and fade. Ahead, the chapel loomed against the grey sky, its sealed doors waiting, and somewhere behind those doors lay the bargain-stone, and her mother's voice, and the price.

"No," Maren said honestly. "Words don't bind her. But they bound *me*. That's what renunciation is — it's not a sentence on the guilty. It's cutting the rope so you can swim." She put a hand briefly on Edmund's shoulder. "Come on. We've got a feast to hide inside and a king to dig out of his grave. And Edmund — whatever your mother left in that chapel, whatever it asks of you—"

"I know," Edmund said. "I've known since Ysolde named the price."

She walked on toward the doors, and the snow swallowed the sound of her footsteps, and Maren followed her in.
