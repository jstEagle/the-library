## 19. The Long Night Turns

Pip woke because the cold had changed its mind.

That was the only way she could put it, and she'd spent the whole night trying to find a better way and failing. Cold was a thing with teeth in Aldermarch now — it bit through walls, through blankets, through skin, looking for the warm parts to eat. But this morning the cold lay on the city like an old dog across a doorstep: heavy, but harmless. Sleeping. Not hunting.

She sat up in the loft of the tanner's house they'd borrowed — borrowed meaning nobody was alive inside it to argue, which Pip tried not to think about too hard — and pressed her ear to the rafters.

*Hello,* said something small and dry, up near the chimney. A house-god. She hadn't heard one in days. The little gods had been going out like candles, one by one, all winter.

"Hello," Pip whispered back, and her throat went tight, because it answered.

*The tall one is coming,* it said. *The tall sad one.*

"That's Brannoc," Pip said. "He's not sad. He's just built like a dropped wall."

The god made a sound like dust settling, which Pip had learned was laughter, and she scrambled down the ladder still pulling her boots on.

* * *

Brannoc's wound was weeping again.

Pip found him sitting on a bench by the kitchen fire with his shirt off, staring at his own side the way you stare at a debt you can't pay. The wound the crown-thing had given him didn't bleed right. It bled slow and grey-edged, and no poultice touched it, and every morning it looked a little less like a cut and a little more like a door that something had once walked out of.

Edmund was already there, kneeling, hands hovering over it without touching. Maren stood behind them both with her arms crossed, wearing the face of someone counting seconds.

"It's not worse," Edmund said.

"It's not better."

"It's not worse." Edmund said it like she was arguing with winter itself. "Whatever fed on him is starving. It has to be. After the chapel—" She stopped. Everyone stopped at that word now. *After the chapel.* Two days since Edmund had spoken the unbinding in her mother's voice and burned her gift down to ash doing it, and the whole world had tilted, and none of them quite knew how to stand yet.

"I'm fine," Brannoc rumbled.

"You're not."

"I've been not-fine since before you were born, girl. I'm used to it."

Pip hopped up onto the bench beside him before anyone could stop her, which nobody ever could, and put both her small cold hands flat on the grey-edged wound.

She wasn't a god-speaker. That had always been the joke of her — she talked to things that weren't there, everyone said, except they *were* there, and she'd never needed a bloodline or a blessing to hear them. She was just a girl who listened. And what she heard when she touched Brannoc's wound was silence where there should have been a whisper, a hole where there should have been hum, and underneath the silence — faint as a knock through a thick wall — something old and enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Oh," Pip said. "Oh, she's awake. The big one. Ysolde."

Everyone looked at her. They always did that, like she was a window and they were checking the weather.

"She says—" Pip frowned, listening harder, tilting her head the way she did. "She says the winter was never hers anymore. It was being worn. Like a coat. Like a *skin*." Her voice went quiet without her deciding to. "And she wants it back."

* * *

It happened at noon, and Pip saw all of it.

They'd gone up onto the walls — Brannoc insisted, said if the sky was going to do anything he wanted to see it come — and the whole broken city seemed to have the same idea. People stood in the streets with their faces tipped up. Refugees from the frozen villages, servants from the palace who'd slipped away the moment the king screamed himself free, soldiers who'd stopped saluting Vessia's captains and started simply standing around looking lost. All of them watching the low iron sky.

And the sky *opened*.

Not sun — Pip would have understood sun. This was different. The clouds thinned like breath fading off glass, and behind them was a light that didn't come from anywhere, a grey-gold brightness like dawn remembered instead of seen. And in that light, snow began to fall — but soft. Slow. Fat flakes drifting down like feathers, like ash from a fire that had finally gone out, and where each flake landed it *melted*, and where it melted the ice let go.

Pip heard the sound of the river under the bridge ice cracking loose and starting, groaning, to move.

She heard the little gods wake up all over the city — dozens of them, hundreds, a chorus of small dry voices saying *oh* and *ah* and *at last* and, from somewhere near the granary, a very old door-god sobbing with relief until Pip had to laugh.

And she felt Ysolde pass overhead.

There was no seeing her, exactly. There was just a moment when the falling snow bent around a shape the size of weather itself, and the whole city held its breath without being told to, and Pip felt a vast attention sweep across the rooftops like a hand smoothing a bedsheet — and pause. On her. Just for a heartbeat.

*You listened,* Ysolde said. Her voice was what Pip imagined the inside of a glacier sounded like, if glaciers were kind. *When everything else went out, you kept listening. Small ears are how the gods survive. Remember that.*

Then she moved on, northward, easing the winter behind her like a woman gathering up a skirt she'd lent out and never meant to give away, and Pip stood on the wall with snow melting on her eyelashes and cried without any idea why, and didn't care.

* * *

The king came to the great hall three days later, on his own feet, which surprised everyone including — by his face — himself.

Aldous of Aldermarch had been buried alive inside his own body for eight months. Pip had seen him through a grate once, mouthing words only a god-speaker could read: *not dead, buried*. Now he walked the length of his hall leaning on a stick and Maren's arm, thin as a bundle of kindling, hair gone white in patches, and when he reached the empty throne he did not sit in it.

He stood next to it. Pip thought that was the most important thing anyone had done all year.

"People of Aldermarch," he said, and his voice cracked and wobbled and he kept going anyway. "I don't remember most of the winter. I remember pieces. I remember hearing prayers and being unable to answer them, and I want you to know that I heard every one." His hand tightened on the stick. "I am told things were done in my name. Terrible things. I believe it, because I can feel the shape of them in me like missing teeth. So I will not tell you I was innocent. I will tell you I intend to find out exactly what was done, and by whom, and I will do it in the open, in this hall, where anyone may come and speak. Outlaw. Servant. Soldier. God, if any of you are listening." He paused, and something flickered across his hollowed face that might have been a smile. "I'm told some of you are."

Pip, standing on a bench at the back between Brannoc and Maren, felt about forty small voices go *he means us* at once.

"He's going to be all right," she whispered.

"He's going to be *exhausting*," Brannoc muttered, but his voice had gone rough, and Pip leaned against his arm and pretended not to notice.

The testimony began that same afternoon, and it went on for days.

Servants came first — the ones who'd carried food to locked rooms, who'd seen guards vanish, who'd kept ledgers in cipher because writing things plainly got people killed. Then soldiers, one after another, some weeping, some stone-faced, describing the night in the chapel and the queen regent's smile. Then folk from the frozen villages, walking in out of a thaw that was eating the drifts week by week, telling what it was to pray for your child's fever and feel something answer with cold.

And then, on the fourth day, a god gave testimony.

Pip had known it was going to happen; the old door-god from the granary had been rehearsing for two days straight. But knowing didn't prepare the hall for the moment the great doors swung open on their own and a voice like hinges spoke — not through Pip, not through any speaker, just *spoke*, into the air, while every candle in the room bowed its flame sideways.

*I kept the granary doors for six hundred years,* it said. *This winter, I starved. Your king was stolen. Your regent fed the thief. I ask only this: count us. Count the shrines left standing and the shrines torn down, and let the counting be public, and let it be law. We do not need worship. We need remembering. It is the same food, but it keeps longer.*

The hall sat in absolute silence. Then an old woman in the third row — a baker, flour still on her sleeves — stood up and said, "I remember you. You opened for my mother during the flood year." And the door-god made a sound like a latch clicking home, gentle as anything, and Pip had to put her face in Brannoc's sleeve for a minute.

* * *

On the sixth day, Brannoc's wound closed.

Pip was the first to see it. She'd taken to checking every morning, hauling herself up beside him at breakfast without asking, peeling back the bandage with the shameless efficiency of a much younger sister. And this morning the grey edges were gone, and the skin beneath was new-pink and ordinary and whole, and when she pressed her palm flat against it there was nothing underneath but warmth and a heartbeat and a faint far-off whisper she recognized — Ysolde's attention, passing south, checking on her work.

"Healing!" she announced, loud enough to turn heads all down the table. "Proper healing! No more grey bits!"

Brannoc looked down at it for a long moment. He was not a man whose face did much, but Pip had gotten good at reading the small movements, and what crossed it now was something old and heavy setting itself down.

"The old gods are back," he said quietly, "and the first thing they did was mend a deserter."

"They mended a man who stayed," Pip corrected. "Different thing."

Maren, across the table, snorted into her porridge. Edmund — pale still, quieter than before the chapel, hands sometimes rising toward things they couldn't hear anymore and then falling — smiled at Pip across the bread, and it reached her eyes, mostly, and Pip decided that counted.

* * *

But it was evening, on the steps of the hall, that Pip heard the thing she wasn't supposed to hear yet.

The thaw had brought messengers with it — riders on mud-splashed horses, letters under seal, the kingdom waking up and wanting to talk to itself. One rider had come in at dusk, hard-pressed, snow-melt streaming off his cloak, and handed a letter to the king's steward, and the steward had read it and gone very still, and then hurried inside.

Pip was on the steps because she liked the steps; the step-gods gossiped, and theirs had opinions about everyone.

*Bad news coming,* the step-god murmured under her. *Cold news. News shaped like a woman.*

"What kind of woman?" Pip asked, too low for the guards to hear.

*The kind who writes letters instead of confessing.*

Inside, through the great doors left open against the warmth of the crowd, Pip could hear the king's voice rise — not angry, worse than angry, careful — and then Maren's, sharp, and then a silence with weight in it.

Brannoc came out and stood beside her a moment, arms folded, watching the muddy street.

"Well?" Pip said.

"Letter from the tower where they're holding Lady Vessia." His jaw worked. "She's asking for Edmund. By name. Says she'll speak the full truth of it — the ritual, the feeding, all of it — to one person only. Says if the answer's no, the truth goes in the ground with her."

Pip looked up at him. Down the hall behind them, she could see Edmund by the fire, laughing at something a servant girl was showing her, hands loose and easy for the first time in weeks. Magicless. Lighter. Done, she'd said. *Done with crowns and chapels and other people's sins.*

"Pip," Brannoc said. "Don't—"

But Pip was already turning toward the fire, because she knew — the way she always knew, the way the small gods knew, the way somebody in every story has to know first — that Edmund had heard her name, and that whatever peace she'd found on those steps was about to be tested by a woman in a cell who had run a kingdom on stolen prayers and never once said sorry.

The step-god sighed under Pip's feet, creaking like an old chair.

*Told you,* it said. *Cold news. Woman-shaped.*
