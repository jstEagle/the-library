## 20. Judgment Without a Throne

They held the judgment in the great hall, because that was where judgments had always been held, and King Aldous — thin, grey, still learning how to be a man again — wanted it that way. He wanted witnesses. He said so himself, in his first public words in over a year: *Let them see it done in daylight.*

Edmund stood at the edge of the crowd with her hands folded to keep them from shaking.

The hall had been scrubbed of winter. Servants had pried the frost-ferns off the windows and burned the prayer-ledgers in the yard; she could smell the ash through the high slits of glass, a smell like burnt sugar, like something sweet finally allowed to rot. Ysolde's winter still lay on the city outside, but it was an honest winter now, falling at its own pace, melting where it pleased. The small gods were coming back. Pip had spent the morning introducing Edmund to a hearth-sprite living in the kitchen chimney who was, apparently, *delighted* about everything.

But here, in the hall, under the banners of Aldermarch, there was only Vessia.

She came up the aisle between two guards, stripped of her regency the night before — no crown, no chain of office, just a plain grey dress that made her look smaller than Edmund remembered. Smaller, but not diminished. She walked like a woman attending someone else's wedding: composed, faintly bored, entirely present.

Brannoc stood among the former guards near the front, his arm bound where the crown-thing's touch had lingered longest. The wound had closed at last, days ago, when Ysolde took back her season — but he kept his hand near it out of habit, the way you touch a tooth that has stopped aching. Maren stood beside him in a plain soldier's coat, having formally renounced her oath in front of three hundred witnesses and then, immediately after, wept in a stairwell where she thought no one saw. Pip sat cross-legged on the dais steps next to the king's chair because nobody had the heart to move her, and because the king had asked for her specifically.

*You're the one who heard me,* he'd told her. *Through the grate. You and one other.*

Edmund had been that other. She could have claimed the seat beside him. She had not. Some distances felt safer from the edges of rooms.

King Aldous rose. He moved carefully, a man relearning the weight of his own skeleton, and when he spoke his voice was hoarse and unhurried, the voice of someone who intended never to waste a word again.

"Eight months," he said. "I was buried alive in my own body for eight months, and every day I watched what was done in my name. I have heard testimony for six days now. Outlaws and servants and cooks and gods." A ripple went through the hall at that last word; a hedge-god of thresholds sat by the door, politely visible, taking notes on a slate. "I am not going to pretend I can weigh all of it alone. No one should carry that alone. That is the whole lesson, isn't it."

His eyes found Vessia.

"Lady Vessia. You ruled in my name while I could not rule at all. The court asks me to condemn you. Half the court asks me to execute you. I would like to know what you have to say before I decide whether either of those things would mean anything."

Vessia inclined her head. When she spoke, her voice carried the way it always had — level, precise, the voice of a woman who had never once needed to shout.

"Majesty. I will not beg, and I will not lie, so this will be brief. Yes, I brought the thing into the chapel. Yes, I fed it. I knew within a month what it was and what it ate, and I chose to keep feeding it." Murmurs. She let them die. "And I would do most of it again, which is why killing me accomplishes nothing. Execute a woman, and you've executed a woman. But the famine ended in eleven weeks. The border war ended without a battle. The plague in Marrowford — the one that killed four thousand people the year before — it stopped in nine days. Nine days. Because something on the throne answered prayers faster than any living king ever could, and didn't need to sleep, or grieve, or doubt."

Her gaze swept the hall, and then, deliberately, found Edmund at the edge of the room.

"I did not do it for power. Power was the tool. I did it because I watched a good king deliberate for four years over a grain tariff while children starved on roads he rode past hunting season after season. I did it because deliberation is a luxury paid for by whoever can't afford to wait. Something answered. I let it answer. And I would let it answer again, and I suspect—" the faintest tilt of her head toward Edmund, "—so would our guest here, if the starving were hers."

The hall turned. Three hundred faces, and Edmund felt every one of them.

She hadn't expected to be called into it. But of course Vessia would call her into it — that was the whole architecture of the woman's argument, that complicity was universal and therefore excusable, that everyone would have opened the chapel door if they'd seen what she'd seen.

Aldous looked at Edmund too, and his eyes were tired and kind and terribly awake. "Edmund Ravensworth. You unbound it. You heard the bargain and took your mother's consent back. If you have words for this judgment, they belong here."

Edmund's mouth was dry. She thought about staying silent, about being a pot-mender from a quiet valley, about the beautiful anonymity she had once fled into like a house with the door barred.

Then she thought of Pip's face when the small gods began winking out. Of Brannoc's wound that wouldn't heal. Of her mother, deceived, believing to the end that she was saving a kingdom. Of Maren in the stairwell, weeping for an oath.

She walked forward. The crowd parted the way crowds do, reluctantly, curious.

"You want honesty," Edmund said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Here's mine. Lady Vessia asked whether I'd have done differently with the same power. Everyone in this hall wants me to say yes. It would be tidier."

Vessia's expression didn't change. But something behind it leaned forward.

"The truth is I don't know," Edmund said. "I stood in that chapel and read what my mother consented to, and I understood her completely. She watched a dying king and a frightened kingdom and she gave what she was asked to give, freely, because she believed she was saving lives. She wasn't wicked. She was *kind*, in the way that gets used. And if I had stood where she stood — young, grieving, told the price was small and the good was enormous — I might have signed the same page." She paused. "I don't say that to excuse anyone. I say it because it's the actual truth, and I think everyone here knows some version of it. Every person in this hall has, at least once, chosen the fast mercy over the slow right thing. Most of us just never had the power to make it permanent."

Silence. Somewhere near the ceiling, a raven shifted on a beam.

"So no," Edmund said, turning to face Vessia fully. "I don't know that I'd have done differently. And that is exactly why neither of us should hold it. Not you, not me, not any single soul, however kind, however clever, however certain. The moment one person answers every prayer alone, they stop being a person answering and become a thing that answers, and things that answers eat the world. You proved it. I nearly proved it twice — once as a girl who almost sold her gift, and once last week, when I stood at the bargain-stone with everything undone and thought, for one black moment, *I could take it instead.*"

That was the confession no one had asked for. She felt it leave her like a splinter drawn out.

"I didn't," she said. "Because of the people in this room. Because someone taught me I'm allowed to be loved anyway. So here's my judgment, Majesty, for whatever it weighs against three hundred others: don't kill her. Killing her tells us the problem was one woman, and it wasn't. It was loneliness wearing a crown. Make her pay in the only coin that costs her anything."

Aldous studied her for a long moment. Then he looked at Vessia. "And what coin is that?"

"Service," Edmund said. "Restitution. Let her rebuild what the winter broke — with her hands, under watch, for as long as it takes. Village by village. Let her stand in the market squares she froze over and listen to what her order cost. Let her be *slow* for the rest of her life. No more answered prayers. Only work."

Vessia laughed — short, genuine, surprised out of her. "That is crueler than the block."

"It's meant to be," Edmund said.

The king rose. His legs shook and he gripped the arm of his chair and did not hide it.

"Hear the judgment of Aldermarch," he said, and his cracked voice filled the hall. "Lady Vessia is stripped of title and lands, which is already done. She is sentenced to service — to the restoration of every settlement harmed by the false winter, beginning with Marrowford, whose dead will be named aloud, one by one, until the list is finished. Her conduct shall be reviewed yearly, by a council of commons, clergy, and— " he nodded toward the threshold-god, who looked up from its slate, "—the gods, who were owed an apology long before this trial. There will be no execution. There will also be no forgetting. So let it be written, and let it be *slow.*"

The hall exhaled. Some of it was relief; some of it was fury that would take longer to settle; both were fair. Brannoc's jaw was tight, and Edmund caught his eye and shrugged slightly, as if to say *it's the best I had*, and after a moment he nodded once, the nod of a man accepting an imperfect peace because he'd buried enough perfect vengeance to know what it grew.

Vessia was led away down the aisle, past Edmund, and she slowed — just barely — as she passed.

"Clever answer," she murmured. "You'll find out eventually whether it was true."

"I already know it wasn't tidy," Edmund said. "That's enough for now."

* * *

That evening the palace kitchens smelled of bread for the first time in a year, real bread, risen slowly, because the hearth-sprites had come back to the ovens and refused to permit shortcuts. Edmund sat on a bench near the fire with a bowl of stew, and Pip dropped down beside her with the particular thud of a child claiming territory.

"You told the truth in there," Pip said. "Even the ugly bit."

"Especially the ugly bit."

Pip swung her feet. "Ysolde says ugly truths are load-bearing." She paused, then added, in the careful voice she used for questions that mattered: "Are you scared? That you almost took it? At the stone?"

Edmund looked into her stew. "Terrified."

"Good," Pip said briskly. "Maren says scared people check themselves. It's the ones who are sure that you watch." She leaned her head briefly against Edmund's shoulder, a gesture as quick and light as a bird landing, then was gone again, off across the hall toward the king, dragging Maren by the sleeve to meet the chimney sprite.

Edmund stayed by the fire a while longer.

In her pocket, her fingers found a letter — sealed with the king's own signet, delivered that afternoon by a servant who wouldn't meet her eye. She hadn't opened it yet. She knew the seal, and she suspected the offer inside: a place at court, a title, a tower room, the keeper of god-speakers now that god-speakers mattered again. Everything she'd spent six years hiding from, offered back to her on clean parchment.

She turned it over once in her hands. Outside the window, snow fell honestly onto a city learning to be cold the ordinary way, and far beyond the ridgeline, a valley waited, quiet as a held breath, keeping her pots and her peace and — she was almost ready to admit it — her people.

Tomorrow, perhaps, she would open the letter.

Tonight she finished her stew, and let the fire do the talking.
