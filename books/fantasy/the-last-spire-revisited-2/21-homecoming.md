## 21. Homecoming

They offered her a title on a Tuesday.

That was the thing about the new court — everything happened in daylight now. Vessia's winter court had conducted its business at midnight, by candlelight, behind doors that locked from the inside. King Aldous — thin, gray-voiced, forever flinching from loud sounds — held his audiences at midmorning with the shutters open, so the sun fell across the floor of the great hall and everyone could see everyone's face. It was a small reform. It was also the most radical thing anyone had done in Aldermarch in eight years.

"Keeper of the Quiet Valley," Chancellor Ives read from the scroll, his voice carrying to the assembled court. "With authority over the ridgeline territories, a stipend from the crown, and a seat at this council whenever she chooses to take it."

Edmund stood in the middle of the hall in the same coat she'd worn over the ridge the first time, patched at both elbows, and felt every eye in the room settle on her like weather.

*Say no,* said something in her chest that wasn't magic anymore. It had never been magic. It was just the part of her that had survived.

She looked at the king. He sat very still in his chair, hands folded on the arms like a man afraid of breaking them. Eight months hollowed out and replaced, and here he was, awake, weeping sometimes without warning, insisting on hearing every testimony himself even when it made him shake. He met her eyes now. He'd done that since the unbinding — looked at people directly, hungrily, as if faces were food he'd been starved of.

"Edmund," he said. His voice was rough, underused, honest. "You don't owe me anything. You know that. You've already paid."

"I know." She turned to the chancellor. "I'll keep the valley. That part I accept — someone should mind the ridgeline, and the folk coming over it. But not the stipend. And not the seat."

Murmurs moved through the hall like wind through wheat. Somewhere near the back, an old servant woman who'd smuggled bread into their cells smiled and didn't try to hide it.

"The council needs voices it can trust," Ives pressed gently.

"You have Brannoc."

Brannoc, standing at the hall's edge where ex-soldiers always seemed to end up, shifted his weight off his bad leg. His wound had finally closed — truly closed, two weeks after Ysolde's return, when the last of the crown-thing's cold had gone out of it — but it had left him with a stiffness that would never fully leave, a permanent reminder written into his body. He scowled at being named, which was how he expressed most feelings.

"He's better company than me anyway," Edmund said. "I mend pots."

The king laughed — a startled, rusty sound, like a door opening after years shut — and for a moment the whole hall breathed easier, because if the king could laugh then maybe the country could too.

"I'll take the valley," Edmund said. "The rest is yours."

* * *

They left three days later, before the court could organize itself around them again. Edmund had learned that much about institutions: they didn't devour you all at once. They just kept offering you chairs until one day you were sitting down and couldn't remember standing up.

Pip rode in the cart with her feet dangling off the tailboard and her invisible friends arranged around her like a delegation. She was nine going on ten now, or she claimed to be, and she'd grown half a head in the months since the Ashwood, which she attributed to "being fed regularly and not being chased," delivered with the flat certainty of a physician stating a diagnosis.

"Ysolde says the roads will be clear past Harrow Ford," Pip announced. "She says winter's going to be *normal* this year. Boring winter. She's very proud of boring."

"Boring winter," Edmund agreed, flicking the reins. "Best kind."

Maren rode alongside on her bay mare, upright out of habit rather than comfort. The renunciation had cost her something no healer could name — twenty years of sworn service, unwound in a single sentence spoken before the court — and some days she wore the loss like armor and some days like a stone in her boot. Today it was armor. "Harrow Ford's got a shrine," she said. "Small one. Hearth-god, I think. We could stop."

"We could always stop," Pip said. "You're always finding shrines."

"Somebody has to feed them. They're all skin and bones." Maren's mouth twitched. It was the closest she came to a joke, these days, and Pip rewarded it with the solemn nod of a girl who understood that some people's laughter arrived late and needed coaxing.

Brannoc brought up the rear on a borrowed plow horse, which offended him visibly and which he refused to swap for anything faster, on the grounds that "a horse that's been lied to its whole life deserves honest work." His saddlebags held more than anyone else's: letters of commission, a warrant signed in the restored king's own shaking hand, and — though he'd deny it to his grave — a carved wooden bird Pip had made him, tucked into the lining like a saint's medal.

They were four on a road in early spring, and the world smelled like mud and thaw, and Edmund caught herself humming.

She stopped, surprised. She hadn't hummed since before the valley. Since before her mistress had looked at her across the herb-drying racks and said, in that reasonable voice, *There are buyers in the capital who would pay a fortune for what you have, child, and you'd be stupid and I'd be cruel not to arrange it.*

"What?" Pip asked, catching her expression.

"Nothing." Edmund let the humming come back. "Just a song my mother used to sing. I'm remembering it wrong, probably. But I'm remembering it."

* * *

The ridge appeared on the fourth day, blue-gray against the sky, wearing a ragged collar of melting snow.

Edmund reined in and just looked at it for a while.

She had crossed this line twice now. The first time, fleeing downhill with nothing, trading her gift for silence like a coin bitten and found true. The second time, dragged uphill by a dying knight and a prayer-stone that glowed, magic flooding back into her like cold water while the old gods turned their attention toward her like a thousand heads lifting in a dark field.

Now she crossed a third time, and nothing happened at all.

No flood. No noticing. No voice calling a buried name. She sat in the cart as the horses picked their way up the switchback, and the quiet of the valley reached out to meet her, and it was just — quiet. Air and distance and the smell of pine. A silence with no teeth in it.

"It's different," Maren said quietly beside her. "Isn't it."

"Yeah."

"Before, it felt like holding your breath."

"That's exactly what it felt like." Edmund laughed, short and wondering. "Four years of holding my breath and I called it peace."

Pip leaned between them from the cart bed. "The small gods can't come over," she said, matter-of-fact, watching the ridgeline the way other children watched a horizon full of ships. "Magic doesn't work here. But Ysolde says borders aren't walls. They're doors. Doors swing."

"Doors swing," Edmund repeated, and filed it away next to everything else the god-touched child had ever said that sounded like nonsense and later turned out to be load-bearing truth.

They came down into the valley in late afternoon light. The village was where they'd left it, because villages are, mostly: the same crooked lane, the same well with its mossy lip, Widow Cass's goats screaming their eternal grievances at the fence line. Smoke rose from chimneys. Someone was baking something involving onions. A dog trotted out to inspect the cart, decided they were acceptable, and followed them in like an honor guard.

Old Tam the smith spotted them first and shouted the news down the lane, and by the time they reached the green there were thirty people gathered, and Edmund — who had spent four years in this valley being politely invisible, the quiet mender-of-pots nobody asked questions of — stood in the middle of it all and was *known*.

Not the fugitive. Not the god-speaker. Not the woman who'd unbound a king. Just Edmund, back, with friends, and hadn't she said she'd be gone a season? It had been nearly two. Shame on her.

"They don't care," she murmured to Maren, dazed.

"Why would they?" Maren dismounted stiffly. "Kings change. Bread still burns. Pots still crack." She glanced at Edmund sidelong, and the ghost of the twitch returned to her mouth. "And you're the best pot-mender within fifty miles. Court titles are cheap. Craftsmanship isn't."

Widow Cass pushed through the crowd, seized Edmund by both shoulders, inspected her at arm's length, and pronounced: "Too thin. All of you. There's stew on." Which settled lodging, dinner, and the entire question of the evening in eleven words, as Widow Cass's pronouncements tended to.

That night, in the warm kitchen with the stew gone and the fire low, Pip fell asleep against Maren's side mid-sentence, one hand curled loosely around nothing — or around something, depending on your eyes. Maren sat unmoving so as not to wake her, looking down at the sleeping weight of her with an expression Edmund had never seen on her face during the whole hard road south: unguarded, unhurried, undone.

"She talks in her sleep now," Maren said softly. "Did you know? Used to be silent as the grave. Now she mutters about doors and rivers and somebody named Osk who apparently owes her a beetle."

"People sleep differently when they're safe," Edmund said.

"Yes." Maren was quiet a moment. "So I'm told."

* * *

Spring became summer the way it does in valleys: gradually, then all at once. The garden went in. Brannoc, discovering that a body heals poorly when it has nothing to do, took over the repair of Tam's forge roof and then, inexorably, everything else in the village that sagged, leaned, or groaned in the wind. He grumbled through all of it. He also built Pip a treehouse, without being asked, over six weekends, using joinery good enough that Tam accused him of showing off. Brannoc denied showing off. The treehouse had a carved lintel.

Maren took over the training yard behind the smithy, teaching village children how to fall down properly, which she insisted was ninety percent of soldiery. On feast days she walked to the ridgeline alone and stood there a while, facing outward. Nobody asked what she did there. Everybody guessed.

And Edmund mended pots.

It should have been a diminishment, she thought sometimes, working the glaze in her little workshop behind Widow Cass's house. She had carried a god-speaker's gift — had heard the old gods speaking in the roots of things, had taken back a bargain older than the kingdom — and now her greatest talent was making a cracked bowl hold water. Some nights the loss itched like a phantom limb.

But more often — more and more often, as the seasons turned — it felt like setting something down.

Her mother had been born with the gift. Her mother had spent it, deceived, trying to save a dying king, and died with the debt unpaid and the shame passed down like an heirloom. Edmund had carried that shame over one ridge and across half a kingdom and back again, and had set it down in a sealed chapel with her mother's own words in her mouth. What remained afterward was not emptiness. She'd expected emptiness. What remained was *room* — space where the fear used to live, available now for other things. For the smell of bread. For Pip's laugh. For the particular satisfaction of a hairline crack sealed so well it would outlive her.

The gift had been inherited. The peace, it turned out, she'd had to choose.

On midsummer evening the whole valley came out to the green, and someone produced a fiddle, and Brannoc danced — badly, stiff-legged, refusing all correction — with Widow Cass, who laughed like a girl. Maren taught Pip a reel in four slow pieces until Pip got it and then taught it wrong to three smaller children on purpose, cackling. The dog stole a sausage. The fire burned down to orange.

Edmund sat a little apart, the way she liked to, close enough to the warmth and far enough to see all of it at once.

*Home,* she thought, testing the word.

For four years she'd thought home was the valley — a location, a geography, a place where magic couldn't follow and therefore neither could her past. She'd been wrong in the way she'd been wrong about so much. Home wasn't the valley. Home was a sleeping child with her head on a tired knight's shoulder. It was a lame knight building treehouses. It was a widow who said *there's stew on* and meant *you belong to us now.* The valley was just where they'd all happened to land, the way seeds land. Anywhere they were together would grow the same.

The silence rose off the ridgeline and settled over the green, soft as a blanket, and for the first time since she'd run down that mountain with nothing but fear in her pockets, Edmund heard it for what it was.

Not hiding. Rest.

* * *

The letter came in autumn, on a day of high thin cloud and geese.

A rider climbed the switchback road — the first rider in memory to come over the ridge rather than around it — and handed down a packet sealed with the crest of Aldermarch: the spire and the seven stars, redrawn, Edmund noticed, with the stars a little brighter than the old designs. The king's seal hung beneath in wax the color of honey.

"From the capital," the rider said, unnecessarily, and accepted cider and a heel of bread, and went on his way whistling.

Edmund carried the packet to her workshop and sat with it a long moment before breaking the seal. Through the window she could hear Pip shrieking joyfully at something in the treehouse, and Brannoc's voice rumbling a warning about ladders, and beneath it all the hammer-song of Tam's forge keeping the afternoon's time.

Inside were two documents.

The first bore the chancellor's formal hand: notice of the autumn assizes in the capital, the ongoing trials of Vessia's remaining faction, and — this appended almost shyly, in a different, shakier script she recognized as the king's own — a personal line. *If you ever wish to speak, there will always be a chair, and the shutters will be open.*

The second document was shorter. Older. Its edges were browned, its ink faded, and Edmund knew the hand at once because it was her own hand, learned from its original: her mother's slanted, careful letters.

Someone — the king, she suspected; he'd promised the chapel records to her — had recovered it from among her mother's papers. It was dated thirty-one years ago, before Edmund was born. It was addressed to no one. It began:

*If you are reading this, then either the bargain held, or it broke, and I am sorry for whichever it was—*

Edmund read it once by the light of the window. Then she banked the fire, lit the lamp, and read it again slowly, her thumb resting on her mother's handwriting the way you'd rest your hand on a sleeping animal.

Outside, footsteps came up the path — three sets, mismatched, familiar as her own heartbeat. Pip's voice floated in through the door, breathless with whatever discovery the afternoon had produced. The latch lifted.

Edmund looked at the letter. Looked at the door.

"Well," she said, as her family came in out of the cold. "Sit down. My mother wrote to us."
