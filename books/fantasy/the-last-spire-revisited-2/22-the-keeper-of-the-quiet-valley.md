## 22. The Keeper of the Quiet Valley

The first snow came in October, and Edmund let it.

She stood at the ridgeline where the magic died — where it had always died, that soft gray hush she had once thought of as a wall and now understood as a door held closed — and watched the flakes come down over the valley. They fell straight and slow, unhurried, the way snow fell when no god was bending the wind. Ordinary snow. She'd missed ordinary things more than she'd expected to.

A year ago she would have counted the flakes for omens. Now she just counted them because Pip was counting them too, somewhere behind her, and Pip always won.

"Forty-one," Pip called up the slope. "You're losing."

"I'm not playing."

"You said you weren't playing last time and then you got to sixty and got smug about it."

Edmund laughed, which still surprised her sometimes — how easily it came now, how little it cost. She turned back down toward the village. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys, and three of those chimneys belonged to houses that hadn't been occupied two winters past. The Hollis farm had a family in it again, refugees from the frozen north counties who'd walked most of a year to get here, following rumors of a valley where nothing hunted you. The tanner's cottage, empty since before Edmund arrived, now sheltered an old couple whose village had been eaten by Ysolde's first hard winter — the winter that wasn't Ysolde's fault, though the goddess still apologized for it every time she visited, which was often, and at length.

And the mill house, closest to the stream, belonged to four of them now, and had ever since spring: Edmund, Maren, Brannoc, and Pip, who technically slept in the loft but technically slept wherever she pleased.

Runaways found the valley the way water finds low ground. A hedge-witch's apprentice who'd fled her mistress rather than be sold. Two brothers deserting a border garrison that had sworn itself to Vessia out of fear. A girl with a burned face who wouldn't say what burned her, only that it wore a crown. Edmund took their names, gave them beds, taught them mending and planting and the fine art of lying low. Word spread, as word does. By midsummer there was a list of names kept in the mill house, written in Edmund's careful hand, and by autumn the list needed a second page.

Ser Maren had wanted to call it something grand. *Sanctuary*, she'd suggested. *The Quiet Refuge.* Brannoc had wanted to fortify it, which was Brannoc's answer to everything, and had gotten as far as sketching palisade lines on the back of a flour sack before Edmund took the flour sack away.

"It's not a refuge," Edmund told them both. "It's a valley. People live here. That's all."

"That's never all," Maren said.

But it was, mostly. That was the thing Edmund had learned over the long slow year — that most of the great machinery of the world ran without anyone operating it, that bread rose and children grew and grief dulled whether or not anyone was managing the process. She had spent so many years believing that safety was something you built and guarded, plank by paranoid plank. It turned out safety was mostly a habit. You swept the step. You fed whoever came. You didn't ask questions until people were ready to answer them.

* * *

Pip's gods came over the ridge in November.

They couldn't stay — the valley's quiet pressed against anything divine like deep water against a lantern — but they could visit, crossing the ridgeline for a few minutes at a time, flickering like candles carried into wind. The hearth-god came first, as he always did, arriving in the gap between the door and its frame, a warmth with eyes. Edmund had saved him once, starving in a roadside shrine, and he had never forgotten it, and he made sure she knew it, which was occasionally exhausting.

"You've let the chimney mortar go," he said, his voice like banked coals. "I can feel it from here. The draft is criminal."

"I'll fix it."

"You'll fix it the way you fixed the well pulley, which is to say eventually, and badly, and with Brannoc doing the actual work."

Brannoc, mending a harness by the fire, didn't look up. "He's right."

"He's a god of small doors," Edmund said. "He should mind one."

But she fixed the mortar the next morning, properly, because that was the other thing she'd learned: you didn't argue with gratitude. You just tried to deserve it.

After the hearth-god came the others, trickling in through the autumn like birds ahead of weather. A god of footbridges who could only cross running water and therefore spent her visits hovering anxiously over the stream. A god of lost mittens, newly recovered from a bad century, who followed Pip around the yard depositing single gloves in her pockets. And once — once, in the first real cold of the season, when the whole valley lay white and silent under a fat gray sky — Ysolde herself, the Long Night, goddess of winter, standing at the edge of the village green in a cloak of falling snow, looking embarrassed.

"I owe this place a debt," Ysolde said. Her voice was the creak of glaciers. "This is awkward for me."

"Then don't apologize again," Edmund said. "Just do me a favor. When your winter comes down off the mountains—" she nodded at the ridgeline, at the world beyond it— "be gentle with the ones walking through it. The ones heading here. They'll be tired."

Ysolde considered her for a long moment. Snow gathered in Edmund's hair and did not melt.

"You were better at being a god-speaker than you think," the goddess said finally. "You're better at being a keeper. Fewer voices, same ears." And she was gone, leaving the green dusted white and the air smelling briefly of clean ice, and Pip shrieking with joy because the goddess had left a perfect snowman standing in the middle of the green, complete with a crown of icicles, which Pip declared was "a message," and which everyone else agreed was probably just a goddess showing off.

* * *

Maren trained the runaways in the spring field, those who wanted training, drilling them with wooden staves in the slush while the rest of the valley pretended not to watch. She was slower than she'd been — the wound from the escape had never fully left her shoulder, and the years weighed on her in ways she stopped apologizing for around midsummer — but she was patient in a way Edmund suspected the knight-sergeant she'd once been had never been allowed to be. Disgrace, it turned out, made excellent teachers.

"They're asking about you," Maren said one evening, coming in stamping snow off her boots. "The new ones. The girl with the burned face. They want to know if it's true you unmade a king."

"I didn't unmake a king. I unmade a thing wearing a king."

"They want to know if it's true you heard gods."

"I used to. I don't anymore." Edmund held up her hands, turning them in the firelight. Empty hands. She'd stopped flinching at them sometime around midsummer too. "The gift burned out. You know that."

"And yet they keep asking."

"Because people want to believe someone's listening," Edmund said. "Even if it's just another person." She looked at the fire. "Tell them the truth. Tell them I mend pots and keep a list of names, and that the listening part is over."

Maren sat down across from her. For a while neither spoke, and the fire cracked, and upstairs Pip's footsteps thumped around the loft in the particular rhythm that meant she was talking to a mitten-god again.

"Do you regret it?" Maren asked quietly. "The price. You could have refused. Let someone else stand at the stone."

"No," Edmund said, and found, testing it, that it was true. "I spent my whole life thinking my gift was a debt I owed someone. My mother. The crown. The gods. Something. It wasn't a debt. It was a tool, and tools wear out, and mine wore out doing the one thing it was actually for." She smiled, tired. "I got to give my mother's consent back. Do you know what that's worth? There are people who die without ever returning anything."

Maren studied her for a moment longer, then nodded, satisfied, and reached for the whetstone, and that was the whole conversation, because that was how conversations worked between people who had bled together in the snow. Some things didn't need saying twice. Most things, really.

* * *

Brannoc healed crooked and complained about it constantly, which everyone agreed was an improvement over him nearly dying, which he had done twice, once in the Ashwood ambush and once during the escape, and both times with tremendous drama. The wound along his ribs — the crown-thing's touch, cold and lingering even after its death — had faded slowly through the spring, and by summer it was only a pale seam of scar that ached when rain came. He'd gone from royal guard to outlaw captain to wounded houseguest, and had settled into the last role with the sullen grace of a large dog pretending it wasn't happy indoors.

He fixed things. That was his penance and his pleasure. Fences, roofs, the well pulley Edmund had indeed fixed badly, wagon axles for refugees arriving on the north road. He grumbled through all of it. He was teaching the burned-faced girl to use a staff, gently, in the hour before dusk, and if anyone mentioned it he would deny it with his dying breath.

"The king wrote again," Brannoc said one night in December, dropping a folded paper on the table without ceremony. "Third letter this year. Rider brought it to the ridge and wouldn't cross. Superstitious lot, couriers."

"Did you read it?"

"Course I read it. I read all your mail." He settled into his chair with a groan. "It's the usual. Thanks, honors, come south, blah blah. He wants to give you a title. *Warden of the Marches*, this time. Last time it was *Lady Protector of the Vale*. Before that, some nonsense with 'Grace' in it."

"What did you tell him?"

"I didn't tell him anything, I'm not your secretary." Brannoc scratched his beard. "Though between us, Warden of the Marches sounds like a man who has to ride around in the cold checking fences, so honestly it might suit you."

Edmund left the letter on the table for three days. That was also becoming a habit — letters accumulating, court business piling up like snow against a door. Aldous, restored, frail, honest, had opened his court to testimony the way he'd promised, and the testimony had changed things, and the changing things generated correspondence, and somehow every third piece of it seemed addressed to *the woman who unbound the king*. She answered what she could. She declined the rest.

On the fourth evening, Pip snatched the letter off the table, climbed onto a chair, and cleared her throat with tremendous formality.

"'To Edmund Ravensworth,'" she read, "'keeper of the quiet valley, from Aldous, son of Marrec, king in Aldermarch, restored by her hand—'"

"You don't have to do the whole style."

"I'm doing the whole style. *'—restored by her hand, greetings. The courts sit well. Vessia serves out her restitution at the granaries of the north, and the people she starved eat first from what she measures, which is justice of a kind, though I confess I do not know if it is enough. I suspect it never will be, and I have decided to build the not-enoughness into the law itself, so that no one forgets. My healers say I will see spring, which surprises us both.'"*

Pip paused. The fire popped. Outside, snow ticked softly against the shutters, and somewhere in the walls the hearth-god's presence warmed the draft he'd been complaining about, listening, as he always listened, without pretending otherwise.

"*'I write to offer, once more, whatever you will accept — title, land, gold, or merely the assurance that your valley stands inside my protection, though I begin to understand you may consider that last item less a gift than an imposition. So instead I will write only this: the small gods are returning to the shrines. Children leave bread out again. Whatever silence you keep, know that the kingdom keeps a noise underneath it now, a hum of thanks, and much of it travels north to one gray valley. If you ever wish to hear it, you need only listen. But I suspect you already do.'"*

Pip lowered the letter. "That's nice," she said. "He writes good letters for a man who was dead for a year."

"Buried," Edmund corrected automatically. "Not dead. Buried."

"Same thing if you ask me."

"It's very much not the same thing."

But she took the letter from Pip and read it again herself, by the fire, slowly, and something in the middle of it caught — the way a fishhook catches, small and barbless, more curiosity than pain. *If you ever wish to hear it, you need only listen.*

She couldn't listen. Not the old way. The gift was ash; she'd felt it go, felt the brand lift and leave smooth unscarred skin behind. Whatever sense had once let her hear the gods across the dark miles of Aldermarch was gone the way her childhood was gone, completely and permanently, and she had made her peace with that peace itself.

Except.

Except that some nights, standing at the ridgeline, she almost heard something. Not words. Not voices. Just — a hum, maybe, like the king had written. Like the memory of a sound, the way an amputated hand itches. She'd told herself it was wind in the pass, or blood in her own ears, or wanting. Wanting explained a great deal.

But tonight, reading the letter with her family around her — Maren darning by the fire, Brannoc snoring lightly in his chair, Pip swinging her feet and watching her with that unnervingly direct look — Edmund let herself consider the possibility she'd been avoiding all winter.

The gift had burned out. But gifts, her mother's notes had said, buried in the ledger's final pages, were not the same as blood. The gift was the candle. The blood was the wax. Burn the candle down, and the wax remained, waiting for a new wick — in her, or in a child of hers, or in some runaway girl arriving on the north road with a burned face and questions she wouldn't answer.

*Blood remembers,* her mother had written. *God help whoever it chooses next.*

"Maren," Edmund said slowly. "When the burned-faced girl came. What did she ask you? Exactly?"

Maren looked up from her darning. "Why?"

"Humor me."

Maren set down the sock. "She asked if the valley was true. The one where prayers stop at the ridge. Then she asked—" the old knight frowned, working at the memory— "she asked whether it was true that the woman who lived here had given her voice to the gods. Her exact words were, 'Did she spend it, or did she save any?'"

The fire crackled. In the walls, something warm went very still.

"She said *save*," Edmund said quietly. "Not *spend*. Save. Like change from a purchase. Like something left over."

Brannoc had stopped snoring. Pip had stopped swinging her feet. Even the wind outside seemed to lean in against the shutters, and Edmund sat with the letter in her lap and her family around her and felt the shape of the next year assembling itself out of the snow-dark, patient as winter, inevitable as spring.

"Well," said Pip, after a moment. "That's not going to be a problem at all, is it."

Edmund looked at the fire, and at her empty hands, and at the second page of names on the list by the door — names of people who had walked through Ysolde's winter to reach a quiet valley, carrying whatever they carried.

"No," she said. "Probably not."

Outside, far off past the ridge, something in the kingdom hummed — faint, wordless, and getting warmer.
