## 1. Ink and Small Mercies

The ink was the first betrayal of every morning.

Rafael ground it themselves, in the back room of the scriptorium annex, from oak galls and iron salts and a pinch of ash that no one ever asked about. The Order's scribes were forbidden many things—strong drink, unlicensed travel, the company of marked persons—but they were not forbidden to make their own ink, and so Rafael made it, and it was good ink, black as a closed door, and with it they copied out, line after patient line, the edicts that kept their city afraid.

*Let it be known,* the morning's page began, *that the practice of unlicensed resonance is an abomination before the Ninefold Light and shall be punished by sequestration of goods and person.*

Rafael's pen moved without hesitation. That was the trick of it—twenty years had taught the hand to move without hesitation, the way a man learns to walk across ice by never once looking down. The letters came out even and beautiful, because beautiful was safe, and safe was the only prayer Vell answered anymore.

Outside the annex window, the city was waking into its gray ritual. Carts on wet cobbles. A fishwife arguing with a porter about the price of eels. Two children chasing each other around the base of the old spire ruins, until their mother caught them by the collars and dragged them back, glancing over her shoulder at the temple bell tower as if the stones themselves might inform. Nobody let their children play near the spire. The spire remembered things, people said, and memory was contraband now too, more or less.

Rafael dipped the pen. *Further, any charm, sigil, or spoken working offered for sale or barter constitutes resonance under Article Seven, and the maker thereof shall be bound to the Order's service until such debt is discharged.*

They wrote the words that condemned them and were paid by the word. There was an elegance to it that Rafael could appreciate, in the way one appreciates a well-made gallows.

"Master Eriksen."

Brother Aldous stood in the doorway, a narrow man with the complexion of someone who had spent his whole life indoors being disappointed. He held the day's second folio against his chest like a shield. "Master Ondrel wants the Vell district census copies by noon. All of them."

"They'll be done."

"He said—" Aldous paused, and something flickered behind his eyes that might have been pity or might have been indigestion; with Aldous the two were often indistinguishable. "He said to remind you that your term is up at midwinter. He said you'd want to be... diligent."

"I'm always diligent, Brother."

"Mm." Aldous lingered a moment too long, the way men did when they wanted to say something kind and couldn't find a version of it that wouldn't cost them. Then he left, and his footsteps faded down the stone corridor, and Rafael sat alone with the edict drying in front of them.

Midwinter. The debt discharged. Twenty years of copying other people's certainties, and then freedom—or whatever the Order called the thing they handed you at the end, which was mostly just the absence of them, and the habit of flinching.

Rafael looked down at their hands. Ink in the cuticles, as always, worked so deep into the skin it looked like a stain of character. Scribe's hands. Charm-seller's hands. They turned them over and thought, not for the first time, about the small warmth that lived in the left palm, coiled tight as a sleeping wren: the little inheritance their mother had pressed into them in a dark kitchen, whispering, *never show it, never use it where anyone can see, promise me.* The magic of Rafael's blood was not the kind that moved mountains. It was the kind that found lost buttons and sweetened milk gone slightly off and eased a fever down by half a degree. Useless magic. Survival-sized magic.

It was also, under Article Seven, worth twenty years' bondage if discovered, and Rafael had been discovering ways to hide it since before they understood what hiding was.

The pen scratched on. By noon the census copies were stacked and tied, and Master Ondrel himself came to collect them, which he never did, which meant something, though Rafael couldn't yet say what.

Ondrel was a large man built like a ledger—everything squared, everything accounted, nothing wasted. His robes were the plain brown of the accounting branch, which everyone knew was where the real power lived, because the fighting branches made noise and the accounting branch made lists. He set his ringed hand flat on the stack of folios and did not pick them up.

"Diligent work," he said.

"Thank you, Master."

"The census interests me, Eriksen." His voice was mild, almost warm. Mildness was worse than anger. Anger in Ondrel was a tool used deliberately, like a hammer; mildness was the sound of him thinking. "Vell has grown smaller every year for nine years. Fewer births. Fewer marriages. And yet the number of confiscated charms coming through my office—" he tapped the folio once, softly, "—has grown. Do you find that interesting?"

"I find most numbers interesting, Master. It's a failing of my trade."

Something moved at the corner of Ondrel's mouth. Not quite a smile. An entry in a column. "Yes," he said. "I imagine it is." He gathered the folios and went, and Rafael stood in the emptying room and felt, faintly, like a page that had already been written on and hadn't noticed the ink going down.

* * *

The night market began, as it always did, with the lamps.

Not the Order's lamps—the great white globes outside the temple that burned with licensed flame and lit nothing honestly—but the small ones, tallow and rushlight, that vendors hung under awnings and inside cart canopies so that the whole of Tanner's Row became a cave of low gold light, intimate and slightly rotten, smelling of frying onions and wet wool and secrets. By day, Tanner's Row sold rope and hides. By night it sold everything else.

Rafael's stall was a plank across two barrels at the row's dead end, past the woman who sold luck (dried four-leaf clovers, entirely botanical, entirely legal), past the man who claimed to have the ear of the harbor master (he did not), down where the lamplight gave out and buyers had to trust you a little just to approach.

The charms lay on black velvet worn shiny at the folds. Knots of red thread against headache. Tin discs stamped with a sun that was only a sun. Little sewn birds of felt, stuffed with lavender, that mothers bought for colicky children because a mother's fear needed somewhere to go and the Order had outlawed all the honest destinations.

None of it worked. That was the beauty of it. None of it *worked*, and so none of it was magic, and Rafael sold hope at three coppers an ounce and slept at night with a clean conscience, or what remained of one, which was roughly the shape of one.

"A bird for the little one?" Rafael asked the fishwife from that morning, who had appeared out of the crowd with her youngest on her hip.

"How much?"

"For you? Two coppers." Rafael held up the felt wren. "It sings if you squeeze it."

"It does not sing."

"No," Rafael agreed. "But the child will believe it for a week, and belief is worth two coppers, and we both know it."

The fishwife laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, the way people laughed in Vell now, as if joy might be reported—and paid, and squeezed the bird, and the child shrieked with delight at nothing, and Rafael watched them go and felt the old familiar ache, the one that lived between the shoulder blades like a knot of cold wire: *this is what you are now. A seller of placebo birds. A man—no, not a man, they'd taught you early not to be anything so legible as a man or a woman, just a scribe, just Eriksen—who copies the chains in the morning and sells the keys' shadows at night.*

Because that was the joke, the private joke, the one that kept Rafael company through twenty years of careful nights: the charms didn't work, but Rafael did. Every third purchase, when the buyer's need was true and their desperation clean, Rafael would touch the trinket with the pad of one finger, and the small warmth in the left palm would breathe out, just barely, just enough. The thread-knot really would ease the headache. The tin sun really would warm a cold room by a degree. The felt bird—well, the bird stayed a bird, but the child's fever would break that night, and the mother would credit the lavender.

Small mercies. Illegal ones. Article Seven violations the size of a sigh, committed nightly under the white lamps of the Order, in the shadow of the bell tower whose watchers were watching everything except the one thing directly beneath them.

"You look tired, Eriksen."

Rafael didn't startle. Twenty years of double living had burned the startle out of them; what remained was a stillness that some people mistook for calm and others, correctly, for caution. They turned.

Old Maret stood at the edge of the lamplight, leaning on her stick, wrapped in a shawl that had outlived several fashion eras and possibly an empire. She was the closest thing the night market had to a priestess, which meant she remembered things aloud, quietly, to people she trusted, which was its own quiet crime.

"You look tired," she repeated. "And your charms are working better than usual tonight. Third one I've watched sell. People are walking away from this stall standing straighter than they arrived."

"Lavender," Rafael said. "And good thread."

"Mm." Maret's eyes were pale and sharp as a gull's. "My grandmother used to say there were two kinds of hidden fire. The kind that burns you, and the kind you burn to keep others warm." She leaned closer, stick tapping the cobbles. "You should be careful, scribe. Fires get noticed. Even small ones. Especially small ones—the big ones make noise, but the little ones leave smoke."

"Goodnight, Maret."

"Buy my silence with a headache knot. My hip's been singing vespers since dawn."

Rafael tied the knot, breathed the smallest breath of warmth into it—half a degree of mercy, invisible—and handed it over. Maret shuffled off into the dark, humming something old, and Rafael told themselves, as they told themselves every night, that the risk was measured, that the warmth was too small to trace, that a lifetime of caution was itself a kind of mastery.

They almost believed it. That was the trick of making peace with being small: you had to keep re-making it, nightly, like bread, or it went stale and you could see the mold of what you'd actually become.

Near midnight the market thinned. Rain started—not the storm the sky had been promising all day, just a thin cold drizzle that made the lamps hiss—and Rafael packed the velvet, counted the coppers (a good night, enough for the week's grain and a candle), and was folding the last of the felt birds when they heard the footsteps.

Not Maret's shuffle. Not a drunkard's weave. Measured steps, deliberate, coming down the dead-end alley where no legitimate customer had any reason to walk.

Rafael's hand closed around the last charm—a simple tin disc, unstamped, unsold—and slid it into their sleeve without deciding to. The body knew its work. The warmth in the left palm curled tighter, awake now, listening.

A figure stopped at the edge of the lamplight. Small. Hooded. Barefoot, despite the cold, despite the wet cobbles, and standing in a puddle without any sign of noticing it.

"Market's closing," Rafael said, keeping their voice level, vendor-flat. "Come back tomorrow."

The figure tilted its head. Under the hood's shadow, Rafael caught the pale gleam of a face too young to be out here alone—a child, eight years old perhaps, soaked to the skin and shivering not at all, which was somehow worse than shivering.

And then the child spoke, and the words that came out were words Rafael had never heard, in a voice like water moving over older water, and every hair on Rafael's arms rose, and the wards their mother had stitched into the hem of their coat twenty years ago—wards that had slept through inquisitions and inspections and the night the Order raided Wrenna's first sanctuary—woke up all at once and *sang.*

The child looked at Rafael's coat hem. Looked at Rafael. Held out one small hand, palm up, in a gesture that was unmistakably, impossibly, a request.

Behind them, far up the row, a temple bell began to toll the hour—and stopped mid-stroke, as if it had forgotten how.

Rafael's mouth was dry. "What," they managed, "are you?"

The child crouched, dipped one finger in the puddle-mud and ash at the alley's edge, and wrote on the cobblestones between them, in a careful, childish hand, a single word:

*MINE.*
