## 4. The Order Knocks

The knock came at the hour when Vell's lamps gutter lowest—that gray, salted dark before dawn when even the night market had packed its charms away and the honest were not yet awake to be honest.

Rafael knew before opening the door. Three knocks, evenly spaced, no urgency in them. Urgency was for people who feared the answer. The Order never feared.

Through the frosted pane of the shop's front window, a shape stood in the rain: tall, still as a signpost, wearing the slate-gray traveling cloak that shed water the way the Order shed doubt—completely, and without effort.

Behind Rafael, from the loft above the stairs, came a small sound. Not a word. Isolde had not spoken aloud since she'd arrived four nights past, except in that tongue that made Rafael's teeth ache and their wards hum like struck glass. This was smaller—a breath drawn sharp, the sound of a child hearing a knock and understanding, in some bone-deep way, exactly what it meant.

*Hide,* Rafael wanted to say. *Now.* But there was no time, and no place good enough, and saying it aloud would only put fear into the air where the visitor could taste it.

Rafael pressed one finger to their own lips, then pointed down—toward the floor, toward the trapdoor beneath the counter that opened onto the crawlspace where the contraband lived: the luck-knots braided from red thread, the fever-charms stamped with saints the Order had uncanonized, the little wax hands against nightmare that mothers bought at midnight and never asked where they came from.

The child understood. Children always understood hiding; it was the first literacy anyone taught them. She slipped off the loft ladder without a creak, crossed the floor in bare feet, and folded herself into the dark among the wax hands and knotted thread. Rafael pulled the trapdoor shut over her and slid the ledger chest on top of it—the one piece of furniture heavy enough to matter and ordinary enough not to invite a second look.

Then Rafael smoothed their apron, checked that the lamp on the counter burned steady and dull, and opened the door.

Rain blew in sideways. So did the smell of the Order: cold iron, clean linen, and underneath it something faintly scorched, like the memory of pyres.

"Inquisitor Halvorsen," Rafael said, and was proud of how level it came out.

The woman on the step did not return the courtesy immediately. She was younger than Rafael expected—younger than her reputation, which said she'd closed eleven unlicensed practitioners in the eastern districts alone. Gray eyes, the color of weather about to decide. Rain ran off the brim of her hood and she didn't wipe her face.

"Scribe Eriksen." Her voice was flat, unhurried, the voice of someone reading aloud. "You know me."

"Everyone in Vell knows your seal." Which was true, and therefore safe to say. Rafael stepped back. "You're standing in the rain."

"I'm aware." But she came inside anyway, dripping on the flagstones, and stood a moment just inside the door with her head slightly tilted, the way a hound tilts at a wall.

Rafael's heart was doing something disgraceful. They kept their face still through long practice; twenty years of selling charms by night while copying edicts against them by day had built a mask so worn it fit like skin. But masks are made for liars protecting themselves, and this morning Rafael discovered the difference between that and what came now: every second Serane Halvorsen stood in the shop, there was a child under the floor breathing through her nose very carefully, and the mask had to protect *her.*

It was astonishing how much heavier the same lie became.

"You keep late hours," the Inquisitor said, walking the length of the shop without invitation. Her gloved fingers trailed along the shelf of legitimate stock—hymnals, ink, candle-wax blessed at the temple, all of it clean, all of it boring, all of it arranged years ago precisely for mornings like this. "Your neighbors report light burning past third bell."

"I copy at night. The Order pays by the page, not the hour."

"The Order does pay by the page." She stopped at the counter. Her eyes moved across it with terrible patience: the inkwell, the sand-dryer, the stack of finished edicts awaiting delivery. *An Edict Concerning the Suppression of Resonant Practices,* said the topmost sheet, in Rafael's own careful hand. "And yet your neighbors report something else as well. A sound."

"A sound."

"They've called it singing. Under the wind, during the storm two nights ago. A resonance." She said the word the way the edicts said it—*resonance*—as though the word itself might bloom if planted correctly. "Unlicensed, obviously. All resonance is unlicensed now. You'd know the phrasing better than I, scribe. You write it often enough."

"It was a storm," Rafael said. And here, God forgive them, they heard their own voice do the thing they hated: pitch too high, half a beat too fast, the lie of an amateur. "Old buildings sing in storms. The eaves. The chimney draws wrong."

Serane looked at the ceiling. Followed the line of the rafters down the walls. Looked, at last and briefly, at the floor.

Rafael felt the look go through the floorboards, through the ledger chest, through wax and thread and a child holding her breath among contraband, and land somewhere in Rafael's own sternum like a nail driven home.

"May I?" she said, and touched the counter's edge—not asking permission to touch, but announcing it—and lifted the ledger chest's lid an inch.

Every charm in the shop seemed to hold its name in its throat. Rafael thought, absurdly, of the fever-charms, of the little wax hands, of the red-thread knots, and of how each one would read as nothing more than craft to her trained senses—but the space beneath the floor was not nothing. Four nights of a child steeped in the First Tongue had soaked into the boards. If the Inquisitor knelt. If she listened the way she was listening now, head tilted, hound-still—

She let the lid fall.

"Your ledgers are in order," she said.

"I should hope so. I keep them myself."

"That's what I mean, scribe." She turned, finally, and faced Rafael fully, and up close her gray eyes were not cold at all but *tired*, which was somehow worse. "Everything you keep is in order. Your accounts, your hours, your edicts. I've read your file. Twenty years in the Order's service, debt-bonded, no incidents, no associations flagged, no—" a small pause, precise as a knife laid flat, "—no deviations. Men and women who hide things have ragged edges, scribe. You are the smoothest record I have ever opened."

"And yet you knocked."

"And yet I knocked." Almost—almost—a smile, gone before it could be confirmed. "Because reports don't care how smooth the record is. Three households on this street filed independently. Singing during the storm. One of them mentioned lights. And one of them—" she paused, and took from her sleeve a folded paper, and did not open it, only held it, "—mentioned a child. Seen at your window. During curfew hours. Four nights ago."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere down the street a cart rattled past, early to market, its driver mercifully incurious.

"There is no child here," Rafael said.

"No," Serane agreed. "There isn't. I can see that. The question that interests the Order is whether there was one, and the question that interests me—" she tucked the paper away again, unopened, unread, and Rafael would think about that unread paper for days, "—is rather different. You've been bonded to the Temple since you were nineteen, scribe. That's a long time to owe a man like Ondrel. Tell me: has he ever asked you for anything besides pages?"

The pivot was so sudden Rafael nearly lost footing on it. "Master Ondrel and I have a strictly professional arrangement."

"Everyone says that about Ondrel. Usually in the tone you just used." She pulled up her hood again. At the door she stopped, one hand on the latch, rain waiting beyond her like a congregation. "I'm required to file a report within three days. What I file depends on what I find, and what I find depends on where I look, and where I look—" the gray eyes came around, level and unhurried, "—depends somewhat on whether the city's most reliable scribe chooses to be reliable in this instance as well."

"I don't take your meaning, Inquisitor."

"No," she said. "But you will. Reports of resonance will continue. Streets get searched when reports continue. Floors get pulled up. It's tedious work, but we're paid by the floorboard." She opened the door onto the wet gray morning. "Three days, scribe. Come to the chapterhouse, or don't. If you come, ask for me and say—" she considered, then supplied it with the same flat care she gave everything, "—say that the storm passed over. If you don't come, I'll assume your roof leaks worse than your neighbors think, and I'll send men with lanterns to check it personally."

Then she was gone, into the rain, her footfalls making no more sound than the weather, leaving Rafael standing in a shop that smelled suddenly of iron and scorched linen and everything Rafael had spent twenty years pretending not to be.

* * *

Rafael waited until the footsteps faded, until the cart returned, until a full quarter-hour of ordinary street sounds had washed over the door—then crossed the room, dragged the ledger chest aside, and lifted the trapdoor.

Isolde looked up at them out of the dark, wedged among the wax hands and red-thread knots, her face smudged with dust and her eyes enormous and absolutely calm. Children who have hidden before. Children for whom hiding is not an event but a condition.

"She's gone," Rafael said softly, in Common, and then—feeling foolish and doing it anyway—in the few words of the First Tongue Isolde had taught them in dreams: *safe, now, safe.*

The child climbed out stiffly, joints popped from the cramped dark, and stood a moment swaying. Then she reached up and took Rafael's hand. Not for comfort. Rafael understood that slowly, watching her small fingers close around theirs with deliberate pressure. She was checking their pulse. Reading their fear the way she read everything—directly, wordlessly, through the skin.

Her thumb moved once across Rafael's wrist. A question.

"Lying," Rafael said. "That's all. I'm good at it."

Which was true, and had been true for twenty years, and had never once felt like this—had never left their mouth tasting of copper, their knees humming, their whole body ringing like the wards after a storm. Because all those years of lies had been small mercies told to keep Rafael small and safe and untouched. This lie had been told to keep someone else whole, and it had cost something the others never had.

It felt, Rafael realized with a vertigo that had nothing to do with exhaustion, like the first honest thing they'd done in decades.

Isolde released their wrist. She went to the cold hearth, dipped one finger in the fine ash there, and wrote on the hearthstone in quick, sure strokes. First Tongue characters, the ones that made the wards murmur. Then, below them, laboriously, in blocky Common letters learned from Rafael's hymnals:

**THREE DAYS?**

"You understood that."

A nod. Of course she had. She'd been under the floor for every word.

**GO?** she wrote, and drew a crude shape that Rafael took a moment to parse: the chapterhouse. Its spire. She knew the building; every soul in Vell knew the building, the way sheep know the shear-barn.

"I don't know," Rafael said honestly. "She wants something. People like that always want something, and what they want costs more than they say." Rafael crouched, bringing their eyes level with the child's. "But I know what happens if I don't go. Lanterns. Floorboards. Men who pull up floors."

Isolde studied them for a long moment. Then she wiped the ash with her palm and wrote again, single word, pressing hard enough to score the stone:

**GO.**

And beneath it, after a pause, another word in the First Tongue—one of the few Rafael had learned to recognize by feel rather than meaning, because it surfaced in every dream-lesson like a refrain. The word that meant, as near as Rafael could tell, *owed*. Or possibly *owed-to*. The language was old enough that debts and gifts shared roots.

The child pressed Rafael's hand flat over the written word and held it there, and looked at them with those enormous calm eyes, and Rafael understood that she was not advising. She was confirming what they both already knew: that the visit had changed the arithmetic of everything. That a favor offered by an inquisitor is not a favor. It is a loan, and the Undercourt of the living keeps books too.

Rafael rose, banked the fire, and began—because the hands needed occupation or they'd shake—to trim the lamp and square the finished edicts for delivery. Halfway down the stack, their own handwriting caught their eye. *An Edict Concerning the Suppression of Resonant Practices.* Due at the chapterhouse by ninth bell anyway.

Due, in fact, at the desk of the department that answered to Inquisitor Halvorsen.

Rafael set the stack down, took their coat from its hook, and looked at the child who had rewritten the shape of their life in four nights and one morning.

"Stay away from the windows," they said. "Bar the trapdoor behind me. And if anyone knocks—" they held up one finger, "—one knock means me. Two means run to Wrenna's kitchen on Tanner's Row, the one with the blue door, and tell them—" Rafael paused, realizing they had no idea how a mute child tells anyone anything. "Write *Rafael sent you.* Anyone in that kitchen will understand."

Isolde nodded once. Then, as Rafael reached the door, she caught their sleeve, and wrote one last thing in the air with her finger, slow, so they'd follow: a First Tongue character that in the dreams meant *come-back.*

"I'll come back," Rafael said. And found, to their considerable surprise, that it was not a lie, and not a promise either, but simply a fact arranging itself around them like weather.

Outside, the rain had thinned to mist. The chapterhouse spire rose over Vell's rooftops ahead, gray against gray, and Rafael walked toward it carrying a stack of edicts outlawing everything they loved, to meet an inquisitor whose price they could not yet read, owing favors on every side like a man crossing a frozen river—each step sounding, somewhere far beneath the ice, the patient scratch of a pen adding figures to a ledger.

At the chapterhouse door, the sentry waved them through with the bored courtesy owed to a familiar scribe. Rafael climbed the stairs to the inquisitorial wing, rehearsed *the storm passed over* twice under their breath, and knocked.

From within, a voice said, "Enter, scribe," and something else besides—something in the pause after, in the quality of the silence, that told Rafael, before the door even opened, that Serane Halvorsen had been expecting them since the moment she walked out into the rain.

And that whatever she wanted, it had never been the child at all.
