## 6. The Heretic's Kitchen

The list of eleven names burned a hole in Rafael's pocket for two days before they found the courage to use it.

They had copied the names out in their best ledger hand, the hand that wrote edicts against magic every morning, and then, in a fit of something between guilt and self-preservation, had nearly fed the paper to the stove. But the child's song would not leave them. It threaded through their sleep like smoke under a door, and each morning Rafael woke with new syllables lodged behind their teeth—syllables that meant nothing in Common and everything in some older grammar of the blood. The dreams came too, unbidden: a hall of green stone, voices braided into one long note, and hands reaching up out of shadow as if from water.

*Find the kitchen,* Serane had not said, but her parting words had pointed there all the same—the way she'd mentioned Wrenna of the Ashes with a casualness too deliberate to be casual, a name dropped like a coin into Rafael's palm. *If you're harboring what I think you're harboring, scribe, you'll want someone who remembers the old rites.* And then she had looked at Rafael with those gray eyes that missed nothing, and left.

So on the third evening, with Isolde wrapped in an oversized coat and a scarf wound twice around her lower face—a disguise against recognition that fooled no one, least of all Rafael—Rafael locked the shop, hung the *closed for repairs* sign that had never once been true, and walked into the wet dark of Vell toward the river district.

Isolde did not ask where they were going. She had stopped asking anything after the first night; questions, Rafael suspected, cost her more than silence did. Instead she walked close, her small mittened hand hooked into Rafael's sleeve, and when they passed the Order's waystation on Candlemaker's Row—its lantern burning the harsh white of sanctioned flame—her grip tightened until Rafael felt each finger separately through the wool.

"It's all right," Rafael murmured, and hated how much it sounded like a lie, because it was one. Nothing was all right. They were walking toward the last forbidden sanctuary in Vell carrying the most forbidden thing in Vell, and the streets had that particular hush of a city holding its breath before snow—or before soldiers.

The river district smelled of fish rot and tallow and, beneath it, faintly, of woodsmoke that didn't quite belong. Rafael followed the smell. Three streets down from the drowned chapel, past a shuttered dye-house whose vats still stained the cobbles purple, there was a doorway with no sign above it at all—which was itself a kind of sign—and a queue of people who did not look like a queue so much as a congregation pretending to be hungry.

They were the ones you learned to see only if you had spent twenty years looking: the woman with the burn scar climbing her throat like ivy, the shape of a ward-gone-wrong beneath it; the old man whose shadow moved a half-second out of step with him; the boy of perhaps twelve who kept his hands buried in his sleeves because his fingertips glowed faintly when he was cold, like banked coals. Marked ones. The Order called them contaminated. The edicts Rafael transcribed called them abominations, in a fine clerical hand, forty copies per week.

The soup kitchen occupied the gutted shell of a granary. Inside, steam rose from cauldrons in clouds that caught the lamplight and turned it amber, and the noise was enormous—spoons, bowls, argument, laughter, a baby crying, someone singing off-key—and it took Rafael a moment to understand why the noise felt so strange.

It was joy. Unguarded, unsanctioned, unafraid. Rafael could not remember the last time they had stood inside a room that made this much sound on purpose.

And then the crowd parted—not ceremonially, just the way crowds do—and there was Wrenna.

She was ladling soup with the economy of a woman who had fed armies, a big-boned, gray-streaked woman in an apron gone translucent with age and scrubbing, and when she looked up and saw Rafael, her ladle did not stop moving but her eyes went very still, the way a hawk goes still.

"New faces," she said, in a voice pitched to carry over cauldrons. "Bowl or business?"

Rafael hesitated. "Both," they admitted. "I think."

"Then bowl first." She thrust two bowls across the counter, and only then did her gaze drop to the small figure beside Rafael, to the scarf, to the mittens, to the way the child stood slightly behind them. Something moved through Wrenna's face—recognition, yes, but recognition of what, Rafael could not yet say. "Sit by the far wall. Under the beam with the hooks. No one bothers anyone under the hooks."

* * *

The soup was barley and root vegetables and something smoked, and it was the best thing Rafael had eaten in months, which was its own quiet indictment of the life Rafael had been living. Isolde ate with the concentrated solemnity of a child who had known hunger in ways that predated kitchens. Around them the hall roared on, and beneath the beam with the iron hooks—old meat hooks, Rafael realized, from the building's granary days, now hung with dried herbs and bundles of candles—no one looked at them twice.

Except everyone looked at them once. That was the difference between this room and every other room in Vell. Here, the looking was frank and brief and strangely gentle: *you are seen; we know what seeing costs; carry on.*

"You have her in a scarf." Wrenna dropped onto the bench opposite without asking, setting down her own bowl. Up close she smelled of onions and lamp oil and, underneath both, of something resinous and old—incense, maybe, from another life. "In winter. In Vell. You might as well hang a sign around her neck."

"She can't—" Rafael lowered their voice until it was mostly breath. "She doesn't speak Common. She speaks—"

"I know what she speaks." Wrenna tore her bread in half with unnecessary violence. "Word reached me three nights ago that a mute child walked into a charm-seller's shop during the storm and the wards sang for her. Do you know how many people heard that story? Four. Two of them are dead already. I'm the third. You're the fourth."

The soup turned to lead in Rafael's stomach. "Who told you?"

"A dead man." She said it flatly, without drama, and ate her bread. "He sold candles. He listened at doors. He heard about your shop and he came to me because he thought I'd pay for information, and I do pay for information, and now he's dead, which tells you the Order pays better and faster than I do." Her eyes came up, gray and flinty. "Which means, scribe, that whatever window you had is closing. So let's not waste it on soup."

"They killed him? For talking?"

"They killed him for knowing. Talking just made it convenient." Wrenna leaned forward, forearms on the table, and for a moment the kitchen-noise seemed to recede around her, the way water recedes before a wave. "Now. Show me."

"Show you what?"

"Don't insult us both. The word. The word she writes."

Rafael's hand moved before their mind consented to it—reaching for the charcoal stub in their pocket, the one they carried for marking crates—and Isolde, watching, held out her mitten. Rafael pulled it off. Her fingers were cold as river stones. And in the spilled salt near the bread, without being asked, the child drew four sure strokes:

**MINE.**

Wrenna looked at it for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed—lower, rougher, the voice of someone standing at the edge of a hole and listening to the wind come up out of it.

"That's not a claim," she said. "That's a *title*. In the First Tongue, that word doesn't mean 'this belongs to me.' It means 'the last of mine.' The final entry. The one the ledger still wants." She looked up at Rafael, and her face had aged ten years between spoonfuls. "Where did you find her?"

"The storm. My doorstep. I don't know where she came from before that."

"No. You wouldn't." Wrenna pushed her bowl aside and laced her fingers together, cracking each knuckle in turn, a ritual, a counting. "Then hear me, and hear me once, because I won't say it twice. There is a court under Mount Cael. Not a legend, not a priest-story to frighten children with candle-snuffing tales. A real court, with a real king, and it does one thing: it keeps accounts. Souls, mostly. Bought, pawned, held against debt. Two hundred years ago, the founders of this city—the pious, beloved founders whose statues line the Processional—made a bargain with that court. Protection for Vell. Prosperity for their heirs. And the price was written in a language nobody alive could read, so nobody alive ever checked it."

Rafael's mouth had gone dry. "What price?"

"Speakers." Wrenna's voice did not soften. "Every soul that spoke the First Tongue. Every singer, every speaker, every carrier of the oldest language there is—sold, all of them, sealed into the mountain, and the city got its peace and built its temples on top of the payment and called it holy." She jerked her chin at Isolde. "She's what's left over. A missed entry. A soul the ledger never collected. And ledgers, scribe—you of all people should know this—ledgers *hate* open entries. The court will send for her. Maybe slowly. Maybe politely. But it will send."

"And the Order?" Rafael asked. "The Order hunts magic. Wouldn't they—"

"The Order?" For the first time, Wrenna laughed, and it was not a kind sound. "Child, who do you think countersigns the bargain? Who do you think has been collecting the interest payments for two centuries? The Order didn't outlaw magic to protect Vell. It outlawed magic to keep the market cornered." She stood, wiping her hands on the ruined apron. "Walk with me."

* * *

The back room of the granary had been a counting office once; you could still see the ghost-rectangle on the wall where a shelf of ledgers had stood. Now it held cots, a cold stove, crates of bandages, and a map of Vell annotated in a dozen different hands. Wrenna lit a candle stub, closed the door, and turned to face Rafael across the little room like a magistrate across a bench.

"I can help you. I can hide her, ward her, teach you both enough of the old rites to buy time. There are six people in this city who remember the First Tongue well enough to read a contract, and three of them trust me, and one of them owes me his life and hates it, which makes him reliable." She held up a hand before relief could bloom. "But nothing in this world is given, scribe. Everything is traded. So here is my price."

"Name it."

"There is a book in the Order's archive vault, three levels under the Temple of the Veil. Bound in gray calfskin, chained to its lectern, warded so that only a credentialed scribe of the second rank or higher can touch it without leaving their fingerprints burned into their own hands." Wrenna watched Rafael's face as she said it, cataloguing every flicker. "The Ledger of Debts. Every bargain the Order has ever struck, every contract, every collateral, every interest payment, entered in its own hand since the founding. Including—" and here her voice dropped to almost nothing, though there was no one to hear, "—including, I believe, the founding bargain itself. The original. The one with the mountain."

"You want me to steal it." Rafael heard their own voice go flat and strange. "From the vault. The vault I've walked past my whole life. You want me to walk into the heart of the Order and take the one book that proves everything."

"I want you to *copy* it, if stealing's beyond you—though copying takes a night you may not have, and the wards log every touch. Stealing is cleaner." Wrenna stepped closer. "You have credentials. You have access. You have twenty years of being invisible inside that building, and invisible is the most valuable thing a thief can be. And you have"—her gaze flicked past Rafael's shoulder, to where Isolde waited in the doorway with her enormous eyes—"a reason bigger than fear. Which is the only kind of reason that works."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then take the child and run tonight, and pray you outrun the Order's riders and the mountain's collectors both, and know that every marked soul in this room dies a little slower without what's in that book." Wrenna's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "I'm not threatening you, scribe. I'm telling you the exchange rate. Belonging always costs. This is the price of ours."

Rafael looked down at their hands—the hands that wrote edicts, the hands that sold charms, the hands that had signed receipts for a life they'd believed they chose. Twenty years of small mercies and smaller treasons, and every road had narrowed to this: a gray book under a temple, and a child who had written MINE in ash on their floor.

"When?" they asked.

"Soon," Wrenna said. "Before the dead candle-seller's story finishes traveling. Before the Order stops investigating and starts arresting." She put out her hand—broad, scarred, steady—and Rafael took it, and the grip was like being sworn into something, though neither of them said so. "Three nights from now, the archive runs a skeleton watch for the equinox inventory. Scribes' credentials, after hours, one night only. After that, the window shuts for a season."

"Three nights," Rafael repeated. It sounded like a sentence being passed.

"One more thing." Wrenna retrieved a folded scrap of paper from her apron and pressed it into Rafael's palm. On it, in a cramped and ancient-looking script, was a single phrase. "When you find the founding bargain—if you find it—read this word first. Aloud, in your head, before you read anything else. It's a key of comprehension. Old heretics used it to open contracts written in the First Tongue. Without it, the text will show you only what it wants you to see."

Rafael looked at the scrap. The letters writhed faintly, like things asleep.

"What does it say?"

Wrenna smiled, and it was the saddest expression Rafael had seen in years. "It says *truth*. In the language the mountain eats. Read it first, and the contract cannot lie to you." She turned back toward the door, then paused, her hand on the frame. "One warning, freely given. Ledgers keep their own counsel, scribe—but sometimes, when you open one, it reads *you* back."

* * *

They walked home through streets gone soft with beginning snow, Isolde's hand in Rafael's, neither speaking. At the corner of Candlemaker's Row, the child stopped and tugged Rafael's sleeve and pointed—at the Temple of the Veil, its spire black against the clouds, its sanctioned lanterns burning white and cold above the sleeping city.

Below that spire, three levels down, a gray book waited with Rafael's whole life in it. Perhaps literally. The thought arrived uninvited and refused to leave: *what if my name is in it? What if it has always been in it?*

Isolde looked up at Rafael then, and in the lamplight her breath made small ghosts in the cold, and she said—in Common, her first word of it, cracked and careful as an egg opening—

"Debt?"

Rafael knelt in the snow, took the child's cold hands in theirs, and found they had no answer that was not a lie.

"Yes," they said at last. "Debt. Three nights from now, we go and see how much."
