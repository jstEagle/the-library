## 7. Lessons in a Dead Language

The dreams began on the fourth night, and they did not ask permission.

Rafael had slept in the narrow room above the shop for twenty years without dreaming anything worth remembering—the gray sleep of a man who has made his peace with being small. But the night after Wrenna's soup kitchen, after the heretic priestess had looked at the mute child across a bowl of barley broth and gone pale as candle wax, something changed. Rafael lay down as usual, blew out the lamp as usual, and fell not into sleep but into somewhere else.

It began with singing.

Not music, exactly. Not any melody that could be hummed or written down. It was older than melody—a sound like stone remembering it had once been liquid, like water remembering it had once been light. It came from everywhere at once, from beneath the floorboards of the dream, from behind the walls, from inside Rafael's own chest, and every note of it was in a language Rafael did not know and yet somehow understood, the way you understand warmth or falling or the taste of salt.

*Come home,* the song said. *Come home. The doors are open. Come home.*

And then Rafael was standing in a hall.

* * *

It was not a hall built by hands. That was the first thing the dream taught them—that the court beneath Mount Cael had not been carved but grown, or perhaps remembered into being, its pillars rising like the ribs of some vast sleeping animal, ribbed and pale and veined with veins of silver that pulsed faintly, slowly, the way a heart pulses when the body holding it is very old and very patient. The ceiling was so far above that it vanished into a darkness that was not empty; stars hung there, or things that had agreed to be stars, each one a small cold flame burning without heat.

The floor was smooth as still water and just as reflective. When Rafael looked down, they did not see their own face.

They saw hundreds of faces.

They were arranged in rows, these reflections, ranks upon ranks of them receding into the dark like a congregation, and none of them were Rafael's. They were old faces and young ones, round faces and hollow ones, and every single one of them was looking up—not at Rafael, but past Rafael, toward something at the far end of the hall that the dream would not let them see clearly. A dais. A desk. A shape seated behind it, patient as arithmetic.

And the singing went on. It came from the reflected faces. Their mouths were moving, all of them, all together, and though no breath stirred in the dream, the sound filled everything: *come home, come home, come home.*

A small hand took Rafael's.

Rafael turned, and the child was there—standing beside them in the dream as naturally as if she had always been there, which perhaps she had. She wore the same threadbare coat she wore by day, the one with the torn pocket Rafael kept meaning to mend. Her bare feet made no prints on the mirror-floor. And she was not afraid.

That was the thing that undid Rafael, later, when they woke: not the hall, not the faces, not the patient shape at the far end. The child stood in the middle of the Undercourt's memory as if it were her own kitchen. She pointed at the dais with the solemn authority of a very small tour guide, and then she pointed at the rows of faces, and then she looked up at Rafael and said—in the First Tongue, in that voice she never used waking, a voice like a bell rung underwater—one word.

Rafael did not know what it meant. But the dream translated it anyway, the way dreams do, pouring meaning directly into the understanding without bothering with language:

*Before.*

* * *

The second dream came three nights later, and this time the child led.

She walked Rafael through the hall's side door, through corridors that bent in ways corridors should not, and Rafael understood, with the strange clarity of dreams, that they were walking backward through time. The silver veins in the walls brightened as they went. The air grew warmer. Somewhere ahead, water dripped in a rhythm too regular to be natural, and when they emerged at last into an open gallery, Rafael saw that the regularity was a clock—a great wheel of dripping stone, marking hours that had stopped being counted a very long time ago.

Beyond the gallery, the mountain opened onto a city.

It spread below them like spilled starlight: terraces cut into the living rock, bridges of pale bone-white stone arching between towers that spiraled upward like instruments, like flutes, like throats. Every surface sang. Rafael could feel it through the dream-body's feet—the whole city humming at a frequency just below hearing, a chord held for centuries, sustained by ten thousand voices that had never once needed to stop for breath because they were not breathing. They were singing the way bells sing. They were singing the way mountains sing, deep down, where the rock remembers pressure and fire.

And the people—there were people. Thousands of them, moving through the terraces, tending gardens of luminous moss, gathering at fountains whose water rose instead of fell. They were beautiful in the way that things are beautiful when nothing about them is afraid. Children ran along the bridge-rails laughing in the First Tongue, and their laughter wove itself into the great chord and became part of it. An old woman sat at a loom weaving light into cloth, and the shuttle's rhythm was a drumbeat under the song.

The child stood at the gallery rail, both hands gripping the stone, and watched.

Her face did something Rafael had never seen it do in waking life. By day she was careful, watchful, a small creature folded shut like a knife. But here, looking down at the singing city, her face opened. Her eyes went wide and wet and full, and her mouth trembled, and she pressed one hand flat against her chest as though holding something in—or holding something together.

Then she spoke again, and again the dream translated:

*This was mine. This was ours. Listen—it is still going. They are still singing. No one told them to stop.*

And Rafael, standing in a dead city inside a borrowed dream, listened, and heard what the child heard: the chord was still sounding. Fainter now, worn thin, stretched across however many years lay between that terrace and this one—but sounding. Underneath the silence of the waking world, underneath the edicts and the fear-thinned streets of Vell, underneath twenty years of Rafael's careful small life, something beneath the mountain was still holding its note.

* * *

The third dream was the one Rafael would have refused to enter, if dreams allowed refusal.

There was no gentle walking backward this time. There was no tour. Rafael simply found themselves in the great hall again—the ribbed pillars, the star-dark ceiling, the mirror floor—and the hall was full.

Not with reflections this time. With people.

They stood in the ranks where the reflections had been, real and solid and warm-faced, and they were singing, all of them, the great chord swelling until the pillars themselves vibrated with it, and Rafael understood that this was the day. This was the last day. The air had the specific brightness of a moment that will never come again, the way the light before a storm has a color you only recognize afterward.

At the far end of the hall, the shape behind the dais resolved at last into something Rafael could almost see: a figure robed in ink-black, its face a page, its hands stained to the wrist with writing that moved. It sat behind a desk of black stone, and on the desk lay a book, and the book was open, and the book was enormous—each page taller than a man—and the ink on those pages glistened wetly, freshly, as though the writing were happening now, as though it had been happening forever.

The figure did not speak. It did not need to. It simply held up one stained hand, palm outward, and the singing faltered.

One voice at a time, the singers stopped.

Rafael watched it happen and could not look away, the way you cannot look away from a wound. A young man mid-verse went silent, his mouth still open around a word that would never be finished. A grandmother set down the song she had carried for eighty years and stood still, her hands folded, her face serene in a way that made Rafael's stomach turn, because serenity like that is not peace—it is surrender wearing peace's clothing. One by one, in order, as though following a list, as though their names were being read from the great wet pages, the people of the mountain closed their mouths and bowed their heads and stepped forward, out of the ranks, toward the desk.

And where they stepped, they left themselves behind.

Rafael saw it clearly, horribly: each person who walked to the desk left a shape in the air where they had stood, a human-shaped absence, a silhouette of light that lingered a moment and then folded inward, downward, sinking into the mirror-floor like a coin dropped into deep water. The floor swallowed them. The floor kept them. And the people who walked away from their own silhouettes walked differently—straighter, quieter, emptier, their eyes fixed on the ink-stained figure with perfect, terrible trust.

*They are not being dragged,* the dream translated, in the child's voice, in the child's grief. *No one is forcing them. They are signing. They think it is a bargain. They think they are buying something. Look at their faces—they are grateful.*

The last of the singers reached the desk. The hall went silent except for the drip of the stone clock, far away, keeping hours no one counted anymore.

And then, in the vast emptied hall, one voice remained.

It was small. It was high. It was coming from behind a pillar near the back, from a gap in the stonework barely wide enough for a child, and it was still singing. Singing the chord alone, the whole chord, every part of it, a single throat carrying what ten thousand had carried—an impossible, stubborn, magnificent sound, thin as wire and unbroken.

The ink-stained figure turned its page-face toward the sound.

And the singing stopped—not because the voice gave up, but because the pillar it hid behind shuddered, and the gap began, slowly, silently, to close.

The dream ended before Rafael could see whether the gap finished closing. They woke with their heart slamming and the child's name half-formed in their mouth, except they did not know it yet. They woke knowing only that they had witnessed a massacre conducted entirely with paperwork, and that somewhere in it, a child had hidden in the stone and sung until the stone tried to swallow her too.

* * *

In the morning, everything was ordinary, which felt obscene. The kettle. The bread. The child sitting at the table swinging legs too short to reach the floor, eating her porridge with the methodical seriousness of someone who has known hunger and does not intend to be casual about abundance. Rafael moved through the shop's morning rituals in a daze, dusting charms they no longer believed in, avoiding the ledger, avoiding the thought of Master Ondrel, avoiding the memory of the ink-stained figure—which was difficult, because every ledger in Vell suddenly looked like that book, every debt like that signature, every polite transaction like a small consenting betrayal.

The child watched Rafael avoid things. Children always watch more than adults credit.

After breakfast, she climbed down from her chair, went to the cold hearth, dipped her finger in the fine white ash, and wrote on the hearthstone. Not MINE this time. Something longer. Rafael crouched to read it, and the letters were careful, practiced, the handwriting of someone who had learned to write before she had learned anything else—learned it, perhaps, in a city of light where writing and singing were the same act.

The ash said: *ISOLDE.*

Rafael said it aloud, testing it, and the child's whole body changed. It was as though a hand long clenched had finally been given permission to open. She said it back—her voice, her rare underwater-bell voice, shaping the syllables of her own name in Common for the first time—and then she said it again in the First Tongue, and the two versions braided together in the little shop, and Rafael understood that they had just heard the same word spoken in two languages, and that this was important, though they would not know how important, how weaponizable, until much later.

Isolde. It meant, roughly, in the old lexicon Rafael had consulted in the forbidden archives—"she who remains." A name given, perhaps, to a child born late, after losses; a name that was already a prophecy. She who remains. The last unclaimed soul. The voice that kept singing when the pillar closed.

She wrote more, in the days that followed. Ash on the hearthstone, wiped and rewritten, wiped and rewritten, a slate that cost nothing and betrayed nothing. She taught Rafael the way children teach—without curriculum, without mercy, without letting you skip the parts that hurt. She drew the shapes of First Tongue letters, which were not letters at all really but knots, each character a small tied thing, a knot of meaning that could not be untied without changing what it held. She showed Rafael that in her language, there was no separate word for *song* and *name*. The same knot meant both. To sing someone's name was to hold them. To erase someone's name was to unsing them.

And that, Rafael realized one gray afternoon while Isolde traced knots in spilled flour on the table, was what the Tallyman had done. He had not killed the people of the mountain. He had unsung them. He had taken their names—their songs, their selves, the knots that held them together—and entered them in his book, and a name in that book was a name owned, and the people who walked to the desk had walked there because they believed, each of them, each small consenting soul, that they were trading the song for safety, the name for security, the self for survival.

Twenty years, Rafael thought, staring at the flour-knots. Twenty years I have been selling charms against exactly this. Little knotted things, little protections, little bargains. And every customer who bought one was making the same trade the mountain made, in miniature, in good faith, believing that small surrenders don't add up.

They add up. They add up into a hall full of silhouettes sinking into a mirror floor.

Isolde watched Rafael arrive at this understanding, and something in her expression softened—not pity, exactly. Recognition. She took Rafael's hand, the scarred scribe's hand, the hand that had copied a thousand edicts against magic, and pressed it flat against the table over the flour, over the knot that meant *name*, over the knot that meant *song*, and held it there, and looked up at Rafael with eyes that had seen a civilization sign itself away, and said one word in her underwater voice:

The dream translated it before Rafael's waking mind could: *Now you see. Now you can testify.*

Testify. The word landed in Rafael like a hook set in flesh. Because testimony was exactly what Rafael could not afford to give—to Wrenna, to the heretics, to anyone. Testimony required surviving. Testimony required a counterparty. Testimony, in the end, required standing in front of the desk and saying *no* out loud, in a language the desk understood.

That night, the dreams changed. Isolde stopped showing Rafael the past and began showing her the present—dreams of the Order's archive, of vault doors, of a great ledger with a binding of black stone. Rafael woke from the third such dream with the layout of a building they had never entered printed behind their eyes, and with the growing, dreadful, exhilarating suspicion that the child was not teaching them a language anymore.

She was teaching them a route.

And far across the sleeping city, in a narrow bed in the Inquisitors' Quarter, Inquisitor Serane Halvorsen dreamed, for the first time in years, of her sister's voice.
