## 9. Twenty Years of Interest

The Ledger did not close when Rafael dropped it.

It lay open on the archive floor where it had fallen, its pages breathing in the candle-draft like something asleep, and Rafael knelt over it the way one kneels over a wound—unable to look away, unable to press a hand to it, unable to do anything at all but bleed.

*Eriksen, R.* Born the ninth day of Thaw-Month, in the year the river froze twice. *Collateral: latent resonance, one soul, unpledged at time of entry. Held against founding account, Mount Cael. Interest accruing.*

Not debt. Not the debt they had spent twenty years paying down with ink-stained fingers and charm-sellers' lies and the slow erosion of every hour they were not sleeping. The word was there, plain as any edict they had ever copied in their neat, obedient hand: *collateral*. A thing held. A thing belonging to another party until such time as the primary obligation was satisfied.

Rafael read the entry eleven times. Twelve. The candle burned down a finger's width between readings, and each time the words resolved into the same shapes, the same terrible ordinary bookkeeping, as though the universe had written them in the voice of a clerk dictating inventory. *One soul. Unpledged at time of entry.* As if Rafael had been a cask in a cellar. As if they had been set on a shelf to age toward some purpose they had never consented to.

Their parents' names were not in the entry. That was the detail that undid them first, before the meaning even arrived—the absence. There was no signature, no mark of the sellers, nothing to hate, nothing to curse, no face to put to the transaction. Only the dry notation that the collateral had been *received* from unnamed parties, and the date, and the small chilling word *unpledged*, which meant only that Rafael's magic had lain dormant, waiting, like a coin buried too deep for anyone to spend.

The founders' bargain. Wrenna had said the words and Rafael had thought them history—old stone, old grief, someone else's sin. But here it was in the Order's own vault, in the Order's own ledger, entered in an archivist's careful hand: the city of Vell had been bought with more than Isolde's people. It had been bought with a child who had not yet been born. A child who grew up copying edicts against magic while the magic itself sat in a ledger beneath the temple, accruing interest.

Twenty years of interest.

Rafael laughed. It came out wrong—a single cracked syllable that echoed off the vault shelves and came back sounding like someone else's voice, a stranger's, frightened and thin. They pressed both hands flat against the cold flagstones and breathed until the laughing stopped, and then they could not remember how to breathe properly again, because every breath now belonged to an account.

They had chosen this life. That was the lie they had built their whole self around, the load-bearing wall of everything Rafael Eriksen believed about themselves. They had chosen the scribe's bond, chosen the debt, chosen the small safe compromises, chosen to sell charms by night that they copied condemnations by morning—and they had hated themselves for those choices with a quiet, patient, twenty-year contempt, believing all the while that contempt was deserved, that weakness was a moral failure, that if they had been braver or better they would have burned brighter and been consumed.

But there had been no choice. There had never been a choice. The debt had been written the day they were born. Every door that had closed before them, every narrow path, every time the world had seemed to contract around them like a fist—they had called it fate, called it temperament, called it their own smallness. It had been a ledger line. It had been arithmetic.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above, the temple bells rang the third hour after midnight, and Rafael flinched at the sound as if the bells themselves were collectors.

They took the Ledger.

It was not a decision so much as a reflex of the hands—the same reflex that had slipped charms into market-goers' palms for two decades, the thief's grace of a person whose body had always known what their mind refused to admit. They wrapped it in a scribe's oilcloth, bound it with archival cord, tucked it beneath their robe against their ribs, where it lay heavy and warm as a second heartbeat. Then they walked out of the vault past Wrenna's contact—a stooped brother of the third circle who kept his eyes fixed on his own shoes and his keys turning very quietly in his pocket—and up through the archive, out into the rain-washed dark, and did not stop walking until they reached the shop.

Isolde was awake.

Of course she was awake. She was sitting cross-legged on the floor beside the cold stove, wrapped in Rafael's oldest blanket, and the wards along the threshold were glowing faintly silver in her presence, the way they had glowed the storm-night she first appeared—as if the shop itself recognized her, or recognized what she carried. When Rafael shut the door behind them, she looked up, and her eyes went immediately to the bundle under their arm, and then to Rafael's face, and whatever she saw there made her rise without a sound.

She could not speak Common. She could not ask. She simply stood there, eight years old and older than the city, and waited, and the terrible patience of her—that patience of a child who had already survived the worst thing—was more than Rafael could bear.

"I found my name," Rafael said. Their voice sounded scraped. "In the Ledger. I found my name, and it isn't mine."

Isolde crossed the room and took the oilcloth bundle from them with both hands, solemnly, the way she did everything, and carried it to the table, and unwrapped it, and turned pages with fingers that understood old paper better than most archivists ever would. Rafael watched her find the entry. Watched her read it. Watched her face change—not with shock. With recognition. With the weary confirmation of someone who has suspected a thing for a long time and finally sees it written down.

Then she looked at Rafael and said a word in the First Tongue.

Rafael had heard the language now for weeks—in her sleep-talk, in her hummed fragments, in the dreams that had begun arriving uninvited, thick and strange, full of singing stone and a court lit by no flame. In waking hours it was music without meaning. But this word landed in Rafael's chest like a key finding its lock, and understanding arrived whole, unbidden, terrifying in its clarity:

*I know.*

"You know," Rafael whispered. "You knew. You've known since—" Since when? Since the dreams? Since the night the wards answered her? Since the mountain? "How do I understand you?"

Isolde tilted her head. And then, gently, deliberately, she spoke again—not one word this time but a sentence, low and unhurried, and Rafael stood in their own shop listening to a dead language come alive in a living throat, and understood every syllable.

*The tongue knows its own. You have been carrying it unspoken all your life. It is why the wards wake for you. It is why you dreamed our songs before I ever sang them. Collateral is not taken away, scribe. It is held close. The mountain has kept your magic warm for twenty years, and warmth teaches.*

"That can't be—" Rafael gripped the table's edge. The wood was solid; the wood had always been solid; the wood was one of the few things left that made sense. "I don't speak it. I've never spoken it."

*You speak it the way the seed speaks the tree,* Isolde said, and her hands moved as she spoke, small precise gestures, and Rafael realized with a lurch that they had seen those gestures before—in their own fingers, tapping out rhythms against the desk during long transcriptions, patterns they had always assumed were nervous habit. *Every night you slept, some part of you lay in the mountain's keeping, and the keeping is not silent. Nothing in the Undercourt is silent. You have been listening your whole life. You simply called it dreaming.*

The room tilted. Rafael sat down—missed the chair, ended up on the floor, did not care—and put their head in their hands, and the Ledger lay open on the table above them with its terrible tidy columns, and outside, Vell went on being Vell: a cart rattling over cobbles, a drunk singing three streets off, the watch-cry calling the hour, all of it purchased, all of it paid for, all of it mortgaged against souls that included, apparently, Rafael Eriksen's.

* * *

Grief, Rafael discovered, had layers, and they fell through them one at a time through the rest of that night.

First there was the anger, hot and useless, aimed at parents thirty years dead who had traded their unborn child's magic for the safety of a city—no, not even for the safety of the city; for the *promise* of safety, a protection racket written in a founding contract, and Rafael would never know whether they had done it desperately, weeping, coerced, or whether they had signed with a merchant's shrug and gone home to supper. The ledger did not record motives. Ledgers never did. That was the whole horror of ledgers.

Then the anger cooled into something worse: inventory. Rafael sat on the floor with their back against the chair leg and audited their own life, line by line, looking for the hand of the Undercourt in every disappointment. The scholarship that had fallen through at sixteen. The master scribe who had praised their work and never advanced them. The friendships that had thinned inexplicably, the loves that had stalled just short of commitment, the persistent sense—which Rafael had carried so long they'd mistaken it for personality—that they were a guest in their own existence, present on tolerance, subject to recall.

Had any of it been engineered? Or was it simply that a person who believes, bone-deep, that they are held—that their self is not entirely their own property—lives differently, chooses smaller, reaches shorter? Did it matter? Was there a difference between a cage and a creature trained to walk in circles?

The candle died. Rafael did not light another.

Somewhere in the black hour before dawn, Isolde came and sat beside them on the floor. She did not touch them at first. She sat with her shoulder a hand's width from theirs, radiating warmth like a small banked fire, and let the silence be what it was. When she finally leaned against Rafael's side, it was the lean of someone claiming a place, not offering comfort—comfort, Rafael understood dimly, was a currency she had learned to distrust, given how rarely anyone in her short long life had offered it without a price. This was different. This was simply: *here. This spot is mine now. You are in it.*

And Rafael, who had spent twenty years being collateral, being held, being an asset with accrued interest, found that being claimed by a child was an entirely different thing than being claimed by a mountain.

"Tell me," Rafael said hoarsely, into the dark. "Tell me what I am. All of it. Don't soften it."

So Isolde told them.

She spoke for a long time, and the First Tongue poured through the dark room like water finding its level, and Rafael understood it all—not translated, nothing so mechanical as translation, but *received*, the way one receives heat from a flame. She told them of the court beneath the mountain, which had not begun wicked. It had begun as a treasury, a place where people pledged things they could not bear to carry: a memory of shame, a grief too heavy, a talent that brought only envy. The Tallyman—who had not always been the Tallyman, who had perhaps once been a person—had kept the accounts fairly, at first. Fairness had been the whole seduction. Fair terms, fair rates, fair storage. People had come willingly, generation upon generation, pawning pieces of themselves against hard winters and harder wars, and the court had grown vast and patient and increasingly indifferent to the distinction between what was pawned and what was owned.

Then the kingdoms above had wanted the mountain's protections—the deep roads, the warding stones, the weather-binding—and the founders of Vell, like Rafael's parents after them, had looked at their children and calculated. The bargain had been struck: the city's safety, perpetual, in exchange for the souls of the First Tongue's speakers, who were the court's oldest depositors and therefore, in the Tallyman's arithmetic, the most convertible assets. The singing Rafael heard in dreams had been real. It had been the sound of an entire people being entered into a book.

All except one. One speaker missed. One entry left blank—one line in the great ledger marked *pending*, waiting twenty years and more, because the last unclaimed soul of the First Tongue had been hidden, smuggled, lost, and had wandered at last through a storm to the door of a scribe whose own name sat two pages earlier in the same book.

"We're in it together," Rafael said. The realization came quietly, almost gently, after all the violence of the night. "Your line and mine. We've been in the same ledger all along. Two entries in the same account."

Isolde nodded against Rafael's shoulder. Then she lifted her hand and, in the ash of the long-dead hearth, wrote four characters of the First Tongue—characters Rafael had never studied and read without hesitation, the way one reads one's own handwriting.

*Sister-entry,* it said. Or possibly *sibling-line*. The grammar was fluid in a way Common refused to be. The meaning was not.

Rafael looked at the ash-writing, at the small sure hand that had once written MINE on their doorstep and now wrote this, and something broke in them—but it broke the way ice breaks on a river in thaw, not the way stone breaks. Beneath the collapse there was movement. Beneath the ruin of everything Rafael had believed about their own smallness, their own chosen cowardice, their own well-earned mediocrity, there was current. Cold, yes. Terrifying, yes. But moving.

They had not been weak. They had been *held*. And held things, Isolde was telling them in her patient ash-and-dream language, could be released.

"The interest," Rafael said slowly, watching their breath fog in the cold room. "The entry says interest accruing. Twenty years of interest on latent resonance." A laugh stirred in their chest again, but it came out different this time—harder, edged, almost hungry. "Do you know what I've got in that cellar, Isolde? Twenty years of charms I sold and never once used myself. Twenty years of sparks I swallowed instead of spending. I thought I was hiding it. Hiding it from the Order, hiding it from Ondrel, hiding it from myself. But you're saying—"

*You were storing it,* Isolde finished, and her eyes in the dark were bright as coins. *Every unspent spark is still yours. The court holds the pledge, not the principal. Principal stays with the debtor until collection.* Her mouth curved—the first true smile Rafael had ever seen on her, sharp and young and eight years old and ancient. *And collection, scribe, requires presentation of the account. Which means the Tallyman must show you the book. Which means—*

"—we go down," Rafael said. "We walk into the Undercourt, and I ask to see my statement."

Outside, the sky was going grey over the rooftops of Vell, the color of old vellum, of erased mistakes, of pages waiting to be written on. Rafael Eriksen sat on the floor of their own shop—except, they thought wryly, it was hardly theirs, was it, probably it was mortgaged somewhere too, probably the very floorboards had liens against them—and felt, for the first time in twenty years, not small.

Felt, instead, like a figure in a ledger who had just learned to read.

Which was when the knock came at the door—three measured blows, unhurried, official—and a voice through the wood, smooth as sealing wax:

"Scribe Eriksen. Master Ondrel requests the pleasure of your attendance. Today."
