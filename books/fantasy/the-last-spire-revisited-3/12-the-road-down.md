## 12. The Road Down

They left Vell before the fourth bell, when the fog lay so thick on the cobbles that the city seemed to be drowning in its own breath.

Rafael had dressed in the dark, moving through the shop they would never see again with the blind efficiency of grief. Charms still hung from their hooks—little braided knots of horsehair and river-clay, each one harmless, each one a crime. Twenty years of small mercies, abandoned like shed skin. Rafael had taken only what mattered: Wrenna's map, folded into a oilcloth packet against the chest; a knife; bread wrapped in linen; and Isolde's hand, which had found theirs in the dark without being sought, the way roots find water.

The back door of the scribe's shop opened onto the tannery lane, and the tannery lane opened onto the marsh road, and the marsh road ran north and east toward a mountain that most people in Vell spoke of only the way one speaks of a grave: with lowered voices and averted eyes.

Mount Cael stood two hundred miles away. Rafael intended to cover them before the Order finished its morning prayers.

* * *

The fog thinned as the city fell behind, and then it was dawn, grey and slow, and the marshes spread out on either side of the raised causeway like a held breath. Reeds stood in the black water by the ten thousand. Herons moved among them with the patience of monks. Somewhere out in the wet dark, frogs sang in ragged chorus, and Rafael thought—not for the first time—that everything in this country sang except its people.

Isolde rode behind them on the borrowed mule, her small arms locked around Rafael's waist, her chin tucked between their shoulder blades. She had not slept. Rafael could feel it in the way she held herself: not loose the way children are when they trust the world to carry them, but rigid, watchful, a small animal conserving every advantage. She had been like that since the sanctuary scattered—since the night Rafael stood in Wrenna's kitchen and said, *the Order knows, you have days*, and watched a room full of frightened people become a room full of people deciding whom to blame.

Some had cursed them. An old glassworker named Tam had spat at Rafael's feet and said, *you brought the storm to our door*, and Rafael had taken the words because there was no honest reply. It was true. Every kindness Rafael had accepted had drawn a line straight from Wrenna's kitchen to the Inquisitor's ledger. Serane Halvorsen was coming. Ondrel was coming. The whole patient machinery of the Order was grinding itself awake, and all its teeth were pointed north-east, toward two fugitives on a borrowed mule.

And yet.

And yet Wrenna had pressed the map into Rafael's hands with both of hers, as if handing over something alive. *The salt road past Ferrow's Cross,* she'd said. *Then the pilgrim tracks—no rider takes pilgrim tracks, too narrow for horses. Brannoc will meet you at the gate. He'll know you're coming; he always does.* And then, gripping Rafael's wrists hard enough to bruise: *You come back down that mountain, scribe. You hear me? Whatever you find down there, you bring her home and you come back, because someone has to tell it true, and I'm too old and too ugly to be believed.*

Rafael had promised nothing. Promises were debts, and Rafael had spent the last three days learning exactly how much debts cost.

* * *

They made Ferrow's Cross by midday—a crossroads hamlet of six houses and a shrine whose saint had been chiseled off the plinth during the Purification, leaving a headless block of granite that farmers still left offerings to, out of habit or defiance. A turnip here. A twist of wool there. Rafael looked at the little pile and felt something in their chest go tight and strange.

People remembered. That was the thing the Order had never understood, in two centuries of burning books and scraping saints off stones. You could outlaw a word but you could not unteach a tongue. The faith went underground and grew sideways, like heather, like root systems under a pavement—and one day the pavement cracked.

They bought eggs and hard cheese from a woman who did not ask questions, which cost double, and watered the mule at the trough, and Rafael kept their hood up and their eyes down and felt Isolde's grip tighten whenever a stranger passed too near. At the edge of the village, where the salt road climbed away from the wetlands toward the high moors, Rafael paused and looked back once.

Vell was invisible already, dissolved into haze and distance. Somewhere inside it were the temple archives, the vault with its broken lock, the ledger with its burned page. Somewhere inside it was Master Ondrel, who owned twenty years of Rafael's life and knew it now—or would, as soon as the breach was traced. And somewhere inside it, walking the streets with her grey cloak and her patient eyes, was Serane Halvorsen, who had offered to look away and been refused, and who must by now understand that refusal for exactly what it was: not betrayal, but the first honest thing either of them had done.

*She'll follow us,* Rafael thought. Not as an enemy. That was the terrible part. Serane would follow as a woman chasing something of her own, and Rafael could no longer say whether that made her more dangerous or less.

"Rafael." Isolde's voice, small and rough from disuse. Not the First Tongue—she was pointing, urgent, at Rafael's satchel, where the map packet showed a corner of oilcloth.

"I know. We're going." Rafael swung up onto the mule. "Hold on."

Isolde held on. She held on the way she did everything—absolutely, as if the act itself were a vow.

* * *

The moors began that evening, and with them the wind.

It came off the high ground in long, low sweeps, bending the heather flat, carrying the smell of peat and cold stone and, faintly, iron—the old iron smell of the mountain far to the northeast, though it was still days away and invisible below the horizon. The salt road narrowed to a track, the track to a path, and the path wandered up onto treeless rolling country where a rider could be seen a mile off and there was nowhere to hide except the hollows between the hills.

Rafael counted those hills like beads on a rosary. Behind each one, a moment of blindness. After each one, the same ritual: stop, listen, scan the skyline behind them for the dust-plume of riders. Twice they saw movement and both times it resolved into sheep. The third time it was a shepherd, who looked at them with the flat incurious gaze of the moorland-born and touched his forehead and moved on, and Rafael's heart took a long time coming down.

They walked after dark, which was madness on that terrain, but rest was a luxury the schedule forbade. Ondrel's deadline had been moved up; the sanctuary had scattered early; every hour they lingered on the open moor was an hour the Order's outriders gained. So Rafael led the mule by its halter and Isolde walked with one fist knotted in the back of Rafael's coat, and the moon rose fat and white over the heather and threw their shadows out long and thin across the ground, three figures and a beast making a small crooked procession through all that emptiness.

It was during those night walks that the dreams came again.

Not sleeping, exactly—there was no time for sleep—but the trance-state that deep exhaustion falls into, where the mind walks while the body walks. In that state Isolde reached for Rafael, the way she had since the first night, and the shared dream opened like a door.

Heather again, but different—heather under a warmer sky, on slopes that climbed toward a mountain wreathed in cloud, and everywhere the sound of singing. Dozens of voices. Hundreds. A language rising off the mountainside like heat-shimmer, and Rafael understood none of it and all of it, the way you understand music. This was Cael before. Cael whole. Cael singing.

And then the dream turned, as it always turned, and the warmth drained out of the light, and Rafael saw the doors of the Undercourt standing open, and the singers walking in two by two, unafraid, because the bargains had been sweet and the paper had been fair and no one had read to the bottom of the page.

Isolde, in the dream, was small even by the dream's standards. She stood apart from the paired walkers, holding someone's hand—an adult's hand, gone now, unnameable—and she did not go in. She was the one who did not go in. Two centuries ago, or however long the bargain had stood, she had stood at that threshold and refused, or been overlooked, or been hidden by whoever loved her enough to lie to a king of accountants. And the court had waited. Courts of that kind can afford patience. They had entered her in the ledger as outstanding and closed the book, and the book had waited two hundred years for a scribe with a flaw in his blood to walk through the wrong door.

Rafael woke—or surfaced—on the fifth morning, stiff and cold, with Isolde shaking their shoulder and pointing northeast with a gravity that made their stomach drop before they understood why.

The mountain was visible.

It rose above the moorland horizon like something that had been waiting to be noticed: blue-grey at this distance, crowned with cloud, unmistakably shaped—as if the land had once tried to build a spire and given up halfway. The old spire of Vell had been built, Wrenna said, in imitation of it, by founders who wanted their godless city to have a horizon worth kneeling to. Looking at the true thing now, Rafael felt the imitation for what it was. This was not architecture. This was intention, older than any city, wearing rock the way a man wears a coat.

Isolde stared at it, and her face did something Rafael had never seen it do in all their weeks together. It opened. All the vigilance, the animal caution, the locked-down stillness—gone, and underneath was simply a child looking at the place where everyone she had ever loved had gone.

She said a word.

It was not a word of the First Tongue. Rafael knew enough of that language now to know its shape—those long vowels, that gravity, each syllable worn smooth like river stone. This word was short and clumsy and new-minted, a coin struck fresh from rough metal, and it was Common, and it was:

"Home."

Rafael's hands went still on the mule's halter.

Isolde pointed at the mountain. "Home," she said again, firmer, tasting it, testing whether the word would bear her weight. And then she looked up at Rafael, and her eyes asked the question the grammar could not: *do you understand what I am telling you?*

Rafael understood. That was the wound of it. The child had learned her first word of the conquerors' language, the tongue of edicts and burnings and scraped-off saints, and the first thing she had chosen to say in it was not *food* or *hurt* or *run*. It was the name of the place where her people had sold themselves. She was not fleeing to safety. She was walking, bright-eyed and certain, toward the mouth that had swallowed her family, and she called it home because for her it was not a metaphor—it was address. It was where the souls went. It was where she had been headed, all along, alone at a threshold, holding a dead person's hand.

"It might not be," Rafael said quietly, in Common, knowing she could not follow the words. Then, in the halting First Tongue Isolde had taught them dream by dream, syllable by patient syllable: *"Home is a door. Doors open both ways."*

Isolde considered this with the seriousness she gave everything. Then she took Rafael's hand and pressed it flat against the mule's warm neck, over the heartbeat, and looked up with an expression that needed no language at all: *and yet we go.*

* * *

They crossed the river Sennet on the second day, poling a leaky ferry flat across brown water while the ferryman watched them with eyes that missed nothing and priced everything. On the far bank, the land changed. The moor gave way to pine forest, dark and resinous, and the salt road became the pilgrim track Wrenna had promised—barely a deer trail, switchbacking upward through trees old enough to remember the Purification firsthand. Here the mule could not pass, and Rafael turned it loose with a slap on its rump and a silent apology, and they went on foot, climbing, climbing, the mountain growing overhead until it filled half the sky and the air thinned and cooled and smelled of snowmelt and stone.

On the third day of the climb, Rafael heard the hoofbeats.

Not close. Not yet. But below them, back along the forested slope, faint through the pines—the rhythmic iron knock of shod horses on rock, several animals, moving with the unhurried confidence of riders following a certainty rather than a guess. Rafael pulled Isolde off the track into a stand of boulders and waited, hardly breathing, while the sound swelled and faded and swelled again, never quite reaching them, paralleling them somewhere below like a second pulse.

"They've found the ferryman," Rafael whispered. There was no other explanation. The moors had offered a hundred false trails; the ferryman had offered one true one, and men who price everything sell anything. "We have maybe half a day. Less."

Isolde tugged their sleeve. She pointed up—through the trees, to where the slope steepened toward a ridgeline—and then made a gesture Rafael had learned on the road: fingers walking, then fingers falling, then a flat hand held level. *Up fast, over the top, safe.*

Above them, the ridge cut sharp against a sky going the color of pewter. Beyond it, if Wrenna's map told truth, the track descended into a high valley of grey scree, and at the valley's end stood the gate—gates of salt and bone, the old stories said, though the stories disagreed about everything else. And beyond the gate, the Undercourt, with its patient ledgers and its courteous king, waiting for the last entry on its oldest page.

Every instinct Rafael owned said *run the other way.* Every debt they carried said the same. And yet the child's hand in theirs was warm and certain, and the mountain stood over them like a sentence awaiting completion, and somewhere below, the Order's riders were climbing through the pines with Wrenna's network in their fists and Serane Halvorsen—Serane, who had lost a sister to this machine and knew it now—riding at their head or riding after them or perhaps, if there was any mercy left in the world, riding neither.

"We go up," Rafael said.

They climbed. The light failed, the wind rose, and the first flakes of snow came down fine and dry as ash—the same ash, Rafael thought wildly, in which a mute child had once written MINE on a shop floor, claiming a stranger with four letters and a storm.

Near dark, cresting the ridge, they saw the valley: a bowl of grey stone under the mountain's shoulder, silent, untenanted, and at its far end, catching the last light like a blade laid flat—

A wall. And set into the wall, vast and pale and older than any nation Rafael could name, a gate.

Before it, small against all that stone, a figure stood waiting, as Wrenna had promised. He raised one hand as they approached—not in greeting, Rafael would learn later, but in warning—and when they drew close enough to see him plainly, Rafael saw that his throat bore a scar old and pale as moonlight, and that he carried chalk, and that he was already writing on the gatepost, in careful verse, the first words he meant them to read:

*Turn back, debtor. Buyers always collect.*

Behind them, far below in the darkening forest, the hoofbeats had stopped.

They had not gone away.
