## 13. Gates of Salt and Bone

The mountain announced itself long before we saw it.

We heard it first—a low, continuous hum in the ground beneath our feet, like a hymn sung by something that had forgotten the words and kept singing anyway. Isolde felt it through her boots and stopped dead on the switchback trail, one small hand pressed flat against the rock of the mountainside as though taking a pulse.

"It's singing," she signed to me, using the gestures we had made together on the road—our private grammar of necessity, cobbled from her hands and my stubbornness. *The mountain is singing.*

"I hear it," I said.

*It's singing the counting song.* Her fingers moved quickly, agitated, the signs tumbling over each other. *The one they sang when the tallying started. When they called your name and you had to answer or be written down as absent.*

I did not know what to say to that, so I said nothing, which was becoming a habit I did not like in myself. I offered her my hand instead, and she took it, and we climbed.

Three days out of Vell, the road had given up pretending to be a road. It narrowed to a goat track stitched along the shoulder of the foothills, and then to a scramble of scree, and then to nothing but the mountain's own intentions. The Order's riders had fallen behind at the river—we had burned the ferry landing behind us, a small arson I tried not to think about too closely, because thinking about it meant admitting I had become someone who burned things—but Wrenna's map showed no other way down, and so we climbed upward instead, toward Mount Cael, toward the sealed gate that every map in Vell marked with a warning rather than a destination.

The air changed as we rose. It grew thin, yes, but also strange—sweetish, mineral, like the smell of a coin held too long in the mouth. And always the hum, deepening, until by the second day I felt it in my teeth and in the roots of my hair, felt it in the scar tissue where my wards used to sit before I'd spent them all crossing the borderlands. The mountain was not silent. The mountain had never been silent. We simply lived too far above its voice to hear it, the way fish do not hear the sea.

Isolde heard everything. She walked with her head tilted, her whole body a listening instrument, and sometimes she would stop and sign fragments to me without context—*the third verse is wrong, someone is singing it flat; there are more voices today than yesterday; the low one at the bottom never sleeps*—and I would nod as though I understood, because she needed me to nod, and because the alternative was to admit how far beyond my depth we both were.

On the morning of the fourth day, we came around the mountain's northern flank and saw the gate.

* *

I had imagined a door. That was the failure of imagination I could not shake—that somewhere in the living rock there would be a door, however vast, however ancient, something with hinges or seams or at least an edge, something that admitted the concept of opening. What stood before us instead was a wall of salt.

It rose three hundred feet if it rose one, sheer and glistening, buttressed against the grey stone of the mountain itself like a cataract grown solid. The light of the late morning caught it and broke into colors I had no names for—not the clean spectrum of rain, but something older and muddier, amber and rust and a green like pond ice. Salt veins ran through it in branching rivers, and where the sun struck those veins they glowed faintly from within, each one lit like a lamp behind horn. The wall sweated in the cold air; beads of brine crawled slowly down its face and pooled at its base in shallow terraces that steamed faintly, though the morning was cold enough to frost our breath.

And carved into the salt, at the height of a man's shoulder and repeated in descending rows into the depths below, were names.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Cut in a script I almost recognized—the First Tongue, but formalized, monumentalized, each character rendered with a precision that spoke of tools and patience and terrible institutional care. The topmost row was worn nearly smooth, its names half-erased by centuries of salt-sweat, unreadable now, their letters melting back into the wall that recorded them. The lower rows were sharper. The lowest row of all, just above the terraced pools, had been cut within living memory. I knew this the way one knows a fresh grave among old ones—by the rawness of the edges, by the fact that the salt had not yet begun to soften the strokes.

Isolde walked to the wall and put her palm against it.

She did not flinch, though the surface must have been cold enough to burn. She stood there for a long moment, reading, her lips moving silently through the shapes of the names, and then she turned to me with an expression I had learned to dread on the road: not fear exactly, but recognition. The look of someone finding their own street burned down.

*These are the sold ones,* she signed. One-handed, because the other still touched the wall. *Not all of them. Only the ones who were paid for honestly.*

"What does that mean?" I asked. "Paid for honestly?"

But before she could answer—or perhaps because she had already answered, and the mountain had heard—a sound came from the base of the wall, from a fissure in the living stone beside the salt that I had taken for shadow.

A bell. Small, cracked, rung once.

* *

He stepped out of the fissure as though the mountain had exhaled him, and my first thought—and I am ashamed of it, and I record it anyway—was *demon*. My second thought was *old man*, and my third, arriving after the panic had ebbed enough to let actual seeing through, was *gatekeeper*, because that was what he plainly was: a man who belonged to a threshold the way barnacles belong to pilings.

He was tall and bent both, stooped like a question mark, with a beard gone the yellow-grey of old ivory and skin weathered to the texture of the salt itself—cracked, translucent at the knuckles, faintly luminous where the light caught it. He wore layers of undyed wool gone grey with age and dust, and around his neck, on a cord of braided hair, hung a slate the size of a serving platter, blank on both sides. At his belt: chalk in a leather cup, and a rag, and a small hammer of the kind jewelers use, and a chisel.

He looked at Isolde first. Of course he did. Everyone who had ever truly seen her had looked at her first.

Then he looked at me, and his eyes were pale as weak tea and terribly, patiently awake, and I understood that whatever else this man was, he had been standing at this gate watching the world come and go since before I was born, and very little that arrived here surprised him.

Isolde, however, surprised him. Because she dropped my hand, crossed the frozen ground between them, and bowed to him—deeply, formally, the bow of a child greeting an elder of some court that no longer existed above ground. And then she signed to him. Not our makeshift road-signs. Something older, fuller, her hands moving with a fluency I had never seen from her, a complete language of gesture that I suddenly understood must have been the native tongue of the soul-court itself—the language of those who had lost their voices to the bargain, or never traded them in the first place.

The old man went very still.

Then he answered her.

His hands were not quick—they moved like branches in a slow wind, stiff-jointed, deliberate—but they were certain, and they were eloquent, and watching the two of them converse in silence at the foot of that weeping wall of salt was like watching two instruments tuned to each other finally play. I stood apart, holding Wrenna's map I no longer needed, understanding perhaps one sign in twenty, feeling precisely what I had felt my entire life in the scriptorium: present, necessary for the ink, excluded from the meaning.

At last the old man turned to me. He took the slate from around his neck, unslung it with care, laid it flat against the salt wall, and wrote.

His handwriting was beautiful. That was the first thing. Even upside down, even in chalk, it was the hand of a man who had been writing for a century with nothing else to do—each letter placed like a stone in a wall that would have to stand forever.

**YOU BRING THE LAST ENTRY,** he wrote. **SHE IS EXPECTED. SHE HAS ALWAYS BEEN EXPECTED.**

"We've come to end the entry," I said. "To close it. To take her home."

He regarded me for a moment with those tea-pale eyes. Then he wiped the slate with the rag, unhurried, and wrote again.

**HOME IS A WORD THE COURT DOES NOT KNOW. IT HAS NO LEDGER FOR IT. COME INSIDE AND YOU WILL LEARN WHAT WORDS IT DOES KEEP.**

He turned and walked toward the fissure, expecting us to follow, because men who have guarded a gate for a hundred years do not ask whether you will follow. They simply begin, and trust gravity to do the rest.

I hesitated. Isolde did not. She caught my sleeve and pulled, and her signing was urgent now, stripped down to our simple shared vocabulary: *Come. Come now. He knows the way. He knows the songs. He knows the flat verse.*

"Wait," I said—to the old man, to the mountain, to my own cowardice, I'm not sure which. "Before we go in. Tell me what we're walking into. Tell me what the court does."

The old man stopped. He did not turn around right away. When he did, his face had changed—not hardened, but settled, the way water settles when the wind dies, and I saw that beneath the age and the patience there was grief of such long standing that it had become structural, load-bearing, part of how he stood upright.

He wrote for a long time. Longer than he had written anything yet. The chalk whispered, and the mountain hummed beneath us, and Isolde watched the old man's face with an expression of dawning sorrow, as if she could read the words before he finished them.

Then he turned the slate.

**THEY WILL TELL YOU THE COURT STEALS SOULS. THE PRIESTS SAY IT. THE BALLADS SAY IT. A THIEF IS EASY TO HATE. A THIEF TAKES WHAT IS NOT HIS.**

He paused. His hand, I noticed, was trembling very slightly, and he steadied it against the salt wall, against the carved names, resting his palm on them the way Isolde had, the way one rests a hand on the shoulder of the dead.

**BUT LOOK AT THIS WALL. EVERY NAME HERE WAS BOUGHT. NOT ONE WAS STOLEN. SOME SOLD THEMSELVES. SOME WERE SOLD BY FATHERS, MOTHERS, KINGS WHO NEEDED RAIN OR PEACE OR ONE MORE HARVEST. THE COURT PAYS FAIR. THE COURT PAYS GENEROUSLY. THAT IS ITS GENIUS, SCRIBE. ANY ROBBER CAN BE RESISTED. BUT WHO RESISTS A GOOD PRICE?**

The chalk hovered. He looked at me, and I looked back, and I thought of a ledger in a vault beneath a temple in Vell, and a line in it bearing my own name, entered on the day I was born, in a transaction I had never agreed to and had benefited from every day of my life.

**BUYERS ALWAYS COLLECT,** he wrote. **THAT IS THE FIRST LAW OF THE DEEP PLACES, OLDER THAN THE COURT ITSELF. REMEMBER IT WHEN THE TALLYMAN SMILES AT YOU. HE WILL SMILE. HE IS KIND THE WAY WINTER IS KIND TO THE DYING—HE ONLY FINISHES WHAT YOU STARTED.**

He wiped the slate. He slung it back around his neck. He picked up his little jeweler's hammer and his chisel, and I saw then what work a gatekeeper does at a sealed gate: he maintained the wall. He recut the eroding names. For a hundred years, this man had kept legible the record of everyone his neighbors had sold, carving letter by letter while the salt wept them away, alone, voiceless, in the cold.

Isolde touched his sleeve. He looked down at her. She signed something slow and solemn, and whatever it was, it landed in him visibly, like a stone dropped into a well whose bottom you had thought you knew.

Then he led us into the fissure, and the mountain closed its throat behind us, and the humming became a chord, and the chord became a choir, and we began to descend.

* *

The passage down was not carved; it was grown, in the way that teeth are grown—layered, patient, slightly wrong. The walls were veined with the same glowing salt as the gate, and the veins pulsed faintly, dimming and brightening in rhythms almost but not quite synchronized, so that walking through that corridor felt like walking through the inside of some enormous sleeping heart. The air grew warmer and thicker. The sweet mineral taste strengthened until I could taste nothing else. Somewhere far below, water moved—no, not water. Pages. It sounded like pages turning, ten thousand of them, endlessly, in a wind that never reached us.

Isolde stayed close to me now, closer than she had on any mile of the road. Whatever she read in the old man's face at the gate, whatever the walls were saying to her in their language of pulse and glow, it had frightened her in a way the Order's riders never had. She held the hem of my coat in one fist, the way children hold the hem of a parent in crowds, and I found that I did not want her to let go, and that this frightened me more than the mountain did—because wanting things, I had learned, was how debts began.

The old man walked ahead, tapping his chisel against the wall at intervals—one tap, two taps, pause, one tap. At first I thought it was habit, a blind man's cane of sound. Then I realized the walls answered. Each tap drew a faint flare from the nearest vein of salt, and the flares traveled ahead of us down the passage, flickering from vein to vein, announcing us.

*Telling them,* I thought. *Telling the court we're coming.*

I caught the old man's sleeve. He stopped. He looked back at me with those patient tea-pale eyes, and I pointed at the traveling lights, and I made my voice as steady as I could.

"You're announcing us."

He considered me for a long moment. Then he took down the slate and wrote, in his terrible beautiful hand:

**YES.**

"That's it? Just—yes?"

**THEY ALREADY KNOW, SCRIBE. THEY HAVE KNOWN SINCE THE CHILD SPOKE HER FIRST WORD IN YOUR SHOP. THE WALLS OF CAEL ARE THE WALLS OF THE LEDGER. THERE IS NOTHING DOWN HERE THAT IS NOT ALREADY WRITTEN.** He paused, chalk resting on slate, and then added, smaller:

**THE ANNOUNCEMENT IS COURTESY. COURTESY IS ALL A GATEKEEPER HAS LEFT TO GIVE.**

He resumed walking. I stood a moment longer in the pulsing dark, listening to the page-turning wind below, feeling Isolde's fist knotted in my coat, feeling the twenty years of hoarded magic sitting in my chest like coins in a purse I had never dared open.

Buyers always collect.

Ahead, the passage opened onto light—not torchlight, not daylight, but the white glare of paper under a lamp—and the old man's silhouette stood framed against it, waiting, his slate already raised, chalk moving, writing the only warning he had left in him:

**WELCOME TO THE MARKET. SPEND CAREFULLY.**

Beyond his shoulder, in galleries carved like the inside of a cathedral built by accountants, the Undercourt glittered—and something in the glittering turned, as one, to look at us.
