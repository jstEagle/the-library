## 14. The Market of Owed Things

The stairs went down longer than any staircase had a right to go.

Rafael counted the steps at first—scribe's habit, the mind grasping for order when the body wants to flee—and gave up somewhere past four hundred, when the numbers began to feel like a prayer no god was listening to. The stone underfoot was warm. That was the first wrongness that refused to be explained away. Mountains are cold in their bones; Rafael had climbed enough of Mount Cael's upper paths as a boy to know the chill that lived in granite like an old tenant. But this stone was blood-warm, faintly pulsing beneath their boots, and when they pressed a palm against the wall during a rest—their legs shaking, Isolde riding on Brannoc's back with her cheek against his shoulder blade—they felt it: a rhythm. Slow. Patient. A heartbeat measured not in seconds but in centuries.

"It's the ledgers," Brannoc wrote later, on the slate he carried slung across his chest like a breastplate. His hand moved in quick, sure strokes; he had clearly written more words than he had ever spoken. "Every contract signed here is copied into the mountain itself. It remembers what it holds. That is why it breathes."

*What it holds.* Rafael turned the phrase over and felt its weight settle somewhere below their ribs.

Isolde had been quiet for two days—not her usual quiet, which was a listening kind of silence, but something tighter. She held Rafael's sleeve with two fingers, always two fingers, as if gripping harder would admit how much she needed the grip. And she flinched every time the walls hummed.

Because they did hum. As they descended past the salt-crusted gate and into the galleries proper, the sound grew from a suggestion to a fact: a vast, low chord woven out of ten thousand smaller sounds, none of them distinct, all of them human. Voices. Or the memory of voices. Rafael thought of the archive vaults of the Order, the rustle of ten thousand documents breathing dry air, and understood with a lurch that this was the same thing—except here, the documents were alive.

They came down the last flight into light, and Rafael stopped walking.

* * *

No one had told them there would be light.

The gallery opened above them like the ribcage of some enormous patient animal, vaulted in black stone veined with threads of silver that glowed without flickering—steady, unwavering, the way lamplight never could. The veins ran everywhere, branching along arches and pooling in the ceiling like constellations pinned by a careful hand, and Rafael realized after a long moment that they were not random. They were lines. Writing. The whole ceiling was script, miles of it, contracts rendered in silver fire, and the light that fell from it was the light of obligations being honored, moment by moment, forever.

The market filled the floor of the cavern in terraces, tier upon tier descending toward a central well that glowed brighter than everything else—a column of soft gold rising out of sight both up and down, humming the deepest note of the great chord. Stalls lined the terraces the way stalls line any market Rafael had ever known, and that was the horror of it, they thought later. Not the strangeness. The familiarity.

There were awnings. There were scales. There were vendors calling out wares in voices worn smooth by repetition, and customers browsing with the unhurried air of people running errands. An old woman weighed something on a brass balance—Rafael could not see what—and nodded, satisfied, and handed over a small clay jar in exchange. A man in merchant's grays haggled cheerfully over a folded scrap of cloth, laughing, slapping the counter, and the vendor laughed too, and Rafael watched the man walk away lighter and wondered what he had left behind in that cloth. A name, maybe. A summer. The smell of his mother's kitchen.

"Memories," Brannoc wrote, following Rafael's gaze. He had seen this before. His face said so: a face like a door kept closed for decades. "Pawned. You can redeem them, if you come back with the interest. Most don't."

"How much for a memory?" Rafael asked, and heard their own voice come out thin.

Brannoc shrugged and wrote: *Depends whose. Depends which. A stranger's first kiss costs less than your own grief. Grief keeps its value.*

A stall nearby sold names. Rafael only understood this when they saw the ledger open on the counter—rows of names in a fine clerkly hand, each struck through or annotated, each one a person who had walked in whole and walked out without that particular word in their mouth anymore. The buyer of names sat behind the counter with the mild expression of a fishmonger. A young woman stood before him, arguing, gesturing at a line in the book, and Rafael watched her lips move and caught fragments: *"—only until spring—my sister's dowry, you understand, I'll buy it back, I swear it—"*

The fishmonger-named-merchant slid a coin purse across the counter. The woman took it. She hesitated, then opened her mouth and—Rafael saw it happen, saw it the way you see a bird strike a window—one moment her face was full of a word she loved, and the next moment it was full of a small confused blankness, like a room with a picture taken off the wall. She touched her own lips. She looked at the purse. She did not know what she had sold, only that she had been paid for it, and the not-knowing was already smoothing her brow, because grief for a thing you cannot remember is just a draft through an empty house.

She walked away counting coins. The merchant licked his thumb and made a note.

Rafael's stomach turned over. Beside them, Isolde made a sound—small, stricken, the first sound she'd made in two days—and pressed both hands flat against Rafael's arm.

The walls answered her.

It was slight at first, a tremor in the great chord, the way a single string plucked in a cathedral makes every pillar aware of it. Then the veins of silver light nearest them brightened, rippling outward from where Isolde stood, and the hum shifted—resolved, almost, into something with shape and melody. Rafael knew melody like that. They had spent twenty years pretending they didn't, but they knew it: it was the cadence of the First Tongue, the language that lived in the dreams Isolde lent them, the language of souls. The mountain was singing to her.

And the market stopped.

Not all at once. It happened the way weather changes—a stillness spreading outward from them in rings. A vendor mid-cry let the cry die on his tongue. The woman with the coins froze between one step and the next. Heads turned, dozens of them, hundreds, faces lifting toward the gallery where three figures stood in the silver light, and Rafael saw in those faces not malice, not even curiosity at first, but something worse: recognition. Hunger. The look of debtors seeing money.

Isolde stepped behind Rafael. The song dimmed but didn't stop. The walls kept singing, softly, insistently, a mother humming to find a lost child in a dark house, and every soul in the market turned toward the sound.

"We go," Brannoc wrote, fast now, the slate scraping. "Now. Head down. Walk."

They walked. Rafael kept Isolde close against their hip, one hand cupped around the back of her head as if they could shield her from being heard, which they couldn't, which no one could. The crowd parted for them—not politely, not hostilely, just parting, the way water parts around a keel—and Rafael heard the whispers start. They came in every direction, in accents dead for a thousand years and accents alive that morning, and though Rafael could not understand most of them, they understood the shape of the words, because debts have a grammar all their own:

*That's the last entry. That's the outstanding balance. That's the child the books never closed.*

* * *

They found shelter in an alcove off the third terrace, behind a stall that had gone dark, its owner absent or dissolved or simply elsewhere. Brannoc set Isolde down and posted himself at the alcove's mouth, a wall of a man who communicated in gestures now: *stay*, *quiet*, *I watch*. Rafael crouched beside Isolde and took her hands. Her fingers were cold as the stone should have been.

"You knew," Rafael whispered in Common. "You knew this place."

She nodded. Her eyes were enormous in the silver light, and in them Rafael saw reflected the glowing script overhead, and understood that she could read it—that she had been reading it since they entered, every contract, every clause, the way a musician cannot help hearing every note in a chord.

She pulled Rafael's hand toward the wall, toward a patch of blank stone, and looked at Rafael expectantly. *Write,* the look said. So Rafael drew in the dust with one finger, slowly, the question that had been growing in them since the gate: *What is this place? What happens here?*

Isolde studied the words. Then she took Rafael's finger in both her small hands and guided it to the wall itself—to the living stone—and pressed it there.

The knowledge came like water into a dry sponge.

Rafael had expected images, scenes, a vision. What came instead was understanding, clean and total and terrible, the way you understand arithmetic. This was not a hell. That was the first truth, and it arrived with the force of a blow. Hell would have been easier. Hell implies cruelty, and cruelty implies passion, someone *wanting* something. This place wanted nothing. It was not wicked. It was *solvent*. Every soul in the market above had chosen, in some hour of fear or love or simple want, to sign. The old woman pawning a memory to feed grandchildren she could no longer quite recall. The girl mortgaging her name for a sister's dowry. Kings, once—Rafael felt the ledgers' weight of them, kings and farmers and priests, each transaction reasonable, each one small, each signer certain they were different from everyone else who had ever knelt at these counters. No one had been dragged here. Everyone had been *billed*.

And the interest compounded.

That was the second truth, and it came colder than the first: nothing here was stolen outright. Things were borrowed. Pawned. Held in trust. And the terms were such that redemption grew steadily more impossible, year by year, until the collateral converted—gently, administratively, without anyone's signature required—into the deep gold of the central well. The Tallyman did not devour souls. He *amortized* them.

Rafael pulled their hand from the wall as if it had been scalded. Isolde watched them, and her expression was not surprise. She had grown up inside this arithmetic. She had been, all her short life, a line item.

"That's what they meant," Rafael said aloud, forgetting for a moment that she couldn't parse Common, needing anyway to say it to a human face. "Wrenna. Brannoc. The court doesn't steal. It buys." They laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Twenty years I've been afraid of the Order kicking down my door for charms worth three coppers. Twenty years thinking evil wore red robes and shouted. And it's *this*. It's a counter. It's a fair price and a polite clerk and a contract you sign yourself."

Isolde touched their cheek. Small fingers, cold, deliberate. Then she pointed—at Rafael, at herself, at the glowing well far below—and drew in the dust with terrible clarity:

*YOU TOO. ALREADY SIGNED.*

"I know," Rafael whispered. "I know. My parents sold my magic before I could walk. I'm collateral. I've been walking around for thirty years as somebody's asset." They closed their eyes. "But I came to contest it. That has to count for something. Even here. *Especially* here."

Isolde's mouth twisted—the ghost of a smile, the ghost of grief. She began to write again, and her hand shook now, the ash-and-dust letters wobbling:

*THEY KNOW WE'RE HERE. THE WALLS TOLD THEM. THE WALLS ALWAYS TELL THEM.*

As if summoned by the sentence, the hum changed.

It was subtle—a tightening, a key turning—and then Rafael felt it in their teeth: the silver veins in the ceiling above their alcove dimming, one stretch of script going dark, and in the darkness Rafael could suddenly read what had been hidden beneath the glow, the way stars vanish when a brighter light rises. Words. A single line of script, ancient and enormous, arcing across the vault directly above them, written in the First Tongue and legible to Rafael now only because Isolde had spent weeks pressing it into their sleeping mind:

**ALL ACCOUNTS WILL BE SETTLED AT AUDIENCE.**

And beneath it, newer, the ink still wet with silver, a second line appeared while they watched—letters drawing themselves stroke by stroke, unhurried, courteous, inevitable, in the handwriting of a clerk who has all the time in the world because all the time in the world is his inventory:

**THE SCRIBE AND THE LAST SPEAKER ARE EXPECTED.**

Brannoc turned from his watch at the alcove's mouth. He had seen it too. His slate came up, and the verse he wrote on it was brief, and his hand was steady in the way of men who traded away their trembling long ago:

*He knows you've come. He has known since the gate.*
*Down here, child, nobody arrives uninvited.*
*Nobody arrives late, either.*

From the depths of the golden well, faint but unmistakable, rose a sound Rafael had heard only once before—in the dreams Isolde shared, on the night the others were taken. A bell. One clear note, rung once, the way a shopkeeper rings a bell when a customer crosses the threshold.

Somewhere far below, a door was opening for them.
