## 15. Audience with the Tallyman

They came for them at the turn of a bell Rafael could not hear.

There were no guards, no chains, no procession—only a change in the light of the gallery where they had been held, a slow brightening along the walls as though lamps of amber oil were being lit somewhere far above and their glow was draining downward through the mountain's veins. Brannoc's verse had warned of this. *When the stone grows warm as skin, the Tallyman is counting you in.*

Rafael stood. Isolde stood with them, her small hand finding theirs without looking, the way she always did now, as if they were two halves of one habit.

"They're going to be polite," Rafael murmured. "That's how I know we've already lost something."

The door—if it could be called a door, being simply a place where the salt-and-bone wall consented to open—swung inward on air that smelled of ink and candlewax and old, old paper. The smell struck Rafael like a fist. It was the smell of the Order's archive in Vell, of every scriptorium they had ever knelt in, and to find it here, a mile beneath the roots of the world, was its own kind of obscenity.

*This way,* said no voice at all, but the meaning arrived anyway, pressed gently against the inside of Rafael's skull like a seal into wax.

Isolde flinched. Rafael squeezed her hand and stepped through.

* * *

The hall of audience was not what Rafael had imagined. They had pictured a cavern, a throne of black iron, a king robed in shadow with ledgers chained to his wrists. Instead there was a room that might have belonged to any prosperous merchant in Vell: paneled in pale wood gone silver with age, floored in carpet worn soft down its center by centuries of feet, lit by candles in brass sconces whose flames burned perfectly upright, perfectly still, as if even fire here had been entered into an account and knew better than to flicker unbidden.

And at the center of it, behind a desk of dark oak scarred by pen-knives and candle-spit, sat an old man.

That was the first horror: he looked like a man. Small, thin, bald as a river stone, dressed in a clerk's grey robe with the sleeves bound back at the forearm the way scribes did when they meant serious work. His hands were folded on the desk before him. They were beautiful hands—the hands of a master calligrapher, long-fingered, unblemished, without a single inkstain, which was somehow worse. Inkstains meant labor. These hands had never labored. They had only ever decided.

"Master Eriksen." His voice was dry and kind, the voice of a grandfather reading aloud. "And Isolde." He pronounced the name correctly, all its First Tongue weight intact, and Rafael felt Isolde go rigid beside them. "Please sit. You have walked a very long way, and audiences conducted standing tend to become arguments."

Chairs waited. Of course chairs waited. Rafael took the nearer one and drew Isolde onto their knee despite her age; eight years old or eighty, she weighed nothing, and Rafael needed the weight of her against their chest the way a drowning sailor needs rope.

"I am called the Tallyman," the old man said, "though that is a title of convenience. In truth I am only the last hand still writing in these books. Kings come and go. Contracts endure." He opened a ledger before him—not a large one, but thick, its cover the color of dried blood, its pages edged in gold leaf gone dull. "You are owed an explanation. Courtesy demands it, and courtesy—" a faint, papery smile, "—is the cheapest currency in circulation, so I spend it freely."

Rafael made themselves speak. Their throat was dust. "Where are the others? The souls in the cells below?"

"Exactly where they have consented to be." He said it mildly, as if reporting the weather. "You saw them. No one is held here who did not, at some moment, hold out their own hand and ask to be kept safe. That is the whole of my trade, Master Eriksen. I do not take. I receive."

"You buy," Rafael said. "Brannoc told us. Buyers collect."

"And sellers spend." The Tallyman inclined his head, granting the point like a fencing master conceding a touch. "Yes. But let us not begin with philosophy. Let us begin with the book, because everything begins with the book."

He turned the ledger around and slid it across the desk.

* * *

The page was written in a hand Rafael recognized with a sick lurch—the flowing, formal script of the founding era, the same hand that copied the oldest edicts in the Order's vaults, the hand that had penned the Accord of Vell itself. And there, laid out in columns as clean as scripture, was the bargain.

*The city above shall be kept. Frost, famine, plague, and the wars of kings shall pass over Vell as water over a raised stone. In exchange: all speakers of the First Tongue within its walls and its generations, unto the last, are delivered to the court beneath Mount Cael, soul entire, in perpetuity.*

Below it, entries. Hundreds of names across two hundred years, each with a date of delivery, each closed with a small, precise flourish. Rafael's eyes moved down the column and found what they had already known since the vault, but knowing it here, under this man's courteous gaze, was different. It went through them like cold water through cloth.

Near the bottom of the page, one entry remained unclosed.

*One (1) speaker outstanding. Deferred by oversight of the delivering party.* And then, in fresher ink, in a different hand—a hand Rafael knew intimately because it was their own, because they had signed it at fourteen years old without understanding, the debt-bond that bound them to the Order—

*Pledged against final delivery: the latent gift of R. Eriksen, held in escrow, twenty years' accrual, value assessed and growing.*

"There it is," the Tallyman said softly. "I imagine you have questions. Most people do, at first. Then they read the terms again and the questions stop."

Rafael read. The words swam and steadied. "My parents."

"Your parents were signatories of the second instrument, yes." He turned the page with one perfect finger. There it was: the record Rafael had half-hoped never to see completed. *In consideration of the protection of the city of Vell, the undersigned deliver unto the court the magical faculty of their issue, to be held until such time as the final speaker is surrendered, at which point both accounts shall be settled together.* Two signatures. Rafael had spent years trying to remember their mother's face and here was her handwriting instead, patient and careful, the *r*s looped like little cages.

"They didn't sell me," Rafael heard themselves say. "They sold my magic."

"The distinction matters greatly to the living," the Tallyman agreed. "It matters not at all to the book. A thing pledged is a thing owned, whatever name you give the grief." He laced his fingers. "But understand your position fully before we speak of choices. Your gift has been accruing interest for twenty years, Master Eriksen. Every charm you sold in that night market—every small warmth, every warding knot, every harmless spark—you were spending collateral. My collateral. And yet." He smiled again, and there was something almost tender in it, which was the most frightening thing yet. "Yet the principal remains. Twenty years of hoarded power, untouched at its root, compounding in the dark. Do you know what that is worth down here? Souls are the coin of this realm, and yours—your single unspent life of magic—is worth more than half the holdings in my outer galleries. I have pawns older than kingdoms worth less than you."

Isolde stirred on Rafael's knee. She was staring at the open ledger, at the unclosed line bearing her deferred name, and Rafael felt her breathing go shallow and quick.

"Why?" Rafael asked. "Why was she missed? Two hundred years of deliveries and one child slips through?"

The Tallyman's composure shifted—barely, a hairline crack in fired clay. "Because the ones who delivered her generation were careless," he said, "and then they were dead. Your Order's founders grew squeamish in the end, as founders do. The final surrender was to be arranged quietly, an orphanage fire, a fever season—there were plans, Master Eriksen, beautifully documented plans—and then the men who made them died of ordinary old age, and the Order they left behind preferred to forget the mountain entirely rather than look at what it was built upon. So the account stayed open." He tapped the unclosed line. "For sixty years. An embarrassment. A dangling thread in an otherwise immaculate tapestry. And then a mute child walks out of nowhere speaking the First Tongue, and every ward in Vell rings like a struck bell, and I think: ah. At last. The book may be balanced."

Silence. The candles did not flicker.

"So take her," Rafael said. The words came out flat, dead, surprising them with their own calm. "We walked into your mountain. Here she is. Close your book."

"Master Eriksen." The Tallyman's voice gentled further, and Rafael understood suddenly why the cells below were full of people who had chosen this—who had sat in this chair, across from this kind dry voice, and handed over their own souls just to make the conversation end. "No one surrenders anything in this room. That is the first law of the court, older than me. Value must move freely or it is worthless. She must choose. Or"—he raised one finger—"an equivalent value must be exchanged by a party with standing. Which brings me, at last, to why you have been invited to my desk instead of my galleries."

He closed the ledger and set both hands upon it.

"I am going to offer you a choice. I want you to understand that it is a genuine offer, extended in good faith, and that many reasonable people have taken it. Option the first: you take the child and walk out of this mountain, home free, both of you, no riders following, no inquisitors waiting. The price is everything pledged against her entry. Your magic. Twenty years of it, principal and interest, transferred in full at the gate. You will leave this mountain the most ordinary man alive. You will never again warm a cup with a thought or quiet a storm with a word. Everything you have hidden, hoarded, protected, defined yourself by—gone. In exchange, the girl goes free, unwritten, unclaimed, forever."

Isolde's hand closed hard on Rafael's collar.

"Option the second." The Tallyman leaned back, and the candlelight lay along his face like a mask of gold. "You leave her. You walk away owing nothing—to the Order, to the court, to anyone. Your debt-bond dissolves. Your parents' pledge is voided. Ondrel's claims, Serane Halvorsen's warrant, the heretic circles' expectations—all of it, erased. You will be the freest man in Vell. Free to live, free to write, free even to practice your small charms openly, for the book will show no claim on you whatsoever. And the child remains, comfortably kept, among people who will treasure her language and sing to her in it every day. She will want for nothing. She will be safe. Safer, I would argue, than she could ever be above ground, hunted by an Order that burns what it cannot file." He spread his hands, a merchant displaying honest wares. "Mercy either way, Master Eriksen. Mercy for her, or mercy for you. I only regret that mercy, like everything else in this hall, must be paid for."

* * *

Rafael looked down at Isolde.

She was looking up at them. She did not cry. He had seen her cry in dreams, in memories, in the singing dark of the holding cells—but here, in front of the ancient king of debts, she only watched Rafael with those grave, patient eyes, and lifted one hand, and touched their cheek.

And into the silence, in the First Tongue, she spoke.

Rafael understood it now—understood all of it, every syllable, the way you understand your own heartbeat. What she said was not *save me*. It was not *don't leave me*. It was:

*Whatever you pay, pay it with your eyes open. That is what they never did. That is why they are all still here.*

The Tallyman's smile did not waver, but his fingers moved, very slightly, on the cover of the ledger—the involuntary twitch of a man watching a figure appear in a column where he had not written one.

"You understand her," he observed. "How interesting. Sixty days ago you did not speak a word of it."

"She taught me," Rafael said.

"In dreams." He nodded slowly, and for the first time something entered his voice that was not courtesy. It took Rafael a moment to name it, and when they did, it chilled them to the marrow of their teeth. It was hunger. "A debt paid in the debtor's own coin. Yes. Yes, I see it now." He rose, small and straight-backed, and came around the desk, and stood before them looking up—for he was shorter than Rafael, much shorter, a frail old clerk craning at a stranger in his shop. "Then hear my final term, scribe, before you answer. Whatever you choose, choose it aloud, in this room, and choose it soon. Because the book is open, the candles are lit, and I have been balancing this account for three hundred years." His eyes glittered, wet and bright as fresh ink. "I am very tired of waiting. And old men who count everything—" he glanced once, briefly, at Isolde, "—eventually learn what it costs to be owed a child."

Outside the paneled walls, deep in the stone, something began to hum. Not the walls singing for Isolde this time.

It sounded almost like turning pages.
