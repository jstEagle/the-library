## 16. What Loyalty Costs

The cells had no doors.

That was the first thing Rafael understood about the Undercourt's holding galleries: there was nothing to lock. The walls themselves were the locks, humming with a low mineral patience, and the people inside them sat in their small round rooms like coins in a purse—held not by bars but by the weight of what they owed.

Isolde walked ahead of him, her small hand trailing along the stone as though reading a page. She had stopped speaking to the guards—there had been no guards, only doorways that opened when she approached—and now she moved through the Undercourt's lower reaches with the certainty of someone returning to a house she hated. Rafael followed because there was nowhere else to go, and because every step deeper felt less like descent than like sinking into water he had been drowning in for twenty years without knowing it.

*They are not prisoners,* she said—not aloud, never aloud here; the First Tongue did not trouble itself with air. It arrived in Rafael's mind the way a memory of warmth arrives in cold hands. *They are inventory.*

The first cell held an old man.

He sat on a bench of living rock, hands folded in his lap, and he looked up at them with mild, courteous eyes—the eyes of a shopkeeper interrupted at his ledgers. Nothing about him was monstrous. His coat was patched at the elbows. His beard had been combed, recently, with care. When he saw Isolde his face changed, and the change was worse than any snarl would have been: he looked *ashamed.*

"Oh," he said, in Common, in a voice like a draft under a door. "Oh, you're one of the little ones. From before." He turned his gaze to the wall. "I'm sorry, child. I'm so sorry. We didn't know what we were signing."

"You knew," said Isolde.

Rafael flinched. In twenty days of knowing her he had heard her voice perhaps six times, all of it in dreams, and each time it landed in his chest like a struck bell. Here in the court beneath the mountain, where the walls drank sound, her Common came out small and hard and old.

"We knew," the old man agreed, after a long silence. He smiled then, and it was the gentlest thing Rafael had seen since descending past the gate of salt and bone, and it made him want to be sick. "But the winter was coming, and the Order's riders were burning the hedge-villages, and the mountain offered protection. Protection for the speakers. All it asked—" he spread his soft hands— "was that no one stand in the doorway when the tallymen came for the children who were missed. That we *step aside.* Just once. Just each of us, just once."

"And how many times did you step aside?" Isolde asked.

The old man's smile stayed fixed, but behind it something folded up, quietly, like a moth closing its wings. "I stopped counting," he said, "because counting hurt. And then I stopped hurting, because they offered to take that too. The pain, I mean. For a small consideration. And now I sit here, and I am comfortable, and I cannot remember my daughter's name, and the walls are warm." He looked at Rafael with those mild eyes. "You think we're kept here against our will. But there is no will left to keep. There is only the balance. And the balance must be maintained."

*Come,* Isolde said, already moving. *There is worse. There is always worse, and it looks like this.*

* * *

The second gallery was full of light.

Not torchlight—the cells down here glowed faintly from within, each one lit by the soul it held, the way a lantern is lit by the candle inside it. Rafael walked between them slowly, and the light touched his face, and he began to understand what the Tallyman had meant by *inventory*, and understanding was a kind of wound.

A woman pressed her palms to the inner surface of her cell-wall. Her glow was gold, and steady, and sad. She had been beautiful once, Rafael thought; she was still beautiful, in the way that a portrait is beautiful, everything essential painted over. Her lips moved behind the stone.

"She says she traded her memories of her sister," Isolde translated, flatly. "One year at a time. The first year bought her mother a cure. The second bought passage out of Vell during the purges. By the tenth year she could no longer remember why she had started selling." Isolde paused. "She remembers that there was a sister. She keeps the empty shape of her, the way you keep a dent in a pillow. She says the emptiness hurts more than the memories ever did, and that is why she stays. If she left, she might someday fill the shape with something else, and then even the emptiness would be gone."

"That's—" Rafael began, and found he had no word for it. Horror was too loud a word for something so quiet. This was not horror. This was *bookkeeping.* A woman had sold herself away in installments, reasonably, at fair prices, each receipt signed in her own hand, and now she lived inside her own transaction like a fly in amber, and somewhere above them a ledger balanced perfectly.

They passed a cell where a young man glowed green-white and paced, endlessly pacing, and Isolde did not translate for him because he was not saying anything. He had sold his *refusals*, Rafael learned later—all of them, every moment of his life when he might have said no and didn't. The court had purchased the whole collection, centuries of swallowed objections, and without them the young man could not stop agreeing. He had agreed to come here. He agreed to stay. He would agree to anything, forever, because agreement was all that remained of him, worn smooth as river stone.

They passed a cell that was nearly dark, holding a shape that might have been a person, that murmured numbers in its sleep. They passed a cell where two souls had been merged—one debt too tangled to separate—and the resulting glow flickered between blue and brown like a candle deciding what color to be, and both voices spoke from the same mouth, politely taking turns.

"Stop," Rafael said finally. His voice came out cracked. "Isolde. Stop. I can't—"

*Yes,* she said. *You can. You have to see all of it. Wrenna told you truth cuts. Well, look at what it has to cut through.*

She took his hand. Her fingers were cold, small, certain. And she pushed—not physically, not exactly—she turned him, the way you turn a lamp toward a dark corner, to face the last cell in the gallery.

It was brighter than all the others. Inside it sat a soul so whole, so fiercely itself, that the light hurt to look at, and the soul was screaming. Silently. The walls drank the sound, but the light stuttered with it, flare and dim, flare and dim, like a heartbeat refusing to slow.

"She refused," Isolde said. In Common again, so that the glowing prisoner might hear herself spoken of. "She is the only one in this gallery who refused. Every offer, every trade, every reasonable arrangement—they offered her comfort, safety, her life back, her debts forgiven, and she said no. Two hundred years of no." Isolde's grip tightened on Rafael's hand. "Look at her, scribe. Look carefully. Because everyone below told us she would be broken by now. Everyone above told us she'd have taken the deal eventually, because everyone does. But she is bright enough to read by, and the ones who chose safety are almost out of oil."

Rafael stood before the cell and let the light strike his face, and the prisoner turned toward him—or her light turned, the way a flower turns—and for a moment he felt seen by something older and more stubborn than the entire mountain above them.

"What do I call you?" he whispered.

Her lips moved. The stone ate the words. But Isolde tilted her head, listening in whatever way she listened, and translated:

"She says: don't use my name yet. Names are currency here. She says—" and Isolde's voice caught, the first time Rafael had ever heard it catch— "she says, tell whoever comes next that the ledger is only paper. Paper burns. She has been saying it for two hundred years and no one has listened, because listening would mean admitting what they gave away was theirs to give."

* * *

Afterward they sat in an antechamber of black stone, and Isolde put her head against Rafael's shoulder, and for a long time neither of them spoke.

The silence down here was not empty. The whole Undercourt hummed with it—the accumulated quiet of ten thousand consented surrenders, each one small, each one reasonable, each one signed. Rafael turned it over in his mind like a coin he had finally been allowed to examine, and the face of the coin was his own.

Because he knew. Gods forgive him, he *knew.* He had spent twenty years doing exactly this, in miniature, in the city above. Selling harmless charms while copying edicts against magic. Lying to Ondrel in the morning and to his customers at night. Each compromise small. Each one necessary. Each one a single grain of sand, and here was the desert they built. The Tallyman had not conquered anyone. He had never needed to. He had simply *opened a shop*, and waited, and let fear do the selling, and people had queued around the block to trade each other away—one winter's safety for a daughter's name, one pardon for a neighbor's secret, one comfortable cell for a lifetime of refusals.

"He doesn't hold them here," Rafael said aloud, slowly, testing the shape of the thought. "He can't. There are no locks. He holds the *debts,* and the debts hold them. Every soul in this mountain walked in on its own feet and signed its own entry, and the contract is just... the record. The receipt." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, thin and awful in the humming dark. "His whole kingdom is made of yeses. Of moments when it would have cost something—something real—to say no, and the price seemed too high, and people paid in each other instead."

*And yours?* Isolde asked, gently, in the First Tongue, and the question went through him like a needle finding the exact center of a bruise. *Your twenty years of small mercies. Your twenty years of stepping aside. Are they so different from the old man's?*

"I don't know," Rafael said. It was the truest sentence he had spoken in two decades. "I used to tell myself I was keeping my head down to survive. Keeping *you* safe, after you came. But Serane offered me a bargain, Isolde—the child for my freedom—and I refused it, and refusing felt—" He groped for it. "It felt like the first honest thing I'd done in years. Not because it was noble. Because it *cost* me. Everything honest costs. Everything else is just bookkeeping."

Isolde lifted her head. In the dim light her eyes were very old and very young at once, eight years old and eight hundred, and she studied him the way she studied the ash she wrote in, looking for the shape underneath.

*Loyalty isn't a feeling,* she said. *I thought it was, down here, for a long time. I watched people love each other terribly and betray each other completely, in the same breath, and I couldn't understand it. But now—* she touched the black stone wall, and the hum beneath his palm shifted, subtly, like a great beast shifting its weight in sleep— *now I understand. Love is what you feel. Loyalty is what you do, over and over, when feeling would be easier. It's the refusal. Repeated. Until the refusal is the shape your soul has grown into.*

"The woman in the bright cell," Rafael said.

"Yes. Two hundred years of no. One syllable, ten thousand times." Isolde smiled, and it was fierce. "The Tallyman fears her more than armies. Do you see why? He can price anything—grief, memory, names, years. He has a column for everything. But a refusal has no price, because it isn't for sale. It's the one entry he can't make. Every time she says no, his arithmetic fails, and he has to start the whole ledger over."

Rafael closed his eyes. Somewhere above them, muffled through leagues of stone, the court was assembling—he could feel it in the way the walls' hum had changed since their audience, a new note threaded through the ancient drone, anticipation or appetite. The Tallyman had given them until tomorrow. *Take the child home and forfeit everything,* he had offered, courteous as a banker, *or leave her and walk free of all debt.* Mercy dressed in a merchant's coat. And Rafael had asked for time, and time had been granted, because time was also a commodity, and everything granted was entered somewhere in a column.

But sitting here in the dark with a child asleep against his shoulder, having walked the galleries of the consented dead, Rafael understood at last what he actually had to do. Not escape. Not negotiate. Not find some clever clause to slip through while leaving the machine intact.

The machine ran on surrender. Therefore the weapon was refusal—but refusal alone wasn't enough; the bright woman had refused for two hundred years and was still in her cell. Refusal had to be *backed*. It had to pledge something. It had to stand in the marketplace of owed things and put something real on the table against the Tallyman's claim, the way a counterparty does, the way a contest requires—

He froze.

*Isolde.* He shook her gently, and her eyes opened, luminous in the dark. *The founding contract. The one that claims you. I need to see it. Every word. Tonight, before the assembly. Where does he keep the original?*

She blinked at him, sleep dissolving, and then something kindled in her face—a small, fierce dawn.

*In his chest,* she said. *Where all his treasures are. But there is a copy in the outer galleries, in the Hall of Instruments, because the copy is what the clerks work from. I can take you there. But Rafael—* her hand closed around his wrist, hard, urgent— *a contract that old can only be contested by a counterparty pledging equal value. That is the oldest rule. Older than the mountain. Whatever you're planning—you should know what it might cost before you ask to see the page.*

Rafael looked back down the gallery, toward the distant gold flare of the woman who had said no for two hundred years, and then at the sleeping weight of the last unclaimed soul in the world, warm against his side.

"I've spent twenty years protecting something of mine," he said. "I suppose it was always going to come down to spending it."

Isolde rose, took his hand, and led him toward the Hall of Instruments—and behind them, faint through the humming stone, the bright cell flickered once, flare and dim, like a heartbeat answering.
