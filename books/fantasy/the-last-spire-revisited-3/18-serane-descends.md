## 18. Serane Descends

The gate at Mount Cael's foot did not look like a door. It looked like a wound that had healed wrong.

Serane Halvorsen stood before it in the grey hour before dawn, her Order's cloak beaded with road-rain, and felt the mountain breathing. That was the only word for it—a slow exhalation of warm air through the salt-crusted stone, carrying a smell like old candle smoke and something sweeter beneath, something that made her think of her sister's room when they were girls, of dried lavender and ink.

*Ilse.*

She had told herself this was duty. The vault breach was three weeks old; the trail of a scribe and a stolen child led here as surely as rivers lead to sea. Inquisitors did not follow trails into the Undercourt out of sentiment. Inquisitors followed them because the Order required it, and the Order required it because—

Because she had let the thought finish itself too many times already, and each time it finished the same way: *because you want to know if the stories are true. If the court keeps what it buys. If what was bought can be found again.*

Brannoc had not stopped her. That was its own kind of omen. The voiceless gatekeeper had watched her dismount, watched her check her blade and her vial of consecrated salt, and then he had simply stepped aside, gesturing once toward the dark mouth of the gate with an open palm—the way one gestures toward a table set for a guest. On the stone beside him, chalked in his careful verse-hand, he had written:

*The mountain does not bar the door to those who come to pay.*

She should have read it as warning. She read it as invitation, and hated herself for the relief.

The passage beyond the gate descended in long spiral galleries, and the walls were alive with light—not torchlight, nothing so honest, but a pale luminescence that seeped from the stone itself, as though the mountain had swallowed moonlight centuries ago and never digested it. Serane walked with her hand on her sword hilt and her consecrated salt in her pocket, and neither gave her any comfort. The salt stayed cold. The blade stayed quiet. Holiness, apparently, had no jurisdiction down here.

She passed alcoves where things were kept behind glass older than glass had a right to be: a jar of laughter, labeled in a script that hurt to read. A folded shadow, pinned like a moth. A wedding vow, still faintly warm. Each bore a small brass plate with a number, and beneath the numbers, smaller plates with names—some scratched out, some worn to ghosts, some freshly engraved.

In the third gallery she began to hear the humming. Not sound, exactly. It lived behind her teeth, in the fillings of her molars, in the marrow of her forearms. The whole mountain was a ledger, she realized, and every wall was a page, and everything on those pages was owed or owned, and the interest compounded in the dark whether anyone watched or not.

Her sister's name was Ilse Halvorsen, and she had been taken nineteen years ago, on a Tuesday, by four men in temple grey who did not knock.

Serane had been eleven. She remembered the tea going cold on the table. She remembered their mother signing something without reading it, because the men said signing was only a formality, only bookkeeping, and their mother had spent her whole life believing that formality meant mercy. She remembered Ilse looking back over her shoulder once, at the door, at Serane, mouthing something—and Serane had spent nineteen years deciding what that mouthed word had been. *Goodbye.* *Wait.* *Remember.* Lately, in the worst nights, she had decided it was *run*, and lately she suspected it had been none of these, that Ilse had been saying something in the language children invent between themselves, the private tongue of two sisters, and that Serane had lost it forever along with everything else.

The Order had told her family that Ilse had been sent somewhere kind. Somewhere with gardens. Nineteen years of faithful service had earned Serane the rank to learn otherwise: there were no gardens. There had never been gardens. There was only the mountain, and the mountain kept books.

* *

The Tallyman's outer offices were staffed.

That was the horror of it, Serane thought, descending the last stair into the great counting-hall—that it was staffed. Clerks at desks of black wood, quills scratching, ink-pots glowing faintly. Not demons, not monsters. People. Some wore the vestments of faiths extinct for five hundred years. One wore the grey of her own Order, decades out of fashion. They looked up at her as she entered with the mild, tired courtesy of clerks everywhere, as if inquisitors came down here often, as if the descent were merely paperwork in another office.

"You'll want a claim number," said the nearest clerk, an ancient woman whose eyes had gone the color of unpolished silver. "Or a name. Names take longer."

"I'm looking for a soul," Serane said, and heard how the words sounded, and said them anyway. "Taken nineteen years ago. From Vell. Her name was Ilse."

The clerk turned to a shelf behind her—a shelf, Serane saw, that ran back into the dark farther than the hall was wide—and drew down a ledger with hands that trembled not from age but from care. She licked her thumb. She turned pages. The hall hummed.

"Halvorsen," the clerk murmured. "Halvorsen. Ah." Her finger stopped. "Here we are. Acquired under the Consolidation, year of the second census. Entered against the founding bargain as—hm." She looked up, and her silver eyes were not unkind, which made it worse. "You're the sister."

"I want to see her."

"That won't be possible."

"Then I'll buy her back. Whatever the rate. I have twenty years of pension accrued to the Order, a house in the Temple Quarter, a—"

"Sister." The clerk closed the ledger gently, the way one closes the eyes of the dead. "You don't understand what you're asking about yet. Sit down."

Serane sat. It was not surrender; her legs simply decided for her.

"The court doesn't keep souls," the clerk said. "It spends them. A soul is value, child, and value that sits idle is waste, and the Tallyman wastes nothing. Your sister's entry was drawn down against the founding bargain—do you know the founding bargain? No, of course not; the Order burns its own histories. Very tidy arrangement, from the Order's side. They sell, the court holds, everyone's ledgers balance." She opened the ledger again, tapped one line with a knuckle like a root. "Drawn down here. And here. And here. Small drafts. A ward strengthened. A famine averted—one famine, ninety-one years back, cost the court dearly, your sister's entry covered the margin. A plague turned aside from the city walls. Every protection Vell has ever enjoyed, every year the Order's edicts held and the streets stayed safe—" she spread her hands, "—paid for. With entries like hers. Spent in installments."

"She could still be—" Serane's voice came out very small. "There might be something left. A residue. Souls aren't—"

"A soul is not a coin," the clerk agreed. "A soul is a candle. And yours burned well, Sister Halvorsen. She lit the winter of '41. She warmed the hospital quarter during the flux. When the levees held at Greyford—" the clerk paused, and for a moment her silver eyes seemed to look at something far away, some page of memory filed among the pages of account, "—that was her, mostly. She was generous even as fuel. They usually are, the young ones. It makes the drafting easier."

Serane did not remember standing. She remembered the ledger's edge under her palm, and the clerk making no move to stop her, and her own voice, flat as a coffin lid: "Show me the final entry."

The clerk showed her.

*H. Ilse — remainder consolidated into general reserve, year 412. Balance: zero.*

Zero.

Nineteen years of searching, of rising through the ranks, of perfect conduct and careful heresy, of telling herself *one day, one audit, one loophole*—and the answer was a word she had written ten thousand times herself in other people's files. Zero. Her sister had not been imprisoned in the dark. She had been *used up*, in pieces, to keep a city safe that would have spat on her name, and the last piece had gone out thirty years before Serane ever learned to spell her own surname.

The humming of the hall went on. The quills went on scratching. Somewhere a clerk coughed politely.

When Serane could speak again, she asked, "Who authorized the drafts?"

"The founding signatories. On behalf of the Order of Vell." The clerk hesitated, then added, with the scrupulous honesty of someone who has spent eternity at a desk: "And Master Ondrel countersigned the consolidation personally. He was junior then. He initialled in the corner. See?"

Serane saw. Three letters, small and neat, in faded brown ink: *O.H.M.* Ondrel had signed her sister into oblivion and risen to Master of the Order on the strength of balanced books.

She thanked the clerk. She did not know why. The clerk inclined her head and said, "We get many visitors who thank us," which was somehow the most terrible thing anyone had said to Serane in nineteen years.

* *

She found them by accident, three galleries deeper than any sane person should go, in a corridor where the walls sang.

Not hummed—sang. A high, clear thread of melody ran through the stone here, and Serane followed it the way a drowning woman follows light, past cells of salt-glass, past shelves of numbered jars, until she rounded a corner and saw them: the scribe, Rafael Eriksen, kneeling beside a girl-child who was pressing both palms flat against the wall as if warming her hands at a fire only she could feel. The wall sang louder where the child touched it. Where Rafael touched it, the song stuttered, thinned—Rafael's magic, Serane realized, whatever remained of it, being drawn out of them and into the stone like water into sand.

They looked up. Four eyes met hers across twenty feet of singing dark: the child's, vast and calm as deep water; the scribe's, hollowed with exhaustion and braced for the end.

"Inquisitor," Rafael said. Even now, even here, the formal address. Even now the little courtesies of a life built on lying correctly.

"I found my sister," Serane said.

Rafael waited. That was the thing she had never understood about them, all those years of watching the night market, all those suspicions she'd filed and buried—Rafael Eriksen knew how to wait for bad news. Had practiced it, probably. Twenty years of it.

"There's nothing left," Serane said. "She was spent. In installments. To protect Vell. Ondrel signed it himself." Her voice held. She noted distantly that it held, that nineteen years of discipline would hold her spine even now, even here, even with the ground gone. "I came down here to trade for her. I had prepared offers. I had—" A laugh escaped her, dry and awful. "I had drafted terms. Like a fool. Like a clerk."

The child spoke. Not Common—the First Tongue, that syllable-structure Serane had heard described in forbidden reports, language like architecture. But she said it gently, and she reached one small hand toward Serane, palm up, an offering or an invitation, and the wall behind her sang a chord that went through Serane's chest like a hand closing around her heart.

"What did she say?" Serane whispered.

Rafael translated, and their voice cracked on it: "She says your sister is in the walls. All of them. She says the ones who are spent aren't gone—they became part of the song. She says—" Rafael stopped, pressed their lips together, started again. "She says the mountain remembers everyone it used. That's why it sings. It's not a prison, Serane. It's a memorial that got ideas above itself."

"And that's supposed to comfort me?"

"No," Rafael said simply. "Nothing will. But she wanted you to know your sister isn't zero. The ledger says zero. The song says otherwise."

Serane stood in the singing dark and felt the two truths pull her apart like tides. Nineteen years she had served the Order because the Order was the only structure large enough to hold her grief without buckling—duty as a load-bearing wall. And now here was the truth underneath: the Order had fed her sister to the stove, log by log, and called it safety, and promoted the man who signed the receipts, and given Serane a sword and a rank and told her it was honor.

Loyalty, she thought. She had confused loyalty with debt her whole life. The Order had taught her that. Everything the Order taught was debt: owe, obey, repay, serve. And down here, in the mountain that invented debt, she could finally see the shape of the trick, because she was standing inside it.

"I swore an oath," she said aloud. It mattered, suddenly, to say it in front of witnesses. "When I took the grey cloak. I swore to guard the people of Vell from magic." She looked at Rafael. "No one ever asked me who guards the people of Vell from the Order."

The child answered first—in Common, halting, accented, precious as contraband: "You do. Now."

Serane laughed again, and this time it broke in the middle and came out as something else, and she let it, because there was no one left down here to report her for it.

She unbuckled her sword-belt and laid it on the stone, blade toward herself, the way one surrenders. Then she took from inside her cloak the thing she had carried for nineteen years without knowing why: her inquisitor's warrant, heavy vellum, sealed with the Order's sun-and-key. Authority. License. The power to enter, to demand, to witness—and, written in the fine print she had never been permitted to refuse, to testify.

"When I walk into that court," she said, "and when the Tallyman opens his books, someone must stand for the injured party. Someone with standing. Someone the contract recognizes." She held the warrant out to Rafael. "My testimony carries weight of law, Eriksen. Sworn witness for the wronged. It's the only weapon I have left that cuts, and I am so tired of holding it pointed the wrong way."

Rafael didn't take it. "Serane. If you do this, you can't go back. He'll know. Ondrel will burn everything you are."

"He already did," Serane said. "Thirty years ago, in a corner of a ledger, in three little letters. He just hadn't told me yet." Her hand did not waver. "Take it. Contest the contract. And when they ask who stands witness—" she lifted her chin, and nineteen years fell off her like a shed cloak, and what stood underneath was eleven years old again, watching a door close, memorizing a mouthed word she finally understood:

*Remember.*

"—tell them Serane Halvorsen remembers."

Behind the child, the wall sang one long, low note, like a bell struck once, like a name spoken at last after thirty years of silence.
