## 19. The Contest of Tongues

The hall where the Tallyman held his court had not been built for mercy.

Rafael understood that the moment they were led in. It was a long gallery of black stone, ribbed like the inside of a throat, and every surface was inscribed—not carved, *inscribed*, as if the rock itself had been a page and something vast had written across it until no blank space remained. The script ran floor to ceiling, wall to wall, in columns so dense they blurred at a distance into something like weather. Rafael recognized fragments now. After weeks of Isolde's dream-lessons, after nights spent tracing ash-strokes on slate while she corrected him with small, patient frowns, he could read the ledger-hall the way a drowning man reads the surface of water.

*Owed. Owed. Paid in part. Forfeited. Owed.*

The whole mountain was a book, and every soul in Vell was a line in it.

Isolde walked beside him with her hand in his. She had refused to let go since the holding cells, and the guards—those grey, courteous figures whose faces seemed always slightly out of focus, like portraits viewed through smoke—had not tried to separate them. Perhaps they did not think it mattered. Perhaps, in this place, a child's hand in an adult's was simply another entry awaiting its column.

Serane walked behind them. Her armor had been taken at the gate, all but her oath-ring, and without it she looked strangely unfinished, a sentence with the verb missing. But her spine was straight as a stylus, and when one of the grey guards drifted too close she turned her head just enough that the thing reconsidered.

Brannoc had stayed above, at the gate of salt and bone. He could not have come down even if he'd wished; the court still held his voice in escrow, and debts were drawn toward their holders like iron filings. He had pressed his written verse into Rafael's palm at the threshold—a scrap of bark-paper, lines in a careful hand:

*The buyer counts on silence, friend.*
*The bought are counted. Speak, and end*
*the arithmetic of the grave.*
*One voice can make a ledger wave.*

Rafael had read it three times and put it away like a knife.

At the far end of the gallery rose the Tallyman's dais—or rather, his *desk*, for that was what it was, an accountant's desk grown to obscene proportion, its surface a slab of polished obsidian on which light moved of its own accord, sorting itself into rows. Behind it sat the Tallyman.

He was smaller than Rafael remembered from the audience chamber. That was the trick of him, Rafael realized—the court made you expect a king, a colossus, some horned majesty of the deep places, and instead you found a thin old figure in ink-stained robes with eyes like two ledgers opened to the same page. His hands were folded. They were very beautiful hands, the hands of a master calligrapher, and they did not move once during everything that followed.

"Debtor," the Tallyman said. His voice was dry and kind, the voice of a tutor about to explain why your sums were wrong. "You have requested a formal contest of the founding instrument. Do you understand what you have asked?"

"I understand," Rafael said, "that contracts bind both ways."

A murmur went through the assembled gallery—and there *was* an assembly, Rafael saw now, ranks of seated shapes along the walls, the senior clerks of the Undercourt, things that might once have been people before centuries of bookkeeping wore them smooth. Their murmur was not sound exactly. It was the feeling of a quill scratching, multiplied ten thousand times.

"The instrument," the Tallyman continued, unbothered, "was sealed by the founders of your city. It is older than any law you know. It has been honored—" his mouth curved, "—*maintained*, at least, for six hundred years. What standing do you bring against it?"

"Standing," Rafael said, and his heart was hammering so hard he was sure the whole court could hear it, "or argument? Because I've brought both, but only one of them matters here. Isn't that what you told me? Truth is the only weapon that cuts."

"I said debts are currency," the Tallyman said mildly. "Truth is merely the scale we weigh it in. Proceed."

Rafael stepped forward. The black stone was cold through his worn boots. Somewhere above them—miles above, past galleries and gates and the long climb to the world—Vell was waking to a gray morning, bakers lighting ovens, temple bells ringing the hour for a city that did not know its own name was mortgaged. He thought of that deliberately. He would need it. Fear made the tongue stumble, but anger, properly banked, burned clean.

He spoke in the First Tongue.

It came out of him slowly at first, each word set down like a foot on ice, testing. The syllables were strange in his mouth—not because they were hard, but because they were *old*, older than the Order, older than Vell, older than the idea of outlawing anything. They tasted of woodsmoke and rain on stone and a time before kingdoms when language belonged to everyone who could grieve.

"*I speak as counterparty,*" he said. "*I speak as kin to the last unclaimed speaker. I speak to contest.*"

The gallery went absolutely still.

Even the light on the obsidian desk seemed to pause mid-sorting. And Rafael felt it then—the thing Isolde had promised him in dreams, the thing he had half disbelieved until this moment. The words themselves had weight here. In the First Tongue, a statement was not a statement; it was a *placement*, an act, a stone set into a wall. When he said *I speak*, the hall registered that someone was speaking who had no entry in the ledger. It rippled outward through the inscriptions like a crack through glaze.

The Tallyman's hands unfolded—one finger, precisely, the way a man might mark his place.

"Where did you learn that?" he asked. And for the first time, the kindness in his voice had a hairline fracture in it.

"From her." Rafael drew Isolde forward. "From the debtor herself. Which brings me to my argument."

He laid it out the way Wrenna had taught him to lay out scripture—slowly, in order, letting each piece bear weight before adding the next. The founding bargain, struck six centuries ago between the founders of Vell and the court beneath Mount Cael: protection for the city, in exchange for the speakers of the First Tongue, their souls surrendered into the court's keeping, their language silenced above ground forever. A terrible contract, but a *clean* one, and cleanliness was the only morality the Undercourt recognized.

"And a clean contract," Rafael said, still in the First Tongue, watching the clerk-things flinch at each word like horses at a whip, "specifies its subject completely. It does not say *some speakers*. It does not say *speakers yet living*. It says—all of them. Every soul that speaks the First Tongue. Am I reading it correctly?"

"You are," the Tallyman said.

"Then read this correctly." Rafael lifted Isolde's hand. "Six hundred years, and your ledger shows one outstanding entry. One missed claim. This child. And tell me—what is she now?"

"She is a speaker of the First Tongue," the Tallyman said. "Unclaimed. Outstanding. Due."

"She is a speaker of the First Tongue," Rafael agreed, "and she is also a speaker of Common. She learned it on my floor, in my shop, over a winter of soup and stolen books. Two tongues. One soul. And your contract—which claims *all speakers of the First Tongue*—now attempts to seize a soul that speaks *also otherwise*. A bilingual soul. Tell me, Tallyman: under your own law, which tongue does a soul belong to? The first? Or all of them?" He let the question hang. "Because if it belongs to all of them, then Common has a claim on her too, and Common was never party to your bargain. And if it belongs only to the First Tongue—" he smiled, and it was not a kind smile, "—then I stand here speaking it, fluent, taught by the debtor's own gift, and I am telling you the claim is contested."

Silence. The kind of silence that has mass.

Then the light on the obsidian desk began to move very fast, sorting and resorting, and Rafael knew the court was checking. Somewhere in those walls of inscription, the founding instrument was being reread, every clause weighed, every comma interrogated. Six hundred years of perfect bookkeeping, and a scribe from Vell had found the loose thread—not by cleverness alone, but because a mute child had been patient enough to teach him the language of the loom.

"The reading is admitted," the Tallyman said at last, and the words came out like teeth being pulled. "The contract may be contested. By a counterparty." His eyes lifted to Rafael's, and for the first time they were not dry or kind or anything at all; they were simply ancient, and cold as vault air. "But hear the rest of your own law, scribe. A contest requires pledge against pledge. You may void our claim on the child's soul—but only by pledging value equal to it. A soul's worth is not a trifle. It is the sum of everything a life might have been. Can you pay it?"

And there it was. The trap inside the loophole, the tollgate at the end of the bridge. Rafael had known it was coming—Isolde had shown him in dreams, Brannoc's verse had warned him—but knowing and hearing were different creatures, and hearing it in that cold dry voice, in that throat of a hall, made his knees want to inform him of their resignation.

Equal value. A soul's worth. What did he have?

He thought, absurdly, of the night market. Of the little charms sold under awnings while the Order's lanterns swept the far street—knots of thread that eased a fever, tokens that kept nightmares mild, blessings whispered into copper for widows who couldn't afford the temple's prices. Twenty years of small mercies, each one a crime, each one hidden, each one a spark of the magic his parents had sold before he was born. He had hoarded it the way misers hoard, the way frightened people hoard, telling himself he was saving it, that one day he'd need it, that a small fire kept covered was a life preserved.

Twenty years of hiding. Twenty years of being small on purpose.

Isolde tugged his hand. He looked down. She was looking up at him with those grave dark eyes, and she signed against his palm—she still spoke little, even now, even in dreams; some languages live deeper than speech—but her fingers moved in the grammar he knew, and what she wrote was: *You don't owe them. You choose.*

"No," Rafael said aloud, in Common, and let the word fall into the First Tongue's silence like a coin into a well. Then he switched back, and his voice steadied, and it did not shake at all, which surprised him more than anything else that day. "I can pay it. I will pay it. I pledge—"

He took a breath, and gave himself away.

"—every spark of magic in my blood. All of it. The gift my parents pawned before I drew breath, and everything it has grown in the shadow since. Twenty years of it. Every charm I ever wove, every ward I ever whispered, every healing knot and quiet blessing and small unlawful warmth—I release my hold on all of it. Into the court's ledger, against the child's entry. Paid in full."

The words, in the First Tongue, did not feel like words. They felt like opening a door in his chest that he had nailed shut at nine years old, the night he first understood what the warmth in his hands was and what the Order did to people who had it. Something rose in him—not pain, not yet, but pressure, like water finding a breach—and he watched it leave. He felt it go out through his palms, through his breath, streaming upward in threads of faint gold that only he and perhaps Isolde could see, threading down into the obsidian desk, into the waiting ledger-light, which received it with a hunger that was almost gentle.

Every charm. Every hidden spark. The self he had been protecting all his life, poured out like ink from a tipped bottle.

It didn't hurt. That was the terrible part. It felt like *rest*.

When it was done, Rafael stood in his own body the way one stands in a house after the furniture has been sold—familiar rooms, sudden echoes. His hands were just hands. The constant low warmth he had lived beside for forty years, so long he'd stopped hearing it, was gone, and the silence it left behind was enormous.

Isolde was gripping his fingers hard enough to hurt. Good. He held on.

The Tallyman looked down at the desk, where the new entry was writing itself in light. His expression did not change, but something behind it did—a recalculation, vast and slow, like a glacier deciding.

"Pledged," he said. "Received. The contest is—" 

"Wait."

The voice came from the gallery. From behind. Serane Halvorsen stepped forward into the aisle, oath-ring bare on her hand, her face white as fresh paper, and Rafael's stomach dropped through the floor of the world—because whatever she was about to do, there was no column in any ledger for it.

"I am Serane Halvorsen," she said, in accented but unmistakable First Tongue—where she had learned it Rafael could not imagine, and her jaw said the learning had cost her something permanent, "Inquisitor of the Order of Vell. And I am witness to this pledge, freely given, before the assembled court. Let it be entered."

The clerk-things stirred. The Tallyman's finger paused above his desk.

"Witnesses are not required," he said.

"No," Serane agreed. "But testimony is admissible. And I have more than witnessing to enter." She reached into her collar and drew out a chain, and on it a small worn locket, and her hands shook, and her voice did not. "I have a name. A name your books dissolved sixty years ago and never reported to the family that paid for it. I have been searching for my sister since I swore my first oath, Tallyman, and I found her tonight—in your margins. So enter this too: the Order of Vell will hear how its founders' bargain was kept. Enter it, or I will spend the rest of my considerable life making certain every soul in that city learns to read your walls."

For the first time in six hundred years, perhaps, the Tallyman of the Undercourt looked at something he had not already counted.

And beneath Rafael's feet, in the deep foundations of Mount Cael, in the walls thick with owed and owed and owed, something began—very softly, very far away—to sing.

* * *

The Tallyman closed the contest with a formality, in a voice gone thin as old vellum: *"Pledge met. Witness entered. The instrument is... contested."*

The light on the obsidian desk flickered.

Then, all around the gallery, in columns of script six hundred years deep, the first letters began to lift off the stone—peeling upward like ash from a page, glowing gold at the edges, and the hum of the ledger changed key, and somewhere below them a lock the size of a mountain turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to open.

"Hold on to her," Serane said. "Whatever happens next—don't let go."

Rafael already hadn't.
