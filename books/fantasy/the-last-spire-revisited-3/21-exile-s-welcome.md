## 21. Exile's Welcome

The mountain let them go at dawn.

Rafael remembered it afterward as a kind of exhalation—the whole mass of Mount Cael shuddering once, like a sleeper turning over, and then the gates of salt and bone standing open with no one left to guard them. The freed souls came up the long galleries in silence, hundreds of them, thousands, a procession that had no order and needed none. They passed Rafael where they stood braced against the outer wall, Isolde's small hand buried in theirs, and not one of them stopped. Why would they? They had been owed their leaving for centuries. They walked through the morning light and scattered like ash off a page, each one drifting toward whatever remained of the life that had been stolen from under them.

Some went as light. That was the truth of it, and Rafael did not look away from it. Not every soul had a body waiting, or a life worth returning to, or anything at all but the memory of a name. Those simply thinned into the bright air above the mountain, and the air seemed to hold them a moment longer than it needed to, the way a hand holds a bird it knows it must open.

Brannoc stood at the gate and watched them go. He had no voice to sing with—had traded it away long before any of this—but when the last of them passed him he raised both hands, palms out, and held them there until his arms shook. A farewell in the only grammar he had left. Then he turned back toward his gate, because someone had to keep it now, though what it kept out or in, no one could say anymore.

Serane walked down the mountain road behind them, gray-faced, soot-burned, her inquisitor's coat scorched along one sleeve where the tunnel had come down around her shoulders. She carried nothing. The Order would have stripped her name from its rolls by nightfall; Rafael could see her already doing the arithmetic of it, measuring the distance between the woman she had been yesterday and the woman walking downhill in borrowed boots. Once, passing a spring that trickled out of the rocks, she stopped and drank from her cupped hands like anyone else, and Rafael pretended not to see how much that cost her.

They reached the lowlands in three days. At the crossroads where the pilgrim track met the Vell road, Serane stopped.

"They'll have my testimony by now," she said. "Ondrel will have burned it twice and read it three times. He'll want you brought in—not hanged, worse than hanged, made an example. And the child—" Her jaw tightened. "The child they'll want most of all."

"Then we don't go to Vell," Rafael said.

"You can't hide forever. You have no magic left." She said it without cruelty; it was simply the ledger, balanced. "You gave all of it away. Every charm you ever sold in that market, every spark you hoarded for twenty years—it's spent. You're the emptiest man in Vell province, Eriksen, and I'm the second. We are two people with nothing left to take." Something moved behind her eyes then, something that was almost a smile and almost grief. "Which makes us, for the first time in our lives, free."

She left them at the crossroads, walking north toward the coast, toward whatever reckoning she intended to have with her sister's memory. Rafael watched her go until the road bent, and Isolde watched too, and when the inquisitor was gone the child looked up and said, in her careful, new-learned Common: "She is sad."

"Yes."

"But she chose." Isolde considered this with the terrible gravity of eight years old and older-than-mountains. "That is a good sadness."

* * *

They did not go to Vell. They went around it, keeping to hedgerows and drainage ditches, to the backs of fields where the Order's riders did not bother looking, and came at last to the old spire ruins on the third evening, because there was nowhere else.

That was the whole reason. Rafael wanted to be honest about it, even alone inside their own skull: they went because there was nowhere else. The spire had been wrecked for a century—since the Order's first great purging, when the tower of the old faith had been pulled down stone by stone and its stones used to pave the temple courtyard where edicts against magic were read aloud. What remained was a stump of wall, a broken stair climbing to nowhere, and a floor of packed earth where rain pooled and moss wrote its slow green sentences. It stood on the moor above Vell, visible from nowhere important, forgotten by everyone who mattered.

Rafael sat down against the broken wall with Isolde beside them and felt, for the first time since childhood, precisely nothing ahead of them.

No debt. That should have been the miracle. Twenty years of their life had been structured around the debt—Master Ondrel's patient arithmetic, the quarterly reckonings, the knowledge that everything they earned and everything they were flowed uphill to the Order. Now the Ledger was ash in a mountain, the Undercourt was dust and ink, and Rafael Eriksen owed nothing to anyone alive or dead.

And they owned nothing either. No magic—that had gone into the contest, poured out of them like water from a cracked jug, and the place inside where it had lived was not even empty now; empty implied walls. It was just absence, open to weather. No shop. No name worth speaking in the city below, where by now the criers would be reading their description aloud: *traitor, heretic, trafficker in forbidden arts.* No craft, even. A scribe without an Order was a man who knew how to make letters and had no permission to make any.

"I'm going to teach you more Common," Rafael said, mostly to fill the silence. "Properly. All of it. You shouldn't have to live in my head to talk."

Isolde was drawing in the dirt with a stick. She drew the mountain first, then the road down, then—smaller, with fierce little strokes—a figure that was unmistakably Rafael, complete with the ink-stain on the left hand that never washed out. She pointed at the figure, then at herself, then pressed her stick-hand flat against her chest.

"MINE," she said. In the First Tongue first, and then, deliberately, in Common. "Mine. You are mine. I said it the first night. I am saying it again."

Rafael's throat closed. "You can't claim people, little one. That's what the Tallyman thought."

"Not claim." She frowned, hunting through the small vocabulary of Common she'd built on the road. "Own-no. Belong-to. I belong to you. You belong to me." She tapped the ground between them. "This is how family works. Wrenna told me. While you slept."

"Wrenna told you how family works?"

"Wrenna tells everyone everything. It is why they took her collar away." Isolde smiled then, quick and crooked, and Rafael felt the ache of it—because the smile was ordinary, child-shaped, and everything else about her was a language the world had tried to bury.

They made a poor camp of the ruin. Rafael had learned, in twenty years of double lives, to sleep in snatches and eat very little, and these skills transferred without alteration to poverty. Isolde sang while Rafael dozed—an old song, older than kingdoms, and now that the court beneath the mountain was unmade, the song had nowhere to pull toward. It was only a song. It filled the ruined spire like water filling a cup, and Rafael slept better than they had in twenty years.

* * *

On the fourth morning, there was a woman at the edge of the ruin.

Rafael woke to the wrongness of it—footsteps on the moor, unhurried, unhidden—and came up off the packed earth with a scribe's knife in their hand before their mind caught up with their body. Old habits. The knife was for sharpening pens. It had never cut anything but quills and, once, badly, a thumb.

The woman stopped at the fallen lintel stone and waited. She was perhaps fifty, dressed in a fishwife's shawl over a clerk's posture, and she carried a basket covered with cloth. Behind her, further down the slope, stood two others: a boy of maybe sixteen with the faint silver scarring of a burn-hex across his cheek, and an old man leaning on a staff, whose shadow—Rafael saw it clearly in the slanting morning light—fell the wrong way, east when the sun stood west. Marked. All three of them marked, the way half the hidden city was marked, the way Rafael had been marked for twenty years and had sold charms to them anyway, harmless little things, harmless, harmless—

"You're the scribe," the woman said. "Eriksen. From the night market."

Rafael did not lower the knife. "I'm a traitor. If you've come for the reward, the Order pays better for the child taken alive. I'd wait if I were you."

Something flickered across the woman's face—hurt, quickly mastered. She set the basket down on the lintel stone and folded back the cloth. Bread, still warm. Salt fish. Apples. A clay jar that smelled, when the wind turned, of Wrenna's kitchen: onions and barley and the ghost of a hundred meals stretched past reason to feed the unwanted.

"Wrenna sent us," she said. "Before they took her in. She had time to send word to everyone, and what she said was this: *the bargain is broken, the mountain is opened, and the scribe paid the price. Go to the spire. Go and ask.*" She straightened, and Rafael saw now that her hands were shaking, that she had been frightened the whole walk up the moor and had come anyway. "So we're asking. My son has the resonance—you've seen it, the burn-scars, they tried to scourge it out of him when he was nine. The old man is Havel; his grandmother spoke the First Tongue before the purging, and he remembers four words of it, and he says them every night so they won't die with him." She swallowed. "We heard what happened under the mountain. Everyone's hearing it, in the kitchens and the cellars and the tanneries, everywhere the Order doesn't reach. That the language isn't dead. That a child speaks it. That a soul can't be sold after all—not truly sold—if someone is willing to stand in the court and say *no.*"

"We heard," said the boy, from down the slope. His voice cracked on the word and he pushed through anyway. "We heard somebody finally said no."

Rafael lowered the knife.

It occurred to them, standing in the ruins of the faith that had raised them, being looked at by people who had every reason to look away, that they had expected nothing. That was not self-pity; it was bookkeeping, and Rafael had always been good at books. They were magicless, masterless, officially damned. The story of what they'd done might spread through the cellars of Vell, but stories were cheap, and gratitude cheaper, and neither fed anyone through a winter. They had climbed down into hell and given away the only thing of value they possessed, and they had done it knowing—knowing absolutely—that the world does not reward that kind of arithmetic. The Order taught that lesson early and well: virtue is a loss, and losses must be written down.

And here was a woman with warm bread, asking.

"What is it you want?" Rafael asked, and heard their own voice come out strange, unarmored.

"To learn," the woman said. "What she taught you. The language. The—" she groped for it— "the standing-there. The saying no. Whatever it is you know now that lets a person refuse a king made of ledgers. Teach us that."

"I don't know if it can be taught."

"Then we'll sit here while you fail," said old Havel, in a voice like dry leaves, "and we'll keep coming back until you succeed. I'm eighty-one years old, scribe. I've been failing at things for seventy years. I'm very patient company."

Behind Rafael, small feet on packed earth. Isolde came forward and stood at the broken threshold of the spire, looking at the three strangers on the moor, and Rafael felt her listening—not with ears—with whatever it was she was made of. Then she lifted her hand, palm out, the way Brannoc had at the gate, and said four words.

Havel made a sound like a man struck. He dropped his staff. He got down on his knees in the wet grass of the moor, this ancient marked man, and answered her in the same tongue, stumbling, mispronouncing, four words worn smooth by sixty years of secret repetition—and Isolde laughed, a bright startled sound, and corrected his grammar.

The woman began, quietly, to cry.

* * *

By the ninth day there were eleven of them. By the twelfth, nineteen.

They came up from Vell in twos and threes, at dusk and before dawn, carrying what they could: blankets, a cooking pot, candles, a hand-mill, someone's grandmother's chair. A tanner named Odile who could make water flow uphill when she hummed. Two brothers who shared one gift between them—neither could light a candle alone, together they could light a field. A widow whose dead husband's voice sometimes came through her mouth in his accent, saying sensible domestic things, which she had never told another living soul. A temple acolyte who had fled with nothing but a stolen copy of the Edict Against Magic, which he proposed, with a nervous laugh, that Rafael correct for errors.

Rafael corrected the errors. It was, absurdly, the first piece of honest work they had done in twenty years.

The lessons happened in the mornings, in the shell of the spire, with Isolde at the center like a hearthstone. She taught the sounds first—the First Tongue was not learned the way Common was learned, Rafael discovered; it was remembered, the way you remember a house you lived in before you could walk. Words surfaced in people whole. The widow wept the first time she said her own true name aloud, the one her grandmother had used, the one that had never appeared in any ledger. The brothers lit their field. Old Havel, four words grown to forty in a fortnight, sat in the corner of the ruin every evening singing to himself in a language the Order had spent a century declaring extinct, and no rider came, and the moor wind carried the sound down toward the city where ten thousand frightened people were deciding, one household at a time, whether to hope.

Rafael taught what they could: letters, mostly. Grammar. The discipline of writing things down so they could not be unsaid. They had no magic and never would again—they checked, some nights, in private, reaching into that open absence inside themselves the way a tongue finds a missing tooth—but they found they did not need it. It was a strange discovery, and a slow one: that the thing they had guarded for twenty years, hoarded and hidden and finally spent, had never actually been the valuable part. The valuable part was the standing-there. The saying no. The willingness to write your own name in a contract and mean it.

Wrenna arrived on the seventeenth day, walking up the moor road with a bruise fading yellow along her jaw and no collar and no apology, carrying a sack of flour over one shoulder like a sack of flour.

"They released me," she said, dropping it by the fire. "Couldn't prove anything. Couldn't disprove anything either. Ondrel hates that more than heresy, I think—he likes his ledgers clean." She looked around the ruin—at the shelters going up against the broken wall, at the children already orbiting Isolde, at the smoke rising from a fire that had no business burning anywhere within thirty miles of the temple city—and she grinned with her split lip. "Well, scribe. Look what you started."

"I didn't start anything. I ran away."

"You ran away *here.*" Wrenna dropped onto the old chair someone had carried up the moor, the grandmother's chair, which had somehow become hers by universal unspoken consent. "There's a difference. Everything that happens next happens because of the difference."

"And what happens next?"

Wrenna took her time answering. She looked out over the moor, where the evening was coming down gold and violet, and where the path from Vell showed, far off, two more small figures walking toward the spire with bundles on their backs.

"That depends," she said, "on whether you can hold what's coming. Because it's coming, Rafael. Word travels faster than riders when it wants to. Half the marked in Vell are going to end up on this moor, and the other half are going to ask the Order why they haven't been invited. Ondrel won't tolerate a lit beacon on his horizon. Sooner or later—and I'd wager sooner—he sends someone up this hill." She paused. "Not soldiers. He's learned that lesson. Someone quieter."

Rafael looked at the smoke rising from their fire, thin and visible for miles against the darkening sky, and found—to their own quiet astonishment—that they were not afraid. Or rather: they were afraid, thoroughly, in the old familiar way, but the fear sat in them differently now. It sat beside the bread and the singing and the child correcting old men's grammar, instead of above it. Fear as a fact among facts, not a law.

"Let him send someone," Rafael said.

Isolde appeared at their elbow, drawn back from her circle of students by some instinct of her own, and slipped her hand into theirs. She followed their gaze to the two figures on the distant road, and then she did something she had done only once before, on a storm-night at a shop door that no longer existed: she knelt, and wrote in the ash at the edge of the fire-pit, in careful letters that glowed briefly with borrowed light before settling into plain char:

**MINE.**

But this time there was more than one word. This time, below it, in her quickening hand, she added a line that made Rafael's breath catch—because it was not the First Tongue and it was not quite Common either, but something new, something being born right there in the ashes of a ruined faith, a grammar that belonged to no ledger and no king:

*All of us.*

Down the moor, the two figures crested the rise and paused, silhouetted against the last light, and Rafael saw that one of them was limping, and that the taller wore the scorched coat of an inquisitor, and that she was not walking north to the sea at all.

Serane Halvorsen had come home.
