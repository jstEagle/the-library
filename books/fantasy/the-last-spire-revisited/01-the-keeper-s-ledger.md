## 1. The Keeper's Ledger

There are three things a gatekeeper of the Last Spire must never do, according to the founding charter that hangs, water-stained and mostly illegible, above my desk: she must never open the gate, she must never speak the gate's true name aloud, and she must never, under any circumstances, fall asleep during the autumn inventory.

I had managed the first two for eleven years. The third I managed most years.

The ledger smelled of mildew and old ink, which is to say it smelled like every autumn of my life here. I sat at my desk in the gatehouse's back room with a cup of tea gone cold beside me and wrote down, in careful clerk's hand, everything the mountain had told me this season — or rather, everything a woman who paid very close attention to her surroundings might plausibly have noticed without anyone getting suspicious about *how* closely she was paying attention.

*Route three, the Goat's Stair,* I wrote. *Used twice since midsummer. Boot prints, size small, likely Rhea Voss's people. Cargo smell of salt fish and something sweeter underneath — southern wine, unlicensed.*

*Route seven, under the Widow's Tooth. One passage, heavy loads. Someone is moving iron north, which is stupid, because there is nothing north worth selling iron to except trouble.*

*Route eleven, the river cave.* Here I paused, because route eleven was interesting, and interesting things in the Thornfell mountains were usually the kind of interesting that got people killed. *Fresh rope burns on the stone at head height. New anchors. Whoever used it knew what they were doing and expected to come back.*

The truth — the truth I would burn this page rather than record anywhere — was that I hadn't noticed any of it with my eyes. My eyes were forty-one years old and had spent too many winters squinting into snow glare to be trusted at fifty paces. No. I had felt it. Pressed my palm flat against cold granite in the grey dark before dawn and let the stone tell me its stories: the weight of feet that had crossed it days ago, the vibration still humming faintly in the rock like the last note of a struck bell, the particular signature of Rhea Voss's boots, which she wore down on the outside edge because she walked like she was perpetually about to change her mind.

Stone-sense, my mother had called it, before the word *gifted* became something you whispered instead of said. A small gift, she'd told me. Useless, nearly. A party trick. Keep it hidden, keep it quiet, keep it *small*, Wendell-girl, because the world has no patience for anything it can't tax or own.

My mother had been right about almost everything, and dead twenty years, so she'd had no chance to revise her opinions when the world got worse.

I dipped the pen again. Outside the shuttered window, October was doing what October did in the Thornfell — throwing sleet against the slates one minute and offering thin gold sunlight the next, as if the sky couldn't commit. The gatehouse creaked around me in the wind, that familiar complaint of timber and old mortar that I'd long ago stopped hearing and only noticed now, writing it down, the way you notice your own heartbeat.

"Keeper?"

Tam's voice through the door, accompanied by the knock he still hadn't learned to time properly — two raps and then a third, apologetic, always late.

"It's not locked, Tam. It's never locked. We've discussed this."

He came in carrying an armful of split kindling and the particular expression he wore when he wanted something. He was nineteen, all elbows and ambition, with his dead father's jaw and his living mother's talent for making you feel guilty without saying a single word. I'd taken him on four years ago because his family needed the wage and the Spire needed hands, and in four years he'd become the closest thing I had to — well. To what, exactly, I tried not to examine.

"Wood for the stove," he said, unnecessarily, dumping it in the basket. "And the west stair's iced over again. I salted it."

"Thank you."

"And—" There it was. "Riders on the high road yesterday. Three of them, crown colors. They stopped at the toll house and asked after traffic through the pass."

My pen did not stop moving, because stopping would have been interesting, and I have made a life's discipline of being the least interesting thing in any room. "Traffic through the pass," I repeated. "In autumn. What did they expect, a trade caravan and a dancing bear?"

"They asked if anyone strange had come through." He shifted the empty basket from arm to arm. "Strange. That was the word."

"Mm." I finished the line I was writing — *route eleven, see above* — and looked up at him with all the bland patience I could manufacture. "And what did you tell them?"

"That we get smugglers and pilgrims and once a man who claimed to be a bishop of somewhere that doesn't exist. Same as everyone gets." He hesitated. "They gave out papers. Proclamation. I took one because they were handing them out and refusing felt — you know. Memorable."

He set it on my desk, face down, the way you'd deliver news of a death.

I didn't pick it up right away. I finished my tea instead, which was cold and tasted of the iron pipe, and I listened — really listened, the way I hadn't let myself do in front of another living soul since I was a girl — to the mountain beneath us.

Granite doesn't lie. That's the first thing you learn, if you're cursed the way I'm cursed, or blessed, depending on which century you were born into. Wood swells and shrinks and tells you whatever the weather wants. Water says whatever's upstream. But stone keeps honest accounts. And the stone under the Last Spire, that morning, was keeping an account I didn't like: a faint wrongness deep below, like a tooth aching at the root, like something old and vast shifting its weight in its sleep.

The wards. Eleven years I'd kept this gate, and I had never once felt the wards do anything but hold — a deep, patient thrum under everything, the founders' work, older than the kingdom, older than the names carved on the vault doors I wasn't allowed to open. It held the way the mountains held. You built your whole life on top of it and forgot it was a thing that could ever be otherwise.

Now it hummed a half-tone flat. Like a bell with a crack in it.

"Keeper?" Tam said. "You've gone somewhere."

"I'm deciding whether the bishop of nowhere counts as strange," I said, and picked up the proclamation.

It was crown stock, good vellum, the royal seal stamped deep enough to feel with a thumbnail — someone had spent real money on this. The script was the chancellor's cramped hand, or a copyist doing a fair impression of it. I read it twice, because the first time I was sure I'd misunderstood.

*By order of the Crown: that persons possessing unnatural faculties — herein named the gifted — are declared subject to seizure and examination for the security of the realm. Information leading to the identification of such persons shall be rewarded with silver, at the discretion of the Crown's officers. Concealment of such persons shall be treated as treason.*

*Treason.* Not a fine. Not exile. Treason, which in Edran's kingdom had exactly one price, and it was paid at a scaffold.

"Well," Tam said, when I'd been silent too long. "That's grim."

"It's efficient," I said. "Grim would at least require effort."

"You think it's real? Half these proclamations are theater. The crown likes reminding everyone it exists."

I thought about the stone under my feet, humming flat. I thought about the iron moving north along route seven, and the new anchors in the river cave, and three riders in crown colors asking after *strange*. Pieces don't usually start moving all at once unless a board's been tilted.

"I think," I said carefully, "that it concerns the cities, and the lowlands, and people with fire in their fingers or voices that make men weep. It does not concern us. We keep a door. Nobody hunts the woman who sweeps a porch."

Which was a lie of such craftsmanship I nearly admired it myself. Tam looked at me a moment too long — he'd gotten better at reading me this past year, which was inconvenient — but then he shrugged and reached for the basket.

"There's stew on," he said. "If the bishop of nowhere taught me anything, it's eat while it's hot."

He left. I waited until his footsteps faded down the hall, until the gatehouse settled back into its wind-creak silence, and then I put both palms flat on the desk, on the old oak that had grown up rooted in Thornfell granite, and I listened the way you listen when you're alone and allowed.

The ache in the deep stone was still there. Fainter here than it had been at the gate itself, muffled by a hundred yards of rock, but present, patient, wrong. And threaded through it now — I pressed harder, closed my eyes, went deeper than I liked to go — something else. A rhythm. Very slow. Very far below.

Almost like breathing.

I sat back. My hands had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

Eleven years of ledgers, and every season's entry ended the same way: *All wards sound. Gate sealed. No anomalies.* It was practically liturgy. I dipped my pen, hovered it over the line, and found I couldn't write it. Not tonight. Not with the mountain muttering in its sleep.

Instead I wrote: *All wards sound.* A pause, ink blotting. Then, smaller: *Perhaps.*

* * *

The next morning I went up to the gate itself, because I am a great believer in checking whether the floor is actually falling through before you start rearranging the furniture.

The Last Spire does not look like much, which is its first and best defense. Travelers coming up the pass see a spur of black granite jutting from the shoulder of Mount Cairn, weathered into something like a broken tower, and below it a wall across the throat of the valley, and in the wall a gate — sixty feet of seamless grey stone with no hinges, no handle, no seam a knife could find, covered in carvings worn nearly smooth by ten centuries of wind. Pilgrims leave ribbons on the iron fence Rhea's grandfather's generation installed for exactly that purpose. Children dare each other to touch the gate and report that it feels cold. Everything about it says *old, dull, finished*.

Everything except what it says to me.

I sent Tam off to re-hang the shrine shutters — the wind had torn one loose, and he needed the errand more than the shrine needed him — and stood before the gate alone with my bare hand against the stone.

Cold, yes. But under the cold, the ledger of the world.

The gate was singing. That's the nearest word I have; I've never been a poet, gods know, but there's no other way to say it. The founders' work ran through the stone like grain through wood, layer upon layer of intent laid down by hands ten hundred years dead, each thread still taut, still holding — and I could read them the way Rhea reads tide charts, easily, the way you read your own handwriting. *Hold. Close. Remember.* Three words, essentially, woven ten thousand times. Hold the hollow kingdom behind us. Close the road between. Remember what was done here, and why.

Except.

Except that near the base of the gate, where the stone met the bedrock, one thread had gone slack. Just one, out of thousands — a hairline of silence in the song, the way a lute string sounds when it's a quarter-tone flat and dying. And through that silence seeped the thing I'd felt from the gatehouse: the slow, vast rhythm underneath. Breathing. Or breathing's poor cousin. Something on the far side of the seal, drawing on it, the way frost draws heat out of a windowpane.

I snatched my hand back. My heart was going hard enough that I could hear it in my ears.

Steady, Wendell. You've felt tremors before. Earthquakes come and go; the mountain groans; the wardens in the low country write alarmed letters and nothing comes of it. This might be nothing. This might be a settling of the deep strata, a cold snap working into cracks, any of the hundred ordinary explanations.

But the ordinary explanations didn't explain the slack thread. Wards don't fray from weather. The founders built them to outlast the language they were carved in, and they had, so far.

I did what a keeper does. I got out the chalk and marked the spot — a small sigil, keeper's shorthand, meaningless to anyone but me and the two dead women whose notes I'd inherited. I inspected the rest of the wall, the buttresses, the drainage channel Tam had cleared last week. I noted the frost-heave on the eastern stair for spring repair. Routine, routine, routine, performed with the particular thoroughness of a woman buying time to think.

Then I went back down and pulled the oldest ledger off the shelf — my predecessor's predecessor, Maren Ostwick, keeper for thirty-one years, whose entries I had inherited along with the chalk and the keys and the loneliness. Maren had been dry as gravel in her records, except once. Once, in her ninth year, there was a page where the handwriting lost its composure, the letters climbing uphill:

*Felt the gate dreaming today. Told no one. Telling no one. If it wakes, it wakes, and I will have kept the door to the end.*

I had read that entry eleven years ago and filed it under *eccentricities of the elderly.* Now I sat with it open on my knee while the stove ticked and the sleet came back, and I understood it completely, and hated her a little for leaving me no instructions.

Because here was the arithmetic of my position, plain as a merchant's column. If I reported the tremor — to whom? The warden-marshal at Greyford, who reported to the capital — then men in crown colors would come crawling over my pass asking questions, and the proclamation on my desk had just informed me what happened to people who answered those questions interestingly. If I stayed silent and the silence held, I'd gained nothing but a bad night's sleep. And if I stayed silent and it didn't hold —

Well. Then I'd have kept the door to the end, in Maren's phrase, though I suspected she hadn't meant it quite so literally.

There was a third option, and her name was Rhea Voss, and she owed me eleven years of favors she'd have cheerfully denied owing. Rhea ran the passes the way water runs downhill: inevitably, and around anything in her path. She had ears in every toll house from here to the coast, and more importantly she had no loyalty whatsoever to the crown, which in the current climate amounted to a virtue.

She was due within the fortnight — route eleven, the river cave, if my stone had read her anchors rightly. I could wait for her. Ask her, casually, over cheap wine and expensive silences, what the low country was saying about this hunt for the gifted. What the crown was really doing with the people it seized. Whether anyone else had felt the mountains mutter.

Whether anyone, anywhere, was still safe to tell.

Outside, the light was failing early the way it did this time of year, the sleet turning to proper snow, fat and slow. Somewhere below the floor, below the cellar, below the roots of the Spire, the old work of the founders sang its ten-thousand-year song, one thread flat.

I opened the current ledger and wrote the day's entry in my best clerk's hand, the picture of a boring woman keeping boring records: *Gate inspected. All sound. Weather deteriorating. Shrine repairs ongoing.*

Then, beneath it, where only I would ever look, I added a single line:

*The stone is breathing. Counting the days until Voss comes, and wondering, for the first time in eleven years, who I would run to if I had to run at all.*
