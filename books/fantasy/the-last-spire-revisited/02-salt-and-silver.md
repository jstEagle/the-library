## 2. Salt and Silver

Rhea Voss arrived at the Spire on the first cold morning of autumn, which was fitting, because Rhea had always been the kind of weather you dressed for.

I heard her before I saw her—the particular jingle of her mule's harness bells, tuned deliberately out of rhythm so that no ward-listener could mistake them for anything official. Then her boots on the switchback stones, unhurried, and finally her voice carrying up the cliff face ahead of her like a herald announcing itself.

"Wendell Kavanagh! Are you dead? Blink twice if you're dead. If you're dead I'm taking your stove."

She came around the last bend of the path with salt frost in her dark hair and a grin that had gotten three men hanged and one knight married. Forty years old if she was a day, and she'd have told you thirty-five with the same straight face she used to swear she wasn't carrying contraband. She wore a captain's coat cut for a taller woman and a knife in each boot and, I suspected, a third somewhere I'd never found.

"You're early," I said.

"I'm punctual. The world is late." She dropped her pack on my doorstep without being invited, which was how she said hello. "Tea. And don't give me the pine-needle swill you drink when no one's watching. I climbed a mountain for this."

"It *is* pine needle," I said. "But it's the good pine needle."

"That's what you said last time." She settled herself onto my bench as though the bench owed her rent. "And the time before. One of these years I'm going to find out what happens to smugglers who drink bad tea."

"Nothing. They just complain about it forever."

"Exactly. Eternal suffering. Very biblical." She held out her cup before I'd even filled the kettle, an act of faith or presumption, and with Rhea those were always the same thing.

I should explain what Rhea Voss was to me, though I'm not sure I ever managed to explain it to myself. She ran the Salt Road—the old smugglers' track through the Thornfells that bypassed the crown tolls—and she had run it for twenty years, which meant she knew every goat path, every collapsed tunnel, every blind spot in the border wards better than I did, and I kept the gate. Twice a season she came up to trade: her news of the lowlands for my silence about her routes. It was a transaction that had been running so long it had grown moss and called itself a friendship, and neither of us ever examined it too closely, the way you don't look too hard at a load-bearing wall.

She brought me things, too. Books, mostly—smuggled, naturally, because everything Rhea touched arrived pre-smuggled whether it needed to be or not. Last spring it had been a treatise on ward-theory printed in Vhalen, where they still taught such things openly. This time, when I looked in her pack while the kettle muttered, there were three books wrapped in oilcloth and something else, something flat and heavy that I decided not to ask about until she offered, which she wouldn't, unless she wanted me to know.

"So," I said. "The mountain moved again. Two nights ago. A deep roll, down under the third gallery. My teacup walked off the shelf by itself."

"Your mountain's always grumbling. It's a mountain. That's the job description."

"This wasn't grumbling. This was—" I paused, because saying what it was meant admitting how much I could feel, and I hadn't survived forty-one years in the Thornfells by advertising. "It was structural. Something shifted that doesn't usually shift."

Rhea watched me over the rim of her cup with eyes the color of harbor water in November. She didn't push. That was one of her better qualities; she collected secrets the way other captains collected cargo, but she never pried at yours unless she thought she could sell it.

"The deep roads are still open?" she asked instead.

"As of yesterday. Though if the stone keeps talking to itself, I can't promise next month."

"Then we'll do business today." She set the cup down and got to it, because under all the theater Rhea Voss was the most efficient person I have ever known. "New route through the Widow's Teeth—the old quarry road, north fork. Crown surveyors walked it in high summer, marked it for a toll station come spring. If they build there, half my traffic dies. I need your word the passage logs stay... vague."

"My passage logs say 'weather, poor; wolves, numerous,'" I said. "They've said that for six years. The crown archivists think the Thornfells are uninhabited except by wolves and bad weather."

"Bless your tedious little ledger." She pressed her hand to her heart. "What do you want for it?"

I considered. "News," I said. "Real news, not tavern gossip. What's moving in the capital?"

Something crossed her face then—quick, gone before I could name it, the way a fish crosses beneath ice. Rhea was better than most people at controlling her face, but not better than me, and not better than the fact that I'd known her since we were both hungry teenagers stealing from the same granary in Dunmarrow.

"Why?" she asked. "You've never cared about the capital. You once described the capital as 'a tumor with a harbor.'"

"I still do. But tumors metastasize, and mine has been quiet too long. No edicts, no tax riders, nothing from the crown in four months. The gate records go unanswered. Even the tolls haven't gone up, and the tolls *always* go up. A quiet king frightens me more than a loud one."

"A quiet king," Rhea repeated, and there it was again, that flicker under the ice. She turned her cup slowly on its saucer, aligning the handle with some private meridian. "Wendell. When did you last leave the Spire?"

"Eight years ago. You know that."

"And you remember why."

"I remember that the last time I went down into Dunmarrow, a child grabbed my hand in the market to steady herself on the stairs, and I felt every stone in the street at once, and the street felt *me*, and the cobbles shifted under three hundred feet of traffic because a rock remembers being touched by one of my kind the way a dog remembers being kicked." I kept my voice level, which took work. "So no, I don't go down into cities."

"You could learn to mask it. Sefa the healer supposedly teaches—"

"Sefa the healer lives in Ashmarket, which is a week away, and masking is a skill for people whose gift matters enough to hide. Mine is pebbles and doorframes. Nobody hunts a woman who knows when a bridge is tired."

Rhea was silent for long enough that the kettle began its second mutter. When she spoke again, she'd put her captain's voice back on, smooth and pleasant and utterly false, and I realized with a coldness in my stomach that I had heard that voice only a handful of times in twenty years, and never aimed at me.

"Then let's keep it that way," she said. "Logs stay vague. Route stays open. And you stay up here, keeper, with your wolves and your weather and your tedious ledger, and whatever comes out of the capital stays down there where it belongs."

"*Whatever* comes out of the capital," I said slowly, "is clearly already here, because you just told me to stay out of its way, and Rhea Voss does not warn people off roads for free. Warning people is expensive. I've watched you charge for it."

She laughed—that real laugh, sudden and rough, the counterfeit falling away for a heartbeat. "Damn you. Twenty years and you still read me like a tide table." She reached into her pack and drew out the flat heavy thing I'd declined to ask about. Not contraband after all. Paper. A folded proclamation, the seal broken, the wax stamped with the crowned tower of Edran's dynasty. She laid it on my table between us the way a physician lays out a saw.

"Read it," she said. "Then decide how much tea you want left in that kettle."

I unfolded it. The script was chancery-formal, the kind of handwriting that exists to be difficult rather than read:

*By order of His Majesty King Edran, Third of His Name, Protector of the Realm: Let it be proclaimed throughout the free cities and crown lands that the practice of unlicensed gift-craft is henceforth forbidden. All persons possessing or concealing gifts of any nature shall present themselves to the nearest crown office within ninety days for assessment and licensing. Reward of fifty crowns silver shall be paid to any subject providing true information leading to the discovery of an unlicensed gifted person. Concealment of a gifted person shall be adjudged treason, and punished accordingly.*

Fifty crowns silver. I read that number twice, because it took a moment to understand its size. Fifty crowns was a fishing boat. Fifty crowns was a decade of a laborer's wages. Fifty crowns was enough to make a neighbor into an informant, a friend into a witness, a sister into a stranger with good arithmetic.

"Licensing," I said. My voice sounded far away, like it belonged to someone standing outside my own door. "They've never licensed gifts before. Gifts aren't illegal. They've never been *anything*. There's no law about them. There's never needed to be."

"There is now." Rhea refilled her own cup, which I took as a sign that even she wanted something to hold. "It went up in the capital a month ago. Criers in every city square. Church pulpits reading it Sunday last—the priests were ordered to, Wendell. Ordered. And the assessment offices are already staffed. That's not a proclamation, that's a machine, built and oiled before anyone announced it. Somebody's been preparing this for years."

"Assessment," I repeated. "And what happens to people who fail their assessment?"

Nobody answered, because nobody knew, and the not-knowing sat on the table between us like a third guest.

I thought of the market in Dunmarrow, the child's hand, the cobbles waking under my touch. I thought of how many people in the free cities had small gifts like mine—a knack for stone, a way with birds, dreams that came true one time in ten—and how many neighbors had debts, grudges, ambitions, and now a price list. Ninety days. After that, concealment was treason. Which meant that everyone who'd ever been kind to a gifted neighbor, everyone who'd ever looked away, was suddenly holding a hot coal and being asked, politely, by their king, to drop it on someone else.

"Your crew," I said. "Any of them gifted?"

"Two. Old Maren reads weather like scripture—you knew that, you let her through the pass on the strength of it. And young Piet has a hand with rope, knots untie themselves around him, which sounds charming until you realize it's a gift and he's seventeen and stupid and will brag about it in the wrong tavern within a fortnight." She rubbed her jaw. "I've told them both. Assessment offices, ninety days, and after that I can't carry them. Carrying them makes me a traitor, and I have eleven souls on the *Kestrel* who didn't sign on to hang for two."

"And you'll send them in. To be assessed."

"I'll put them on a ship to Vhalen," Rhea said sharply. "What do you take me for? But that's *my* problem, Wendell, and it's handled. Yours—" she gestured at the proclamation, at the mountains visible past my window, at the whole careful life I'd stacked like dry stones— "yours is sitting right there drinking your tea."

"I'm not gifted," I said. "Officially, I'm a gatekeeper with a good ear for avalanches."

"Officially." She leaned forward. "And unofficially? Unofficially, I have watched you lay your palm on a bridge strut and tell me which cable would snap, three weeks before it snapped. Unofficially, you once redirected a rockslide by shouting at it, and I have spent twenty years telling myself I imagined that, because imagining it was safer than knowing it. Unofficially, Wendell Kavanagh, you are worth fifty crowns silver to every desperate soul between here and the coast, and I am the only person south of Vhalen who knows it."

The wind moved through the pines outside. Somewhere below us, faint as a pulse, the mountain murmured along its old seams, and for the first time in my life I wondered whether it murmured *about* me.

"Why tell me?" I asked quietly. "You could sell me. Fifty crowns. Don't tell me the *Kestrel* doesn't need her hull patched."

For a moment—for just one unguarded moment—I saw something move behind Rhea's harbor-water eyes that wasn't arithmetic. Then it was gone, packed away in the hold with everything else she never unloaded.

"Because selling you buys me one winter," she said, "and keeping you buys me a gatekeeper who owes me. That's all this is. That's all it's ever been. Don't make it ugly by pretending otherwise—we're both too old and too fond of each other to survive the embarrassment."

"No," I agreed. "God forbid." But my hands weren't quite steady when I gathered the cups, and she noticed, because she noticed everything.

She stayed the night, which she rarely did, and left before dawn with her books and her secrets and her mule's deliberately discordant bells. At the edge of the switchback she stopped, turned, and called up to me in the gray light:

"Ninety days, Wendell! Count them! And when the counting gets short, you send for me—I know roads out of these mountains that even the mountain doesn't!"

"I'm not going anywhere," I called back. "I'm a fixture."

"Fixtures get ripped out when the building changes hands!" And she was gone, laughing, down into the pines, leaving me alone with the proclamation, the cold, and a ledger full of lies I had suddenly, urgently, to make more convincing.

I stood on my threshold a long while. Below me the Thornfells fell away in folds of dark forest toward the lowlands, toward the cities, toward ninety days ticking down in chancery ink. Above me the Spire rose against the pale sky, sealed and patient, keeping whatever it kept.

I put my bare hand against the doorpost—an old habit, a comfort, the stone cool and familiar under my palm, murmuring its slow granite thoughts—and for the first time it occurred to me that the mountain had felt the tremor coming for longer than I knew. That maybe it had been trying to tell me something all autumn, and I, fluent as I was, hadn't been listening closely enough.

*They're coming,* the stone might have said, if stone said things plainly. *They are coming up your road.*

I didn't hear that yet, though. What I heard was only the ordinary creak of the world, and I went inside to falsify my ledgers, believing I had until winter.

I had nineteen days.

* * *

Tam found the proclamation before I mentioned it to him—which I mention now because it matters, because I have gone over that morning ten thousand times looking for the hinge on which everything turned, and the hinge, God help me, was me.

He came in from the woodpile with snow in his hair and a strange brightness in his face he couldn't quite arrange into innocence, and he said, "There's a rider at the toll house showing a paper to Marta. Fifty crowns, he's saying. For word of gifted folk." He was watching me as he said it. I noted that he was watching me, and I filed the note in the drawer where I kept everything I refused to think about.

"Fifty crowns buys a lot of firewood," I said lightly.

"It buys a lot of things," Tam said.

"Yes," I said. "It does." And I gave him bread and sent him to check the lower wards, and told myself the brightness in his face was only the cold, and the hunger in it was only breakfast.

Nineteen days. I counted wrong.
