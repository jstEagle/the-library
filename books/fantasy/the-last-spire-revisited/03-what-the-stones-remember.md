## 3. What the Stones Remember

The thing nobody tells you about being an apprentice is that you spend most of your life carrying things for people who could carry them themselves.

I want to be clear about that up front, because the stories they tell in taverns make it sound like apprenticeship is a road to mastery. You sweep the floor of a wizard's tower, you peek at his grimoires when he's asleep, and twenty years later you're turning lead into gold. What actually happens is you sweep the floor of a gatekeeper's house at the top of the world where nothing grows but regret and lichen, and you carry water up two hundred steps twice a day because Wendell Kavanagh says the well water tastes better than the cistern water, which is a lie, it's all the same water, she just likes watching me climb.

My name is Tam. I'm seventeen, I can read three languages, and last winter I ate pine bark soup for eleven days straight because the smuggler convoy was late and Wendell said — this is a direct quote — "Hunger builds character."

She said it while eating the last of the smoked goat, mind you. Her character must be magnificent by now.

* * *

You'd think living with a woman for six years would teach you everything about her. It taught me everything except the one thing that mattered.

I knew how she took her tea (bitter enough to strip paint). I knew she hummed when she oiled the gate hinges, old Thornfell mining songs that predated the free cities. I knew she talked to the mountain like it was listening, which I always assumed was just what happened to people who lived alone too long. I knew her ledger hand was beautiful and her temper was short and her generosity was real but always came wrapped in an insult.

What I didn't know was why, on certain nights, she went out to the gate alone.

I followed her once. Third night of the autumn moon, cold enough that my breath froze on my scarf. She stood before the sealed doors of the Last Spire — black stone shot through with veins of something that glimmered like frost — and she pressed both palms flat against them, and she *listened*. That's the only word for it. Eyes closed, head tilted, the way you listen for a sound under a floor.

And then the door moved.

Not opened. The Spire hasn't opened in a hundred years, not since the kingdom beyond went hollow. But the great seal shifted, maybe a finger's width, groaning like a ship in ice, and the light around its edges bent — actually bent, like heat shimmer — and Wendell made a small sound, low in her throat, and pressed harder, and the door settled back into place as if ashamed of itself.

Then she knelt and touched the threshold stones and stayed there so long I lost feeling in my own feet waiting behind a boulder.

When she came back inside, cheeks flushed, eyes bright in a way I'd never seen them, I asked her what was wrong with the door.

"Nothing's wrong with the door," she said. "Go to bed."

"The whole mountain shook. I heard it from—"

"You heard wind. Go to bed, Tam."

I went to bed. And I lay there in the dark doing arithmetic, because that's what I do instead of sleeping. Six years of swept floors and carried water. Six years of "when you're ready" and "not yet" and "the gift isn't given, boy, it's grown." Six years of her telling me I had no aptitude while she held conversations with solid rock through her bare hands.

She had lied to me. Or worse — she'd told me the truth in a way designed to keep me small, because a keeper needs someone to carry the water, and a keeper who tells her apprentice the truth might end up carrying it herself.

That's what I believed that night. Envy is very good at building comfortable furniture out of rotten wood. I sat in it for months.

* * *

The crown rider came down from the high pass on a gray morning in late spring, and I want to tell you I didn't know what he was. I want to tell you I thought he was a tax assessor, or a surveyor, or anything other than what he obviously was: a man in royal blue with a sword too fine for the mountains and a purse too heavy for his belt.

His name was Ser Corwin Hale. I learned it later, from the proclamation nailed to the Ashmarket post. At the time he was just "sir," because I was raised polite even if I wasn't raised rich.

He stopped at our gatehouse on his way down from surveying the pass, and Wendell gave him tea bitter enough to dissolve his teeth and answered his questions about traffic through the mountains in her flattest voice. He asked about smugglers. She said she kept records of routes in season, as required, and produced her ledger, and he skimmed it with the bored expression of a man who already knew everything in it and was looking for what wasn't.

I poured his second cup. My hands were steady. I remember being proud of that.

"The crown pays informants now," he said, apropos of nothing, to the room in general, to the tea in particular. "For word of the gifted. Silver, and a lot of it. The king's taken an interest." He smiled at Wendell over the rim of his cup. "Dangerous times for people who keep secrets."

"We're mountain folk," Wendell said. "We don't have secrets. We have weather."

He laughed like she'd said something charming, thanked her for the hospitality, and rode south toward the free cities.

That should have been the end of it. If it had been the end of it, I'd be a different person writing this, or possibly no person at all, just a name in somebody else's ledger. But he left something behind. Not deliberately. A scrap of paper, dropped near the fire, half-burned. The crown's seal on one corner, and on the rest, printed in neat official type:

*By order of His Majesty: any person found possessing unregistered gifts of body, mind, or craft shall be taken into custody for the realm's protection. Reward for true information: fifty crowns silver.*

Fifty crowns.

I did the arithmetic again. Fifty crowns was four years of the stipend Wendell paid me, which was generous by gatekeeper standards and starvation wages by any honest measure. Fifty crowns was a horse. Fifty crowns was passage off this mountain, down to Ashmarket, where there were theaters and universities and girls who hadn't watched me eat pine bark soup. Fifty crowns was a life that wasn't this life, this frozen ledge at the edge of the map where I'd been promised everything and handed a yoke.

Wendell came in from checking the wards and found me holding the paper.

"Burn it," she said, and took it, and fed it to the fire herself. "Paper's dangerous. It makes promises."

"Fifty crowns," I said. I meant it as a joke. It came out sounding like a question.

She looked at me for a long moment. Wendell has gray eyes, the color of the granite she loves, and when she looks at you properly it's like being weighed on a merchant's scale.

"If you're ever tempted," she said quietly, "come to me first. Whatever the sum, I'll match it, and we'll call it tuition."

That should have saved me. It nearly did. But she said it the way she said everything — as though she were doing me a favor, as though my loyalty were a line item in her ledger, as though six years of my life were a debt I owed rather than a life I'd given. And the envy that had been building its rotten furniture all winter leaned back comfortably and whispered: *she thinks she can buy you. She thinks that's all you are. Something to be bought.*

I hate that whisper. I want to state that clearly, here, now, wherever this ends up. I hated it then and I hate it now. But hating a whisper doesn't stop you from obeying it. Sometimes it's the opposite.

* * *

Ser Corwin Hale was easy to find. The crown riders lodged at the Waystone inn at the mountain's foot whenever they passed through, and I had legitimate business there — Wendell sent me monthly for salt and lamp oil, because apparently the Keeper of the Last Spire couldn't be bothered to shop for herself either.

He was drinking alone in the common room, and I sat down across from him before I could talk myself out of it, and I said, "About the reward."

Here is what I told myself as the words came out: *I'm not selling her. I'm protecting her. If the crown learns about her from me, from someone who cares about her, they'll be gentle. They'll register the gift instead of taking it. If they learn about her from someone else — some smuggler, some stranger — they'll come with ropes.* I believed it the way you believe a rope will hold when you're falling. Desperately, and without evidence, and mostly because the alternative is looking down.

I told him the Keeper of the Last Spire had a gift of stone-sense. I told him I'd seen her open the sealed gate — not open, I corrected, shift it, a finger's width, but the ward-light bent, sir, I swear it bent. I told him she hid it from everyone, even the crown, even the ledger, and that a woman who could move a hundred-year seal with her hands was worth more than fifty crowns to whoever held her leash.

He bought me a drink while I talked. That's the detail that comes back to me at night. He listened with real attention, nodded, and pushed the cup toward me like we were friends, like I'd done him a kindness, and when I finished he counted out the silver right there on the table — fifty crowns, exact, as if he'd been carrying it for me specifically, as if the crown had minted it knowing my face.

"There's a bonus structure," he said, "for information leading to capture. Another hundred if she doesn't run."

"I don't—" My voice cracked. "I'm not selling her. This is so she'll be treated fairly. She's — she's the best person I know."

Something flickered across his face. Pity, maybe. Or indigestion. Hard to say with career soldiers.

"They usually are," he said. "The gifted. Best people I know, most of them." He gathered his coins. "The king has his reasons, lad. Get home safe."

I walked back up the mountain with fifty crowns of silver sewn into my coat lining, and every step of those two hundred stairs to the gatehouse felt like climbing out of my own grave.

* * *

They came nine days later. Twelve soldiers and Ser Hale, riding hard, and I heard the hoofbeats from the watchtower and knew — knew with my whole body, the way you know a storm — what they'd come for.

Wendell was at the well. Of course she was. She was filling the big jug because I'd been slow about it that morning, and when I came running down shouting their names, she looked up at me over the bucket's rim, and I watched her understand. All of it. In about a heartbeat. The riders, the timing, the way I couldn't meet her eyes.

"Tam," she said. Not angry. Worse than angry. *Gentle.* "Get inside."

"Wendell—"

"Inside."

I went inside. I stood at the window with my forehead against the cold glass like a child, and I watched twelve men take the strongest person I have ever known out of her own gatehouse with chains they didn't even need, because she went quietly, because she saw Rhea Voss's masts down in the valley and chose not to start a war in front of witnesses, because that's who she was — someone who calculated the cost of everything, including her own freedom, and always paid the price that hurt her least and protected others most.

Before they marched her out, she looked up at my window. Just once. Just long enough.

I don't know what I expected to see. Hatred, maybe. Something I could deserve.

What I saw was worry. She was worried about *me*, standing there in chains, worried I'd do something stupid, worried about what this would make of me. And then the soldiers closed around her and the column started moving, down the switchbacks, south, toward the capital, and I watched until the trees took her.

* * *

I stayed on the mountain for three days. Someone had to feed the goats; that was the lie I used. The truth is I couldn't leave, because leaving meant admitting what I'd done, and staying meant I could pretend I was keeping the gate, honoring her post, being loyal in the ways that mattered.

On the third day I climbed to the Spire itself. The sealed gate stood silent in its frame of black stone, exactly as it had stood for a hundred years, and I put my hands against it the way I'd seen her do, and I waited for it to speak to me.

Nothing. Cold stone, and beneath the cold stone, nothing at all.

That was when I finally understood the shape of what I'd thrown away. Not the money — the silver was still sewn in my coat, heavier than sin and worth less than dirt. Not even the betrayal itself. What I'd thrown away was the only person in the world who had ever looked at a scrawny orphan boy stealing bread at the Ashmarket docks and said, "You'll do. Can you carry a bucket?" — and meant, hidden under all that flint, *you belong to something now.*

She'd never told me about her gift because telling me would have made me responsible for it, and she was trying to keep me safe. Every secret was a wall she built around me. I'd spent six years cursing the walls and calling them cages.

The mountain rumbled. Deep down, far below, a tremor rolled through the rock like a sigh, like grief, like something vast shifting in its sleep — and I felt it through my boots and my palms and my teeth, plain as speech, and I knew it wasn't meant for me. It was meant for her. Everything in this mountain spoke to her. I was just standing in the room.

I went down to the valley the next morning. I didn't go to the capital — I wasn't brave enough for that, not yet, though the not-yet was already curdling into something with edges. I went to the Waystone inn, where the gossip ran thick, and I bought a meal with crown silver, and I listened.

They said the prisoner column would take the southern road through the Thornfell foothills. They said it traveled slow, burdened. They said there were others in it — more of the gifted, caught in the net since the proclamation, a dozen at least, chained and marching.

A dozen. Chained. Heading for whatever the crown does to people whose only crime is being born with something extra.

I sat in that smoky common room with fifty crowns of blood money in my coat and a guilt the size of the Thornfells sitting on my chest, and I understood that following that column wouldn't fix anything. Nothing fixes this. There is no version of events, going forward, in which I come out clean.

But there is a version where I follow anyway. Down the southern road, past the tree line, out of the mountains I was born under and into whatever comes next. Carrying things, the way I've always done. Only now the heaviest thing I carry is mine by right.

The column left at dawn. So did I.
