## 4. The Hunt Begins

The trouble with living alone on a mountain for eleven years is that you start believing the mountain will warn you before anything else does.

I'd felt the tremor at dawn—a deep, grinding unease in the bedrock, like an old man shifting in a chair that no longer fits him. I'd logged it. I'd checked the wards twice. I'd eaten breakfast. And then I'd gone out to the gatehouse to argue with a smuggler about tariffs, because the end of the world, as it turns out, keeps office hours.

"Three coppers a crate," Rhea Voss said, arms crossed against the cold. "Four if you keep smiling."

"I don't smile for coppers."

"No," she agreed. "You really don't." Her grin was all teeth and salt-wind, the same grin she'd worn at fifteen when we'd first met—her running contraband through the Thornfell passes, me pretending not to see. Eleven years of transactional friendship had worn smooth grooves between us, comfortable as a cart track. She brought me news, whiskey, and occasionally decent tea. I brought her blind spots in my ledgers and the occasional warning about rockfall. Neither of us called it love. We called it fair.

"The proclamation's real, Wendell." All the grin went out of her at once, which was how I knew it was bad. Rhea's face was a ledger too, and she kept it balanced. "Posted in every market from Greyhollow to the coast. The crown's hunting the gifted. Silver for informants. Silver enough that even honest people will lie."

"People aren't honest, Rhea. That's why the silver works."

"You're not listening."

"I'm always listening. It's my least favorite quality." I tapped the ledger against my thigh. "What exactly does the proclamation say? Word for word. I want it precise."

"'All persons harboring or concealing the gift of unmaking, of healing, of storm-calling, of stone-sense—'" she recited, watching me carefully as she said it, "'—are declared enemies of the realm. Shelter them and be hanged with them. Reveal them and be paid.'" She paused. "Stone-sense, Wendell. They named yours specifically. Do you know how specific you have to be to get named?"

"They've been looking for a long time." The words came out flat. I made them come out flat. "Which tells me they haven't found what they're looking for yet, or there'd be no need for proclamations. There'd just be silence, and a new tax."

"And if they're looking *here*?"

"Then they'll find a gatekeeper who inspects seals, records weather, and drinks more than is strictly dignified. Nothing else."

Rhea looked at me for a long moment. Behind her, her crew was loading crates into the pass road's shadow—salt, iron nails, a few things I didn't ask about, because asking would make me complicit and not-asking was the arrangement. Somewhere above us, a hawk turned circles against the grey.

"When do you next run south?" I asked.

"Six days. Why?"

"Because I'm going to give you a letter for the archivist at Greyhollow, and you're going to lose it somewhere between here and there, and he's going to find it in his coat pocket and have no idea how it got there."

"That's a lot of steps for one letter."

"It's a lot of years for one friendship. We've managed." I wrote the letter that evening—coded, of course, in the old gatekeeper's cipher—and gave it to her at dawn with instructions to lose it gracefully. She took it, saluted me with two fingers, and said something I'd think about later, much later, when there was nothing left to do but think:

"You know I'd sell you out for my crew, right? If it came to that. You know that."

"I know," I said. "It's why I trust you. Sentiment rots. Self-interest holds its shape."

She laughed and sailed off down the pass with forty crates of other people's secrets, and I went back to my ledgers, and I did not feel the soldiers coming up the mountain road, because I wasn't listening for men.

I was only ever listening for stone.

* * *

They came three days later. Not up the smugglers' road, which would have been sensible, but over the ridge from the north—the hard way, the way nobody went because the Thornfell didn't forgive shortcuts. Which meant either they were very good or very desperate, and when I counted twenty-two armed men cresting the treeline in crown grey, I decided both.

Tam saw them first. He came pelting into the gatehouse with his eyes wide and his breath ragged, and for one stupid, warm moment I thought: *wolves*. Wolves I could handle. Wolves were honest about wanting to eat you.

"Crown riders," he gasped. "Twenty of them, maybe more, coming down the north ridge. Captain says—they say they have a writ. They're calling it a writ of seizure."

"A writ for what?"

He wouldn't look at me. That should have been my second clue. My first clue had been the tremor; my second should have been Tam's inability to meet my eyes; my third was that the soldiers hadn't taken the easy road, which meant someone had told them exactly where to find me and exactly where I wouldn't be looking.

But grief makes you slow, and pride makes you slower, and so I simply said, "Well. Then let's go hear what the crown wants," and walked out to meet them with my ledger under my arm like a shield, which is the most gatekeeper thing I have ever done.

Their captain was a woman named Brannoch—I learned the name later, from the trial records I never got to read—and she was the kind of soldier who'd been promised this promotion by someone else's suffering and knew it. She read the writ aloud at the gatehouse door while her men fanned out around the yard with the practiced looseness of people who'd done this before, in other villages, to other names on other lists.

"Wendell Kavanagh, Keeper of the Last Spire's gate, is hereby seized under authority of the crown for the crime of concealed gift. She is to be conveyed to the capital for assessment and judgment."

"Assessment," I repeated. "That's a gentle word."

"It's the word I was given."

"Well, then. Give the word back and tell them I decline. The gate requires a keeper. That's rather the point of me."

"The gate," Captain Brannoch said, not unkindly, "requires a keeper until it doesn't. Move."

Two soldiers took my arms. I could have fought—I want that noted somewhere, by someone, that in the privacy of my own skull I fought magnificently—but there were twenty-two of them and one of me and Tam standing white-faced in the doorway behind, and the arithmetic of heroism has never once worked out in my favor. So I stood very still and felt, through my palms where they pressed against nothing, through the soles of my boots, through every stone in the yard: *the Spire's wards flicker when I am afraid.*

That was new. That was eleven years of careful anonymity cracking open like river ice, and beneath it the black water of everything I'd never let myself know: that the wards answered me. That they had always answered me. That I was not the keeper of the gate so much as the gate's keeper, and those were different jobs, and I had only just now been promoted without being asked.

"Hold still," one of the soldiers muttered, fastening the manacles. Cold iron, old design. Designed, I realized with a lurch, for people like me. Someone in the capital had been making these for a long time. "It's nothing personal, keeper."

"Nothing ever is," I said. "That's what makes it unforgivable."

* * *

Rhea Voss's ship was in the harbor below the pass—I could feel it, absurdly, the way you can feel a familiar footstep in a dark house. The *Salt Wren*, riding at anchor with her crew aboard and her captain on the quay, because Rhea had heard, of course she'd heard, because Rhea heard everything on that coast before the gulls did.

They marched me down through the pass, and the mountain grieved around me the whole way.

I don't know how else to describe it. Stone-sense isn't hearing, exactly, and it isn't feeling, exactly—it's knowing what the stone knows, and the stone knows everything that has ever leaned against it, fallen upon it, bled into it. And the Thornfell knew me. Eleven years of my footsteps were written into its passes. Every patrol, every smuggling run I'd pretended not to see, every midnight walk to check the wards, all of it recorded in quartz and granite like ink in a ledger that would outlast us all. And as the soldiers marched me down through it, the stone *grieved*, a low tectonic sorrow, the mountain mourning the loss of its keeper the way a house mourns the loss of its owner—not usefully, not helpfully, just endlessly, just sadly, the way stone does everything.

By the time we reached the quay my head was ringing with it. I staggered. A soldier caught my elbow, almost kindly.

"She's sick," he said to Brannoch. "Sea air doesn't agree with her."

"The sea agrees with everyone," Brannoch said. "It's the chains that disagree."

And there on the quay, arms crossed, expression perfectly balanced between apology and arithmetic, stood Rhea Voss.

We looked at each other across forty feet of wet stone and twenty-two armed men and eleven years of fair dealing, and I watched her do the sum. I watched her weigh my life against her crew's—forty souls aboard the *Wren*, every one of them known to the crown's conscription lists now, every one of them hangable under the same proclamation that named me. I watched her arrive at the answer we'd both always known was coming, the answer we'd built our whole friendship to avoid ever having to speak aloud.

"Wendell," she said.

"Rhea."

"You look terrible."

"I've been told the sea disagrees with me." My voice held. I want that noted too. Whatever else failed that day, my voice held. "Your crew—"

"Are aboard. Are safe. Are staying that way." She said it steadily, but her hands were fists at her sides, and Rhea Voss's hands were never fists unless she was paying for something. "There was nothing I could do. Twenty-two men, Wendell. What was I going to do, die theatrically?"

"It's generally considered a classic option."

"Not one I'm shopping for today." She stepped closer, close enough that the soldiers tensed, close enough that I could smell salt and tar and the particular brand of cheap brandy she'd drunk since we were girls. Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "There's a column leaving for the capital inside the week. Prisoners move slow. Roads through Ashmarket are broken—burned last spring, half a city of rubble and ghosts, and no garrison worth the name. If you get loose anywhere, get loose *there*."

"I'm not planning to get loose anywhere. I'm planning to stand very still and be unjustly assessed."

"Wendell."

"Rhea."

"*Ashmarket.*" Her eyes were bright and furious and wet, and she blinked it all away with the efficiency of long practice. "You stubborn, self-sufficient, insufferable woman. You never needed anyone your whole life. Look what it got you."

"It got me eleven quiet years and excellent tea. Most people do worse."

She stepped back. The moment closed over like water. And I understood, standing there in the chains with the mountain grieving behind me and the sea waiting in front of me, that I had finally found the exact weight of my own life—that it weighed less than forty crewmen, less than one ship's safety, less than the space between Rhea's loyalty and her survival. It was not a crushing discovery. It was worse than that. It was a *measuring* discovery. I had spent eleven years believing myself self-sufficient, and here at the end of it, the ledger balanced against me: one keeper, seized, expendable, mourned by stone and abandoned by salt.

"Goodbye, Rhea."

"Don't say goodbye." She was already turning away. "Say 'see you in Ashmarket,' you superstitious bastard."

"See you in Ashmarket," I said, because she asked, and because I have always been weak about giving people the last word.

They put me on a wagon. The mountain's grief followed me for six miles and then faded, the way all voices fade when you travel far enough from the source, and I sat in the rattling dark of the canvas-covered cart with my manacles and my thoughts and the growing, horrible understanding that for the first time since I was nineteen years old, no one was coming.

Not the smugglers. Not the mountain. Not Tam, whom I'd left standing in the gatehouse doorway with his guilt already written across him in letters large enough to read at a distance, though I hadn't let myself read them yet. No one. The free cities had their walls, the crown had its hunt, and I had a wagon seat and a view of the road south.

Self-sufficiency, it turns out, is just loneliness with better marketing. I'd been selling it to myself for eleven years, and the market had finally crashed.

I put my head back against the wagon's frame and listened, one last time, for the stone beneath the road. It murmured its small, indifferent knowledge—wheel-ruts and rainwater, the bones of the hills rolling away southward—and somewhere in that murmur, faint as a coin dropped in another room, I felt something I had never felt before.

Another listener. Far to the south. Faint, and frightened, and gifted.

Someone else had heard the proclamation too.

* * *

The column assembled at dawn two days later, and it was worse than I'd imagined, because imagination had failed to supply the children.

There were eleven of us chained in the wagons by the time the wheels started turning: a storm-caller from the coast whose hands shook constantly now, as if lightning had gotten into her blood and couldn't get out; a hedge-healer from the hill country who hummed to herself and would not stop; twin sisters who shared a gift I never learned the name of, who communicated entirely in glances; and a boy of perhaps fourteen, farm-thin, sunburnt, whose gift I also never learned, because the soldiers shot him on the second day when it flared wild in his sleep and set the wagon canvas smoldering.

I won't describe it. Some ledgers are better left with blank pages.

But I will tell you what I did, which was nothing, which was sit very still with my manacles clanking while they kicked the boy's body off the road and told us all it was mercy, quick at least, and better than the alternative. And I will tell you what happened inside me, which was that the last soft thing in my chest—the part that had believed if I just stayed small enough, quiet enough, useful enough, the world would leave me alone to my ledgers and my tea—finally died, quietly, without ceremony, somewhere on a rutted road south of the Thornfell.

In its place, something harder set like poured stone.

*Stop running,* I thought, and it wasn't a thought, really, it was a vow, and vows are just promises that have found their spine. *Stop hiding. Stop being small. You have spent eleven years keeping a gate, Wendell Kavanagh. Time to find out what the gate was keeping.*

That night, the healer—the humming one, whose name was Sefa—worked her manacled hands through the bars until our little fingers touched, and I felt her gift move into me like warmth into cold rooms. Not much. Just enough. Enough to take the edge off the bruising, enough to quiet the shaking in the storm-caller's hands three wagons down, enough to remind all of us, in one small stolen moment, that power shared is power doubled, and the crown had chained together the only army it should ever have feared.

"Keep that," Sefa whispered, nodding at the warmth still lingering between us. "They can chain the gift. They can't chain what it touches."

Ahead of us, the road bent south toward the burned ruins of Ashmarket, and beyond it, the capital, where something wearing a king was waiting to assess me.

Behind us, the Thornfell kept its grief to itself, and the Last Spire's gate stood sealed, and empty, and mine.

For now.
