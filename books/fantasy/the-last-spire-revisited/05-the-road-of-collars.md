## 5. The Road of Collars

There is a particular sound a chain makes when it settles against your wrists for the first time. Not the clink—that comes later, once you've stopped noticing it and started hearing it again in the small hours. The first sound is heavier than that. It's the sound of a decision being finished by other people.

I had been on the road out of the Thornfell for two days before I heard it properly, because for two days I had been too busy cataloguing everything I'd lost to notice what I was wearing. My gate-keys, gone into a sergeant's pocket. My ledger—my father's ledger, and his father's before him—left open on the gatehouse desk like an accusation or a eulogy. Tam's face at the window as they marched me past, doing a very good impression of a boy who had nothing to do with any of it.

And Rhea Voss's ship still sitting in the harbor below, sails furled, her crew on deck watching me go without one hand raised to so much as wave goodbye.

I'd have waved, if my hands had been free. Something obscene, probably. It seemed fitting.

* * *

The prisoner column was eleven strong when they pulled me into it, and we were fourteen by the time we reached the lowlands, because the crown's hunt had been busy while I'd been keeping my gate. They marched us in pairs, chained ankle to ankle, with soldiers ahead and behind and a wagon rattling along at the rear to collect anyone who fell. I did not ask what happened to anyone who fell. The empty space at the back of the wagon told its own story.

My partner was a woman perhaps ten years my senior, gray coming in at her temples, with the calloused hands of someone who had worked a trade rather than a talent. She walked with her chin up and her breathing even, which after two days of mountain switchbacks marked her as either very tough or very stupid, and I was beginning to suspect both.

"You're the gatekeeper," she said, on the third morning. Not a question. News travels faster than prisoners.

"Was."

"Wendell Kavanagh." She said it the way you'd read a name off a monument. "They were singing about you in the camp before they moved us. *The Keeper of the Last Door.* Very dramatic. The soldiers hate it."

"The soldiers can join the club."

That earned me something that wasn't quite a smile but lived in the same neighborhood. "Sefa," she said. "Healer. Or I was, until healing became treason."

"It has a way of doing that lately."

"Mm. I set a broken leg in Millbrook. The boy's mother paid me in bread. The boy's uncle paid the crown in silver." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "So now I walk east in irons instead of west to my own bed. And the leg healed beautifully, if you're wondering. I do good work. It's the gratitude that's inconsistent."

I liked her immediately, in the way you like anything that reminds you the world outside your chains is still made of people who complain about bread. But liking her didn't stop me from doing what I'd done for thirty years, which was keeping things close. So when she glanced down at my wrists—when she looked at them a beat too long, the way you look at a door you suspect is locked—I said nothing about the stone-sense humming faintly under my skin, reading the road through the soles of my boots. Granite underfoot here, old and tired, shot through with quartz veins like scars. It murmured. It always murmured. I just hadn't told it anything worth overhearing.

Sefa, however, was not a woman who required telling.

"You feel it too," she said quietly, that evening, as we sat in the ditch where they let us rest. "The road."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Wendell." She said my name like a key turning. "I've been walking beside you for three days. You flinch every time the column crosses a streambed. You lean toward the cliff face like a woman listening at a wall. And this morning, when the rockslide blocked the pass—" She paused. "You knew it would fall before it fell. You stepped left. Everyone else stepped wrong."

Damn the quartz veins. Damn the chatty, gossiping stone. Thirty years of discipline, undone by geology.

"They'll kill you for saying that," I said. "Louder."

"They'll kill us anyway. That's rather the point of the exercise." She rubbed her chained wrists absently, the way you'd rub an old scar. "I'm not asking you to perform, Keeper. I'm asking you to know that you're not the only one in these collars who hears things."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about power: it gets lonely, and then it gets quiet, and then one day you realize you built the loneliness yourself, stone by stone, because you thought it was safety.

I had spent my whole life believing that my gift was safest unused. Small magic, hidden magic—the gatekeeper's creed. Feel the mountain, never speak to it. Read the ward-stones, never feed them. A gift that no one sees is a gift no one can report, and a gift no one reports keeps its keeper alive. I had held to it through famine years and border wars and Rhea Voss's increasingly inventive smuggling schemes, and I had congratulated myself on my wisdom every single time the Spire stood another winter.

And now here I was in irons anyway, marched east by men who'd bought my name from a child, and the only difference between me and Sefa was that she'd used her gift to mend a boy's leg and I'd used mine to count smugglers' wagons. The crown didn't care what you did with it. The crown cared that you *had* it.

Which meant my entire philosophy had been not caution but superstition. A charm against a storm that came through the walls anyway.

I lay awake that night on frozen ground, listening to the granite mutter beneath me, and for the first time in thirty years I considered answering back.

* * *

It was Sefa who taught me, though teaching is too generous a word for what passed between two women who could barely move their hands.

"There's a trick to it," she said on the fifth day, as the mountains gave way to foothills and the foothills to farmland that looked recently and thoroughly robbed. "When I heal, everyone thinks the gift flows one way. Into the patient. Out of me. Like pouring from a jug." We walked, we talked, we kept our voices under the rattle of the chains. "But that's not how it works. The body I'm mending answers back. It knows how it ought to be. I don't impose the pattern—I listen for it, and I lend it strength to remember itself. Power shared, Keeper. Never spent. Shared. And shared power is doubled, because the body wants to heal far more than I want it to."

"And if the body doesn't want to?"

Her jaw tightened. "Then I learn what grief is for." She said nothing more about it for a mile, and when she spoke again it was lighter, deliberately so. "Your stones are the same. You think you read them. But I'd wager they read you right back, and I'd wager further that you've never once asked them for help, because asking means admitting you need it."

"You've known me five days."

"I've known stubborn keepers my whole life. You all have the same face. It's the face of someone carrying water in cupped hands and refusing to use a bucket because buckets can be stolen." She glanced at my manacles. "Look how well the cupping went."

I wanted to argue. I genuinely did. There was a whole speech in me about the virtues of silence, about the hundred years of keepers who'd survived precisely because they kept their gifts folded small and their mouths shut tighter. But the speech died somewhere around the point where I noticed that survival had gotten me exactly here: chained to a stranger on a slave road, my gate unmanned, my ledger abandoned, my apprentice silent in my wake.

The bucket, as it turned out, could be stolen regardless. All the cupped hands had done was make sure I'd die thirsty.

"What would I even ask them?" I said finally. It was the closest thing to surrender I'd offered anyone in thirty years, and Sefa took it as gently as she'd have taken a splinter.

"Start small. Tonight, when we rest. Ask the ground to tell you where the hard places are, so you can sleep on soft ones." A pause. "Ask it to hold you. Stones like being useful. Nobody ever asks them for anything except weight-bearing."

That night, lying in the ditch with frost forming on my eyelashes, I pressed my palm flat against the earth and did the most frightening thing I had done since the soldiers came over the ridge at the Spire.

*I'm here,* I said to the ground, in the wordless way of the gift. *What do you need?*

The answer came back like a tide. Not words—stone doesn't do words—but a slow, deep attention, the feeling of being regarded by something older than kingdoms. And underneath the attention, unmistakable despite my rustiness: welcome. The ground under the Thornfell had always felt like a duty. This anonymous lowland dirt, this nameless field with no wards and no history, simply *held* me, the way Sefa said bodies want to heal, the way stone wants to bear weight.

I slept four hours without dreaming, which for me was a miracle on the order of loaves and fishes.

* * *

The boy's name was Pell, and he was maybe fifteen, and he had the kind of gift that couldn't hide.

We saw it on the eighth day, in a market town whose name I never learned. The column had halted in the square while the captain argued with the town reeve about provisions, and Pell—chained beside a miller's daughter with a touch of weather-sense—had begun to shiver. Not fear-shivering. Gift-shivering. I knew it intimately; I'd spent a lifetime strangling my own. The air around him thickened. Frost feathered across his collar. The puddle at his feet skinned over with ice, crackling softly in the noon warmth, and the girl beside him whispered *stop, stop, stop* in a voice that carried further than she wanted.

The soldiers noticed the way soldiers notice anything: loudly, and with enthusiasm.

What followed took less time than the writing of this sentence takes to read. A sergeant crossed the square. Pell raised his bare, blue-lit hands—not to fight, I will swear this until I die, he raised them the way children raise hands in schoolrooms, the way you raise hands when you want to be *seen*, when you want someone to say *it's alright, we can fix this*—

The sergeant's blade came down and the light went out and the frost stopped spreading mid-feather, hanging on the boy's collar like lace on a corpse, which is exactly what it now adorned.

Nobody in the square moved. The captain finished his argument with the reeve about barley. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

And I stood there in my chains and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade between my own ribs, that I had watched this coming for eight days and done nothing. That I had learned Sefa's lesson, touched the earth, asked it to hold me—and when it came time to spend anything at all on someone else, I had cupped my hands and kept walking. Pell had flared because he was young and frightened and his gift was too big for hiding. But part of me—a cold, honest part—knew that if I had reached out through my boots into that square's foundations, if I had cracked the cobbles under the sergeant's feet, stumbled the horses, bought the boy ten confused seconds—

Ten seconds wouldn't have saved him. Probably. Almost certainly.

I am aware of how it sounds. I have had eight days and then some years to rehearse the excuse, and it still curdles in my mouth, because the truth isn't that ten seconds wouldn't have saved him. The truth is that I never tried, and *probably* is the coin cowards pay themselves in, and I was rich in it.

Sefa's hand found mine in the chains, cold fingers lacing through colder ones. She didn't say *it wasn't your fault.* She was too honest for comfort and too kind for cruelty, so she said nothing at all, and her silence was the worst sermon I ever sat through.

That night, in a walled stockade that stank of wet straw and older despair, I found her sitting against the fence post with our fellow captives gathered close around her—the miller's girl, a hedge-witch with shaking hands, a ferryman who could taste storms coming, all of them leaning toward her like plants toward light.

She was teaching them. In whispers, in the dark, under the noses of bored sentries: how to breathe through a flare, how to fold a gift small without strangling it, how to steady each other. The miller's girl had her palms out and Sefa's hands over them, and between their joined fingers there was a faint warmth visible in the cold air, passing back and forth, back and forth, like a coin that grew heavier each time it changed hands.

Power shared. Never spent. Doubled.

I stood at the edge of that little circle for a long moment, feeling like the ghost at my own funeral, until Sefa looked up and saw me. She didn't smile. She just shifted, making room against the fence post, and the others made room too, and the ferryman—big man, beard full of straw—grunted at me like I'd been expected for days.

"I watched them kill him," I said. My voice came out lower than I intended. "In the square. I stood there."

"Yes," Sefa said. No judgment in it. Just the fact, laid out between us like a bone to be set.

"I keep things safe. That's all I've ever done. One gate, one mountain, one life folded small enough to survive." I looked at my hands, at the manacles, at the frost-scarred memory of a boy's collar. "And it turns out I was never keeping anything safe. I was just deciding whose safety mattered. Mine. Every time. Mine."

The hedge-witch stopped shaking long enough to look at me. The miller's girl closed her empty hands.

"So what now?" Sefa asked, and it wasn't a challenge. It was a door, held open, the way she'd taught me the earth holds.

I sat down among them. I put my palm flat against the frozen ground, and this time I didn't whisper. I opened the gift wide—wide as it had ever been, wider, wide enough that every captive in that circle felt the stone beneath the stockade wake and attend, felt the old hill the fort was cut into lean in close like a grandmother at a sickbed. I let them borrow it. I let it borrow them. The warmth passed around our circle, back and forth, growing.

"I'm done running," I said, and heard thirty years of careful silence break apart like ice on a spring river. "I'm done folding small. Whatever's waiting for us at the end of this road—they mean to parade us, cage us, bleed us for what we are. Fine. Let them gather us together. Let them bring all their gifted enemies to one place, chained and furious and—for the first time in any of our lives—together."

Sefa's eyes caught the faint light rising off our joined hands. "You have a plan."

"I have a gate," I said. "And I am going to get back to it."

* * *

Two mornings later, on a stretch of road where the hills pinched close and the pines crowded dark to the verges, the column's rhythm broke.

Not gradually. All at once—the way a rockslide breaks, the way a gate breaks, the way every certainty I'd ever owned had broken since the crown's riders came over my ridge. Horses screaming somewhere ahead. Arrows out of the tree line, fast and economical. Soldiers shouting orders that collapsed halfway into shouts.

Through my boot-soles, through my shackles, through the raw and newly-opened channel Sefa had helped me widen, I felt it coming before any eye saw it: a company of riders moving off the high track, heavy with iron and purpose, cutting the road ahead of us like a blade through bread.

And leading them, riding a gray horse like he'd been carved out of the same winter as the mountains behind us, a man with an exiled prince's posture and a rebel's beard and eyes that swept the prisoner column once—quickly, professionally, looking for weapons—

—and stopped on me, because whatever he was looking for, I was clearly not it.

"Well," I said, to no one, to the stone, to the whole ruined year. "You'd better be worth the chains."
