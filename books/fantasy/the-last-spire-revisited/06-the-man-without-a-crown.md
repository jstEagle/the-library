## 6. The Man Without a Crown

I had been a prince for eleven years, an exile for ten, and a bandit for nine, and I will tell you which of the three taught me the most: it was the banditry. Princes learn to speak. Exiles learn to listen. Bandits learn that everything you were told about how the world works was written by men who never had to dig their own latrines.

The latrine lesson is relevant here, believe it or not, because it's the reason I knew the prisoner column would stop at Witherstone Ford. Columns always stopped at fords. Soldiers, like princes, hated getting their boots wet, and like exiles, they'd learned to take their comforts where they could find them. So we waited in the alder scrub above the ford with forty rebels, two days of rain in our collars, and my brother's sigil scraped off every shield we owned.

"Remember," I said, for the fourth time, because repetition is all a leader really is. "We want the gifted alive. The guards are not the enemy—they're conscripts with bad boots and worse pay. Kill only if they make you."

Dara, my sergeant, a woman built like a door and twice as hard to get past, spat into the ferns. "And if they make *you*?"

"Then someone remind me of my principles. Loudly."

She almost smiled. Dara rationed her smiles the way the crown rationed bread, which is to say according to a formula nobody understood but everybody resented.

Below us, the column came down through the pines: sixty soldiers, twenty wagons, and somewhere among them—according to the fisherman who'd sold me the intelligence for the price of a warm coat—the first haul of the crown's great hunt. The gifted. My brother's new census of everyone in his kingdom who could do something the crown couldn't explain, which, knowing Edran's mind as it now was, meant everyone who could do anything at all.

Ten years ago I'd have said hunting the gifted was beneath even the thing wearing my brother's face. Ten years ago I still believed there was a floor to what it would do.

There wasn't. There never had been. I'd just been standing on it.

* * *

The ambush went the way ambushes go in the songs, if the songs were written by liars with good posture. Which is to say: it went badly, then it went well, then it went badly again, in that order, quickly.

Dara took the rearguard with a whistle and a wall of spears. Brenn dropped the lead wagon's team with a crossbow shot I'd have paid real money to see twice. The middle of the column—the wagons, the prisoners, the whole point of the morning—bunched up in the ford like sheep remembering, all at once, that they were sheep.

I rode into it because that's what the man without a crown does; he rides into the mess so no one else has to feel guilty about it later.

"Rebels of the Thornfell!" I shouted, because you have to shout something, and *good morning, we're robbing you for political reasons* lacked dignity. "Soldiers! Drop your arms and walk away! You have my word no one will chase you!"

Here is what nobody tells you about giving that speech: sometimes it works. Most times, though, the soldiers look at you, and then at their sergeants, and then at the gallows waiting behind them at home, and they decide that the devil they know has better dental.

They decided that. Sixty of them, more or less, all at once.

The next three minutes were noise. Not the clean, ringing noise of the tourney grounds where I'd won my spurs at nineteen and promptly vomited behind a tent from the nerves—real noise, wet and stupid and close. A spear glanced off my shoulder plate. Someone's horse screamed. I cut down a boy who couldn't have been older than Tam-of-nowhere, because I hadn't met him yet, and felt the specific shame of a man discovering that ten years of exile had not, in fact, made killing easier. It had only made it faster.

Then we had them. That was the ugly miracle of it. Dara's line held, the conscripts broke, and by the time the ford ran quiet we had thirty prisoners kneeling in the shallows with their hands laced behind their heads, and we had the wagon doors open, and I was about to meet the weapon that would win my war.

That's what I'd told my people, anyway. That's what I'd let myself believe. Somewhere in those wagons, surely, was a fire-caller or a storm-singer, someone whose gift could crack the capital's walls like an egg, someone I could point at my enemy and say *there, that's justice, that has a shape.*

The first wagon held three children and a woman who'd been crying so long she'd gone silent about it. The second held an old man who flinched when I touched his shoulder, and whose gift, it turned out, was making flowers grow slightly out of season. He apologized to me for this. I want that recorded. A prisoner of the crown apologized to me for insufficient horticulture.

The third wagon was locked from the outside with a black iron chain, which interested me, and stank of old blood, which interested me more.

"Well," I said to Brenn, who'd come up beside me with his crossbow held like a rosary. "Either this is our weapon, or it's the crown's lunch."

We cut the chain. I pulled the door open. And a voice out of the dark inside said:

"If you're crown men, I want you to know your general owes me four months' back pay."

Not a scream. Not begging. Bookkeeping.

I leaned in. The light through the door showed me a woman sitting against the far wall like she owned the deed to it—thirty-ish, road-worn, wrists raw from irons, dark hair going grey early at the temples in the way of people who've spent decades arguing with weather. She looked at me the way a gatekeeper looks at a stranger knocking after curfew: not afraid, exactly. Assessing. Deciding whether I was worth the effort of the bar.

"You're not crown men," she said, before I'd said a word. "Crown men don't hesitate in doorways. They think hesitation is a disease." She squinted. "You're the brother. The dead one. Aldric."

"The *exiled* one," I corrected. "Dead requires more initiative than the crown has shown lately."

"Hm." She stood, swayed, caught herself on the wagon frame, and refused—absolutely refused—to accept my arm. "Wendell Kavanagh," she said, as if the name should mean something to me. "Keeper of the Last Spire. And before you ask: no."

"No what?"

"No, I'm not your weapon. I can see you doing the arithmetic behind your eyes. Pretty face, poor poker game. Whatever you think I am, put it back on the shelf."

Brenn made a sound that in a politer man would have been a laugh.

I looked at her hands—calloused, stone-marked, the hands of someone who worked—and at her eyes, which were doing something strange, flicking sideways, downward, as if she were reading a book written on the ground. Later I would understand that she was listening to the riverbed. That morning I thought she was concussed.

"Keeper of the Spire," I repeated. "The sealed gate. The one thing between us and whatever's left of the hollow kingdom."

"Someone reads."

"I read everything they banned. It's the one advantage of treason—you get the uncensored edition." I stepped back to let her climb down. "You're coming with us, Keeper. All of you are. After that, we'll discuss what you are."

"We'll discuss nothing," she said, and stepped down past me without using the offered hand, landing in the mud like she'd been born to it, which possibly she had. "You'll discuss. I'll stand here being right."

I have commanded soldiers, argued with ambassadors, and once spent six hours negotiating a grain contract with a merchant who communicated primarily through sighs. None of it prepared me for Wendell Kavanagh.

* * *

Here is the part of the day I do not enjoy recounting, but honesty is the one luxury exile affords, so: the part where I nearly died, and was saved by the person I'd just decided was useless.

It happened during the withdrawal. We were moving the freed prisoners up the logging trail toward the tree-line, and the crown's cavalry—which had been conspicuously absent all morning, which should have worried me, and did worry me, but in the abstract way worries work when you're busy—came back around through the pine break. Twelve riders. Veyra's outriders, we learned later, flying no banner because Veyra considered banners a form of bragging.

One of them came straight at me while I was dragging a child clear of a wagon wheel. I heard the hoofbeats late. I turned, brought my blade up, got the angle wrong—I was tired, I was bleeding from a cut I hadn't noticed taking, and I hesitated, because the rider was another boy, eighteen at most, and some ancient stupid princely reflex in me still wanted to give him a chance to rein in.

He didn't rein in.

His lance came down and my strike faltered—not failed, faltered, there's a difference, and the difference was about half a second—and half a second is a very generous amount of time to die in.

What saved me wasn't skill. It was the ground. The whole logging trail shuddered, a single deep thump like the mountain clearing its throat, and the boy's horse stumbled mid-gallop, pitched forward, and threw him into the bracken hard enough to fold him politely around a stump. Unconscious. Alive. I checked, afterward. I always check, now.

I turned to find Wendell Kavanagh standing ten feet away with one bare hand pressed flat against the earth, her eyes shut, looking less like a sorceress than a physician taking a pulse.

"That was you," I said. Not a question. My voice had gone strange.

"That was the mountain," she said, opening her eyes. "I asked nicely. It's old. It doesn't like cavalry." She flexed her fingers like a woman shaking off cold water, and I saw—just for a moment—something flicker under her skin, a faint grey shimmer, there and gone. Then her face closed like a ledger. "Your horse is loose, by the way. Third pine on the left."

I retrieved my horse. I would like the record to show I walked with dignity.

* * *

We made the tree-line by dusk, and camped in a hollow the scouts called the Kettle because it trapped smoke and secrets equally well. Fires low. Watches set. Forty-odd rebels, thirty freed conscripts, and seventeen people the crown had chained in wagons for the crime of existing strangely.

I found Wendell at the edge of the camp, sitting on a fallen birch, watching the mountains go violet in the last light. She'd cleaned the wagon filth off herself in a stream and looked, if anything, more formidable for it. Clean, you could see the lines around her eyes, the ones carved by years of squinting at weather and worse.

I sat down on the other end of the log, because I'm a prince and princes are trained from birth to intrude gracefully.

"You saved my life," I said.

"The mountain saved your life. I translated." She didn't look at me. "You hesitated."

"I did."

"Why? He'd have run you through."

"He was sixteen. Maybe seventeen." I turned my sword-belt buckle over in my fingers. "When I was that age, my brother used to sneak me into the kitchens after the household slept, and we'd eat cold plum tarts and he'd tell me every secret he knew, which at the time was four." I shrugged. "Every soldier is somebody's little brother. Some days I remember it faster than others."

Silence. I'd expected mockery—I'd had a decade of mercenaries mocking exactly that speech—but she said nothing for a long moment, and when she spoke it was quieter.

"My apprentice sold me to the crown riders," she said. "Sixteen years old. I raised him from nothing. He needed coin, or he needed to matter, and I was too busy keeping secrets to notice which." Her jaw tightened. "So. We're both fools about children."

"I'm a fool about children, brothers, kingdoms, and myself. I like to be thorough."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. It might, under laboratory conditions, have been the ancestor of a smile.

"Ask me," she said abruptly.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been circling since the wagon. You've got the look of a man composing a question and rehearsing the follow-ups. Get it over with. The suspense is interfering with my sunset."

Fair. I gave her the direct version. "The Spire seals the hollow kingdom. Everyone agrees on that much. What I want to know is what the crown thinks *you* know that makes you worth chains instead of a headsman."

For the first time she turned and looked at me fully, and I understood that everything before had been the courtesy version of her attention. This was the real thing, and it was like being inventoried.

"You really don't know," she said. "About the crown itself."

"I know it's cursed. Old stories say forged too greedily, bound too deep. My brother changed the month he crowned himself, and I've spent ten years assuming grief did it."

"Grief didn't do it." Her hand drifted toward the ground again, an unconscious gesture, fingertips brushing the soil like touching base with an ally. "The crown isn't cursed. Cursed things are dead. This thing is *hungry*. It wears kings the way a leech wears skin, and it's been wearing them for a hundred years, and it's started to fail, and failing things eat faster." She paused. "That's why the hunt. Your brother isn't collecting the gifted out of malice. He's collecting them the way a starving man collects bread."

The fire cracked somewhere behind us. Down in the valley, faintly, a wolf was complaining about politics.

"And you know this how?" I asked carefully.

"Because I keep the gate it was forged to seal, and the stones under the Spire remember everything, and I have been listening to them my whole life." She stood, brushing off her trousers, done with the conversation on her own schedule as, I was beginning to suspect, she scheduled everything. "You wanted a weapon, Prince Aldric. What you got is a librarian with opinions. Try not to be disappointed loudly; the children are sleeping."

She walked off toward the fires, and I stayed on the log, doing what I'd told her I was doing earlier, which was arithmetic.

Because here's the thing. I'd spent ten years building a rebellion out of grievances, and grievances are excellent fuel and terrible maps. I had never once, in all that time, had a destination. Overthrow the usurper, yes—and then? Put another head under that iron and wait a century for the leech to finish digesting?

But if she was right—if the crown could be unbound rather than inherited—

"Kavanagh," I called after her.

She stopped. Didn't turn.

"When we take my brother back—when we unbind him, if it can be done—what happens to the keeper who does it?"

The pause stretched long enough that I watched her shoulders settle, the way people square themselves before lifting something heavy.

"Ask the mountain," she said finally. "It knows. It's just not telling either of us yet."

Then she was gone into the firelight, and I sat alone with the wolves and the arithmetic, and for the first time in ten years, the sum came out somewhere other than revenge.

It came out at the Spire. And something in my chest, some muscle I'd thought atrophied past use, tightened around the knowledge that I was going to spend the rest of this war trying to save a stubborn gatekeeper who would absolutely refuse to be saved.

Dara found me there an hour later, and reported that the sentries had counted torches on the southern ridgeline.

A lot of torches.

Veyra had found our trail.
