## 7. Ashmarket

I had imagined burned cities the way you imagine storms: as a thing with edges. A fire starts here, stops there, and what lies beyond the char is untouched. I was wrong about storms too, it turns out, but that's a story for another day.

Ashmarket had no edges. The burning had gone through it like grief through a family—skipping some houses out of sheer caprice, hollowing others to their rafters, leaving one street so pristine it looked ashamed of itself. We came down off the ridge at dusk, forty-odd rebels and freed prisoners moving single file through ash that rose in soft gray puffs around our boots, and I felt the city before I saw it.

That's the thing about my gift nobody warns you about. Stone-sense isn't a talent; it's an affliction of attention. Every rock has a memory, and most memories are dull—a river's patience, a glacier's slow argument, the small dramas of roots cracking pavement. But a city is stone that people have *insisted upon*. Streets are arguments won against the land. Foundations are promises made in mortar. And when the people who made those promises die or scatter, the stone remembers them anyway, the way a widower keeps setting two cups on the table.

Ashmarket's stones remembered a hundred thousand mornings.

Market bells. Fish carts on cobbles worn glassy by feet. Children racing along the seawall, daring each other toward the drop. The harbor had been dredged three times in living memory; I could feel the shovels in the silt. A bakery on Copper Lane whose oven had run so hot for sixty years the hearthstones had changed color all the way through—I could taste that warmth even now, under everything else.

And over it, like ink poured into milk: the burning. Not just the physical scorching, which was bad enough, but the way the stones had *recorded* it. Stone doesn't flinch. It holds what happens to it without judgment, which makes it the worst possible witness, because it never looks away. I walked through Ashmarket and the ground told me about every door that had been broken, every cellar where families had hidden until they couldn't hide anymore, every beam that had groaned and given.

"You're doing the thing," Sefa said quietly beside me.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face goes gray and you stop hearing people. You did it at the pass, when we crossed the old boundary stones. You're listening."

"I'm always listening," I said. "It's not optional. That's rather the point."

"Well," she said, "try listening less loudly. The others need to see you steady."

The healer had a gift for saying devastating things in the tone of a woman discussing soup. I straightened my spine and stopped listening—or performed stopping, which is nearly as good—and took stock of our refuge instead.

Aldric's rebels had chosen well, in the way of desperate people everywhere, which is to say they'd chosen what remained. The southern quarter of Ashmarket still stood: a knot of warehouses and merchant houses around an old customs hall built of granite so dense it had laughed at the fires. The customs hall became our barracks, our kitchen, our map room, and—for those of us who slept at all—our dormitory. Its walls were thick enough that even I got only murmurs from them. Granite, I've decided, is the introvert of stones, and I say that with love.

* * *

We'd been there three days when I finally understood why the city bothered me more than the prison column had.

The column had been horror, but honest horror. Soldiers, chains, a boy named Pell executed in the road because his gift flared when he panicked—his hands had turned everything they touched briefly to glass, and a sergeant had decided that was witchcraft worth a bullet rather than paperwork. I had stood in that column and watched, and vowed something wordless and enormous, and moved on, because prisoners move on or they die.

Ashmarket was worse because it wasn't horror. It was *absence*.

On the fourth morning I climbed to the roof of the customs hall to be alone with the dawn—the one hour when the stones sleep, or seem to, their memories settling like sediment—and I looked out over the dead city and did arithmetic.

The free cities had held perhaps two million souls when I was born. My mother used to trade stories of Ashmarket's fish market the way other women traded recipes. Now I stood above a city that could house sixty thousand and counted, by the smoke of its cookfires, maybe four hundred living residents. Squatters, scavengers, a few stubborn fishing families who'd rebuilt on the one street the fire had skipped. Four hundred people rattling around in the bones of a metropolis.

And it wasn't only Ashmarket. The prisoner column had come through three towns, and I'd kept count the way you keep count of wounds: Vellin's Crossing, half-empty. Dunmore, half-empty. The villages between them were names on signposts with nothing behind the signs. A century of the hunt—not this new proclamation, but the quiet grinding version that came before it, the disappearances, the "conscriptions," the gifted children taken to the capital "for assessment" and never returned—had been eating the realm alive, one mouthful at a time, so slowly no one had choked.

I sat on the rooftop ledge with my legs dangling over a four-story drop and let myself feel the full shape of it. Somewhere below, a gull cried, absurdly, over a harbor where no ships loaded. The stones beneath me hummed with the memory of a world that had been stolen in installments.

"That's a long face for someone sitting on the only intact roof in the city," said a voice behind me.

Aldric. Of course. The man had a genius for finding me at moments I'd have paid money to keep private.

"Go away," I said, without heat. We'd developed a rhythm in the days since the ambush: he needled, I deflected, neither of us acknowledged that he'd pulled me out of a chain gang and I'd saved his life ten minutes later when a soldier's blade found the gap in his guard and my thrown rock found the gap in the soldier's skull. Debts sat between us like a third person at every conversation, and both of us preferred pretending otherwise.

He sat down next to me anyway, close enough that the drop seemed suddenly theoretical. He was eating an apple, which struck me as obscenely cheerful, and after a moment he held it out. I took it. Siege rations had taught me never to refuse food on principle.

"Tell me what you see," he said.

"Ruins."

"Try again. You're the one who talks to rocks. What do they say?"

I chewed the apple, which was mealy and perfect. "They remember being full," I said finally. "That's the worst of it. Fire destroys things, fine, I understand destruction, I've lived my whole life beside a gate built to hold back something destructive. But this—" I gestured at the gray sprawl below. "This isn't destruction. This is a city that was *emptied*. Carefully. Over decades. Like lard scraped off a bone. Someone wanted the gifted gone, then wanted anyone who sheltered the gifted gone, then wanted anyone who *might* someday produce a gifted child gone, and the whole realm just... thinned. Nobody noticed because it happened at the speed of generations." I looked at him. "Your rebels fight soldiers. Soldiers aren't the disease. They're the fever."

Aldric was quiet for a while. Below us, the rebel camp was waking: Sefa drilling the younger gifted in breathing exercises that looked suspiciously like naps; Rhea Voss's shore party arguing with a quartermaster about salt pork; the sentries on the customs tower trading watches. Forty-one people, give or take. Against a kingdom.

"You're right," he said at last, "and it's worse than you know."

"There's a sentence I've come to dread."

"Then stop asking questions you don't want answered." He turned the apple core in his fingers. "You've heard the men call me the prince in exile. The true heir. Edran's brother. You've heard them say the usurper sits the throne."

"Everyone says it. Usurper, tyrant, the Hollow King—pick your insult."

"Do you know how my brother became king?"

"Heir apparent. Father died. Standard tragedy, standard coronation."

"Standard," Aldric agreed. "Except that three weeks before the coronation, Edran fell ill. A fever the physicians couldn't name. He burned for nine days, and on the ninth night the court physician brought in a relic—an old thing, a crown of black iron that had sat in the royal vault longer than the dynasty had sat on the throne. No one knew its origin. The vault records called it *the Binding*, and older records than that called it nothing at all, because it predates writing in this kingdom." He said it flatly, the way a man recites a confession he's rehearsed until the words wore smooth. "They put it on his head to draw the fever out. Folk medicine. Sympathy magic. Draw the sickness into iron, they said."

"And?"

"And the fever left him. He woke clear-eyed and grateful and crowned himself with the thing because it seemed lucky now, and he ruled for a year, and he was a good king. Genuinely. Fair judgments, open courts, he repealed two of father's crueller taxes. I was seventeen. I worshipped him." The apple core went over the edge, spinning down into the dark. "And then, slowly, over years, the taxes came back. The assessments began. The first hunts. Small ones. Deniable ones. And Edran changed—not cruel, exactly. *Hungry.* There was a look in his eyes like a man listening to a conversation happening somewhere else, all the time, everywhere he went."

I felt cold despite the morning sun. "You're telling me the crown did this."

"I'm telling you the crown *is* doing this. Has been doing it. To him." He looked at me, and for once there was no barb in his face, no princely armor, just a tired man on a roof. "It's not a man, Wendell. It was never a man. It's a thing that learned, a century ago, that if you wear the shape of a king, no one ever asks the king to account for himself. Kings answer to God and history. Who dares audit either? So it takes a host, and rules through him, and feeds—on the gifted, on their minds, their power—and when the host burns out, as hosts do, it arranges a succession. An illness. A relic produced from a vault at the right moment. A brother or a son, grieving and desperate, who *chooses* the iron." His voice caught, barely, on the last word, and I understood that I was hearing the exact place where his exile lived. "My grandfather. My great-grandmother. Three kings back, five. All of them the same man, wearing different faces. All of them my family."

The wind came up off the dead harbor and smelled of salt and ash, and I thought of the gate I'd kept for eleven years. The Last Spire, sealing the pass into the hollowed kingdom beyond the mountains—the kingdom the old wars had emptied, the one my ledger said hadn't seen a living traveler cross *into* in recorded history. Everyone assumed the Spire kept something out.

"The Binding," I said slowly. "Black iron. Older than the dynasty."

"You know it."

"I know *of* it. The founders' records at the Spire reference a pact, sealed in iron, that the gate wards depend on. The founding oaths. I always read it as metaphor. Founders loved metaphor." My mouth had gone dry. "Aldric. Where was the Binding forged?"

Something shifted in his face—the first crack I'd seen in the careful composure, a flicker of genuine alarm, as if I'd asked the one question he'd hoped no one would think to ask.

"At the mountain," he said. "At the pass. The oldest chronicles agree on that much and disagree on everything else. The crown was forged at the Last Spire, Wendell. Whatever your gate keeps—whatever pact your founders sealed—it's bound to the same iron that's eating my brother alive. Which means either your gate is the cage it escaped from..."

"...or the cage it built for itself," I finished.

We sat with that. Below us the camp went on being stubbornly alive—Sefa's voice calling the breathing count, a hammer ringing somewhere, the sea doing its eternal indifferent work against a shore built for ten thousand ships. Four hundred people in a city of bones. One dying gift-keeper, one exiled prince, and a smuggler captain downstairs haggling over pork.

"One more thing," Aldric said quietly. "When I fled the capital, ten years ago—after I understood what my brother had become, after I failed to—" He stopped. Started again. "Edran surfaced. Once. For a moment, through it all, *he* surfaced, and he looked at me, and he begged me for a knife. Begged, Wendell. With his own eyes, his own voice, tears running down a face that wasn't entirely his anymore. And I hesitated. One heartbeat. Two. Long enough for it to go under again, and I've spent ten years telling myself the hesitation was mercy."

"And was it?"

"No," he said. "It was cowardice wearing mercy's clothes. And I'm telling you this because you should know what you've tied yourself to. Not a cause. Not a throne. A debt I can't pay and a thing wearing my brother's face that I mean to destroy even if it kills the last part of him that's real." He stood, brushing ash from his knees, and offered me a hand up with the ghost of his usual crooked smile. "Still glad you threw that rock?"

I took his hand and let him pull me up. "Ask me again after breakfast."

But as we climbed down through the customs hall, past the maps and the sleeping pallets and the smell of porridge, one thought kept circling me like the gull outside, patient and hungry:

If the crown had been forged at my gate—if the founders' pact and the Binding were one and the same—then the answers weren't in the capital at all. They'd been sitting behind my locked door for eleven years, and I had walked away from them carrying the key in my own blood.

Sefa found me at the porridge pot, staring at nothing, and touched my arm.

"Eat," she said. "Then tell me what the stones told you. Because you've gone pale as a wraith, and tomorrow we start training—and something tells me you've just realized exactly what we'll need to train for."
