## 8. Lessons in Borrowed Fire

I have been a healer for thirty years, and in that time I have learned exactly two things about people. The first is that everyone lies about how much it hurts. The second is that nobody—no one, not kings, not generals, not the wisest woman in a burned city—knows what their own gift is actually for until someone makes them use it wrong.

Ashmarket was teaching me a third thing: that grief, given enough people and enough time, starts to look like hope. Or maybe hope just looks like grief that hasn't found a place to sit down yet. Hard to say. The distinction mattered less each morning.

We had taken over the shell of a granary near the river gate, one of the few structures in Ashmarket with its roof half intact, which in this city counted as palatial. Forty-three of us now. Forty-three gifted who had run, hidden, or been dragged out of crown cages by Aldric's ragged band of exiles, all of us camped among the rat-chewed grain sacks like the world's most dangerous family reunion.

Every dawn I made them train.

Not because I wanted to. Because the alternative was watching them die one at a time when Veyra's riders finally found us, and I am many things but I am not sentimental about my own survival.

"All right," I called, clapping my hands over the murmur of the hall. "You know the rules. Small fires. Controlled burns. If you scorch a wall, you patch it. If you scorch *Marek*, you patch Marek."

"I'm fine," said Marek, who was not fine, whose left sleeve was still smoking from yesterday.

"See? Patchable." I pointed at the circle of stones we'd laid in the center of the floor. "Pairs today. One holds, one pushes. Then swap. And before anyone asks—yes, again. Every day, again. Power alone is a candle in wind. Power shared is a hearth. You want to survive what's coming, you learn to lend your flame to someone else's hands without flinching."

A boy at the edge of the group—young Tam-shaped, all elbows and old resentments, though this one was named Perrin—muttered something to his neighbor. I caught only the word *nursemaid*.

"Nursemaid," I agreed pleasantly. "And when the crown's witch-hunters come through that door, I'll be the nursemaid holding the bowl of water while they drown. Pair up."

They paired up. They always did, eventually. Fear is an excellent classroom assistant.

I worked the edges of the room as they practiced, hands hovering over shoulders, correcting grips on gifts the way you'd correct a sword arm. Little Corla, whose gift was warmth—useless-seeming, until you saw her pull fever out of a dying man like drawing thread from cloth. The twins, Hesta and Orla, who could trade senses between them and got nosebleeds doing it, because they were fourteen and stupid about limits, as fourteen-year-olds are about everything. Old Bren with his weather-sense that had gotten his village burned when he sneezed out a hailstorm during a crown census.

And at the far end, apart from the others the way she was always slightly apart, Wendell Kavanagh sat cross-legged on the cold flagstones with her palm flat against the floor, eyes closed, looking less like a student and more like a woman listening to a very long story told very slowly.

She'd been with us nine days. Nine days of saying almost nothing, eating little, sleeping less, and pressing her hand against every vertical surface in Ashmarket like a physician taking a pulse. I'd seen her do it to walls, to wells, to the charred spine of the temple arch, once, memorably, to the ground itself while the rest of us were burying our dead.

Stone-sense, she'd called it, when Aldric pressed her for specifics. As if that were a complete sentence. As if that explained why she sometimes went grey-faced after touching a wall, or why she'd flinched away from the prison wagons' iron wheels like they were screaming at her.

"She's not training," Perrin complained to me that morning, jerking his chin toward her. "She just... sits there. Touching things. How's that going to help when soldiers come?"

"Watch her hands," I said.

He watched. Wendell's fingers spread against the flagstone, and the dust on them stirred—not much, just a shimmer, like breath on a drum skin. A crack in the floor three feet away widened by a hair's width and closed again, deliberate as a knuckle cracking.

"What—" Perrin started.

"That," I said, "is a woman having a conversation you can't hear, with something that has been here since before your grandmother's grandmother learned to walk. Go practice. And if you ever make her angry, apologize quickly."

* * *

It was three days later that she broke through—or rather, that I pushed her into breaking through, though I have tried since to forgive myself for the timing.

The morning was cold enough that our breath hung in the granary like ghosts queueing for admission. Training went badly. Corla burned herself pulling heat out of a frostbitten mule we'd acquired; the twins gave each other matching migraines; Marek set fire to Marek again, which at this point felt less like accident and more like devotion.

Wendell sat at her wall, hand flat, silent, and I watched her strain against the boundary of her own gift the way a swimmer strains against ice. Reaching down. Getting nothing back but surface. She'd told me once, in one of her rare whole sentences, that she could feel stone only through touch—that the mountain spoke to her palms and nowhere else, and that lately the voice had been getting quieter, or farther, or both.

"You're listening wrong," I said, crouching beside her.

Her eyes opened. Grey, level, unimpressed. She had that look common to keepers and cats: the certainty that everything within reach is hers to manage, and everything beyond it is beneath comment.

"I've been listening my whole life," she said.

"With your hands." I sat down across from her, knees cracking—I'm sixty-one and I no longer pretend otherwise. "That's not listening. That's eavesdropping at a keyhole. You want the whole song, you have to open the door."

"There is no door. There's rock, and there's me, and there's my skin between."

"There's always a door. It's usually just somewhere you haven't thought to knock." I held out my hand, palm up. "Give me yours."

"Why?"

"Because I'm the teacher and you're too proud to refuse in front of the students, and because you've been bleeding yourself thin against that wall for nine days and it's making the rest of us tired just watching." I kept my voice light. Underneath it, I'll admit, ran something less generous: curiosity. Thirty years of healing teaches you to read bodies the way she read stone, and everything about Wendell Kavanagh's body read *failing*. Not dying. Just—a lamp turned down too low, burning its own oil.

She put her hand in mine. Her calluses were keeper's calluses, rope and iron and stone, and her fingers were cold as a cellar.

"Now close your eyes. Keep your palm on the floor. And instead of pushing your gift *down* into the stone—" I wrapped my other hand around hers, and let a thread of my own gift flow into her, the way I'd lend warmth to a shivering man, the borrowed-fire trick I'd been drilling into the students for days. "—let mine carry yours past the skin. Past the floor. Down."

"This is—"

"Hush. Listening."

She hushed. Remarkable woman.

What happened next, I felt through her hand as much as saw on her face. Her gift went out of her like water finding a crack in a dam—and then it didn't trickle, it *fell*, plunging through flagstone and foundation and the packed ash of dead Ashmarket, down and down, and I watched her pupils blow wide and her breath go ragged and her whole body sway like a reed in current.

"Sefa," she whispered. "Oh. Oh, it's—it never stops. It goes *down* forever—"

"Breathe. Follow the oldest voice. The deepest stratum. Where the mountain remembers being young."

I don't know how long she stayed under. Long enough that the training circle stopped and gathered, silent, around us. Long enough that frost formed on her eyelashes in the cold air. Long enough that Aldric came looking, drawn by whatever instinct princes develop for trouble, and stood at the edge of the crowd with his jaw tight and his hand nowhere near his sword, which for him was restraint verging on sainthood.

When Wendell surfaced, she came up gasping, tearing her hand free of mine, and for one unguarded moment her face was open as a child's—wonder and terror in equal measure, the expression of someone who has just learned the world is vastly larger than advertised.

"The founding," she said hoarsely. "I found—the founding. Sefa, the Spire. I saw them *build* it."

* * *

She wouldn't speak of it inside. Too many ears, and some of those ears belonged to people we hadn't yet decided to trust—refugees arrived in waves now, some vetted, some merely desperate, and desperation is the easiest door in any fortress. So she waited until dusk, and then she led me and Aldric up onto the granary roof, where the burned city spread below us like a black tide frozen mid-swallow, and the mountains stood off to the north with snow on their shoulders, and the Last Spire—if you knew where to look, and she always knew where to look—was a needle-thin shadow against the last light.

She told it standing up, arms crossed against the cold, in the clipped voice of a woman delivering a report she wished she didn't have to give.

"The founders weren't sealing something out," she said. "That's the story. That's the story the wardens tell, that the smugglers tell, that I told my apprentice. The gate keeps the hollow kingdom out. Badlands beyond, monsters beyond, curse beyond." She shook her head. "The stones remember differently."

"Tell us differently," Aldric said.

"They built the gate from the inside." Her breath fogged. "The kingdom wasn't hollow yet. It was whole—cities, rivers, a million people. And there was a thing. Not a king. Older than crowns. The stones don't have a word for it; they show me a shape like a hunger wearing a shape, if that means anything to you."

"It does," Aldric said quietly, and did not elaborate, and Wendell let him not.

"The founders couldn't kill it. Couldn't cage it in any ordinary way, because it ate cages—it ate *minds*, it wore people, it wore a whole court at one point, the memory of that is—" she stopped, steadied. "So they forged a binding. A collar, not a wall. They poured themselves into it—every gifted founder, their gifts, their lives, poured into a single working and sealed into the mountain at the gate. The gate isn't a barrier against the thing. The gate is the *lock on the collar*. And the key—" her voice thinned, "the key was meant to be tended. Renewed. By keepers. Generations of keepers feeding a little of themselves into the wards so the binding stayed fed."

"And?" I asked, though I already dreaded the answer, the way you dread answers in rooms where someone is about to die.

"And somewhere along a hundred years, the tending stopped being renewal and became consumption." Wendell turned to face us, and the last light caught her eyes, and there was dread in them, yes, but underneath the dread something worse: recognition. "The stones show me the crown, Aldric. Not your brother's crown—the original. The founders' binding was reforged, or it reforged itself, I can't tell, the memory blurs there, deliberately blurred, someone *wanted* it blurred. But the shape is the same. A ring of power made to bind a hungry mind. Except a binding worn becomes a throne. A lock worn long enough starts choosing who wears *it*."

"A century of kings," Aldric said. His voice was very flat, the flatness of a man standing on ice and hearing it sing.

"A century of hosts." Wendell's arms tightened. "It doesn't rule through them. It *eats* through them. Slowly. Each king a little more hollow than the last. Your brother is just the latest meal—and he's nearly done, which is why the hunt, why the hunger for gifted minds. It's failing, and it's trying to feed."

The wind moved through the burned streets below, carrying ash and river-smell and the distant sound of our people singing, badly, around a cookfire. Life, stubbornly continuing. It seemed obscene.

"So the gate," I said slowly, working it through, healer's logic, diagnosis before treatment. "The gate your family has kept for generations—your gift, Wendell, your stone-sense, the reason your line was placed there—"

"—is the lock's mechanism," she finished. "Yes."

"Then the crown can't be destroyed in the capital. It can't be cut off his head, it can't be melted down, because the binding isn't *in* the crown. The binding is in the gate. In the mountain. In—"

"In the wards," Wendell said. "Which are starving. Which is why the tremors. Which is why the stones are getting quieter." She looked north, toward the Spire, and her face did something complicated—grief and relief fighting for the same ground. "The binding is dying, Sefa. And when it dies, the thing behind it wakes up unfed and unbound, with a hundred years of stolen kings' knowledge and a hunt for the gifted already running."

Silence on the rooftop. Even the wind seemed to hold its tongue.

Aldric spoke first, and his voice had changed—hardened into purpose, the softness scoured out of it. "Then we don't march on the capital. We march home."

"We?" Wendell raised an eyebrow. "There is no *we* yet, princeling. I've known you ten days and spent most of them wishing you'd stop giving orders."

"You'll want me there when the time comes."

"Will I."

"Yes," Aldric said, "because whatever breaks that binding will need someone the realm believes has a right to stand at that gate—and because you know I'm right and it's irritating you."

It was, visibly, irritating her enormously. I bit down on a smile. Thirty years of medicine teaches you to recognize the early symptoms of certain conditions, and those two were presenting with textbook clarity.

But I didn't smile long. Because I had seen what I'd seen when I lent Wendell my fire—felt, through our joined hands, the true depth of what she carried, and the true thinness of what remained. A lamp turned low. A well drawn down to mud.

Wonder, I thought, looking north with them both at the small dark needle of the Spire. Wonder at what the stones remembered, and what they'd shown her. And under it, cold as the frost forming on the railing, dread—because I was a healer, and healers know better than anyone that the cheapest cures are the ones that cost the patient everything, and somebody in this story was going to be asked to pay in full.

Below us, in the granary, someone screamed.

Not training-scream. Real scream. We were moving before the sound finished, Aldric's boots hammering the stairs, my skirts gathered in my fists, Wendell's palm already pressed flat against the roof stones as she took the ladder in a single drop—reading the building, reading the panic, reading *who and where*.

By the time we reached the training floor, the crowd had broken open around a knot of figures, and at the center of it stood a mud-spattered rider in no colors at all, holding out a sealed letter with a free-city crest, saying the words that would turn forty-three refugees into forty-three suspects and one of our own number into something we had not yet imagined:

"I've a message for the exile prince. From inside his own camp."
