## 9. The Usurper's Summons

I had been a prince for eleven years and a fugitive for ten of them, and I will tell you the thing no ballad ever gets right: exile is mostly waiting. Waiting in ditches. Waiting in burned-out granaries. Waiting, as we were now, in the ribs of an old watchtower above Ashmarket while a man named Corren Hale decided whether to sell me.

I should say at once that I liked Corren. That is not a confession I make comfortably, but there it was. He was a broad, cheerful man with a smith's forearms and a deserter's conscience, and he made the best barley stew this side of the mountains, which in our camp counted for statesmanship. He'd followed me out of the garrison at Cael Ford because his sister had been taken in the first sweep of the hunt, and he asked about her every week with the stubborn hope of a man watering a dead tree.

So when I caught him at midnight on the tower stair, folding a scrap of paper into his coat like a man hiding a wound, my first thought was not *traitor*. My first thought was *please don't let it be you*.

The gods have never much cared for my please-don't-lets.

"Aldric." He didn't startle, which told me how long he'd known I was behind him. A guilty man jumps; a resolved one just turns around. "Couldn't sleep either?"

"I sleep beautifully," I said. "I'm standing here for the view." The view was Ashmarket by starlight, all its black rafters laid open to the sky like the city had been gutted and left to cure. "What's in your pocket, Corren."

His hand went flat over his coat. Another bad sign. Guilty men fidget; resolved men guard. "Nothing you need worry about."

"Everything anyone hides from me at midnight is something I need worry about. It's practically my coat of arms." I held out my hand. "Give it here, or give me a reason, and I warn you I'm very easy to reason with before dawn and insufferable after."

He looked at me a long moment. In the dark his face did something complicated—grief and apology and a strange kind of pity, which frightened me more than anger would have. Then he took out the paper and put it in my palm without letting go of it first, so that for a heartbeat we were both holding it, like men carrying a body together.

It was a proclamation, printed neat and official on good stock—the crown could still afford good stock, I noted, even if its ink smelled of ash and old bargains.

**TO ALDRIC OF HOUSE VARENE, STYLING HIMSELF PROTECTOR OF THE GIFTED:**

**THE CROWN OFFERS FULL AMNESTY TO EVERY SOUL NOW SHELTERING IN REBELLION, PARDON FOR ALL OFFENSES GIVEN OR CONTEMPLATED, AND RESTORATION OF PROPERTY AND NAME—**

**UPON TWO CONDITIONS ONLY:**

**THAT THE PRINCE ALDRIC KNEEL AT THE CITY GATE AND RENOUNCE HIS CLAIM;**

**AND THAT HE DELIVER INTO CROWN HANDS THE WOMAN CALLED WENDELL KAVANAGH, KEEPER OF THE LAST SPIRE, WHOSE GIFT IS AN AFFRONT TO ORDER AND WHOSE CAPTURE SHALL MARK THE CLOSE OF THE HUNT FOREVER.**

**ALL OTHERS GO FREE. THIS IS THE CROWN'S WHOLE WORD.**

Below it, in different ink, smaller, someone had added a line that turned my stomach: *Deliver her alive and unspoiled.*

"They came through the fish-market woman," Corren said quietly. "The one who sells us eels. She didn't know what she carried. Or she says she didn't." He rubbed his jaw. "There were copies. I burned the others. I was going to burn this one too."

"Were you."

"Yes," he said, and then, because Corren Hale had never in his life managed a clean lie: "After I read it. After I thought about it. After—" His voice cracked down the middle. "My sister, Aldric. Maren. They took her in the second sweep. If this ends the hunt—"

"If she delivers me Wendell Kavanagh."

He flinched. "I haven't given it to anyone. You're the first."

"That's meant to comfort me, I think." I folded the paper along its creases, slowly, giving my hands something honest to do so they wouldn't shake. "And how many others got copies? Before the burning."

"Three that I know of. Maybe more. The eel woman travels the whole camp."

Of course she did. Of course. I looked out over Ashmarket—at the cookfires dotting the ruins like embers refusing to die—and tried to count, in my head, how many desperate people were awake right now, holding the same offer, doing the same arithmetic Corren had done. *One stranger's life against everyone you love.* It was not even a hard sum. That was the whole genius of it. The crown hadn't written an ultimatum; it had written a mirror, and handed one to every household in camp.

"You know I can't do it," Corren said. It wasn't a question. He needed to hear me say it, maybe. Or he needed to hear himself argue against it, so that tomorrow, when the vote went however it went, he could tell himself he'd stood somewhere.

"No," I said. "I can't kneel and I can't deliver her. But thank you for asking, Corren. Most traitors skip that part entirely."

"I'm not—" He stopped. Started again. "I don't know what I am."

"That," I said, "is the most honest sentence spoken in this camp all month, and I include my own."

* * *

By morning, of course, it was everywhere. Secrets travel faster than fire in a camp of two hundred people, especially secrets shaped like pardons.

I called a council in the granary—the big one with half a roof, where we'd held every meeting since Ashmarket—and I put the proclamation on the table myself, face up, because I'd learned long ago that a hidden truth devours a camp and a displayed one only wounds it. Around the table sat the people I trusted most: Brannoc the ex-legionary who ran our fighting company; old Mother Ilse, who was neither a mother nor named Ilse but had healed half the camp through the winter; Tam's friend Doss, a wiry river-boy who scouted for us; and a dozen more whose names are carved now on the memorial wall and I will not cheapen them by listing them here like inventory.

Wendell was not invited. That was deliberate, and I am not proud of how deliberate. Some animal part of me wanted to solve this before she heard of it, to stand between her and the arithmetic the way I'd failed to stand between Edran and his crown. Pride dressed up as protection. It has always been my best tailoring.

"This is bait," Brannoc said, before I'd even finished laying out the terms. He said everything early and loudly; it was why he was good in a fight and terrible at cards. "The usurper doesn't pardon people. It eats them. Amnesty means it knows where every gifted soul in this camp sleeps."

"It also might mean amnesty," said Hesta, a dyer from the coast whose son had the gift of finding lost things—a useless gift until the hunt made it a death sentence, and useful again now, when half our supplies were buried caches. She said it carefully, testing the words like ice. "We've got forty-one gifted here, Aldric. Forty-one children too. Winter's coming. If kneeling buys them their lives—"

"It doesn't buy anything," I said. "Read the last condition. Deliver Wendell *alive and unspoiled*. Why does the crown care whether she's spoiled, Hesta? What do you imagine it wants her *for*?"

Silence did its work around the table. I watched people arrive, one by one, at the answer I'd arrived at weeks ago in the dark: the crown wasn't hunting the gifted to destroy them. It was hunting them the way a starving man hunts bread. And a keeper of the Last Spire, with a gift old enough and deep enough to touch the founding wards—that wasn't a loaf. That was a feast.

"She'd refuse anyway," someone said near the back. "She'd go willingly, maybe. To save the rest of us. Have you thought of that? The keeper strikes me as the type."

I had thought of it. I thought of it constantly. Wendell Kavanagh had walked into my ambush expecting nothing and negotiated terms from inside a rope net; she had crossed a burned province carrying other people's wounds without once asking who would carry hers. If the camp voted to trade her, she would climb onto the cart herself and thank them for the ride, and that possibility scared me more than Veyra's legions.

"The vote isn't today," Mother Ilse said, in the tone she used to end things. "Let people chew. Fear digests slow."

Fear digests slow, but it digests completely. By evening the camp had split itself into little congregations—the ones calling it betrayal, the ones calling it mercy, the ones saying nothing and watching everyone else say things. I walked the fires and heard my own arguments coming back at me wearing other voices, and understood, with the particular clarity available only to exhausted princes, that the crown hadn't needed an army for this. One sheet of good paper. Two conditions. The camp was fracturing along lines that had always been there, drawn in advance, waiting for ink.

* * *

I found her on the wall.

Not the granary, not her tent, not anywhere sensible—the old curtain wall above the south gate, sitting on a fallen merlon with her legs dangling over a fifty-foot drop into rubble, eating a heel of bread as though she were watching a festival rather than contemplating the abyss. I had learned that Wendell treated heights and dangers the way other people treated furniture. You lived with them. You didn't make a fuss.

"How long have you been up here?" I asked.

"Since midday." She broke the bread and handed me half without looking at me. It was not an invitation so much as a tax; I paid it. "Corren told me. An hour ago. Don't be angry with him—he reasoned himself around to my door eventually, which is more than some managed."

"I'm not angry with him."

"You're furious with everyone. You get very polite when you're furious. It's your worst quality." She brushed crumbs from her knees. "So. The council voted?"

"Not yet. Tomorrow."

"And tonight they're voting with their feet. Who's left?"

"Nobody's left. Nobody's gone. Yet." I sat down beside her on the merlon, which was a foolish place to sit and therefore exactly where she was. Below us Ashmarket lay in its long ruin, and beyond it the plain ran silver toward the capital, where my brother wore a thing on his head that wore him back. "Forty-one gifted, Wendell. Plus children. Plus two hundred others who followed me for reasons they're currently re-examining. The offer is generous. That's what makes it obscene. The crown priced you fairly and everyone in camp is checking its sums."

"I heard Hesta in the cook line," Wendell said. Her voice was level, gatekeeper-level, the voice she probably used to log smuggler routes and collapsing wards. "And Brannoc's people. And a boy—I don't know his name—who said, quite reasonably, that one keeper against forty families wasn't a hard choice at all." She tore another piece off the bread, though she didn't eat it. Just held it. "He's right, you know. Arithmetically."

"Don't."

"He's *right*, Aldric. That's the horror of it. The crown didn't write an unfair bargain. It wrote a fair one. It knew I'd agree, and it knew the camp would agree, and it built the whole trap out of the fact that I'm the sort of person who—" She stopped. Looked at the bread in her hand. "When I kept the Spire, I used to tell myself I was the gate. Impassable. Impersonal. Nothing gets through Wendell Kavanagh. Turns out I'm not the gate at all. I'm the toll. Everyone's counting coins."

For a moment neither of us spoke. Somewhere below, a child laughed—one of the camp children, chasing another through the ruins, their small lives still innocent enough to find game in rubble. Wendell listened to that laugh the way a drowning woman listens to weather reports.

"I want to be clear about what happens tomorrow," I said. "If they vote to consider the offer—if they vote to *negotiate*, which is what cowards call surrendering other people—I won't allow it. I'll burn the proclamation, dissolve the council, and take whatever comes. Which may be nothing. Which may be half the camp walking out with their pardons and leaving us thirty souls and a grudge."

"You'd tear the rebellion apart to keep one gatekeeper?"

"I'd tear the rebellion apart to keep the *only person who knows how to unbind the crown*," I said. "But yes. Also that. Both things are true and I've stopped pretending otherwise, at least to myself. To everyone else I maintain a dignified fiction about strategy."

That earned me half a breath of a laugh—her laugh was always half a breath, rationed, like everything else about her—and then it died, because below us, torchlight was gathering in the market square. Voices. Dozens. Not a mob, not yet, but the raw material of one: the camp assembling itself without being called, the way water finds a crack.

"They're meeting now," Wendell said. "Tonight. Without you."

"Without me," I agreed. "Which tells you everything about how the vote would go with me in the room."

"Then we shouldn't be in the room either." She stood, dusted off her trousers, and looked down at me with those gray eyes that had assessed a sealed gate every day for years and found it wanting. "Come on, prince. If your people are going to decide whether to sell me, I intend to hear the price they set. Every word of it. I've spent my whole life knowing what the mountain thinks of me. I can stand to learn what the living do."

"Wendell—"

"You said it yourself. Hidden truths devour camps." She started down the wall stair, sure-footed on broken stone. "Displayed ones only wound. So let's be wounded honestly."

* * *

We stood in the shadow of the granary doorway, close enough to hear everything, far enough to be nobody. It is remarkable, the invisibility of two people who decline to speak. The crowd filled the square—most of the camp, truly, two hundred souls pressed among the burnt stalls, torchlight making a moving country of every face—and Brannoc stood on a wagon bed because Brannoc always ended up on elevated surfaces, shouting that the offer was poison, while Hesta stood opposite with her dyer's hands folded calm, saying that poison was easy to name from inside walls full of food.

"We don't vote to accept," Hesta was saying. "Nobody's proposing that. We vote to *send word*. To ask. Asking costs nothing."

"Asking tells the crown exactly where we are!" Brannoc.

"The crown already knows where we are. We've been cooking cabbage in a ruined city for a month. Ask any nose."

Laughter, uneasy, splitting along the old fault lines. And then Corren's voice, hoarse, from somewhere near the front: "My sister's in their cells. Maren Hale. Taken in the second sweep. Some of you knew her. If a letter asking questions might reach her captors—might buy her a warm cell instead of a cold one—I don't see how asking is treason." His voice broke and steadied. "I brought the paper to Aldric first. I want that known. But I won't pretend I don't understand why people are tempted. Temptation isn't treason. Yet."

*Temptation isn't treason. Yet.* The crowd murmured around the phrase like around a coin being bitten.

Beside me in the doorway, Wendell had gone very still. Not afraid-still. Ledger-still. She was listening the way she'd once described reading stone—not the words, but the weight beneath them, the stresses in the structure, where the load-bearing grief ran.

"They're going to vote to consider it," she murmured. "Look at them. No one wants to be the one who said sell her, and no one wants to be the one who said let my child starve for a stranger. So they'll build a middle word to hide in. *Consider*. *Parley*. *Terms*." Her jaw tightened. "I've watched smugglers do the same thing over a disputed cargo. It always ends the same way. The cargo goes to whoever wanted it least loudly."

"You're not cargo."

"I'm the toll, Aldric. I told you." But her hand had found the wall beside her, fingers spread against the stone, and I saw—just saw, in the torch-glow—the faint way the dust settled toward her palm, the way the rubble at the doorway's foot seemed to lean a half-degree her direction. Even guttering, her gift reached for the stone like a hand reaching in sleep. "Vote's starting," she said. "Hands. Count them."

They raised hands to send envoys under a parley flag—to *open discussion* of the crown's terms, nothing binding, merely inquiry, merely asking, merely a door held ajar. I counted as they rose across the square, torch by torch, and the number climbed past half and kept climbing, and I watched Wendell Kavanagh watch her own price being set by people she had crossed a province to protect, and her face did not change at all, which was somehow worse than tears.

Only three hands stayed down that I could see for certain: Corren's, fists clenched at his sides as if holding himself down; Mother Ilse's, arms folded, glaring the vote into shame it did not feel; and Brannoc's, raised high in furious opposition, alone on his wagon like a man hailing a ship that has decided to sink.

"There," Wendell said softly, when the shout went up and the vote was called—*aye*, a forest of grim, apologetic hands, *the ayes carry it, envoys at dawn*. "Considered. That's the word. They'll all be able to live with 'considered.'"

She stepped out of the doorway then. Into the light. Into the middle of the square, with two hundred faces turning toward her one by one, the murmur dying in ripples, and I swear on my brother's grave I moved to stop her and my feet refused, because some part of me understood that this was a gatekeeper doing the one thing she knew how to do: standing in the opening.

"Envoys at dawn," she said, in the level voice that had logged ten thousand safe passages. "Good. Send them. But know what you're sending them to discuss." She lifted her chin at the square, at the children, at the cookfires, at me. "The crown offered you all free, if you hand it one woman. Fine. Here is what your arithmetic missed: the crown does not release what it catches. It *consumes* it. Amnesty is a menu. Every gifted soul in this camp who walks through that gate believing themselves pardoned will simply be eaten in a different room." Her voice did not rise. It lowered, if anything, until the whole square leaned in to hear, the way crowds lean toward wells. "I don't blame you for considering it. I'd have considered it too. But if you open that door, understand that you aren't trading me away. You're telling the crown which of you it may take next, and in what order, and it will be grateful, and gratitude is not a thing it knows how to feel."

Silence, total, the kind that has weight.

And then, from the back of the square, a sound I will hear until I die: a single pair of hands beginning to clap. Not applause—counting. Slow, deliberate claps, one per name, and the boy Doss's clear river-voice carrying over them: "That's one. That's two. That's three." Counting the camp, aloud, the way you'd count soldiers before a march—except everyone understood he was counting something else. Counting the cost. Counting what each hand raised tonight might weigh come spring.

Wendell found me in the doorway with her eyes. *Well,* that look said. *Now you know the price they set.*

No, I thought, pushing off the wall, shouldering through the crowd toward her, the torches, the wagon, the vote that had just sold her and the boy who had just unsold her with nothing but numbers. Now I know the price *they* set.

Tomorrow I intended to show them mine.

Because while they had been weighing one woman against their children, I had remembered something the proclamation's fine print had almost made me miss—something in the phrasing, *alive and unspoiled*, something only a man who had grown up hearing the crown's true voice through his brother's mouth would catch—and it meant the usurper was not bargaining from strength at all.

It was bargaining from hunger. And hungry things make mistakes.
