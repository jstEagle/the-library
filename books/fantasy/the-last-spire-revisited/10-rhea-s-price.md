## 10. Rhea's Price

The parley flag was white with a black keel stitched in the corner, and I knew it the way you know a knife by its handle—before you see the blade.

I was on the wall of Ashmarket's eastern ruin, sharing a watch rotation with a boy whose gift was making bread rise twice as fast as it should. He'd been telling me about his mother's bakery in Vell when the lookout shouted, and then we were both staring down at the salt flats where the crown's banners had burned a week ago. A single rider walked her horse across the white ground, slow as a funeral procession, that rag of surrender snapping above her like something ashamed of itself.

"Captain Voss," I said, and my mouth went dry around the name.

"You know her?" the baker boy asked.

"I taught her to read tide charts." I turned from the wall before he could see my face do whatever my face was doing. "Get Aldric."

He ran. I stayed, because running felt too much like what I'd been doing since they dragged me out of the Thornfell in chains—moving away from things instead of toward them. Below, Rhea Voss stopped her horse at the edge of bowshot and waited, hands visible on the reins, the way she used to wait at my gatehouse with contraband silk and a smile full of arithmetic.

Aldric arrived in under a minute, which told me he'd been sleeping in his boots again. Sefa came behind him, and behind her a knot of rebels with opinions and crossbows. The camp had been fracturing for three days—not over whether to fight, but over whether I was worth the fighting. Word of the crown's offer had spread the way rot spreads through a ship's hold: quietly, evenly, into every timber. Amnesty for all gifted, if the exiled prince knelt and handed them one gatekeeper. Some of them had looked at me differently after that. Not with hate. Worse—with weighing scales behind their eyes.

"Parley flag," Aldric said. "We're bound to hear her out."

"We are," I agreed, "and I'm going down there."

"Wendell."

"She didn't come for you." I was already moving toward the stair. "She came for me. It'd be rude to make her shout across a salt flat about it."

* * *

They let me walk out alone. That was its own kind of message, and I chose to read it as trust rather than indifference, because the alternative reading would have kept me up all night, and I'd had enough sleepless nights to last until the next war.

The salt crunched under my boots. Rhea swung down from her horse when I was ten paces out, and up close she looked wrong in ways that took me a moment to catalogue. Thinner. Gray at the temples she used to dye. Her left hand wrapped in dirty linen. And her eyes—Rhea Voss's eyes had always been the sharpest instruments on the Salt Road, quick and amused and always counting. They were still sharp. But the amusement was gone, and what was left looked like a woman who'd been counting for a long time and hadn't liked the sum.

"Gatekeeper," she said.

"Captain." I crossed my arms. "Come to finish the job? The soldiers missed a few ribs."

Something moved behind her face—flinched, maybe, or flared. With Rhea it had always been hard to tell the difference. "I'm not here under their flag."

"No," I said. "You're here under a bedsheet with your own sigil on it, which is very nearly the same thing, given how many crowns' cargo you've carried."

"Careful." But there was no edge in it. That frightened me more than an edge would have. Rhea without edges was Rhea without ballast.

"Careful?" I heard my voice climb and made myself stop, breathe, start again lower. "You want to talk careful? I kept your routes off every ledger for nine years, Rhea. Nine years. Every patrol schedule, every ward rotation, every blind spot in the Thornfell passes—I wrote them down in cipher and burned the copies and never once asked you what you were carrying or for whom. I made myself complicit in everything that came through my gate because it was you, because we were—" I stopped. Swallowed. "Because of what we were."

"What were we?" she said quietly.

And there it was. The question underneath all of it, the one I'd been avoiding since the salt flat first showed me her silhouette. We had never been lovers, exactly, though there had been a winter in the gatehouse that lived in my memory like embers under ash. We had been something rarer and less nameable: two women who trusted each other precisely because neither could afford to trust anyone else. Transactional, yes. Every kindness between us had come with a ledger line attached. But I had believed—I had needed to believe—that the ledger had a floor somewhere. That there were entries even Rhea wouldn't make.

"That's the question, isn't it," I said. "Because from where I stood, chained in a prisoner column watching a boy get shot off a wagon, I thought we were the kind of friends who don't sell each other to the crown."

Rhea looked away. Out at the mountains, at the Spire's distant gray finger against the sky. When she spoke, her voice had gone flat, the way voices go when someone has rehearsed honesty so long it's worn smooth.

"The conscription edict came in early spring. Every ship over forty tons, every crew, pressed into the crown's coastal fleet as transport. You know what the crown does with smuggler crews, Wendell. It doesn't make them sailors. It makes them ballast. My people—Marta, who's been with me since she was fourteen. Old Joss with his bad lungs. The Ferro twins, who are children, Wendell, whatever else they are." She flexed her bandaged hand. "The harbormaster offered me a trade. One name, verified, and my crew stays flagged as private contractors. Crown business, but mine. My deck, my rules."

"So you gave him my name."

"I gave him your gift." Her jaw tightened. "There's a difference. I told myself there was a difference. I said, she's clever, she's careful, she'll hide it better than that, she's survived worse than a few crown riders." A short, ugly laugh. "Ten years keeping other people's secrets and I've gotten good at lying to myself. It's the one cargo I can always move."

"Why?" The word came out of me raw. "You had other names. Half the free cities is crawling with hedge-witches and storm-readers. Why mine?"

"Because yours was worth a whole crew, and theirs weren't. Because the keeper of the Last Spire, gifted—that's not an informant's rumor, that's a prize. And because—" She stopped. Started again. "Because I thought you'd survive it. I needed you to survive it. I built the whole lie on top of that, Wendell. I put you on a cart headed for the capital because I couldn't bear to put Marta on a ship headed for the same place."

The wind came off the flats, carrying salt and old ash. Somewhere behind me, on the wall, I knew thirty pairs of eyes were watching two small figures on white ground, and I hated every one of those eyes, and I understood every one of them too. This was what the crown did. It didn't just hunt the gifted. It made everyone into a price, and then it made everyone into a merchant.

"Do you know what they did on the road?" I asked. "Between the pass and here. There was a boy. Fifteen, maybe. His gift flared when they were binding his wrists—just a little warmth, just enough to melt his ropes—and a sergeant shot him off the wagon like a rabid dog and the column cheered." I stepped closer. "That's what my name bought, Rhea. That column. Those ropes. That boy."

"I know." Her voice cracked on the second word, and she let it. "I've known since the day I signed the paper. I've been drinking to forget and drinking to remember and mostly just drinking. And then the summons came—the amnesty offer, kneel-and-deliver—and I realized the harbormaster's protection only runs until the crown decides my crew is worth more than my name. So." She reached slowly into her coat, and I didn't move, and some part of me noted clinically that I should have moved, that a smarter woman would have moved. She drew out a folded packet, oilcloth-wrapped, and held it out on her open palm. "Coastal charts of the capital approach. Harbor defenses, current strength, the crown fleet's rotation schedules—which I know intimately, having spent a month pretending to ferry supplies for them. Tide tables for the estuary narrows. And this." From the packet's fold, a slip of paper. "The palace's water-gate code. Changed last month. A guard captain sold it to me for wine and flattery, because nobody guards secrets against a smuggler they think is bought."

I didn't take it. "Why bring this to us? To me?"

"Because you're the only person alive who knows what I am and hasn't tried to hang me for it yet." She almost smiled. Almost. "And because I did the arithmetic, finally. All of it. The crown eats people, Wendell. I've seen the ports it owns. There won't be ships to sail or crews to save or free cities to run smuggling through if it wins. My loyalty was always transactional. Fine. Here's the transaction." She pushed the packet toward me. "My seven ships, my crews, everything I have that floats—for your cause. Not for forgiveness. I know the difference. Forgiveness isn't for sale, and I couldn't afford it anyway."

For a long moment I just looked at her. At the gray roots and the bandaged hand and the packet sitting on her palm like an offering on an altar to a god who owed her nothing.

"You could have brought this to the crown instead," I said. "The water-gate code alone. They'd have made you an admiral."

"They'd have made me a tool. Same thing, different pay." She shrugged. "Besides. You'd have beaten me to betraying them somehow. You were always faster than me at the important things. Just slower at knowing what they're worth."

* * *

I took the packet back to the wall, and I took Rhea with me, because leaving her standing on a salt flat while thirty armed people debated her fate seemed like a poor way to honor either our history or my judgment.

The council that followed was short and vicious. Aldric wanted her ships and didn't want her anywhere near command. Sefa argued that anyone willing to defect under a parley flag deserved a chance to prove it with more than paper. The rebel quartermaster, a stone-faced woman named Brakka who had lost a son to the crown's hunts, wanted Rhea hanged from the eastern arch and her charts read over her corpse. I listened to all of it from the back, arms crossed, saying nothing, which everyone correctly read as the loudest statement in the room.

Finally Aldric turned to me. "It's your name she sold."

"It is."

"Then say what you want done."

I looked at Rhea, seated on a crate near the door with her bandaged hand in her lap like a student awaiting punishment. She met my eyes. Didn't look away. Whatever else Rhea Voss was, she had never once flinched from a bill coming due.

Here is what I wanted. I wanted to hate her cleanly. I wanted the luxury of rage with no arithmetic in it, no *but*, no understanding. I had earned that much, hadn't I? Sold, marched, collared, betrayed by my apprentice and my oldest friend inside a single season. The world owed me one uncomplicated feeling, and fury was right there, ready to be had.

But Sefa's voice was in my head, steady as a heartbeat: *power shared is power doubled.* And underneath it, colder, my own: *so is grief.* If I fed this to my hatred, I'd be doing the crown's work for it. Division by a thousand cuts, each one named Rhea, each one Tam, each one every person who'd ever chosen survival over someone they loved. The usurper on its throne had built an empire out of exactly that.

So I chose the third path. The one that wasn't fury and wasn't forgiveness. The one that was just work.

"Her ships join the cause," I said. "All seven. Crews stay intact, under her flag, answering to Aldric's overall command but not to any rebel captain who thinks a reformed smuggler needs babysitting." I looked at Brakka. "And she carries the water-gate code to the assault planners herself, in front of witnesses, so if it's false we know exactly whose neck to stretch."

"And if it's true?" Rhea asked quietly.

"Then when the capital falls, you get what you actually came for." I held her gaze. "Not absolution. A berth. You sail with us afterward. You rebuild what the crown burned, starting with Ashmarket's harbor, and you spend the rest of your life being useful to people who will never quite trust you, and you'll find, if you're lucky, that usefulness is its own kind of penance. I'd know."

Silence. Then Brakka spat on the floor—a verdict, not an argument—and stalked out. Sefa nodded once, the nod of a healer who has set a bone she suspects will ache forever. Aldric studied me for a long moment with an expression I was learning to read: the look of a man recalibrating how dangerous the stubborn gatekeeper actually was.

"Done," he said.

* * *

Rhea found me at dusk on the wall, where I'd gone to be alone and failed, because she had always been able to find me the way water finds cracks.

"Marta says thank you," she said. "The crew doesn't know yet. I'll tell them tonight."

"Don't thank me. I didn't forgive you."

"I know." She leaned on the parapet beside me, shoulder a hand-width from mine, closer than she'd stood in years and not close at all. "What was it, then? What do you call it, when someone burns your ledger and puts you back to work?"

I watched the sun go down behind the Thornfell, behind the Spire, behind everything I'd been and everything I'd failed to protect. The mountain was quiet tonight. Or perhaps I was simply learning to listen past its grief.

"I call it the only move that beats her," I said. "The crown. She wants us all turned into prices. Every friendship, every loyalty, every kindness—converted to currency, traded, spent. So we refuse the exchange rate." I glanced at her. "Your ships aren't payment for what you did. Nothing is. They're a down payment on what comes next. That's the only ledger I'm keeping anymore."

Rhea was quiet a while. When she spoke, her voice was rough. "Nine years, and you've become someone who talks like that. I'm sorry I missed it."

"You didn't miss it. You cost it." I pushed off the parapet. "Same coin, though. Come on. If your charts are half as good as your lies, we've got an assault to plan—and I need you to tell me everything you know about the man who wears the crown."

At that, Rhea went very still.

"That," she said slowly, "is why I really came. The harbormaster wasn't the only one who made me an offer. Something made me another one. Through a mouth that wasn't its own."

The salt wind picked up along the wall, and somewhere below us, the sea beyond the ruins sighed against the broken harbor stones, patient as anything that has all the time in the world.

"It talked to you," I said.

"On my own ship," said Rhea Voss, "in my own cabin, in the dark. In a voice I'd have sworn belonged to a dead friend."
