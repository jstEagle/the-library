## 11. The King in the Mirror

I had a speech prepared for the night I finally told someone about my brother. I'd been drafting it for ten years—revising it in the margins of marches and mutinies, polishing it like a soldier polishes a boot he'll never actually wear to court. In the good versions, I told it to a war council of hardened rebels who wept and swore vengeance. In the honest versions, which I never let myself keep past dawn, I told it to no one at all, because there was no version of that story that didn't end with me kneeling in the mud outside my brother's door, holding an unlit lamp, doing nothing.

The mud was real. The rest, I could have invented.

It happened in Ashmarket, three nights after Rhea Voss signed her ships over to a cause she claimed to despise, on the evening when the camp finally stopped looking at Wendell Kavanagh like a bargaining chip and started looking at her like something worse: a person with a plan. Sefa had set up her healing tents along the aqueduct's broken spine. Tam—the boy, the apprentice, the crown's hollow-eyed ward turned penitent shadow—had taken to sitting near our fires without being invited, saying nothing, mending gear that wasn't broken. And I had found myself, for the first time since exile, sleeping more than four hours a night.

Which is, of course, exactly when you should be suspicious.

Wendell found me on the wall.

Not the old city wall—that was rubble and gull bones—but the rebel palisade we'd raised along its footprint, pine trunks sharpened at the top, ugly as honesty. She climbed the ladder with the particular gracelessness of a woman who has spent her life walking on stone that tells her where to put her feet and resents any surface that won't cooperate. She arrived at my shoulder carrying two cups of Sefa's willowbark tea, which tastes like punishment and works like mercy.

"You're on watch," she said.

"I'm thinking."

"On watch," she repeated, and handed me a cup anyway. "Thinking is how people fall off walls."

"Gatekeepers don't fall off walls?"

"Gatekeepers have stones under them. Stones catch you." She looked out over the dark valley, where the fires of our little doomed army made constellations on the ground. "You've been circling me for three days, Aldric. Like a dog deciding whether the thing in the corner is a rat or a snake."

"That's flattering."

"I've been waiting for you to bite or bark. You've done neither. So." She drank. Grimaced. "Either tell me what's eating you or stop watching me sleep through the tent canvas. It's unbecoming in a prince."

"I'm not a prince. Princes have kingdoms."

"You have a brother on a throne and a face everyone in this camp pretends isn't royal. Close enough." She turned and looked at me directly, and I understood then why smugglers trusted her with their lives and why the crown wanted her chained: there is no performance that survives being seen by Wendell Kavanagh. She doesn't read faces. She reads whatever's underneath them, the way I imagine she reads stone—weight, fracture, the places where pressure has been applied too long.

So I told her. Not the polished speech. The mud one.

* * *

"My brother Edran was crowned nineteen years ago," I said. "He was twenty-two. Our father died in autumn, of the lung-fever, and by midwinter Edran sat the throne wearing our grandfather's crown, and everything was—normal. That's the part nobody believes. For six years, everything was normal. He taxed stupidly, hunted badly, laughed at his own jokes. A perfectly ordinary king. I used to write him letters complaining about it."

Wendell said nothing. She had the patience of someone accustomed to listening to mountains take their time.

"The seventh year, he changed." I turned the cup in my hands. "Not all at once. Kings change; power pickles them; I'd seen it before and so had everyone. But this was different. It was small things first. He stopped blinking. Did you know that? There's a thing a face does—a rhythm—and his lost it. Courtiers thought it was dignity. Then he stopped dreaming. He told me that himself, laughing, delighted: *Aldric, I haven't dreamed in a month. Think of the hours saved.* And then the edicts began. The registries. The first quiet disappearances of gifted children from the eastern provinces, explained away as plague, as flight, as anything but what they were."

"And you knew?"

"I suspected. Which is worse." The words came out flat and bitter, ten years steeped. "Because here's what I actually did, Wendell. Here's the part I've rehearsed telling you and never once managed aloud. Nine years ago I went back to the capital. Under a false name, in a merchant's train, because by then the stories were bad enough that I couldn't sleep either. I got into the palace—I still don't entirely know how; bribery and a coat that fit badly. And I saw him. My brother. Alone, in his chambers, at night."

I had to stop. Not because the memory failed, but because it was too well-preserved. Ten years, and I could still smell the tallow candles and the cold hearth and the strange sweet-rotten scent that hung around the crown itself, like flowers left too long in a sealed room.

"He was standing at the window," I said, "and he was crying. Silently. Both hands pressed flat against the glass like a man drowning behind it. And when he heard me—when he turned—he said my name. Just once. *Aldric.* And it was him. His voice, his grief, my brother, looking out at me through eyes that were not his own. Do you understand? They moved right. Everything else was Edran. The eyes belonged to something else, something old, and it was using him like a glove."

"What did you do?" Wendell asked softly.

"What do you think? What does a coward do?" I laughed, and hated the sound. "I asked questions. *Edran, what's happening, what can I do.* Sensible questions. Questions a man asks when he wants the world to be a problem he can solve by morning. And my brother—wept harder, and fought, I could see him fighting, some part of him surfacing like a swimmer—and he said four words to me. I have carried them for nine years like a knife between my ribs."

I made myself say them. Out loud. To another living person. First time.

"He said: *Bring me a knife.*"

Wendell's breath caught.

"And I stood there," I said, "in my stolen coat, in my brother's room, with a belt-knife at my hip, and I did nothing. Because if I cut him—if I killed the king—what then? Civil war. Succession chaos. And worse, underneath, the thought I've never confessed to anyone until now: *what if it doesn't work?* What if the thing in the crown just moves to the next head, and my brother dies for nothing, and I'm a kingslayer and a fool? So I hesitated. I hesitated for maybe thirty seconds. Thirty seconds of a man begging his brother for a knife. And then his eyes—" my hand rose without my permission, toward my own face, "—they changed. The swimmer went under. And the thing wearing him smiled with Edran's mouth and said, in a voice like a well, *Your brother sends his love,* and called the guard."

"They took you?"

"They escorted me out. Politely. That was the message, you see—it wanted me to know it had seen me, catalogued me, found me harmless. And it was right. I ran. I've been running for nine years, gathering an army, telling myself I'm building toward liberation, and every night the truth is smaller and heavier: I had one chance, knife in reach, my brother begging, and I chose my own skin and my own certainty that there must be a cleverer way." I drained the terrible tea. "There wasn't a cleverer way. He's been drowning for nine years while I organized committees."

* * *

Silence, on the wall. Wind moving through the pines below, carrying woodsmoke and the distant sound of Sefa singing to a feverish child—one of the low, wordless songs she used when medicine needed help.

I braced for pity. Pity I had armor for. Or horror—horror I could redirect into purpose, *yes, and so we must act.*

What Wendell gave me instead was worse. Recognition.

"When I was sixteen," she said, "there was a rockslide above a smuggler camp in the Grey Teeth. Fourteen people asleep. I felt it coming—felt it for an hour, the mountain grinding its teeth, the fault shifting—and I said nothing."

"Why?" The question came out gentler than I intended.

"Because the camp belonged to a crew that had cheated Rhea on a cargo split, and I was seventeen months into learning that loyalty meant keeping your mouth shut, and because—" her jaw worked, "—because I told myself I might be wrong. Stones are never wrong, Aldric. I knew I wasn't wrong. I lay awake in my bedroll feeling fourteen heartbeats' worth of weight gathering above them, and I constructed reasons not to move. And in the morning I dug bodies out of the scree with my bare hands, and I told Rhea I'd arrived too late to warn anyone, and she believed me because she wanted to, and I have kept that lie for twenty years. Buried it deeper than the dead." She looked at me. "So. You stood in front of your brother for thirty seconds and did nothing. I lay in the dark for an entire hour. Don't ask me which of us carries it worse. We both know the answer is *neither*, because guilt isn't a competition, it's just a weight, and weights don't care who holds them."

"It should have been you on that mountain," I said, meaning the Spire, the gift, the whole improbable shape of her. "Someone who understands that hesitation kills."

"No. It should have been neither of us. That's the lesson nobody teaches." She set down her cup. "Here's what I learned digging bodies out of scree, Aldric. Shame is only useful twice: once, to teach you; once, to point you. After that it's just rot, and it eats the hand that holds it."

"And yours? Has it pointed?"

"It pointed me at that gate. Every ward I strengthened, every route I mapped, every winter I stayed alone in the cold rather than come down and be known—it was penance dressed up as duty. I told myself I was keeping the kingdom safe." Her mouth twisted. "Mostly I was keeping myself safe. From being seen by anyone who might look closely enough to find the girl under the scree."

* * *

We stayed on the wall a long time. Somewhere past midnight, the clouds tore open and let the moon through, and the valley below went silver and blue, our campfires shrinking into embers scattered across a dark cloth. It would have been beautiful if you didn't know what it cost to gather that many desperate people in one place.

"You know the worst part," I said eventually. "Of the Edran story."

"There's a worst part beyond the rest?"

"He begged me, Wendell. My brother—who taught me to swim, who lied to our father so I wouldn't be flogged for breaking the chapel glass, who wrote me letters full of terrible jokes—surfaced for one moment inside his own body, in agony, and asked his little brother for a knife. And what I gave him was a conversation." I shook my head. "When we take the capital—when we stand in that throne room—I need to be the man who doesn't hesitate. I need to know, going in, that when my brother looks at me and begs, I'll give him what he asks. Can you march with a man planning regicide?"

"I can march with a man planning mercy," Wendell said. "They wear the same coat."

She moved closer on the narrow wall-walk, shoulder almost touching mine, and the warmth of her was absurdly distracting for a conversation about fratricide. "Tell me the true thing, though. Not the tactical thing. When you picture it—when you picture freeing him—what do you see?"

I hadn't let myself picture it. That was the discipline of nine years. But she asked, and something in me had apparently decided she was owed answers, so the image came whether I willed it or not: Edran at that window, hands flat against the glass, tears running down a face I loved, and this time the door opening, and this time—

"This time I cross the room," I said quietly. "This time I don't ask questions. This time I hold him while it happens, if it must happen, so the last thing he feels isn't the thing in his head but his brother's arms. And if there's any way—any way at all—to pull him free instead, to unbind him alive, then by every god my father pretended to worship, I want that way. Even if it costs everything."

Wendell went very still beside me. Stillness, I had learned, was her tell. Wendell Kavanagh moved constantly—hands, weight, eyes—except when something struck bone.

"Aldric," she said, and her voice had changed, careful, the gatekeeper's voice, the one she used for reporting structural damage, "at the Last Spire. The vault beneath the threshold. There were inscriptions. Founders' script. I've been translating fragments since I was twelve years old, and tonight—" She stopped. Started again. "Tonight, hearing you say *unbind him alive*—some of those fragments rearranged themselves. About what the crown was forged *from*. Where it has to be unmade."

"Where?"

Her eyes came up to mine, moonlit and grave, and I watched her decide to spend a secret she'd been hoarding longer than I'd been hoarding mine.

"The gate," she said. "The crown was forged to seal the Last Spire's gate—and it can only be unbound at the gate it was forged to seal. By a keeper willing to pour their gift into the wards." A pause, barely a heartbeat, but I heard what lived inside it. "And burn it out forever."

Below us, Sefa's song ended. The wind picked up, rattling the palisade, carrying from the far passes a sound I would learn to dread before the week was out: hoofbeats, many, moving fast through the Thornfell night.
