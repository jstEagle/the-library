## 12. The Traitor's Gate

I had been a keeper of gates for eleven years, and in that time I had learned one iron truth: no wall ever fails from the outside. Walls fail from the inside. They rot at the hinges. They are opened by hands that know them.

So when the screaming started an hour before dawn, I did not run toward the pass. I ran toward the man who was supposed to be watching it.

His name was Corren Hale, and he'd been with us since Ashmarket—a broad, quiet man who mended bridles and wept once, drunk, about his sister in the capital. I had liked him. That is my confession and my wound: I liked him, and liking him, I never watched him.

He wasn't at the pass. He was already gone down it, and behind him, coming up through the dark like a river finding its old bed, were torches. Dozens of them. Crown riders in their grey-and-gold, and above them on the ridge line, banners snapping in the cold wind off the Thornfell.

"They're in the high road," someone shouted—Dara, one of Sefa's students, her voice cracked down the middle. "Wendell, they're *in*—"

"I can hear them." The mountain could too. Through my gift I felt the drumbeat of hooves and boots in the stone, felt the tremor of the traitor's gate swinging wide on its ancient hinge, and beneath all of it, faint as a scar, something cold and wrong moving up the pass. Not a horse. Not a man.

The crown itself had sent its regards.

"Aldric," I said, and found him already beside me, sword half-drawn, hair loose, looking like a portrait of a king someone had left out in the rain. "Corren opened the pass."

"I know."

"You knew?"

"I suspected. He asked me last week where we'd go if the camp fell." Aldric's jaw tightened. "I thought he was being careful. Turns out he was being thorough—for someone else."

There was no time for recrimination, which was fortunate, because I had a list and it was long. There was only time for fire.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about your home burning: you spend the first minutes cataloguing. The cook-tent, gone. The archive tent with three years of rebel correspondence, gone. The pen where we kept the mules—I heard the poor creatures shrieking before Dara cut them loose—and gone, or going, a column of black smoke climbing into the stars like the mountain itself had taken offense and decided to exhale.

"Get the gifted out first," Sefa said at my elbow, soot already streaking her brown face white in two clean stripes where she'd pushed her sleeves back. She had a boy under each arm like she was made for exactly this. "Wendell. *Listen.* Gifted first. They'll burn us for what we are before they burn us for what we've done."

She was right, and I hated how right she was, and I obeyed.

Aldric took twenty fighters to hold the camp's mouth while we funneled people into the deep roads—those old smuggler tunnels Rhea had mapped for us, back when loyalty meant something more negotiable. I stood at the tunnel mouth doing arithmetic I despised: counting heads, marking the missing, watching the firelight turn every running figure into a silhouette from some illustrated end-of-the-world.

That's when I saw Tam.

He was not running. He was standing among the soldiers.

* * *

I want to tell you that I recognized him instantly, that some keeper's instinct or some older, softer thing cried out his name. But the truth is uglier. What I saw first was a crown ward—the grey livery, the silver collar-band, the face like a window with the shutters nailed shut from inside. I'd seen four of them by then, prisoners the rebels had freed or captured, and every one wore the same look: present, breathing, and profoundly elsewhere. The crown didn't kill its wards. It hollowed them, poured out whatever it didn't need, and used the rest as vessels. Sefa said it was like meeting someone through frosted glass. She was generous. It was like meeting someone's coat.

Then he turned toward the firelight, and the light caught the scar on his brow—the one from falling out of my storeroom loft at fifteen, stealing dried apples, swearing up and down it was rats.

"Tam." His name came out of me before any sense could stop it.

He looked at me. I will spend the rest of my life trying to describe that look and failing. His eyes found mine and there was nothing behind them—no guilt, no recognition, no boy who had once sat on my doorstep in the rain because home was worse than wet. Just a body wearing orders.

Behind him, a sergeant barked something, and Tam raised his crossbow.

The bolt went past my ear close enough to part my hair and buried itself in the tunnel frame. And still his face didn't change. Somewhere inside that borrowed calm, maybe, the boy was screaming. Or maybe the boy was simply gone, and what remained was a weapon with my apprentice's handwriting on it.

"Ward!" the sergeant snapped. "Take her!"

Three soldiers moved. Tam moved fastest, because wards move like clockwork, all precision and no hesitation, and suddenly he was in front of me, blade out, and the world had narrowed to the width of a killing stroke.

I could have killed him. Let me be clear about that, because later, when people told this story—and they would tell it, stories get around camps faster than plague—they'd want it to have been impossible, a miracle of restraint. It wasn't. He left his flank open. He fought like something that had never been taught fear, and things that have never been taught fear fight stupid against someone who has. I had a knife at his throat in six heartbeats. One push. The whole war simplified itself down to the weight of my hand.

And the thing was—he didn't flinch. Didn't beg. Didn't even blink. Whatever the crown had left in there understood death the way a door understands closing.

"Tam," I said again, quieter. "You stole my apples. You blamed the rats. You were a *terrible* liar."

Nothing. The blank eyes. The pulse jumping under my knife, fast but steady, the pulse of something that did not care whether it stopped.

I let him live.

Not because I'm good. Don't mistake me—I have no patience for the version of this story where mercy comes haloed. I let him live because in the moment my hand decided, I realized I couldn't tell anymore which of us the crown had hollowed. If I cut his throat, I did the usurper's work for it, tidily, free of charge. Every gifted life spent, every ward destroyed, every keeper turned executioner—it was all just the crown eating the realm with our own teeth. I would not be a tooth.

So I hit him instead. Pommel to the temple, hard enough to drop him, not hard enough to break him, and I stepped over his body and ran.

The choice marked me anyway. Mercy always does. It's not a clean wound; it's a brand. From that night forward I knew something about myself I hadn't known at the Spire gate, something I liked considerably less: I would always pay the cost of sparing. Other people would bleed for my scruples eventually. That's the ledger mercy keeps, and it never balances.

* * *

We lost nineteen dead and eleven taken in the burning of Ashmark camp—which is what the survivors took to calling it, though it had never had a name before; camps don't earn names until they're graves. We lost the archives, the stores, two-thirds of our horses, and any illusion that we were hidden.

What we kept: forty-three gifted, give or take, most of them children of the hunt's worst months. What we kept: Sefa, who spent the night after the fire walking the treeline and healing strangers until her hands shook so badly she couldn't lace her own boots, and refused help with a look that could have cauterized a wound. What we kept: Aldric, bleeding from a graze along his ribs he insisted was "a scratch" in the tone men use for wounds that require stitching.

And what we kept, without meaning to, without wanting to, was a question.

It came up around the second watch, in the cave we'd crawled into off the deep roads, while the fire was small and guarded and everyone pretended not to watch the dark mouth of the pass. A woman named Brannoc—stone-hands, a builder's gift, useful and unglamorous—said it plainly, because builders do.

"The ward you dropped. The young one. Why didn't you finish it?"

The cave went quiet the way water goes still.

"Because he's sixteen," I said.

"He's a crown ward. You've seen what they are."

"I've seen what they're *made into.*"

Brannoc shrugged, not cruelly—that was the worst of it, the reasonableness. "Kindness costs, keeper. Tonight kindness cost us Marta's brother, because that ward sounded the alarm on the east picket before anyone knew the pass was open. Maybe if he'd died at your feet, the next ward goes easier on us. Maybe not. But somebody should say the sum out loud."

Somebody should say the sum out loud. I looked around the fire—at Sefa, exhausted, saying nothing; at Dara, avoiding my eyes; at Aldric, watching me with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to, because I was afraid of what either of us would find in it.

"No," I said. "Nobody says the sum out loud. Because the moment we start pricing lives by how convenient their deaths are, we're not fighting the crown anymore. We're auditioning for it."

"It's not a philosophy, Wendell. It's arithmetic."

"Then find another accountant."

Brannoc held my gaze a moment longer, then nodded slowly, not in agreement—in filing. She would keep her own ledger, and my name was in it now, under a heading I didn't like. That's what mercy buys you. Not peace. A place in other people's sums.

Aldric found me later, sitting apart where the cave wall met my shoulder and the stone murmured its usual low comfort—*here, here, still here*, the mountain's idiot litany, the one thing that had never betrayed me.

"He's alive," Aldric said. No preamble. He'd stopped pretending he didn't know who I meant. "Your apprentice. Veyra's column pulled back west with their wounded. If he lived, the crown has him again."

"Or he's lying in a ravine with a headache and no army."

"And if he's with them?"

"Then he's with them." I pulled my knees up. "You want to ask me the same question Brannoc asked."

"I don't." He sat down beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, which was new, and which neither of us remarked on, which was also new. "I want to tell you something worse. Ten years ago, I had a knife at my brother's throat. Not a ward. My brother, with the crown on his head and something else behind his eyes. One push." His voice went very level, the way voices go when a man has rehearsed a fall. "I hesitated. Everyone paid for the hesitation since. So no, Wendell. I'm the last man alive entitled to question your arithmetic."

We sat there while the fire burned down to its red arithmetic, and the mountain hummed its one note, and somewhere west, a boy with my apprentice's face slept in a grey tent with a silver band at his throat, dreaming whatever the crown let him dream.

I should have felt absolved. I'd spared him; I'd defended the sparing; I'd been vindicated by a prince's confession. Instead I felt branded. Marked. The way the Spire's gate was marked—sealed, and waiting, and knowing that someday everything it held back would come due.

Because here was the thing I hadn't said aloud, not to Brannoc, not to Aldric, not to the stone: when my knife was at Tam's throat and his empty eyes looked through me, I had seen, just for a heartbeat, at the very bottom of all that hollowness—

something looking back.

Not the boy. Something older. Something patient. Something that had noticed me, the way a trap notices the foot.

The crown knew my face now. And in the morning, when we broke camp for the deep roads, I finally understood that we weren't running from Veyra's legion at all.

We were being driven. Herded down the mountain's spine, toward the one place in the world the crown could not follow but desperately needed us to open.

Toward the Last Spire.

Toward home.
