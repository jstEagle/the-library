## 13. Into the Hollow Kingdom

I have stitched men back together with thread made from my own hair, pulled fever out of a dying child like drawing a splinter, and once—do not ask me how, I have stopped asking myself how—held a man's soul in place for three days while his body remembered how to live. But I had never, until that night in the Thornfell's deep roads, watched someone spend themselves the way Wendell Kavanagh spent herself.

We fled the burning camp with what we could carry, which was to say: almost nothing. I carried my satchel of needles and salves, a skin of water, and a boy named Perrin whose arm I had set badly in the dark. Aldric carried his sword and his guilt, which weighed more. And at the head of our ragged line walked Wendell with her bare palm flat against the mountain wall, reading the stone like a priestess reading scripture, leading us down into roads that had not seen daylight since before the crown was forged.

"They're called the deep roads," she'd said, when Aldric balked at the black mouth of the pass. "The founders cut them when they built the Spire. Supply lines, escape routes, pilgrim ways. The maps were burned a century ago." She glanced back at us, and there was something in her face I didn't have a name for yet—not fear, not resolve. Something older than either. "The stones remember them even if no one does."

"Can you find the way?" Aldric asked.

"The way remembers itself," she said, which was not an answer, and we went anyway, because Veyra's riders were already cresting the ridge behind us with torches like a spilled constellation.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being a healer in a fleeing column: you learn everyone by their wounds before you learn them by their names. There was Maren, whose gift let her coax plants through frozen ground, who had burned the soles of her feet on hot coals during the camp attack and would not let me look at them because "others need you more." There was old Doss, who could taste lies on the air, who kept walking though a rider's blade had opened his side to the ribs—I closed it twice that first night, and both times he waved me off before I finished. And there was Perrin, twelve years old, gift unknown, orphaned in an afternoon, who held onto my belt with one small hand and did not cry once, which frightened me more than crying would have.

Wendell learned all their names too, though she never turned around to do it. She walked at the front with her hand on the wall and called out turns—"left here, mind the drop, low ceiling, duck"—in a voice gone rough as gravel. She hadn't slept since the camp burned. None of us had. But there is tired, and then there is whatever Wendell was doing.

By the second day I understood enough of her gift to be afraid for her. Stone-sense, she called it, when she deigned to call it anything at all—a keeper's knack for knowing what the earth knows. Feeling water through rock. Hearing the mountain's slow dreams. A small thing, she'd always claimed. A parlor trick. Useful for finding smugglers' caches and nothing more.

But the deep roads had collapsed in places. Ward-stones older than kingdoms flickered along the walls like guttering candles, and every time one failed ahead of us, Wendell poured something into it. Not touch, anymore. Not the little trick she'd described. She stood before dead ward-stones with her eyes rolled white and her breath coming in sobs, and the stone would kindle blue-white under her palm, and we would pass through a gate that had been sealed against us for a hundred years.

"You shouldn't be able to do that," Aldric told her, the second night. We'd stopped in a vaulted chamber where an underground river sang somewhere below us, and I was re-wrapping Maren's feet by the light of the rekindled wards. "Those are founder-work. That's beyond any keeper."

"It's not beyond," Wendell said. Her voice was thin. "It's just expensive."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," she said, with the particular patience people reserve for explaining things they don't want explained, "that the stone doesn't give its memory for free. You pay in the only coin you have." She looked at her own hands then, flexed them once, and I saw—because I am a healer, because seeing what bodies hide from their owners is my whole cursed trade—that her fingers trembled, and that the tremor wasn't fear.

It was depletion. Deep, structural depletion, the kind I had seen in gifted healers who worked past their limits and never fully came back. The kind that starts in the extremities and moves inward, toward everything that matters.

She caught me looking. Of course she did.

"Don't," she said quietly.

"I haven't said anything."

"You've said it with your whole face."

* * *

On the third day the road behind us began to die.

I felt it before anyone told me—a wrongness in the air, the way a room feels after a scream. Doss tasted it too. "Iron," he said, wrinkling his nose. "And horses. Lots of horses."

Wendell pressed both palms flat to the wall and went still for so long I thought she'd fainted. When she came back to herself, she was gray-faced and certain.

"Veyra's found the upper entrances. She's collapsing them behind her as she comes—no, wait." Her brow furrowed. "Not collapsing. Sealing. She's using gifted prisoners to seal them. Ward-craft, forced." Something moved across her face, quick and terrible. "She's spending them the same way I'm spending the stones. Except she doesn't care if they survive the transaction."

"How long?" Aldric asked.

"Two days. Maybe less. She has more fuel than I do." Wendell laughed, and it was the worst sound I heard on the whole march. "She has an army of fuel."

That was when I saw it happen—the thing I will carry until I die, the thing I still can't decide whether it was beautiful or obscene, though I suspect the answer is that it was both, the way a birth is both.

Ahead of us, the road ended.

Not blocked. Ended—a collapse, ancient and total, a hundred tons of mountain filling the passage like a cork in a bottle. Beyond it, according to Wendell's sense of things, lay the last three miles of deep road and the exit into the high Thornfell passes, where Rhea Voss's people supposedly waited with mules and mercy.

"We dig?" Aldric asked.

"We dig, we die here," Wendell said. "They seal the rear exits faster than we can open the front ones." She stood before the rubble with her head bowed, and I watched her shoulders rise and fall, rise and fall, the way a woman breathes before she steps off a cliff.

"Sefa." She didn't turn around. "You said power shared is power doubled. Back in the column. Did you mean it?"

"With my whole heart."

"Then hold onto Perrin. All of you hold onto something." She raised her hands, and the world changed.

I have tried, in the year since, to describe what it looks like when a person burns. Not flesh—I want to be clear about that; her skin never blistered, her hair never caught. It was deeper than flesh. Light came *out* of her. It came up through the soles of her boots and out through her fingertips and out of her open mouth when she spoke words in a language that predated every language I know, and the rubble before us didn't move so much as *remember moving*, stones grinding apart along seams a century old, the mountain recalling its own architecture because Wendell demanded it recall hers.

The passage opened. Blue light flooded the deep road. And Wendell Kavanagh stood swaying in the middle of it with blood running freely from her nose and both ears, laughing like a woman at her own funeral, saying, "Well. That's the last of the easy coin."

We got her walking. I won't pretend it was dignified—Aldric carried her half the time, and I fed her willow bark and honey-water and did everything I knew, which amounted to almost nothing, because there is no salve for this wound. This wound was in the part of her that isn't meat.

But here is what I saw, when I took her wrist to check her pulse, and here is why I write this down even now with my hands shaking:

Her gift was guttering. Like a lamp out of oil. I have touched a thousand gifts in my years of healing—felt them like warmth under the skin, each one shaped differently, each one alive. Hers was cold. Not gone. Cold the way a hearth is cold at dawn, with embers buried somewhere under ash, but thinner than it had been five days ago, and I am a healer, and I could do the arithmetic that she clearly refused to do.

Every ward she lit cost her. Every mile of road she held open cost her. And the Spire—the Spire and whatever she intended to do there, because I'd seen it in her face since Ashmarket, that terrible clarity—would cost her everything.

* * *

We came up into daylight on the fourth morning, into the high passes, into snow and wind and the astonishing, ordinary blue of sky. Behind us, far below, the mountain breathed dust from its sealed mouths. Somewhere in those dark roads, Veyra's forced warders were paying the same price Wendell paid, except they paid it on someone else's orders.

Wendell sat down hard in the snow and looked at her hands for a long time.

"Perrin," she said finally, and the boy came over, and she took his small hand in both of hers. "You feel that? What's left?"

He frowned. "Warmth? Like... like a banked fire?"

"That's right." She smiled, and it reached her eyes, mostly. "Fire keeps, if you feed it. Remember that. Whatever they tell you your gift costs—remember that fire keeps."

Then she looked up at me, and she knew. Of course she knew; she'd known since the vault chamber, since my face said it with its whole face. Healers don't get to lie with their faces. She nodded once, slowly, the way you nod at a diagnosis.

"Not yet," she said. "But soon. And Sefa—when it comes—"

"When it comes, I'll be there."

"No." She shook her head, and snow fell from her hair, and she was smiling again, that awful, brave, unbearable smile. "When it comes, you'll be saving everyone else. Promise me that instead. Promise me the living."

I promised. It was the hardest promise I have ever made, and I have made promises over open graves.

We were two hours into the high passes, following the cairns toward the smugglers' rendezvous, when Doss went rigid mid-stride and tasted the wind.

"Riders," he said. "Ahead of us. Flying no banner I know—and behind them, coming fast through the pass we just left—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Because over the eastern ridge, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who has never once been late, came General Veyra—alone, unarmed, hands open and raised, riding straight toward the woman whose kingdom she had been sent to burn.
