## 14. Veyra's Mercy

The prisoner smelled of woodsmoke and stubbornness, which in my experience was the standard cologne of the Thornfell rebels. He sat chained to the interrogation chair with his chin up and his blood running freely down one temple, and he looked at me the way men look at a storm they've decided not to be impressed by.

"You're the general," he said. "Veyra. They said you'd be taller."

"They say a lot of things about me." I pulled the second chair around backward and sat on it the wrong way, arms folded over the backrest, because I'd learned long ago that standing over a man makes him brave and sitting across from him makes him honest. "Most of them are flattering. All of them are wrong."

The tent was mine — command tent, campaign tent, whatever the quartermasters were calling it this week — pitched in the mud below the Ashmarket road where my legion had finally stopped chasing ghosts through the mountains and started catching them instead. One ghost, specifically. A scout taken at the edge of the burned city with rebel dispatches sewn into his boot heel, which was either very clever or very stupid depending on whether the dispatches were real. I hadn't decided yet. That was why he was still breathing.

That, and other reasons I didn't examine too closely.

"Name," I said.

"Does it matter? You'll write *rebel* on the report either way."

"It matters to me." This was true, though not for the reasons he assumed. Names were useful. A man with a name could be reasoned with; a man without one had already made himself a symbol, and symbols die badly. "Mine's Sera. The crown took the family name when it gave me the legion. You can have that information for free. Consider it a gift."

He blinked. Prisoners always blinked at that, as if a general admitting to a first name were some kind of trap. It wasn't. It was just true, and truth is cheaper than most people think.

"Corren," he said finally.

"Good. Corren." I let the silence stretch. Outside the tent canvas, the wind came down off the Thornfell carrying ash and pine and the faint mineral tang of old stone, a smell I knew better than my own heartbeat. Somewhere up there, past three days' hard march, was the Spire. Past the Spire was the hollow kingdom. And past everything, wearing a king like a glove wears a hand, was the thing we all pretended was merely a throne. "Here's how this goes, Corren. I ask questions. You answer them or you don't. If you don't, I don't do anything theatrical. No hot irons, no fingers, none of General Hask's poetry with the pincers. I'll simply conclude that you value your cause more than your comfort, note it in my report, and feed you. You'll be surprised how often that works."

"And if I answer?"

"Then I learn what I need, and you live to be someone else's problem later."

He studied me for a long moment. His eye had swollen half-shut from the scuffle at his capture, and the open one was quick and brown and far too intelligent for a boot-courier. "You're not what they said."

"No one ever is." I leaned forward. "Question one. Where is Aldric staging?"

"I don't know."

"Question two. How many gifted does he have?"

"I don't know."

"Corren."

"Genuinely," he said, and something in his face shifted — not defiance now but something rawer. "I'm a runner. I carry paper. They don't tell me where the fires are because runners talk when they're caught, and everyone knows it. What I know is routes and countersigns, and half the countersigns changed after the camp burned, so even those are probably worthless now." He swallowed. "You burned us out of the pass. Did you know that? Three hundred people asleep, and you burned us out."

"I know," I said.

My voice did something strange on those two words. Flat. Too flat. He heard it — quick people always hear it — and his brow creased, trying to work out what a crown general was doing sounding like that.

"The order came from the capital," I went on, before he could decide what to make of it. "Signed with the king's seal. Burn the camps, take the gifted alive, kill anyone who resists. Alive, Corren. Do you find that curious? Because I did. Armies ransom princes and nobles. Armies do not go to such lengths for hedge-witches and gatekeepers and boys who can light candles with a sneeze."

"Unless the crown's afraid of us."

"Afraid." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too short. "No. Not afraid. Hungry."

The word dropped between us like a stone into a well. I watched it fall all the way down and hit bottom, and I watched him understand, slowly, that I hadn't meant it as a figure of speech.

* * *

Here is what nobody outside the palace walls knows, and what maybe forty people inside them suspect: the crown is not a symbol. It never was. Oh, it's gold and old iron and seven points set with stones that no jeweler will admit to cutting, and kings have worn it for a hundred years and called themselves its masters. But crowns are forged, Corren, and forging requires fire, and fire requires fuel, and somewhere in the founding of that thing the smiths — or the thing that guided the smiths — got the recipe wrong. Or right, depending on whose side of the ledger you read.

It binds. That was its purpose. Bind the gate, bind the wards, bind the wild gifts of the north into something tame and lawful. And a binding wants to hold. It has held for a century. It holds Edran now, and Edran is failing, and a binding that cannot bear to let go will look for new hands to hold.

Gifted hands.

I learned this the way I learn everything: by listening where I wasn't supposed to. Twelve years in the legions buys you certain privileges, and the chief among them is being underestimated. Generals are furniture. Furniture hears everything. I stood behind Marshal Ottrek in the war room while the crown's own clerks — pale men with ink-stained cuffs and eyes that never quite focused — laid out the arithmetic of the hunt. Quotas. That's what they called them. Quotas of gifted, by province, by season, delivered to the capital under guard, entered into ledgers, and never, ever seen again.

"What happens to them?" I had asked. Just once. Just to see.

Ottrek didn't answer. One of the clerks did, without looking up, in a voice like dry paper: *"Sustenance."*

I have commanded executions. I have ordered fields burned and villages emptied and once, in the marsh war, a bridge full of refugees collapsed on my order because the enemy column was behind them and the mathematics of the day demanded it. I am not a soft woman. I want that understood. Whatever else you take from this account, take this: I did not flinch easily, and I flinched then. In front of the clerks. And the clerk looked up at me with those unfocused eyes and smiled, and I understood that he was not entirely a man either, that the crown's servants wear their own smaller bindings, and that I had just asked a question that furniture does not ask.

I saluted and left. And I did not sleep that night, or many nights since, and every gifted my soldiers bring me in chains sits in my tent and breathes at me, alive and warm and catalogued, and I think: *sustenance*, and I think: *how long before the ledger runs short of provinces, and the clerks come looking at their own army?* Because here is the joke, the beautiful rotten joke at the heart of the crown's great design: the gifted are not rare. We are everywhere. We are simply good at hiding.

I would know.

* * *

"Say that again," Corren said.

"Hungry," I repeated. "The crown eats. It has been eating for a hundred years, slowly, carefully, one mind at a time — the ones who wandered too close to court, the ones whose families couldn't buy protection, the ones who thought a royal summons was an honor. Every king since the Founding has worn it thinner. Edran—" I paused. Careful, Sera. Even here. "Edran is nearly empty. And an empty vessel draws harder. The quotas aren't punishment, Corren. Punishment ends. This is provisioning."

He stared at me. The blood on his temple had dried to a dull rust, and his hands, chained to the chair arms, had gone very still — the stillness of a man recalculating everything. "Why are you telling me this?"

Excellent question. I had been asking myself versions of it for three days.

Because he was the first person I'd said it aloud to? Because saying it aloud makes it real, and making it real is the first step off a ledge you've been standing on for a year? Because somewhere up in those mountains a woman named Kavanagh was doing — according to my scouts' increasingly frantic reports — something impossible to the wards of the Last Spire, and every soldier who deserted my column to follow her took a piece of my conscience with them?

"Because you're going to repeat it," I said. "To whoever sent you. To Aldric. To the gatekeeper, if you live long enough to meet her. I'm telling you because a message carried by a prisoner I released will travel faster and truer than any parley flag, and because—" I stopped. Started again. "Because when the crown finishes with the free cities, it will start on its own. On its legions. On its clerks. There is no *safe side* of this war, Corren. There is only the side that gets eaten last. I have spent twelve years believing I was on the safe side, and I am done lying to myself. It's undignified."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

I rose, crossed to the tent flap, and spoke to the sentry. "Water and bread for the prisoner. Then cut him loose at the picket line and point him west. If he's fool enough to come back, that's his business." Behind me I heard Corren's chains shift — surprise, mostly, and the particular creak of a man deciding not to hope too loudly.

"You're letting me go," he said. "Just like that."

"Just like that. Tell whoever sent you that General Veyra interrogated you for six hours and found nothing. Let them believe I'm thorough and blind. Both impressions serve me." I turned back. He was standing now, rubbing his wrists, watching me with that quick brown eye, and I saw the question in it before he found the courage for it.

"You're gifted," he said quietly. "Aren't you. That's why you know. That's why—"

"Get out of my tent, Corren."

He went. At the flap he paused — I felt him weighing some final word, some thanks or curse or offer — and then thought better of it, which showed sense, and slipped out into the grey afternoon. Through the gap in the canvas I watched the sentry hand him bread and water, watch him eat standing up like a man afraid the meal might be revoked, and then walk west, fast, not running, not quite.

* * *

I sat alone for a long time after.

The wind kept coming off the mountain with that stone-smell underneath it, and I let myself do the thing I almost never allowed: I reached, gently, the way you test ice, with the small quiet talent I had buried under twenty years of discipline and a general's cloak. Earth-sense. Nothing dramatic — no lightning, no levitation, nothing the clerks could put in a ledger with a straight face. Just the ground's slow gossip. Footfalls a mile off. The tremor of a buried stream. And lately, these past weeks, something else: a vast, patient attention rising out of the Thornfell itself, focused, deliberate, *aware* — the signature of a keeper working wards older than any kingdom, pouring herself into stone.

She was burning bright. Far too bright. I could feel her expenditure the way you feel a bonfire from across a valley, generous and reckless, and I understood with cold clarity what she was spending, and what it would cost, and that she knew the price and was paying it anyway.

*Sustenance,* whispered the memory of a dry paper voice, and for the first time in twelve years of service, Sera Veyra, general of the crown, marshal of the eastern reach, furniture of the war room, imagined a different report. One written in a different hand. One that began: *The enemy is not the rebellion. The rebellion is only the first door.*

Beyond the tent, a horn sounded — recall, dusk formation. My legion, eleven thousand strong, waiting for orders that would march them deeper into the mountains, toward the Spire, toward her, with instructions I had not yet written and did not want to write.

I picked up the quill. I held it over the dispatch form for a long moment, ink gathering at the nib like a held breath.

Then I turned the page over and began writing on the back.
