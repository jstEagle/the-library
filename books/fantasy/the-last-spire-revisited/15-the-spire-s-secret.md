## 15. The Spire's Secret

The mountain knew I was coming before I did.

I felt it three miles out, a low hum in my bones like a tuning fork struck against the world's teeth. Ten years I had kept the Last Spire's gate, and the stone had never once sung to me from this far away. It sang now because it was frightened. Or because I was. With my gift it was always difficult to tell where the mountain ended and I began, which was precisely the sort of thing that got people burned these days.

"Stop doing that," Sefa said, riding at my side. She'd learned to read my silences the way I read tremors—by feel.

"Doing what?"

"That thing with your face. Like you're listening to a conversation nobody else can hear."

"I am."

"And?"

"It's saying hello." I kept my eyes on the pass ahead, where the Thornfell folded itself into shadows the sun had never bothered to negotiate with. "It hasn't said hello to me in ten years. It usually just grumbles about weather and complains when Tam drags his heels on the stair."

Sefa was quiet for a moment. "Does it know why we've come?"

* * *

We made camp that night in the hollow below the gatehouse road, close enough that I could feel the Spire breathing above us—the slow, mineral patience of ten thousand tons of granite pretending not to be afraid. Aldric crouched over a map by firelight, drawing lines with a charred stick as though charcoal could outdraw fate.

"The crown will know we're here," he said. "Veyra's riders were two days behind us in the deep roads. Less now."

"Then we don't have long." I knelt beside him. "There's something I need to show you. Both of you."

Aldric looked up. In the firelight he had the look of a man who had spent ten years carrying a knife for his brother and praying never to use it. "Show me what?"

"The founders' vault."

His stick stopped moving. "There is no vault. The archives say—"

"The archives say the Spire was built as a fortress. The archives were written by people who thought the gate was built to keep things *out*." I stood, brushing ash from my knees. "It wasn't. Come with me and I'll show you what it was actually built to keep in."

* * *

The gate of the Last Spire had never opened for soldiers, smugglers, or kings. It opened for me because the mountain had spent ten years learning the shape of my gift the way a lock learns the shape of its key—and because, I suspected, it had been waiting.

I pressed both palms flat against the cold basalt and let myself fall into the stone's memory.

This is the part I have never been able to explain well to anyone who isn't gifted: reading stone is not seeing. It is *remembering on behalf of something that cannot forget*. The rock held every footstep ever set upon it, every oath spoken at its threshold, every drop of blood and rain. And beneath all of it, older than any of it, there was a door.

Not the gate. A door *behind* the gate, cut into the living rock, sealed so long ago that even the mountain had half-forgotten it. I found it now the way you find an old scar on your own body—by touch, by the slight wrongness of it, by the way the stone flinched when I asked it to open.

It opened.

The wall parted along a seam no mason's eye could find, and cold air breathed out of the dark, air that smelled of dust and iron and something older than either—something like the inside of a promise.

"Well," said Aldric, behind me. His voice was carefully level, which is how I knew it wasn't. "That's new."

* * *

The vault was small. That surprised me. I had expected grandeur—frescoes, thrones of black glass, the usual theater of ancient power. Instead there was a round chamber barely wide enough for the five of us, lit by veins of pale luminescence running through the ceiling like frozen lightning. The walls were carved floor to ceiling with script in a hand so old it predated the free cities' letters entirely.

And in the center, on a plinth of unpolished stone, lay a single object under a shroud of woven silver:

A crown.

Not *the* crown—that one sat on King Edran's head in the capital, wearing him the way a hermit crab wears a stolen shell. This was its twin. Its mold. The thing that had shaped it.

Aldric reached for it. I caught his wrist.

"Don't. Please." My voice came out smaller than I intended. "Let me read it first."

He stepped back. Sefa moved to the doorway with two of the gifted—a storm-caller named Bren whose hands crackled faintly even at rest, and a quiet girl called Maren who could still water and had saved six lives since Ashmarket. They took up positions without being asked. We had all learned, on the road, that in this company you guarded each other's backs before you were told to.

I laid my palm against the plinth.

The memory rose up through my arm like floodwater.

---

*I am the founder. I do not remember my name; the forging took it.*

*We built the crown to bind—not a king, but a hunger. There was a thing beneath the mountains in those days, old and patient and hollow, that ate kingdoms the way rot eats timber—from the inside, slowly, leaving the shape standing while the heart went soft. We could not kill it. Killing requires a death, and it did not die; it only changed hands, changed shapes, changed kings.*

*So we forged instead. Gold and grief and the last of our gifts, hammered into a circlet that would hold the hunger still—one mind wrapped around it forever, a warden-king, a living seal. We chose our strongest. He agreed knowing what it cost. The crown would bind the hunger, yes—but binding is not gentle. To hold a thing that devours, the binder must be devoured slowly in turn, fed upon, used up, replaced.*

*We built the gate to be its anchor. The wards in this mountain are the other half of the crown's geometry. As long as the gate holds, the crown holds. As long as the crown holds, the hunger sleeps.*

*But hear the last of it, keeper, because we hid this truth where only stone-sense could find it, where no usurper would ever think to look:*

*The binding can be undone. Not broken—broken is what the hunger wants; a shattered crown releases it whole. Unbound. Unwoven, thread by thread, back into the wards that first gave it shape. The gate can drink the crown dry—but only if someone pours their own gift into the wards to open them wide enough to drink.*

*All of it. Every thread of power they carry. Burned out forever.*

*The gate does not kill the giver. But nothing survives the burning empty except the body. What they were, they will not be again.*

*We are sorry. We searched forty years for another way.*

*If you are reading this, you are the keeper. We chose the word carefully. Keep what is entrusted to you—including, if it comes to it, the cost.*

---

I came back to myself on my knees. Someone was holding my shoulders. Sefa. Her healer's hands were warm and steady, and her face, above me, had gone carefully blank in the way of someone trying not to hope too loudly or grieve too soon.

"How long?" she asked.

"A few minutes."

"You screamed."

"I don't remember screaming."

"You screamed," Aldric said quietly.

I got up. My legs worked, mostly. I turned to face them, and I made myself say it plainly, because if I dressed it in softer language I would find the soft language convincing, and I could not afford to be convinced.

"The crown can be unwoven. Here. At this gate—it's the anchor point; the wards and the crown are two halves of the same working. But the wards need a keeper's gift poured into them to open wide enough. All of it. Everything I am." I swallowed. "Afterward I'd be—empty. Giftless. Alive, probably. But whatever I am now, I wouldn't be."

The silence lasted four heartbeats. I counted them against the mountain's pulse, because counting is what I do instead of panicking.

"No," Aldric said.

"It wasn't a request."

"No," he said again, louder, and the vault's old acoustics carried it around the walls like an accusation. "Absolutely not. We find another way. We assault the capital—we take Edran by force, we find some—"

"There is no other way. They searched forty years, Aldric. Forty years of founders stronger and cleverer than either of us, and the answer they carved into stone was *we are sorry*." I heard my own voice go flat and hard, the voice I used at the gate when smugglers argued tolls. "You want to assault the capital? Fine. Bring your rebels, bring Rhea's ships, bring Veyra if her conscience holds. You'll get within a mile of the throne room and the crown will reach into every gifted mind in your army and turn them inside out. Unless the gate drinks it first, it wins. It has already won for a hundred years. It's just eating slowly."

"Then we lose slower," he snapped, "but we don't feed the mountain the best chance we have!"

"Bren," I said, without turning around. "What happens to a storm-caller if the storm stops answering?"

Bren's voice came low and reluctant from the doorway. "Nothing. I'd just be... a man who remembers lightning."

"And if I burn out?" I finally faced Aldric. "I'll be a woman who remembers stone. That's all. I keep my memories. My mind. My—" I gestured vaguely at all of myself. "*Me*. Just not the part the mountain lent me."

"You don't know that."

"No. I don't." Honesty mattered here; lies would be cruelty dressed up as kindness. "The founders didn't know either. That's why they wrote *probably*. But I've read enough of this mountain's memory to know how it treats its keepers. It doesn't discard them. It just... lets them go."

"Letting you go is exactly what I'm refusing to do."

Something in my chest pulled tight at that, and I filed the feeling away for later examination, because now was not the time and the vault was definitely not the place.

"Aldric." I softened my voice, which somehow felt more dangerous than raising it. "Ten years ago your brother begged you for a knife, and you couldn't give it to him, and you've hated yourself every day since. Am I wrong?"

His jaw went white.

"If you take this choice from me—if you charge off to the capital on some doomed noble errand to avoid watching me pay a price I've already agreed to pay—you'll spend the rest of your life hating yourself twice as much. And worse, you'll have failed. Both of us." I stepped closer. "I am the keeper of this gate. That word means something. It means I keep what's entrusted to me. Including the cost."

"The cost isn't yours alone," Sefa said quietly, from the doorway. "You should know that before you decide anything, Wendell. Power shared is power doubled—you taught me that on the Road of Collars, though I taught it to you first. When you pour yourself into the wards, we'll be there. All of us gifted. We can shield you. Slow the burning. Maybe—*maybe*—leave something behind."

"Or maybe you'll all just burn alongside me."

"Maybe." Sefa smiled, tired and fierce. "Gifted folk have been dying for the crown's hunger since before either of us was born. Dying *for each other* is considerably better arithmetic."

* * *

We climbed back up to the gatehouse as dawn came gray through the pass. Below us the world was still dark, but above the peaks there was a thin line of gold like a wound in the sky's belly, and I felt the mountain's mood shift beneath my feet—an unease, almost an eagerness, as though it had waited ten thousand years for this conversation and grown impatient with the delay.

Aldric walked beside me in silence the whole way. At the top of the stair he stopped and gripped the railing, looking out over the free cities' distant smudge, and when he spoke his voice was rough.

"When I was a boy," he said, "Edran used to tell me the Spire was haunted. He'd stand at the bottom of this very stair and refuse to climb, and Father would laugh and say the ghosts were only old kings keeping watch." He paused. "He was right, in a way. Just wrong about which kings."

"Aldric—"

"One condition." He turned to face me fully, and his eyes were wet and furious and steady all at once. "When the day comes—and it will come soon, Veyra's less than a day behind us—I don't want you deciding alone in the dark. You tell me. You tell Sefa. You tell everyone. We do this together or not at all. If you sneak off to sacrifice yourself like some tragic heroine in one of Mother's ballads, I swear on Edran's head I will drag you back from the wards myself and do it *my* way, and my way involves significantly more shouting."

Despite everything, despite the vault and the carving and the founders' forty years of apology, I laughed. It came out cracked, but it was real.

"Agreed," I said. "Together or not at all."

He nodded once, sharp, and turned back toward the horizon.

And that was when I felt it.

Far below, down through the roots of the mountain, through miles of stone and older darkness, something stirred. Not the mountain's pulse—that was steady, familiar, mine. This was *other*. A cold attention turning toward us across the distance, vast and patient and hungry, like an eye opening at the bottom of a well.

The crown had felt me touch the vault.

It knew, now, what I knew.

And somewhere far to the south, wearing a king like a coat, it began to move.
