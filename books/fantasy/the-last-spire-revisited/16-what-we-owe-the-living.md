## 16. What We Owe the Living

I had commanded men in three wars before the exile, and I had learned one iron truth about command: the hardest order you ever give is the one you refuse to give yourself.

Wendell Kavanagh stood in the founders' vault with dust on her sleeves and certainty in her spine, and she told me—calmly, the way she'd tell me the weather was turning—that she intended to die at her own gate.

"No," I said.

She didn't even look up from the vault's ledger. "You'll want to argue. Argue later. I'm trying to read."

"I'm not arguing. I'm declining. There's a difference, and it matters to me."

That got her eyes up. Grey as slate in the lamplight, and just as likely to crack under pressure and bring the whole roof down on your head. "Aldric."

"Wendell." I folded my arms, which is a posture that has won me exactly zero arguments in my life but makes me feel marginally more like a prince and less like a man begging. "Tell me what the vault says again. Slowly. Because I've heard it twice now and both times it sounded like nonsense dressed up as prophecy."

"The crown was forged here." She tapped the ledger, old vellum gone the color of weak tea. "Forged *at* this gate, by the first keepers, to bind something out of a king who couldn't hold his own mind together. It worked. It worked too well. The binding turned around. Now the binding wears the king. And the only way to unmake a binding made at this threshold is to unmake it from this threshold—which means pouring a keeper's gift into the wards until the gift burns out." She said it like she was reading a supply manifest. "And since the gift is what keeps my heart beating, when the gift goes, so do I. Shortly after. Messily, Sefa thinks. She's very reassuring that way."

"And your plan for surviving this is—"

"I don't have one."

"Good. Then we're agreed there's a problem to solve."

"Aldric." She set down the ledger with the deliberate care of someone who wanted very much to throw it at me instead. "I've been keeping this gate for eleven years. I know every ward in it the way you know the back of your hand. If anyone can pour a gift into those wards and walk away—"

"You can't. You said so yourself."

"*If* anyone could—"

"Then let me finish the sentence for once in your life: if anyone could, it would still be a gamble, and you're not a gambler. You're a keeper. Keepers count things. So count with me." I held up fingers. "One: we don't know the unbinding works at all until you're mid-pour and dying. Two: the crown will feel you coming. Edran will send everything he has, and Veyra—"

"Veyra freed her prisoners. Her legion rides under our banner now, or nearly."

"Half her legion. The other half is garrisoning the capital, and the crown has had a century to dig itself in." Third finger. "Three: even if it works, we win a sealed gate and lose the only person in this rebellion who understands how the gate works. That's not victory, Wendell. That's trading the kingdom for a corpse and calling it a bargain."

Her jaw did the thing it does—the small set, like a door being locked from the inside. I had learned to read that jaw over the past months the way sailors read clouds. Storm coming.

"You're proposing an alternative," she said. Not a question.

"I'm proposing an assault on the capital. Direct. Everything we have. Rhea's fleet up the Serpent's Mouth, Veyra's veterans at the river gate, Tam's gate-words for the palace postern. We take the throne room, we put a blade through whatever is sitting on the throne wearing my brother's face, and we do it *before* anyone has to burn out their soul."

"That's not an alternative. That's a funeral with better scenery."

"It's a chance."

"It's a *gamble*," she shot back, and there it was—my own word, returned with interest. "You want to bet thousands of lives, Veyra's legion, the gifted survivors, all of it, on storming the strongest fortress in the hollow kingdom to stab a thing that's been eating kings for a hundred years. You think it doesn't have defenses? You think it hasn't imagined someone doing exactly this?"

"Of course it has. But it's imagined *me* doing it. It's been waiting ten years for me to come crawling back with an army. What it hasn't imagined—" I stepped closer, close enough to see the pulse jump in her throat, "—is its own gatekeeper walking into the throne room and putting her hands on the floor."

She went still. I pressed it, because pressing is the one skill exile taught me well.

"You told me yourself what the stones remember. This whole palace sits on foundations older than the crown. Older than my family's claim. Your gift reads stone, Wendell—not pebbles, *stone*, the bones of the world. In the capital, you wouldn't be a woman with a knife. You'd be the ground itself deciding to object."

For a long moment she said nothing. The lamp guttered. Somewhere above us, in the Spire's upper galleries, Tam was arguing with one of Sefa's healers about ration counts—he argued about ration counts the way other men argued about theology—and the ordinary sound of it made the vault feel colder, quieter, more like the inside of a tomb, which I suppose is what it was.

"It might work," she admitted finally, grudging as a mule.

"It might."

"And if it doesn't—if the crown is ready, if the palace stone is too deep-wrought, if Edran—" She stopped. Started again. "If it fails, everyone dies. If my way fails, *I* die. Those aren't equivalent losses, Aldric."

"They're not," I agreed. "Mine's worse."

That landed. I watched it land—watched her blink, watch the argument falter in her face like a horse stumbling on the stair.

"Don't," she said quietly. "Don't say things like that to win points."

"I'm not winning points. I've never won anything against you. I'm stating facts." I took a breath, because the next part had been sitting in my chest for weeks like a swallowed coal, and there was never going to be a better moment than this one—here, in the vault where death had been inventoried and shelved, with the man I used to be three wars dead and the future reduced to two bad options. "I love you."

The lamp flickered. Above us, Tam's voice went on about barley.

"I've loved you since Ashmarket," I went on, before she could find a weapon, which she would, because she always did. "Since you stood in the burned square counting the dead with your hand flat against the cobblestones, and you looked up at me and said *we bury them facing east, they'd want to see the mountains*. Nobody says things like that. Nobody thinks like that. And I thought, well, there it is, and also I'm doomed, because you are the most stubborn creature the Thornfell ever produced and you were going to get yourself killed out of duty long before I could talk you out of it."

"Aldric—"

"So here is my proposal, Keeper. Not as a prince—I haven't been one in any way that matters for ten years. As a man." I took her hands. They were rough, keeper's hands, scarred from rope and rock, and they did not pull away, which I chose to count as a victory worth more than any I'd won in battle. "Come at the capital with me. Both of us, side by side. And if it works—if we cut the crown off my brother's head and he dies free and the city stands—we come back here, and you teach me the wards, and I learn to carry water and mend fences and do whatever else a road-warden's husband does. And if it fails—"

"If it fails we're both dead and none of it matters."

"If it fails, we fail *together*, and I will not spend the rest of a life I don't want living anyway knowing I let you walk into the fire alone because I was too much a coward to stand beside you." I squeezed her hands. "That's the offer. Take it or call me a fool."

She called me a fool.

She said several other things besides, most of them unflattering, some of them anatomically creative for a woman who claimed to have no imagination, and she said them while gripping my hands hard enough to grind bone, which I found more honest than anything she actually articulated. Then she stopped, and her forehead came down against mine, and for a while neither of us spoke, and the lamp burned, and Tam upstairs discovered a discrepancy in the flour counts that apparently required divine intervention.

"I'm afraid," she said at last. Just that. No ornament.

"I know. So am I."

"You're not afraid of dying. I've seen you fight. You fight like a man doing arithmetic."

"I'm not afraid of dying," I agreed. "I'm afraid of the arithmetic coming out wrong. Of standing over your body in a throne room and thinking *there, that was the price of my pride*. Or worse—of winning. Winning and having to build something afterward without you in it." I laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know what the rebels keep asking me? Whether I'll take the throne when Edran falls. As though crowns grow on trees and you simply pick a ripe one. As though I want to sit in that room, on that chair, with that thing's memory soaked into the wood."

"What do you tell them?"

"I tell them the truth. That I'll take it if the realm needs taking care of, and not one hour longer, and then I intend to retire to a mountain pass and be ordered around by a gatekeeper for the rest of my days." I tilted my head back enough to look at her. "Assuming she'll have me."

"She's considering it," Wendell said. Her voice was rough. Her eyes were wet, though she'd have denied it under torture. "She thinks you're an idiot."

"Noted. Frequently."

"But she—" And here Wendell Kavanagh, who had stared down soldiers and smugglers and the grief of an entire mountain, faltered like a girl at her first dance. "She loves you too. For the record. Since Ashmarket, probably. Possibly since the prisoner column. She's terrible at saying it, so consider this her saying it badly."

"Considered," I said, and kissed her, and the vault with all its ledgers of death receded to the far edge of the world.

* * *

We argued through the night anyway. Love, it turns out, does not settle strategy; it only raises the stakes of losing.

By lamplight we spread the maps across the vault floor—capital, harbor, river gates, palace—and I played crown-general while she played doom. Every approach I proposed, she found the flaw. Fleet up the Serpent's Mouth: chain-boom across the narrows, sunk there a century ago and never raised. River gate: Veyra's own designs, rebuilt after the last rebellion, murder-holes stacked three deep. Palace postern with Tam's gate-words: fine, right up until we learned whether the words changed with the moon, and Tam couldn't swear they didn't.

"You're enjoying this," I accused somewhere past midnight.

"I'm keeping you honest."

"You're demolishing my army with relish."

"Both things can be true." She leaned over the map, hair falling loose, lamplight catching the grey in it I'd never noticed before this winter. "Here. What if we don't choose? What if we do both?"

I looked at her.

"Rhea takes the harbor. Veyra takes the river gate. Loud, obvious, every soldier in the capital running to the walls. And while the crown is watching the smoke and shouting orders through Edran's mouth—" she put her finger on the map, on the old pilgrim road that climbed from the lowlands toward the Thornfell, toward the Spire, toward us, "—the gifted and I come here. To the threshold. I pour the gift. We end it at the root while their army is busy dying at the gates."

"That's still your plan. That's still you burning out."

"It's *both* plans," she said, with the satisfaction of a woman checkmating a king. "Your assault buys mine its silence. Mine ends the war before your assault has to climb the palace stairs. And if yours goes badly—if they take the harbor, if Veyra breaks—we've lost a battle, not the war, because the gate still gets sealed either way." She sat back. "Nobody wins clean, Aldric. We just choose which losses we can carry."

I stared at the map for a long time. The candle burned down another inch.

"It's good," I said finally, hating every word. "It's better than mine. It's still your funeral with better scenery."

"It's still my choice." She reached across the maps and took my hand again. "You asked me to stand beside you. Fine. Stand beside me. Don't stand in front of me. I've been keeping this gate alone for eleven years; the one thing I cannot survive is being managed."

"Then hear my terms," I said. "Sefa shields you at the threshold. The strongest of the gifted with her, all of them if we can spare them. Veyra's best company escorting you the whole road. And me—at your shoulder, the entire way, and if the crown's power so much as flickers in your direction, I put my body between it and yours and let the historians sort out the rest."

"That last part is stupid."

"That last part is non-negotiable."

We glared at each other across the maps of a war, two people negotiating terms of engagement and marriage in the same breath, and somewhere in the small hours the arguments stopped being arguments. They became planning, and the planning became quieter, and the quiet became the kind where two people who have run out of reasons to be apart simply stop pretending otherwise.

Near dawn she fell asleep against my shoulder, mid-sentence, in the middle of recalculating Rhea's landing timetable. I sat very still so as not to wake her, which meant sitting still while my arm went numb and the candle drowned in its own wax, and I looked at the vault around us—at the shelves where the founders had written down, a thousand years ago, exactly how to spend a life—and I made my peace with it the only way I could.

Not acceptance. I would never accept it. I would fight it at the threshold itself if I had to, wrestle the wards for her soul like Jacob wrestling the angel. But I understood it, at last, the way I hadn't at the prisoner column, at Ashmarket, at any point in this long slow fall from prince to rebel to man in love with a woman scheduled to die. Some prices are not paid by the strong for the weak. Some are paid by the willing for everyone, and the only honor left to the rest of us is to make sure the payment buys what it should.

She stirred. "What hour is it?"

"Almost dawn."

"We ride tomorrow, then. Today." She rubbed her eyes, and for one unguarded moment she looked young and terrified and utterly resolved, all three at once. "Aldric. If it works—if I somehow live—"

"Road-warden's husband," I said. "Water-carrier. Fence-mender. At your service."

"And if it doesn't—"

"Then we'll have owed the living everything," I said, "and paid it. That's more than most get."

Above us, the Spire creaked in the morning wind, and far below, in the camp, the first fires were being lit, and the horns sounded the call that meant: *the march begins.*

Wendell stood, straightened her dusty sleeves, and became the Keeper again before my eyes—spine first, then jaw, then the grey slate eyes going hard and clear as mountain water.

"Then let's go owe it," she said.

And we went—together, down out of the vault, into the noise and smoke of an army waking, toward the capital and the crown and the gate between all of us and the dark.
