## 17. The March on the Capital

There is a particular quality to hope that arrives late in a war: it is loud, it is unearned, and it makes sensible people do foolish things with remarkable speed. I had spent my whole life mistrusting it. Hope was what got gatekeepers killed—hope that the wards would hold one more winter, hope that the smugglers would keep their bargains, hope that the mountain's tremors were only the mountain dreaming.

And yet, on the morning we marched out of Ashmarket, I caught myself humming.

Aldric noticed. Of course he noticed. He had developed an unerring talent for noticing everything I did, which was either devotion or surveillance, and I had decided to stop asking which.

"You're humming," he said, falling into step beside me on the coast road. Behind us stretched perhaps four thousand people—gifted and ungifted, farmers with sharpened scythes, deserters from three different crown garrisons, a contingent of Ashmarket orphans who had appointed themselves our unofficial drum corps and were, at this moment, banging pot lids in something adjacent to rhythm.

"I'm breathing with intent," I said. "It's a mountain practice."

"It's 'The Miller's Daughter,' and it's a drinking song."

"Everything is a drinking song if you commit."

He laughed—that low, startled laugh he still seemed to surprise himself with—and for half a mile neither of us said anything, because the road did the talking for us. It ran along the cliffs above the Sea of Spires, and below us, filling the gray water like spilled coins, was Rhea Voss's fleet.

Not smuggler ships anymore. Not quite warships either. Rhea had spent two weeks refitting her hulls in the coves south of Thornfell, and what she'd produced was something in between: merchant vessels with ballistae bolted to their decks, crewed by men and women who had spent decades lying to customs officers and could therefore lie to anything, including, presumably, the capital's harbor chain. Eleven sails. Rhea had told me she'd once commanded thirty. The sea had taken its tax over the years; the crown had taken the rest.

"She'll round the point by dusk," Aldric said. "If the wind holds."

"If the wind holds," I agreed. "If the harbor chain falls. If Veyra keeps her word. If Edran can hold the crown's attention long enough for us to reach him." I ticked them off on my fingers. "I've been keeping count. We're up to nine ifs since breakfast. At this rate we'll be marching on certainty by never."

"Gatekeeper optimism."

"Someone has to model it. You certainly won't."

But here is the thing I didn't say, walking that cliff road with the drums banging and the fleet glittering below: nine days ago, Veyra had turned her legion. Nine days ago, the usurper's own general—the woman who had hunted us through the deep roads of the Thornfell, who had burned our camp, who had put my name on more than one execution order—had freed her prisoners, knelt in the mud before four hundred people she'd spent a year trying to kill, and asked to be allowed to fight.

Sefa had vouched for her. Sefa, who read hearts the way I read stone, had looked at Veyra and said only, "She's cracked all the way through. But cracks let light in." Which was either wisdom or a very poetic way of endorsing a war criminal, and I still hadn't decided.

Veyra marched at the column's head now, a half-mile ahead of Aldric, deliberately apart, wearing no general's insignia and carrying a plain spear. Her soldiers flanked us on both sides—a full legion, eight thousand strong, moving with the terrible competence of people who had done nothing but march for years. When they'd first joined us, the rebel farmers had given them a wide berth. Now the children ran between the ranks stealing dried plums, and the soldiers pretended not to notice, which is how armies learn to be human again.

It should have felt like victory. Four thousand rebels, eight thousand legionnaires, eleven ships converging on a capital held by a king who was not a king. Instead it felt like the last hour before a storm front comes over the mountains—the light goes strange, the birds go quiet, and everything holds its breath because it knows.

The crown knew too. I could feel it.

That was the part nobody had warned me about, when I'd learned to push my gift beyond touch. Distance-reading cost me headaches like split timber, but the land itself remembered the crown the way a body remembers a fever. Every milestone we passed carried a faint wrongness, a residue of a century of hungry attention. The roads themselves had been watched so long they'd grown used to it, the way a prisoner grows used to walls. And ahead—east, past the salt marshes, past the river gates—something vast and patient was paying attention to us, and it was amused.

"You feel it too," Aldric said. Not a question.

"Every hour. It's getting closer." I rubbed my temples. "It knows exactly where we are, Aldric. It's known since the pass. It's not sending an army to meet us because—" I searched for the word and hated it when I found it. "Because it doesn't think it needs one."

"Veyra thinks it does need one. She says there are ten thousand more troops inside the walls."

"Ten thousand troops wearing collars made of fear." I kicked a stone off the cliff edge and listened for it to land; the mountain answered me even here, faintly, a whisper instead of a voice, and I treasured every syllable. My gift had been guttering for weeks now, burning down like a lamp left lit all night. I hadn't told anyone except Sefa. Some ledgers you keep to yourself. "The crown doesn't command loyalty. It commands obedience. Obedience evaporates the moment the collar breaks. That's why it's afraid of us—not the spears. The example."

"And if the collar doesn't break?"

"Then we're a footnote," I said cheerfully, "and someone else writes history."

He gave me a look—the look, the one that said *you are doing the thing where you joke so you don't have to be afraid*, which was rich coming from a man who'd spent ten years in exile perfecting the art of grim silence—and I squeezed his hand once, briefly, because some arguments you settle with your hands rather than your mouth.

We crested the bluff above Saltmere Cross at midday, and the world changed.

* * *

The capital had many names across its history. The old books called it Corvenna, City of Nine Bridges. The smugglers called it the Gullet, on account of the harbor's shape and the harbor's appetites. The crown called it the Seat Eternal, which told you everything about the crown's modesty.

From the bluff, it lay spread along the river delta like a knife on a table. White walls, red roofs, the palace rising from its hill in the center with the Spire-road spiraling up behind it—and beyond the palace, far beyond, hazy with distance but unmistakable to my gift, the Thornfell range. Our mountains. Somewhere in those peaks, invisible from here, stood the Last Spire and the sealed gate, and I understood suddenly, with a lurch, why the crown had built its seat here. It sat facing the thing it had been forged to guard. A century of kings, and every one of them sleeping each night with their back to the gate. Watching it. Wanting it.

"They'll see us from the walls by tomorrow evening," Veyra said. She had come up the bluff without a sound, which for a woman in armor remained one of her more unsettling talents. "They'll close the river gates tonight. If we want the harbor, we take it at dawn, while the tide serves Rhea's shallow draft."

"If the chain falls," Aldric said.

"The chain will fall." Veyra's voice was flat as slate. "I commanded its defense for six years. The winches rot. Half the crews are press-ganged fishermen who would rather be anywhere else. And I know the watch-signals." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes—the crack Sefa had spoken of, letting its light in. "I know a great deal I am not proud of knowing."

"We've all got ledgers," I said.

She glanced at me. For a moment I thought she might smile. What happened instead was worse and better: she nodded, once, like a soldier accepting an order, and went back down to her ranks.

The camp went up on the bluffs, and I spent the evening doing the thing I was actually good at, which was not strategy or speechmaking but listening to ground. I walked the camp perimeter with my palms nearly brushing the turf, reading the earth under us the way Tam used to read his stolen letters—slowly, sounding out each word. The bluff was stable. Good. The old kings' road east was hollowed with drainage tunnels—useful. And beneath the whole eastern approach, running under the marshes like a buried vein, was something I hadn't expected: an aqueduct. Ancient, pre-crown, dry for centuries, running straight beneath the river gates into the city's bones.

I brought it to the council fire that night, where Aldric, Veyra, Sefa, and a dozen rebel captains sat arguing over maps older than any of them.

"Aqueduct," I said, and drew it in the dirt with a stick. "Four feet wide. Runs from the hills under the marsh, under the river, under the wall. It hasn't carried water since before Edran's grandfather wore the crown. The stones remember being useful. They'd like to be useful again."

"That's the gift talking," one captain said, not unkindly.

"That's the masonry talking. The gift just takes notes." I tapped the line. "Send fifty picked people through it tonight, and at dawn, when Rhea's ships hit the harbor chain, they open the river gate from the inside. The crown expects armies. Armies are visible. Nobody guards a dead pipe."

Silence around the fire. Then Veyra leaned forward and studied my scratch-map with an expression I recognized, because I'd worn it myself looking at ward-schematics: the expression of a professional realizing an amateur has found the flaw she missed.

"The Founders' Conduit," she said quietly. "I heard of it once. A rumor. They sealed the records eighty years ago." She looked up at me. "Keeper Kavanagh, remind me never to make you angry."

"No promises," I said.

Aldric caught my eye across the fire and something passed between us that needed no words—or rather, words that we'd already spent, that night in the Spire's shadow, when duty and love had finally stopped pretending to be separate things. He'd argued for the doomed assault on the capital precisely because it was doomed; I'd refused to let him die for my sake; and in the end we'd compromised on the truth, which was that we would both do the necessary thing and neither of us would pretend it didn't terrify us.

Tomorrow we would go up against the crown itself. Tonight, the fire, the maps, the faces.

I almost made it through the evening without crying. Almost.

* * *

Tam came back to us an hour before midnight.

I was at the camp's eastern edge, checking the pickets, when the sentry challenge went up and a familiar figure walked into the torchlight—thinner than I remembered, hollow-eyed, wearing a soldier's coat with the insignia torn off, hands raised to show they were empty.

My apprentice. My traitor. My boy.

The last time I'd seen him, he'd been standing among crown riders at the burning camp, a ward-collar glinting at his throat, and I had spared him, and the choice had marked me like a brand. I'd told myself I didn't regret it. I'd told myself a great many things.

"Hold," I said to the sentries, and walked forward alone.

"Wendell." His voice cracked on the second syllable. "I—I need to talk to you. To Aldric. To everyone. I have—" He fumbled at his coat and produced a folded scrap of paper, grease-spotted, handled so often it had gone soft as cloth. "I have the gate-words."

The camp went quiet around us. Even the drum-corps orphans, who slept nowhere near their blankets, had gone still.

"Slowly," I said.

"They rotate the palace passwords every nine days. Wardens' countersigns, the river-gate signals, the postern rotations. I copied them before I ran. Six months serving in the palace guard, Wendell, six months polishing floors and memorizing everything, waiting for—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Waiting to be worth something again. I know I'm not. I know what I did. I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm asking to be used."

I took the paper. My hands, I noted distantly, were shaking. Gate-words. The actual keys, written down, delivered into our laps by the boy whose betrayal had started the hunt in the first place. The crown had hunted the gifted with silver bought from informants, and now an informant had turned his silver into a knife pointed back at it. There was a symmetry there that the poets would enjoy, if any of us lived to hire poets.

"Why?" I asked. Not cruelly. I genuinely wanted to know.

He looked at the ground. "Because you spared me," he said, "when sparing me cost you something. Everyone else who was kind to me, it was cheap kindness. Yours wasn't." His jaw worked. "And because I saw the king. Up close, at a ceremony. I saw his face when the crown spoke through him." His eyes came up, and they were wet and steady at once. "Nobody should have to wear that. Nobody should have to *be* that. Whatever I owe you—it's less than what owes doing."

For a long moment I couldn't speak. The mountain whispered under my feet, faintly, the way it always did now—memory, not magic—and I thought about all the ledgers I kept, all the debts I tracked, and how the oldest one, the one I'd never written down, was the debt I owed a resentful boy for being the reason I understood that secrets kept too hard become cages.

"Come with me," I said finally. "You're going to say all of that again to the council. Word for word. And then you're going to guide the conduit party—you know the palace drains better than any of us, and I'm not wasting you on sentiment." I held up a hand before he could speak. "This isn't forgiveness, Tam. Forgiveness is a door that opens from my side, and I haven't found the key yet. This is employment. Earn the rest."

"Yes," he said. Just that. Yes, like a man handed bread after a famine.

When I turned back toward the council fire, Aldric was standing at the edge of the light, arms crossed, watching. He'd heard enough—he always heard enough.

"Well?" he said.

"He's ours now. Whatever that means."

"It means," Aldric said, falling into step beside me, "that the crown taught a boy to sell his teacher, and you taught him to buy her back. That's the whole war, isn't it? Right there. Two curricula."

"Go to sleep, Your Highness."

"Can't. Too busy being terrified."

"Good," I said. "Terrified people check their work."

* * *

Dawn came up bruise-colored over the sea, and the storm broke.

Not weather—though there was weather too, low clouds dragging their bellies on the water. I mean the plan. From the bluff where Aldric and I stood with the banner-party, we watched it unfold the way you watch a lock mechanism turn: Rhea's eleven ships sweeping out of the gray with the tide under them, ballistae firing grapnel and chain-shot at the harbor boom while her boarding crews screamed smugglers' blasphemies loud enough to carry across the water; Veyra's legion advancing on the landward gates in parade formation, banners high, drawing every eye on the wall; and beneath all of it, unseen, fifty people crawling through a dead emperor's pipe with Tam's copied words and my stick-drawn map.

And it worked.

God help me, it worked. The harbor chain parted with a groan I felt through the bedrock, a deep structural sigh, and Rhea's lead ship slipped through the gap flying a flag I couldn't read at that distance but could guess at. An hour later the river gate swung open from the inside—not forced, *opened*, the sentries on it throwing down their weapons or joining us outright—and Veyra's legion poured into the lower city meeting resistance so scattered it barely qualified as fighting. Collar-fear evaporating, exactly as promised. Ten thousand defenders, and most of them simply stood down, staring at the rebel banners with the expressions of people waking from a long fever.

By noon we held the lower city. By late afternoon, the bridges. And the palace rose above us on its hill, white and silent, its gates shut, its windows dark, and the crown inside it was very, very quiet.

Too quiet. I stood on the bridge with my hand flat on the ancient stonework and read the palace the way I'd read the Spire, and what came back made my blood run cold.

No panic in there. No scrambling garrison. The palace stones remembered fear for a century, layers of it—but today, underneath, patient as bedrock: *amusement*. The same amusement I'd felt on the road, closer now, concentrated, waiting.

"It wants us to come," I said to Aldric.

"I know." He was looking up at the palace, and his face was the face from his story—the brother who'd begged for a knife, the failure he'd carried ten years. "Edran's in there. Or it is. Or both."

"Tomorrow, then?"

"Tonight," he said. "While the city's ours and the gates are open. Tomorrow it finds its voice again, and we lose the moment." He turned to me, and took my hand, and pressed it once, briefly, the way I'd pressed his on the cliff road—arguments settled by touch. "Get Sefa. Get the gifted. Get everyone who can shield. We end this tonight."

Behind us, across the captured city, the rebel drums began to beat—pot lids and tanners' skins and proper military cadres, all of it together, an ugly glorious racket rolling up the hill toward the silent white palace.

And in my palm, against the bridge stone, the mountain whispered one word, clear as a bell, the only word it had offered me in days:

*Hurry.*
