## 18. The Crown's Voice

I had imagined this room a thousand times in ten years of exile. In my imaginings it was always smaller. Memory is a kind liar—it shrank the throne hall of Vael Corren to something a man might cross in ten strides, something I might storm with a knife and my brother's name. Standing in it again, I understood that memory had also been merciful. The hall was vast as a swallowed grief, and every step toward the throne was a step through everything I'd failed to do.

The doors had opened for us.

That was Tam's doing—his gate-words, whispered to the wardens at the palace's throat, his penance paid in the only coin he had left. Wendell had taken them from him without a word, which was its own particular mercy, and then she had led us through corridors that should have killed us and didn't, because the stones remembered her, or perhaps because they pitied us. Hard to say with mountains. Harder still with palaces.

Now we stood at the foot of the dais, forty rebels and gifted behind us, Rhea's people holding the doors, Sefa's shield-song humming so faintly beneath the marble that you felt it in your teeth rather than heard it. And on the throne sat my brother.

Edran looked terrible. That was the first thing—the small human thing—and I hated myself for noticing it with relief. Ten years ago he had been golden, broad, the kind of king ballads get written about before they're finished being written. Now he was a gray man in a gilded cage of posture, hands gripping the arms of the throne like a drowning sailor grips wreckage. His eyes were closed.

The crown sat on his head and did not look like gold. It looked like a decision that had never once been reconsidered.

"Brother Aldric," said the throne room, in Edran's voice.

Except it wasn't Edran's voice. It was close—too close—a voice wearing Edran's voice the way a hand wears a glove, and there was no warmth in it at all, only the patient arithmetic of something very old doing sums. My spine tried to leave my body by way of my shoulders. Behind me I heard someone breathe out a curse.

"You've brought me such interesting guests." The eyes opened. Gray, flat, lit from somewhere behind like windows in an empty house. "A smuggler captain. A healer. A boy who sold his teacher for silver and has regretted it ever since—I do keep accounts, Aldric, even of the small debts." Tam made a sound like he'd been struck. "And a keeper."

The gaze slid past me and found Wendell, and the whole temperature of the hall changed. Not colder. *Hungrier.*

"Wendell Kavanagh," the crown said, tasting the syllables. "Keeper of the Last Spire. Do you know how long I have wanted to meet you? Not you specifically. Your kind. The ones who hold doors I am not permitted through."

"It talks," Wendell said, to me, conversationally, as if we were inspecting a horse with bad teeth. "You didn't mention it talks."

"I mentioned," I said, "repeatedly, that it was not a man."

"Not a man," the crown agreed pleasantly, "and yet here sits a man, and here stands a woman, and between us there is a great deal that could be arranged. Shall we dispense with the theater? You came to kill me. Or rather—" the mouth smiled, and it was Edran's smile, and that was the cruelest thing yet, "—you came to free him. How touching. Brothers. Keepers. Sacrifice. I have watched a century of it from under this circlet, and I will tell you what I told the last keeper who climbed these stairs with fire in her fists: it changes nothing. The gate holds because I permit it to hold. The kingdom breathes because I permit it to breathe. I am not the usurper, Aldric. I am the mortgage. Someone must pay, and everyone before you chose to make someone else pay."

Wendell stepped forward. Of course she did. She walked like a woman crossing her own courtyard, chin down, hands loose, utterly unimpressed by the architecture of power—which, I was beginning to understand, was the most dangerous way to walk into a throne room.

"Here's what I know about mortgages," she said. "They end. Either you pay them off or you default, but nobody lives in the bank forever."

Something moved behind Edran's eyes. Interest, maybe. Or appetite.

"You want to know what I'm offering her," the crown said to me, though its gaze never left Wendell. "Shall I tell you what I've seen in her? I have worn kings, general. I have sat inside a hundred skulls and read their hungers like menus. This one wants so little. It's almost embarrassing. She wants her mountain quiet. She wants the gate sealed and left alone. She wants her gift back—yes, keeper, I know what it costs you, I can smell it guttering in you from here, a candle cupped in two hands against the wind. And underneath all of it, so deep she's never once said it aloud: she wants to stop being alone."

The hall was silent enough that I could hear the torches.

"So here is my offer," the crown said, and now the voice gentled, took on warmth it had no right to, Edran's warmth, borrowed like everything else. "Walk away. All of you. The hunt ends tonight. Every gifted soul in my dungeons walks free by morning—Sefa's patients, Veyra's prisoners, the children in the cells under the Salt Quarter, all of them. No amnesty papers, no conditions, no collars. And for you, Wendell Kavanagh: your gift restored. Whole. Brighter than before, if you like—I have a century of hoarded fire and nowhere to spend it but on generosity. The Thornfell passes warded so nothing crosses without your leave, in your lifetime and your apprentice's and theirs after. The mountains yours. Quiet. Safe. Sealed."

It paused, letting the words settle into the room like snow.

"All you must do is walk away from this one man and his war. Leave the crown on the head it has grown comfortable upon. Go home, keeper. Be the last Spire's keeper until you die old and warm, and let the world beyond the pass arrange its own funeral."

* * *

I want to be honest, since honesty is apparently the theme of my late-life redemption arc: for one full heartbeat, I hoped she would take it.

Not because I wanted her gone. God knows—not because of that. But because she deserved it. Because I had spent three chapters' worth of nights watching her burn herself thinner to hold passes open for people who'd voted, only days ago, to consider selling her, and if some ancient parasite wanted to buy her peace with a mountain and a lifetime of quiet, well. There were worse brokers. There were worse gods. Half the religions of the free cities promised less and delivered nothing.

She looked at Edran. Not at the crown—at Edran. At the gray hands on the throne's arms.

"That's a good offer," she said quietly. "You know that, don't you? That's the best offer anyone's made me in my whole life."

"I know precisely what it's worth," said the crown.

"And that's how I know you're lying somewhere in it." She turned her head, slow, taking in the hall, the guards frozen at their posts, Rhea's crew at the doors, Sefa pale and steady, me. "Because you don't give anything away. You're forged to bind. You bind kings, you bind gifts, you bind gates. Everything you 'give,' you leash. So either my gift comes home with hooks in it, or my mountains come quiet because everyone on the far side of them is dead and eaten, or—" she shrugged, "—or the amnesty lasts exactly as long as it takes me to turn my back. Pick one. I'm curious which lie it is."

For a moment—just a moment—the mask slipped.

It wasn't rage. Rage I could have handled; I'd been raised on royal rage, it has a flavor like overcooked venison. What showed instead was something older and tireder, a vast impatience, the expression of a thing that had explained itself ten thousand times to meat that refused to understand. Edran's face went slack around it, a puppet whose hand had briefly forgotten it.

"*Little stone-listener,*" the crown said, and the voice dropped out of Edran's register entirely, became layered, became a choir arguing with itself, "*you are standing in my house on my patience. I offered you the pretty version. Here is the true one: I am dying. The host fails. They all fail, eventually—they were never built to carry me. I need a new vessel, and vessels of your caliber are not common. So I will have you, or I will have the boy, or I will have the healer, or I will crack this city open and drink whatever talent runs in its gutters. The offer stands because I prefer consent—it takes better, it lasts longer—but do not mistake preference for need.*"

The torches guttered. Somewhere above us, the whole palace groaned like a ship changing tack, and I felt it through the floor—felt Wendell feel it, her jaw tightening, her gift reaching instinctively for stone that belonged to the enemy now.

"There it is," she said. "Thank you. I hate shopping without prices."

"Then refuse me," said the crown, "and watch what I do with the ones you love while you climb your mountain. I have had ten years to practice cruelty in this body. Ask him." The gray eyes swung to me. "Ask your brother what his king did to the household guard who spoke kindly of exile. Ask him why he really ran."

"Aldric." Wendell's voice, low, beside me. "Don't take the bait. Whatever it shows you, it's a hook."

But my brother's face had changed.

* * *

Here is what ten years of guilt does: it builds a door in you and leaves it unlatched. The crown found mine in half a heartbeat.

I saw it happen. I saw Edran's shoulders drop half an inch, saw the gray film over his eyes thin like ice going out, saw something surface through it gasping—my brother, my actual brother, ten years drowned and surfacing for air.

"Aldric." His voice cracked in the middle, human, wrecked, *his*. His hands came off the throne's arms and gripped each other in his lap, knuckles white, and I knew that gesture, I had known it across a childhood of shared beds and shared beatings and one shared secret too many. "Aldric, oh—oh, it let me up, it does this sometimes, it likes to show me what it's bought—listen to me, listen—"

"I'm here." I was moving before I decided to move, three steps up the dais before Wendell's hand caught my sleeve and I shook her off, because I am an idiot, because I am a younger brother, because ten years of discipline evaporates when your brother says your name in his own voice. "Edran. Edran, look at me. We came for you. We came to take it off you."

"Don't." He was crying. Kings shouldn't cry like that, open-mouthed, graceless, a boy's crying. "Don't come closer, it'll use it, it'll use my mouth—Aldric, listen, there's a moment, when it surfaces me it has to spend strength to hold the shape, there's a moment where it thins—" His eyes darted sideways, downward, to the space just below his own breastbone, and I understood. "There. When it reaches for her, when it spends itself—that's your moment. That's the only one you'll get. Finish it then. Promise me. Promise me you'll finish it."

"Edran—"

"*Promise me.*" And then, quieter, breaking apart: "Ten years, little brother. Ten years I've screamed inside my own skull. I begged you with my eyes at the coronation. I begged you in the garden, do you remember the garden, I said *the crown whispers at night* and you thought I meant politics. I have begged every god there is and most of them are dead or worse than this thing. You are the only prayer I have left. Please. Please finish it. Don't save *me*. Save whoever comes after. Let me be the last one it wears."

The hall was absolutely still. Behind me I heard Sefa crying without any noise at all, tears simply falling, and Rhea Voss—who does not weep, who has told me so twice while visibly swallowing it—staring at the flagstones.

I had imagined this moment for ten years. In every imagining I was brave in it. In every imagining I said something worthy, something a bard could set to strings.

What I said was: "I promise. I promise, Edran. I'm sorry. I'm sorry it took me ten years to hear you."

"No." He managed half a smile, my brother's crooked smile, the one our mother said would get him crowned or killed. "No, you heard me years ago. You just needed help carrying it." His eyes flicked past me—to Wendell—and something passed between them, keeper to host, one professional to another, an acknowledgment I wasn't party to. "Take care of the gate, keeper. Take care of him. He's sturdier than he looks and stupider than he acts."

Then the light went out of him.

It went out like a lamp snuffed between fingers—one blink, and the gray came flooding back, and the spine straightened, and the hands returned to the throne's arms with the patience of a thing that owns all the time in the world.

"Well," said the crown, through my brother's mouth, lightly. "That was undignified. For both of us." It rose from the throne. Guards throughout the hall came to attention as one body, and I saw Veyra among them, second column, her face giving nothing away except—maybe, possibly, if you'd spent a decade learning to read the faces of soldiers—nothing away at all, which from Veyra was a speech. "You have your answer, then. Sentiment. Sentiment every time. A century of kings and they all choose sentiment, and it kills them, and I inherit the mess."

"The gate," Wendell said. Her voice had gone very level, the levelness of drawn steel. "You said the gate holds because you permit it. Say it plainly. Can you open it?"

The crown considered her. And I watched it decide—watched something ancient weigh whether to spend truth as a weapon, and decide yes, because fear was cheaper than force and it had already spent strength surfacing my brother.

"When I fall," it said, "everything I hold falls with me. The wards. The bindings. The seals." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Including yours, keeper. The Last Spire was bound by the same hand that bound me. Unbind me at your threshold, and you will learn what else comes undone."

Silence.

Behind me, Sefa's shield-song stopped.

I turned. Wendell had gone white—not afraid, I realized. Recalculating. Every plan we had carried across the Thornfell, every argument, every desperate geometry of wards and sacrifice, all of it tearing itself down and rebuilding in her face in real time. And what rebuilt there was not despair. It was worse. It was certainty.

"Good," she said softly.

"Wendell," I said. "Whatever you just thought—don't."

"Oh, I'm going to." She rolled her shoulders like a woman about to lift something heavy, and looked up at the crown on my brother's head with an expression I will see every time I close my eyes for the rest of my life: pity, fury, and arithmetic. "You just told me exactly what I needed to know. You told me where to stand."

The crown's smile died.

Guards began to move.
