## 19. Unbinding

The Last Spire looked smaller than I remembered.

That's a strange thing to say about the tallest structure in the Thornfells—a needle of black stone that had stood before any kingdom worth naming—but grief does funny things to scale. I had left it as a prisoner in chains, dragged down its switchbacks with my hands bound and the mountain screaming through my bones. I came back with an army of sorts: two hundred rebels, forty-some gifted refugees, one smuggler fleet anchored in the lowland rivers, one general who'd turned her coat three days ago and still hadn't decided whether she regretted it, and one exiled prince who kept looking at me like I was a door he was afraid to open.

And I came back to die. But we'll get to that.

"Say something wry," Aldric said beside me. "You always say something wry when you're terrified."

"I'm not terrified. I'm conducting a structural assessment." I squinted up at the Spire's crown of stone against a bruised dawn sky. "The masonry's holding. The keeper is another matter."

"That's not wry, that's morbid."

"You've known me nine weeks. Pace yourself."

He didn't laugh. He'd stopped laughing somewhere on the third day of our march, when Veyra's defected legion brought word that Edran—the thing wearing Edran—had emptied the capital's prisons and armories and was marching everything it had toward the Thornfell. Toward us. Toward *me*, specifically, though the crown didn't know yet that I knew what it was, or what it had been forged for, or that I intended to burn it out of the world at the only threshold where that was possible.

It thought I was coming home to hide behind my gate.

Let it.

* * *

We made the gatehouse by midmorning. The wards were still mine—I could feel them the way you feel your own heartbeat, a hum in the teeth and the wrists—and they parted for me like water around a familiar stone. The soldiers posted there took one look at two hundred armed rebels flowing out of the pass and made the sensible choice, which is to say they ran.

Tam was waiting at the sealed gate itself.

I should explain Tam. My apprentice. The boy who sold my name to a crown rider for eleven silver pieces and a promise, because his mother was starving and envy makes excellent arithmetic out of love. I had spared him once, in a burning camp, and he had repaid me by stealing the palace's gate-words and walking half the hollow kingdom alone to bring them to me. Penance, he called it. I called it what it was: a boy trying to build himself back into someone worth keeping.

"The inner vault's already open," he said, breathless. "I did what you showed me—the listening trick, the way you read the locks through the stone. It took all night but the founders' mechanism answered." He hesitated. "It felt like it was waiting for you."

"It has been waiting a hundred years," I said. "Everyone in this story has been waiting a hundred years. We're just the ones who showed up late."

Inside the threshold chamber, the air tasted of ozone and old iron. Sefa was already there, kneeling at the center of the founder's circle with the other gifted arranged around her in the pattern we'd drilled for six nights running—healer and stormcaller, emberwitch and the quiet twins from Saltmarsh who could bend light until it lied about distances. Forty hands joined. Forty gifts braided.

Sefa looked up at me, and her face did the thing it always did when she was frightened and refusing to show it: went very still, very kind. "Are you certain?"

"Sefa."

"Wendell. Are you *certain*?"

I knelt across from her in the circle. The stone under my palms was warm, warmer than mountain stone had any right to be, warm as a sleeping animal. Through my gift I could feel the whole Spire above us, feel the wards threaded through every block of it like veins through a hand, feel the ancient working coiled at the gate's heart—the binding the founders had poured their own lives into, the seal the crown had been forged to protect and pervert.

A century ago, the founders had built the Last Spire to hold shut a wound in the world. And then, fearing their seal would fail, they had forged a crown to bind the gifted—every gifted mind in the realm—to sustain it. A parasite dressed as a monarchy. A leash dressed as a coronet. It had eaten king after king, wearing each one hollow, feeding their strength and the stolen fire of every hunted mage into the wards, keeping the gate shut the way a miser keeps a vault: by starving everyone else.

Unbind the crown, and the gate needed a new anchor. A willing one. A keeper who would pour her gift into the wards and let the burning take it—all of it, forever.

My gift was the only thing keeping my heart beating. The healers had told me plainly: whatever the fever left in me as a child, the stone-sense held it at bay. Burn the gift, and the fever comes home to roost.

So yes. Certain.

"I'm going to need you to be extremely good at your job," I told her.

Sefa smiled the way people smile at funerals. "I'm always extremely good at my job."

* * *

It began, as most catastrophes do, with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

The crown knew. Of course it knew—the moment I touched the founder's circle, the moment my gift slid into the old wards, the parasitic intelligence wearing my king felt a hand on the root of its power and understood exactly whose hand it was. I felt it turn its attention toward the Thornfell like a lighthouse beam, vast and cold and *hungry*, and beneath that hunger, faint as a drowning man under ice, something else. Someone else. Edran, ten years buried alive inside his own skull, feeling his brother come for him at last.

*"Keeper."* The voice arrived inside my head without asking permission, which was rude even by usurper standards. *"Wendell Kavanagh. Gatekeeper of the Last Spire. You have walked far to die in a small room."*

"You've worn seven kings," I said aloud, because the others needed to hear it too, needed to know the shape of the enemy. "You've eaten a kingdom. And you're talking to a woman kneeling in the dirt. That's the real scandal here."

*"I kept your mountains standing."* The voice was patient, reasonable, the voice of every tyrant explaining arithmetic. *"Beyond this gate waits the hollow dark the founders feared. I have held it. One hundred years. Give me your gift freely and I will give you peace—your mountains safe, your name honored, your heart's desire—"*

"My heart's desire," I said, "is for you to shut up and let me work."

I opened the floodgates.

Pouring your gift into ancient wards is hard to describe without lying. People imagine fire, lightning, some cinematic conflagration. What it actually felt like was opening a vein into an ocean—my stone-sense, the sense that had been mine since childhood, that had told me the mood of mountains and the secrets of locked doors and the tremor of coming avalanches, all of it rushing out of me into the founding-work, and the founding-work *waking*. The whole Spire sang. Not metaphorically; the stones themselves rang, a note so deep I heard it in my spine, and the wards that had been starved for a century drank like a man dying of thirst.

And the crown *screamed*.

It hit us the way a wave hits a seawall. Not the voice now—the will, the full weight of a hundred years of accumulated theft, every gifted mind it had ever consumed thrown against the forty of us in one crushing blow. Sefa gasped beside me. The twins from Saltmarsh bled from the nose. Somewhere behind us a stormcaller went to her knees, and I heard Aldric shouting orders, heard steel drawn at the threshold as the first of the crown's vanguard crested the pass—

"Hold!" Sefa's voice cracked like a whip. "Hold the circle! Wendell, don't stop, whatever happens outside, *don't stop*—"

I didn't stop. I couldn't. Stopping meant the wards collapsed, meant the crown won, meant Edran stayed buried alive forever. So I held while the battle broke over us like weather.

They came down the pass in their hundreds—Veyra's former comrades, conscripts and zealots, and above them, riding a palanquin of forced wind, the general the crown trusted least and used most. Veyra herself fought at our line, traitor's colors flying, her quiet gift turning arrows aside in lazy handfuls. Aldric was everywhere along the wall of shields, sword work I'd only seen him practice, and twice I felt him look back at the circle, at me, and tear himself away again.

The crown pressed harder. It stopped arguing and started *taking*—reaching into the circle for our gifts directly, and I felt it find the edges of my mind and pull, and gods, it was strong, it was a century of kings and a thousand stolen magics, and my grip on the wards started to slip—

Then Sefa did the thing she'd taught me on the road, in a prison column, with a dead boy cooling in the mud behind us.

*Power shared is power doubled.*

She broke her own hands free of the circle's pattern and laid them flat on my shoulders, and instead of shielding me, she *joined*—her healer's gift pouring into mine, not fighting the drain but feeding it, doubling the current. And then the twins, bleeding, laughing, did the same. Then the stormcaller. Then all of them, forty gifts braided into one river, and the river ran through me into the founding-work, and the founding-work blazed like a second dawn.

The crown's scream changed pitch. From fury to fear.

*"You do not know what you unseal,"* it hissed, and for the first time it sounded small. *"The founders built me because they were afraid. They were RIGHT to be afraid. Beyond the gate—"*

"Beyond the gate," I said, through teeth gritted against the pull, "is not your problem anymore. You've spent a hundred years telling everyone the dark was why. But I've read the stones, Your Majesty. I've read the *memory* in them. The founders sealed the gate and then built you to keep the *gifted* down—that's all you ever were. A leash that learned to lie. The dark beyond the gate was never hungry. You were."

I gave the wards everything.

Everything. The last of it, the dregs, the part of the gift that lived closest to my heart and kept my blood moving. I felt it go, felt the fever stir in its long sleep and begin to stretch, and I kept pouring anyway, because Edran was right there, right *there*, at the end of the thread—one drowning man under the ice, and the crown cracking around him like a shell.

*"Brother."*

Not the crown's voice. A human voice, ragged and young and wrecked. Edran, surfacing through the ruin of his prison for the last time, and I felt him reach—not for the wards, not for power—for Aldric, who had fought his way back to the circle's edge with blood on his face and his brother's name torn out of him.

"Aldric," the ghost-voice whispered through the shattering crown. "*Do it.*"

Aldric drove his blade—not into flesh, never into Edran's flesh, but into the founder's sigil at the circle's heart, the keystone strike we'd planned, the mortal blow delivered by the last honest hand of the old royal line. Steel and stone and gift met crown.

And the crown shattered.

There was light. There was a sound like every bell in the realm rung at once and then broken. The parasitic intelligence came apart the way a lie comes apart—slowly, then all at once—and I felt a hundred years of stolen fire release in one vast exhale, washing over the battlefield, sinking into the earth, and every gifted soul within a hundred miles felt it: freedom, brief and blazing, like sun after a decade of cloud.

Edran died four seconds later. Free. I felt him go through the wards—felt the last flicker of him, unburdened, unbound, a tired man finally allowed to set down his load. His last breath carried two words across the link between crown and keeper, and they weren't for me.

*Thank you.*

* * *

After that, things got simple, which is how I knew I was in trouble.

The gate sealed itself—or rather, the founding-work finished what I'd fed it, drawing the last of my gift out in one final golden thread and knitting the wards whole. Stronger than they'd been in a century. Sealed for another hundred years, maybe a thousand, needing no crown, no parasite, no keeper's slow sacrifice to maintain them. Just the mountain, remembering what it was for.

The vanguard broke when the crown died. Veyra's officers threw down their arms in ones and twos, then in dozens—whatever spell the usurper had held over its army dissolved with it. I registered all of it distantly, from a great height, the way you register a party happening in the apartment below yours.

Because my gift was gone.

All of it. Guttered out like a candle pinched between wet fingers. And into the space it left, right on schedule, faithful as a debt collector, came the fever.

I remember Sefa catching me before I hit the stones. I remember her hands on my chest, hot as forge-coals, her voice saying words I couldn't parse—"burning out, the fever's in the blood, hold on, HOLD ON"—and I remember thinking, with the detached clarity of the nearly departed, that the floor of the threshold chamber was warm, and that the mountain felt different without the gift. Not silent. Just... ordinary. Stone being stone. A friend who'd stepped out of the room.

I remember Aldric's arms. He'd gotten to me somehow through the chaos, and he was saying my name in a tone I'd never heard him use—not the prince, not the commander, not the exiled brother carrying ten years of guilt. Just Aldric, scared, holding a woman who'd promised him she'd survive this and was beginning to suspect she'd lied.

"Wendell. Look at me. You look at me. You don't get to do this, not after everything, not after I—" His voice broke. "Stay."

Darkness took the rest.

But here is the thing about darkness, learned from a lifetime of listening to stone in the black places under the world: it is rarely the end of the story. It is usually just the part where the mountain holds its breath.

Somewhere far away, a heartbeat that was not entirely my own kept time.

Somewhere closer, a man refused to let go.

And the Last Spire, sealed and singing, kept watch over us both.
