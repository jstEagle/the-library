## 21. What the Gate Keeps

I woke on the third morning to the sound of Tam crying in the next room, which was how I knew I wasn't dead. The dead, in my experience—and I had recently acquired a great deal of experience with nearly dying—do not have to listen to apprentices weep into their sleeves at dawn.

The second thing I noticed was the silence where my gift used to be.

Imagine losing a tooth. Not one you can see when you smile, but one of the back ones, the kind your tongue finds anyway, worrying at that smooth absence in the dark. That was what waking felt like. My mind kept reaching for the mountain the way a tongue reaches for the gap, and finding nothing there but ordinary air and ordinary stone and an ordinary woman lying in an infirmary bed in Ashmarket with her hands too clean and her head too quiet.

I lay there for a while, cataloguing. Ribs: bruised. Lungs: scorched, apparently, though Sefa's healers had patched them before she left for the south. Legs: functional, if I didn't ask anything ambitious of them. Heart: beating. That last one surprised me most of all. I had poured everything I was into the Spire's wards—every scrap of stone-sense, every buried year of listening—and the old magic had taken it all and sealed the gate behind me like a door closing on a lit room. I remembered the dark coming up through my bones. I remembered thinking, quite calmly, *so this is what it costs,* and then thinking, less calmly, *Aldric is going to be insufferable about this.*

And then nothing, until now.

Tam came in with a basin of water and froze when he saw my eyes open. He was thinner than I remembered. There was a new scar along his jaw, pale as a fish bone, and the hollow look of a boy who had done something unforgivable and then been forgiven anyway, which is harder to carry than the sin.

"You're awake," he said.

"Observant," I rasped. "You'll make a fine gatekeeper."

His face did something complicated. He set the basin down, poured water into a cup with exaggerated care, and held it to my lips while I drank like an invalid, because I was one, and dignity is for people who haven't spent eleven days unconscious.

"The whole city knows," he said. "What you did. They're calling you the Last Keeper."

"I'm the *last* keeper because I'm the only one left, not because I'm good at it."

"Wendell." He set the cup down and looked at me with those earnest, ruined eyes. "You broke the crown. You killed a hundred-year-old parasite wearing the king's face. You can let yourself be a story for five minutes."

I considered telling him that stories are how crowns get started, but I was tired, and he'd earned better from me than barbs. So instead I said, "Help me sit up," and he did, and the world tilted and steadied, and through the narrow window I saw Ashmarket in the spring light—the burned quarter still black, but scaffolding climbing the bones of the granary like green shoots, and smoke rising from chimneys that hadn't smoked in a generation.

The city was rebuilding itself. It had decided to, without asking anyone's permission. There was a lesson in that, and I was too weak to pretend I couldn't learn it.

* * *

Aldric came that afternoon, carrying a basket.

This deserves emphasis. Aldric Veyan—exiled prince, rebel commander, the man who once ambushed a prisoner column expecting a weapon and got me instead—came through the door of my sickroom holding a woven basket containing bread, cheese, two apples, and a jar of honey, arranged with the nervous precision of a man who had asked three separate people what one brings to a convalescent and received three contradictory answers.

"You look terrible," he said.

"You brought me fruit."

"It seemed appropriate."

"Did someone tell you it seemed appropriate?"

"My sister-in-law would have said wine," he admitted, setting the basket down. "But Sefa left instructions that you're not to have any until your lungs stop sounding like a bellows with a rat in it. So. Apples."

He sat in the chair by the bed, and for a moment neither of us spoke. This was new between us. We had always been good at talking—barbed talk, careful talk, the kind of talk two guarded people do instead of the other thing. But since the Spire, since I collapsed at the threshold and he caught me with both arms and screamed for Sefa in a voice I'd never heard him use, some of the fencing had gone out of our conversations. What was left underneath was more frightening and much better.

"How long?" I asked.

"Eleven days. You scared everyone. You scared me especially, but I'm told I hide it beautifully."

"You're doing it right now."

"I am." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Edran is buried. Properly, with his own name on the stone. Veyra stood vigil the first night—she said he deserved one honest mourner who knew what he'd suffered. The council has offered me the throne four times. I've refused it four times. They're getting creative about phrasing."

"What did they try this time?"

"'Regent until a suitable heir is found.' Which means regent forever, since no heir will ever be suitable enough to displace them." He smiled without humor. "They don't understand. I watched what a century of sitting on that seat did to my brother. I wouldn't take that chair if it were made of gold and forgiveness."

"And what will you tell them?"

"That's partly why I'm here." He hesitated, and I realized—I actually realized, watching him—that the man who had faced down a legion and a parasitic intelligence older than his grandfather was afraid. Of me. Of a half-dead gatekeeper eating his apple. "Wendell. How much of you is left?"

It was a crueler question than he meant it to be, or perhaps exactly as cruel as he meant it to be, because Aldric never asked anything he wasn't prepared to hear the answer to. I put my hand flat against the wall beside the bed, palm to plaster, the way I had done ten thousand times against stone.

Nothing. No hum. No memory seeping up through the grain of things. Just wall.

Then—not nothing.

Not the gift. Let me be clear about that, because Sefa would want me precise and precision is a form of honesty. There was no power in it, no sense of the building's bones, no whisper of mortar and strain. What there was, faint as a smell that reminds you of a house you grew up in, was *memory*. The wall remembered being quarried. It remembered the mason's chisel, and rain on scaffolding, and the particular weight of a winter when the mortar froze before it set. And somehow, in the place where my gift had lived and burned out, there was a shape left behind—a hollow, exactly the size of remembering—that fit against that faintness like a key worn smooth.

"I felt something," I said slowly. "Just now. Not magic. Memory. The wall remembers being built."

Aldric went very still. "Is that possible?"

"Sefa said the gift doesn't vanish. She said it becomes part of you, the way a scar is part of you." I pulled my hand back and looked at it. "Maybe the wards didn't take everything. Maybe they took the power and left the... the listening. The habit of attention. I spent thirty years reading stone. Apparently thirty years doesn't just go away."

"Can you use it?"

"To know that a wall remembers being cold in winter? Marvelous. I'll revolutionize architecture." But even as I said it, something loosened in my chest—not hope exactly, hope was a currency I'd stopped trusting, but its poorer cousin, comfort. "No. I can't use it for anything. It isn't a tool anymore. It's just..." I searched for the word. "*Attention.* That's all that's left. Attention, and whatever the mountain chooses to give back."

"The mountain," Aldric repeated.

"There's something else." I had been turning this over since dawn, and I trusted him with it the way I trusted him with very few things: completely, and with advance warning that I might be wrong. "When I sealed the gate, I was inside the wards. All of them. For about four seconds I was the Spire—all its founding, all its purpose. And the founders built it for more than sealing. They built it to *keep*. A gate keeps things out, but a keep—" I shook my head. "There are rooms under the Spire that the vault didn't show us. Rooms the wards protect even now, with no keeper and no gift to tend them. Something is down there, Aldric. Something patient. And last night, half-dreaming, I could have sworn the mountain answered me. Faintly. Like a voice through a wall."

"Faintly," he said. "Patient."

"Yes."

"We've had enough patient things in our lives lately."

"We certainly have." I reached over and took one of the apples, mostly to have something to do with my hands. "So. You came here with fruit and a question. Ask it."

* * *

He didn't ask it immediately. He got up and walked to the window instead, and I understood that the walking was him gathering courage, and that he knew I understood, and that we were both going to pretend otherwise because pretending is also a form of courtesy.

"The free cities are meeting in six weeks," he said. "All of them. Even Corvess, which has never agreed to anything except mutual distrust. The crown is shattered. The legions are broken or turned—Veyra's among us now, gods help us all. And there is a hole where the government used to be, and everyone with an army or a fleet or a grudge is trying to fill it." He turned. "Rhea wants the harbors. The guilds want the treasuries. The council wants me on the throne because I'm familiar and damaged in ways they think they understand."

"And what do you want?"

"To burn the chair," he said simply. "To send word to the cities that the kingdom is over—not conquered, not reformed, *over*—and that what comes after should be built by people who remember what it cost. A league of free cities, each governing itself, bound by treaty and trade and nothing else. With one thing held in common." His eyes came to mine. "The Spire."

"The Spire is sealed."

"The Spire is *ours*. Sealed, yes—but it needs keeping. Not a king's garrison. Not a priesthood. Keepers. People who understand what the gate holds back and what it costs to hold it. Wardens on the roads, like you wanted. A school, maybe, someday, for anyone whose gift shows early—taught to serve themselves and their towns instead of being hunted for their blood." He paused. "And someone to watch those rooms beneath it. Whatever is patient down there."

I ate the apple slowly, giving myself time. Through the window, the scaffolding on the granary caught the late light.

"You're describing a country with no head," I said.

"I'm describing a country that learned what heads cost."

"And you want me—" I stopped, because the shape of it had arrived all at once, and it was so far from what I'd feared that I almost laughed. "Aldric. If the next words out of your mouth are 'queen,' I'm throwing this apple core at you."

"They are not." He came back to the chair and sat down, and took my hand—my scarred, washed-too-clean, entirely unmagical hand—with both of his. "The next words are: Warden of the Thornfell. Keeper of the Last Spire. First voice of the League, if you'll take it, because no one else alive understands the gate the way you do, giftless or not. Not a crown. A *charge*. Duty you choose instead of duty that was imposed—which is, as far as I can tell after forty years of watching the alternative, the only kind worth having."

"And you?"

"I'll be at the table. Negotiating. Arguing. Being familiar and damaged in ways they think they understand." He smiled then, really smiled, the one that made the exile lines around his eyes look like they'd been earned by laughter instead. "Someone has to keep the wardens funded. Bureaucracy is the true hero of any rebellion; it simply arrives too late for the songs."

"Aldric."

"Yes."

"Why me? Truly. There are cleverer people. Wholeer people. People whose lungs don't rattle."

He looked at our hands for a moment. When he answered, his voice had dropped all its polish, all the princely cadence, down to the bare man underneath.

"Because when I struck at the usurper and faltered—because it wore my brother's face—you stepped in front of a blade for a stranger. Because you spared Tam when every sensible commander would have executed him, and he's become the finest young keeper I've ever seen. Because you chose mercy twice and sacrifice once and never once asked what any of it would buy you. And because—" his grip tightened, "—because somewhere between Ashmarket and the Spire's threshold, keeping you alive became the only duty I cared about, and I found I liked it, and I have no intention of stopping. Not as your prince. Not as your regent. As yours. If you'll have me. Freely given, freely kept—or not at all."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a hammer rang on scaffolding, patient as a heartbeat.

"You understand," I said, when I could trust my voice, "that I'm a terrible prospect. I'm half-dead. I argue professionally. I will want to inspect every road-warden station personally, including the awful ones, and I will complain about the food in front of ambassadors."

"Duly noted."

"And I'm not gifted anymore. Whatever I was, the Spire has it now. What's left is memory and stubbornness and a great deal of experience being underestimated."

"Which describes roughly half of Ashmarket," he said, "and the best half, I'd wager."

I looked at him—at this man who had carried his brother's begging for ten years and laid it down at last on clean stone; who had been offered a crown four times and chosen a basket of apples instead; who was asking, not commanding, asking, which no one born to that family had ever learned to do—and I thought about the hollow in me where the gift had been, and how it had turned out not to be empty after all, but shaped. Shaped like listening. Shaped like choosing whom to listen to.

"Well," I said. "Someone has to watch what's waiting under the Spire."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's a yes with conditions. There will be many conditions. I'm drafting them tonight."

He laughed—actually laughed, head back, the sound bouncing off the sickroom walls—and then he kissed me, carefully, the way you handle something you've already lost once and refuse to lose again, and outside the window Ashmarket went on rebuilding itself, nail by nail and vow by vow, and far away in the Thornfell mountains the sealed gate of the Last Spire kept what it kept.

And deep beneath its threshold—in rooms no map named, in the dark the founders had warded against a future none of us had imagined yet—something old and patient turned over in its sleep, and listened back.
