## 22. The Last Spire Revisited

A year ago, if you'd told me I'd be walking these roads with a ledger in my satchel and a badge on my coat instead of a gate-key, I'd have laughed at you. Then again, a year ago I was still lying to myself about half a dozen things, so my judgment then is hardly worth citing.

The smugglers' road out of Thornfell has a new name now. Officially, the council of the free cities calls it the Voss Route, which Rhea pretends to hate and has had painted on her flagship's stern in letters tall enough to read from the harbor at Saltmere. Unofficially, everyone still calls it what it always was: the back way, the quiet road, the path that keeps its mouth shut. Some things shouldn't be renamed. A road that spent a century learning to keep secrets deserves to keep its name too.

I walk it as a road-warden. The title was Aldric's idea—he's full of ideas like that, ideas that sound like insults and turn out to be gifts. *You kept one road your whole life,* he said. *Keep all of them.* And so I do, or I try. There are forty-one miles of switchback between Saltmere and the high pass, and I have walked every one of them more times than I can count, checking culverts, mending cairns, settling disputes between carters who treat the mountain like a tavern brawl they can win.

Here is what nobody tells you about losing a gift: the mountain does not go silent. It simply stops answering. For the first months after the unbinding I would press my palm to stone and wait, the way you call a name into an empty house, and nothing came back. No tremor, no memory, no voice of roots and deep places. Just rock. Honest, dumb rock, which is apparently what most people get their whole lives, and which most people seem to survive.

I survived it too. Mostly because of what the mountain left behind when the magic burned out of me—not power, but something quieter. Memory. My own. The stones remember me the way an old dog remembers a hand that fed it, not because I ask anything of them anymore but because we spent years being acquainted, and acquaintance doesn't die just because the conversation ends.

Or that's what I told Sefa, before she left for the south. She put her warm healer's hands over mine on the gatehouse wall and said, "Wendell, that's not how any of this works," and then she felt whatever it is I feel, and went quiet, and said, "All right. Maybe. But don't tell anyone. They'll want to study you."

So I don't tell anyone. Old habits.

* * *

Saltmere harbor on a market morning is a thing I will never tire of, mostly because a year ago it was a thing that nearly didn't exist anymore. Rhea's fleet sits at anchor—six ships now, not four, two of them former crown vessels captured or bought or, in one memorable case, simply walked away from by a crew that decided smuggling under a free flag paid better than conscription under a hollow king. Rhea herself stands on the quarterdeck of the *Thornrose*, shouting at a rigger in language that would make a Thornfell goat blush, and when she spots me on the quay she interrupts her own tirade mid-word to cup her hands and bellow:

"Warden Kavanagh! Still ugly! Good!"

"Lady Voss! Still loud! Tragic!"

The harbor crowd laughs. A year ago they'd have flinched. That's the part I notice now, everywhere I go—the flinching is gone. People stand up straighter. They haggle louder. Children run along the seawall without looking over their shoulders for riders, because there are no riders anymore, no proclamations nailed to doors offering silver for the names of the gifted. We took down the last of those notices in Ashmarket in the spring. Tam did the prying. He kept the nails.

Rhea comes down the gangplank with the unhurried swagger of a woman who owns everything she surveys and knows it annoys people. She's wearing silk. Actual silk, dyed the deep green of the Thornfell pines, and I refuse to comment on it because she'll only say she wore it for me and then preen about it for a month.

"You're headed up," she says. It isn't a question. "This time of year."

"Annual inspection."

"Of a sealed door."

"The seal requires inspecting."

"Wendell." She stops at the bottom of the plank, close enough that I can see the gray coming into her hair that she'd gut anyone for mentioning. Her voice drops out of captain-register into something older, something from before the hunt, from the years when passage secrets were our currency and loyalty was a word we both pretended not to know the meaning of. "It's been sealed a year. It held through the thaw floods. It held through the quake in the western passes."

"I know."

"It held through *Veyra's* siege engines being dragged past it and back again, and that woman drives her legions like she's racing a funeral."

"I know, Rhea."

"So why do you climb up there every season like it owes you money?"

I look past her, at the mountain rising white-shouldered over the town, at the notch in the ridgeline where the Last Spire stands against the sky like a held breath.

"Because I'm the one who sealed it," I say. "And because once upon a time, somebody checked on me every season whether I needed checking on or not, and never told me why, and it turned out the why mattered." I shrug. "Also I leave flowers. You can't inspect a seal and leave flowers?"

"You could," Rhea allows. "Most people don't."

"Most people didn't spend ten years pretending they weren't someone's friend either."

She grins, sharp as a hook. "Careful, Kavanagh. Sentiment. Next you'll be telling me the parley flag meant something."

"The parley flag meant I hadn't decided yet whether to throw you off my wall."

"And now?"

"Now I bring you harbor manifests to check for toll evasion, so draw your own conclusions."

She laughs, and it echoes off the water, and somewhere in the laugh is the girl who used to trade me route-secrets for salt and silver, back when transactional was the only kind of loyal either of us knew how to be. We renegotiated that loyalty a long time ago. Turns out it holds better than the original contract ever did.

* * *

Tam meets me at the treeline, because Tam always meets me at the treeline, because Tam has turned punctuality into a personality.

He's taller than he was a year ago. They grow fast when they stop being afraid, apprentices; it's the fear that stunts them. He wears the keeper's grey now, real grey, not borrowed, and the gate-key hangs at his belt on a cord braided by Sefa before she went south—she braids things when she's nervous, and she was very nervous about leaving us, though she covered it by lecturing us both about hydration until we begged her to board the ship.

"You're late," Tam says.

"I'm never late. I'm geologically prompt."

"That's not a thing."

"It's absolutely a thing. Ask any stone."

He almost smiles. This is our ritual, this exchange, this careful dance around the thing between us—the fact that he sold me, that his betrayal put me in chains, that mercy marked me worse than a scar would have. We don't talk about it anymore. Not because it's forgotten. Because he carries it correctly now, the way a good gatekeeper carries a key: always, quietly, aware of its weight. Penance isn't a speech. Penance is showing up at the treeline on time, every time, forever.

"Report," I say.

"Gate stable. Wards cold and quiet—I've checked them daily per the protocols you wrote, which, for the record, are excessively thorough."

"They're meant to be thorough. I wrote them while dying."

"Noted." He falls into step beside me as we take the pilgrim path upward. "There's something else. Probably nothing."

"Probably-nothing is my favorite category. Proceed."

"A traveler came through three days ago. Trader, southbound, wanted water and directions. Harmless. But while I filled his skins he asked about the Spire—casual, the way people ask. How old it was. Whether anything lived up here besides me." Tam's jaw works. "And Wendell, he asked whether the gate had ever *sounded*. His word. Sounded."

I stop walking.

"What did you tell him?"

"That gates don't sound. That he'd been in the sun too long." Tam looks up at me, and I see it in him, the thing I both love and grieve: he's inherited my habit of hiding, and I hate that I taught it to him. "But after he left I went and listened at the door. Properly. Like you showed me. And I didn't hear anything. I swear I didn't. But the wards—" He hesitates. "They warmed. Just a little. Under my hand. Like a hearthstone hours after the fire's out."

The wind moves through the pines above us, and for a moment neither of us speaks, and the mountain stands around us with all its patient weight.

"Show me," I say.

* * *

The Last Spire in autumn is beautiful the way old soldiers are beautiful: scarred, weathered, standing anyway. The crown's shattering cracked the great arch of the threshold, and we chose not to repair it. Aldric argued for rebuilding; I argued for leaving the crack visible, and for once duty lost to sentiment. Let the crack stay. Let everyone who makes the pilgrimage up here—and they come now, gifted and ungifted alike, leaving ribbons and prayers and the occasional truly terrible poem—let them see what it cost.

The gate itself is a slab of black stone fused into the living mountain, seamless as frozen water, and across its face the wards lie like frost-flowers made of light that isn't quite light. Cold. Quiet. Sealed. I poured everything I was into them, once. Everything I had, everything I was, the gift that had kept my heart beating since childhood—the gift that was killing me slowly and keeping me alive quickly, which is a sentence only another keeper would understand. And when it guttered out, I thought that was the end of my story with this door.

Then Aldric caught me. That's the short version. The long version involves his hands shaking, and words I'd waited my whole cautious life to hear, and a binding that had nothing to do with magic—a vow spoken at the Spire's threshold with the mountain listening. Loyalty first, he said. Heart second, or possibly simultaneously; we've never managed to establish the order. The rebels offered him a throne afterward. He refused it until I woke, and refused it again after, and the free cities settled for making him Steward of the Reconciliation, which he performs with the grim diligence of a man doing penance for crimes that were never his.

I press my palm flat against the black stone.

Nothing. As always. Rock is rock. The gift is ash. I am a woman of entirely ordinary senses pressing her hand to a door, and I have made my peace with that, most days, on the days when peace is available.

But.

There it is. What Tam described. Not warmth exactly, not sound, nothing the gift would have translated for me in the old grammar of tremors and memories. Something fainter. Fainter even than memory. More like—

Attentiveness.

The wards aren't warming. They're *listening*. The way a sentry listens when footsteps come up a road at night. The way I used to listen, back when I was the only thing between the free cities and whatever waits in the hollow kingdom, back before I learned that the waiting itself is a kind of vigil, and vigil is a kind of love.

"Wendell?" Tam's voice comes small. "What do you feel?"

"Honest answer? Nothing. Less than nothing. My gift's gone, Tam, you know that." I keep my hand where it is. "But I spent fifteen years reading this mountain, and some habits live deeper than magic. And this feels like the feeling you get"—how do I explain it?—"when you pass a house at night and know, without seeing, that someone inside is awake."

"Do you think something's trying to get through?"

I consider the question properly, because he deserves proper answers now, no more of my old locked ledgers. The crown is dust. Edran died free beneath our hands, whispering thanks, and we buried him where he asked—at the threshold, facing the gate, keeping watch in death the way he never got to in life. The flowers I brought are for him, white ones, and for Sefa too, though she's not dead—she's in the south with a caravan of healers, teaching shared-power to villages that spent a century burning their gifted or hiding them, and her last letter said the work was *ruinous and wonderful*, which is the most Sefa sentence ever written.

"No," I say finally. "If something wanted through, we'd know. The wards would blaze, not listen. Whatever this is..." I trail off, because I genuinely don't know, and I promised myself I'd stop performing certainty the day I stopped hiding.

"Then what?"

"I think the mountain might be dreaming," I say. "And I think dreams start small. A flicker. A warming. A trader asking strange questions with casual eyes." I take my hand off the stone and immediately miss the contact, stupidly, the way you miss a conversation you can no longer have. "Or I think it might be nothing. A year of thaws and quakes and grief settling into the deep rock. Stone shifts. Even honest, dumb rock shifts."

"And if it's not nothing?"

I look at him—my apprentice, my betrayer, my penance, my partner in this long slow work of keeping—and I give him the truest thing I own.

"Then we do what keepers do. We watch. We write it down. We tell each other the truth even when the truth is inconvenient, especially then. And we don't hide it, not from each other, not from the cities, not from anyone." I tap the ledger in his satchel, then mine. "Two entries tonight. Yours and mine. Same page."

"Same page," he agrees, and something in his shoulders lets go, some old guilt easing another degree, and I think: there. That's the inheritance worth passing on. Not the gift. The telling.

* * *

I lay the flowers at Edran's marker as the sun goes down behind the western peaks, setting the peaks on fire the color of old crowns. White blossoms on grey stone. *Edran, king in spite of himself, keeper of nothing, prisoner of everything, free at last.* Tam stands beside me with his head bowed, and I catch him murmuring something—apology, or gratitude, or both; with Tam they've become the same word.

Sefa's flowers go at the base of the ward-stone she helped raise, the one inscribed with the names of the gifted who fell in the march on the capital. Her name isn't carved there because she isn't dead, and I tell the stone so, firmly, as if it might spread rumors otherwise. *Ruinous and wonderful*, Sefa wrote. Come home sometime and prove it.

Aldric is waiting at the gatehouse when we descend, having ridden up from Saltmere behind me because he cannot help himself, because the man survived exile and war and the loss of a brother twice over and still cannot bear for me to walk a mountain without him at the far end of it. He doesn't ask what we found. He reads it off my face, because he can, because a year of partnership has made him fluent in a language I didn't know I spoke.

"Well?" he says.

"Cold and quiet," I say. "Sealed and holding. And also—"

I hesitate. He waits. That's the other thing he learned: when I hesitate, don't push. Stand in the doorway of the pause and let me walk through it at my own pace.

"There was something listening," I finish. "Small. Patient. Not hostile. Not friendly. Attentive." I look up at the Spire darkening against the first stars. "I don't know what it means. It might mean nothing."

"Might it?"

"Might it what?"

"Mean nothing." He takes my hand, and his thumb finds the old calluses on my palm where the gate-key sat for fifteen years, calluses that remain though the key hangs on Tam's belt now. "You told me once the mountain remembers you. So tell me honestly, Wendell. When it listens like that—does it feel like a stranger?"

I think about it. Really think, the way I've learned to, with all my cards face-up on the table, no hidden ledgers, no locked drawers. The honest answer rises slow, like water finding its level.

"No," I admit. "It feels familiar. It feels like—" I stop, startled by my own conclusion. "It feels like the wards recognize something. Or someone. The way a lock recognizes a key it hasn't seen in a long time."

We stand there in the blue dusk, the three of us, keeper and apprentice and steward, with the sealed gate above us and the whole mended world below, and none of us says the obvious next thing, because saying it would make it real, and it's been such a short, hard-won year of peace.

Then the mountain trembles.

Not a quake—quakes I know, quakes are rude, sprawling, indifferent. This is different. This is a single, deliberate pulse under our feet, soft as a heartbeat, traveling up through bedrock and gatehouse floor and boot-leather into the bones of us, and it comes from the direction of the Spire, and it is unmistakably, impossibly, patient.

Like something turning over in its sleep.

Like something, after a hundred years of hunger and a year of silence, beginning to dream.

Tam's hand goes to the key at his belt. Aldric's arm comes around me. And I, who gave everything I had to seal that door, stand very still in the gathering dark and listen—with no gift at all, with nothing but memory and attention and love—to the mountain answering.

One pulse. Then stillness.

Waiting.
