## 1. The Taster's Oath

There are two rules to surviving nine years as the king's food-taster, and I learned them both in my first month, which is longer than most men in the profession last.

Rule one: never be hungry. A hungry man eats too fast and tastes too little. Rule two: never be certain. The day you decide a dish is safe because it looks safe is the day it isn't. Certainty kills tasters faster than arsenic.

I had broken neither rule in nine years. I intended to keep it that way until they carried me out feet-first at a respectable age, with a pension and a liver that had outlived three poisoners, two wars, and one archbishop.

So when I lifted the silver spoon to the king's venison stew that morning — a Tuesday, grey and ordinary as any other — I was not thinking about death. I was thinking about whether the cook had used too much juniper again. He had. He always did when his wife was angry at him, which was often, because he spent their savings on knives he didn't need.

The stew was clean. The bread was clean, though the crust on the second loaf tasted faintly of iron, which meant someone had stored it wrong or wanted me to think so. The honey was honey. The cheese had been cut with the same blade as the onions, which I noted and forgave, because the king liked his cheese sharp enough to argue back anyway.

Then came the wine.

It was a red from the southern valleys, the kind the cellar-master called "the bishop's choice" because it was expensive enough to make clergy feel forgiven. I poured an inch into the tasting glass, held it to the window light, swirled it, and did what I always do: I let it sit on my tongue and listened.

You learn to listen with your whole mouth, in this trade. Bitterness has a voice. So does sweetness that arrives too quickly, like a stranger calling you by your first name. And beneath the plum and the oak and the little lie of the vintage year, I heard something else — faint, green, the taste of a hedgerow at dusk.

Nightshade. Not enough to kill outright. Enough to make a man sleep very deeply and wake up someone else's problem.

I want to say my heart stopped. It didn't. Nine years in the kitchens of Aldric III teaches you that fear is just information arriving early. My heart kept its usual pace, and my mind began its usual arithmetic: who had access to the cellars between midnight and dawn, how much nightshade it would take, where a person even *buys* nightshade inside palace walls without the herb-garden ledger noticing.

What I did not do — and this is the part I still turn over at night, like a stone with something underneath it — was spit it out properly.

I swallowed.

A tasting swallow. Half a mouthful, the professional minimum, enough to confirm what my tongue already knew. I stood there holding the glass and thought, quite calmly: *you have just drunk poison meant for the king, and you have perhaps an hour before your knees stop taking orders.*

Then I did the second thing a taster must never do.

I hesitated before reporting it.

* * *

Here is what should have happened. I should have set down the glass, walked to the steward's desk, and said the words every kitchen dreads: "The wine is fouled." Guards would have sealed the cellars. Cooks would have wept. Someone would have been dragged to the dungeons by suppertime, guilty or not, and by evening the matter would have been a closed book with my signature as the seal. That is the machine. That is the oath working as designed. I am the sieve; the kingdom drinks through me.

Instead I stood at the sideboard with half a mouthful of death going warm in my blood and looked at the king.

Aldric III sat at the far end of the breakfast table with a map unrolled before him and a piece of bread he hadn't touched. Forty-six years old, they said, though the grey had come into his temples early and lately there were days the grey seemed to be spreading from the inside. He had a habit — new this winter — of stopping mid-sentence and looking at nothing, the way a man looks when a word has fallen out of his pocket and he can't remember which one it was.

"Blackwood." His voice carried down the long table. "You've gone quiet. In you that's either wisdom or disaster."

"The stew wants less juniper, Majesty."

"So does everything else in this kingdom." He gestured for the wine.

And there it was. The whole shape of my life compressed into one gesture: a man's hand reaching for a cup, and me standing between the cup and whatever the cup contained. For nine years I had been the space between those two things. It was, I realize now, the only place I had ever felt useful, and possibly the only place I had ever felt safe — because a man who tastes everything cannot be surprised by anything.

"Majesty," I said, "there may be a fault in the vintage—"

He looked at me then. Really looked, the way he rarely did, with attention that felt less like a king appraising a servant than like something older appraising everything at once. And he smiled.

Not the court smile. Not the smile he wore at audiences, polished and weightless. This was smaller, wearier, and — I will swear this on whatever grave eventually holds me — *sad*.

"I know," he said quietly.

I don't think anyone else heard it. The room went on being a room: the steward scratching figures, the under-cooks arguing about chestnuts, the fire popping in the grate. But I heard it, and the words landed in me like a coin dropped down a well — a long time falling, no bottom to strike.

He knew.

He took the glass from my hand — took it *from me*, which kings do not do, kings are handed things — and he drank. All of it. Slowly, watching me over the rim the whole time, and there was such exhaustion in his eyes that I nearly forgot the nightshade spreading through my own body and felt sorry for him instead.

"Good year," he said, setting the glass down. "Sweet at the end."

Then he returned to his map as if nothing had occurred, and the room continued believing itself ordinary, and I walked out of the hall on legs that were beginning to disagree with me.

* * *

Maren found me twenty minutes later in the tasters' antechamber, sitting very still on a bench with my hands flat on my knees, doing the breathing exercises Corvin taught me in my first year. Small sips of air. Count backward from a hundred. Do not fight the poison; negotiate with it.

Corvin taught me that too. Old Corvin, spymaster, keeper of secrets, the man who pulled a half-starved orphan off the streets eleven years ago and said, *you have a suspicious face, boy — that's worth more than an honest one.* Everything I knew about staying alive fit in a box with his handwriting on it.

Maren was the court physician, which meant she was the only other person in the palace whose job description included expecting the worst. She was sixty, small, and built like a question nobody wanted answered. She took one look at me and didn't ask what happened. She knelt, pressed her thumb hard into the soft part of my wrist, and counted.

"Nightshade," she said.

"You can smell it?"

"I can smell it on *you*." She was already pulling vials from her bag. "Dose?"

"A sip. Maybe less."

"And the king drank the rest of the glass."

"He did."

She stopped moving. Just for a moment — half a breath — but I've watched people freeze their whole life, and I know what it means when a woman who deals in poisons forgets her own hands. "All of it?"

"All of it."

"How much did you take?"

"Enough."

She worked in silence then: bitter draught, charcoal, something that smelled like a swamp having a bad decade. I drank it all without complaint. This is the other thing about my profession — you get very good at swallowing things you'd rather not.

"It should have dropped him," Maren said at last, not looking at me, packing her vials away with unnecessary care. "That dose, undiluted, in a man his age? He should be on the floor by now. Instead he walks, talks, and compliments the vintage." Her eyes came up, sharp and grey. "Either someone diluted the cup badly, or—"

"Or," I finished for her, because somebody had to say it, "the king is not a man who can be poisoned."

We let that sit between us. Outside the narrow window, snow was starting again over the capital, settling on the rooftops the way lies settle on a court: softly, evenly, everywhere.

"There's a story," Maren said slowly, "that my grandmother told. About the old kings. Before the Unification. She said the Thornkings didn't die like other men. She said—" She stopped herself with visible effort, the way you'd snatch a child back from a well's edge. "Old women's talk. Forget it."

"I'm good at forgetting," I said. "It's most of my job."

But I wasn't good at forgetting this. Because here is the thing I haven't confessed yet, the thing that made me hesitate in that hall, the crack running quietly through the middle of my careful life:

When the king drank that wine, when he watched me over the rim of the glass with that exhausted, ancient sadness — for half a heartbeat, I understood him. Not sympathized. *Understood*, the way you understand a language you were never taught but somehow speak. Something in him reached across the room and something in me answered, and for that half-heartbeat I could feel what he felt: a weight like a mountain sitting on a man's shoulders, old and patient and *hungry*, pressing down through the crown.

Then it passed, and I was Gideon Blackwood again, food-taster, orphan, survivor, a man with a suspicious face and an empty stomach, standing in a warm hall full of people who had no idea anything was wrong.

Maren left. I stayed on the bench, negotiating with my own pulse.

By evening, the nightshade had loosened its grip — my tolerances are a strange inheritance, built dose by dose over nine years, and a single sip was a tax I could pay. By nightfall I was steady enough to walk home to my cottage outside the walls, past guards who nodded at me the way you nod at furniture.

I should have reported the incident. I told you that already. Here is what I actually wrote in the log, in my neat taster's hand:

*Wine, southern red, fault suspected in storage. Discarded. No action required.*

A lie, entered into the record of a kingdom built on them. My first. Nine years of honesty, ended on a Tuesday.

I told myself it was prudence — that reporting would raise questions, questions would raise Corvin, and Corvin would start pulling threads, and threads, in this city, tend to be attached to necks. I told myself the safest place for a dangerous secret was inside the one man trained to keep secrets.

That was true. It just wasn't the whole truth.

The whole truth is that when the king said *I know*, some part of me — some part older than my name, buried deeper than Corvin's training — believed he wasn't talking about the wine at all.

I banked my fire, barred my door, and lay awake listening to the snow.

Near dawn, half-dreaming, I heard my grandmother's voice singing in the dark of memory — one of her forbidden lullabies in the dead tongue of the Thornkings, the ones she'd hummed when she thought I was asleep, the ones I'd forgotten for thirty years. *Sleep, little root, the mountain holds you.*

And in the grey hour before sunrise, when the capital was nothing but white silence and smoke, someone knocked on my door.

Three knocks. Small ones. Low down, at the height of a child's hand.

I lay perfectly still and did not breathe, because rule two had just shattered along with rule one, and everything I knew about the world suddenly tasted of hedgerows and old sadness and something coming up the mountain road through the snow toward my door.

I got up, and answered it.
