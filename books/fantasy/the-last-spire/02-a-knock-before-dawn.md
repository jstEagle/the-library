## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

There are three sounds a food-taster learns to fear. The first is silence in the kitchens — silence means someone has stopped pretending to work and started watching you. The second is the scrape of a chair in a private dining room when the king leans forward to watch you swallow. The third is a knock at your door before dawn.

The first two I had lived with for nine years. The third was new.

I want it on record that my cottage outside the palace walls was chosen with care. It sits at the end of Tanner's Lane, where the lane gives up on being a street and becomes mud. The tanners' reek keeps casual visitors honest; nobody strolls past vats of rotting hides unless they mean to be there. My neighbors are men who work with their hands and ask no questions of the pale fellow who leaves before light and returns smelling faintly of other people's dinners. Perfect. A tomb with a hearth.

So when something knocked — three small raps, spaced like a heartbeat stumbling — I lay flat under my blanket and did what any sensible man trained in suspicion would do.

I listened.

Wind. The drip of eaves. Somewhere down the lane, a dog that had been barking at nothing all night finally gave up on nothing and went to sleep. Then, again: three little knocks, softer now, as if whatever waited at my door was losing its nerve.

I have poisoned myself so many times, in such careful increments, that my body is less a temple than a ledger — every entry a small death survived, every column balanced against the day some clever cook gets ambitious. I know exactly how long aconite takes to stop a heart, how hemlock tastes like a lie about parsley, how arsenic makes a man's bowels betray him in front of the court he's spent his life serving. I am, by the crown's own accounting, one of the most valuable objects in the kingdom. Objects do not open doors before dawn.

But here is the flaw in nine years of training: it teaches you to weigh danger, not kindness. And the knocking had gone quiet in a way that suggested whoever was out there might simply lie down and die on my step, which even at my most cynical struck me as poor manners to leave unattended.

I took the knife from under the floorboard — not the kitchen knife I keep visible for burglars, but the real one — wrapped a blanket around my shoulders, and opened the door on a world gone grey and wet with the hour before sunrise.

The child stood ankle-deep in the mud of my step like something the river had tried to return and failed to deliver.

* * *

Let me describe her properly, because the details mattered and I was too cold and too surprised to understand why at the time. A girl, perhaps six years old, maybe seven — children that age are all elbows and eyes until they grow into themselves. Mud to the knees. Mud on her hands, in her hair, a smear across one cheek as if she'd wiped her face with a dirty sleeve and thought that solved things. She wore what might once have been a wool coat of good make, now ruined beyond redemption, and no shoes that I could see. Her feet were blue.

She was not shivering.

That should have frightened me more than it did. Anything else about her — the stillness, the way she looked up at me without the wariness children usually carry toward strange men with knives half-hidden behind doors — anything else I could have filed away. But she wasn't shivering, her lips weren't blue the way frozen children's lips go blue, and when I crouched to look at her properly her eyes were clear and dark and calm as well-water.

"Well," I said, because I'm a fool, not a mute. "You're a long way from anywhere."

She answered me.

Not in Low Court speech, nor High, nor the dockside cant I'd learned tasting for harbor officials, nor the border dialects I'd picked up from merchants' wines. She spoke a language that came out of her like birdsong with bones in it — liquid vowels, consonants that clicked soft at the back of the throat, a rhythm that rose and fell in patterns too regular to be random. Words, certainly. Sentences, probably. A language I had never heard spoken aloud in my life.

And yet.

Here is the thing I have told no one but this page: I understood her the way you understand music. Not word by word. Not sense by sense. But somewhere beneath my ribs, in the place where a man keeps things he doesn't examine, something stirred and said, *you knew this once.*

"*Cael en dorath?*" she said, tilting her head. "*En dorath, cael?*"

Cold, said something deep in me, some drowned grandmother-voice. She's asking if you're cold. No — asking if *she's* cold, or telling you she isn't. Asking permission, or offering reassurance. The words were gone the moment I reached for them, like names in a dream.

"I don't speak that," I said, which was true, and also somehow a lie.

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then reached out and took hold of my blanket with two muddy fingers and pulled. Toward the warmth. Away from the door.

Nine years of training says: never let anyone pull you anywhere. Nine years of training says: an unknown child at your door before dawn, speaking a dead tongue, feet blue with cold and body strangely warm, is either a trap or a plague or worse, and the correct response is to close the door, bar it, and report the anomaly through channels.

But channels meant Corvin, and Corvin's channels had felt wrong since the night before, since the king drank nightshade-laced wine and smiled at me with his mouth while his eyes did something I couldn't name. And besides — and I record this with all the dignity I can muster — she had mud on her face and her fingers were shaking now, finally, the cold catching up to whatever had kept it out, and I am apparently the kind of man who cannot stand in his own doorway and watch a child shake.

I stepped aside. She walked in like she'd always lived there.

* * *

I put her by the fire, which required first building the fire, which required admitting to the pre-dawn dark that I was awake and doing something unusual. I found the old blanket from the chest — the one I'd never thrown out because throwing it out would have meant deciding something about my grandmother — and wrapped her in it. I heated water. I put bread and honey in front of her because it was what there was, and watched her eat with the focused, unhurried attention of someone who has known real hunger and intends never to perform gratitude for it.

While she ate, I examined her, because examination is the only prayer I know.

No wounds. No marks of binding or rope. Her wrists were thin but not scarred. Under the mud, her skin was pale in the way of someone who hasn't seen much sun lately, though her hands were odd — calloused in strange places, the fingertips nicked and worn as if she'd spent months handling stone. There was dirt ground so deep into the creases of her palms that no single day's wandering could account for it. She had been outside a long time. Weeks, maybe. In winter.

And there — when she pushed back the wet hair from her forehead — the markings. Thin lines, grey-white as old scars, tracing her brow and cheekbones in patterns too deliberate to be injury. They caught firelight oddly. Not shining, exactly. More like the firelight *recognized* them and behaved differently where it touched.

I had seen those lines before. That was the thing sitting cold in my stomach while I pretended to be a competent adult making breakfast. I had seen them carved, not worn — cut into stone, deep and ancient, in a place I had no business ever having seen. My grandmother's hands over mine in the dark of her cottage, guiding my finger along a rubbing she'd taken from somewhere she never named. *This one means the mountain remembers,* she'd said, in the tongue scholars swore no living person spoke, in lullabies the priests would have burned her for singing. *Say it back, little thief. Words are doors.*

I'd forgotten. That's what I'd tell myself. You forget things at eight years old; that's what childhood is for. I built a whole life on having forgotten — the orphaned nephew of a dead hedge-witch, apprenticed young to a spymaster who valued a boy with no history, trained into the crown's service, immune by chemistry and anonymous by design. Forgotten.

The child looked up from her bread and sang four notes.

My hands stopped working. I stood there with a wet cloth in my grip and my heart going like a trapped bird, because I knew the song. Four notes rising, then a fifth that bent downward like a branch under snow. My grandmother's song. The one she sang when the lamps went out. The one I had not heard in thirty years and could not have written down if you held a knife to my kidney — and I would know, I've had knives held to considerably more than that.

The girl finished the phrase and watched me with those still dark eyes, waiting, the way you wait for someone to catch a ball you've already thrown.

"*Thaeli,*" she said softly. And then, slower, pointing one grimy finger at my chest: "*Thaeli en varr.*"

Keeper. The word surfaced like a body in a lake, bloated and unmistakable. *Keeper.* Or watcher, or warden — the root was the same, something that stays awake while others sleep. She pointed at my chest again. "*En varr. Thaeli.*"

"You have me confused with someone," I said.

She shook her head with terrible patience, the way you'd correct a slow student, and went back to her bread.

* * *

I'll confess what happened next, since this page has already heard worse: I sat down across from her and I talked. To a child who could not understand a word of it, I laid out my situation like a merchant displaying damaged goods.

"Here is what I know," I told her. "Last night the King of Thornmark drank wine I had tasted and pronounced safe — except it wasn't safe, it was full of nightshade enough to drop a bull, and he drank it anyway, smiling at me, and this morning he will be holding court as if nothing happened. Either someone switched the cup after I tasted it, which makes me a dead man walking, or the king is something that doesn't die, which makes everything a dead man walking. And now you arrive at my door speaking a language from before the kingdom existed, wearing marks I've only seen on stones older than the crown, and singing my grandmother's songs." I rubbed my face. "I am a cautious man. I have survived by noticing things and doing nothing. Do you understand the position you've put me in?"

"*Varr thaeli,*" she agreed solemnly.

"That's not — we're not communicating, you understand that, yes?"

She set down the bread crust with great care, leaned forward, and pressed her small muddy palm flat against the floorboards beside the hearth. Then she looked at me expectantly.

I nearly laughed. Then I felt what she wanted me to feel — no, that's imprecise, I'm a skeptic by trade and I don't use words like *felt* lightly — I became aware of something. Through the floor. A low, slow pulse, far beneath the cottage, beneath the lane, beneath the tanners' yards and the city walls. Steady as a sleeping heart. I'd lived above it for six years and taken it for the millrace, the deep water turning the wheels of the lower town. Except the millrace didn't come up through my palm and settle in my teeth.

The girl lifted her hand. The pulse faded, or my awareness of it did.

"*En dorath,*" she said, and touched the floor again. Home. The mountain. She was telling me where she came from, in the only way that survives translation: *the thing underneath us. That's where I belong. That's what I am.*

"And you came to me," I said slowly, "because your grandmother's grandmother decided my grandmother should sing songs to a child who didn't know what he was being given."

She didn't answer, because she didn't need to. Outside, the sky had gone from grey to the bruised violet that precedes actual sunrise, and down the lane I heard the sound I dreaded almost as much as silence: boots. Several pairs. The particular unhurried cadence of men authorized to be wherever they are.

I moved to the window slit and looked out through the gap in the shutter.

Four of them. Palace guard, off-duty dress, which is to say armed and pretending not to be. Not searching house to house — not yet. Walking Tanner's Lane slowly, looking at doors. One carried a leather folio, and I knew what was in it before I knew anything else about the morning: questions. About the wine. About the taster. About whether anyone had seen anything unusual.

They hadn't come for me. Not yet. But the folio meant my name was in someone's mouth, and names, in my profession, are how the eating starts.

Behind me, the child began humming my grandmother's lullaby again, soft and unbothered, as though royal guardsmen were weather.

Here is what I should have done. Walk out my own door, greet them pleasantly, volunteer for questioning, tell my tidy story — *yes, captain, the vintage seemed off, I flagged it, I'm sure the cellars are investigating* — and hand the girl's existence to Corvin's people, who would fold her into a report, seal it, bury it, and bury me somewhere nearby for neatness. That is the play that keeps a tool alive. Tools that notice things get sharpened; tools that act get replaced.

Instead I heard myself say, quietly, to a six-year-old in a dead woman's blanket:

"When they knock, you're my sister's girl. Sick. You don't speak — fever took your hearing last winter, poor lamb. Can you look sick?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she closed her eyes, sagged against the blanket, and produced from somewhere in her small theatrical soul a shivering, damp-cheeked, glassy-eyed invalid so convincing that I felt my own forehead for fever.

"Right," I said. "Good. Wonderful. We're both going to hang."

The boots stopped outside my door.

Three knocks. Spaced like a heartbeat — steady, official, nothing stumbling in them at all.

I crossed the room, and just before I opened it, the child caught my sleeve. Two muddy fingers, same as before. When I looked down she wasn't playing sick anymore; she was looking at me with those well-dark eyes, and she said one word in the old tongue, very clearly, the way you hand someone a key.

"*Thaeli.*"

Keeper.

I opened the door.
