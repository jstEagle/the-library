## 3. What the Wine Did Not Kill

I had buried the wine bottle under the floorboards before dawn, which tells you everything about how I spent my first night as a man with a secret worth hanging for.

Not that burying it was a good plan. It was a terrible plan. But there is a particular kind of panic that makes even bad plans feel like action, and I had drunk deep of it. The bottle held proof — nightshade in the king's cup, poured by someone inside the palace, drunk by the king without effect. Either my tongue had failed me after nine years of flawless service, or something was wrong with the king. And since my tongue had never once failed me — I'd have been dead at twenty if it had — I was left with the worse of the two possibilities.

So I sat in my cottage with a dead fire, a locked door, and a child who did not sleep.

She sat on the floor by the hearth's ashes, cross-legged, watching me the way cats watch things they intend to kill eventually. She hadn't spoken since dawn. Her language had gone quiet, but her eyes hadn't. They tracked every move I made: when I banked the coals, when I checked the window shutters, when I took down the little knife I use for trimming lamp wicks and set it within reach. She approved of the knife. I could tell. It was the first thing she'd looked at all morning without suspicion.

"Breakfast," I said, because talking to her kept me from talking to myself, and talking to myself had stopped producing useful results somewhere around midnight.

I set out bread, hard cheese, and the last of last autumn's apples. She ate like a soldier home from a long campaign — methodically, gratefully, wasting nothing — and then she ate mine too when I looked away. I decided not to hold it against her. Anyone who eats like that has known real hunger, and real hunger leaves marks no one talks about.

It was midmorning when the knock came. Three raps, spaced evenly, unhurried. I knew the rhythm before I knew the visitor.

Maren.

The court physician had keys to half the palace and opinions about all of it, and she was the only person in Thornmark who could walk into my cottage uninvited and make it feel like an inspection rather than a trespass. She was sixty, built like a rain barrel, and she had delivered three royal children, poisoned two traitors on royal orders, and saved my life twice — once from aconite, once from my own arrogance. I trusted her the way you trust a surgeon: completely, and only while unconscious.

I opened the door a hand's width. "This is a bad time."

"Wonderful." She pushed past me into the cottage, bringing cold air and the smell of yarrow and horse. She stopped two steps in and looked at the child. The child looked back. Neither blinked for a length of time I found frankly unsettling on both their parts.

"Gideon," Maren said quietly. "What is that?"

"A child."

"I can see it's a child. Children don't appear in tasters' cottages in the middle of the night. Children don't—" She crouched slowly, keeping her hands visible, which told me she'd already catalogued the child as a potential threat, which meant the child had already done something threatening, or simply existed with enough intensity to qualify. "Where did you find her?"

"At my door. Mud to the eyebrows. Speaking—"

"The old tongue," Maren said.

My stomach dropped through the floorboards and kept going. "You understood it?"

"No." She was still crouched, studying the girl's forearms where the sleeves had slipped. "But I know what it sounds like. There are wax cylinders in the palace archive. Recordings from the Thornking excavations, forty years ago, before the crypt was sealed. A scholar named Ellery Vance made them. He went mad afterward, or so the story goes. Said the language was still *inhabited*." She finally looked up at me. "May I?"

I didn't know what she was asking permission for. She took the child's arm anyway, gently, turning it toward the grey window light.

The markings ran from wrist to elbow — thin lines, silver-pale against the brown skin, like veins seen through ice. They weren't scars. They weren't paint. They followed a pattern, branching and rebranching, and at the crook of each elbow the branches gathered into a knotwork shape I recognized with a sick lurch, because I had seen it before. Everyone who worked the palace had seen it. It was carved above the sealed doors of the old chapel crypt, worn nearly smooth by centuries, the sigil nobody spoke about because speaking about it invited questions, and questions about the Thornkings were career-limiting at best.

"That matches the crypt carvings," Maren said. She said it flatly, the way she announced symptoms. "Precisely. Not approximately. Precisely."

"She's a child," I said. "Children get birthmarks."

"Birthmarks don't match four-hundred-year-old masonry, Gideon. Birthmarks don't branch along lymph lines. I've treated a thousand children and I've never seen anything like this, and neither have you, and we both know it." She released the girl's arm and sat back on her heels, looking suddenly very tired. "Show me her eyes."

"Why?"

"*Her eyes.*"

I crouched beside them. The child tolerated my proximity with the wary patience of someone allowing a dog to sniff her. Up close, in proper light, I saw what I should have seen the night before: her irises were shot through with fine silver threads, radiating outward from the pupil like frost forming on glass.

"When did you first notice?" Maren asked.

"Last night. I assumed it was lantern light."

"It wasn't lantern light."

"No," I agreed. "It wasn't."

* * *

Maren examined the child for two hours. I learned more anatomy in those two hours than I had in nine years of learning poisons, because poisoners learn bodies only as targets, whereas Maren knew them as territories. She listened to the child's heart with a brass tube and frowned. She pressed fingertips along the silver markings and watched the child watch her. She asked the girl questions in plain speech, in trade-tongue, in the few words of hill-dialect she knew, and got nothing but that level, ancient stare.

Then, near the end, the child did something that made Maren go white.

Maren had unpacked her kit on my table — vials, scales, a mortar, a small flame burning under a crucible. The child reached across, deliberate as a magistrate, and touched the rim of a vial of distilled spirits of wine. Just touched it. And the liquid inside went still. Not frozen — stilled, the way water goes still when something vast passes beneath it. Then it settled again, ordinary, and the child withdrew her hand and looked at Maren with an expression I can only describe as *apologetic.*

"What was that?" I whispered.

"I don't know." Maren capped the vial with hands that were not quite steady. "I don't know, and I am going to lie awake tonight inventing answers, and none of them will be good." She began packing her kit with brisk, overprecise movements. "Here is what happens now. You are going to bring this child to the palace. Tonight. My chambers. There are texts in the restricted archive I need to consult, and I'm not carrying them here."

"No."

"No?"

"No. Whatever this is, the palace is the last place—" I stopped myself. Careful, Gideon. "The guards are already asking about the wine. If anyone sees me arrive with a marked child who speaks a dead language, I'll be answering questions until spring. Or until I stop being able to answer them."

Maren paused, a vial halfway into its slot. "What do you know about the wine?"

I told her. All of it — the bitter edge under the plum, the nightshade signature, the king drinking it anyway, the king standing unharmed while his cupbearer hovered and his steward smiled. I watched her face while I talked. Physicians have faces like open ledgers; everything gets entered somewhere.

She didn't gasp. She didn't go pale. What she did was close her eyes for a moment, the way you do when a diagnosis you've been dreading turns out to be correct.

"How much nightshade?" she asked.

"Enough to stop a horse. Enough that I spat it out and reached for the emetic before I'd finished tasting. The king drank the full cup."

"And nothing."

"Not a stumble. Not a sweat. He set the cup down and complimented the vintage."

Maren sat down heavily on my one good chair. For a long moment the only sound was the child, humming. Low, tuneless, in that dead language — the same three notes over and over, patient as dripping water. Maren stared at her.

"Gideon. In thirty years of court medicine, I have compiled a private list. Things observed that cannot be, filed under explanations that are worse than the observation." She rubbed her eyes. "Nightshade tolerance isn't acquired. It isn't immunity like yours — yours is built dose by dose over years, and even you would be ill after that quantity. That king drank enough belladonna extract to kill a bull and his pupils didn't so much as dilate. There are exactly two kinds of creature in this world that don't die of poison. The dead ones, and whatever your grandmother used to sing about."

I went cold from the scalp down. "My grandmother sang about weather. And goats."

"Mm." Maren stood, buckled her kit, and fixed me with a look I had last received from her at age nineteen, lying in the infirmary after the aconite. "Bring her tonight. If not to the palace, then somewhere I can work. Because I promise you, Gideon — if the guards are asking questions about the wine today, tomorrow they'll ask different questions. Questions like *who else knows.* And you want to know what you know before they decide what you know."

She let herself out. The child hummed. I sat down on the floor next to her because my legs had opinions of their own, and after a moment she leaned against my shoulder, feather-light, and kept humming.

Three notes. Over and over. Patient as dripping water.

Somewhere in the back of my skull, in the place where my grandmother's forbidden lullabies lived, something almost remembered the words.

* * *

They came at dusk.

I heard the horses first — shod hooves on the frost-hardened lane, too many of them, moving too slow for travelers and too deliberate for drunks. I had maybe ninety seconds. Long enough to snuff the lamps, not long enough to hide a child. Where, exactly? Under the floorboards with the wine? In the woodshed, where her footprints would print fresh snow like a signed confession?

I settled on the worst option available, which was the truth: I opened the door and stood in it.

Four riders. Palace guard livery under travel cloaks, and leading them a sergeant I knew by sight and reputation — Brannoc Hale, a square, patient man with a voice like a landslide and a memory that never forgave. He'd once arrested a chamberlain for stealing candle stubs. He took pride in thoroughness the way other men take pride in courage.

"Taster Blackwood." He didn't dismount. "Odd hours for a walk."

"I live here. These are my hours."

He swung down anyway, and his men fanned out with the easy, awful competence of people doing routine work. One drifted toward the woodshed. One toward the well. Hale came up the path and stopped an arm's length away, close enough that I could smell his horse.

"You tasted the king's wine yesterday evening."

"I taste the king's wine every evening."

"This one turned. Kitchen boy swears he saw you spit. Steward says you called for the emetic and cleared the room." He said it mildly, conversationally, a man discussing grain prices. "Yet His Majesty suffered no ill effect. The physician's report says so. Signed by Physician Maren herself."

"Maren examined him personally," I said. Which was true. She had. What she'd written in the report was another matter, and I'd bet my cottage that Maren had written *no adverse effects* with her jaw tight and her eyes on the middle distance.

"So the wine was clean," Hale said.

"The wine was clean," I agreed.

His eyes flicked past my shoulder, into the cottage, into the dark beyond the lamplight. Behind me, faint as breath, the child had gone silent. Utterly silent — no humming, no rustle. Even her breathing seemed to have folded itself away. It was, I thought distantly, a very useful talent for a child to have, and a very strange one.

"But you spat," Hale said.

"Because I'm paid to spit. That's the whole office. I taste, I judge, I err on the side of spitting. If the king wants a taster who swallows anything, he can hire one cheaper."

Something flickered behind Hale's eyes — not belief, not suspicion. Filing. He was filing me away, and I felt it happen, the way you feel a splinter go in.

"There was a second matter." He produced a folded paper, unfolded it with ceremony. "A gatewarden at the River Gate reports a mud-covered child, age six or seven, slipped past the toll post two nights ago, heading into the low town. No one reported a missing child since. No household claims her." He refolded the paper. "Guards ask around. It's what we're for. You see any such child?"

"No."

"You're sure."

"I'm sure the answer is no."

Hale looked at me for a long, quiet moment. Snow had started falling again — fat, slow flakes that landed on his shoulders and didn't melt, which told me more about the temperature than any almanac. Then he nodded, once, and turned back toward his horse.

At the mounting block he paused. "Blackwood. Nine years you've served. Good service. Clean service." He didn't look back. "Clean service is a thing men protect. Remember that, when someone offers you something dirty."

They rode out. I counted to two hundred before I closed the door.

The child was standing in the middle of the room. When she saw me, she pointed — not at me, at the door, at the road beyond it — and then she drew her finger slowly down her own forearm, tracing the silver markings, and looked up at me with an expression of enormous, exasperated clarity.

*I know,* the gesture said. *They're coming for me too.*

"Yes," I said aloud. "I've noticed."

She shook her head. Impatient. She pointed at the door again, then pressed her palm flat against her own chest, then — this was the part that undid me — she pointed at me, and mimed, with surprising accuracy, my own motion from the tasting hall: fingers to lips, the spit, the flinch.

*They will make you swallow something,* she was telling me. *And you will not be able to spit it out.*

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, the laugh of a man whose options had all died of exposure. She didn't smile. She waited, arms crossed now, a six-year-old magistrate awaiting my formal response.

Outside, the snow thickened. Somewhere down the valley, muffled by miles and weather, a horn sounded — one long note, then silence. Palace signal. I'd heard it perhaps five times in nine years, always for matters of succession or war.

The child cocked her head at the sound, listening the way you listen to a familiar song. Then she began, softly, to hum — and this time the melody rose and fell in shapes I knew. Shapes my grandmother had sung over the wash-basin when she thought no one listened, songs she'd sworn me to forget, songs she'd called *mountain-mother's tunes*, sung in a language she claimed was just old border dialect.

It was not old border dialect. It was the same dead tongue she'd spoken at my door. And I understood the words now, or some of them, the way you understand a dream:

*Come home before the ice closes.*
*Come home, little piece of the mountain.*
*Before the thief finishes wearing our king.*

I stood in my cottage with my heart going like a siege drum, and I understood two things with perfect certainty. Someone had sent this child — sent her deliberately, at cost, through the gates and the guards and the winter — because there was no one left inside the walls they trusted to carry a message. Whoever they were, they were probably dead. Messages like this one are written in blood before they're ever written in words.

And the second thing: come morning, I was going to have to decide whether I was a man who swallowed what the crown fed him, or a man who finally, after nine years, spat it out.

The child watched me decide. She was very patient.

She'd had, I suspected, a great deal of practice waiting for fools to catch up.
