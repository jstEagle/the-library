## 4. The Roads I Was Never Supposed to Know

There are three ways out of Thornmark, if you're a soldier. There's the King's Road, which is the king's road in the way a cage is a bird's house. There's the pilgrim track through the lowlands, wide enough for carts and narrow enough for ambushes, and every guard captain worth his salt knows it. And then there are the roads I was never supposed to know.

Corvin taught me the first two himself, back when I was nineteen and hungry and flattered that a spymaster had noticed me. He taught me maps and cipher, poison and patience, and he drew the borders of my world with a ruler: here is the kingdom, here is your duty, here is everything outside it, which does not concern you.

It was my grandmother who taught me the third way, though she never called it teaching. She'd take me mushrooming on the mountain's southern flank when I was small enough to still believe grandmothers were ordinary, and she'd walk us along goat tracks and dry creek beds and say things like *the stream remembers where it used to run, so follow the water when you've forgotten where you're going*, and *never trust a pass with a pretty name; ugly names were given by survivors*. I thought she was just old. It took nine years of tasting death in thimblefuls before I understood she'd been handing me an inheritance, and that the crown had never known about it, because the crown only counts what it can tax or hang.

So when I slipped out of the capital at dawn with a mud-caked child under one arm and Maren's stolen satchel of medicines over my shoulder, I did not go south to the gates. I went down.

The tanners' district sits against the old city wall like a drunk leaning on a fence, and beneath it there is a drainage channel from the reign of some king so forgettable even Wren couldn't have named him. The grate has been loose since before Corvin started buying secrets, because I loosened it myself six years ago, on a night I told myself was idle curiosity and now recognize as a man building a door out of a life he hadn't yet admitted he wanted to escape. I'd spent that whole evening watching Aldric — the real Aldric, the flickering one — laugh at something his fool said, and the laugh had been so brief and so human and so quickly smothered that I'd walked home shaking and cut iron all night without knowing why.

The child fit through the gap easily. So did I, barely, after leaving my coat snagged on the bars as a gift to whoever came looking. Let them read what they liked into a discarded coat. A taster who flees leaves a coat; a taster who's murdered leaves a body, and they wouldn't find one, and that discrepancy would buy me half a day while Corvin's people argued about it.

Half a day. In my experience, half a day is what you get between making a terrible decision and learning its price.

* * *

Wren did not talk much that first morning, which suited me, because I did not want to hear her voice and remember how much of it I understood. We climbed north through the birch scrub above the river, keeping to the shadow line where the slopes went steep enough that no rider would bother, and I taught her the first lesson of survival the way my grandmother taught me: how to walk.

"You're walking like someone being chased," I told her. "Being chased gets you killed. A hunted animal runs flat-out until it drops. A survivor moves slow, looks back often, and never walks the same ground twice."

She looked at me with those eyes — too dark, too old, the eyes of a child who had been something else recently and hadn't finished grieving it — and then deliberately exaggerated her stride into a comical stalk, knees high, like a heron hunting.

"That's worse."

Her mouth twitched. First almost-smile. I noted it the way I'd note an unfamiliar flavor on the tongue: carefully, and with a suspicion it might be poison.

By midday we reached the first of the smuggler caches, a hollow beneath a fallen slab where decades of contraband had passed through and left their leavings. My grandmother had shown me this one too, though what she'd cached I never asked — dried fish, mostly, and rope, and a clay jar of honey gone to sugar but still sweet. I fed Wren honey off the end of a knife and watched her eat like someone relearning the concept of hunger, and something in my chest did a thing I decided not to examine.

"Water," I said, pointing at the stream. "Water." Then I pointed at her, and at myself. "Gideon. Wren."

"Wren," she agreed solemnly, as if confirming a treaty.

I pointed at the stream again. She considered it for a long moment, then said a word — soft, consonants flowing together like water over stones, and I felt it land somewhere below language, in that cellar of memory where my grandmother's lullabies had been fermenting for thirty years.

*I know that word,* I wanted to say. *My grandmother sang that word to me when the fever took my mother, and I didn't know it meant water, I thought it was just the sound grandmothers make.*

Instead I said, "Close enough," and drank.

That was the shape of our days, that first week. I taught her fire — small fires, smokeless, fed on dry wood and regret — and she taught me words. I taught her which mushrooms lie and which tell the truth, holding each specimen up to the light like a magistrate examining testimony, and she taught me the word for *mountain*, which turned out to be nearly the same word as the word for *bone*, and I sat with that for a long time, turning it over, thinking about carvings in sealed crypts and markings on a child's skin.

I taught her to sleep in shifts. She taught me that the old tongue has no separate word for sleep and death — or rather, it has one word, and the distinction is made by context, and she demonstrated the context by sleeping curled against my side with the absolute trust of someone who had apparently decided I was the context.

Loyalty, I realized somewhere around the fourth day, had always been a seal to me. Wax and ribbon and the weight of an oath spoken in a room where the wrong breath could kill you. I'd been loyal the way a lock is loyal: mechanically, professionally, to whatever key the crown put in me. This — a child's breathing weight against my ribs, her fingers hooked into my shirt in her sleep like a claim staked — this was loyalty with a face. It was significantly less convenient.

* * *

On the sixth night, the weather turned, and so did she.

We'd holed up in a shepherd's bothy, one of a dozen abandoned when the wool trade moved east, and the rain came down like the sky was settling accounts. I got a fire going in the old hearth, and Wren stood at the window slit watching the water sheet off the mountainside, humming.

Not a tune I knew. Not a tune anyone living would know. It had a rising-falling pattern, five notes repeated with variations, and the hair on my arms stood up before my mind caught up, because I *knew* it — not the melody, but the grammar of it, the way it circled back on itself the way grief circles back, the way my grandmother's lullabies had done.

"What is that?" I asked.

She stopped. Turned. Looked at me with an expression I couldn't parse, and then sang it again, slower, and pointed at the rain. Then at the mountain through the rain. Then at herself.

I sat down slowly on the cold hearthstone.

"It's not a song about them," I said. "It's a song *to* them. To the rain and the mountain and—" I gestured at her, uncertain. "And you. It's a song about belonging to a place."

She nodded once, gravely, and then did something that undid me more thoroughly than any poison ever had: she took my hand, pressed it flat against the hearthstone, and sang the five-note phrase again, pointing at my hand, at the stone, at me.

You also belong. That's what she was saying. I'm fairly sure that's what she was saying, though the old tongue is slippery and children are imprecise and I have spent nine years learning that anything can be a trap.

But I let my hand stay on the stone, and I listened to her sing the rain down the mountain, and I thought about my grandmother walking me along dry creek beds, singing in a forbidden tongue, teaching me roads I was never supposed to know — and I wondered, for the first time, whether she'd been hiding me from something or preparing me for it. Whether the lullabies had been love, or assignment, or both, which is the answer that fits most things in my experience.

"I don't know what you are," I told her quietly, in the trade-tongue she half-followed. "But I know what they'll do if they catch you, and it isn't going to happen. Whatever else is true, that isn't."

She watched my face the whole time I spoke, reading it the way I read a dish — not the ingredients, but the intent. Then she leaned her head against my shoulder and kept humming, and outside the rain kept falling, and inside something I had kept locked for nine years quietly came off its hinges.

* * *

We made the high country by the ninth day, and that's where the trouble found us, because trouble has a nose for people who've started feeling safe.

The pass my grandmother called — in her own idiom, translated — *the one the goats won't take* runs north through a fold in the ridge where the rock narrows to a corridor maybe eight feet wide, walled on either side by stone slick with seep-water. No soldier uses it because no soldier knows it, and no smuggler uses it because the toll is paid in broken ankles. I use it because the alternative is the valley road, and on the tenth morning the valley road had riders on it.

I spotted them from the ridgeline: six horses moving in formation, unhurried, methodical, quartering the ground the way Corvin had taught me to quarter ground twenty years ago. They weren't chasing us yet. They were mowing the grass ahead of the chase.

"Change of plans," I murmured, and Wren, who had learned to read the change in my spine before she learned the words for *food* and *fire*, crouched without being asked.

The goat-pass cost us most of the day and one bad moment when the ledge crumbled under my heel and I hung by my fingers over a drop that would have ended this story early, with Wren hauling on my wrist with a strength no child should have, her face set in a ferocity that looked ancient on her small bones. When I got my knee back onto solid rock I lay there panting and she knelt over me and said a word — sharp, urgent — and touched my chest, then her own.

I didn't need translation for that one. Some words cross languages intact.

We camped that night in a hollow below the ridge with the wind combing the grass flat, and I built the smallest fire of my career and we ate the last of the smoked fish and I caught her watching me across the flames with an expression I recognized, because I'd worn it myself at nineteen, sitting across from Corvin: the look of someone deciding whom to be loyal to, knowing the choosing will hurt.

"Ask me," I said. "Whatever it is. Ask."

She spoke slowly, in the fragments of trade-tongue she'd gathered from me like burrs, mixed with the old tongue I half-dreamed in now. What I assembled, word by word, was a question: *When we arrive — what will you lose?*

Because she'd seen it. Children see everything; they lack only the vocabulary to file it. She'd seen me counting my own immunities the way a miser counts coin, dosing myself with the mild toxins I'd built tolerance to, checking my reflexes, watching my hands for tremor. She'd seen me understand that whatever waited for us at the end of these roads, the man walking them was spending himself to walk them.

I could have lied to her. I'm good at lying; it was on the job description.

"The same thing everyone loses," I said instead. "Everything. Eventually. The trick is choosing what it buys."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, then reached into the folds of her rag-wrapped bundle — the one thing she'd carried out of the capital besides herself — and produced a smooth gray pebble, river-worn, unremarkable except that when she pressed it into my palm it was warm, though it had been sitting in cold cloth all day.

She said the old-tongue word for *mountain*. Then the word for *bone*. Then a third word I didn't know, and pressed my fingers closed around the stone.

A promise. A ward. An anchor. Possibly all three. I closed my fist around it and found I couldn't give it back.

"Get some sleep," I said gruffly, because the alternative voice available to me was not one I was prepared to use. "Tomorrow we drop into Bracken Hollow, and the woman who runs that town would sell her own shadow, so keep your marks covered and your mouth shut unless I ask."

She lay down against my side, already half-asleep, trusting as gravity.

I stayed awake longer, turning the warm pebble in my fingers and listening to the wind move down from the peaks, and it was in that hour — cold, exhausted, wanted by six riders and whatever stood behind them — that I finally admitted what I'd known since the doorway: I wasn't running *from* the palace anymore.

I was running *to* something. I just didn't know yet that the something had teeth.

Far down the valley, faint as a struck match, a light appeared where no light should be — three flashes, spaced evenly, the signal Corvin had drilled into me twenty years ago, the one that meant *I know where you are, and we need to talk.*

He always did hate sending men when a message would do.
