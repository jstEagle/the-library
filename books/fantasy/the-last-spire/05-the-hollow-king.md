## 5. The Hollow King

I remember snow.

Not the snow that falls now, grey and grudging, melting before it touches the flagstones because the palace is built on old fires that never quite die. I mean real snow — the kind that came down over Thornfell when I was nine years old and my mother still called me *Aldie* and nobody had yet taught me that a king's body belongs to the crown rather than to himself. Snow you could eat. Snow that made the whole world quiet, as if the mountain itself had put a finger to its lips.

That memory comes back to me on Tuesdays.

I don't know why Tuesdays. The thing that lives behind my eyes keeps no calendar I can read, though I suspect it keeps one of its own, and I suspect the solstice matters to it a great deal. But every seventh morning, more or less, the grip loosens for a moment — like a hand uncurling in its sleep — and I get to be a man again for the length of a breath or two. Sometimes less. Once, gloriously, nearly an hour, during which I wept into my breakfast porridge while the thing screamed at me from inside my own skull, furious as a wasp trapped under a cup.

This morning is a Tuesday. I can tell because the grip has loosened and I am tasting salt.

*You are crying,* I inform myself, with the detached interest of a man reading someone else's letter. *How undignified.*

The chamberlain — Pellam, his name is Pellam, hold onto it, hold on, names are ropes and you are drowning — stands by the window with the day's petitions. He does not look at me. None of them look at me anymore if they can help it, which suits us all: they are frightened of whatever it is they see sitting on the throne when I'm not driving, and I am frightened of what they see too, because I have caught my reflection in the wine-dark bottom of a goblet and it is not entirely my face. There is something around the eyes. Something patient. A king should never look patient. Patience is for things that are waiting, and kings are supposed to be already here.

"Petitions, Majesty," Pellam says. "The harvest tithe from the eastern valleys. A dispute over mill rights in—"

"Mill rights," I say, and my voice comes out rusted, a door not opened in years. "Whose mills?"

Pellam blinks. It is a small blink, but I have learned to treasure small things. "The... the Dunmore mills, Majesty. As petitioned."

"And what does the Dunmore man want?"

"He wants—" Pellam falters, and I understand why. I have not asked a question with genuine curiosity in it since — since when? Since the winter my daughter was born? Since the night I woke with a headache like a nail driven through the crown of my skull and a voice in the dark of me saying, softly, *there you are, there you are, we've been waiting such a long time*?

"He wants fair water shares," Pellam finishes carefully. "Majesty."

"Then give him fair water shares," I say. "It's his river. Well. His father's. Well." I rub my face, feeling the strange topography of it, the cheekbones that don't sit where mine sat. "Give him the river. Give everyone the rivers. What are they doing with them all this time?"

Something shifts behind my ribs. Not pain, exactly. Pressure. The sensation of a landlord arriving early to inspect the property.

*Aldric.* The voice does not use sound. It has never used sound; it speaks directly into the meat of me, the way cold speaks into bone. *You are tired. Rest.*

"I am resting," I say aloud, and Pellam takes a discreet step backward, because from the outside I suppose I appear to be conversing with the tapestries. In fairness, the tapestries are better company than most of my court. At least the weavers put honest work into them. *Go back to sleep, little king,* the voice says, gentling itself in that way it has, that horrible parody of tenderness. *The petitions will keep. Everything keeps. That is what we do. We keep.*

"No," I say.

It has been three hundred years, I think, since anyone in this palace told it no. Perhaps I am the first. It is difficult to feel proud of being first at anything when your predecessors' only legacy is the shape of their surrender.

*No?*

"No," I say again, louder, and Pellam drops the petitions, and somewhere a guard's armor clinks as he reaches for a sword he suddenly imagines he needs. "I want — write this down, Pellam, write it down before it goes — I want to see my daughter."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down on the room like snowfall. Real snowfall. Thornfell snowfall.

"Majesty," Pellam says at last, very quietly, "the princess is at Silverholt."

"I know where she is," I snap. "I sent her there. Did I send her there?" Because that's the cruelty of it, the exquisite engineering of the thing: I can no longer tell which decisions were mine. Nine years ago I began waking up inside choices I did not remember making, standing at windows I did not remember walking to, signing papers in handwriting that was mine and somehow not. The thing doesn't erase me. Erasure would be mercy. It edits. It keeps enough of Aldric to make the puppet credible and cuts away everything that might notice the strings.

But it made a mistake once. It let me keep the snow.

"You will send word to Silverholt," I say, gripping the arm of the throne — my chair, my chair, I paid for this chair with thirty years of a life I barely got to live — "and you will tell her that her father loves her, and that her father remembers her birthday, which is in the spring, and that she was born in the east tower during a storm and the midwife said she had her mother's temper before she'd finished drawing her first breath, and if any of you have ever wondered whether the king is still in here—"

My fist strikes the armrest. The crack is satisfying. Less satisfying is what happens next, which is that my hand stops obeying me, fingers uncurling one by one like a flower deciding to bloom, and settles flat and calm upon the carved oak.

*Majesty,* says the voice, and now it is not gentle at all. Now it uses the voice it saves for special occasions, the voice of a thing that has worn thirty kings and buried twenty-nine of them politely. *You are embarrassing yourself. Sit quietly. Smile at Pellam. We have work today.*

And I smile at Pellam.

God help me, I smile at Pellam, and the smile is warm and kingly and utterly empty, and I watch through my own eyes as the chamberlain gathers his scattered papers with shaking hands, and I scream — I scream the way a man screams underwater, full-throated and utterly silent, and no one hears it, because the mouth it would come out of is busy smiling.

* * *

Here is what I know about them, gathered across nine years of stolen minutes, hoarded like a miser's coins:

They call themselves the Spires. I found the name once, scratched into the underside of a drawer in my grandfather's study — my grandfather, who died at forty-one with his mind gone soft, mumbling about mountains — and I have spent fragments of Tuesdays piecing together what it means. They are old. Older than the dynasty, older than the capital, maybe older than the kingdom, which would explain a great deal about how the kingdom has been run. They bind themselves to the crown line the way ivy binds itself to a wall, and they drink what kings have most of: memory. Years. The taste of being a man who matters. And in exchange—

In exchange, nothing. That's the joke. There is no exchange. There is no ancient bargain struck for the realm's protection, no necessary evil, no burden nobly borne. I used to imagine there must be. I used to lie awake in the hours I could call my own constructing justifications, because the alternative — that three centuries of kings were farmed like cattle by something that wanted nothing but the living of our lives secondhand — the alternative was unbearable.

It is also, as far as I can determine, true.

They want the binding made permanent. I felt that much clearly last solstice, when the grip tightened and something vast turned its attention toward the north, toward Thornfell, toward the mountain where the Spires were born or bound or buried — even they seem careful with that word — and I understood that a clock is running. Whatever ritual holds them to us wears thin with each generation. Each binding costs them more. Each king lasts them less. My grandfather lasted eleven years. My father, six. I am at nine and fraying, and they know it, and they are looking for thread.

Which brings me to the wine.

Three nights ago — or four; time slides when you're a passenger — the evening cup arrived and I smelled it before the taster did. Nightshade, faint as a rumor, folded into the dark fruit of the vintage so skillfully that only someone who has spent nine years cataloguing poisons against his own palate could catch it. I waited for the taster to stop me. That is his whole office, poor devil: Gideon Blackwood, a quiet man with careful hands and eyes that miss nothing, who has swallowed death on my behalf more times than either of us can count. I watched him taste. I watched him go still, that particular stillness, the stillness of a man finding a coin in his soup. I thought: *now. Now he raises the alarm. Now the cup is dashed out and I am spared and perhaps, perhaps, whoever poured the poison is caught and questioned, and perhaps the questioning leads somewhere, anywhere, out of this.*

And then the Spires took the cup from my taster's caution the way you'd take a toy from a child, poured the wine down my throat themselves, and laughed — not outwardly, never outwardly, but somewhere in the cellar of me, a dry chuckle like stones settling.

Because nightshade cannot kill what is already dead. And because they recognized the vintage.

*Someone knows,* the voice murmured afterward, savoring it, and I felt its attention swing south and east, toward the city, toward a cottage I could not see. *Someone has been careless. Or brave. We will find which.*

So whoever tried to kill me — and I want you to appreciate the absurdity of a poisoned king longing for his poisoner the way other men long for rescue — whoever it was knew something. Knew the wine, knew the court, knew a way to reach a king guarded by a parasite that tastes every danger long before it arrives. An enemy of the Spires is the only friend I have left, and I don't even know their name.

I know one thing more, though. Last Tuesday, in one of my borrowed hours, I dreamed of the crypt beneath the old chapel — sealed these two hundred years, off-limits even to me, which for a king is a remarkable feat of off-limits. In the dream I stood among the stone kings of my line, and every effigy had its hands crossed over its chest not in peace but in restraint, as if holding something down. And I heard, in the dream, a language I have never studied and somehow knew — my great-grandmother's tongue, the Thornkings' tongue, the tongue the histories say died with the mountain's old magic — and the words meant, as near as I could render them:

*What was split must be carried home.*

I woke with the phrase in my mouth like a stone. I have been carrying it ever since. I do not know what was split. I do not know who carries. But the Spires heard the dream too — I felt them flinch, actually flinch, the first fear I have ever sensed in the thing that owns me — and since that morning the guards at the gates have doubled, and riders go out daily, and the whole palace has begun to lean northward like a plant toward light.

Or toward fire. With the Spires it is difficult to say.

* * *

Evening. The grip is loose again — a gift, or a trap; with them the two are often indistinguishable — and I have bribed a scullery boy with a silver piece I kept hidden in the heel of a boot for exactly this kind of hour, and he has brought me what I asked for: paper, ink, and no witnesses.

My hand shakes. Not from the parasite. From age, or disuse, or simply from the enormity of writing down what I am about to write, because ink makes things real and reality, in this palace, is fatal.

*If you are reading this,* I begin, and stop. Who reads it? No one. Everyone. The next king, hollowed before his coronation oil dries. Some servant clearing out my effects after the thing discards me. A stranger, centuries hence, prying up floorboards in a palace that may no longer stand.

I write anyway. What else does a ghost do but leave messages?

*If you are reading this, then the king is dead, or worse, and you should know he was in here the whole time. Every year. Every Tuesday. Listening. If the crown sits heavy on your head, it is heavier than you think, and it is not the gold that weighs it.*

I scratch on through the candle's guttering — my childhood, my marriage, my daughter's laugh, the night the headache came and the voice with it, nine years of secondhand kingship — and when I finish I fold the letter twice and seal it with wax and press into the wax not the royal seal, which they would recognize, but the plain ring my mother gave me, the one with the mountain sigil worn nearly smooth.

Then the question of hiding it. The drawer trick is known to them. Under the mattress, behind the loose stone — they taught my tutors those tricks, probably. No. It must go where they do not look, and they do not look at the crypt beneath the chapel, because whatever lies entombed down there frightens them, and I have waited nine years to learn that anything does.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow I will find a reason — a devotion, a penance, some pious fiction my stolen mouth can perform — and I will carry the truth down among the stone kings, and hide it where only someone who knows the dead tongue would ever think to search.

It is a slender hope. A thread. But I was a king once, before I was furniture, and I remember what hope feels like in the hand: light, and sharp at both ends, and worth bleeding for.

Somewhere below, in the warren of the palace, a bell rings the ninth hour, and the pressure begins to build behind my eyes like weather coming over the mountain.

*There you are,* says the voice, returning, settling into me like a hand into a glove. *Miss us?*

Every Tuesday, I think. You give me Tuesdays.

I wonder, sometimes, if it is mercy.

I know better. It is bait. Even a caged animal sings sweeter when it believes the cage has a door — and they need me singing, need this vessel pliant and plausible, until the solstice. Until the binding goes permanent. Until whatever remains of Aldric, son of Merric, father of one, lover of snow, is filed away among the thirty kings who came before him, worn smooth as my mother's ring.

Enjoy it while it lasts, I almost say aloud. Something is coming for you out of the north. Something split and small and carried in arms that do not know yet what they carry.

I felt it this morning, faintly, when the grip was loose: a note in the old tongue riding the wind off the mountains, thin as a child's voice, growing nearer.

*What was split must be carried home.*

Hold on, little king, I tell myself, smiling at nothing, alone in the dark of my own head. Help is coming. I think it is very small, and I think it is very lost.

But it is coming.
