## 6. Salt and Silver

Bracken Hollow smelled the way it always had: woodsmoke, wet wool, and the particular stink of a town that made its living by not asking questions. Nine years since I'd last walked its crooked streets. It hadn't improved. Neither had I, probably.

I'd dressed Wren in a boy's coat two sizes too large and taught her three rules before we crossed the boundary stones. One: don't speak. Two: don't look at anyone's hands. Three: if I squeeze your shoulder twice, you run for the tannery ditch and stay under the boards until I come or until dawn, whichever comes second.

She'd looked at me with those pale eyes — eyes the color of frost on slate — and nodded once, solemn as a magistrate. Then she'd held up four fingers.

"No fourth rule," I said.

She pointed at herself, then at me, then made a shape with her hands that I didn't understand but understood anyway, the way you understand a lullaby in a dead tongue. Something about *staying*. Something about *not leaving*.

"Rule four: nobody gets left," I said, feeling like an idiot and a liar both, because rule four was the one rule I couldn't promise to keep. She seemed satisfied regardless. Children forgive you your lies in advance. It's one of their few mercies.

* * *

Vessa kept court in the back of a salt-house on Tannery Row, which told you everything about how far she'd risen. When I was young and stupid and running goods through Corpse Pass with more nerve than sense, she'd operated out of a proper house with a sign and everything. Now she worked behind barrels of brine, because a queen who can't smell her own kingdom is no queen at all — her words, once, over cards I still owed her money from.

The guard outside the salt-house was new. The guard inside was not.

"Hal," I said. "You've gotten fat."

"You've gotten dead-looking," Hal said, lowering his crossbow by degrees. "Gideon Blackwood. Vessa said you were hanged in the capital."

"Rumors of my hanging are only slightly exaggerated." I stepped aside so he could see Wren. "I need passage north. Quietly. Tonight if it can be managed, tomorrow if it must be."

Hal looked at Wren the way men look at anything small and strange in Bracken Hollow, which is to say he looked at what she might be carrying rather than what she was. "She yours?"

"She's mine to get where she's going."

"That answer's going to cost you extra."

"It usually does."

He grunted, banged twice on the door behind him, and let us into the warm dark, where the air bit at the sinuses like a debt collector. And there she was, Vessa Marrow, smuggler queen of the Thornfell roads, sitting behind a table scarred by thirty years of knives, dice, and bad decisions — some of them mine — looking exactly as I remembered and somehow ten years older, the way mountains look the same and aren't.

"Gideon Blackwood," she said, without looking up from the ledger she was marking. "They said you were the king's dog now."

"They say a lot of things in the capital."

"They do." She set down the pen. Looked up. Her eyes did the thing they'd always done — took inventory of me in one pass, itemized my weapons, my exhaustion, the mud on my boots, the child half-hidden in my shadow, and arrived at a total before I'd finished clearing my throat. "You're running. Not errands. Running-running."

"I need the high road through Thornfell. Your road. Before solstice."

"Solstice is eleven days off."

"Which is why I'm here instead of somewhere sensible."

She leaned back. Around us, her people went very carefully about their business, pretending not to listen, which in Bracken Hollow was the loudest form of attention available. "Sit," she said. "Both of you. Hal, wine."

"I'll pass on the wine," I said.

Something flickered across her face — not hurt; Vessa didn't do hurt. Recalculation. "That bad?"

"The king drank poisoned wine last week and stood up afterward. Since then, everyone who's asked me questions has been asking them in that particular voice people use when the answers have already been decided." I sat, and pulled Wren onto the bench beside me, and she sat the way she did everything, perfectly still, watching. "I need to be gone from anywhere with walls before the walls start asking."

Vessa poured her own cup, drank, and studied Wren over the rim. I watched her study. That was the danger with Vessa — not that she was cruel, though she could be, but that she was thorough.

"Whose is she?" she asked.

"Complicated."

"Everything worth money is complicated. Whose. Is. She."

"A dead woman's," I said, which was true in ways I only half understood. "And a mountain's."

That got me a raised eyebrow. Vessa Marrow had been raised eyebrowed at by kings' men and never returned the favor; when she did it to you, you knew you'd said something interesting.

Then Wren moved. She reached across the table — quick as a heron striking — and turned Vessa's left hand palm-up before either of us could react. And there, in the webbing between thumb and forefinger, old and faded as a watermark, was a mark. A spiral. A spire drawn in a single unbroken line, coiling inward.

Wren touched it with one finger. Said something in the old tongue, soft, and the word — I felt it land in my chest before my ears got involved, the way grandmother's lullabies used to — meant something like *sister*, or maybe *ember*, or possibly both at once, because the old tongue had never cared much about the difference between a thing and what it warms.

Vessa snatched her hand back like she'd been burned. For one long moment the smuggler queen of the Thornfell roads looked genuinely, nakedly afraid, and I learned more in that moment than any interrogation would have given me.

"Everyone out," she said quietly. "Except these two."

Her people filed out with the practiced indifference of professionals. Hal hesitated at the door. "Hal," Vessa said, and he went.

* * *

When the door closed, she rolled her sleeve up past the elbow.

There were more marks. Three spirals now, climbing her forearm like rings on a tree — the oldest faded nearly to nothing, the newest sharp enough to sting. She held her arm in the lamplight and let me look, jaw tight, daring me to comment.

"My grandmother had one," she said. "My mother had none. I thought it ended with Gran — a birthmark, a superstition, a story old women tell." She lowered the arm slowly, like putting down something heavy. "Then this one came in last winter. On the solstice night. I woke up with my arm burning and ink spreading under my skin like it was being written from inside the bone."

"What did your grandmother say about it?"

"She died when I was nine. All I remember is that she wouldn't go near the mountain after dark, and she sang songs in a language nobody spoke, and when she was drunk she'd say our family name wasn't ours. That it had been taken and written down somewhere, and until it was written back, we were all just borrowing ourselves." She smiled without any humor in it whatsoever. "I built a smuggling empire on the assumption that was drunk-talk."

"And now?"

"Now there's a child in my salt-house who looked at my hand like she recognized the handwriting." Vessa turned to Wren, and her voice changed — dropped its armor, or most of it. "What are you, little one?"

Wren looked at me. Asking permission, or translation, or both.

"She doesn't speak the common tongue," I said. "Or she does and won't. She speaks the old tongue. And before you ask — yes, I understand some of it, no, I don't know why, and no, I don't want to talk about my grandmother right now either."

"Your grandmother too?" Vessa barked a laugh. "Gods below. Half the Thornfell families have one of those grandmothers. Mine called hers 'the listening years.' Thought it was senility." She leaned forward. "So here's my position, Gideon. Royal guards came through three days ago asking after a missing food-taster. Paid well. Very well. I told them I hadn't seen you, which was true, and also that I'd send word if I did, which was a lie, because I'd already decided that whatever you'd done, you'd done it running *away* from Aldric's court, and in my experience anyone fleeing that court in winter has better reasons than the men chasing him." She tapped the table. "But now there's this. Now there's marks waking up on my skin and a child speaking the Thornkings' tongue, and I find myself wanting things I can't buy. So let's negotiate."

"Name your price."

"Passage through Corpse Pass, my best guides, safe houses the whole way to the mountain's foot. In exchange—" she paused, and for the first time since I'd known her, Vessa Marrow looked like she was choosing her words rather than spending them, "—when you reach wherever you're reaching, you open a door for me. There's a vault. Under the Last Spire. Every smuggler's grandmother sings about it — the Vault of Names. My family's name is written there. Taken. I want it back."

The candle flame leaned in a draft that hadn't touched the rest of the room. Wren had gone very still beside me — that particular stillness, the one I'd learned meant the mountain was paying attention through her.

"I don't know what's in that vault," I said. Which was honest. I didn't know what the Spire was, not really. I knew Wren was a piece of something broken loose, and I knew the king drank poison like water, and I knew every road I'd been forced down since that night in the kitchens led uphill. "And I can't promise you a door I might not live to open."

"I know." Vessa smiled, and it was almost the old smile, the card-table smile, all teeth and arithmetic. "That's why the price is fair. You're promising me something that costs you nothing today and might cost you everything later. Best kind of contract. The only kind that ever holds between people like us."

"Because it's counterfeit," I said.

"Because it's *real*," she corrected. "A coin that's only good later, if we both survive, is the only currency that's never been counterfeited in this town. Everything else is brass." She stuck out her marked hand. "Do we have terms?"

I should have negotiated harder. I should have asked what she knew about the Spires, about the bindings, about why her family's name had been taken and whose ledger it sat in. I should have noticed that she'd said *the Vault of Names* without flinching, as if she'd rehearsed it, as if she'd been waiting years for someone to walk through that door heading uphill.

Instead I shook her hand. Her grip was strong and her palm was cold, and beneath our joined hands, faintly, I saw the spiral on her wrist darken — just a shade, just for a heartbeat — as if the ink had tasted the moment and approved.

"Terms," I said.

* * *

We left within the hour. Vessa gave us oiled cloaks, dried fish, and a guide named Sorrel who was seventeen and silent and walked like snowfall. At the edge of town, where the road climbed toward the tree line and the last lamps of Bracken Hollow shrank to embers behind us, Vessa caught my sleeve.

"One more thing," she said, low, so Sorrel and Wren wouldn't hear. Or so she thought. Wren heard everything; I'd stopped believing otherwise days ago. "The guards who came asking — they weren't Aldric's usual men. They wore his colors, but they moved wrong. Like puppets learning to walk." She glanced back at the town, at the darkening east, at the mountain waiting under its snow. "And Corvin's with them. The old spymaster himself. He asked for you by name and by habit — asked what roads you liked, what wine you drank, whether you still counted exits in every room you entered." Her mouth twisted. "Sounded like a man describing his own work."

Ice went down my spine wearing Corvin's face. Of course he'd come himself. You didn't send soldiers after a student; you came to close the lesson personally.

"He trained me," I said. Uselessly. Vessa knew. Everyone who'd ever mattered to me knew everything about everyone, that was the whole disease of it.

"Then you know he doesn't chase," she said. "He arrives. There's a difference, and the difference will kill you if you don't learn it tonight." She squeezed my arm once — hard, unceremonious, the closest thing to tenderness the free-towns permitted — and turned back toward her kingdom of salt and smoke.

Behind us, ahead of us, around us in the dark: the mountain, breathing snow. And somewhere on the roads below, moving with a puppeteer's patience, the man who had taught me that trust was just a slower form of betrayal.

Wren slipped her small cold hand into mine and said a word in the dead tongue. I knew this one. Grandmother's lullabies again — the verse where the night asks the traveler how many miles, and the traveler answers *enough*, and means *too many*, and keeps walking.

"Enough," I agreed, and we climbed.
