## 7. The Mentor's Shadow

There is a particular sound a man makes when he steps on dry pine needles in the dark, and I had spent nine years in the king's kitchens learning to hear it over the noise of my own heartbeat. So when the third night of our flight from Bracken Hollow brought that sound — soft, deliberate, *patient* — to the edge of our campfire, I did not reach for my knife.

I reached for Wren instead, and put myself between her and the trees.

"You always were quick," said a voice I knew better than my own father's. "Quicker than you are smart, but we're working on that."

Corvin stepped into the firelight like a man arriving at his own dinner party. Grey cloak, grey beard trimmed with military precision, grey eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. He carried no drawn weapon, which frightened me more than any blade would have. Corvin unarmed was Corvin certain.

"Six days," I said. "It took you six days to find us."

"It took me six days to decide whether to come alone." He lowered himself onto the flat stone across the fire from me, uninvited, as he had done ten thousand times across twenty years of training yards and back rooms and the long education of Gideon Blackwood. "The alternative was four hunters who don't ask questions. You should thank me for my indecision."

Wren stirred against my side. She had been asleep for an hour, her small body finally surrendering after two days of forced marching through the high passes, and now she woke with the sudden total alertness of something wild. Her eyes found Corvin and went wide — not with fear. With *recognition*.

She spoke then, three syllables of the dead tongue, low and urgent, tugging at my sleeve.

"She says she knows him," I translated before I could stop myself. It was becoming habit, speaking for her, thinking in her language half the time. My grandmother's lullabies lived in my mouth now whether I wanted them or not.

"I imagine she does." Corvin watched her with an expression I couldn't read, and I had been reading Corvin since I was fourteen years old. That was the first wrong thing. The second wrong thing came when he said: "How much has she told you?"

"About what?"

"About what you're carrying her toward."

"The Last Spire."

He closed his eyes briefly, the way he did when a student gave a correct answer to the wrong question. "And do you know what waits there? What has waited there for three hundred years?"

"Corvin." I kept my voice level. Nine years of tasting poison teaches a man to keep his voice level while his stomach turns. "You tracked us across half a kingdom to trade riddles by my own fire?"

"I tracked you," he said quietly, "because they sent hunters, and I wanted to be the hunter who arrived first."

* * *

We drank tea because neither of us suggested anything stronger. That too was old ritual — Corvin never drank with a man whose loyalty he was testing, and I never drank anything I hadn't prepared, even from my own stores, even now.

Wren stayed awake, curled against my knee, watching him with those unsettling amber eyes. Every few minutes she murmured something in the old tongue, soft as breath, and I began to understand she wasn't talking to me or to Corvin. She was talking to the fire. To the dark past the fire. To whatever of the mountain's magic had split itself apart three centuries ago and ended up wearing the shape of a mud-caked child at my door.

"Tell me about the wine," Corvin said at last.

"You know about the wine."

"I know you detected nightshade in the king's cup and announced it, as trained. And I know Aldric drank it anyway, in front of forty witnesses, and smiled." He turned his hands palm-up over the flames. "Do you understand what that means, Gideon? A man drinks poison that should kill him. There are only two explanations. Either the wine was harmless — which makes your detection a failure, and your career a fraud — or the man was already dead, and the thing sitting on the throne doesn't care what goes down its throat."

I had spent five nights not saying this aloud. Hearing it in Corvin's gravel voice made it real in a way my private dread never could.

"That's why I ran," I said.

"No. You ran because the guards started asking questions and your instincts screamed. Which served you well enough. But instincts aren't understanding." He leaned forward, and the firelight carved deep shadows under his cheekbones. "Let me give you the understanding, since you were always better at surviving lessons than attending them. Three hundred years ago, the kingdom was tearing itself apart. Nine lords, nine armies, winters eating whole villages. The Thornkings held the mountain and its old magic, and every faction wanted it, and none could take it. So a group of men — clever men, tired men — found another way. They didn't take the mountain's power. They *bound* it. Chained it into service, split it into pieces small enough to control, and used one piece to make their king unkillable. Unpoisonable. Untouchable."

My skin went cold despite the fire. "The Spires."

"They called themselves the Custodians first. Humble name for men doing arrogant work. They bound the mountain, crowned their puppet, and ended the wars inside a decade. Peace, prosperity, order." His mouth twisted. "The binding needed maintaining. A king dies, a new vessel must be prepared. The Custodians became the Spires became a handful of offices scattered through the crown's machinery, passing their secret from keeper to keeper. And someone has to identify each new vessel. Someone has to train the tasters who guard the vessels' throats without ever knowing why the throat cannot fail. Someone has to recruit—"

His voice caught. Just once. A hairline fracture in thirty years of stone.

"Recruit boys with the right bloodline," I finished. "Boys whose grandmothers sang forbidden songs. Boys whose bodies could be built up against poison, dose by dose, year by year. Boys who'd swallow anything for a crown that never told them why."

"Gideon—"

"How long?" I heard my own voice go very quiet. Quiet was worse than shouting. Wren's hand tightened on my knee. "How long have you been one of them?"

* * *

"Thirty-one years," Corvin said.

The number sat between us like a corpse.

"I was twenty-two," he went on, staring into the fire. "A war orphan with a talent for secrets and a head full of ideals. My mentor found me the way I found you. Told me the truth — all of it — and asked me to help hold together the only peace this kingdom had ever known. And I believed it. Gods help me, I believed it the way you believe in rain. It fell on everything; it must simply be the nature of the sky."

"And the kings?" I thought of Aldric's smile as the poisoned wine slid down. Of the rumors, over the years — the good years and the strange ones, the edicts that came out of nowhere, the seasons when the king seemed almost... elsewhere. "You stood in the shadow of nine years of my service and let me think I was guarding a *man*."

"You were guarding a vessel. And yes, I let you believe the lie, because the lie is load-bearing, boy. Tear it out and the whole structure comes down — the succession, the courts, the treaties, the peace. Thirty centuries of order resting on one sealed crypt and a handful of keepers willing to carry the weight."

"And the child?" I put my hand on Wren's shoulder. She didn't flinch. She was watching Corvin with something I slowly recognized as sorrow, which was somehow more frightening than anger. "Is she load-bearing too? When the binding frays — and it's fraying, Corvin, even I can feel it — do they cut new pieces off the mountain? Make new children? Spend them like coins?"

He didn't answer fast enough.

"That's what I thought."

"The binding is failing," he admitted. "It was always meant to be renewed at the solstice, and the last three renewals have taken more than they returned. This solstice will be the strongest binding in three centuries — permanent, they say. Complete. Whatever remains loose of the mountain's magic gets pulled in, ground down, made part of the machine forever." He looked at Wren, and for one naked moment I saw the old man behind the spymaster, the one who'd taught me to gut a fish and pick a lock and lie to a priest. "That's why I'm here. Not to arrest you. To beg you."

"Beg me to turn back."

"Give her to me. Tonight. I'll take her through the eastern passes to the Spire myself, gently, quickly, before the hunters find you. She'll feel nothing. And you walk away — I'll see to it. New name, new city, coin enough to live fat. You've earned that, at least."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me ugly and short. "You'll *see to it*. The way you saw to my grandmother? The way you saw to every guardian's bloodline for three hundred years — breeding us like hounds, dosing us like test subjects, and then striking our names from the rolls the moment we stopped being useful?"

"You don't know what you're asking for." For the first time, his composure cracked clean through. "At the Spire, Gideon. At the heart of it. The binding demands a guardian's blood, freely given — that's the oldest clause, the one even the Custodians feared. If you walk into that mountain with her, the mountain will *know* what you are. Your grandmother's blood, the immunity they built into you, all of it — it's not yours. It was never yours. It was *preparation*. Do you understand? You weren't hired as a food-taster. You were *raised* as one."

The fire popped. Somewhere up-slope, a branch cracked under snow-weight.

Wren said something then. Four words in the old tongue, clear and unhurried, and she looked directly at Corvin as she said them.

"What did she say?"

My mouth was dry. "She said: *the cage remembers the bird.*"

Corvin went still. Then, slowly, like an old hinge giving way, he bowed his head.

"Yes," he said softly. "Yes, it does."

* * *

He left an hour before dawn, and he did not take her.

I don't know how to explain what happened in that last hour except that it was the longest conversation I've ever had where almost nothing was said. He told me the hunters were two days behind him, maybe less — royal guard now, fully leashed, moving with a purpose no mere fugitive warranted. He told me the Corpse Pass would be snowed shut within the week and the hidden route through it was marked in no map he'd ever burned. He told me, standing, pulling his grey cloak around his shoulders, that if I turned back even now, he would lie for me until the day he died.

And then he looked at Wren — really looked at her, the way you look at something you helped break — and he said, "Your grandmother used to sing that same song. Did you know that? The one she sings in her sleep. I heard it once, through a window, thirty years ago. I reported it." His jaw worked. "Three weeks later she was gone, and a boy was brought to the training yard, and I told myself it was coincidence for twenty years."

"That boy was me."

"That boy was you."

He walked into the dark before dawn without a backward glance, because Corvin never gave anyone the satisfaction — not even the people he loved. Especially them. That was the cage he'd built around himself long before he built mine: the belief that love was a liability and order was mercy, and that a man could spend thirty years feeding children to a machine and call it protection.

I sat by the dying fire and felt something in my chest that had no clean name. Not hatred. Hatred would have been simple, and nothing about Corvin was simple. He had taught me everything I knew. He had also been the bars. Both things true at once, like a door and its lock.

Wren climbed into my lap, small and warm and smelling of woodsmoke, and pressed her palm flat over my heart.

"He is sad," she said in the old tongue, and I translated it without meaning to, because translating for her had become the same as breathing.

"Aye," I said. "He is."

"He wants to be forgiven."

I thought about that for a long time, watching the embers breathe. "Maybe. But wanting isn't the same as earning."

She nodded, solemn as a magistrate, and then yawned hugely, and slept, trusting me absolutely, which was either the greatest gift or the heaviest burden anyone had ever placed in these two traitor's hands.

Two days, Corvin had said. Maybe less.

I kicked dirt over the coals, woke Wren gently, and pointed us east — toward the Corpse Pass, toward the route no map recorded, toward the mountain that knew my name before I did.

Behind us, far to the south, I imagined I could already hear the hunters coming.

Ahead, the peaks stood black against the greying sky, and somewhere in their frozen heart, the Last Spire waited — patient as a debt, older than the crown, hungry for the solstice.

"We move fast," I told Wren, lifting her onto my shoulders. "And from now on, little bird, we trust no one."

She patted my hair, the way you comfort a fool, and said something in the dead language of the Thornkings that I chose, just then, not to translate.
