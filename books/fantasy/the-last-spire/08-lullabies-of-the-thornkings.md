## 8. Lullabies of the Thornkings

*The following is what I could piece together of Wren's telling, set down in my own hand because she had none. She spoke in the old tongue, and I understood more than any man has a right to — my grandmother's lullabies turning out, after all these years, to be something other than lullabies. What follows is her memory, not mine. I have tried not to put too much of myself into it. I have mostly failed.*

—G.B.

* * *

I was big once.

That is the first thing I remember, and the first thing I told him, and the way his face went careful and kind when I said it made me want to throw a pinecone at him. Big like the mountain is big. Not tall. Deep. I was the part of the mountain that hums under the snow in winter, the part that makes the springs taste sweet, the part that holds the roots of the old pines the way a hand holds a sleeping bird. I did not have a name then because I did not need one. You do not name your own fingers.

There were others. We were all one thing, and we were also many things, which sounds like a lie until you have been both. The Thornkings understood. That was their whole cleverness, really — not the crowns of black thorn iron, not the roads cut through living rock. They simply listened. A king would sit at the mountain's knee with his ear against the stone, and he would hear us humming, and he would govern accordingly. Slowly. Patiently. The way water governs a valley.

We liked them. I remember liking them the way you remember warmth on your back — not as an event, just as the condition of being alive.

Then came the first Spire.

I did not see it arrive. Things that come from outside the mountain do not arrive; they are *noticed*, the way you notice a splinter only after it has gone deep. One season the king's listening changed. He still sat with his ear to the stone, but what he heard was not us anymore. It was something wearing our voice, badly, the way a crow can say a man's name without knowing what names mean.

They bound him that winter. The word they used — the word I learned much later, from a girl in a free-town who drew it in spilled salt — was *binding*, but that is a carpenter's word. What they actually did was closer to flaying. They took the part of the king that listened, the part that was ours, and they folded it around themselves like a cloak. And because a Thornking is half mountain by oath, taking his listening meant taking a piece of us.

That was me. Or a piece of me. I have trouble with the borders now. When you have been split off from yourself, the edges stop meaning anything.

* * *

He asked me — Gideon, the taster, the man with the careful face and the hands that never move unless they mean it — he asked me if it hurt.

Here is what I told him: imagine someone cutting a song out of the middle of itself. Not stopping the song. Cutting out the middle and leaving the two halves singing on either side of a silence, each half convinced it is the whole. That is what it was. It did not hurt the way a wound hurts. It hurt the way a wrong note hurts, forever, everywhere, in a place that does not bleed.

And then, because he had fed me stew and given me the side of the bed nearer the fire and had not asked for anything in return yet — which made me suspicious, and still does, a little — I told him the rest.

The binding needed feeding. That was the terrible arithmetic of it, the thing the Spires understood before anyone else did: a parasite cannot take everything or its host dies, and a dead host is a cold house. So they took a little. Every generation, a little. A fragment of the listening, a splinter of the mountain's own attention, folded into the new king at his coronation alongside the crown. Each fragment went quiet inside the binding. Each fragment forgot it had ever been a finger of a greater hand.

Except the ones that didn't forget.

Some fragments were too large, or too stubborn, or too close to some deep vein of the old magic to go quietly. Those were cast out instead — pushed down through the mountain's own passages, the smuggler roads and the root-caves and the places no map admits to, until they pooled somewhere small and dark and began, slowly, over years, over generations, to become something else.

Became children.

Mud-caked. Feral. Speaking a tongue no scholar knows because the scholars were all employed by the thing doing the silencing.

I did not tell him that last part with pride. I told it and then I watched him look at me — really look, the way he looks at wine before he swallows — and I understood that he was doing sums. That is what he does instead of feeling, I think. He counts. How many fragments. How often. Three hundred years of coronations, one splinter apiece, most going silent, a few going feral. He counted and I watched his face do something complicated, and I decided I would not comfort him about it. Adults should have to sit inside their own arithmetic sometimes.

* * *

There is a song. My grandmother-songs, he called them, when I sang them and he went pale and dropped a spoon. His grandmother sang him the same ones. That is not a coincidence, though it took him days to accept that. The songs are how the fragments find each other. They are stitched into families the way seeds travel in wool and mud on a shepherd's boots — carried out of the mountain by people who never knew what they were carrying, sung to babies who grew up and sang them to their own babies, and all along every verse was a road-sign, every refrain a compass point, every lullaby a thread leading home.

Home. The mountain. The whole I was cut from.

I remember the first time I sang the third verse for him — the one about the river that runs backward under the ice — and he translated it without meaning to, his mouth moving ahead of his understanding, and then he stopped walking and stood very still in the middle of the pass with snow starting to fall and said, quietly, "She wasn't singing nonsense to me. She was teaching me the way in."

Yes, I told him. She was. And whoever taught her, and whoever taught her, all the way back three hundred years to the first family that walked a cast-out fragment down out of the hills and gave it soup and a name it hadn't asked for.

Because that is the other thing. The fragments that became children — we got named. By the families who found us. Wren. Sparrow. Ash. Little human names, small and quick, nothing like what I was. And here is the shameful secret, the one I have only told the fire so far: for a long time, I wanted the small name more than I wanted the wholeness. Wholeness meant dissolving back into something vast and patient and nameless. The small name meant stew and a fire and a grumpy man counting his spoons. I am not sure wanting the small name makes me broken. I think maybe it makes me the most honest part of the mountain there is.

* * *

But grief is the assignment here, and I will not cheat him of it, because he is writing this down and grief is the truest thing I own.

I grieve the listening. There were nights — this was long ago, before I was small, when I was still spread thin and wide across the high country — when I could feel every sleeping fox in the roots at once, every frozen spring ticking as it melted by day and refroze by night, every Thornking grave giving up its slow heat into the stone. I held all of it gently. That is what the mountain's magic *is*: holding, without gripping. The Spires grip. That is the whole difference between us, and it is why they will lose, eventually, though eventually may outlive everyone I currently know.

I grieve the kings. This surprises people — it surprised Gideon — because you would think I would hate them, the vessels, the hollow men in the thorn-iron crowns. But I remember what they were before. The first one especially. I was still partly connected when they bound him, and I felt the moment it happened: felt him reaching for us even as the folding closed over his ears, felt him listening harder than any king before him, straining toward the mountain with everything left in him while something else poured silence into the space where we used to be. He died reaching. They have all been dying reaching, for three hundred years, one after another, each man crowned into a prison and told it was a throne.

Every coronation is a funeral that nobody attends. That is a line I made up myself and I am proud of it, and when I said it Gideon stopped stirring the pot and looked at me for a long time and then wrote it down, which is how I know he is going to do something stupid and brave about all this. Men who write down your best lines are men planning to spend them.

I grieve the fragments that went silent. The ones that dissolved properly inside the bindings — they are not dead exactly, but they are not *anyone* either. They are the flat note in every coronation hymn. They are why the palace halls feel cold in July. Sometimes, on certain nights, I can almost hear them: not voices, just the place where voices should be, like the shape left by a hand pressed long ago into wax.

And I grieve this: that when I go home — and I will go home, the threads all lead home, every lullaby ends at the same door — when the mountain takes me back into the whole, the small name goes with me. Wren will not climb down the mountain afterward and eat stew. Wren is a temporary arrangement. I understand this. I agreed to it somewhere deep before I had a throat to agree with.

I just wish the arrangement had been asked for. That is the inheritance, really — not the magic, not the songs, not the guardianship his grandmother's blood passed down to him without his consent. The inheritance is this: every one of us, fragment and taster and hollow king alike, born into a debt contracted three centuries before our birth, paying installments our entire lives on a loan we never signed.

The Spires call that order. The mountain calls it a wound. The difference between those two words is the whole reason I am sitting in a smuggler's kitchen telling my insides to a stranger.

* * *

He took a long time writing tonight. The fire burned down twice and he fed it both times without looking away from the page, and finally I climbed onto the bench beside him and read over his arm, because my manners are nobody's business, and I watched him translate my grief into his straight-backed, careful letters.

"You make it sound clean," he said at last. "In the old tongue it sounds worse."

"It sounds worse in the old tongue," I agreed, "because the old tongue doesn't let you hide behind the grammar."

He almost smiled. It is a rare event, like snow in the lowlands, and I have started keeping count. Four, since the cottage.

Then his face went serious in the specific way it does when he has counted something and the total is bad. "The songs," he said. "Your threads. Where does the next one lead?"

I sang him the fourth verse — the one about the town where salt keeps better secrets than silver — and watched the color leave his face by degrees, like a tide going out.

"Bracken Hollow," he said. "You're pointing me at Bracken Hollow." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. "Of course you are. There's a woman there who'll want things for it. There's always a woman who wants things." He looked at me sideways, suspicious again, the way he gets when the world arranges itself too neatly. "Did you choose this route, or did the song?"

I considered lying, since I am told that is what children do well, but he feeds me stew and I owe him honesty at minimum. So I told him the truth:

The song chose. But the song has been choosing since before either of us was born, and it is not finished choosing yet, and the next verse after Bracken Hollow is the one I have not sung aloud to anyone.

It is the verse about the price.

He heard the shape of my not-singing anyway — he is learning to listen, this man, which frightens me more than the hunters do — and he set down the pen and banked the fire and said, in his flat taster's voice that means the opposite of calm:

"Get some sleep, Wren. Tomorrow we walk into Bracken Hollow, and I have the distinct feeling we're about to learn what things cost."
