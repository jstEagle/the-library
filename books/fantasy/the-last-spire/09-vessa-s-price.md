## 9. Vessa's Price

Vessa didn't answer right away, which was the first bad sign.

In my experience — nine years of watching powerful people decide whether to kill me — the dangerous ones never answered quickly. The quick ones wanted something simple: coin, silence, a body dumped in the river before dawn. But a woman who let a question sit while she turned her cup three times, slow as a millwheel, was pricing your soul against her ledger. And Vessa of Bracken Hollow kept the best ledger on the Thornfell roads. I'd seen it once. It had names in it going back four generations, and every name had a debt beside it, and not one of those debts read *paid*.

The back room of her counting-house smelled of tallow, pipe smoke, and old paper — the honest smells of dishonest work. Wren sat beside me on the bench with her boots swinging, because I'd learned by now that separating from the child was like separating from my own shadow: technically possible, practically a mistake. She'd gone quiet when Vessa entered the way she went quiet around certain people — not frightened, exactly. *Attentive.* The way you go quiet near a deep hole in the dark.

"So," Vessa said at last. "You want passage through the Corpse Pass in winter. Two travelers, no cargo worth naming, pursued by the crown." She smiled without warmth, the way a knife smiles. "And you want it *quiet*, which is the expensive part."

"I can pay in silver."

"Everyone can pay in silver. Silver is what poor people think rich people want." She leaned forward. "I want a name, taster."

"You have mine already. Well—" I spread my hands. "Half of it. Blackwood's real enough."

"Not yours." Her eyes flicked to Wren, then back, and there it was — the thing she'd been circling since the moment she'd pulled down the child's collar in her own kitchen and looked at those markings like a woman reading a letter from a dead husband. "I want a name that was taken. Mine."

I kept my face still. That's the one skill they actually train into you in the tasting yards, above the palate and the iron stomach and the art of dying politely: stillness. A taster who flinches tells the poisoner he's succeeded.

"Tell me what you mean," I said.

Vessa rose and went to a chest in the corner — iron-banded, locked twice, which for Vessa was practically an open invitation to theft — and came back with a strip of vellum so old it had gone the color of tea. She laid it flat between us with the care other women reserve for infants.

It was a genealogy. Or the ruin of one. Names ran down it in a fine clerk's hand, and somewhere past the third generation the ink changed — became official, heavy, stamped — and after the stamp, the family line simply *stopped*. Not ended. Stopped. As if someone had set down the pen and decided the family no longer existed.

"The Vessarans," she said. "That was us. Before we were smugglers, before Bracken Hollow, before anyone thought to give a queen of thieves a name like a fishmonger's daughter. We were wardens. Road-wardens on the Thornfell passes, sworn under the old kings — the *real* kings, the ones in the mountain." Her finger traced the stamped seal at the bottom, some sigil I half-recognized from crypt carvings I hadn't seen yet and would later wish I'd never seen at all. "When the Spires took the crown line, they didn't just hollow out kings. They cleaned house. Every family that could remember the old bindings, every bloodline that might object — struck. Their names taken out of the rolls. You can't inherit what officially never existed."

"And the rolls are kept where?"

She looked at me like I was being deliberately stupid, which was fair, because I was giving her my best performance of it.

"The Last Spire, taster. There's a vault under it. Everything the Spires ever wrote down is under that mountain — every binding, every bargain, every stolen name. My grandmother died believing her family had been invented three generations ago by liars." She rolled the vellup up again — the vellum, I mean, though the sentence felt true either way — and tucked it away. "You're going to the Spire. Everyone knows it now; the whole road hums with it; the king's guard is buying horses in three towns like winter itself is chasing them, which, funny thing, it is. So here is my price. You get me into that vault, or you get me proof from it — the record of my name, restored or copied or torn out and brought back bleeding, I don't much care which. In exchange: my guides, my routes, my silence, and my smugglers standing between you and whatever the crown sends."

* * *

Here's the trouble with bargains, and I say this as a man who once watched a duke die because he shook hands too readily over a wine cask: the price is never the price. The price is what the price costs *later*.

I walked out into the gray slush of Bracken Hollow's high street with Wren's mittened hand in mine and did arithmetic I didn't like.

Because I knew something about that vault. Not from scholarship — scholars and I kept different company — but from Corvin. Old spymasters talk when they're drunk, and Corvin talked most when he was pretending not to drink. He'd told me once, in the flat voice he used for facts too large to feel, that the Spire's vault held the *ledger of the bound*. Three centuries of kings, written down like entries in a merchant's book. And ledgers, in my experience, do not survive their reckoning. When a debt comes due that big, the room where it's recorded tends to come due with it.

And I knew — though I hadn't said it aloud even to myself yet — that whatever I meant to do at the heart of that Spire would not be gentle. Wren's songs were getting clearer every night. The unbinding she hummed about in that dead tongue needed things. Blood freely given, for one. And rituals of that size, in places that old, tend to be hard on the architecture.

So Vessa was asking me to promise her a document from a building I might very well bring down on our heads.

I could have lied to her. That was the tempting part, the clean part. Lie now, apologize to a memory later. I've lied to better people for smaller reasons. But there was Wren's hand in mine, and there was the fact that Vessa had fed us, armed us, and put two of her own riders on the road north to draw off the guard's scouts — and there was also this: a woman who'd spent her whole life being told her family was a fiction deserved better than a taster's counterfeit oath. Trust, Maren used to say, is the only currency where counterfeiting always gets caught eventually. Usually by the counterfeiter.

We found her that evening in the stable loft, checking harness by lamplight, and I gave her the truth the way I'd learned to give it: plain, and braced for impact.

"I'll get you into the vault, or I'll get your name out of it. Whichever the mountain allows. But I won't lie to you about the odds, because you'd smell it anyway. What I have to do there may break things. It may break everything down there, including the records. Including me."

Vessa coiled a strap slowly, thinking. "You're telling me your word is only good if the mountain permits."

"I'm telling you everyone who's ever promised you certainty has robbed you. I'm offering you odds instead."

For a long moment the only sound was leather creaking and, below us, Wren singing softly to the horses in a language three centuries dead — and the horses, I noticed, all had their ears turned toward her like congregations toward a bell.

Then Vessa laughed. Short, harsh, real. "*Odds.* Gods help me, that's the first honest offer anyone's made me since I was fifteen." She stuck out her hand, callused as a dock rope. "Done. Odds it is. But hear the rest of the terms, taster, because there are always rest-of-the-terms. If the vault burns, you bring me the ash. If you die, the child carries my claim — I've heard her sing; the mountain listens to her, and I'll wager its records listen too. And if you succeed—" her eyes went narrow and bright, "—you come find me afterward, wherever I am, alive, and tell me yourself. No letters. Letters lie. Faces can't afford to."

I shook. Her grip hurt, which I understood to be a compliment.

"One more thing," she said as I turned to climb down. "Your old teacher. Corvin."

The name landed in my chest like a swallowed stone — I should mention I know exactly how swallowed stones land; it's practically my profession.

"He was seen at Graywater Ford yesterday. Asking after a man and a child traveling north. He wasn't alone, and he wasn't asking like a friend." She watched me take that. "I'm sorry. Truly. But you needed to hear it from someone who isn't paid to break it gently."

* * *

Wren found me on the wall behind the counting-house, because she always found me when the finding was least convenient. She climbed up next to me without asking, the way children and cats and other forces of nature do, and for a while we just watched the pass mouth away to the north, a black seam in a white shoulder of world.

"Sad," she said. One of her words. Her vocabulary in the common tongue was maybe forty words strong, deployed like a sniper's ammunition.

"Yes."

"Corvin-man?"

"How do you know about Corvin?"

She tapped her own temple, then pointed at me, then made a gesture I'd come to understand as her word for *songs* — the things she remembered, the things the mountain remembered, the things apparently leaking into me through proximity and grandmother's lullabies and sins I hadn't committed yet.

"He taught you songs too?" she asked. "Bad songs?"

I huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "He taught me that everyone lies. He said it was the first kindness he ever did me. Taught me to taste a lie the way I taste wine — hold it, roll it, find the note underneath." I stared at the pass. "Turns out he held me the same way for thirty years. Rolled me around. Found my notes."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then she reached over and put her small mittened hand flat on my chest, directly over the sternum, which is where she put her hands when she was being serious, and said:

"Name-still-yours."

I looked at her.

"Vessa-woman. Name taken." She tapped her chest. "Yours—" tap, "—still yours. For now." And then, with the terrible precision children have for the exact wrong emphasis: "Spend good."

*Spend good.* As in: your name is coin, taster, and you're spending it, so spend it well.

I lifted her onto my shoulders and carried her down to where Vessa's guides waited with lanterns and snowshoes and faces that had looked at the Corpse Pass before and not flinched, which takes either courage or practice, and in Bracken Hollow it's usually practice.

* * *

We left before dawn, four of us plus Wren: me, the child, and two of Vessa's people — Dree, a silent woman with a crossbow and frostburn scars on both cheeks, and Tammas, a boy of perhaps nineteen who talked enough for both of them and was, I suspected, Vessa's insurance policy as much as her guide. Smugglers don't send their best into a death-pass out of friendship. They send someone expendable who's also reporting back. I respected it. I'd have done the same.

At the town gate Vessa herself stood waiting in the blue dark, breath steaming, and pressed something into my hand: a scrap of oilcloth wrapped around a square of lead, and inside the lead, stamped deep, a seal. The Vessaran warden's mark — the same as on the ruined vellum.

"When you find the vault," she said, "put this on the record. Lead takes an impression. Let whoever reads it next know the name was claimed, even if the claiming took three hundred years." She stepped back, and for one unguarded second the smuggler queen looked like a granddaughter. "My grandmother used to say the mountain keeps what men throw away. Prove her right, taster."

Then the gate opened, the cold poured in, and we walked north into the white throat of the world.

I won't pretend I didn't look back. I always look back; it's how I've stayed alive — the last thing you see of a place is often the first thing that kills you. Vessa stood on the wall until the snow took her shape. Somewhere south of us, Corvin was moving with hunters and thirty years of my habits folded into his head like a knife in a book. Somewhere farther south, a king who wasn't a king sat on a throne wearing his own body like an ill-fitted coat, and the days were peeling off the calendar toward solstice faster than any of us could climb.

And in the vault beneath the Last Spire, if Wren's songs spoke true, three centuries of names lay filed in the dark — stolen names, bound names, and, though I couldn't have known it yet, a name I'd known all my life without knowing what it cost.

Every ally has a price. I'd paid Vessa's in promises I might not live to keep.

What I didn't know — walking into that pass with a dead language humming on my shoulders — was that the mountain had a price too.

And it had already picked mine.
