## 10. The Pass That Eats Travelers

The Corpse Pass got its name honestly, which is more than I can say for most things in this kingdom. The smugglers who ran it a century ago had a habit of dying in it — avalanches, cold, the occasional knife between the ribs when a deal went sour — and rather than drag the bodies out, they'd leave them where they fell and let the snow do the burying. Come spring thaw, the pass gave up its dead like an apology it never meant.

I'd crossed it four times in my smuggling days. I'd sworn, after the third time, never again. The fourth time was because Vessa double-crossed me over a shipment of Thornfell bluewine and left me no better option, but that's another story and one I intend to die without telling.

Now here I was, fifth crossing, with a nine-year-old girl on my back, a blizzard chewing at the mountain's throat, and six of Corvin's hunters somewhere behind us, closing like a fist.

"Tell me again why we didn't take the low road," Wren said into my ear. She'd learned enough of our tongue by now to be sarcastic in it, which I considered a personal failure as her teacher.

"The low road has gates," I said. "Gates have guards."

"You could taste their food until they died of boredom."

"I'm retired from tasting. Mostly. Partially." I adjusted her weight on my shoulders. "Also, the low road goes through Harrowgate, and Harrowgate is where Corvin would look first, second, and third. He trained me. He knows I hate gates."

"He knows you hate everything."

"That's what makes me predictable," I said. "Consistency is a virtue."

The wind disagreed with everything either of us said, which was fair. It came down the pass in long white screams, filling the air so completely that up and down became suggestions rather than facts. I couldn't see twenty feet. I couldn't hear my own thoughts. All I could do was count paces and trust a memory of this rock face that was already nine years stale.

Wren, for her part, had gone quiet in the way she did when the old tongue started whispering in her head. I could feel it through her grip on my collar — little tremors, like a dog hearing thunder before the sky opens. Sometimes those whispers were useful. Sometimes they were just grief wearing the costume of prophecy.

"Gideon," she said suddenly, small against the storm. "The mountain says we should stop."

"The mountain can mind its own business."

"The mountain *is* its own business. It says the snow is going to eat us if we keep walking."

"The mountain says that every year. Some years it's right." But I stopped anyway, because there are limits to how much you argue with a child who is technically a fragment of ancient magic, and because my knees had begun making the sort of promises they couldn't keep.

* * *

We found shelter the way you always find shelter in the Corpse Pass: by falling into it. A seam in the rock wall, maybe eight feet deep, half-choked with old scree, deep enough to break the wind. I got Wren inside, dug out the space with my hands, and built a fire so small it was less a fire than a rumor of one. Enough heat for two people to huddle around. Not enough to draw eyes.

Not enough warmth, either. Nothing in the Corpse Pass was ever enough.

I shared out the last of the hard bread and the cheese that had gone the texture of candle wax. Wren ate hers with the grim efficiency she applied to all food, as though meals were debts she intended to settle quickly. Then she leaned against my side, and for a while there was only the wind and the fire's tiny conspiracy.

"Gideon," she said. "If the hunters come, will you fight them?"

"If I have to."

"You're bad at fighting."

"I'm bad at *fair* fighting. Nobody survives nine years in a palace by being good at fair fighting. Fair fighting is how you end up in a crypt with your name spelled wrong on the stone."

She thought about that. "Then what will you do?"

"Something unfair."

She nodded, satisfied, and I felt the peculiar weight of being believed by someone who had no idea how thin my answers were getting. Because the truth — the truth I wasn't saying, the truth I'd been not-saying since the ridge below Bracken Hollow — was that Corvin's hunters weren't soldiers. They were the other kind. The kind he kept for work he needed done quietly and completely. I'd known two of them, back when I still pretended to be his student instead of his tool. Hesk. And the twins, Aldous and Merrit, who communicated in glances and killed in unison like a pair of scissors.

Corvin wouldn't send scissors after a runaway taster unless he wanted the job finished. And Corvin did not send his scissors out with instructions to negotiate.

I fed the rumor-fire another stick and tried not to do arithmetic about how far behind us they were.

* * *

They found us near dawn, or what passes for dawn inside a blizzard, which is to say the darkness turned slightly gray and resentful.

I heard them before I saw them, because Hesk always whistled when he hunted — three notes, low, patient, a habit he thought was camouflage and was actually arrogance. I'd told Corvin once that the whistle would get him killed someday. Corvin had said, "Only if someone smarter hears it," and looked at me in a way I hadn't understood then and understood entirely now.

Three notes floated down the pass, muffled by snow, closer than I'd let myself hope.

"Wren." I shook her awake. No panic in my voice; panic is a luxury, and besides, she'd feel it through my hands. "Up. Quietly. Boots."

She was moving before I finished the sentence, which is the whole reason I'd spent weeks teaching her to sleep light and wake faster. We killed the fire, packed in silence, and pressed ourselves against the rock while I listened to the storm tell me what it knew.

Two whistles now, answering each other. Flanking. They'd found the dead fire — of course they had; the scree held heat like a grudge — and they were splitting to pinch the seam from both ends. Standard Corvin doctrine: never approach a quarry head-on, because head-on is the only direction a cornered man watches.

Which meant the direction nobody watched was up.

There was a route out of the seam — I'd spotted it yesterday and dismissed it, and I dismissed it now for the same reasons and then, because desperation is an excellent editor, reconsidered. A chimney of broken rock climbing the pass wall, maybe two hundred feet of handholds and prayer, topping out on a shelf above the pass proper. From there, a traverse east along the wall to where the old smugglers had cut a stair into the cliff face. The Stair of Names, Vessa called it, because every smuggler who used it scratched his mark into the stone at the top, a superstition about being remembered by the mountain.

The problem with the chimney was simple: it went up into the worst of the storm, exposed to wind that had already tried to peel my face off twice today, and Wren would be climbing it on fingers too small to trust and a body that was, whatever else she was, still a child's body.

The problem with staying was also simple: Hesk and the twins, and the arithmetic of one taster against three killers.

"Can you climb?" I asked her.

Wren looked at the black slot of the chimney, then at me. In the gray light her eyes had that faraway shine they got when the mountain talked to her. "It's afraid of us," she said. "The rock. It remembers being climbed by people with iron shoes, a long time ago. It says—" she tilted her head— "it says some of them are still here."

"Charming. Can you climb?"

"I can climb if you don't catch me if I fall."

"What?"

"It says the catching is what kills people. People grab each other and both fall. It wants us to climb apart." She said it with the flat certainty of a child reporting weather, and something cold slid down my spine that had nothing to do with the temperature.

"Climb apart," I repeated. "Meaning I watch you fall."

"Meaning you trust the rock." She shrugged. "Or the hunters. Your choice."

Out of the mouths of fragments of ancient magic. I took one last look down the seam, where the snow was beginning to print itself with boot-shapes, and made the choice that survival always makes for you.

"Rope stays on," I said. "Slack. If you fall, I anchor and you swing clear. That's not catching, that's mathematics. The mountain can bill me."

She almost smiled. Then she put her hands on the first hold and went up like water finding its way downhill, and I hated how easy she made it look, and I followed.

* * *

The chimney was everything I'd feared and several things I hadn't. The holds were iced. The wind came up the slot in gusts that tried to fold me backward off the wall. Twice the rock crumbled under my hand in ways that felt personal. And all the while the rope between us hung slack as Wren had insisted, a thin gray line connecting us that connected nothing, a promise shaped like a leash.

Halfway up, she slipped.

No scream. Just a sudden absence where she'd been, a scrape of boots, and then the snap of the rope coming taut as my anchor arm took her weight and my shoulder nearly left its socket doing it. She swung, hit the wall, caught a hold, and hung there breathing hard while the wind screamed congratulations.

"You said no catching," I called down through the storm.

"You caught me."

"I did mathematics. There's a difference. Climb."

But my heart was doing something undignified against my ribs, and I understood, with the clarity that only terror provides, exactly how much of my survival had quietly relocated outside my own body and into a small girl on a frozen cliff. Somewhere between a cottage door and a blizzard, I had become a man with something to lose, and everything since had been the sound of that new fact closing around me like a trap.

We topped out on the shelf as the light improved from gray to slightly-less-gray, and lay gasping among the scratched names of dead smugglers. Below and behind, faint through the storm, came the whistle again — angrier now, shorter notes. They'd reached the seam and found it empty.

"They'll see the rope marks," Wren said.

"They'll see the rope marks," I agreed, "and they'll know exactly where we went, because there's nowhere else to go. Which brings us to the part of the morning I've been dreading."

The traverse east was short and ugly, and then we stood at the foot of the Stair of Names, and I showed her the problem.

The stair climbed the cliff face in switchbacks cut into the living rock — a marvel of old smuggler engineering, wide enough for one man and his guilt, zigzagging four hundred feet up the wall to a notch in the ridge. From the notch, the high country opened toward Thornfell and, eventually, the Last Spire. It was the only way forward. It was also, in this storm, a corridor of ice with a several-hundred-foot drop attached to every turn, and the wind at the notch would be strong enough to strip the paint off sin.

And the hunters were twenty minutes behind us with ropes and no children to slow them down.

"We go up," Wren said.

"We go up," I agreed, "or we stay here and let three professionals introduce themselves. I've met them. You wouldn't like them."

"Hesk whistles."

"Hesk whistles."

We climbed.

* * *

I want to say something noble about the ascent, about courage and the bond between a guardian and his charge, but the honest account is this: it was four hundred feet of misery conducted one switchback at a time, in wind that arrived like a debt collector, on stone that had been iced by a week of storms into something closer to glass than granite. I went first, kicking steps into the rime with my heel, testing every hold twice. Wren followed in my prints, one hand knotted in the rope, her face set in the expression of someone doing arithmetic of her own — counting the steps remaining versus the strength remaining, and not especially liking the totals.

At the third switchback, the wind changed its mind about killing us and decided to toy with us instead. It dropped. Utterly. One moment the roar, the next a silence so complete I could hear the creak of the rope and Wren's breath and, distantly, below us —

Whistling. Three notes. Close.

I flattened against the rock and looked down. Two figures on the shelf we'd vacated, small with distance, moving fast. One pointed at the stair. Even at that range I knew the gesture — Aldous, marking the trail for Merrit. The scissors, opening.

Where was Hesk?

The wind answered before my brain did, arriving from the notch above us in a single flat shove that nearly tore Wren off the wall. The storm wasn't over. It had been gathering at the ridge, the way storms do in the Corpse Pass, pooling above the notch like water behind a dam, and it had chosen this moment to come through.

"Go!" I shouted, and we went, no longer climbing but scrambling, steps abandoned for instinct, the world reduced to rock and rope and the shriek building above us.

We were thirty feet from the notch when the dam broke.

Snow came down the notch like the mountain exhaling a century of held breath. I got an arm around Wren and shoved us both into a hollow under the lip of the last switchback, and the avalanche passed over us — not the great roaring slide of storybooks, but a white hammer, a weight that filled the air and my mouth and my ears and pressed me against the rock until my ribs sang. It lasted ten seconds or ten years. When it cleared, the notch above us was gone — smoothed into a white ramp of fresh powder, climbable, barely — and the stair below us was buried to a depth I refused to measure.

And somewhere under that burial, or above us on the shelf, were Aldous and Merrit. I couldn't tell which. The silence after the slide was absolute.

Wren coughed snow out of her collar and looked at me with eyes gone bright and strange. "The mountain is sorry," she said.

"Is it."

"It says it doesn't choose sides. It says it just... shrugs sometimes." Her small hand found mine. "Gideon, it says there were people under it."

I know. I heard it too — or imagined I did, in the seconds after the roar faded: a sound beneath the snow, muffled, brief, and then not. Maybe one voice. Maybe two. Maybe the mountain settling. In the Corpse Pass, the difference had never mattered much. The pass ate travelers. It had been eating them for a hundred years, and it did not check whose side they were on before it swallowed.

I sat in the snow with a fragment of the mountain's magic leaning against my chest, and I discovered I could not make myself feel what I was probably supposed to feel. Relief came first, enormous and shameful. Beneath it, something colder: the understanding that I hadn't beaten them. The mountain had. And mountains collect debts the way Corvin collected secrets — patiently, and with interest.

"Can we go on?" Wren asked.

I looked up at the notch. The fresh snow ramp was steep but solid — new powder over old pack, the kind of surface that holds if you respect it and kills you if you don't. Beyond it, the high country. Thornfell. The Spire. Twelve days, if the weather held, which it would not.

Behind us, under forty feet of snow, lay the only road back down.

"We go on," I said, and hauled us both to our feet.

We were halfway up the ramp when Wren stopped so abruptly the rope snapped tight. She was staring east, past the notch, to where the storm was shredding apart into ragged streamers — and beyond them, impossibly far, a point of light on the shoulder of the great mountain. Faint. Blue-white. Pulsing, slow and steady, like a heartbeat seen through skin.

The Last Spire. Visible from fifty miles, glowing like a lamp in a window, and Wren was humming as she stared at it — humming in the old tongue, a melody I almost recognized, a lullaby my grandmother used to sing, and her voice carried strangely in the clearing air, and I realized with a lurch that the song wasn't for me.

Something down in the valley, far behind us, answered it. A single horn note, long and low, rolling up the pass like a question.

Corvin had more than three hunters.

"Run," I said, and this time I didn't pretend it was mathematics.
