## 11. What Immunity Is Worth

I have been poisoned eleven times in nine years of service to the crown. I say this not as a boast but as a matter of bookkeeping, because a taster who cannot count his poisons is like a merchant who cannot count his coin — technically employed, practically ruined.

The first was hemlock, disguised under a honey glaze. The second through the ninth were various and inventive, and I survived them all because my grandmother's blood runs strange in me and because Master Corvin spent three years feeding me doses of everything the kingdom's alchemists could brew, building my body into a ledger of the crown's sins. Every entry a poison. Every poison paid in full. That was the arrangement: they poured death into me a sip at a time so that when someone tried to pour it all at once into the king, I would know its name before it knew mine.

It never occurred to me that a ledger could be called in.

Now, lying on a bed of pine boughs in a smuggler's waystation with snow howling through the shutter gaps and my own blood deciding, entry by entry, to collect on old debts — now it occurs to me.

* * *

The trouble started as a taste. That's fitting. Everything in my life starts as a taste.

We had come down out of Corpse Pass two days ahead of the storm, Wren riding my shoulders and humming her dead-tongue songs into my hair, when I noticed that the water from my flask tasted wrong. Not poisoned-wrong. Nothing so clean as that. It tasted like memory — like every drop of arsenic and nightshade and widow's-milk I'd ever swallowed had decided to surface at once, a school of small silver fish rising in my mouth.

By evening my gums were bleeding. By midnight I couldn't keep the broth down. By dawn I was shivering under four blankets while sweat froze on my forehead, and Wren sat cross-legged beside me with her hand flat on my chest, singing something low and urgent that I understood only in fragments:

*Cold-in-the-roots. The borrowed shield is breaking.*

Borrowed shield. That's what she called my immunity. I asked her once where she learned the word, and she looked at me with those too-old eyes and said, in her broken court speech, "You did not make it. Someone made it *in* you."

Children say terrible things with perfect accuracy.

Vessa's people had left us at the waystation with instructions to wait for the guide she'd promised, and then the snow closed the pass behind us like a door, and there was nothing to do but wait for either the guide or the fever to arrive first.

The fever arrived first, and it brought luggage.

* * *

I dreamed of the palace kitchens. In the dream I was tasting again, spoon after spoon, and each dish was a year of my life, and Corvin stood over my shoulder saying *"again"* in the voice he used for lessons, gentle and inexorable, until I realized the spoon was going into my arm instead of my mouth, and the broth was red, and —

"Gideon."

A hand on my face. Cool. I flinched away from it out of habit; cool hands in my experience belong to assassins or physicians, and the difference between the two professions has always been narrower than either would admit.

"Open your eyes, you mule-headed man."

Maren. Court physician Maren, standing in a smuggler waystation eight days' ride from the capital, her travel cloak stiff with snow, her medical kit open on the floor like a surgeon's altar. For one lucid moment I considered that I might be hallucinating her. The alternative explanations were worse.

"You followed us," I said. My voice came out like gravel poured through a sieve.

"Someone had to." She was already pulling stoppers, already working, her hands doing three things while her mouth did none of them. "Drink this. No — don't smell it first, you'll insult me, and anyway if I wanted you dead I've had eight days of easier opportunities."

That logic was sound enough that I drank. It tasted of willow bark and something darker, mossy, mountain-grown. It went down and stayed down, which was more than anything else had managed since the pass.

"What is it?" I asked.

"A chelate. Clay and root and a few things I'm not going to name, because you'd only recognize them from your training and start arguing." She bound my forearm without asking permission, checking the pulse points, frowning at what she found there. "Your body is rejecting the immunity. All of it. At once."

"That's not how it works. Immunity doesn't just — expire."

"No," she said quietly. "It doesn't. Which is why you should ask yourself what actually happens to all that poison you've swallowed over nine years. Where do you imagine it goes, Gideon? Do you think it vanishes?"

I had never thought about it. That was the honest answer. A taster learns very quickly not to think too hard about what he swallows; thinking is how men start refusing the spoon.

"It binds into the bone," Maren said. "Into the liver, the marrow, the fat. Your blood built walls around it — that's what your immunity *is*, Gideon, not an absence of poison but a quarantine of it. Thousands of sealed rooms, and your body the prison around them." Her fingers moved to my wrist, counting. "And now the walls are coming down. Fever, bleeding, the tremors. You're not sick. You're *leaking*."

I lay there and absorbed that. Somewhere outside, wind took the roof and shook it like a dog shaking a rat.

"How long?" I asked, which is the only question that matters once you're leaking.

"If we'd reached you tomorrow instead of today? Days." She didn't soften it; I'd have distrusted her if she had. "As it is — the Spire, if we move slowly and I keep dosing you. Maybe. You'll be weak as a kitten the whole way. You may lose the use of your legs for stretches of it."

"Weak I can manage. I've been weak before." I tried to sit up to prove it and discovered my body had opinions on the matter, all of them vetoed. "Where's Wren?"

"Gathering snowmelt. She wouldn't leave the room at first — stood guard over you like a wolfhound half her size — until I convinced her that ice-water was a medicine she could fetch." Something moved across Maren's face, quickly, there and gone. "She speaks in her sleep, you know. In the old tongue. Full sentences. Whoever taught her taught her *everything*, once."

"She wasn't taught," I said. "She *is* it. Part of it. The mountain's magic, cut into pieces, wearing a child like a coat." I watched Maren's hands still over the bandages. "You knew that already. Didn't you. That's why you really came."

* * *

She waited until Wren came back, deposited the snowmelt, assessed me with one grave look, and curled up against my good side — my side, apparently, now, no discussion held — before Maren spoke again. Even then she spoke low, in physician's Latin, the tongue we used in the infirmary when the guards' ears were close. Wren listened to it with her head tilted, cataloguing sounds, storing them away the way she stored everything.

"I poisoned the wine," Maren said.

I turned my head as far as the pillows allowed. "You *what*?"

"The king's wine. The nightshade you caught." She met my eyes steadily, the way she met everyone's eyes, the way that had fooled me for nine years into thinking she was merely competent rather than dangerous. "I put it there. Measured to the grain. Enough to kill a healthy man in an hour."

The fire popped. Wren stirred against my ribs and settled.

"Why?" My voice cracked on it. "Maren, I nearly died catching that. If I'd missed it — if it had gone to the king —"

"But it went to the king," she said. "He drank it. And he did not die. You saw that with your own eyes, and I needed someone to see it who would understand what it meant. Not a soldier. Not a priest. A *taster* — the one man in Vhalderan whose whole life is knowing the difference between poison that works and poison that doesn't." She leaned forward. "I tested him, Gideon. Nine years I suspected. Three years I planned. One cup to prove it."

"And if I hadn't detected the nightshade at all? If I'd let it pass as clean?"

"Then I'd have learned the king dies like any other man, and I'd have hanged for his murder, and the Spires would simply have worn whoever came next." She said it flatly, without drama, the arithmetic of a woman who had done the sums long ago and kept the totals in a locked drawer of herself. "I weighed you in that calculation too. I'm sorry. I want you to hear me say it plainly: I weighed your life against the truth, and I chose the truth, and I have not slept properly since."

I should have been furious. Some part of me was — a hot, small coal of it, buried deep, the part of any man that objects to being used as a reagent. But most of me was tired, bone-deep tired, and recognized the shape of what she'd done, because I had lived inside its mirror for nine years. Every day I swallowed death so the crown might live. She had brewed death so the crown might be *seen*. We were both instruments. The difference was she had chosen her purpose and I had been issued mine with a livery uniform.

"Corvin trained me to trust no one," I said finally. "You're making his case for him."

"Corvin," Maren said, and stopped. Her hands, which had been steady through everything — through the bleeding, through the confession — went still in a different way now. "Gideon. When we reach the crypt, whatever you find there — remember that Corvin loved you. Hold onto that. You'll want to put it down soon, and you mustn't."

"What did you see?"

"I followed his correspondence for two years before I ever touched the wine. He serves them. He has served them longer than you've been alive." She began repacking the kit, each vial in its loop, precise as prayer. "But love and treason share a house in that man, and I genuinely cannot tell you which one sleeps closer to the door."

Wren had gone quiet against my side. Too quiet. I glanced down and found her awake, watching Maren with that ancient patience, and when she caught me looking she pressed two fingers to my wrist — over the pulse — and said, softly, in the old tongue, a word I knew from her lullabies.

*Witness.* It also meant *the one who counts the dead.*

"Yes," I told her. "That's her. That's both of us, little one. We count."

* * *

Sleep took me after that, the medicated kind, heavy and dreamless except once — a brief, cold flash of the Spire on its ridge, black against a white sky, and a voice that was not a voice saying *the ledger balances at solstice*. I woke to grey light and the smell of Maren brewing another dose, and Wren sitting by the window slit, watching the pass.

"They're coming," she said.

My heart did something complicated. "Corvin's hunters?"

"Not yet." She didn't turn around. Snow-light made her look carved, translucent at the edges, and I remembered what the ascent would cost and hated it in advance. "The other one. The hollow one. He is moving. The mountain feels him like a stone rolling downhill." She pressed her palm flat against the windowsill, against the rock beneath, listening with her skin. "Three days. Four. He comes to the Spire to finish the binding, and he eats the last of the king on the way."

I sat up. My arms shook and my vision swam, and every joint felt packed with wet sand, but I sat up, and Maren watched me do it with a physician's grim approval.

"Can I walk?" I asked her.

"Today? Twenty paces. Tomorrow, a mile. After that, we'll see what the clay pulls out of you." She handed me the cup. "You understand what's happening, don't you. As the immunity fails, the poisons leave you. And when the last of them is gone—"

"I'll be an ordinary man," I said. "Killable by any kitchen in the kingdom."

"Mm. Most people call that *alive*."

I drank. It tasted like dirt and mercy, and somewhere beneath it, faintly, I could almost swear I tasted the shape of my grandmother's lullaby — as if the old magic in my blood and the old poison in my bones were trading places, one leaving, the other settling in.

Outside, far up the valley, a bird called three notes, paused, and repeated them. Wren's head came up sharply.

"That's not a bird," she said. "That's the signal Vessa uses for *guide approaching, alone*."

I got my boots on. Twenty paces, Maren had said. It would have to be enough.
