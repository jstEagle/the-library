## 12. The King Remembers Snow

The thing that lives behind my eyes sleeps after the evening meal. I don't know why. Perhaps digesting a kingdom tires it as much as ruling one.

I have learned its rhythms the way a prisoner learns the guard's boots — by sound, by weight, by the particular quality of silence that means *it is not watching*. It sleeps deepest on nights when the council has gone well, when the ledgers balance and the ambassadors have been flattered in the correct order, because then there is nothing left to chew. On those nights, for an hour or sometimes two, I get myself back.

Tonight the council had gone beautifully. The Thornfell tariffs were approved without discussion. Lord Haskett proposed a new tax on the mountain shrines and I — *it* — smiled and signed. Everyone left the chamber glowing with the warm certainty of having been heard.

Which meant the parasite was fat and drowsy, and somewhere beneath the crown, in the small dark room where Aldric of House Veyra had been locked for thirty years, a man sat up and rubbed his eyes.

*Snow,* was the first thing I thought. Not taxes, not treason, not the nine hundred duties stacked like cordwood against my skull. *Snow.*

I remembered snow.

I was seven years old, and my mother had smuggled me out of the nursery past a governess who could be bought with candied figs, and we had stood on the east terrace while the first snowfall of the year came down over the gardens like the sky deciding to be gentle for once. She caught flakes on her sleeve and told me they were all different, every single one, six-sided and singular, and that no king would ever be as interesting as a snowflake, and she said it laughing, and I had believed her.

My mother died when I was eleven. I remember that too, but from farther away, as if it happened to a cousin. The snow I remember from inside my own chest.

That's how I know the hour is mine. The parasite has all my memories — it wears them like a coat, consults them like a ledger, produces my childhood anecdotes at dinners to charm visiting dignitaries. But it remembers nothing *warmly*. It knows the snow; it does not love it. Love is the one thing it never managed to steal, perhaps because love isn't stored anywhere. It only exists while you're feeling it, and you cannot feel what you do not inhabit.

So when the snow comes back to me whole — cold on my lashes, my mother's laugh, the specific ache of wet wool mittens — I know that for this hour, I am king of at least one country: myself.

I got up from the throne where *it* had slumped after the council — I always leave the body seated when I can, so the guards won't notice the posture change; a king who stands differently is a king someone questions — and I walked. My legs are stiff these days. Thirty years of being a passenger leaves you with the body of a man who has never driven.

* * *

The chapel was empty at this hour. It usually is. Piety keeps court hours, and the gods, if they exist, apparently keep worse ones.

I lit no candles. Light draws the night priests, and the night priests report to the Spire — I understood that long before I had words for it. Instead I stood in the blue dark under the rose window and let the memory run its full length, start to finish, like a man drinking water after years of thirst. My mother's grey cloak. Her breath fogging. The way she knelt and pressed her palm flat to the terrace stone and said, quiet enough that I almost missed it:

*"The mountain remembers, little king. Whatever they tell you later — whatever they make you forget — the mountain remembers."*

I thought she was being poetic. Mothers of kings are permitted poetry the way other mothers are permitted wine.

She was not being poetic. My mother knew. God help me, I think she knew exactly what would be done to me, and she hid the truth in a sentence small enough to slip past the watchers, and I carried it for fifty years without once opening it.

I opened it tonight.

Here is what I know now, laid out plainly, the way you'd lay out tools before surgery. There is something old living in the crown line. Not metaphorically — I mean a thing, with appetites and habits and, I believe, an origin in the mountain itself, though whether it was born there or fled there I cannot say. It binds itself to each heir at coronation. It does not kill us. Killing us would be wasteful. It *hollows* us — takes the will, takes the deep self, leaves the surface intact. A king must still smile at ambassadors. A king must still sign tariffs. The parasite needs the kingdom running smoothly; we are not its victims so much as its livestock, kept healthy and productive.

Every king since — I did the arithmetic once, in another stolen hour, three centuries back to Thornfell the Last, the dynasty they don't put on the coins anymore. Every king since the Thornkings fell has been worn. Three hundred years of crowned meat.

And tonight — tonight of all nights, while the snow-memory still burned in me like a coal — I felt something I had not felt in decades.

Hope. Small, stupid, mortal hope.

Because last week, in the gray half-sleep where I keep whatever consciousness I'm allowed, I felt the parasite *flinch*. Something had touched the binding. Something out in the world — a fragment, loose, singing in the old tongue. The Thornking tongue. It hurt the thing wearing me, and it has been irritable ever since, short-tempered with Haskett, careless with its smiles. For the first time in thirty years, the thing in my head is worried.

Something is coming for it. And whatever it is, it speaks the language my mother spoke on the terrace, the language the mountain remembers.

So I have work to do, and one hour in which to do it, and I intend to spend every minute like a miser spending his last coin.

* * *

Writing is the problem. Everything I write, it can read. It lives in my hands as much as my head; when it wakes fully it can walk my fingers across any page in the palace and review my correspondence like a suspicious merchant. I have tried cipher. It is better at cipher than I am — it has my whole education to draw on, plus whatever ancient cunning it brought to the marriage.

But there are two things it cannot read.

The first is the old tongue. It understands Thornkings' speech the way I understand Latin — formally, scholarly, with a dictionary between us and the soul of it. It can translate; it cannot hear. The old tongue works by resonance, by what a word *does* to the mouth and ear, and the parasite has no mouth and no ear, only my memories of mouths and ears, secondhand all the way down.

The second is the crypt.

Underneath this chapel, below the Veyra tombs with their marble kings lying in state like actors between performances, there is an older crypt. The masons sealed it when the chapel was built and the records call it a foundation void, structural, nothing of interest. But I found it when I was nineteen — before my coronation, before the binding, back when I still went poking into places out of ordinary human curiosity. A crack behind the reliquary of Saint Edrys, a crawl-space, a stair going down into dark that smelled of mountain instead of marble.

Thornkings are buried down there. Or what the Veyras left of them. I saw the sarcophagi once, young and foolish and unafraid, and I saw the carvings — spirals and thorn-script and figures with crowns of branches — and I ran back up and told no one, because even at nineteen I had already learned that telling people things gets you watched.

Now I am going to use it.

I write the letter twice. That's the trick of it, the one piece of genuine cleverness I've managed in three decades of captivity. The visible layer is innocuous — a king's private devotions, meditations on mortality, the sort of thing that would bore the parasite into skipping ahead if it ever bothered to look. Underneath, woven through in the old tongue my mother taught me in fragments, syllable by syllable on that same terrace when I was small — *this is our secret language, little king, just ours* — is the true message.

It takes me most of the hour. My hand shakes. Partly age, partly fear, partly the effort of holding the door of myself open against the thing's slow breathing pull — because even asleep, it tugs. Even asleep, it reels me in by inches, the way a fisherman who has hooked something large simply waits.

I write:

*I was Aldric. I want whoever finds this to know that first, because everything else about me has been edited, and a man should get to sign his own name at least once.*

*I do not know who you are or how you came to read this. If you know the old tongue, then you are either a scholar of forbidden things or something older than scholarship, and either way you are the first true ally I have had since my mother died. Listen: the binding renews at solstice. Each winter it deepens — the vessel thins, the wearer thickens. This solstice, I believe, is the last one I survive as anything but a shell. It is devouring my memories faster now. Yesterday I lost the name of my first horse. Today I lost the smell of bread baking. Tomorrow it will be my mother's face. I can feel it rationing me.*

*But it flinched. Remember that. Something wounded it, something loose and singing, and if the mountain has sent a fragment home then perhaps the mountain intends to take back what was taken. Go to the Last Spire. The map is carved where I have marked — the old masons were Thornkin sympathizers, or fools, which in those days were synonyms. The Spire's heart holds the original binding, and what was bound can, I am told — my grandmother told me, in the same voice, in the same secret tongue — be unbound. It costs blood freely given. It always costs. Nothing honest is cheap.*

*If you fail, burn this letter and tell no one I wrote it, and I will forgive you, because failing is not shameful. I have failed every day for thirty years and the sun still rises, out of what I can only assume is stubbornness.*

*If you succeed — then whoever reads this next will find a king's body and no king in it, and I ask only this: bury me in the old crypt, under the thorn-carvings, with my face uncovered. I spent my whole reign masked. Let the worms at least know who they're eating.*

*Aldric of House Veyra. Taster of nothing, keeper of nothing, king of one hour.*

I sit back. My eyes are wet, which surprises me — I thought they'd wrung me dry decades ago. Apparently there was a little left in the cistern after all.

* * *

Getting down to the crypt is the easy part; I've done it twice before in stolen hours, rehearsing exactly this. The reliquary of Saint Edrys weighs less than its reputation. The crack is exactly where a frightened boy left it, forty years of dust on its lips. I go down sideways, an old man's careful descent, candle stub held in my teeth like a pirate, which would undignify the monarchy considerably if anyone saw.

The dark below smells the way it smelled at nineteen: stone and cold and time, and underneath it something faintly green, impossibly, as if the mountain's pulse still reached this far down. The sarcophagi loom. The thorn-script spirals on their lids catch my candlelight and seem, in the way of old carvings, almost to move.

I kneel at the base of the central tomb — Thornfell the Last, or what remains of him — and I press the letter into a gap in the mortar that some earlier sympathizer clearly cut for exactly this purpose, because the fit is perfect. Someone before me planned for messages. Someone before me hoped too. That is somehow the most comforting thing I have learned all night: hope has a lineage here. It has been passed hand to hand in the dark for three hundred years like contraband.

Then, because the old tongue is a doing-language, not a saying-language, I speak the closing words aloud. My grandmother's cadence. The ones that mean, roughly, *let it wait for the right reader.* The syllables come out rusty and mispronounced and probably an insult to every dead Thornking present, but the mountain doesn't seem to mind. The air in the crypt goes briefly, gently warm, like a hand on the shoulder.

Or like a hand reaching up from below.

I climb back out, replace the reliquary, and stand a moment in the empty chapel with my candle guttering, doing sums.

The hour is nearly spent. I can feel the edges of myself softening already, the snow-memory starting to blur at its borders — my mother's cloak turning grey-on-grey, her laugh flattening toward a fact rather than a feeling. It always takes the warmth first. Warmth is what it eats.

But I have done what I could. Somewhere below my feet, a dead language sits waiting in the dark for a living mouth. Somewhere out beyond the walls — I felt it again tonight, a faint discordant hum in the binding, like a string plucked far away — something is moving toward me through the winter. Something the parasite fears.

Let it fear. Let it spend this solstice looking over its shoulder for the first time in three centuries. Fear is a food I finally get to serve it.

The tug comes, gentle as tide. The thing behind my eyes stirs, stretches, resumes its tenancy, and I feel myself folding away into the small dark room, taking my hour with me like a book I'll reread tomorrow, if there is a tomorrow.

As the door closes, one last thought slips through, and I hold onto it the way you hold a rope:

*The snow remembers too. Every flake falls different, Mother said. And something is falling toward this mountain right now — small, singular, six-sided with secrets.*

I only pray it arrives before the solstice freezes everything solid.

The crown settled back onto thoughts that were not entirely mine, and far below, in the sealed dark, the letter waited — patient as thorns, patient as snow — for someone who could read the old tongue to come digging.
