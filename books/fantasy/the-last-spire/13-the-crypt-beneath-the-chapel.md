## 13. The Crypt Beneath the Chapel

There is a particular kind of silence that lives under old stone. I'd tasted it once before, years ago, in the sealed wine cellars beneath the palace — a silence so complete it felt less like an absence of sound and more like something listening. The silence inside the Thornking crypt was that kind, multiplied by three hundred years of dead kings.

Wren had been singing since dawn.

Not the way children usually sing — no wandering melodies, no made-up nonsense words. This was precise. Ancient. Each note landed like a key turning in a lock, and each time one did, the mountain answered. A rumble here. A groan of shifting stone there. We were somewhere beneath the ruined chapel of Saint Verane, halfway up Thornfell's southern shoulder, following a song older than the kingdom that had tried to bury it.

"Again," I said, when she paused for breath.

She gave me a look that said, quite clearly, *I am seven years old and I have been walking since before the sun came up*, but she sang anyway. The third verse opened a seam in the far wall that I would have sworn on my grandmother's grave had been solid rock.

"That's going to take some getting used to," I muttered.

Vessa shifted beside me, her hand never far from the knife at her hip. "You knew this was here," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I knew it existed. Sealed. Everyone knew that much." I tested the air flowing from the seam — cold, dry, tasting faintly of dust and old cedar. "No one knew how to get in. Three hundred years of scholars have broken their teeth on it."

"And your grandmother's lullabies opened it."

"Wren's songs did. They're the same songs." I didn't look at Vessa when I said it. "My grandmother used to sing them while she baked bread. I thought they were just... hers."

*Just hers.* Nine years as the king's taster had taught me to distrust everything that arrived without a price attached, and here was another thing that had come to me free: a language in my blood, a map in my childhood, and now a door in a mountainside that opened for nursery rhymes. Nothing free is ever free. I'd learned that lesson over poisoned goblets. I was still learning what it cost.

The seam widened enough for us to slip through single file. Wren went first — she insisted, and arguing with her about it was like arguing with the tide — then Vessa, then me with the lantern held high.

The crypt opened around us like the throat of some enormous patient beast.

Columns. Dozens of them, carved from the living rock of the mountain itself, each one wrapped in spiraling script that caught my lantern light and seemed to hold it, slow it down. Between the columns stood sarcophagi — no, not sarcophagi. Thrones. Stone thrones, each one occupied by a seated figure in armor or robes, hands folded, heads bowed forward as if in prayer or exhaustion. Kings. I counted eleven before I lost track, and the dark kept going.

"They're all facing the same direction," Vessa said quietly.

They were. Every throne, every king, faced toward the heart of the crypt, where a dais rose out of the floor and upon the dais—

"Is that what I think it is?" Vessa's voice had gone flat. Vessa's voice only went flat when she was frightened, which I had previously believed was never.

It was a crown. Not a metaphorical one. An actual crown, iron and black silver, resting on a cushion of stone, and around its base wound carvings of serpents — or roots, I couldn't tell which — that climbed up from the dais and spread across the entire floor, into every column, through every wall. The whole crypt was rooted to that crown. Or growing from it.

Wren walked straight to it.

"Wren." My voice cracked out sharper than I intended, echoing off the dead kings. She stopped at the edge of the dais and looked back at me, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — recognition, yes, but also grief. The deep, old grief of someone standing at their own grave.

She sang three notes. Soft ones.

The crown did nothing. But the nearest throne — the most recent, by the freshness of its carving, still sharp-edged, still waiting empty — hummed. Just once. Like a plucked string.

"Oh," I said. Very quietly. Because I understood, suddenly and completely, what the empty throne was for.

Every king who had ever sat the throne of Aldric's line had first sat *this* throne. Had been bound here, in the dark, beneath the chapel where their coronations were celebrated overhead with trumpets and feasts. And the binding —

The binding wasn't finished yet. Aldric was still alive upstairs, still wearing his crown, still signing his decrees. Which meant the thing on the dais was the source. The anchor. The root system.

"The Spires aren't in the palace," I heard myself say. "They're *under* it. Under all of it. The kingdom isn't ruled from the castle. The castle is ruled from here."

Vessa was reading the column script, running her fingers along the spirals with the expression of a woman doing sums she doesn't like. "These are names," she said. "King after king after king. And next to each name—" her finger stopped. Her face changed. "*Gideon.*"

"What?"

"There's a second column. Smaller. Guardians." She turned to look at me, and whatever she saw in my face made her stop talking.

Because I had already found it. Third column from the dais, fourth row up, in script so fine I'd mistaken it for decoration: *Blackwood*. And beneath it, more Blackwoods than I could count, generation after generation, all the way down to a name carved fresher than the rest, in letters that matched my grandmother's handwriting the way her lullabies had matched Wren's songs:

*Gideon Blackwood. Prepared.*

Prepared. Like a meal. Like a taster's portion. Like everything else in my life, decided long before I was born by people — or things — who considered me a tool with a heartbeat.

I don't remember sitting down. I remember the cold of the stone through my coat, and Wren's small hand landing on my knee, and the strange comfort of it. She didn't try to comfort me with words — she didn't have any words I could bear just then. She just stood there, being warm, being real, while the ledger of my life rearranged itself around a single carved word.

Nine years of swallowing poison for a king. Nine years of perfecting my body into a fortress against everything the crown's enemies might brew. I'd always assumed it was training. Discipline. Corvin's merciless curriculum, my own iron will.

It was engineering. Someone had built me the way you build a lock — deliberately, patiently, for a purpose I hadn't chosen.

"Get up," Vessa said, not unkindly. "Grieve later. We're exposed."

She was right, of course. She was always right, which was one of the more annoying things about her. I got up. My knees worked. That was enough.

It was Wren who found the letter.

She had wandered — I let her, within sight, because watching her read this place like a book was the closest thing to hope I'd felt in weeks — to a niche behind the newest throne. There was a loose slab there, and behind the slab, a cavity, and in the cavity, sealed in wax and wrapped in oilcloth, a letter.

The seal was the royal crest. Broken recently — the wax still bright.

"This is fresh," I said. "Days old. Maybe a week."

"Whoever left it had access," Vessa murmured. "And time alone down here."

Which narrowed the list considerably. Access to a sealed crypt, knowledge of the old tongue, and reason to hide a message where only someone who spoke it would find it. I unwrapped the oilcloth with the care of a man defusing a trap, because nine years of tasting had taught me that anything meant to be found easily was meant to be found by someone else.

The letter was written in two languages. The opening lines were in court script — formal, elegant, the hand of a man educated to rule. Then the script degraded, wobbled, and the rest was in the old tongue, the characters growing shakier as they went, as if the writer were fighting for every word.

I read the court-script opening aloud, because Vessa deserved to hear it.

*"To whoever finds this — if anyone finds this — I do not know how much longer I am myself. There is a thing wearing my body. It speaks with my voice and signs with my hand and last night it ordered the execution of a family in Millbrook for treason they did not commit, and I watched myself give the order and I could not stop my own mouth.*

*I am King Aldric. I write this during the hour after dawn, when the snow falls and I remember being a boy. If you can read the old tongue, read the rest. It matters more."*

Then the old tongue. And here is the thing — I could read it. Fluently. Words I had never studied poured into me like water finding a channel already dug, and I understood that this too was inheritance, this too was preparation, my grandmother's bread-baking lullabies doing their quiet work across the decades.

The old tongue portion was worse. It was worse because it was precise. Aldric — or whoever remained of him — had recorded everything. The binding ritual. The parasite's feeding cycle. The solstice deadline, when the current binding would become permanent and whatever was left of the man would be consumed entirely. And at the bottom, drawn with a ruler's care, a map.

The Last Spire.

Not a legend. Not a story smugglers told over bad brandy. A real place, high on Thornfell's northern face, past the Corpse Pass, reachable only in the weeks before deep winter closed the heights. Reachable, in fact, exactly now, exactly in time for the solstice, which was either remarkable luck or the cruelest timing in three hundred years.

"The unbinding happens at the Spire," I said. My voice sounded distant to me, like someone else's. "That's where they cut the child out of the mountain in the first place. That's where it has to be put back."

"And the parasite knows that too," Vessa said. "Which means the Spire is the one place it will defend hardest."

"Yes."

"So we're walking into its mouth."

"I've been doing that for nine years." I folded the letter along its creases and tucked it inside my coat, against my chest, where the important things go. "One more mouth won't matter."

Wren tugged my sleeve. She pointed at the empty throne — the newest one, the humming one — and then at me, and then she made a gesture I didn't understand until she took both my hands in hers and pressed them together, palm to palm, the way you'd press wax onto a seal.

"No," I said immediately.

She tilted her head. Waited. Seven years old and patient as the mountain itself.

"I'm not a king. I'm not anything. I'm a man who tastes soup."

She pressed my hands together again. Then she pointed at the column with my name on it. *Prepared.* Then back at me. And the terrible thing, the thing I would carry up the mountain and through everything that followed, was that she wasn't threatening me or commanding me. She was just telling me the truth, the way children do, with that awful clarity adults spend their whole lives learning to avoid.

The throne hummed again, softly, like it agreed with her.

"We should go," Vessa said, and there was something new in her voice — respect, maybe, or pity, and I honestly couldn't have told you which was worse. "Light's failing. And if the guards found the chapel above—"

She stopped. We all heard it at the same moment.

Footsteps. Above us, filtering down through three feet of chapel floor and three hundred years of silence: boots. Many boots. And beneath them, faint as a dying ember, a voice giving orders in a tone I recognized the way you recognize your own reflection.

Corvin.

He was here. He'd found the chapel, he'd brought the guard, and he was standing directly over the only door out of this tomb, speaking in the calm, reasonable voice of a man explaining to soldiers why the people below them needed to die.

Wren looked up at me. The lantern light caught the old markings on her skin, faintly glowing now, brighter than I'd ever seen them — as if the crypt itself were waking up around her, as if the mountain knew its fragment had come home and was calling the rest of itself to attention.

Eleven dead kings watched us from their thrones. An empty twelfth waited. A crown sat on its cushion, rooted into the bones of the world, and above us the man who taught me everything I knew was giving the order to seal us in.

"Back door?" Vessa whispered.

I looked at Wren. Wren looked at the far wall — the darkest one, the one with no visible seam — and began, very softly, to sing.

* * *

The wall opened like a wound. Not gently — stone ground against stone with a shriek that echoed through the crypt and, I realized with a sinking stomach, echoed up through the chapel floor as well. So much for subtlety. Whatever stealth we'd maintained by sneaking in through Wren's songs, we'd just spent shouting our exact location to every boot upstairs.

"Run," I said, and we ran.

Behind us, the crypt erupted into noise — boots on stone, Corvin's voice sharpening into command, the ring of steel. The passage Wren had opened was narrow, sloping downward, breathing cold air into our faces. Down. Always down, in this mountain. I had a brief, bitter thought that we were probably heading toward something worse than soldiers, and then decided I'd take my chances with ancient evil over royal steel, because at least ancient evil had rules.

We ran until the sounds of pursuit faded into the mountain's own noises — water dripping, stone settling, the vast slow heartbeat of Thornfell itself. Then we walked. Then we stumbled. Then, in a chamber where an underground stream cut across pale rock, Vessa called a halt, and we drank, and I finally allowed myself to think.

I took out the letter and read the old tongue portion again, slowly, by lantern light. There were details I'd missed in the crypt — details I'd been too shaken to absorb.

Aldric had written about the guardians. About what happened to them after a successful unbinding. His handwriting had been very steady for those lines, steadier than anywhere else in the letter, as if he'd known exactly what he was writing and wanted whoever found it to read it twice.

*"The last guardian to stand at the Spire gave freely, and the binding broke, and the kingdom had forty years of peace. What it cost him, the record does not say. Only that his name was struck from every roll in the realm, and that the mountain remembers him, though men do not."*

His name struck from every roll.

I looked at Wren, asleep against Vessa's shoulder, her markings dimmed to embers. I thought about the column in the crypt. *Gideon Blackwood. Prepared.* I thought about the immunity in my blood, engineered across generations, and about the empty throne, and about the fact that every step I'd taken since a mud-caked child knocked on my door had led me, with terrible precision, here — to a freezing stream at the bottom of a mountain, holding a dead man's letter, being aimed like an arrow at a place I'd never heard of six months ago.

"You've gone quiet," Vessa said. "Quieter."

"Just doing sums."

"Bad sums?"

"The kind where the answer is a person." I put the letter away. Above us, muffled by a hundred feet of rock, I imagined I could still hear Corvin's voice, patient and reasonable, explaining to the darkness why some sacrifices were necessary. Thirty years he'd served them. Thirty years, and he'd trained me to be ready for this without ever telling me what *this* was.

The stream chattered on, indifferent. Somewhere far above, winter was closing the passes, and the solstice was coming whether we climbed or not.

"Wake her," I said. "We need to move before the mountain changes its mind about doors."

And far above us, faint even through the stone — or maybe only in my head, I couldn't swear to it — I heard the Spire begin to glow.
