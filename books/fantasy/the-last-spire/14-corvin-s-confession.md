## 14. Corvin's Confession

I have imagined killing Corvin perhaps forty times in the last nine years. Never seriously — the way you imagine winning the lottery or telling a duke exactly what he smells like after a long ride. A fantasy of the powerless. Corvin taught me that a man's first weapon is his imagination, and his second is knowing when to holster it.

So when I heard the crunch of boots on the frost behind us at the crypt mouth — one set of boots, unhurried, the stride of a man who had never once needed to hurry — my imagination did not offer me murder. It offered me arithmetic instead. One old spymaster against a taster with a borrowed knife, a smuggler queen with six crossbows, and a child who could sing stone awake.

The arithmetic favored us, which was how I knew something was wrong. Corvin never walked into bad numbers unless he'd already changed the sum.

"Keep singing, Wren," Vessa said quietly, without turning her head. Her smugglers kept their crossbows level and their breathing even. "If he moves wrong, we'll still be able to hear the song over the noise."

Wren didn't stop singing. She couldn't, not really — she'd been singing since dawn, coaxing the crypt's sealed door to remember it was a door, and the song had a momentum now, like water finding its way downhill. The old Thornkings' tongue poured out of her small chest in a voice too large for it, and the mountain answered in groans and settling dust. But I saw her glance at me sideways, quick as a bird checking for hawks.

She knew the footsteps too. Children who've lived feral learn to catalogue sounds the way scholars catalogue dead languages. Boots: one. Weight: heavy but careful. Age: old. Intent: —

That was the part she looked at me for. Because intent was the word I'd spent nine years learning to taste in everything — in wine, in soup, in the pause before a courtier asks you to sample the pheasant twice.

"Corvin," I said, without turning around either. Two of us refusing to look at him. Very dramatic. Corvin would have hated it, which was half the pleasure.

"Gideon." His voice hadn't aged a day, which was its own kind of obscenity. Voices like his don't age; they just accumulate weight, like stones dropped in a well. "You've led them a merry chase. Through the Corpse Pass, no less. In winter. I told you once you had a talent for surviving things that should kill you. I confess I hoped you'd use it more sensibly."

"You sent hunters through the Corpse Pass."

"I sent *men*. What they became in that pass was between them and the pass." He sighed, and I heard cloth shift — a cloak being unclasped? Hands raised? "Turn around, boy. If this is to be an execution, I'll not have it done to my back like a coward's work."

Vessa's eyes flicked to mine. *Your call,* she said with one eyebrow, the way queens say things they'll deny later.

I turned around.

Corvin stood ten paces off in the grey morning light, snow gathering on his shoulders because he hadn't bothered to brush it away. He was unarmed. No sword, no knives — and Corvin without knives was like the sea without salt, a thing so fundamentally wrong it took the mind a moment to accept it. His hands were empty and open at his sides, and his face —

His face was what stopped the arithmetic cold. I'd seen Corvin wear every expression a face can hold: amusement, contempt, disappointment, the warm counterfeit pride he'd used on me for years like a coin he'd worn smooth passing it off. I had catalogued all of them. This one wasn't in the catalogue.

He looked *old*. Not aged — old the way ruins are old. Like something that had been standing a very long time past the point where standing meant anything.

"You're not here to arrest me," I said.

"No."

"You're not here to kill me."

"Not today." And then, with the ghost of the dry humor I'd grown up on: "Though I appreciate that you checked. Caution becomes you. It never became Aldric, gods rest whatever's left of him."

Behind me Wren's song faltered — one note, a stumble, like a heart skipping — and the great door of the crypt groaned half an inch wider. She caught herself and went on. Corvin's eyes moved to her and stayed there, and what crossed his face then was so naked I almost looked away. It was grief. Not fresh grief. Old grief, cellar-aged, the kind a man learns to live above until someone opens the door.

"The markings," he said softly. "On her arms. How far along are they?"

"They glow when she sings," Vessa said flatly, because I didn't answer fast enough and she never trusted anyone else's silence. "Why do you care?"

"Because I've seen that before." Corvin took one step forward and every crossbow string creaked, and he stopped, and smiled at the crossbows with something close to relief. "Yes. Good. Keep those up. One of us ought to be sensible."

"Corvin." My voice came out harder than I intended. "Thirty years. You trained me. You fed me, housed me, taught me which deaths to fear and which to ignore. You sat across from me not three weeks ago and told me to bring the child back to the palace, told me the king wanted her *protected* —"

"He did want her protected." The words came out cracked down the middle. "That's the hell of it, Gideon. Everything I've ever told you has been true. That's how it works. That's how it *worked*, thirty years, before there was a you to lie to. You can't lie your way through a lifetime. You tell the truth in pieces and let the pieces make a shape that serves you. I learned that from the best liars in the world." He paused. "I learned it from *them*."

Them. He didn't capitalize it aloud — voices can't — but Wren flinched at the word anyway, mid-song, and the door groaned again, and I understood that the mountain knew what he meant before I did.

"The Spires," I said.

"The Spires," Corvin agreed.

And then Corvin — Corvin Hale, spymaster of the crown, the man who had plucked a starving kitchen brat out of the scullery and made him into a weapon with a palate — knelt. Down into the snow, both knees, hands open on his thighs, head bowed. The posture of a man surrendering his sword, except he had no sword, except maybe he'd been surrendering it for thirty years and only now found somewhere to put it down.

Vessa made a sound low in her throat. Her smugglers held steady, but I saw the confusion ripple through them. Men know how to shoot a threat. Nobody knows what to do with a kneeling one.

"What are you doing?" I asked. Stupidly. As if there were more than one possible answer.

"Pleading," Corvin said to the snow. "It's undignified. Watch closely, boy, because it's the last lesson I have for you and it's the one I should have started with: this is what it looks like when a man finally says the true thing. It looks like this. Knees in the wet. There's no dignified version. I've searched."

Wren's song had gone quiet. I don't know when — somewhere in the middle of Corvin's kneeling, the mountain had stopped needing her voice and started holding its own breath. Even the wind seemed to be leaning in.

"Talk," I said.

He talked.

* * *

"I was twenty-three," Corvin said. "Younger than you were when I recruited you. Do you understand that? When I did what I did, I was younger than the man I lied to in the kitchens. Remember that, when you decide what I deserve."

Snow ticked down through the pines. Somewhere below us, a raven complained and gave up.

"There were five kingdoms then. You know this from the histories — the histories love it, the Wars of the Broken Crowns, two hundred years of it, grandfather's axe avenging grandfather's grave, fields salted, rivers running red, the whole glorious pageant. The histories end it neatly: King Osric the Uniter, first of the Thornking line, crowned at the Last Spire, peace forever, amen." His mouth twisted. "The histories were written by the winners, and the winners weren't Osric. The winners were the things that found him up there."

"I know," I said quietly. "We found the letters. Every king for three centuries. Bound."

"You found the *record*. You didn't find the beginning." He lifted his head, and his eyes were wet and furious and aimed somewhere through me at a younger man wearing his face. "There was a cabal. Call them that — it sounds better than what we were. Scholars, mostly. War-tired men and women who'd watched the Broken Crowns eat a generation and decided that if men couldn't stop fighting, then men would be *stopped*. We knew the old magic was real. The mountain's magic — the thing your grandmother sang to, the thing this child is a splinter of. The Thornkings had guarded it, communed with it, been shaped by it. And we asked ourselves the question that damns every idealist since the first one climbed a tower with good intentions:

"*What if the crown itself were bound to it?*

"Not destroyed. Bound. Bridled. A king whose will passed through — call it a filter. Something that would take the ambition out of him, the appetite, the mad little fires that start wars. A peaceful king by construction. Peace, guaranteed. Not won and lost and won again, but *engineered*. Permanent." He laughed, one dry bark with nothing in it. "Permanent. Gods. We used that word like children using a word they've heard adults say.

"We went up the Spire with our diagrams and our certainty, and the mountain — the mountain *let* us. Did you know that? The old magic could have burned us to ash on the threshold. Instead it... watched. I think it wanted to see what we'd become. I think it's been wanting to see ever since. And we cut a piece out of it — a piece of itself, a fragment, a *child-shaped piece* — and used it as the keystone. The binding needed a living anchor. Something innocent. Something that wouldn't resist, because it didn't understand what was happening until it was already apart."

My stomach turned over slowly, the way it does when poison arrives late. Behind me, Wren's small hand found the hem of my coat and held it. She hadn't understood the words. She understood the shape of them. Some things translate without language.

"She was the first," I said.

"She was the first." Corvin's voice went to gravel. "A girl of six, a shepherd's daughter from the high valleys, marked by the mountain the way some children are marked by lightning — touched, chosen, singing songs older than the valleys. We told ourselves she wasn't harmed. We told ourselves she was *elevated*. Part of something eternal. And the binding worked, Gideon. Gods forgive me, it worked. Osric signed the Accords of Union with a gentle heart and no appetite for conquest, and the wars ended inside a decade, and we stood in the halls of power and told each other: *look what we saved.* Two hundred years of slaughter, ended. By us. By the thing we built."

"And the cost?"

"The cost came due slowly, the way costs do. The fragment — the girl — she didn't die. Worse. She *persisted*. Split from the mountain but anchored to the crown line, conscious, alone, for centuries, while the binding deepened and hardened and grew... appetites of its own. You've felt it. You tasted nightshade in the king's cup and the king drank it and *smiled*. That's not the binding we designed. Bindings erode. Or rather — they get *inhabited*. Things move into ruins, boy. The Spires began as our name for the tower. Then it became our name for the order that maintained the work. And somewhere in the last century, the name turned around and started meaning the thing that maintains *us*." He spread his empty hands. "The parasite wears the parasite's name. It's either very funny or it isn't, depending on whether you're currently kneeling in snow."

"And you stayed," I said. "Thirty years. You fed kings into it. You trained tasters — you trained *me* —"

"To guard the vessel's body. Yes." He didn't flinch from it, and I'll grant him that much; he met every word like a man taking counted blows. "The logic was always the same, and it was always *almost* right. If the binding breaks badly, the fragment dies, the crown line shatters, the kingdoms remember they hate each other, and the Broken Crowns come back with new axes and old grudges. Thirty years I told myself: keep it stable, keep it stable, until we can undo it *safely*. And every year the safely receded, like the horizon. And every year the thing in the palace grew stronger and the man grew fainter, and I told myself stories at bedtime the way I told you truths in pieces." He looked up at me fully then. "Do you see it yet? Do you see why I'm kneeling?"

I did. That was the obscene part. I saw it the way you taste almond in wine and know, before any test confirms it, that someone is already dying.

Corvin had been me. Corvin was what I became if I chose order over truth one more year, and then one more, until the choosing wore a groove in me so deep I called the groove my character. He had swallowed the same lie I'd been swallowing — not in wine, but in duty — and his immunity to it was gone, and the poison had become the man.

"You're not asking forgiveness," I said slowly.

"I'm past asking for anything I couldn't carry myself." He got one knee under him, then the other, rising stiffly, an old jointed machine of a man. From inside his coat he drew a single folded paper, creased soft with handling. "But I am asking you to hear the rest, because the rest is why I'm here, alone, unarmed, ahead of the hunters I can no longer slow down. The solstice binding isn't a ceremony, Gideon. It's a *feeding*. The Spires mean to devour the last of Aldric — his memories, his self, the final residue of the man — and use the strength to bind the fragment back into the keystone permanently. Your Wren goes back into the wall. Forever, this time. No erosion, no escape, no singing doors open. And the parasite rides the crown line for another three hundred years, and another, and there is no Corvin coming in a hundred years to kneel in the snow, because I will have made sure of it."

He held out the paper. I took it. It was a list — names, dates, rotations. The royal guard's disposition for the next four days. Written in his own hand.

"The guard reaches the High Ridge tomorrow at dusk," he said. "Full strength, Spire-led, no longer entirely men — you'll see. Maren is already captured; they took her at the ford two days ago, and she bought your trail's silence with her wrists, which you should know, because she'll never tell you herself. Vessa's people cannot hold the ridge. I've read every battle fought in these mountains for thirty years; trust the arithmetic for once." His voice dropped. "But there is a way. Through the crypt, down the under-road the Thornkings cut, and it comes out *inside* the Spire's own root — the parasite's back door, the one flaw in the perfect work, because we built it in a hurry and I was the one who drew the maps. I drew them. I've carried them for thirty years waiting for someone I hated enough to give them to."

"Why me?" I asked. "Why now?"

"Because I watched you refuse a title last spring," Corvin said, "and I realized you were the first person I'd trained who wanted nothing. And a man who wants nothing can't be bought, and a man who can't be bought—" his voice failed, gathered itself, "—is the only kind who can give something freely. The ritual demands blood freely given. I've known that for thirty years too. I just could never find anyone whose freedom I believed in."

Behind me, Wren began to sing again. Softly. Not the door-song — something older, slower, the melody of my grandmother's lullabies, the ones sung in a tongue no living scholar knew. She sang it at Corvin, head tilted, watching him with the ancient, terrible gentleness of the mountain itself.

And the snow around his knees — I swear this, and I have sworn stranger things since — melted in a perfect ring, and through the bare frozen earth pushed a single green shoot, budding, in the dead of winter.

Corvin looked down at it for a long time.

"Well," he said, wrecked and quiet. "At least somebody forgives something."

Then he straightened, wiped his face with the heel of his hand like a soldier clearing rain, and pointed east, toward the ridge, where the light was already going strange and gold in a way that had nothing to do with sunset.

"Now move, boy. They're a day out, and the thing leading them knows I've defected — it's been tasting my thoughts since midnight. Which means it also knows the under-road exists." He smiled, and it was the first honest smile I'd ever seen him wear, thin and terrified and free. "So for the first time in thirty years, the enemy knows everything I know. Let's see how it likes fighting a man with nothing left to hide."
