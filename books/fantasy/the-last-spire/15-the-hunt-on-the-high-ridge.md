## 15. The Hunt on the High Ridge

I have been poisoned forty-one times in my career as a royal food-taster. I can tell you what arsenic tastes like — bitter almonds with a whisper of garlic — and what wolfsbane does to the tongue, and why you should never trust a soup that smells better than it looks. I thought I knew every way a man could be taken by surprise.

Then a crossbow bolt buried itself in the snow six inches from my boot, and I learned there is at least one more.

"Down!" Vessa's voice cracked across the ridge like a whip, and her smugglers dropped into the rocks the way water finds cracks — no orders, no hesitation, just twenty bodies vanishing into cover that had been invisible to me a heartbeat before. That's the thing about smugglers. They don't walk through the world. They *inhabit* its seams.

Wren was already moving. Nine years of tasting for a paranoid king had taught me to read danger early; nine days with this child had taught me she didn't need to be taught at all. She'd gone flat behind an outcrop before the second bolt sang over our heads, her small hands pressed against the stone, her lips moving in that dead tongue — not frightened, I realized. *Counting.*

"What are you counting?" I hissed.

"How many," she said, in the old words first and then, clumsily, in mine. "Twelve bows. Four horses. One man who walks wrong."

"One man who walks wrong?"

She looked up at me with those eyes that were too old for her face, too old for anyone's face, and said nothing, because Wren never wasted words on things you'd understand soon enough anyway.

The ridge was called Cael's Backbone by the smugglers, a spine of black granite running half a mile between two drops into white nothing. It was the only crossing for three days in any direction, which meant it was either the best road or the worst trap in Thornfell, depending entirely on who reached it first.

The royal guard had reached it first.

* * *

They came up the switchbacks in formation, which told me everything. The king's guard I'd served beside for nine years fought like brawlers with better boots — they broke formation the moment blood was drawn, all appetite and noise. These men moved like clockwork. Twelve crossbows laying down covering fire while eight swords advanced in a line, shields locked, no shouting, no curses. No *humanity*.

"The Spires are driving them," Maren said beside me. She'd followed us from Bracken Hollow without invitation, carrying a physician's satchel and a guilt she wore like a wet coat. "Not commanding them. Driving them. There's a difference."

"There is?"

"A command you can disobey." She pulled a bandage from her sleeve and started winding it around my forearm, where a splinter of granite had opened me during the first volley. Her hands were steady. Her eyes weren't. "A driver doesn't care if the horse dies pulling the cart."

I watched the shield line come on, patient as tide. "How do you know so much about it, physician?"

Her hands stopped. For one moment I saw her decide — actually decide — whether to lie to me, and I felt something in my chest go cold, because I'd spent nine years watching people make exactly that calculation across a banquet table, and I knew every flavor of it.

"I'll tell you," she said quietly, "if we live through this hour. Not before. If I'm about to die, I'd rather die having been useful than having been honest."

"That," I told her, "is the most honest thing anyone has said to me in ten years."

Then the line hit Vessa's picket, and there was no more time for conversation.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about watching smugglers fight: they don't fight fair, they don't fight clean, and they don't fight for glory — but by the gods, they fight for *each other*. Vessa had bought these people with silver, or so she claimed. I watched a boy of maybe seventeen take a sword-cut across the shoulder rather than give ground, because giving ground would have exposed old Tam, who was reloading his crossbow with fingers too frostbitten to hurry. Nobody ordered him to stand. Nobody thanked him either. Tam just grunted and put a bolt through the throat of the man who'd cut the boy.

Vessa herself fought like a woman settling accounts. She'd planted herself on the highest rock with two knives and a mouth full of insults aimed at the guard's mothers, their fathers, and their gods, and every insult covered a kill. When a sergeant broke through the picket line and came up the slope toward where Wren crouched, Vessa moved faster than a woman her age should move, took a pommel strike across the jaw that should have felled an ox, and still drove both knives home.

But there were twelve crossbows. And crossbows don't tire.

"We can't hold this," I shouted to her.

"We're not holding it, taster!" Blood in her teeth, grinning anyway. "We're *spending* it! Get your ghost-child to the far side!"

Spending it. Yes. Every minute her people bled on the rocks was a minute closer to the treeline on the far slope, and the pass beyond it, and the Last Spire. She'd named her price back in Bracken Hollow — a vault, a name, a family's stolen honor — and I'd agreed, knowing the cost might ruin us all. What she hadn't named, what no contract holds, was this: that her people would pay in blood for a promise made to *me*.

Loyalty proven in wounds, not words. I would carry that ledger the rest of my life, however long that turned out to be.

I grabbed Wren's hand. "Time to run."

"No," she said. And pointed.

Down the near switchbacks, coming up through the falling snow, rode four horses. And the man who walked wrong — who rode wrong, whose body sat in the saddle the way a puppet sits on a hook — raised one hand, and every crossbow on the ridge stopped firing at once.

* * *

You want to know what the thing wearing King Aldric felt, riding toward Cael's Backbone? You cannot. That is the mercy of it. But I will tell you what *I* felt, in the small hours it allowed me, because someone must remember, even if only the snow hears.

It was pleased.

I know pleasure the way a drowning man knows air — in glimpses, in stolen seconds when its attention turned elsewhere and Aldric rose gasping to the surface of his own skull. In those seconds I felt what it felt, filtered through whatever was left of me, and it was pleased the way a farmer is pleased when the pens are built and the animals have walked themselves into the slaughtering yard.

*Four on the ridge,* it thought, with my mind, in my voice, in a language I did not know I knew until it used it. *The vessel-child. The guardian-seed. The healer who lied. The old traitor.* It catalogued us the way I once catalogued poisons — by effect, by dosage, by how much of each the plan required.

And then, beneath that cold arithmetic, *my* thought, rising like a bubble through tar: *Corvin. It called Corvin the old traitor. What has he done?*

Thirty years. Corvin Ash had taught me to taste, to listen, to survive. He had held my grandmother's lullabies in his memory and never spoken of them. And now the parasite that wore my king counted him among its assets with a word that meant, in the old tongue, *one who betrays twice.*

I screamed into the dark of my own head. It did not hear. It never heard. That was the one gift of my hollowing: my rage was my own, entirely, uselessly my own.

The Spire pulsed ahead of us, beyond the ridge, beyond the storm — a thread of pale light in the mountain's crown, growing brighter as the solstice came on. Three days. It measured time now the way I measured poison doses, precisely, hungrily. Three days, and the binding would close forever, and whatever remained of Aldric of House Merrow would be scraped from the walls of his own body and fed to the roots.

In the last hour before the ridge, it devoured another memory. I felt it leave — my daughter, aged six, laughing in the palace garden, snow catching in her hair. It took her the way a man takes bread. And I was glad, in the end, that it took that one, because I had begun to forget her face myself, and I could not bear to lose her twice.

* * *

The man who rode wrong dismounted thirty yards below us, and the snow seemed to fall around him rather than on him, and Wren made a sound I had never heard from her — a small, cracked note, like a struck bell with a flaw in it.

"It knows me," she whispered. "It remembers being whole. It wants—"

"I know what it wants."

"No." She gripped my wrist hard enough to bruise. "Gideon. It wants to say *thank you*."

I stared at her. Below us, the puppet-king's mouth opened, and a voice came out that was almost Aldric's — warm, reasonable, the voice of a man who had never broken faith with anyone.

"Gideon Blackwood!" it called up the ridge. "Nine years of loyal service! Do you think we haven't noticed? Do you think the wine was chance?" A smile, wide and wrong. "Come down. Bring the fragment. Serve the crown a little longer, and when the binding closes, you may keep your name. We will even let you keep your memories. Most guardians lose those. Consider it generosity."

Beside me, Vessa spat blood into the snow. "Guardians," she muttered. "He talks like he's offering a pension."

"He is," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "That's what makes it terrible."

Because here was the truth of it, laid bare in the puppet-king's reasonable voice: my immunity, my survival, my nine years of swallowing death and walking away — none of it was luck. None of it was skill. Somewhere in a vault on the mountain, my name was already written in a book I'd never seen, and everything I was had been *prepared*. The offer wasn't a trap. It was a job posting.

Wren's hand found mine. Small, cold, steady.

"You are not theirs," she said, in the old tongue, and then again in mine, carefully, like a child carrying water without spilling it. "You are *mine*. I chose you. At the door. First night."

"You chose me because I had bread."

"I chose you," she repeated, "because you gave it before you knew why I knocked."

Below us, the thing wearing the king waited, patient as winter. Above us, the sky thickened toward evening. On the rocks, Vessa's people were dragging their wounded back into cover, counting their dead — two, I could see, maybe three — and Maren was already among them with her satchel open, doing the only honest work there was.

And down the southern slope, moving fast through the trees, came a figure on foot that I recognized even at half a mile: broad-shouldered, grey-cloaked, walking with the particular economy of a man who had taught me everything I knew about staying alive.

Corvin.

Coming not toward the guard. Not toward us, either. Toward the narrow notch in the ridge's spine that only smugglers knew — the path that bypassed the killing ground entirely.

He was clearing our escape route. Or herding us into it. Thirty years serving the Spires, and after all of it, I still could not tell which.

"Vessa," I said. "Can your people fall back through the notch?"

"They can," she said, wiping her knives. "Question is what's waiting on the other end."

"An old man," I said, "who owes me either an apology or a knife in the dark. Possibly both."

Vessa grinned through split lips. "Then let's go collect."

Behind us, the puppet-king's voice followed us up the ridge, warm and reasonable and utterly empty, promising pensions and names and mercy — and ahead of us, the snow swallowed the notch, and the mountain, and everything that waited past it.

Three days to solstice. Two dead smugglers on the rocks behind me. One confession I hadn't yet heard, owed by a woman who poisoned a cup of wine to test a taster's soul.

I ran. For once in my careful, calculating life, I didn't count the cost first.

That, I suspect, was what my grandmother's lullabies had always been trying to teach me.
