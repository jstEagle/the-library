## 16. The Thing Wearing the Crown

I remember snow. That is how I know I am still in here.

The thing that wears me does not remember snow. It remembers *cold*, the way a ledger remembers numbers, but it has never stood at a window at seven years old watching the first flakes come down over Thornfell and felt that particular ache in the chest — the one that is half joy and half grief and entirely human. It files my memories. That's all it does with them. It takes them out, turns them over, decides which ones are useful for walking and talking and signing decrees, and puts the rest away like winter clothes in a chest I cannot open.

But some nights, when it sleeps — if you can call what it does sleeping, that long flat stillness like a pond that has forgotten how to be water — I climb up through whatever is left of myself and I stand in the memory of snow. Seven years old. Mother's hand on my shoulder. The smell of woodsmoke. Mine. All of it mine. For as long as the remembering lasts.

Tonight the remembering lasts four minutes. Last week it was eleven. I am being eaten from the inside out, and the terrible mathematics of it are the only clock I have left.

* * *

It knows something has changed. I can tell because it has stopped bothering to be me.

For nine years — no. Longer. Since before I understood what was happening, since the coronation, since the moment the crown came down and something came down with it — it has performed Aldric with great care. It practiced my laugh in front of mirrors. It studied my father's letters to learn how I sign my name. It asked Maren questions about my childhood fevers so that its lies would have the right texture. There was even, in the early years, a kind of craftsmanship to it that I could almost admire, the way you might admire a counterfeiter's plate, knowing full well the coin is false.

Lately it has grown careless. It walks through the palace like a man walking through rooms he owns but never looks at. Yesterday it held audience for three hours and did not once use my mother's phrase for petitioners — *let them finish, Aldric, the kingdom can spare five minutes* — and when the ambassador from Veyl mentioned the harvest, it smiled with my mouth and said, "The harvest will be sufficient," in a voice like a door closing. Sufficient. My grandfather would have wept. My father would have laughed and poured more wine.

And last night, in the dark, sitting alone at my desk with candles burning down around it like hours being executed one by one, it took out my memories and began counting them.

I felt it happen. That's the worst part. Not the taking — I have learned to endure the taking, the cold pull behind the eyes, the way a whole year of your life goes dim and distant, a room you once lived in seen now through frosted glass. No, the worst part was the *sorting*. It laid me out like a merchant laying out cloth and it marked each piece. This memory: keep, useful. This one: discard, sentiment, dead weight. And then it paused over one — Wren, I think, or rather the shape of a child I dreamed three weeks ago, mud-caked and singing in a language I should not understand and did, God help me, did — and it held that one up to the candlelight for a long time.

Then it burned it.

I don't mean metaphorically. Something in the deep cellar of me went dark, the way a lamp goes out, and where the dream had been there is now a smooth white absence, scar tissue where a thought used to live. It is feeding. It is burning everything warm in me to heat itself for whatever comes at solstice, and it has begun with the things it cannot use.

Which means it expects to win. Which means it is afraid enough to prepare.

Both of those facts are, in their different ways, the best news I have had in thirty years.

* * *

"You are distracted," Chancellor Hesse says this morning. He stands at the foot of the dais with his hands folded, a grey man in grey wool, and I want to grab him by the collar and scream *look at my eyes, look at my eyes, someone in this palace look at my eyes.*

The thing wearing me tilts my head at an angle I would never use. "The solstice approaches," it says with my voice. "There is much to attend."

"Indeed." Hesse's gaze flicks up — just for a heartbeat — to my face, and there is something in it I have never been able to read. Fear? Pity? Recognition? Thirty years I have watched that man and thirty years he has watched me back, and I have never once known whether he serves the crown or merely tolerates it. "The guards report movement in Bracken Hollow. Smuggler traffic. A taster, they believe. Blackwood."

Blackwood. The name lands somewhere in me like a stone dropped down a well. I wait — I have gotten very good at waiting — for the echo. And it comes: a flash of the tasting hall, nine years ago, a young man with careful hands and careful eyes who swallowed poison for me twice a week and never once flinched. Gideon Blackwood. I liked him. That is such a small, stupid thing to cling to — I liked him, the way a king likes a good hound — but it is *mine*, and they haven't taken it yet, and I hold onto it the way a drowning man holds a plank.

"The taster fled?" the thing asks.

"With a child, Majesty. Marked. The physicians who've seen her describe—"

"I know what she is." It says it flatly, and beneath my ribs something old and enormous stirs, turning over in its sleep. "Double the watches on the mountain roads. Nothing reaches the Spire."

"As you command." Hesse bows. And as he straightens, his eyes meet mine — actually mine, the thing having looked away to study a map — and for one impossible second I would swear the old man's face does something. Not kindness. Not quite. More like the expression of a man passing a cell block and hearing, faintly, singing.

Then it is gone, and Hesse is grey again, and I am alone inside my own skull with a monster for a landlord.

*Run faster, boy,* I think at the closed doors, at the smuggler roads I will never see, at the child singing songs my grandmother's grandmother might have sung. *Run faster. I'll burn slow.*

* * *

It rides me out of the palace on the third day before solstice.

No announcement. No procession. It simply rises in the night, dresses my body in travelling leathers instead of state robes, and walks down through corridors where servants bow to it and it does not see them, because it has stopped performing for anyone who doesn't matter. In the stable it saddles my grey mare — hers, not mine; I named her Bess when I was twelve and it has called her *the animal* for twenty years — and it rides west without looking back at the palace, and I realize, with a lurch, that it is not looking back because the palace means nothing to it. The crown, the throne, the kingdom — these were always just the harness. The Spire is the home.

*You could at least pretend to love it,* I snarl inside, the way I've snarled for thirty years, into a silence that has never once answered. It doesn't converse with me. I am furniture to it. I am the house it lives in, and houses do not get addressed.

But tonight, riding under a sky scoured clean and bitter with stars, something changes. Tonight it talks.

Not to me. To itself — or to the others, whichever plural it uses when it thinks in the dark, and I have learned to eavesdrop on those thoughts the way a prisoner learns the footsteps of his jailers. The thoughts come in pressure and cold, in shapes like frost forming on glass:

*The fragment moves toward the mountain. The taster carries it. The guardian-line stirs.* A pause, and then something that in any other creature I would call worry: *Thirty years early. The binding was to hold another generation.*

Another thought follows, and this one I feel in my teeth: *Burn him.*

And then it begins.

I have been eaten slowly for thirty years, in small cold meals, a memory here, an afternoon there, enough of me left standing to keep the body walking and the signature convincing. But tonight, on the high road west with the Spire a black tooth on the horizon, it stops rationing. It opens the chest where it keeps me, all the winters and summers and small shames and large loves of Aldric son of Maric, and it starts feeding them into the fire.

I feel my tenth birthday go. The pony. Father laughing. Gone — not faded, *gone*, a hole shaped exactly like a pony.

I feel my wedding go. Elenna's hands. The rain on the chapel roof. I try to hold it — I throw everything I am against it, I scream without lungs — and for a moment, one shining moment, the memory fights back. Because some things refuse. Some things are load-bearing. Elenna's hands stay, barely, like a painting after the wall has burned, floating on nothing.

*I loved her,* I say into the dark, into the thing, into whatever hears. *She died believing I had stopped loving her, because you wore my face at her deathbed and you couldn't be bothered to grieve. Take the pony. Take the birthdays. But I loved her, and you will choke on that.*

The cold pauses. Considers. And then — and this I will carry into whatever I become — it *takes the memory anyway*, deliberately, spitefully, the way a man kicks a dog on his way past. Elenna's hands go out like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

I do not know how long I lie broken inside myself after that. Time in here is weather, not hours. When I gather what's left of me, I take inventory, and the inventory is short. Snow. My mother's voice. The taste of honeyed bread. The word *sufficient* and my hatred of it. A taster named Blackwood with careful hands. A child in the mud, singing.

That is nearly all. That is the whole kingdom of Aldric, reduced to a handful of coins in a beggar's cup.

And yet.

Here is the joke, and I tell it to myself because no one else in this body will appreciate it: it needs me. Even now, even burning me as fuel, it needs the residue. The binding at solstice requires a king — requires the *shape* of one, the blood-memory, the oath spoken by a throat that carries the coronation oil in its skin. It cannot wear a hollow man to the ritual. It must wear a king. So it burns me and keeps the ash, and the ash is royal, and that is the one thread it cannot cut without unraveling itself.

Which means that everything I have left — every scrap of remembered snow, every coin in the beggar's cup — is not just what remains of me.

It is ammunition.

* * *

We stop at dawn on a ridge, and below us the land falls away into the Thornfell passes, grey and white and endless, and far off — so far it might be imagination — a smudge of smoke rising from a valley road. Travellers. Small figures moving fast.

The thing dismounts and stares, drawing my eyes across miles of cold air, and I feel it reach — probing, tasting the wind like a serpent's tongue — searching for the fragment, for the child, for the guardian-blood walking my roads toward my Spire.

And I do the only thing left to me. I take the memory of snow — my oldest, my truest, the first thing it ever filed away and the last thing it has never managed to burn — and I wrap it around myself like a cloak, and I push.

It is like pushing a mountain. I am less than a whisper; it is a cathedral. But a whisper, aimed, in the right ear, at the right moment —

Below, on the valley road, the small figures vanish behind a fold of the hills. The thing loses them.

It stands there a long moment at the rail, my hands gripping the stone, and the frustration that rolls off it is a blizzard, a furnace, a scream with no mouth. Somewhere in the passes below, a taster and a marked child have slipped behind a hill, and the parasite wearing the King of Thornfell stands on a ridge in the freezing dawn, blind, furious, and — for the first time in thirty years —

afraid.

*Good,* I think, with the last warm corner of myself. *Be afraid. Learn what it costs.*

*Learn what it is to be the one who runs.*

It mounts. It turns my face west, toward the black tooth of the Spire, and it rides, devouring me mile by mile, hour by hour, and behind my eyes the clock is audible now — a great cold ticking, each tick a memory, each tick less of me —

Three days to solstice.

Two days of Aldric left.

Hold on, boy. I'm coming as fast as I'm eaten.
