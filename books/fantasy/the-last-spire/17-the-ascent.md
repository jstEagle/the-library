## 17. The Ascent

The mountain began to breathe on the second day.

I don't mean that poetically. I'd spent nine years learning to distrust poetry — it's what kings say when they want you to die for something beautiful. I mean the passages around us contracted and expanded in a slow rhythm, like lungs, and the air that moved through them was warm and smelled of rain and iron and something older underneath, the way a cellar smells of the house above it.

Wren walked ahead of me with her hand flat against the stone wall, and the wall glowed faintly where her fingers touched, a pale gold light that faded behind us like a wake behind a boat.

"That's new," I said.

She looked back at me. She didn't speak much anymore — not because she'd stopped knowing words, but because she was becoming something that didn't need them. When she did talk, it came out half in the old tongue, half in ours, the two languages braided together like rope.

*"The mountain remembers me,"* she said. *"It is glad."*

"Mountains can't be glad."

She gave me a look. Nine years of tasting poison had taught me to read faces the way other men read ledgers, and hers said: *you are a very stupid man, and I am fond of you anyway.*

Fair enough. I've been called worse by better.

* * *

We had left Vessa and her smugglers two days before, at the mouth of a crevasse no map has ever named because no mapmaker who found it lived to ink it. Vessa had gripped my forearm hard enough to leave marks.

"The vault," she said. "You swear it."

"I swear it. Your name comes out of that vault or I don't come out either."

"And the girl?"

I looked at Wren then, small and mud-streaked still, though the mountain had been cleaning her somehow — the grime wouldn't stay on her anymore. It slid off like water off wax.

"She goes home," I said.

Vessa studied me with those coin-counter's eyes of hers, the ones that could price a man's soul to the copper. "You know what home means for her, taster?"

I knew. I'd known since the crypt, since the letter written by a dying king who remembered snow. The child was a fragment — cut from the mountain's magic three centuries ago when the first Spire bound its first king, split apart so the whole would be too broken to fight back. Every guardian since had carried a piece without knowing it. My grandmother's name was in the vault among the bound. Mine would have been too, if I'd ever been entered in the rolls under my real name instead of the tasting-office alias they'd given me.

Wren wasn't going home the way a child goes home from market. She was going home the way a river goes home to the sea.

"You know what home means," Vessa repeated, softer.

"Yes," I said, and walked into the dark before she could see my face do anything undignified.

* * *

The living passages were nothing like the smuggler roads. I'll tell you what I told myself as we climbed: the roads I knew were made by men running from other men, all elbows and shortcuts and spite. These were made by patience. The walls were veined with quartz that caught Wren's glow and scattered it into constellations. Stairways rose out of solid rock in spirals so long and smooth they might have been poured rather than carved, except no mason alive could pour stone like warm honey and set it in the dark.

And the stairs went *up*. Always up, but never in the punishing switchback way of human construction. They rose the way a tree rises — inevitable, unhurried, as if the mountain had all the time in the world and, being a mountain, actually did.

"How much farther?" I asked, somewhere past noon on the third day. Time went strange in there. My stomach kept count better than my head — it had always been my most reliable instrument, trained over nine years to register everything from arsenic to foxglove — and it told me we'd eaten twice since the crevasse and should have eaten three times.

*"Close,"* Wren said. *"The Spire is close. But close is not the same as soon. Close is a direction. Soon is a promise. The mountain does not promise."*

"Wonderful. A landmark with a lawyer's vocabulary."

She laughed — a bright, startling sound in all that stone — and then pressed both hands to the wall and pulled me forward until my palm touched the rock too.

The mountain showed me things.

I can't explain it better than that. There was a moment where my hand was on the stone and the stone was in my memory, and I saw the passages as they had been when the Thornkings still ruled — full of light and people, guardians walking these stairs with their hands glowing just as Wren's did, singing lullabies my grandmother used to hum when she thought no one was listening. Songs about a spire that watched. About names kept safe in a vault. About a child cut from the whole, hidden away in bloodlines, passed down like an heirloom nobody knew they owned.

Passed down to me.

"She was a guardian," I said aloud, and my voice cracked on it. "My grandmother. That's why I understand you. That's why the wine never killed me. It was never luck. It was never skill. They built me for this."

Wren took my hand off the wall and held it in both of hers, which was absurd, since her hands barely wrapped around two of my fingers.

*"They built you,"* she agreed. *"But building is not choosing. You chose every day since. That part is yours. No one built that."*

I'm a grown man. A former king's taster. I've swallowed things that would drop a warhorse and smiled through court dinners while doing it. And I stood in a glowing stairway inside a breathing mountain and had to turn away and pretend to examine a wall for a while because a child smaller than a quiver had said the only kind thing anyone had said to me in nine years.

The wall didn't need examining. But a man needs his dignity, such as it is.

* * *

That night — if it was night; the passages kept their own hours, dimming and warming on some schedule of their own — we camped in a hollow that felt less like a cave and more like a cupped hand. Wren slept against my side, the way she had every night since Bracken Hollow, and I sat up watching her, because I am apparently incapable of sleeping when there is grief available to do instead.

She was fading. That was the truth I kept not looking at. Her hair, once the brown of wet pine bark, had gone pale gold at the ends, translucent, like amber held to sunlight. When she slept, the edges of her blurred slightly, as though she couldn't quite remember to be solid when no one needed her. In the morning she'd sharpen again, blinking herself back into flesh with a little frown of concentration.

Each day it took her longer to frown herself back.

I thought about Maren, captured on the high ridge so we could run. I thought about Vessa holding the line with her smugglers and her knives and her stolen family name. I thought about Aldric, who had maybe hours left after this, spending them abdicating in the snow like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't his spine. Everyone paying something. That seemed to be the toll on this road: everything costs, and the price is always paid by someone else while you watch.

And Wren's price —

Well. Wren's price was the mission succeeding.

There's a particular cruelty in that arrangement. If we failed, she stayed with me, diminished, hunted, a fragment forever, a feral child speaking a dead tongue in a kingdom that would never make room for her. If we succeeded, she became the mountain again, vast and whole and eternal, and I got exactly one farewell out of the deal.

Some choice. Heads, I lose her slowly. Tails, all at once.

She stirred against my side and murmured something in the old tongue, and I understood it without trying, which frightened me more than any of it. The language was settling into me the way her light settled into the walls. Grandmother's lullabies unlocking, door by door. By the time this was done, I suspected I'd be fluent in a tongue no other living person spoke, which is a lonely kind of inheritance — like being the last customer of a shop that sells nothing you need.

* * *

On the fifth day, we reached the throat.

There's no other word for it. The passage narrowed to a slot barely wide enough for Wren, and for a horrible moment I thought the mountain meant to keep me out — take the girl, refuse the man, fair enough, she's the fragment, you're just the delivery boy. But Wren put her palms flat on either side and spoke a long phrase that ended in my name — my real name, the one struck from the rolls, the one only she and a dead king and a traitor spymaster now knew — and the stone sighed and opened like a fist unclenching.

Beyond it, the passage widened into a gallery that stole the breath right out of me, and I am not a man who gives his breath away cheaply.

Carvings covered every surface, floor to unseen ceiling: three hundred years of guardians, each one rendered walking up these stairs toward a spire rendered at the far end, and below each figure, a name in the old script. Hundreds of names. Generations of them. I ran my fingers along the grooves and found names I recognized from history books — and names I recognized from nowhere at all, except that one of them, near the end of the newest row, half-finished, chisel-marks still sharp, was mine.

Gideon Blackwood. Cut into the living rock of a mountain I'd learned existed three weeks ago.

"They knew," I said. "Whoever carved this — they knew I was coming."

*"The mountain knows,"* Wren said simply. *"It has been waiting a long time. Waiting makes it patient. Patience makes it certain."*

I stood there a while, reading names. Somewhere in the middle rows I found my grandmother's, and next to it a symbol I now understood to mean *sworn*, and beside that another meaning *paid*, and I decided I didn't want to know yet which applied to which. The vault would tell me tomorrow. Let me have tonight.

Wren climbed into my arms — she was getting lighter, that was another thing I wasn't looking at directly, she weighed less every day, as though the mountain were already reabsorbing her by inches — and pointed up the gallery.

*"One more day,"* she said. Then, after a pause, in our tongue, carefully, the way you handle something breakable: *"One more day, Gideon."*

She'd started using my name more. I pretended not to notice why. We're both good at pretending; it runs in the bloodline, apparently, along with the immunity.

"One more day," I said, and set her down, and we climbed.

* * *

The last stretch, we walked in full daylight — mountain daylight, gold-white and sourceless, pouring down a shaft so far above us it might have opened into heaven or just into weather. The air tasted clean in a way that made my mouth ache. After a lifetime of palace kitchens and cellar corridors and the copper tang of other people's poison, clean air felt like an accusation.

Wren stopped walking.

She stood very still in the middle of the path, arms slightly out from her body, and I watched the light pass through her — actually through her, casting her shadow on the far wall in colors shadows aren't supposed to come in. For a heartbeat she was almost entirely transparent, a child-shaped window onto the stone behind her, and I felt something in my chest go tight and cold.

Then she breathed, and was solid again, and turned to me with tears running down her face.

"It hurts," she said — plain speech, no old tongue, and her voice sounded young in a way I hadn't heard since the cottage door. "Going back. Being whole again. It hurts like — like a foot waking up. Like all the years asleep coming back at once."

I knelt. It seemed like the thing to do.

"You can stop," I said. "Right here. We walk back down, we go to Bracken Hollow, I teach you cards and you cheat at them, and the Spires can rot in whatever they're rotting in. Nobody would blame you. Least of all me."

She looked at me for a long time. Then she reached out and pressed her small hand flat against my chest, over the heart, the way she'd done the first night at my door, when I'd opened it expecting royal guards and found instead the beginning of everything.

"You waited," she said. "Three hundred years, my piece waited in your grandmother, and her mother, and theirs. Everyone waited. So the mountain would not have to wait alone." Her hand curled into a fist in my shirt. "I am not stopping. But I wanted — I wanted someone to offer."

"I'll offer every day if you want."

"I know," she said. "That's why I can go."

And that, friends and strangers, is the entire problem with loving anything: eventually it learns exactly which sentence will undo you, and uses it.

I picked her up, because her legs had gone shaky, and I carried her the rest of that day's climb on my shoulders, and she sang — the lullabies, all of them, the forbidden songs my grandmother died humming — and the mountain carried the melody upward ahead of us, from ledge to ledge, so that it arrived everywhere we were going before we did.

* * *

We made the final approach at what I'll call dawn, because the shaft-light had gone pink and the mountain's slow breathing had quickened, excited, an animal scenting home. Around one last bend of living stone, the passage opened without warning into a cavern the size of a cathedral's ambition, and there it stood.

The Last Spire.

Not a tower built by hands — a tower *grown*, a single seamless pillar of pale stone rising out of the cavern floor and vanishing into a shaft of open sky far overhead, its surface moving very slowly, like ice on a river seen from a bridge, like something alive thinking long thoughts. Light pooled at its base. The air rang faintly, a note below hearing, felt in the teeth.

At its foot, a door stood open. It had been waiting open for three hundred years.

And standing in that doorway, framed against the Spire's inner light, was a figure in travel-stained black with grey at his temples, leaning on a staff, breathing hard — a man who had no right to be there, who I had last seen kneeling in the dirt at the crypt mouth confessing thirty years of service to the things I meant to kill.

Corvin raised his head. He looked terrible. He looked like a man who had climbed five days through a breathing mountain on guilt and stubbornness alone, which, knowing him, was precisely what he'd done.

"Gideon," he said. His voice was wrecked. Behind him, deeper in the light, something in the Spire pulsed once, slow and enormous, like a heart turning over in its sleep. "You need to hear what's at the top before you take another step. Because I've seen it. And it isn't what the mountain told you."

Wren's grip tightened on my shoulder.

Her whisper came in the old tongue, and for the first time since the cottage door, I wished I couldn't translate it:

*"He is lying. But he believes it. That is worse."*
