## 18. The Vault of Names

The Last Spire did not want us inside. I could tell the way you can tell when a dog doesn't want you crossing its yard — not by anything it does, but by the quality of the silence, the way the air holds still and watches.

We had climbed since before dawn through passages that were not carved so much as grown, the mountain's ribs curling around us in ribbons of black stone veined with something that caught Wren's light and held it a half-second too long, like a memory deciding whether to keep it. Wren walked ahead of me with her hand flat against the rock, and where she touched it, the stone glowed faintly gold, then dimmed again behind her, the way a face dims after a stranger passes.

She was thinner than when I'd pulled her out of the snow at my door. More *thin*, if that makes sense — like a figure drawn in charcoal that someone was slowly erasing from the edges in. Her hair had gone the color of morning fog. When she spoke now, her voice came from slightly to the left of where she stood.

I'd stopped finding this alarming somewhere around day nine. That's what the roads do to you. They recalibrate your sense of alarming.

"The door knows my song," she said, in the old tongue, and then again in ours, because she'd taken to translating for me without being asked, which was either kindness or pity, and I'd decided not to examine which.

Behind us, Vessa's smugglers made camp at the mouth of the living passage — six of them left of the twelve who'd held the ridge, and every one of them wearing the particular quiet of people doing arithmetic with their own lives. Vessa herself climbed with us, because of course she did. She hadn't come this far to stand outside a vault her family had bled for three hundred years ago.

Corvin came too. He walked ten paces back, hands visible, saying nothing, an old man carrying thirty years of treason like a stone in his chest. I hadn't forgiven him. I wasn't sure I ever would. But there is a difference between forgiveness and usefulness, and Corvin knew the Spire's outer wards better than anyone breathing, having helped design them when he was young and stupid and certain he was saving the world.

The door, when we reached it, was not a door. It was a seam in the mountain, floor to ceiling, hairline-fine, and across it ran lines of script in the old tongue — thousands of characters, dense as frost on glass.

Wren pressed both palms to the seam and began to sing.

I won't pretend I understood all of it. I understood the shape of it: a name being offered, and then another, and then a question. The mountain asked, in the voice of stone shifting against stone, *who comes to be counted?*

"Vessa Marrow," Vessa said, stepping forward, and for once in the entire gods-cursed journey her voice didn't have any iron in it. Just a woman's voice, tired and wanting. "And her mother before her, and hers before hers. Three generations stolen."

*Counted,* said the mountain.

The seam opened.

* * *

The vault was round, and cold in the way of places that have never known a fire, and lit from nowhere — the light simply existed, sourceless, the kind of illumination that makes everything look honest. Shelves of dark stone rose from floor to the vanishing point overhead, and on every shelf stood tablets. Slate, granite, obsidian, materials I couldn't name. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands.

Each tablet bore a single name, cut deep and filled with gold.

"By the salt," Vessa breathed. She walked the aisles like a woman walking a graveyard she'd only heard about in stories, trailing her fingers an inch above the names without touching them. "They kept them. All these years, they *kept them.*"

"What are they?" I asked, though some part of me already knew, the same part of me that had half-understood Wren's tongue from the first word she spoke at my door.

"Names," Wren said softly. "Taken names. The Spires cannot hold a thing unless it is given freely or written down. So they wrote down everyone they bound. Guardians. Wardens. The people who fed the binding." She looked up at me, and her eyes were almost transparent now; I could see the shelf behind them, blurred, like looking at a candle through smoked glass. "Every king. Every guardian. Every hand that ever held the ritual steady."

Vessa found her family's tablet on the fourth aisle. I watched her stop like she'd hit a wall, watched her read the name cut into the slate — *Marrow* — and watched three hundred years of theft land on her shoulders all at once. The smuggler queen of Bracken Hollow, the woman who'd negotiated passage out of me with the warmth of a knife being sharpened, put her hand over the name and shook.

"They took our name," she said, "and made us forget we'd ever had one. Made us think we were nobody. And then—" she laughed, wet and furious— "then they wondered why we turned to smuggling."

"Take it," Wren said.

Vessa looked up sharply. "Take—"

"You can. It's yours. That's why the mountain opened." Wren gestured at the tablet. "The vault gives back to whoever the names belong to. That's the whole cruelty of it. The Spires needed the names written here to hold the bindings — but the bindings only hold while the names are theirs. Take yours back, and the debt your family carried ends."

"And the binding weakens?"

Wren smiled. It was not a happy smile. It was the smile of someone watching a rope fray exactly as planned. "Everything weakens. Come. We should not linger. And you—" she turned those fading eyes on me, and there was something in her expression I didn't like at all, something apologetic, "—you should look for your grandmother's name."

"I don't have a grandmother in this mountain."

"Gideon." She said my name the way Maren used to say *the wound is infected.* "You've been singing fragments of the old tongue your whole life without knowing what they meant. You tasted nightshade in wine sealed in a king's private cellar. You survived nine years of poisons that should have killed a man in nine months. You are not lucky, Gideon Blackwood. You are *built*. Look."

So I looked.

* * *

There is a specific kind of dread that arrives not as fear but as recognition — the moment a stranger's face resolves into your father's, or a lullaby you've hummed all your life turns out to be a funeral dirge. I walked the aisles of that vault feeling that dread arrive in slow installments, because the guardians' tablets were grouped by lineage, and the lineages were grouped by purpose, and one entire wall — a wall near the heart of the vault, close to where the air itself seemed thicker — was dedicated to a single purpose:

*Tasters.*

Not one name. Dozens. Generations of them, each tablet bearing a name and beneath it, smaller, the words I could now read because a feral child had spent a winter teaching me:

*Nourished in bitterness. Prepared from birth. Immunity compounded across bloodlines.*

I found her on the third row from the top.

**ELARA BLACKWOOD.**

Nourished in bitterness. Prepared from birth. Immunity compounded across four generations prior.

I stood in front of that tablet for a long time. Long enough that Vessa finished whatever quiet transaction she was conducting with her ancestors' stolen honor and came to find me. Long enough that Wren drifted over and stood beside me without touching me, because even she understood there are moments when touch is a door you shouldn't knock on.

"She used to sing to me," I said finally. My voice came out level. Nine years of tasting poison teaches you to keep your voice level no matter what's happening behind it. "Those forbidden lullabies you all keep going on about. I thought she was just an old woman who remembered odd songs. I thought—" I laughed, and it sounded wrong in that sourceless light. "Gods. I thought I'd *chosen* my life. Taster. It suited me. Good pay, good lies, nobody asks a taster questions. I told myself I was good at surviving because I was clever."

"You are clever," Wren said.

"I'm *engineered*." Four generations of compounded immunity. Someone had sat down — the Spires, the bound guardians, whoever wore the crown's shadow three centuries deep — and decided that a bloodline would be built like a lockpick, each generation dosed and hardened and married well, until the final product was a man who could drink anything and walk into the Spire alive. Me. A tool with a grandmother and a childhood. "She wasn't humming lullabies, was she? She was maintaining the dosage. Keeping the immunity current. Preparing me."

"Yes," Wren said, gently. "And she hated it. I remember her."

That got through. That got through the way nothing else could have. "You—"

"I was part of the mountain, Gideon. I was in the walls of the crypt when she came to bargain. She offered the Spires a trade: her grandson prepared, perfect, delivered to their threshold — in exchange for one unbound life. One child of Thornfell allowed to stay free." Wren's transparent eyes held mine. "You're looking at the life she bought. You were the price. I was the purchase."

The floor did not move. The mountain held perfectly still. But something in me tilted, quietly, the way a table tilts when one leg finally gives.

All of it. The cottage at the edge of the capital chosen because it sat above old Thornfell tunnels. The taster's post I'd been *lucky* to get. Corvin finding me, training me, shaping me — and how much of that was a mentor's care and how much was the Spires making sure their instrument arrived polished? Even the nightshade in the wine. Even the king drinking poison and living. A test. A summons. *Come, little key. Time to open the lock you were cast for.*

"I never agreed to any of this," I said. My voice was level. Inside, something was not.

"No," Wren agreed. "Neither did I. Neither did Aldric. Neither did Elara. That's rather the point of everything we're about to do." She took my hand then — her fingers cool and going cooler, barely more solid than the light — and drew me to the center of the vault, where a single pedestal stood apart from the shelves, empty except for a shallow depression shaped like a palm.

"This is where a guardian's name goes when the binding is made permanent," she said. "And this—" she touched the depression, "—is where it comes out when the binding breaks. One last thing you should know before we climb to the heart. Your name is not yet on a tablet, Gideon. But it has been *written*. In the ledger the Spires keep. They have been calling you by your true function for nine years. Not taster. Not Blackwood."

"What, then?"

"*Keeper-in-waiting.* The next guardian of the Last Spire. The replacement for the one who died before you were born — the gap in the circle they've been waiting to fill." She looked at me with terrible tenderness. "When we unbind tomorrow, the mountain will need a new keeper. Freely chosen. Blood freely given. It can't take you. But it can *ask*."

"Ask," I repeated. Around us, tens of thousands of names gleamed in their golden channels — every one of them asked, every one of them answered yes, every yes extracted from fear or duty or love or exhaustion, and every yes lasting centuries. "And if I say no?"

"Then you walk out of here free, and the binding fails anyway, because Wren returns to the whole regardless — but the Spire goes dark, and the old magic goes wild and unkept, and the mountain becomes just a mountain with a very dangerous basement." She paused. "Also you would probably die in the backlash, so there's that."

"There's always that."

Vessa appeared at my elbow, slate tablet tucked under her arm like a loaf of bread, her family's name reclaimed and her spine straighter than I'd ever seen it. She looked at me, then at Wren, then at the pedestal, and whatever smuggler's instinct let her price anything in ten seconds gave her the sum of the situation in about five.

"You're going to say yes," she said. Not a question.

"I haven't decided."

"You have. You decided nine years ago, you just thought it was a career." She clapped my shoulder — hard, the way she did everything, affection delivered as impact. "For what it's worth, taster: I've watched men choose freedom my whole life, and it's mostly cowardice wearing good boots. This isn't that."

High above us, muffled through leagues of living stone, came a sound I felt in my teeth before I heard it: a long, grinding pulse, rhythmic as a heartbeat, growing stronger.

Wren's head snapped up. The glow along her arms flared bright — and flickered.

"He's coming," she whispered. "The thing in the king's skin. It's climbing the mountain. It can feel the vault open. It can feel the names coming loose." Another pulse, closer, and somewhere far below us, Corvin — waiting at the passage mouth with his thirty years of sins — would be hearing it too, and knowing exactly what it was.

"How long?" I asked.

Wren looked at her own hands, which were now translucent enough that I could see the pedestal through them, and smiled the saddest smile I have ever seen on a child's face.

"We climb now. Or we never climb at all."
