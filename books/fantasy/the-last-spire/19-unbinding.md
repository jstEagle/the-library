## 19. Unbinding

The heart of the Last Spire was a room the size of a chapel, and it had been waiting for us longer than any chapel had waited for anything.

I knew this because the room told me. Not in words — Wren's tongue had words, and this was older than words — but in the way my grandmother's lullabies rose unbidden to the back of my throat, humming against my teeth like a struck tuning fork. The walls were black stone veined with silver, and the silver pulsed. Slowly. Patiently. Like something breathing in its sleep, or pretending to.

Nine years as the king's taster teaches you to read a room before you trust what's in it. This room I did not trust at all. But I trusted what we'd come to do, and after that, trust stops being a feeling and becomes a decision you make over and over until it holds.

Wren stood beside me, her small hand in mine, and she was singing.

Not the feral scraps of sound she'd given me that first morning at my door — mud-caked, wild-eyed, all teeth and terror. This was the whole song now, or near enough to whole. The Thornking tongue poured out of her in long silver threads, and where it touched the walls, the veins of metal brightened like embers being coaxed back to life. She was more light than girl by then. I could see the stone through her shoulders. Through her chest. Through the hand gripping mine, which was the only part of her still entirely solid, and I suspect she kept it that way on purpose.

For me. Everything she'd done since the crypt had been for me, in the way children do things for adults without ever calling it sacrifice, because they don't know the word yet, or they know it better than we do.

"She says the parasite is coming up the mountain," I said aloud, though no one needed telling. Vessa's people had bought us the night with their blood — Maren taken, Vessa herself bleeding from a spear-graze she refused to acknowledge — and bought us the morning too, sealing doors behind them that would take the royal guard hours to reopen. Hours we'd spent climbing. The thing wearing King Aldric had spent them climbing faster.

"I know," said the voice from the doorway behind us. "It has always known the way home."

* * *

He came through the far arch without guards, without retinue, without ceremony, which was somehow worse than all three. Just a man in a king's body walking with a king's stride into a room built before kings existed.

But it wasn't Aldric's walk anymore. I'd watched the man eat breakfast for nine years — watched him across the tasting table every morning of my life since Corvin put me in that chair and told me my life was now the kingdom's most expendable instrument. I knew how Aldric held his shoulders when he was amused, how his left hand drifted toward his wine cup out of habit rather than thirst. The thing wearing him walked like weather moves. Like pressure fronts. It used Aldric's face the way I use a spoon: competently, without affection.

"Guardian," it said. My name in its mouth sounded like a coin dropped down a well. "You brought the fragment home. That saves me the trouble of fetching her."

"It has a name," I said.

"It has a function."

"So did I, once." I stepped in front of Wren, which was absurd — she was made of the same mountain this thing had been leeching off for three centuries, and I was made of poisons and stubbornness — but some gestures aren't strategy. They're just true. "Her name is Wren."

The parasite considered me with Aldric's eyes, and for one flickering half-second, the eyes were wrong in the other direction — too human, too frightened, too *present*. Aldric, surfacing. Screaming soundlessly behind glass, the way he had in the crypt letter, the way he must have screamed for thirty years while the thing drove his hands through wars and weddings and executions.

Then the shutter fell again, and the cold voice resumed.

"You are the last of the engineered line," it said. "Do you know what your immunity is? It is not tolerance. It is *capacity*. A vessel, prepared across four generations, refined with each poisoning, each swallowed dose, each year of service. You were not trained to taste for kings, Gideon Blackwood. You were aged. Like wine."

I thought about arguing. About the speech I'd been composing since Bracken Hollow, all the righteous fury I'd stockpiled against the crown, the Spires, Corvin, the whole rotting apparatus. And I found I didn't want it. Fury was a luxury for people who still believed the outcome was in doubt.

"No," I said. "I wasn't aged like wine. Wine gets drunk. I'm here to pour *you* out."

The parasite smiled with Aldric's mouth, and even that it did badly.

"The binding renews at solstice. The ritual requires a guardian's blood, freely given — your grandmother's generation understood this, and they gave freely, and their names are carved in the vault below, and they are dust, and I remain. You will give your blood, boy. You will kneel, because kneeling is what guardians do, and I will wear whatever comes next for another three hundred years. Bring me the fragment."

"Wren," I said. "Her name is—"

"—is nothing. A splinter. A grief the mountain never finished mourning. Give it here and be emptied, and I will let the old man die warm."

And that — that was the mistake. Not because it threatened me; threats slid off a taster like grease off slate. But because it offered mercy, casually, as a transaction, and somewhere beneath the parasite's borrowed composure I heard Aldric hear it too. Heard the man inside seize on those five words — *let the old man die warm* — the way a drowning man seizes a rope.

Aldric didn't want to live. He wanted to *end*, on his own terms, as himself. And the thing wearing him had just promised him exactly that, in exchange for my surrender, knowing full well it intended to keep wearing him until the body rotted.

The king's left hand twitched. Drifted, just slightly, toward his wine cup — except there was no cup, so it closed on empty air, and the fingers shaped themselves around something invisible, and I understood.

Nine years. Every poisoned chalice I'd flagged and swapped, every dose I'd taken into my own body so the reports would read clean, every immunity built dose by dose — the parasite thought it was describing a vessel.

It was describing a weapon.

* * *

"Wren." I knelt so we were eye to eye, and the mountain's light moved under her skin like fish under ice. "Tell me the rest of it. The part the song doesn't say out loud."

She sang. Low, halting, the old tongue doing what it does — saying several true things at once and letting you choose which one breaks you first.

The unbinding required a guardian's blood, freely given. True. What the song also said, what the vault carvings said if you read past the honorifics, was that the blood was not the price. It was the *key*. The guardian's body, saturated with three centuries of accumulated poison — every toxin the crown had ever fed its kings, catalogued in my veins like coins in a ledger — was the only substance on earth the Spires could not metabolize. Their whole dominion was built on binding, on hollowing, on wearing. Poison was the one language they'd never learned to speak, because speaking it meant dying.

My immunity wasn't capacity to survive poison. It was capacity to *hold* it — to carry enough death in my blood to write an ending no parasite could edit.

"They knew," I said. My grandmother's lullabies weren't forbidden because they were old. They were forbidden because they were *instructions*. "They bred a line of tasters. Fed us the crown's sins until our bodies became the antidote. And then they hid us in plain sight, waiting for one of us to reach the Spire with a full ledger."

Wren pressed her palm flat against my chest. Where she touched, I felt the poisons stir — not painfully, not yet, but awake, like dogs hearing their names.

"One question," I told her. "And answer honestly, because I'll know. When this is done — when you go back to being the whole — is there any of you left that remembers me?"

Her face did something terrible then. Something six years old and ancient at once. And she sang the answer, and I wished to God she hadn't.

*Everything returns. Nothing remains.*

The mountain takes back its magic, and what was split apart becomes seamless, and seamless things don't remember being pieces. There would be no child at a roadside inn years from now, no ordinary girl eating bread in Bracken Hollow. There would be a mountain, whole and healed, and a man who had carried a piece of it through winter and would carry the memory alone, because memory was the only part of her that couldn't be returned.

"Well," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "That's a rotten bargain."

She laughed — one note, silver, already half wind.

"But it's the one on the table," I said. "And I've never once in nine years sent back a dish without tasting it."

* * *

The parasite did not fight me. That was the horror of it, looking back — how little it understood what was happening until understanding was fatal. It watched me open the vein along my forearm with a knife Vessa had given me, watched my blood strike the black stone, and it thought: *guardian, kneeling, renewing the binding.* Three hundred years of guardians had knelt in this exact spot and bled in this exact pattern. It had attended every single ceremony. It had probably grown bored of them.

It stood there wearing its king, patient as tax season, while the mountain began to sing.

Because that's what happened. My blood hit the silver veins and the veins took it, ran it outward through the walls in branching rivers, and everywhere it went the stone lit — not the patient pulse from before but a blaze, white-gold, rising — and the song that came up through the floor was every lullaby my grandmother ever sang, braided into one voice, and underneath it, fainter, a child's voice keeping time.

Wren had stopped holding my hand. She didn't need to hold on anymore. She was spreading — thinning into the light like ink into water, arms first, then the dark bird-flicker of her hair, and she was smiling, and she was singing louder than I had ever heard anyone sing, and the last solid thing about her was her eyes, fixed on mine, saying without words what she'd already sung:

*Everything returns. Nothing remains. Remember anyway.*

"I will," I promised her. "That's the one clause I'm adding to the contract. Nobody asked me, but I'm a taster. Amending recipes is what I do."

The parasite finally moved.

I'll give it this — it was fast. It crossed the chamber with Aldric's body moving at speeds no king should command, hands out for my throat, and it got within arm's reach before the first vein of silver caught it. Then the second. Then all of them, the whole blazing lattice of the room closing around the thing like a fist around a wasp.

It screamed. Not with Aldric's voice — with its own, and its own voice was the sound a crown makes when it's pried off a dead head, metal and emptiness and three centuries of stolen sleep.

"You are *one* guardian," it howled. "One. We have worn twenty kings. We will wait. We will find—"

"Not one," I said. Blood sheeting down my arm, knees on the stone, grinning like a madman because the pain was arriving now, all nine years of it, every swallowed dose waking up and demanding its wages. "You're wearing the last one. Do the arithmetic, parasite. Your ledger closes tonight."

It fought. Of course it fought. It tore at the light with Aldric's nails and screamed through Aldric's mouth, and through all of it I could feel the man inside, and I did the only merciful thing left in my power.

I spoke to him. In the old tongue. Badly — my accent was atrocious, my grandmother would have boxed my ears — but I sang the lullaby, the real one, the one about snow falling on the high pasture where a boy once caught snowflakes on his tongue and thought the world was kind.

*The king remembers snow,* I sang. *Let him remember snow.*

The screaming changed pitch. The struggle changed shape. And for the final minute of three hundred years, the thing wearing Aldric lost its grip, and Aldric got his body back, and he used it to do the most kingly thing I ever saw him do.

He stopped fighting the light. He opened his arms. And he mouthed two words at me — no sound, the parasite had his throat, but I read his lips across the burning chamber as easily as reading a wine report.

*Thank you.*

Then the mountain took back everything, and the chamber went white, and I went down into the dark with nine years of poison leaving my blood all at once, taking the immunities with it, taking everything, taking—

* * *

I woke on cold stone with dawn-grey light coming through the broken crown of the Spire, and snow drifting down through it, slow and gentle, settling on my face.

Snow. On the solstice. Inside a mountain.

I lay there a long time, taking inventory. Arm: cut, clotted, throbbing. Body: wrung out, lighter, strangely quiet — the constant low hum of tolerated toxins I'd lived with so long I'd forgotten it was there was gone, and its absence felt like deafness. Immunity: gone. All of it. Nine years of armor, spent. From this morning forward, a spoiled oyster could kill me same as any assassin.

Name: struck from the rolls, per the terms Vessa and I had drafted in the pass, pending the regency's ratification. Free, allegedly.

Beside me lay an old man in royal clothes, breathing shallowly, eyes closed, and when I crawled to him I saw his face was slack and peaceful and *his own* — every line of it his own, for the first time in thirty years.

His eyes opened. Grey, tired, amused.

"Blackwood," he rasped. "You sing worse than you taste."

"My grandmother said the same."

"Is it—" He coughed, wet and rattling, and I heard the end in it, the way you hear the end in anything if you've listened to enough things end. "Is it done?"

"The mountain's whole. The Spires are ash. Twenty kings avenged, give or take."

"Good." He tried to sit up, failed, settled for turning his head to watch the snow fall through the shattered crown of the tower. "I remember snow," he said wonderingly. "I remember — there was a pasture. My father took me. I caught flakes on my tongue and he laughed. I haven't thought of that in thirty years. I couldn't. It kept taking it."

"I know," I said. "I sang it back to you."

"A taster." He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "The whole kingdom saved by the one man whose job was to swallow poison and say nothing. There's a lesson in that. Write it down. No — don't. Historians ruin everything." His hand found my sleeve, gripped with the last strength he had. "Hours. Maybe less. Get the physician. Get Vessa. There are things to sign, a kingdom to point at someone competent, and I intend to spend my remaining time doing it properly — abdication, succession, all of it witnessed and legal, because I have spent thirty years watching that *thing* govern illegally, and I will not leave chaos behind me like a bad smell."

"Rest first," I said.

"Rest is for men with years." His eyes drifted shut, then opened again, and there was something in them I hadn't seen since my first week at court, when he'd been briefly, genuinely, himself. "Blackwood. The child. The fragment."

My throat closed. "She went home."

"Did she?" And he smiled, faintly, with the ghost of mischief that thirty years of parasitism had not managed to kill. "The mountain is whole, yes. But whole things are made of parts, Blackwood, and the mountain was generous once. Ask it sometime. Ask it in the spring." He closed his eyes. "Now fetch me paper. A king has work to do, and for the first time in thirty years, the work is actually his."

I stood on legs that shook, in a tower full of snowfall, and outside, far below, the first light of solstice morning hit the valley — and somewhere down that valley, horns. Many horns. The royal guard, or what remained of it, or perhaps Vessa's people, or perhaps both, converging on the Spire where a king who was only a king for a few more hours waited with a kingdom to give away.

And beneath my feet, faint as a heartbeat, the mountain hummed a lullaby I almost recognized.

Almost.
