## 20. The King in the Snow

I remember snow.

That is the thing no one tells you about being eaten alive from the inside — the memories don't leave all at once. They go in pieces, like a house being stripped by thieves who take the silverware first and save the good paintings for last. For thirty years I have watched my own life carried out of me room by room: my mother's face went early, then my brother's laugh, then every lesson Corvin ever taught me, which I confess I did not mourn. But snow stayed. Snow stayed the way a nail stays in a wall after everything else has been taken down.

So when the thing wearing me finally loosened its grip — when Gideon Blackwood's blood hit whatever ancient machinery lives inside that Spire, and the parasite screamed through my mouth with a voice like a mountain grinding its teeth — the first thought that came back to me, whole and mine, was this:

*I know what snow tastes like. It tastes like the roof of the world, and I was eight years old, and my father was still my father.*

The second thought was considerably less poetic: *How many hours do I have left?*

Because I could feel it. Whatever the Spires had been feeding on for three centuries, they had been feeding on more than memory. They had been holding me together the way a puppeteer holds up a marionette — not out of kindness, but because slack strings are useless strings. Cut them, and what's left doesn't stand. It folds.

And I had been cut.

* * *

I woke on stone steps, in falling snow, with a dead god's echo still rattling around my skull and a young woman kneeling beside me with her hands pressed to my chest as if she could hold my ribs shut by willpower alone.

"Your Majesty—" she said, and then caught herself, because even now, even here, the title arrived before the person did. "Aldric. Can you hear me?"

"I can hear everything," I said, and my voice came out rusted, a hinge unused for decades. "It's very rude of it to have waited so long."

She blinked. Then she laughed — a wet, startled laugh, the kind people make when they've been crying and their body betrays them. Maren. The court physician. I knew her the way you know a piece of furniture: present, competent, part of the architecture. Except now I could see her properly, and what I saw was exhaustion carved so deep into her face she looked like one of the old Thornking carvings herself.

"You're dying," she said. No bedside manner at all. I decided immediately that I liked her.

"Yes," I said. "I gathered. It feels rather like the morning after a coronation feast, if the feast lasted thirty years."

"Hours," she said. "Not days. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." I tried to sit up and discovered that my body had opinions about ambition. She caught my shoulder and eased me back against the cold stone, and I let her, because there is a particular freedom in knowing exactly how much time you have left: none of it is worth spending on pride. "Is he dead? The thing. Is it dead?"

Maren's face did something complicated. "Gideon unbound it. Wren—" she stopped, and I saw grief pass over her like weather. "Wren gave herself back to the mountain. And the Spire took the rest. What's left of it is in there." She nodded toward the tower behind us, black against the grey sky, and I noticed — distantly, the way you notice anything when you're busy dying — that it had stopped glowing. Three centuries of that faint, hungry light on solstice nights, and now nothing. Just stone. Just an old, tired tower keeping watch over nothing at all.

"Then someone should write that down," I said. "In the histories. *The Last Spire stands empty.* Let some future fool-king read it and wonder why his predecessors never managed it."

"They'll wonder why you didn't either," Maren said quietly.

"Ah." I closed my eyes. "You know, then. You've seen the crypt."

"Gideon found your letter."

"My letter." A strange warmth moved through me that had nothing to do with my failing body. I had written it in the stolen hour — the one hour in thirty years when Aldric had been Aldric again, alone in the crypt beneath the chapel, scribbling by candlelight like a schoolboy while something vast and patient slept behind his eyes and dreamed his dreams for him. I had written it knowing I might never be free to act on any of it. It had been less a message than a prayer. Prayers aren't meant to be answered; that's what makes them bearable.

"He's alive," Maren said, following my thought the way good physicians follow symptoms. "Barely. His immunities burned out doing — whatever he did in there. He's feverish, half-conscious, cursing your name in between."

"Cursing my name?"

"He blames you," Maren said. "For the crown. For letting it happen. For thirty years of tasting poison on your behalf." Her mouth twisted. "He has a ledger, apparently. Your sins, itemized. He recited several to me while I stitched him up. You come off poorly."

"Well," I said. "Good. Someone should keep accounts. Gods know the crown never did." I looked down at myself — the royal riding clothes, filthy with ash and snow, the hands beneath them trembling like an old man's, though I suppose I qualified now. Thirty years old when they took me. Sixty-one today, if the calendar in my head could be trusted, and it couldn't, because whole decades of it were missing, hollowed out and worn smooth by something that fed on kings.

"How much do you remember?" Maren asked. It was a physician's question, gentle and clinical, and I appreciated that she asked it clinically, because if she'd asked it kindly I might have wept, and I had plans for the hours remaining, and none of them included weeping.

"Enough," I said. "I remember my father teaching me to ride. I remember Corvin telling me the binding would end the wars — I remember believing him, God help me, I was twenty and the border villages were burning and a strong crown sounded like salvation. I remember waking up sometimes. Little windows. An hour here, a day there. Enough to know something was wrong. Never enough to do anything about it." I flexed my fingers, watching the tremor. "Do you know the worst of it, Maren? The thing kept me *comfortable*. It let me hunt. It let me love my wife — or it let me believe I loved her, and I still can't tell the difference, and that may be the debt I can never repay. It wore me gently. Like a favorite coat."

"And now?"

"Now the coat is walking around without the man, and the man is walking around without a country, and we're both standing in the snow deciding what to do about it." I held out my hand. "Help me up. There is work, and I intend to die in the middle of it like a professional."

* * *

They say a king's abdication requires ceremony. Seals, witnesses, parchment, the whole liturgy of power — because power is a superstition, and superstitions die without ritual.

We had snow, a dying king, a captured physician, a smuggler queen with blood on her sleeves, and a taster's apprentice named Tam who had followed Vessa's crew up the ridge carrying nothing but a satchel of ink, a roll of the good vellum from Maren's medical kit, and the wide-eyed expression of a boy who had signed on for adventure and received history instead.

It would do.

Vessa stood apart, arms crossed, watching me with the flat appraisal of a woman pricing a horse. Behind her, her smugglers — what remained of them; two carried wounds that would scar, and one would carry a limp for life, and they had paid those prices for a man most of them had never met — stood in a loose crescent, snow gathering on their shoulders like a congregation.

"You understand," Vessa said, when I told her what I intended, "that the moment you sign that, the guard captains in the capital start choosing sides. Half of them will burn it as forgery. The other half will fight over who gets to 'protect' the regency."

"The guard captains," I said, "have been instruments of a parasite for longer than their grandfathers have been alive. With the parasite gone, they are simply men with swords and no orders. Men with swords and no orders will follow whoever feeds them first. Feed them, Vessa. You're good at logistics."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile keeps its boots. "You've been talking to Blackwood."

"We share a talent for cynicism," I said. "Though his is better earned."

I sat on a boulder — Maren arranged my cloak around me with brisk, unsentimental hands, and I loved her a little for not pretending I wasn't freezing — and I wrote.

Gods, how I wrote. My hand shook and I let it shake. I named Maren regent, because she was the only person in this entire catastrophe who had poisoned a king's wine, examined a feral child, defied a spymaster, crossed a blizzard, and *still kept her ledgers balanced*, and if that isn't the job description for ruling a kingdom I don't know what is. I dissolved the Office of the Taster — formally, with a line about how no person should ever again be bred and dosed into a human instrument, and I felt Gideon's absence like a bruise as I wrote it, because I knew he'd never read it and wouldn't care if he did. I ordered the crypt opened to scholars. I ordered the Thornkings' names restored to the rolls of the realm, all of them, the whole erased dynasty, because a kingdom built on a lie is just a parasite with better tailoring.

Tam took the vellum when I finished, blotted it carefully, and looked at me with the question boys ask when they haven't yet learned which questions are dangerous.

"Sire — Your Majesty — what happens to you?"

"What happens to me," I said, "is that I am going to sit here in the snow for a while, and then I am going to stop sitting here. Which is, when you think about it, the same thing that happens to everyone. I've merely been given a schedule."

"That's grim," said a new voice, hoarse and dry, from the edge of the circle.

Corvin.

My spymaster stood ten paces off, hatless, grey, looking older than the mountains behind him. Two smugglers flanked him with drawn steel and Vessa hadn't given the order, which meant he'd walked in unarmed and let them take it from him. He had knelt to Gideon at the crypt mouth — Maren told me, later, or maybe I dreamed it, the boundary was getting soft. He had served the Spires for thirty years. He had bound my father. He had bound me.

He had also taught me chess, and how to fall without breaking anything, and how to listen to a room.

"Old man," I said.

"Your Majesty." He started toward me and stopped, as if the distance itself were a judgment. "I came to—"

"To apologize?" I let him stand in the silence for a moment. Snow ticked down between us. "Don't. Apology is a transaction, Corvin, and I'm closing my books. Besides — you didn't bind me. You bound a frightened young idealist's ambitions, and then the thing you served wore the result for thirty years, and somewhere in there, the difference stopped mattering. I know exactly how that works." I smiled, and felt my own mouth make the shape, and it was the first smile in years that was entirely mine. "I had a front-row seat."

"Aldric." His voice cracked on the name. Not the title — the name. Nobody had used the name in decades. "I thought I was saving the world."

"I know," I said. "That's what makes it unforgivable. If you'd done it for greed, we could at least have hated you honestly." I held out my hand, palm up. "Give me your knife."

He stared. "Why?"

"Because a king should abdicate with a sword, and I don't have a sword, and I'm not going to make my regent wait while someone climbs back down for one. Give me the knife."

He gave it to me. His hand was shaking worse than mine.

I turned to Maren, who knelt and laid the abdication scroll across my knee, and I pressed my bleeding thumb to the seal-space — wax, ink, blood, the oldest signature there is — and spoke the words aloud because ceremony demands sound, even improvised ceremony, even ceremony conducted in a snowfield by a man with hours to live:

"I, Aldric, called the Third of that name, last vessel of the crown-line binding, surrender the throne of this realm. Not to an heir — I have none, and the line that produced me was never fit to produce anything but food. To a regent. To Maren, physician, who has seen what sits under crowns and governed anyway. Let her rule until the realm remembers how to choose its own. So ends the binding. So ends the lie."

The wind took the words and carried them off toward the Spire, and I would swear — old man's fancy, dying man's fancy, take your pick — that the mountain listened.

* * *

Afterward, they wanted to carry me down. I refused.

"It's four miles to shelter," Maren said, in the tone she probably used on patients who refused medicine. "You'll freeze before the second mile."

"Maren. Look at me." I spread my arms. "Look at what's left. The parasite held this body together for sixty years the way a splint holds a broken leg. The splint is off. Every mile downhill is a mile I spend unconscious instead of awake. I would rather have one hour of snow than six hours of blankets." I softened it, because she deserved softness — she'd earned the right to be lied to a little. "Let me have the hour. Then bring me down however you like."

She held my gaze for a long moment, and then she did the most kingly thing anyone had done in my presence in thirty years: she obeyed a command she disagreed with, because it was freely given. "One hour," she said. "Then I drag you down myself, and I'm not gentle."

"Wouldn't dream of asking you to be."

They withdrew — not far, a decent fiction of privacy, Vessa's people melting back toward the ridge trail with the efficient silence of professionals, Tam lingering until Maren shooed him, Corvin standing at the edge of things like a man waiting outside a sickroom, which, fair enough.

And then it was me and the snow.

I sat against the boulder and let it fall on me. Big slow flakes, the kind that mean the storm has spent its anger, coming down without hurry, as if the sky had all the time in the world. The Spire rose above me, dark and ordinary and done. Somewhere down the mountain, in a smuggler's cart or a warm bed already, Gideon Blackwood was burning with fever and hating me, and I found I could carry that. Hatred means the account is open. Closed accounts get indifference.

I thought about my wife. Elira. Dead eleven years, and I had attended her funeral from behind glass walls of someone else's attention, weeping on schedule, grieving at perhaps forty percent capacity, and I had known it even then — some buried Aldric pounding on the inside of his own skull, screaming *this is mine, this grief is mine, give me back my grief* — and the thing had patted him quiet and produced the correct tears.

Now the grief arrived all at once, undiluted, thirty years late, and it was the best gift I received that day.

"I'm sorry," I said to the snow, because she wasn't here, and the snow seemed like the sort of thing she'd have understood. Elira used to say the mountains were older than the gods and knew more, and everyone laughed at her, and she was right, and I should have told her so while I still owned my own mouth. "I'm sorry I wasn't there. Even when I was there, I wasn't there. You deserved a husband. You got a coat."

The snow fell. Somewhere below, a bird made a small complaint about the weather. My hands had gone numb past feeling, which was fine; they'd done their work.

I thought about the child. Wren. A fragment of the mountain's old magic, split apart three centuries ago by the first binding — split apart by *my inheritance*, by the machine my grandfather's grandfather's grandfather had climbed into willingly, believing Corvin's generation's beautiful lie. She had spent centuries as pieces. She had gone home today, back into the whole, dissolving into the mountain with Gideon's arm around her, and Maren says the Spire went dark, and I choose to believe that means she is everywhere now. In the snow. In the bird. In the numb tips of my fingers.

"Wren," I said aloud, testing the name. "If you can hear me — thank you. And I'm sorry. We were supposed to be your guardians. That was the entire arrangement. Guardianship in exchange for the crown's authority. Three hundred years of kings, and not one of us paid the fee." I laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing hurt in a way I welcomed, because pain was *information*, pain was *mine*, and after decades of borrowed sensation I intended to feel every last itemized inch of my own ending. "Consider the arrears settled. By one payment. From the last account holder. The bookkeeping is atrocious. Tell the mountain to hire a better clerk."

My hour was going. I could feel it going the way you feel daylight going in winter — not suddenly, but certainly.

One more thing. There is always one more thing; that is the trap and the glory of being a person.

"Corvin," I said, not loudly. He heard. He always heard. He came and stood over me, hatless in the snow, an old traitor with an old man's face, and waited.

"When they bury me," I said, "bury me as Aldric. No regnal number. No crown on the coffin. Put *taster's apprentice, failed* on the stone if you like — put *vessel* on it, put *the last one* — but no number. Numbers were theirs. The counting was theirs. I want the stone to say a man is under it."

"It will say Aldric," Corvin said. "Only Aldric."

"Thank you." I closed my eyes, then opened them, because I wasn't finished hoarding. "One more theft, old man. Sit with me. Not as my spymaster. As the man who taught me chess. Tell me about the moves. I can't see the board anymore, so describe it to me. Describe the board."

So he sat down in the snow beside his king, this ruined architect of everything, and he described the board: the white squares and the black, the knight bent mid-leap, the queen advanced too far and paying for it, the game long past saving and both players too stubborn to tip the king over.

And I listened, and the snow fell, and somewhere in the middle of describing a gambit he'd once beaten me with — the same one, every time; I never learned, he never let me learn — the cold stopped being cold.

I was eight years old. My father was teaching me to ride, and the snow was coming down on the training field in big slow flakes, and he was laughing at something I'd said, and I was laughing, and the mountain stood over us keeping watch, and everything was ours, everything was mine, everything —

Everything.

* * *

They brought him down at dusk, wrapped in a smuggler's oilcloth because no one had thought to bring a king's shroud up a mountain, and no one who was there ever thought that was wrong.

Maren rode ahead through the night with the abdication scroll sewn into her coat, and behind her the story traveled faster than any rider: the king is dead, the king is free, the king died a man. By the time she reached the capital, half the guard had already chosen — not her, at first, but the *idea* of her, the idea that orders could come from a living mind instead of a hollow crown. Vessa's people fed them. Vessa always understood logistics.

And in Bracken Hollow, weeks later, a fever-broken ex-taster woke in a roadside inn to the news, and asked who had been regent, and was told, and grunted, and asked what had been done with the body, and was told that too — *no number, only the name, the way he asked* — and turned his face to the wall and was silent a long time, and no one in the room was fool enough to call it grief.

On the stone, in the chapel yard where the Thornkings' names were being chiseled back into the rolls of the living, one marker stood apart, plain granite, no crown, no dates even, because Maren had ruled the dates were nobody's business but his:

**ALDRIC**

*A man.*

And on solstice nights thereafter, travelers on the high road swore the Last Spire glowed — faintly, briefly, the way a lamp glows in a window when someone inside is keeping watch — and that if you climbed far enough, you might meet an innkeeper coming down the path with a lantern, an ordinary man with an ordinary name, walking an ordinary vigil.

But that is another chapter, and the snow had not yet melted from the ridge where a king had finally, at the very last, tasted his own life — and found it sweet enough.
