## 21. The Taster Who Would Not Swallow

They offered me a title three times before I understood it wasn't a test.

The first time was in the regent's antechamber, two weeks after we came down from the mountain. Maren — Regent Maren now, which still made me want to check her wine for hemlock out of habit — sat behind Aldric's old writing desk because the throne room was being scrubbed of three centuries of parasite residue, both literal and otherwise. She slid a scroll across the desk toward me with the same expression she'd once used to tell me the king's broth had gone off.

"Baron Blackwood," she said. "Lands in the eastern valleys. A seat at court."

I looked at the scroll. It had a wax seal on it the size of my fist. Everything about it said permanence, and permanence, in my experience, was just a slower kind of trap.

"No," I said.

"Gideon—"

"You saw what happened to the last baron who ate at this court. Well. You didn't, because he's a smear in the crypt records, but you take my meaning."

Maren sighed the sigh of a woman who had spent thirty years around men who thought suspicion was a personality. "The second offer is better."

"The second offer is always better. That's how they get you."

The second offer came a week later: Lord Protector of the Royal Household, keeper of the king's table, master of tasters. Which was a joke so dark I almost laughed. The office of Royal Taster was being dissolved — there was a ceremony planned, some official burning of the tasting spoons, as if the spoons were ever the problem — and they wanted me to preside over whatever grew in its place.

"No," I said again.

And then the third time, Maren didn't dress it up at all. She came to the little guest room they'd given me in the palace — a room with too many doors, all of them watched, I'd checked — and she said, plainly, tiredly:

"They want to give you something, Gideon. Anything. Because you carried a child up a mountain through a blizzard and unmade a thing that has been eating kings since before anyone alive was born, and the whole kingdom knows it, and a kingdom that owes a debt it can't pay gets nervous."

"Then let them be nervous," I said. "Nervous kingdoms don't poison people."

"Nervous kingdoms do exactly that," she said. "It's the calm ones you have to watch."

I will give Maren this: she never lied to me. Even when the lie would have been kinder. Even when the truth was *I tested you for nine years and you passed every one without knowing you were being tested.* She'd confessed that on a mountainside with my blood soaking into the snow, and she hadn't taken it back since.

So I told her the truth back.

"Maren. My immunity is gone. You know that better than anyone — you spent a week pouring willow-bark and antidote down my throat while my body forgot every lesson it ever learned. I can't taste nightshade anymore. I can't tell arsenic from salt. If I sit at a royal table tomorrow and someone slips something bitter into the cup, I'll drink it like an idiot and die like one, and the kingdom will have traded its last line of defense for a barony with a nice view." I turned the scroll over in my hands, feeling its weight. Paper. Just paper. "You don't need me at court. What you need is for the story to end properly, with the hero getting his reward. Give the story to someone else. I'm taking mine home."

"And where is home?" she asked quietly.

I thought about it honestly, which surprised us both.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about surviving something impossible: afterward, your body doesn't believe it. For weeks after the Spire, I woke at odd hours convinced I could still hear the wind in Corpse Pass, or Wren's thin singing in a language only half-alive, or Corvin's voice saying *turn back, boy* with thirty years of regret packed under every word. My hands shook at meals. Not from withdrawal — Maren checked — but from the sheer strangeness of eating food no one had paid me to test first.

Nine years, I had eaten for the crown. Every bite measured, every mouthful a wager with death, my body a ledger where the crown's sins were written in small doses of everything that kills. And now the ledger was blank. The poisons I'd built my walls against had washed out of me with the fever, and what remained was just a man — a man who could die of a bad oyster like any other fool.

It should have terrified me. Some nights, it did.

But most nights it felt like setting down a load so heavy I'd forgotten my spine had a natural curve.

Wren, meanwhile, was learning to be ordinary, and doing it the way children do everything: completely, ferociously, and with an alarming lack of gratitude.

The mountain magic had gone back where it belonged when the unbinding finished — I still can't describe those minutes without my chest tightening, the way she stood at the heart of the Spire growing brighter and thinner at once, dissolving like sugar in warm water, smiling at me the whole while. I'd thought I was watching her die. I'd been ready to carry that.

Instead, three days later, she'd walked out of the snowmelt at the mountain's foot, solid as bread, brown-haired instead of silver-eyed, and asked me — in the common tongue, plain words, no lullaby-echo, nothing old in her mouth at all — whether I was going to eat that.

She remembered everything. That was the strange mercy and the strange cruelty of it. She remembered being a fragment of the mountain's magic, split apart three hundred years ago to bind the first Thornking's crown; she remembered the long sleep in the crypt-carvings and the longer crawl to my door; she remembered her own name, which was Wren before it was anything else. But the power was gone, poured back into the stone, and what remained was a girl of perhaps eight years with mud under her fingernails and an opinion about porridge.

"I liked it better when I knew things," she told me once, swinging her legs off a palace bench, watching the guards drill in the yard below. "Now I know things like everybody else. One at a time. Slowly. It's very annoying."

"That's called being a person," I said.

"I don't recommend it."

"You get used to it. Eventually."

"How eventually?"

"Working on year thirty-six. I'll let you know."

She grinned at me, and it was such an ordinary grin, gap-toothed and crooked, that something in my chest that had been clenched since the night she appeared at my door finally, grudgingly, unclenched.

* * *

Corvin came to see me the day before I left.

He looked terrible, which was a comfort. There's a particular satisfaction in seeing the man who betrayed you looking like a man who betrayed you — grey-faced, sleepless, his spymaster's neatness fraying at every edge. They hadn't hanged him. The Spires' records, recovered from the vault, showed his binding-oath in full, and Maren had argued — successfully, barely — that a man coerced by a parasite into thirty years of service deserved judgment rather than a rope. He'd been stripped of office, struck from the rolls, sentenced to live.

Which was, I suspected, the crueler sentence.

"I brought you something," he said, standing in the doorway of my guest room like a man visiting a grave. He held out a small book, leather-bound, worn soft at the corners. "Your grandmother's. From her effects, when we... when I took them, after she died. I kept it. I told myself it was for the archives."

I took it. Her lullabies were inside, in her cramped hand, the forbidden songs she'd hummed to me over the washbasin, the ones that had let me half-understand Wren's dead tongue before any scholar in the capital could. Songs of the mountain. Songs of keeping. Songs that said, if you read between the lines the way I could now, *a guardian will come, a guardian will come, born immune and unbowed.*

"She engineered me," I said. "My immunity. It wasn't luck. It wasn't the crown's training. It was her, feeding me tonics since I was a boy, preparing me. I found the records in the vault. Blackwood, listed among the bound guardians. My whole life was a preparation I never agreed to."

"Yes," Corvin said simply. There was no excuse in his face. I'd seen him try excuses in the pass, and they'd died in his mouth. This was what remained when the excuses finished dying: just the truth, bare and ugly. "As mine was. As everyone's is, Gideon. The only question anyone ever gets to answer is what they do once they find out."

I looked down at the book. My thumb moved over her handwriting.

"Why are you really here?"

"To say that when you refused the titles, I laughed. First time in years." His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "A man trained to swallow everything, refusing to swallow the one thing that would have been easy. It's the most honest act I've witnessed in this palace in three decades. Don't ruin it by staying."

* * *

We left on a grey morning in early spring, with frost still on the grass and the capital still arguing about itself behind us.

Maren met us at the west gate. No escort, no ceremony — she'd learned that much about me, at least. She pressed a purse into my hand, and when I tried to give it back she said, "It's not a reward, it's back wages. Nine years of a taster's salary that you never spent because tasters aren't allowed to own anything conspicuous. Consider it an accounting error corrected."

"That's a lot of accounting errors."

"The crown made a lot of errors." Her face went careful. "There's one more thing. Your name. When the rolls were purged — the taster registry, the household lists —"

"I know. Corvin told me. Struck from the rolls. Gideon Blackwood doesn't exist."

"It can be restored. One word from me, truly, and —"

"Maren." I put the purse away. "Do you know what I did for nine years? I ate poison so a hollow man could eat dinner. I watched everything and said nothing. I trusted no one, because trusting no one was the job. My name wasn't mine — it belonged to whoever needed a body to stand between a king and a cup. And now it belongs to nobody at all." I smiled, and meant it, which startled me. "That's not erasure. That's the first honest thing this kingdom has ever given me."

She studied me for a long moment, this woman who had poisoned a king's wine to see if the king would die, who had followed two fugitives into a blizzard out of guilt and science and something warmer than either, who now wore a regency like armor that didn't quite fit.

"Where will you go?" she asked. Same question as before. This time she actually wanted the answer.

"Bracken Hollow."

Her eyebrows rose. "Vessa's town."

"Vessa's town. She's owed me passage twice over since the ridge, and I owe her a debt I intend to inspect at close range. Besides—" I glanced down at Wren, who was crouched by the gatehouse wall teaching a stray cat that it did not want to be taught, "—she needs somewhere ordinary. Somewhere nobody whispers about markings and crypts and the mountain's memory. Bracken Hollow is many things, but nobody there has ever been ordinary enough to be impressed by anything."

Maren hugged me. It was brief and awkward and smelled of feverfew and ink, and I let it happen, which for a man of my profession counted as a public breakdown.

"Come visit," she said.

"Send a letter first. Unpoisoned."

"I make no promises about the ink."

* * *

The road to Bracken Hollow took six days, and they were the best six days of my adult life, which says less about the road than about the life.

We walked slowly. There was no reason not to. Nobody hunted us — the guard that had ambushed the high ridge had been recalled and mostly hanged, their officers having discovered that serving the Spires was no longer a career with advancement opportunities. Nobody needed us anywhere. The world, for once, had no instructions for us, and we wandered through it like coins slipped loose from a strongbox.

Wren talked the entire way. About the cat. About clouds. About why horses were suspicious and rivers were worse. About Vessa ("Is she going to make us work? Because I'm good at working but only at things I pick"). About the mountain, occasionally, in a smaller voice — she'd look back at the white peak on the horizon and go quiet, the way you go quiet passing a house you used to live in, and then she'd shake herself and start talking about horses again.

At night I cooked over a small fire, testing nothing, measuring nothing, seasoning food by taste alone like some reckless amateur, and every meal survived us both. Small miracle. I collected them anyway.

On the fourth night she asked, "Are you sad? About the titles?"

"A little," I admitted. Honesty had gotten easier since the mountain. Everything had gotten easier except sleeping past dawn. "A barony would have been warm in winter."

"But you'd have had to stay."

"Yes."

"And eat things other people gave you."

"...Yes."

"Then you're not sad," she decided, with the absolute authority of eight years old, and pulled the blanket over her head, conversation concluded.

I sat by the fire a while longer, turning my grandmother's book over in my hands. Somewhere in its pages, between the lullabies, I'd found a line I hadn't noticed as a child, scrawled in the margin in her hand: *If they offer him a cage, tell him cages can be gilded.* She'd known. She'd written to me across thirty years, and even her warnings sounded like songs.

Well, grandmother. No cage. Not even gilded.

* * *

Bracken Hollow smelled the same as it always had: woodsmoke, river-mud, frying onions, and the particular undertone of a town where every third building was technically a front. Vessa met us at the door of the Salt Cellar herself, arms crossed, looking us over like merchandise she was deciding whether to fence.

"You're thinner," she said.

"You're richer."

"Solvency looks good on me." Her eyes flicked to Wren, cataloguing her — the ordinary hair, the ordinary eyes, the total absence of silver light — and something in Vessa's hard face eased by a degree. "No more glowing, then?"

"Not even at solstice."

"Pity. Good for business." She stepped aside to let us in. Then, gruffly, in the tone of a woman granting a concession she'd deny under torture: "There's a room above the kitchen. Two beds. Rent's cheap for people who held a ridge against the royal guard."

"What's the catch?" I asked. With Vessa there was always a catch. It was practically her love language.

"The catch is I need a cook. My last one ran off with a tinker and my reputation for hospitality is bleeding out. You feed travelers well, they talk, travelers talk, my roads stay busy." She shrugged, elaborate indifference. "Unless the famous Lord-Who-Refused-A-Barony considers kitchen work beneath him."

I thought about nine years of swallowing. Of trays carried into rooms I couldn't leave, of meals that might be my last, of a career whose entire purpose was to stand between powerful people and consequences. And I thought about a kitchen where the worst hazard was hot fat, where every plate left the pass carrying exactly what it claimed, where a traveler could eat freely because the man cooking had sworn, silently, privately, forever, that nothing he served would ever hurt them.

A taster's oath, inverted. Feed, don't test. Nourish, don't guard.

"I'll need a good knife," I said.

"I'll need half your wages for rent," Vessa said, and stuck out her hand, and we shook on it, and that was the entire negotiation. The easiest deal either of us had ever made.

Wren tugged my sleeve. "Can I help in the kitchen?"

"If you wash your hands."

"Every time?"

"Especially every time."

She sighed like the weight of civilization had landed on her shoulders, and then she grinned, and ran upstairs to claim the bed by the window before I could object, and I stood in the doorway of the Salt Cellar with onion-smoke in my beard and a purse of back wages in my pocket and no name on any roll in the kingdom, and I realized I was happy.

Not the loud kind. Not the kind they write ballads about. The quiet kind — the kind that tastes like nothing at all, which, in my experience, is exactly how you know it's safe to swallow.

* * *

That first evening, I cooked for forty strangers.

Stew, bread, apples from Vessa's cellar, ale that I tasted first out of pure reflex and then caught myself doing and laughed until Wren asked what was wrong with me. Travelers ate and thanked me and asked for more, and nobody tested it, and nobody died, and nobody was saved from dying, and the food was just food, and I was just a cook.

Afterward, Wren fell asleep at the corner table waiting for me to finish, cheek on her arms, and I carried her up to the room above the kitchen. At the window, far off, the mountain stood black against the stars. On solstice nights I'd climb it — I already knew that, the way you know a promise you haven't spoken yet. The old vigil wanted keeping, and I was the last one who knew the way, and some inheritances you don't get to refuse even after you've refused everything else.

But that was months away. Tonight there was only the warm room, the sleeping child, the town murmuring below like a hearth fire, and a man with no title, no immunity, and no name on any roll in the kingdom, lying down without checking the locks for the first time in his life.

Outside, spring rain began to fall on Bracken Hollow, soft and steady, washing the winter off the world.

I slept like a man with nothing left to fear, and dreamed, for the first time in years, of absolutely nothing at all.
