## 22. The Last Spire Stands

The inn sits where the smuggler road meets the king's road, which is to say it sits nowhere in particular, and that is precisely why I built it there.

Travelers ask, sometimes, how a man comes to own an inn at the foot of a mountain everyone else avoids. They ask it the way people ask comfortable questions — expecting an answer that will let them stop thinking. *Bad luck, was it? Ran from debts? Lost a wife?* And I give them the answer I've polished over eight winters: *I got tired of other people's kitchens.* That usually satisfies them. People love the idea that a man might retire from ambition rather than be broken by it.

The truth is longer, and mostly belongs to other people, so I keep it in the cellar with the winter ale.

* * *

Her name is Wren now. Just Wren — no titles, no old tongue rolling off her like water over stones, none of the mountain's voice humming behind her teeth when she slept. She is eleven years old this winter, gap-toothed and loud, and she cheats magnificently at cards, and when she laughs she sounds like every child in Bracken Hollow and no child anywhere else.

She remembers almost nothing. Once a year, maybe, something surfaces — she'll stop mid-chore and stare at the mountain with an expression I can't name, or she'll hum three notes of a lullaby my grandmother used to sing and then lose it before the fourth. When that happens she gets quiet for an evening, and I let her be quiet, because I know better than anyone what it is to have a past that doesn't fit inside your body.

Vessa says I coddle her. Vessa says most things with a sneer, but she says this one softly, which is how I know she means it as a compliment.

She still comes down from Bracken Hollow twice a year, ostensibly to inspect the ledgers of a smuggling operation she retired from and never actually left. In practice she comes to eat my cooking, insult my ale, and check on the girl with the fierce, proprietary tenderness of a woman who held a pass against royal guardsmen and lost four friends doing it. She never names them. She just drinks one extra cup each visit, facing the ridge, and I pour it without being asked.

"You've gone soft," she told me last autumn, watching Wren chase chickens through the yard. "There was a time you'd have counted every coin in that till twice."

"I still count everything twice," I said. "I just don't count what matters."

She snorted into her cup. But she stayed three days, and she fixed my leaky roof herself, because Vessa has never once accepted help and has never once withheld it either.

* * *

Maren writes. Long letters, full of court gossip stripped of anything dangerous, medical treatises nobody asked for, and always, buried somewhere in the middle, a single line that tells me what I actually need to know: *The regency holds. The child-prince is kind. Nobody remembers your name.*

That last part was her gift, though it took me two years to understand it.

When Aldric died in the snow outside the Spire — a man again, for one hour, which was more than the Spires ever gave him in thirty years — Maren named herself regent before his body cooled, and dared anyone to object. Nobody objected. A woman who could poison a king's wine as a *test*, who could follow a fugitive through Corpse Pass in a blizzard out of guilt alone, is not a woman you contradict over paperwork.

They offered me titles afterward. Chancellor of the Interior, whatever that means. Warden of the Northern Reaches. A knighthood, gods preserve us all. I refused all of it, and Maren, to her eternal credit, understood without explanation. Instead she struck my name from every roll in the capital — the taster's registry, the guard rosters, the tax records, even the lists of the dead, which took some doing. Gideon Blackwood ceased to exist by royal decree, which is the only decree I've ever been grateful for.

The immunity went with it. That was the price of the unbinding, written into the ritual itself: the guardian's body gives up its armor along with its blood. Nine years of swallowing death in small doses, undone in a night. My first winter here I caught a fever from standing in rain too long and nearly died of it, which Vessa found hilarious and Wren, then small and serious, did not leave my bedside for three days.

I'm not immune anymore. I'm not a taster, not a guardian by blood, not Blackwood, not anything the old world made me.

I'm the man who feeds travelers.

It turns out that's enough.

* * *

The inn has one rule, and I enforce it with a cudgel if necessary, though in eight years I've only had to lift it once: **no one pays who cannot**. Merchants pay double and pretend not to notice. Refugees eat free and try to sneak coins into the flour barrel anyway, which I allow, because pride needs somewhere to go. Smugglers pay in goods and gossip. Soldiers of the new regency pay properly and salute me, not knowing they're saluting the man whose testimony ended their masters' masters.

Word travels, as word does. They call it the Free Table now, the inn, and there are songs about it — bad ones, mostly, sung by people who clearly ate here while drunk. They say the keeper was once a great man. I encourage this. It keeps the portions generous and the questions few.

But there's one thing the songs get right, because I make sure of it. On solstice nights — the longest night of the year, the night the binding would have become permanent, the night everything ended and began — the Last Spire glows.

Faintly. Like a coal under ash. Green-white, the color of the light that lived in Wren before we gave her back, visible only from the high meadow where the pilgrim path breaks off the old road. Most travelers never see it. The ones who do come down the mountain changed, quieter, kinder, and they don't know why.

They think it's holy.

It isn't holy. It's a door, holding.

* * *

Tonight is solstice. Wren is asleep — genuinely asleep, exhausted from helping me feed forty travelers who came down off the passes ahead of the snow, all of us packed into the common room like herrings, telling stories and burning through my entire winter stock of ale in one glorious, ruinous night.

I banked the fires. I hung the CLOSED FOR THE HOLY NIGHT sign, which is a lie; the sign exists so that no traveler disturbs the mountain, not so that I can rest. Then I took up the lantern and the staff and started up the pilgrim path, because some appointments you keep even when nobody's watching.

Especially then.

The climb takes two hours in deep snow. I'm fifty-one years old and my knees file formal complaints the whole way, but I know every switchback, every handhold, every place where the mountain's living passages breathe warm air up through the stone. Yes — living passages. The mountain is still alive. Less than it was, maybe, sleeping deeper than it did, but when I press my palm to certain rocks I can feel something slow and vast turning over beneath, like a dog dreaming. Wren used to feel it constantly. She says now she feels it only "when the soup is very good," which is either profound or nonsense, and I've stopped asking which.

The high meadow is silvered under a half-moon. And there it is — the Last Spire, black stone rising out of the shoulder of the mountain, wearing eight years of snow like a crown nobody put on it.

And glowing. Faintly. Faithfully.

I set the lantern down. I do what I always do. I press my bare hand flat against the Spire's base, cold enough to burn, and I say the words my grandmother sang, the words Wren taught me properly in the crypt beneath the chapel, the words that are less a spell than a promise:

*"What was split is whole. What was taken stands. Sleep, and I will watch."*

The glow brightens under my palm. Just slightly. Just enough.

Some nights I swear I can feel it lean into my hand, the way a horse leans into a groom it trusts. Some nights I get nothing but cold stone, and I tell myself that's fine too, that guardianship doesn't require reciprocity, that a wall doesn't thank you for holding it up. But tonight — tonight the stone is warm. Tonight the mountain knows me.

Eight years ago I gave my blood freely in that vault, and the ritual took it, and it should have killed me. It didn't, because the mountain decided otherwise, and I have spent eight years not asking why. Some inheritances you accept without reading the terms. This one especially.

Because here's what I learned in the vault, kneeling in the dark with my life running out between my fingers and Wren dissolving into green light around me, singing: guardianship was never meant to be a burden passed down like a debt. It was meant to be a gift given like bread. Freely. To whoever is hungry for it. My grandmother's name is carved in that vault among the bound guardians, and for three hundred years her family served a parasite because they were *made* to. I serve the mountain because I *choose* to, and the difference between those two things is the difference between a cage and a home.

I stay until the moon crosses the ridge. An hour, maybe two. My beard frosts. My knees give up complaining and settle into a sullen truce. Somewhere below, Wren sleeps in a house full of empty barrels and full hearts, and Maren rules a kingdom that no longer eats its children, and Vessa's people run free roads under a crown that forgot to look for them, and a king who was eaten alive for thirty years died standing up in the snow, a man.

It cost everything. Every ally cost something. Every truth cost more.

And I would do it again before I finished the sentence asking me to.

* * *

Coming down, I take the long way, through the living passage where warm air breathes up from the deep places. Old habit. The smuggler's shortcut that eats travelers, except it never eats me, and hasn't since the night the mountain learned my step.

That's when I see the light.

Not the Spire's glow — I know that light the way I know my own hands. This is torchlight, down in the valley, on the king's road, moving toward my inn. A party of riders, six or seven, traveling fast at midnight on the holiest night of the year, which no one does without orders.

I put out my lantern and stand in the dark, listening, an old taster's trick — you learn to read danger by what it carries. Horses blowing hard. Chain mail. Too much silence between the riders, the silence of men who were told exactly what to say and nothing else.

At their head, a rider dismounts in my yard and looks up at my windows with an expression I recognize across eight years and half a mile of darkness, because I wore that expression for nine of the worst years of my life.

It's Corvin's expression. Except Corvin is three years dead, buried under a cairn he asked for himself, and the man wearing his face tonight is younger, and broader, and carries a sealed letter heavy enough to bend him.

He knocks on my door. Three times. Pauses. Then — and this is the part that stops my heart, that sends me down through the living passages at a speed my knees will punish me for tomorrow — he speaks the words to the closed door, in a language no living scholar knows, in an accent that hasn't been heard in three centuries:

*"What was split is whole."*

Wren's voice. Wren's phrasing. From the mouth of a stranger in a royal cloak, standing in my yard on solstice night.

I come out of the dark with my staff raised and my heart going like a war drum, and the young man turns, and I see his face properly for the first time — young, scared, and marked. Marked, faint and green-white along his jaw, in patterns I last saw fading from a dying child's skin in a vault beneath the world.

"The mountain woke me," he says, in accented but fluent Common. "Three days ago. I don't know why me. I don't know what I am." His hands shake around the sealed letter. "But it gave me your name. It said—" He swallows. "It said the innkeeper would understand."

Outside, above us both, through the storm clouds, the Last Spire glows brighter than it has in eight years.

I look at the boy. I look at the mountain. I laugh — tired, terrified, and happier than I have any right to be.

"Come in out of the cold," I say. "You look hungry."

And I hold the door open, because that is the whole of the job, and I have finally learned to love it.
