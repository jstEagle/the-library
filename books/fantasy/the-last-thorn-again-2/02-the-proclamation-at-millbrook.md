## 2. The Proclamation at Millbrook

The mule knew before I did. She stopped dead at the tree line above Millbrook, ears flat, and no amount of swearing or salt-cake would move her. I'd learned to trust that kind of animal sense over ten years of running the Thornfell passes, so I left her hobbled among the firs and walked down alone.

I told myself it was the weather. A week of snowmelt and then a hard freeze had turned the high road to glass, and Millbrook sat in the valley mouth like a stone in a boot—the last town before the pass went truly wild, which meant it was where everyone stopped, drank, talked too loud, and remembered things they shouldn't. If my iron cargo was going to draw attention anywhere between here and Saltmere, it would be here.

But it wasn't the mule's sense of danger that made my palms sweat against the straps of my pack. It was the sound.

Voices carry strangely in cold air. From half a mile out, I could hear a man reading aloud in the flat, official cadence that meant parchment and seals and trouble, and underneath it, the particular silence of a crowd listening hard.

I came down through the alder stands instead of taking the road, and came out behind the tithe barn where I could see the square without being seen.

A crier stood on the mounting block outside the Waystone Inn, a witch-hunter's grey cloak snapping in the wind behind him. Not a full hunter—just a herald wearing their colors—but even the colors were enough to empty half the street of anyone with something to hide. He held a scroll and read from it without inflection, the way you'd read a grain tariff.

"—by order of the Crown Regent, in this season of the realm's need: all persons possessing the gift of swordspeech, known in law as oath-blade binding, are commanded to surrender themselves for service to the war effort. Such persons will be taken alive. Any person found to have harbored, concealed, aided, or failed to report a swordspeaker shall be hanged by the neck until dead. The property of such harborers reverts to the Crown. So it is written. So it is law."

He rolled the scroll with a snap. "The list follows," he said. "Names of the accused."

And he began to read them.

Millbrook's square held maybe sixty people, pressed close because curiosity is stronger than fear right up until the moment it isn't. I watched faces as the names came. An old woman crossed herself with two fingers, the old way, mountain way. A farrier looked at his boots. Someone near the front started quietly crying, and nobody moved toward her, because comfort is evidence now.

"Coram Ashgrove," the crier said.

My whole body went cold, colder than the wind off the pass could make it.

Old Coram. Who taught me to hold a blade like a question instead of an answer. Who sat me down when I was fourteen and furious and showed me what my voice could do to steel—and then made me swear, on the flat of his own sword, never to use it where the wrong ears could hear. *An oath unspoken is your own,* he used to say. *The moment you speak it, it belongs to whoever hears.* He'd been retired up the valley at Hollowmere for fifteen years, growing barley and pretending his hands didn't miss a sword the way other men's hands missed drink.

His name on the list. Which meant someone had talked, somewhere, about something—and once a name is on a crown list, the only thing that comes off it is blood.

"Jude Thornbury," the crier said.

The world narrowed to a point. I stood behind the tithe barn with forty pounds of smuggled iron on my back and heard my own name read into law, and I did not run, because running is movement and movement draws the eye. I stood very still and listened to the rest.

"Wren Thornbury."

*No.*

She was safe. That had been the whole architecture of our lives—she inside the walls, respectable, court-scribe Wren, ink-stained and invisible, and me outside, a ghost with a mule. Her name should never have touched a document. Unless she'd touched it herself. Unless she'd done something in those archives of hers, some small sabotage, some redirected page—

I couldn't know. That was the worst of it. Ten years of building silence around us like a wall, and one herald with a scroll had knocked it flat in under a minute.

The crowd was breaking up now, fast, people remembering errands. The herald stepped down and a man in good boots—merchant quality, not farm quality—bought him a cup at the Waystone's door. I filed that face away. Names got onto lists somehow, and lists got bought and sold like anything else.

* * *

I didn't go into the square. I went around it.

There's a version of me that wanted to walk straight to the inn, buy a cup, sit near the herald, listen for details—the size of the hunting company, which direction they'd ride first. That version of me is the one who spoke a stuck gate open three days ago on the pass with a scout watching, and look how well that decision aged. Three days. One careless word of power, and now my name and Coram's and Wren's were being nailed to doors across three valleys.

So I took the back way, past the tanner's reeking yards where nobody lingers, and knocked on a door I've knocked on for a decade, in a rhythm that means *friend, goods, no questions.*

Marta opened it a hand's width. Saw it was me. Opened it less.

"You're on the list," she said.

"Word travels."

"Word travels faster than you." She glanced past me at the alley, then pulled me inside anyway, because Marta has been selling my iron for nine years and hanging is bad for business too. Her kitchen smelled of tallow and dried thyme. Her boy wasn't home. Small mercies.

"The hunt rides tomorrow," she said, keeping her voice low and her back to the door. "Company out of Greywater Keep. Twelve riders, plus the herald. They'll work the valley bottom first—Hollowmere's on the second day, if they follow the list in order."

"They won't follow it in order. They'll take the easy names first. Old men."

"Coram Ashgrove isn't easy." She said it flatly, and then seemed to hear herself, and softened. "Jude. You can't go to him."

"I know."

"You can't send word ahead, either. Riders on the road get stopped. Every road out of this valley gets watched starting tonight."

"I know that too."

She put bread and hard cheese in front of me without asking, the way she does, and stood over me while I ate because sitting down would mean admitting how much time there was, and neither of us wanted to measure it.

"What will you do?" she asked.

I chewed. I thought about the gate on the pass, the word I'd spoken after years of swallowing every syllable, and how the iron had sung in answer like a dog hearing its name. I thought about Coram teaching me that oaths spoken aloud stop being yours—that the whole art of his teaching, the whole gift of it, was restraint. And I thought about Wren's name on that scroll, and the fact that I couldn't ask her why without crossing half the mountains myself.

Ten years I'd hidden. Ten years of being a ghost, and the crown had still found my shape. Hiding was over. That part was almost a relief—I'll admit that here, in the privacy of my own skull, where it costs nothing. What remained was choosing what came next on purpose instead of by accident.

"I'm going to Hollowmere tonight," I said. "Over the top, not by the road. And then I'm bringing him out over the pass before that company reaches his gate."

Marta set down the jug she'd been holding harder than she needed to. "The pass is freezing shut. Every drover in the valley says it."

"Every drover in the valley says a lot of things they learned from each other."

"And if the ice takes you?"

"Then the ice gets there before the hangman does, and either way Coram Ashgrove dies free." I wiped my knife and put it away. "Sell my iron. All of it, today, cheap. Don't keep anything with my mark on it."

"That iron's worth—"

"It's worth exactly as much as it costs to never have existed. Sell it."

She argued for another minute, because Marta argues the way other people pray, and then she stopped mid-word and just looked at me, and whatever she saw made her nod once and turn to her ledger.

"One more thing," she said, not looking up. "The herald bought his wine from a man named Doss. Doss has been asking after you all autumn. Asking after the Thornbury girl, too—what she does at court, whether she visits."

Doss. I'd sold him iron twice. He'd always paid fair and laughed easily and asked harmless questions, and I'd liked him, which is exactly the kind of mistake that puts your sister's name on a scroll.

"Anything else?" I said.

"He asked if you still kept your father's sword."

The kitchen went very quiet. My father's sword hung wrapped in oilcloth in a cache above the pass, three miles from where I stood. I hadn't spoken so much as a greeting to it in ten years. But Doss had asked. Which meant someone had told Doss to ask, and someone had told them what to ask for, and somewhere behind all of it sat the understanding that a sword is not just a sword when a Thornbury owns one.

"No," I said. "Tell him whatever keeps you safe. After tomorrow, tell him I ran east."

"East is Greywater Keep."

"East is where they'll least expect a man to run toward."

She almost smiled. Then she pushed a sack of provisions across the table and said, "Go out the back. And Jude—" she caught my sleeve, and her hand was shaking, and I pretended not to notice, because kindness survives better unexamined. "Come back through here someday when there's no list."

"Someday," I said, which is the kind of oath you can speak safely, because it belongs to no one but me.

* * *

I climbed out of Millbrook by the goat tracks, moving fast while the light lasted. Behind me the town smoked gently into the dusk, one lamp lit early in a window I knew was Marta's, and beyond it the dark shoulders of the valley where Hollowmere lay, where an old man was probably eating supper and sharpening a scythe blade and knowing—he had to be knowing—that his name was on a list, and planning something stubborn about it.

Above the tree line the wind came off the peaks with teeth in it. The pass would seal within the fortnight; the drovers were right about that. Which meant whatever I was going to do for Coram, I had a window measured in days, and twelve riders with grey cloaks were riding the valley floor beneath me with the same window.

Somewhere south of here, Wren was bending over her ledgers with her name freshly inked onto a crown edict, and I couldn't reach her, couldn't warn her, couldn't ask her what she'd done—and the not-knowing sat in my chest like swallowed river stone.

I adjusted the pack straps and started up. First Coram. Over the ice, tonight, before the hunters reached his gate.

Whatever came after that, the passes would decide.
