## 3. What Wren Sold

The orders came up from the capital in triplicate, sealed with the crown's black wax and the witch-finder's personal sigil—a thorned rose, which Wren had always thought a stupid choice of emblem for men who burned people. She copied them out fair, as she copied everything: *By decree of His Majesty, all practitioners of swordspeech to be taken alive for the war effort. Harborers of same to be hanged from their own doorposts. Reward offered for names.*

Her quill did not shake. Ten years as a court scribe had taught her that much—her hands were stone when they needed to be. It was everything below the hands that shook.

She read the name list twice. Master Aldous Crane, Thornfell. *Taken alive* had been struck through and rewritten in the witch-finder's own hand, small and precise: *or dead.*

Wren set down her quill.

The scriptorium was warm, at least. That was the joke of it, the ugly little joke she'd built her life on: she'd sold her soul to buy her brother's silence, and what it bought him was ten years, and what it bought her was warm rooms and steady work and the slow rot of knowing exactly what she was. The other scribes called her reliable. Diligent. *Wren Thornbury, who never misses an error.* They said it like praise. She heard it like a sentence being served.

She looked at the third copy—the one bound for Captain Vess's company on the high road—and thought about ink.

Ink was her weapon now. It was the only one she'd ever been given. A smudged digit here, a transposed date there, a route order sent to the wrong garrison. Small treasons, deniable ones, the kind a tired scribe makes by accident. She'd been making them for years without letting herself call them treason. Tonight she would let herself call them something else entirely, because tonight the list had a name on it she knew better than her own handwriting.

Not Jude's name. Not yet. But Aldous Crane had taught them both letters, and oaths, and which of those could kill you.

Wren blotted the page, squared the copies, and began to plan a mistake.

* * *

The mistake took her until midnight. By then the scriptorium had emptied, the candles guttering in their brackets, and she sat alone with the fair copy of Vess's marching orders and a knife for sharpening quills that would do for other things if it came to that.

It wouldn't come to that. She was a scribe. Scribes were beneath suspicion the way furniture was beneath suspicion. That was the whole architecture of her survival: be furniture, be paper, be nothing.

But she couldn't stop looking at the second document, the one that wasn't for copying. It had arrived separately, under the witch-finder's private seal, and it contained a single paragraph that made her blood move wrong:

*Interrogation of the scout Harrow confirms visual identification of a swordspeaker operating the salt pass above Millbrook. Subject male, young, unremarkable. Cross-reference against census rolls of Thornfell villages. Priority: immediate.*

Harrow. She knew the name from supply manifests—a lean, quiet man who drank his wages and kept to himself. He'd been on the pass roads for a month. And he had seen someone speak to a gate.

There were perhaps four hundred men in the Thornfell villages who fit *male, young, unremarkable*. There was exactly one who had spent the last decade smuggling iron through the salt pass and who had sworn—he'd sworn it to her face, laughing, the last time she saw him—that he'd sooner freeze than use the gift again.

*Sooner freeze.* Wren pressed her thumb against the desk until the nail went white. Jude. You absolute, stubborn, loving fool. What did you open that gate for?

Someone, probably. Someone stuck, or freezing, or dying. That was the whole shape of him. Their mother used to say Jude had been born with the door already open—every stray dog, every beggar child, every broken thing came inside. And the gift had grown in him the way ivy grows in a wall: not because he wanted it but because there was nowhere else for it to go.

Which meant the hunt would find him. Which meant Wren had maybe two weeks before the census rolls crossed the witch-finder's desk and one name surfaced out of four hundred like a body out of deep water.

Unless the rolls went astray.

She looked at the paragraph again. Then she did the thing she'd practiced in her head for ten years without ever quite believing she'd have to do it: she lifted the fair copy, held it over the candle flame, and watched the witch-finder's orders curl into black lace and ash.

It felt nothing like freedom. It felt like stepping off a ledge she'd been standing on since she was fourteen years old.

Because here was the truth under all of it, the truth she had carried so long it had worn a groove in her: none of this started tonight. None of it even started with Harrow or the gate or the salt pass. It started ten years ago, in a cellar, with a door that opened onto somewhere that was not a cellar.

* * *

They'd been poor the way mountain families are poor—not hungry-poor but thin-poor, proud-poor, one bad winter from ruin. And the winter Wren was fourteen was the bad one.

Their father had died in the autumn. The tithe-collectors came after, and then the debt-men, and by midwinter the house was mortgaged twice and the tax assessor had found the ledger discrepancy that meant fines, and the fines meant the house itself. Wren remembered sitting on the cold hearthstone doing arithmetic that refused to come out survivable.

And she remembered Jude, sixteen, standing in the yard with his hand on the old boundary sword—the Crane blade their mentor had left with them for safekeeping—talking to it. Not idly. *Begging* it. Because Jude had discovered that year that the blades answered him, that when he spoke to steel in the old oath-forms the steel listened the way dogs listen, and because he was Jude he'd decided this was a shameful thing, a dangerous thing, a thing to hide even from himself.

"Say the word," Wren told him through the window glass, breath fogging it. "One word and the blade collects debts for us. One word and we keep the house."

"And then what?" He hadn't turned around. "Then some lord hears about the boy who talks to swords and I'm a weapon, Wren. Or a trophy. Or a head on a spike. You've seen what happens to gifted ones down-country." He finally looked at her, and his eyes were old in a young face. "I'd rather lose the house."

"You'd rather lose *everything*."

"I'd rather lose it than become what they'd make me."

So she'd gone looking for another answer. That was the part she never told anyone, the part that still made her flinch in chapel: it hadn't been desperation alone. It had been pride. Fourteen years old and furious that her brother's goodness was going to cost them everything, and she'd remembered the stories—the tollhouse at the pass head, the door beneath, the court that traded in souls and secrets. Children's stories, nurse-tales. Except in Thornfell the nurse-tales had addresses.

She went up on the night of the long dark, alone, with a candle stub and her father's good knife she never intended to use. The tollhouse stood where the maps stopped, older than the road, its windows lit a color she had no name for. The keeper was a woman made mostly of shadow and courtesy.

"You want a bargain," the keeper said. Not a question.

"I want my brother left alone," Wren said. "No one finds out what he is. Not collectors, not lords, not kings. For his whole life, unless he speaks first."

"That's a large ask."

"He's worth more than anything I have."

The keeper had studied her for a long moment with eyes like frost on a well. "Everything you have isn't currency here. We deal in what you *are*."

Wren had thought she understood. Everyone thinks they understand. "Then take whatever that costs."

"The Undercourt will hold your soul in trust," the keeper said, "against your brother's secret. While the secret holds, the soul stays. If the secret breaks—" the frost-eyes had almost seemed kind, then, "—you'll want to make sure it doesn't break, girl. For your own sake."

She signed. Of course she signed. She was fourteen and in love with her family the way only children and fools are, totally, without collateral. She signed in the old script, the one that predates the crown, and the pen drew something out of her that she felt leave through her sternum like a hook leaving flesh, and afterward she walked home through the snow feeling lighter and hollowed and victorious, and she slept better that night than she had in months.

The miracle worked. That was the obscene thing. The collectors' records corrupted themselves; the debt-men's contracts went illegible; the assessor fell ill and forgot the discrepancy entirely. No one ever found out what Jude was. Ten years, and no one ever found out.

Until a scout named Harrow watched a stuck gate open at a spoken word, and the machinery of the crown began to grind, and Wren realized with a clarity like ice water that the bargain was coming due—not because Jude had broken faith with *her*, but because the world doesn't honor other people's silences forever. Sooner or later the secret breaks. The keeper had warned her. *You'll want to make sure it doesn't break.*

And if the secret broke—if the crown took Jude, named him, paraded him as a swordspeecher for the war effort—then the contract failed, and what the Undercourt held in trust would revert. To them. Forever.

That was the arithmetic she'd done at fourteen and gotten wrong: she'd thought the price was fixed. It wasn't. The price compounded.

* * *

At midnight Wren finished her work. The fair copy of Vess's orders now read *Millbrook* where it should have read *Salt Pass*, and the census requisition bore a delivery date three weeks late, and the whole packet was sealed and addressed with a scribe's flawless hand. Small treasons. Deniable. Enough to buy Jude time to run—which he would, the stubborn fool, the moment word reached him, and he'd run alone, because he didn't know. He'd never known. Ten years of protecting him from the crown and she'd never once been able to protect him from the truth.

She blew out her candle and stood in the dark of the scriptorium, pressing her hand flat against her own chest, over the place the hook had left her.

"Hold on," she whispered—to the jewel of herself somewhere far below the mountain, singing in a case she'd never seen; to her brother, asleep somewhere on the high road, unaware that the hunt was turning toward him like a compass needle. "Hold on. I'm coming."

Because running wasn't enough anymore. Buying time wasn't enough. She'd spent ten years paying interest on a debt whose principal was her own self, and the only way to close the account was to go down to the tollhouse again, walk past the courteous shadow-woman, and demand her property back.

Or trade for it. There was always something to trade. That was the lesson of the court beneath: everything was currency, everything had a price, and love was just the name you gave the things you were willing to spend.

She gathered her cloak. Somewhere above the town, snow was beginning to fall on the passes, early this year, heavy. The witch-finder's company would ride within days. And Wren Thornbury, diligent, reliable, never-missing-an-error, stepped out into the dark to commit the largest error of her careful life—

—and nearly walked into the arms of Captain Vess, waiting in the doorway with her forged orders in his fist.

"You dropped these," he said, "on your way to the tollhouse."
