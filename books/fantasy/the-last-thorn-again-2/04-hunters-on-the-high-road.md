## 4. Hunters on the High Road

The mentor's name was Corliss Vane, and he taught Jude to swear at iron before he taught him to love it.

That had been twenty years ago, in a forge yard in Millbrook, when Jude was nine and already hiding the way blades leaned toward him when he spoke. Corliss had caught him whispering to a rusted scythe—*rise* —and watched it shudder upright like a dog called to heel. Any other man would have run for the witch-tithe. Corliss had knelt in the straw and said, quietly, "Say it again. Slower. Words are load-bearing, boy. Build them right or they'll fall on you."

Now Corliss was seventy-one, half-deaf, and wanted by the crown.

Jude had come down from the pass with three mules of smuggled iron and found the edict nailed to the Millbrook meetinghouse door: ALL SWORDSPEAKERS TO BE TAKEN ALIVE FOR THE WAR EFFORT. HARBORERS HANGED. And beneath it, in the clerk's neat hand, a list of names. Corliss Vane was fourth from the top.

He hadn't even gone home first. He'd bought a mule, loaded the old man's chest of tools and his one good sword—a blade Corliss had forged himself and named *Patience*, which was either sentiment or a joke—and turned them both back toward the mountains before the ink on the proclamation dried.

Three days up the High Road, the ambush came.

---

It came wrong, which is what saved them for the first minute. No arrows from the treeline, no riders sweeping the switchback below. Instead a single figure stood in the road ahead, hands empty, hood back, a young woman with a crown scout's badge pinned crooked at her collar.

"Halt," she said. "In the name of the Regent's Hunt."

Behind her, the forest held its breath. Jude counted the ways this was bad. A scout alone meant scouts not alone. The road bent around a granite shoulder thirty yards ahead—good ground for crossbows. The wind was coming downslope, carrying their scent and sound away from the trees behind, which meant the trees ahead were where the rest of them waited.

Corliss reined his mule to a stop and said, without moving his lips much, "How many?"

"Six," Jude breathed. "Maybe eight. She's talking so we stand still."

"I know." The old man's eyes crinkled. "She's stalling because *they're* stalling. Someone's giving orders from cover. That means someone important." He dismounted, slow, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "Help me down, boy. My knees have opinions about ambushes."

Jude took his arm. Up close, Corliss smelled of coal smoke and liniment, the same as always, the same as the forge yard, and something in Jude's chest went tight and hot.

"Jude Thornbury," the scout called out. "Step forward. You're wanted alive. The old man too."

His name. Not "the smuggler." Not "the iron-runner out of Thornfell." His name, spoken into the mountain air like a coin tossed to buy his surrender. Somewhere behind that granite shoulder sat a man with a paper and a pen, and on that paper was written *Jude Thornbury, swordspeaker*, and every careful year of his life was already ash.

"Thornbury," Corliss repeated softly. "So that's how they'll say it. I always thought you'd be famous for something better than getting caught."

"Keep your voice down."

"Why? They've got crossbows, lad, not ears." He patted Patience's hilt where it hung at his hip. "Listen. When this goes wrong—and it will go wrong—I want you to remember what I told you in the yard. Words are load-bearing. Don't build a wall. Build an arch. Let the weight pass through."

"Corliss—"

"And don't you dare spend yourself saving me. I'm seventy-one. Everything past this is gravy." He grinned, gap-toothed. "Besides, I want to see what you can do. Ten years I've wondered."

The scout raised her hand. "Last warning—"

Jude spoke.

Not loudly. That was the thing people never understood about swordspeech—the oaths didn't need volume, only structure, only the weight of meaning properly stacked. He'd spent ten years not speaking, ten years swallowing the words until they'd gone heavy as ingots in his chest, and now they came out of him in a flood, three oaths braided into one sentence, an arch, exactly as built:

*"Steel in sight, rise and answer; steel in flight, bend and break; steel in any hand but mine, forget the hand that holds you."*

The world rang like a struck bell.

Every blade within earshot answered. The scout's dagger tore itself free of her belt and hung quivering in the air. In the trees ahead, six crossbow strings snapped taut and then *snapped entirely*, the steel limbs of the weapons wrenching sideways with screams of tortured wood. Two hidden hunters cried out as their own swords wrenched from scabbards and spun into the open air above the road, hanging there, trembling, waiting.

And then Jude gave them somewhere to go.

*"Down,"* he said, and it wasn't even an oath, just a word, but the blades obeyed that too—they plunged into the earth at the road's edge in a perfect row, quivering, planted like a fence of graves.

For one heartbeat, silence. Snow sifting off the pines. The scout staring at him with an expression that wasn't fear exactly—it was awe, which was worse.

Then the granite shoulder moved, and it wasn't granite. It was a pavise, a tower shield big as a door, and behind it came the hunt-captain with a war-hammer already swinging, and behind *him* the trees spat men.

What followed took less time than telling it. Jude had never fought like this—never let himself fight like this. Every oath was a lever and the whole mountainside was full of things that wanted leverage. He spoke a hunting knife into a hunter's throat. He spoke the buckle off a shield strap and the shield dropped and the man behind it learned why you don't bring polearms to a swordspeaker. He spoke *Patience* out of Corliss's sheath and sent her singing through the fight like a thrown star, opening a man's forearm, ringing off a helmet, returning to the old man's hand as if she'd never left—which, Jude understood dimly, through the roar in his blood, was exactly the point of the name.

It should have been enough. It was more than enough. Eight trained hunters, and inside forty seconds five of them were down or dying and the sixth was the scout, unarmed, on her knees in the snow with her hands laced behind her head, watching him with those wide unafraid eyes.

Which is when Jude heard the crossbow that hadn't been in the trees.

It had been higher. In the rocks above the bend, a ninth man, a spare, positioned to cover the retreat if the ambush failed—and it had failed, and so the bolt came down the slope at the only target that mattered, at the old man standing in the open road with his arms spread and his heart full of pride.

Jude saw it leave the string.

There is a speed at which speaking stops being speech and becomes reflex, and Jude found it in that instant. *"Turn!"* he shouted—not an oath, not structured, just raw—and Patience leapt from Corliss's grip—

A half-second slow.

The bolt took Corliss high in the chest, and Patience buried herself in the snow a hand's width from his boot, and the old man sat down very carefully in the road, the way a man sits when his legs have stopped being consulted.

---

The ninth man died before he reached the bottom of the rocks. Jude doesn't remember speaking the oath. He remembers the man's own sword leaving his hand on the slope, turning in the air, and finding him, and then Jude was running through the snow and sliding on his knees in the bloody slush and catching Corliss before his head could hit the stone.

"No," Jude said. Stupidly, uselessly, the way anyone says it. "No, no—"

"Boy." Corliss coughed. There was blood on his teeth and somehow he was still smiling. "Load-bearing. Told you. Arch, not wall."

"You're not—" Jude pressed his palm against the wound and the blood came up between his fingers, hot, shockingly fast. "I can get you down the pass. Tollhouse is two days. The tollkeeper knows fieldwork, she—"

"Lad." The old man put a shaking hand over Jude's. "Stop building. Listen." His eyes were going glassy but his voice held, that forge-yard voice, patient, certain. "You just did in one minute what the whole kingdom's scared of. You think that was a waste? You think I mind?" He laughed, wet and short. "I made swords my whole life. Never once seen one sing. Today I saw mine fly. Gravy, boy. All of it, gravy."

"Corliss—"

"One thing." His grip tightened, sudden strength, the last of it. "The words. Yours. Don't you dare bury them again. Whatever it costs—don't you ever pretend again. Promise me. Swear it."

"I swear," Jude said, and the oath left him like a blade leaving a sheath, and somewhere far below them in the stone of the mountain, something ancient stirred and noted it down.

Corliss smiled. "Good lad." Then, quieter, looking past Jude at the sky through the pines: "Look at that. Snow's starting. Always liked the snow." And the light went out of him the way coals settle, gradually and then all at once, and the forge yard in Millbrook, and the rusted scythe, and twenty years of quiet lessons all went with it.

Jude knelt in the road holding his mentor and did not move for a long time.

---

The scout hadn't run.

He noticed it only when he finally stood, numb, wiping his hands uselessly on his coat. She was still kneeling where she'd surrendered, snow gathering on her shoulders, watching him with that terrible awe.

"You could have run," Jude said. His voice sounded like someone else's.

"Where?" She nodded at the dead. "You'd catch me. Or the mountain would." She swallowed. "I'm going to report this. You understand that. I'm the only one left to report it."

"I know."

"They'll come with everything now. Not a company. A legion. They'll seal the passes and burn the tollhouses and dig you out of whatever hole you crawl into, because *Jude Thornbury* is worth a legion now." Her eyes flicked to the planted blades along the roadside, still standing in their row like mourners. "Do you know what they'll call you? What the stories will say by spring?"

"I don't care."

"You should." She rose slowly, keeping her hands visible, and something in her face shifted—from awe to something almost like pity. "Because I watched you do that, and I'll tell you what I saw. I saw a man who spent his whole life holding his breath. And I saw what happened the second he let it out." She picked her way past the bodies, past the mules standing patient and untouched, and paused beside Corliss's body long enough to close his eyes with two fingers, gentle, almost kind.

"The relief," she said quietly. "That's what frightens me. You looked relieved."

Then she walked down the road, south, toward Millbrook, toward the crown, carrying his name like a lit torch, and Jude let her go because chasing her would change nothing and because some part of him—the part that had whispered to a stuck gate three days ago and felt the years of silence crack—was tired, so tired, of pretending.

He buried Corliss above the treeline, under a cairn, with Patience laid across his chest because a smith should keep his best work. Then he stood in the falling snow and looked north, up the pass, toward the tollhouse and the dark beneath the mountain, where Wren was.

Wren. Who had sold something once—he'd never known what—to buy his silence. Whose soul might be the coin the Undercourt kept.

The hunt was coming with everything. Winter was closing. And for the first time in ten years, Jude Thornbury had nothing left to hide behind except the truth.

He whistled up the mules and started walking.
