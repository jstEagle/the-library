## 5. The Tollhouse Bargain

The witch's tollhouse sat where the map said nothing should: a crooked stone tooth wedged into the throat of Greyhollow Pass, smoke rising from a chimney that leaned like a drunk against the wind. Jude had crossed this pass eleven times in ten years of smuggling, and he had never once seen it. Neither, he was certain, had anyone else.

That was the point of witches. They were only there when they were needed, and by then it was too late to turn around.

He stopped thirty paces out and let his horse breathe. The storm behind him had swallowed his backtrail hours ago—which meant it would swallow the hunters' pursuit too, if any survived to follow it. He didn't fool himself that they hadn't. A company of twelve did not simply cease to exist because one man spoke three oaths and unmade them. Someone had crawled away from that ruin on the high road with a name burned into their memory, and that name was already running ahead of him through every garrison, every tavern, every church bell between here and the coast.

*Jude Thornbury. Swordspeaker.*

He'd spent a decade buying silence with other people's coin and other people's secrets, and he'd spent all of it in four minutes with three oaths and a dead mentor at his feet.

The tollhouse door opened before he knocked. Of course it did.

The woman who stood in it was old the way rivers are old—not in years but in patience. Her hair was white as the pass around her, braided with iron rings, and her eyes were the flat grey of a blade left out in weather. She looked at him the way a ferryman looks at a passenger: weighing cargo, not soul.

"You're bleeding," she said.

"It's mostly the horse's."

"Mostly." She stepped aside. "Come in before the mountain decides you're interesting."

***

Inside, the tollhouse was one room, warm and close, hung with things Jude chose not to examine too closely—bundles of feathers, knots of rope, a row of small bells each wrapped in its own cloth so it couldn't ring. A fire burned in a hearth built entirely of road-stones, mortared together from a hundred different miles of highway. He recognized the shape of some of them. Roads remembered where they'd been. So, apparently, did this house.

The witch poured two cups of something dark and bitter without asking what he wanted. She set one in front of him and sat across the table, folding her hands.

"You killed a crown hunting company on the high road yesterday," she said. "Twelve men. You spoke three oaths at once, which no swordspeaker has done since the Sundering, and the road felt every word of it. It's been ringing ever since." She touched the wall; the stones hummed faintly under her palm, like a struck bell dying slowly. "You woke the whole mountain up, boy. Now everything down there knows your name too."

"Then we understand each other," Jude said. "I need to go beneath it."

"I know why you're here." Her voice didn't soften, but something behind it did—a fraction, a degree. "Everyone who comes to me wants something impossible. You want your sister back. The Undercourt has her soul in a box, and you intend to walk into the Pale Magistrate's own hall and take it off his shelf." She drank. "I've ferried six souls' worth of desperate men and women toward that door in my time. None came back. I mention this not to discourage you. I mention it so you can't say I didn't."

"Noted," Jude said. "What's the toll?"

"Straight to business. Good. The sentimental ones die fastest." She set down her cup. "Passage beneath the mountain isn't a road, it's an agreement. The ways below don't open for coin or blood or kindness. They open for *oath*. One spoken oath, freely given, binding you to the service of the road itself. Every road. All of them, above and below, as long as the roads endure."

Jude went very still. "That's not passage. That's servitude."

"That's *toll*." The witch's grey eyes didn't blink. "You'd carry the road's burdens. Guide the lost. Keep the passes when winter tries to close them. Answer when the road calls—and it will call, often, at the worst times, for the rest of your life. You'd never be free of it. You'd hear every mile of stone within a day's walk whispering its troubles, and you'd be obliged to care." She leaned forward. "Now ask yourself, swordspeaker: what is the difference between that and the gift you were born with?"

"The gift was mine," Jude said. "I never asked for it. This you're offering me a choice."

"*There* it is." For the first time, the old woman smiled, and it transformed her face into something ancient and almost kind. "Ten years hiding, and you still think the chains that chose you are worse than the ones you choose. Most men never learn the difference. Most men die still resenting the first pair and too frightened to try the second." She stood and went to the hearth, laying her hand flat on the road-stones. "So. Will you pay?"

Jude looked at his cup. At the fire. At the storm hammering the shutters like a fist demanding entry, somewhere behind which the hunt was closing, and beyond the hunt the court, and beyond the court a cell where Wren sat waiting for a brother who might already be dead.

He thought about oaths. He thought about how every word he'd ever sworn had been a lock—on his tongue, on his life, on the sister who'd sold her soul to keep those locks shut. He had spent ten years believing that silence was safety, and silence had bought him exactly one thing: the certainty that when the price finally came due, he'd have nothing left to pay it with except himself.

Fine. Then let himself be the payment. Let it be chosen.

"One condition," he said.

The witch raised an eyebrow. "Bold."

"When the road calls—I answer. But if my sister needs me, the road waits. Swear it back to me, on the road's own stone, or find another fool."

For a long moment the only sound was the fire and the wind. Then the witch laughed—a short, rough bark, genuinely delighted.

"Oh, you'll do," she said. "The road will hate you. It hates everyone who bargains. Kneel."

***

He knelt on the hearthstones, and she pulled one loose from the mortar—an ordinary cobble, worn smooth by centuries of cartwheels and boots and funeral processions—and pressed it into his hands. It was warm. It was humming. Up close he could feel the whole of it: every road it had ever been part of, stretching away beneath him like veins under skin, the entire circulatory system of the world's travel, tired and patient and endlessly, endlessly used.

"Speak," the witch said. "Freely given. The road will know if it isn't."

Jude closed his hands around the stone. And because he was who he was—because he had never in his life been able to do anything halfway—he did not speak a bargain.

He spoke a vow.

"Hear me, roads of the world, paved and broken, kept and abandoned. I am Jude Thornbury, son of Thornfell, brother of Wren, and I give myself to your keeping. Where you run, I will walk. Where you fail, I will mend. Where travelers are lost upon you, I will find them, though it cost me everything else I am. This I swear freely, and freely means forever."

The stone flared white-hot.

Not pain—*arrival*. Every road within a hundred miles lit up behind his eyes at once: the high road with its dead men cooling in the snow, the smugglers' trails threading the passes, the buried ways beneath the mountain, dark and old and hungry, and one narrow tunnel, deep down, that pulsed like a wound. The road knew him now. The road had *claims* on him now. He could feel them settling over his shoulders like a yoke, heavy and fitted and—God help him—not unwelcome.

When his vision cleared, the witch was watching him with an expression he'd never seen on a tollkeeper's face. Something like grief. Something like envy.

"You swore more than I asked," she said quietly.

"I'm told that's a habit of mine."

"It'll kill you someday. Vows always overshoot; that's what makes them vows." She took the cooled stone from his hands and pressed it back into the hearth, and the whole house sighed around them, walls and roof and leaning chimney settling into place, as if the building itself had just gained a load-bearing member. "But it also means the road loves you now. That's rarer than you know, and worth more. Come. The way opens at dawn, and you'll want to sleep. The descent takes everything."

She showed him to a cot in the corner. As he lay down, bone-tired, the storm outside began to quiet—not fading, but *settling*, the way a dog settles when its master comes home.

Jude stared at the rafters and felt the roads breathing beneath him, hundreds of miles of them, and somewhere far below, through stone and root and older stone, a jewel in a dark hall singing his sister's name to itself in the dark.

*Hold on*, he thought at it, at her, with the whole strength of the new thing in his chest. *I'm coming down the long way.*

And beneath him, deep in the roots of the world, something heard him—and began, very softly, to sing back.
