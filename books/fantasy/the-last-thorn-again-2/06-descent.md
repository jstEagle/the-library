## 6. Descent

The tollhouse door closed behind Jude like a mouth.

He had expected darkness. What he got was light—the wrong kind of it, pale and sourceless, rising from the walls themselves. He stood on a stair that spiraled down into the mountain's root, and the walls were not stone. They were bone. Femurs and ribs and long curved things he had no names for, laid in courses like masonry, and from every one of them seeped a soft white glow that made no shadows.

His lantern was useless here. He shuttered it and hung it from his belt anyway, because putting it out felt like admitting how far under he already was.

The oath sat in his chest where it always sat now—a warm knot behind the sternum, the tollkeeper's mark. *Serve the road.* He still didn't know what the road would ask of him. The witch at the tollhouse had smiled when she took his word, and witches only smiled like that when they'd gotten the better end of a trade.

"Down," he said to himself, because his voice needed something to do. "Just down."

The stairs went down for a long time.

He counted the first thousand steps. By the second thousand he stopped counting, because the numbers started to feel like they were being counted for him—like something below was keeping tally, patient as winter. The air grew warm. That was the worst part, somehow. He'd dressed for cold passes and ice-slick ledges, and now sweat ran down his spine while the bone walls breathed their bloodless light around him.

Somewhere ahead, faint as a memory of music, something was singing.

Not words. Not quite. A thread of sound that rose and fell like breathing, many voices braided into one, coming up through the bones the way water comes up through chalk. He stopped once with his hand flat against a femur wider than his torso and felt the song humming through his palm, through his wrist, into the old scar tissue of his gift.

The knot behind his sternum tightened. The road, pulling its leash.

"I'm going," he muttered. "I'm already going."

*You are,* said something, and it wasn't the road. It came from everywhere and nowhere, courteous and vast, and Jude's skin crawled because he knew that voice. Every child in Thornfell knew it from the fireside stories. The Pale Magistrate did not shout. The Pale Magistrate did not need to.

Jude walked faster.

* * *

Above him, three hundred miles north and a world away, Wren Thornbury dipped her pen in iron-gall ink and committed treason in a clerk's perfect hand.

The hunt's orders lay spread across her desk in the provost's office at Millbrook garrison: four companies, deployment routes, supply schedules, and—circled twice in the captain's impatient scrawl—the order to seal every pass into the Thornfells by month's end. Winter or no winter. The witch-hunters wanted their swordspeaker, and they intended to close the mountain like a fist.

Captain Vess had left her alone with the documents for an hour while he reviewed prisoners. An hour. She'd begged the copying work off his adjutant two days ago specifically so this hour could happen, and she had spent those days memorizing the routes until she could draw them blind.

Now she copied them wrong.

A river crossing moved twelve miles downstream, where the fords were drowned in spring melt. A supply cache shifted to a valley that flooded by Lammas. The company meant to hold the high road was redirected onto the low road, where the passes narrowed to a goat-track and a dozen resolute smugglers with rockfall experience could stall an army for a week. Nothing obvious. Nothing that wouldn't be blamed on a tired clerk or bad intelligence. Just small, quiet errors, seeded through the margins of an empire's paperwork.

Her hand did not shake. Ten years as a court scribe had taught her that much: the state never looks closely at neat handwriting.

What shook was everything else. Because beneath the orders, filed between requisitions like it was nothing, was a prisoner manifest. And third from the top, in her own careful letters, transcribed that morning:

*Thornbury, Wren — scribe, suspected correspondent of fugitive — remand to Undercourt liaison.*

They knew. Not everything—she'd have been in chains already if they knew everything—but they suspected, and suspicion was a hook, and hooks get pulled. When they pulled hers, they'd find her brother's name at the bottom of it. And her brother was, at this moment, walking down into the dark to buy back the soul she'd sold, which meant that whatever time she bought him had to be enough.

She blotted the last page. Sanded it. Stacked it square.

Then she opened the garrison's map cabinet—the locked one, the one with the true routes—and slid the original deployment chart inside her scribe's coat, against her ribs, where it made a paper heartbeat over her own.

There were ways this could still work. There was even a version where it worked cleanly: she delivered the doctored copies, walked out at shift's end, and was over the low pass before anyone compared charts.

Wren looked at the door. At the window, frosted half over. At the little brass bell on the corner of her desk that she'd ring when the work was done.

She thought about Jude at seven years old, holding a kitchen knife that had come alive in his fist at his first unthinking word, both of them laughing, neither of them knowing what it would cost. She thought about the thing beneath the mountain wearing her voice like a borrowed coat, singing in the dark for ten years because she'd traded it away to keep him safe. She thought about how she had told herself, all this time, that the bargain was paid. Settled. Done.

It wasn't done. It had just been deferred, and deferral was a debt too.

Wren rang the bell.

* * *

The halls changed.

Jude noticed it first in the floor: the bone gave way to black stone, polished mirror-smooth, and his boots rang on it like he was walking on a struck bell. Then the light changed. The sourceless glow drew back from the walls and gathered instead into fixtures—chains suspended from ceilings too high to see, each link crusted with gems, and every gem burning with a color that had no business existing. Blue like a scream. Red like a held breath.

And each gem, as he passed beneath it, was singing.

One voice per stone. He understood that with a certainty that arrived whole, the way understanding arrives in dreams. The jewels weren't lamps. They were souls. Hundreds of them, thousands, hung in the dark and made to shine, and the braided music he'd heard on the stairs was this—everyone, all at once, forever.

He stopped in the middle of the great hall and made himself look up. Made himself see it.

"Wren," he said, and his voice cracked apart in the vaulting dark. Somewhere above him, one blue flame flickered. Just once. Like a listener turning its head.

"There you are."

The Magistrate stood at the far end of the hall. Jude hadn't seen him arrive; he was simply there, tall and thin as a drawn blade, robes the gray of old snow, face pleasant and unfinished, like a sketch of a man waiting for its final lines. Around his throat hung a chain of keys, and every key was made of bone.

"You've come about the girl," the Magistrate said. "They usually do. Mothers, mostly. Brothers, sometimes." He tilted his head. "Lovers, once. That ended badly."

"I've come to make a trade."

"They all say that." The Magistrate began to walk, unhurried, and the hall walked with him—or rather, Jude found his feet moving to keep pace, drawn along like a boat in a current. "But you're different. I can hear it. You're full of oaths, swordspeaker. Stuffed with them. Sworn silence, sworn service, sworn secrecy—one after another, ten years deep." He smiled without any lines moving on his face. "Do you know what you sound like? From in here?"

"No."

"A chord," said the Magistrate. "A rich one. I could listen to you for centuries."

Ahead, the hall opened onto a dais of black stone, and on the dais, alone among ten thousand singing lights, sat a single jewel on a cushion of lead. Small. Deep blue. Its voice, when Jude's ear caught it under the chorus, was unmistakable—the particular way she hummed when she thought no one was listening, the tune she used to sing drying dishes in their mother's kitchen before the kitchen, before the mother, before everything.

Something in Jude's chest tore along a seam he hadn't known was there.

"That one isn't for sale," the Magistrate said gently. "That one is collateral. But sit. Let's discuss what is."

* * *

Wren was halfway across the garrison yard with the true charts against her ribs when Captain Vess stepped out of the guardhouse doorway and blocked her path.

"Scribe." He wasn't smiling. Behind him, two hunters in gray stood with their hands loose at their sides, near their blades. "Adjutant Corran says you requested copying duty. Says you asked for it special. Twice."

"The captain knows I take pride in my work."

"Mm." Vess held out one gloved hand. "And the originals?"

Every instinct screamed run. She didn't. Running confirmed; standing was merely suspicious, and suspicious clerks were a nuisance, not a verdict. She let her shoulders slump in the small, weary way of a functionary put upon. "In my desk, sir. Where you left them."

"We'll see." His eyes stayed on her a moment too long. "Walk with me to your office."

The walk lasted forty yards and a lifetime. At the office door, Vess gestured her through first—and as she passed him, his hand brushed her coat, casual as a friend's clap on the shoulder.

The paper crackled.

Vess's expression didn't change, but something behind it did, settling like a trap closing. "Well," he said softly. "Well, well. Open the coat, Mistress Thornbury."

She thought of Jude on the stairs, walking down into the singing dark right now, at this very minute, needing every hour she'd bought him. She looked at the hunters. At the yard gate, where the relief patrol wouldn't return for twenty minutes. At Vess's patient, certain face.

Wren Thornbury, court scribe, did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for ten years.

Then she ran.

She made the woodpile. She made the stable wall. She almost made the drainage culvert—and then the whistle blew, and boots thundered on frost, and a weighted net took her across the shoulders and drove her into the frozen mud hard enough to knock the world white.

They hauled her up by the arms. Vess crouched in front of her, drew the folded charts from her ruined coat, and studied them with the disappointment of a man confirming a foregone conclusion.

"Treason," he said, almost kindly. "Harboring. Correspondence with a marked fugitive." He tucked the charts away and stood. "Take her to the liaison. The Undercourt will want her."

They dragged her past the garrison bell, past the frosted window of her own small office, out toward the mountain's shadow. And Wren, spitting mud, laughing at the sheer stupid audacity of it, called out to the hunters holding her arms:

"You're too late anyway. He's already under."

* * *

On the dais, across a table of black stone, the Pale Magistrate laced his bone-white fingers and made his offer.

"Your gift entire," he said. "Every oath you have ever spoken, unsung from you like thread from a spool—and every oath you ever might have spoken besides. No more swordspeech, Jude Thornbury. No more power. No more being hunted for what you are, because you will no longer *be* anything. Safe. Ordinary. Silent." His unfinished face leaned close. "And the girl walks out of here with her soul in her chest, and neither of you ever sees me again."

Below them, the blue jewel sang its dish-drying tune into the dark.

Jude looked at it for a long, long moment. And then he began, quietly, to laugh.

"You misunderstand the merchandise," he said. "I'm not here to sell. I'm here to break something."

The Magistrate went very still. "Break?"

"My oldest oath." Jude stood, and the knot behind his sternum—the tollkeeper's chain, the road's leash—pulled taut, warning. He ignored it. "The first one I ever spoke. You've heard it your whole life down here. Everyone has." He filled his lungs with the bone-lit air. "I unsay it."

The first jewel in the hall went dark.
