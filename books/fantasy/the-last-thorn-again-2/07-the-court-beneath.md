## 7. The Court Beneath

The stairs went down for a long time.

Not the way stairs go down in the world above—steps and landings, turns and railings—but the way a rope goes down into a well. Jude lost count somewhere past the thousandth, and after that he stopped counting because counting was a way of pretending there would be an end. The walls around him were bone-white and faintly warm, like standing inside something that had once been alive and still remembered it. His breath came out in silver threads that did not disperse but drifted downward, following him, as if even his exhales were being collected.

The tollkeeper's oath hummed in his chest with every step. *I serve the road.* He could feel it now not as words but as weight—a smooth stone lodged behind his sternum, turning slowly. The road wanted him to keep walking. The road, apparently, led here.

At the bottom there was no door. There was only a threshold where the bone-light changed color, from white to a pale gold like candlelight through honey, and beyond it the Undercourt opened up.

Jude had imagined caverns. What he found was a city.

It rose in terraces of white stone carved without seam or chisel-mark, streets curving in arcs too perfect for human hands, and everywhere the light came from below—from veins of gold running through the rock itself, so that the whole city seemed lit from underneath, a thing seen through amber. There were people in the streets. That was the worst part. Figures in court dress and workman's clothes and one in a smuggler's oilskin much like his own, all of them moving with a strange unhurried grace, all of them silent. Not quiet—silent, in the way that a held breath is silent.

He understood, walking among them, that none of them were alive. They were souls wearing shapes, going about the business of a court that had ended its sessions centuries ago but never stopped convening.

"Jude Thornbury."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the gold in the walls. It was a pleasant voice. That was somehow worse than if it hadn't been.

He followed the sound down a street of white arcades to a hall whose doors stood open—doors tall enough for a giant, carved with a thornbush motif so old the thorns had been worn smooth by reverence. Inside, the hall stretched back farther than any building should, and at the far end, on a throne of layered bone, sat the Pale Magistrate.

She looked like a woman who had been beautiful so long ago that beauty had worn away to structure—the architecture of a face without its paint. Her skin was the white of the walls. Her eyes were the gold in the stone. She wore no crown; she didn't need one. The whole city was her crown, and every soul in it a jewel.

"You came by the toll-road," she said. "How economical. Most of them die on the stairs."

"I paid my toll."

"So you did. One oath, freely given, binding you to serve the road." She tilted her head. "Do you know what happens to an oath when it is spoken beneath the mountain? Above ground, words are wind. Down here they are currency. You have been spending yourself the whole way down, swordspeaker."

Jude kept his hand away from his sword. Not because he wasn't tempted, but because he'd learned in the ambush on the high road exactly how much three oaths cost, and he suspected this place charged interest.

"My sister," he said. "Wren Thornbury. Ten years ago she came down here and bought something. I've come to buy it back."

Something moved across the Magistrate's face—not a smile, but the memory of one. "She bought your silence. A decade of it. And it worked, didn't it? Ten years, no word of gift or oath ever crossed your lips until the pass." She leaned forward. "You should know that I have been waiting for you longer than you have been walking. Souls come to us in trade, swordspeaker, but gifts—gifts we must be given. Yours has been accruing interest since before you knew you had it."

"Where is she?"

The Magistrate raised one hand, lazily, the way a queen waves off a servant. Somewhere behind the throne, a sound began.

Singing.

It was small and high and it wound through the hall like smoke, and Jude knew it before his mind caught up to his body—he was moving before he decided to move, striding past the throne's dais toward a niche in the wall where a dozen treasures rested on shelves of bone. Crowns. Rings. A child's shoe. And among them, glowing with a soft inner light, a jewel the size of a plum, singing in a voice he had heard lullabies in, arguments in, goodbyes in through a tavern door while he rode out of town ahead of the witch-hunters.

Wren's soul.

His fingers closed around it and it sang louder, wilder, pressing against his palm like a bird's heartbeat, and the warmth of it went through him and broke something open that ten years of hiding had sealed shut.

"She sings better down here," the Magistrate said conversationally. "Souls do. All that truth in them, finally unhidden. Up there she spent her life lying for you—forging manifests, intercepting edicts, smiling at hunters. Do you know what a lie costs a soul? Every one of them is a small debt. We simply hold the collateral."

Jude turned, the jewel cupped against his chest. "Name your price."

"There it is." The Magistrate rose from her throne, and her shadow moved wrong—slower than she did, arriving late, as though even darkness hesitated to follow her. "You'd think they'd haggle. They never do. Love makes terrible merchants." She descended the dais step by step. "Here is my price, Jude Thornbury, and hear all of it before you speak, because beneath this mountain, speaking is signing."

She stopped three paces away. This close, he could see that her eyes weren't gold at all but doors—tiny lit doorways, thousands of them, receding into her skull.

"Your gift entire. Not one oath, not a year of silence. Every oath you will ever speak, from this moment until your death, belongs to the Undercourt. You will never command another blade. You will never speak another working. The words will come to your tongue and die there, and you will feel them die, each time, for the rest of your ordinary, unremarkable, *safe* life."

*Safe.* The word landed in him like a coin dropped down a well.

"In exchange," she went on, "your sister walks out of here whole. Her soul returns to her, every debt forgiven—including the one she owes for the forgery she'll answer for above. And more: the hunt ends. I hold contracts older than crowns, swordspeaker. One word from me and every witch-hunter in the Thornfells forgets your name. Your mentor's name comes off their lists. No proclamations. No gallows. You go back to your smuggling routes and your snowbound passes and you live to be old, and no one—no one—ever comes for you again."

The singing jewel trembled in his hands.

And oh, it was sweet, the picture she painted. He let himself look at it, really look, the way a starving man looks through a bakery window. Ten years of fear ending in a single sentence. Wren safe. Himself safe. No more running the passes in winter, no more flinching at red cloaks, no more lying awake cataloguing everyone who might have seen. He would be nobody again, gloriously and permanently nobody, and the gift that had gotten his mentor killed and put his name on lists would be buried deeper than this court, and he could finally, finally rest.

All it cost was everything he was.

Because that was the thing about the gift—it wasn't a weapon. It was his voice. The first oath he'd ever spoken had been at seven years old, whispering a rusted gate open so he wouldn't be late to Wren's birthday, and the wonder of it had felt like being told a secret about the world: *words are not just for saying things. Words are for making things true.* Every oath since had been his. Chosen. Paid for. His.

And the Magistrate was offering to take every word he would ever say and make them hers.

"They'd still be mine," he said slowly. "Wouldn't they? In your vaults. Singing."

"Everything here sings." Her smile was gentle. "Yours would sing beautifully."

"And Wren? Would she remember this? The bargain?"

"Wren will remember whatever makes her happy. That's what returning a soul does—it lets people forget what their happiness cost." She spread her white hands. "You can leave here lighter than you came, swordspeaker. Both of you. Whole, safe, forgiven. All you have to do is stop being what you are."

The jewel sang against his chest, and he realized the song had changed—it wasn't just melody anymore. It was words, worn thin by years of being sung but still legible, and he bent his head close and listened.

*I'm sorry. I'm sorry. Take mine instead. I'm sorry.*

Ten years. She'd been apologizing for ten years in the dark, and he'd been hiding, and between the two of them they'd built a prison made entirely of love and called it safety.

Jude straightened.

"No," he said.

The word fell into the hall like a stone into still water. The silent souls in the street outside seemed to lean closer. The Magistrate's expression didn't change, but the tiny golden doors of her eyes flickered—one of them, far back in the depths, closed.

"No?" she said softly. "You would refuse safety itself?"

"I'd refuse *you* having it." He lifted his head. "You want my oaths because oaths are power, and power down here is currency, and you're rich. But you don't make anything, Magistrate. You only collect. And I've spent ten years learning the difference between hiding and keeping, and I'm done confusing them."

"Then you'll leave with nothing." Her voice cooled by degrees. "No sister. No soul returned. Only debts, and hunters, and winter."

"Maybe." His hand drifted to his sword hilt—not to draw, but to rest there, the way you rest a hand on an old friend's shoulder. "But you should know something about swordspeakers, since you collect our gifts. We don't just speak oaths to blades." He met her thousand golden doors. "We unspeak them, too. And there's one I've been carrying since I was seventeen years old. One I swore in a cellar with my sister crying in the corner. One that started all of this."

For the first time, something like uncertainty crossed the ancient face.

"The oath of silence," Jude said. "The first coin Wren ever paid you. I'm going to unsay it—and I want you to hear exactly what ten years of hidden truth sounds like when it stops being quiet."

The jewel in his hands began to sing a warning—or perhaps a hope—and deep in the bones of the mountain, something very old shifted in its sleep and began, at last, to listen.
