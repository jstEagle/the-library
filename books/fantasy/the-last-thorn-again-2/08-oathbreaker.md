## 8. Oathbreaker

The Pale Magistrate's throne was a rib of the mountain itself, grown white as a knucklebone, and the singing jewel sat on a cushion at its foot like something beloved.

Jude could hear Wren in it. Not words—worse than words. A melody he'd known all his life, the tune she hummed when she thought no one listened, worn down to a single silver thread and looped forever. Ten years she had hung here, singing herself thin, and the court drank her voice like wine.

"You understand my offer," the Magistrate said. It did not stand. It did not need to. Its shape suggested a tall man the way frost suggests ferns—something that had never been alive but remembered the pattern. "Your gift entire. Every oath you would ever speak, surrendered before it is sworn. In exchange: your sister, whole. Your silence made permanent. No hunter will ever find what no longer exists."

"And I walk out of here hollow," Jude said.

"You walk out of here *safe*." The Magistrate's voice was kind, which was the cruelest thing about it. "You have spent ten years paying for that gift with fear. I am offering to end the payments. Think, swordspeaker. What has your gift ever given you but graves?"

The jewel sang its thread of Wren.

And that was the thing. That was the whole rotten thing. The Magistrate was right, and it knew it was right, and it wore his ten years of hiding like a tailor wears cloth it cut itself. Every word of the offer fit him perfectly because the court beneath the mountain had measured him for a decade—had held his sister's soul up to the light of his secret and watched how he bent around it.

His gift had given him graves. His mentor's name was on a list now because of it. His sister was a jewel because of it. He had spoken three oaths at once on the high road and shattered men who were, most of them, just soldiers doing what their king asked.

But.

But the tollkeeper below had told him one true thing, in her cracked and road-worn way: *an oath unspoken is still yours.* And the Magistrate's bargain wasn't an offer. It was an oath—a demand that he swear away everything he hadn't yet said. And oaths, freely weighed, could be refused. Could be—

Could be unsaid.

"I have a counteroffer," Jude said.

The court stilled. Bone-lit halls ran off in every direction, arches of pale stone stacked with niches, and in the niches: lights. Hundreds of them. Small, warm, restless—the stolen souls of everyone who had ever bargained badly in this place, shelved like jars of honey. Somewhere in those rows was a stubborn old man who had taught a boy to hold his tongue until the words were worth dying for.

"The old law," Jude said. "Yours. Older than your throne. An oath truly spoken cannot be broken—but it can be *unsaid*. Returned to the speaker. You know this is true. It's why you collect souls instead of taking them. Why you bargain instead of seize. Everything in this court runs on consent, Magistrate. Even yours."

Something moved behind the Magistrate's face, slow as a glacier deciding.

"Speak your counteroffer," it said.

"My oldest oath." Jude's mouth had gone dry, but his voice came out level, and he marveled at it. "Spoken when I was fourteen years old, in this court's presence or its hearing—I never knew which. The oath that bound my gift to silence. The one my sister sold her soul to keep unbroken. You've been holding the payment for it for ten years. So I'm calling the debt. I unsay it. All of it comes back to me—every word I swallowed, every year of hiding—and the court releases what it holds against that oath. Her soul. And the others taken under the same law."

"You would be giving up nothing," the Magistrate said softly, "and gaining everything. That is not how courts work."

"It's exactly how *oaths* work," Jude said. "You taught me that yourself, didn't you? Through her. She learned it here and carried it home. *An oath unspoken is finally, truly your own.*"

For the first time, the Pale Magistrate moved. It rose from the rib-throne by increments, like smoke reversing, and the bone-light dimmed throughout the hall—not from anger, Jude understood suddenly, but from attention. The whole mountain was listening now.

"You are asking me to permit the unraveling of my authority," it said. "Every soul shelved in these walls was taken through a spoken bargain. If oaths may be unsaid, then every bargain is provisional. Forever. Do you understand what you propose?"

"I understand," Jude said, "that you already proposed it first. You offered to take every oath I would *ever speak*. You believed I could be emptied. Well. Emptiness cuts both ways."

The silence went on long enough that Jude heard the jewel again—Wren's thread of song, thinner now, fraying. And beneath it, fainter, other threads. A hundred voices in a hundred niches, all singing some small ruined piece of themselves.

He thought about his mentor's hands, scarred like tree bark, folding over a boy's knuckles on a sword hilt. *Say less, mean more.*

"Refuse, then," Jude said. "Take my gift by force if you can. But you can't. Because I haven't sworn anything since I walked in here. And the tollkeeper's chain on me—" he touched his throat, where the road-mark lay cool under his collar, "—binds me to serve the road, and the road runs *through* your court, Magistrate. Check your own law. I am not yours to judge."

The Magistrate looked at him for a long moment.

Then it laughed. There was no sound in it. The bone-light shivered instead.

"Fourteen years old," it said. "Kneeling in the dark above my ceiling, terrified his father's sword would speak without him and bring the hunt down on their house. And he swore so *carefully*. Such a careful boy. Yes, swordspeaker. I heard that oath. I have been holding it, as you say, in escrow—waiting for the day you came down to settle accounts. I only ever wondered whether you would pay or default."

"I'm not defaulting," Jude said. "I'm *unsaying*."

He opened his mouth, and found the old oath waiting where it had lived for fifteen years, coiled behind his teeth like a swallowed hook. He had spoken it kneeling in the dark with snow coming through the shutters, his father dead upstairs, his sister asleep, and he had sworn his gift into silence with the court beneath the mountain as witness—because witness was power, and power was safety, and he had been fourteen and wanted nothing in the world except to be safe.

*I bind my voice to stillness. Let no blade answer me until I break this word, and let the breaking cost me all I love.*

There it was. The last clause. He'd forgotten it—or buried it—because he'd been fourteen and clever and stupid, and clever stupid boys write terms they don't understand. *Let the breaking cost me all I love.* That was why Wren's soul was here. Not seized. *Collected.* The court hadn't stolen her. His own oath had delivered her, ten years ago, the moment she'd gone looking for a way to buy his silence—and the Undercourt had simply invoiced the family.

The Magistrate hadn't lied once. It had never needed to.

Jude's eyes burned. "You let me hate you for this."

"I permitted you accurate conclusions," the Magistrate said. "It amounts to the same thing, in law."

"I unsay it," Jude said. "I unsay the whole of it—the binding, the clause, the witness, the debt. I unsay it freely, fully, before this court and the road and whatever gods still bother watching the Thornfell passes. What was sworn returns to me. What was taken against it goes free. *So I unsay.*"

He spoke the last two words in swordspeech—the old grammar of command, the language blades understood—and the mountain heard them.

It began at his sternum. Fifteen years of swallowed words came home all at once, every gate he'd frozen shut, every order he'd bitten back, every night he'd lain awake with a blade across his knees and *said nothing*, and they tore up through him like a river reclaiming a dry bed. He went down on one knee. He did not stop speaking. The unsaying needed him to say it—all of it, backward, the oath unwinding syllable by syllable out of his blood—and his voice filled the bone-lit halls with a boy's terror and a man's grief and a smuggler's cold arithmetic and a brother's love, none of it commanded anymore, all of it simply *his*.

The niches shattered.

Not all at once—in waves, following his voice outward through the court, each small warm light bursting free of its shelf and rising, and rising, and going out through the arches like a migration of stars. He saw them go: a miller's wife. A deserter. A child too young to have bargained at all, which meant someone had bargained *for* it, and Jude's rage fed another verse into the unsaying.

And one light rose slower than the rest. Stubborn. Unhurried.

It drifted down and hung in front of him, and it was warm, and it was familiar, and for one heartbeat Jude felt bark-scarred hands fold over his knuckles.

*Say less,* said a voice like a hearth going out. *Mean more. You always were a slow learner, boy. Good.*

Then the light rose, unhurried even in freedom, and joined the others streaming up toward the surface and the snow and the sky.

The throne-room stood empty of everything except Jude, kneeling, hoarse, and the Pale Magistrate, which had not moved—and the cushion, which no longer held a jewel.

Wren's thread of song was inside him now. He could feel it, folded in among the returned words, safe, carried. Not freed yet. Freed *with* him—it would come home when he did, or not at all.

"The court stands," the Magistrate said. There was something new in its voice. Not anger. Accounting. "You have emptied my shelves, swordspeaker. But the law that let you do it binds me also. I must ask: what will you do with a gift that answers to no oath at all?"

Jude stood. His knees shook. His voice, when it came, was rough and quiet and entirely his own.

"I'll spend it like a smuggler spends coin," he said. "Carefully. On the people I choose."

He turned for the ascent, and behind him the bone-light guttered, and far above—muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone—he heard the first deep groan of winter closing the passes, and under it, thinner, sharper: horns. The hunt, moving on Millbrook.

Moving on Wren.
