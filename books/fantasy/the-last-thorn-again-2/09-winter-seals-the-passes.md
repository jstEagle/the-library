## 9. Winter Seals the Passes

The tunnels began to die an hour after her brother unsaid his oldest oath.

Wren felt it first in her teeth—a high, thin ringing, like a wineglass about to crack. Then the walls themselves shuddered, and somewhere far below them a sound rolled up through the mountain that she had no name for: not thunder, not rockfall, but something older, as if the Undercourt were being unpronounced.

She had been running for six hours by then. Her arms burned where they cradled Jude's weight against her chest. He was not heavy—he was lighter than he should have been, lighter than a man of thirty-odd years had any right to be, as though when he gave up his gift the mountain had taken the surplus—but he was limp in the way of the deeply unconscious, and his head lolled against her collarbone with every step, and his breath came shallow and slow against her throat.

He hadn't spoken since the court. Not one word. Not even when the Pale Magistrate's jewel-rack shattered and a hundred stolen souls went shrieking up through the dark like birds released from a burning coop. She'd found him kneeling in the wreckage of the bone-lit hall, hands empty, mouth open, eyes fixed on nothing.

"Jude," she'd said. "Jude, we have to go."

Nothing. So she'd hauled him up over her shoulders—she was smaller than him, but ten years of hauling ledgers and copying edicts until her fingers bled had made her strong in ways the court never noticed—and she'd run.

Now she ran still, up through the service stairs cut into the living rock behind the Magistrate's hall, the ones only scribes knew about because scribes were furniture and furniture saw everything. The air grew colder with every landing. That was wrong. It should have warmed as they climbed toward the surface. Instead the cold came down to meet them, sharp and mineral, tasting of iron and snow.

*Winter,* she thought. *It's early.*

Behind her, faint but persistent, came the other sound: boots. Many boots. The hunt had entered the Undercourt through the Tollhouse gate before the collapse sealed it—or perhaps they hadn't needed a gate at all. Perhaps witch-hunters could smell an oathbreaker's wake the way wolves smelled blood. Either way, they were below her now, two or three landings down, moving fast, and their lantern-light licked up the stairwell like something liquid.

Wren stopped on a landing where a side passage branched east, and pressed herself into its mouth, Jude's weight sagging against her, and listened.

"…the sister," said a voice from below, clipped and cold. Captain Vess. She knew the voice from a hundred intercepted orders; she had copied his signature so many times she could forge it blindfolded. "Thornbury's sister. Scribe-second-class. If she's in these tunnels, take her alive. The Magistrate will want to know what else the family has been hiding for ten years."

*The Magistrate is ash,* Wren thought, and felt a fierce, terrible joy at it. *Your master is a smear on the ceiling of his own court, you bastard.*

But she didn't say it. She shifted Jude higher on her shoulder, turned east into the dark, and ran.

* * *

The east passage was the old salt road, the one the Undercourt used before the Tollhouse was built, and it climbed in long switchbacks toward the Thornfell passes. It was also dying. Twice the floor bucked under her like a ship in weather, and once a section of ceiling ahead of her simply ceased—not fell, *ceased*, stone going to dust midair and raining down as grit—and beyond it yawned forty feet of nothing.

She stood at the edge with Jude breathing against her neck and did the arithmetic. Behind her: hunters. Ahead of her: forty feet of gap, a fallen pillar angled across it like a bridge, slick with frost. Above her: winter, arriving early, arriving angry, as though the mountain itself had decided to settle accounts.

"You absolute fool," she whispered into Jude's hair. "You couldn't just pay the man. You couldn't just give up your little party trick and walk out safe. You had to *unsay* things."

His head stirred against her. For a moment—one heartbeat, maybe two—his hand twitched against her arm, fingers curling into her sleeve like a child's. Then it went slack again.

But it was enough. It told her he was in there somewhere. Hollowed, yes—the Magistrate's bargain had gone wrong in ways she was only beginning to understand, and whatever Jude had poured out of himself when he broke his silence-oath, it had been more than the gift—it was more than the gift, but he was in there. And Wren Ashgrove-Thornbury, scribe-second-class of the Crown Court, keeper of the hunt's ledgers, had spent ten years being careful and clever and quiet, and she was done with all three.

She stepped onto the pillar.

It was narrower than a coffin and twice as long. Frost made the stone treacherous; the wind that funneled up through the gap tried to peel her sideways. She walked heel-to-toe, Jude's weight dragging at every step, counting her breaths because counting kept panic in its box. At the halfway point the pillar groaned and dropped an inch, and her stomach dropped with it, and she heard—behind her, back along the passage—the hunters reach the gap and stop.

"Don't," Vess's voice called across the dark, almost reasonable. "Scribe. Whatever they promised you, whatever your brother swore—the court can be generous. Names can be restored."

Names, she thought. As if that were the coin.

"My name," she called back, not turning around, "is Wren Thornbury. Write it down. I want it legible when they hang you for serving a dead magistrate."

Then the pillar shook again, harder, and she stopped talking and ran the rest of it, four steps and a lunge, and hit solid rock on the far side hard enough to drive the breath from her lungs. Behind her the pillar cracked, tilted, and went down into the dark with a sound like a struck bell. The shouting on the other side became distant very quickly.

She lay there a moment, cheek against frozen stone, Jude's heart beating faintly against her ribs, hers hammering against his.

*That's it,* she thought. *No way back. No court. No career. No name worth having.* She found, to her surprise, that she was smiling. It hurt her cracked lips. She smiled anyway.

* * *

The last mile was surface, and the surface wanted her dead.

The blizzard had come down off the peaks while they were underground, and it met them at the tunnel mouth like a wall with intent. Snow moved horizontally. The sky and the pass had become the same white roaring thing. Wren got Jude's arm over her shoulders and took the first step and learned immediately that the mountain above had no interest in mercy.

The wind took her breath and gave her ice. Snow filled her boots within fifty paces. Jude's body, half-frozen already, was dead weight in every sense, and the pass climbed, and climbed, and there was no shelter that she could see except—

There. A shape in the white. A cairn, old shepherd-work, waist-high, with a lean-to roof of flat stones. She'd marked it herself on the smuggling maps she'd copied for the hunt two winters ago and then quietly mislabeled, sending patrols three miles wide of it. Ten years of small treasons, all of them for him.

She dragged him into the lee of the cairn and collapsed beside him and put her back against the stones and held him against her chest, and for a while she simply breathed, and let the blizzard howl, and listened to the two heartbeats under her own.

Below them, faint through the storm, she heard the hunters reach the tunnel mouth and find it buried. Rockfall had sealed the salt road behind her; whether by the mountain's doing or the collapse of everything her brother had unmade, she couldn't say. The voices argued, thinned, scattered. Some would go around. Some would wait. But none of them were climbing yet.

Not tonight. Tonight the mountain had chosen sides.

"Wren."

She went rigid. The word was barely a word—a scrape of sound, cracked, rusted from disuse—but it was his voice, and it came from six inches away.

Jude's eyes were open. Grey, unfocused, fixed on the middle distance. His lips were blue and barely moving.

"Here," she said. "I'm here. Don't talk. Save it."

"Did—" A swallow. "Did it work?"

She looked at him—at this hollowed, voiceless stranger who had given away the thing that made him hunted and gotten nothing back but freedom, whatever that weighed—and she thought about lying, because she was good at lying, she'd done it professionally for a decade. Then she thought about what he'd done in the court beneath the mountain, and decided he'd earned better than comfort.

"It worked," she said. "They're free. All of them. Master Corvel's soul too—I saw it go up. It sang, Jude. It sounded like him." Her throat closed. "You broke the whole rotten court with one sentence. Do you understand that? One sentence, after ten years of silence."

Something moved in his face. Not quite a smile. Grief and relief tangled together, neither winning.

"And you?" he rasped.

"What about me?"

"They'll know. Your name. Your post. Everything."

Wren leaned her head back against the cairn stones and watched the blizzard erase the world. Somewhere below, men with warrants were saying her name into the wind. Somewhere south, in warm rooms, clerks were striking her from the rolls, unpicking her life stitch by stitch with the same tidy efficiency she'd once prided herself on. By spring there would be nothing left of Scribe Wren Ashgrove but a file with a red string tied round it.

Ten years ago, she had sold her soul to buy her brother's silence. Tonight she'd spent the rest of herself—name, career, future—to buy back what he'd lost setting her free. Between the two transactions, nothing remained of either of them that the crown owned. The ledger, at last, balanced at zero.

"Good," she said.

Jude blinked at her, slow as an owl.

"I spent my whole life being careful," she told him. "Careful was a cage with better upholstery. I'm done." She pulled his arm tighter around her shoulders. "Come spring, there's a pass east of here the patrols don't watch because I've spent two years making sure their maps are wrong. There's iron to move and silver to move and roads that need running, and I know every toll, every blind curve, every lazy garrison captain between here and the coast. You're going to need work, brother. You're useless now at anything requiring a vocabulary."

A sound came out of him then—cracked, rusty, unmistakable. A laugh. Small and broken and real.

They sat in the mouth of the storm while night came down and winter sealed the passes behind them, and Wren counted heartbeats, and planned routes, and did not look south even once.

Far below, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone and a rising sea of snow, something in the Undercourt's drowned halls began—very softly—to sing. Not the Magistrate's song. Something new. Something with her brother's cadence in it, and her own name threaded through, waiting.

By morning, the singing had reached the surface.
