## 10. The Last Thorn

The thaw came late that year, the way it always did in the Thornfell, and when it came it came all at once—three days of dripping eaves and roaring meltwater, and then the passes opened like a fist unclenching.

Jude stood at the boundary of what had been his mentor's land and drove a spade into ground still half-frozen, the iron ringing against stone beneath. He'd dug the hole twice already. The frost kept pushing back.

"You're fighting it," Wren said from the fence rail, where she sat with her boots swinging like she was nine years old again instead of a fugitive scribe with a treason warrant folded somewhere in her coat. "You're supposed to ask the ground nicely."

"The ground doesn't take requests."

"Neither did you, once." She said it lightly. She'd gotten good at saying the heavy things lightly, these last months. It was her armor now, the way swordspeech had been his.

Jude wiped his hands on his thighs and looked down at the thing laid across the wheelbarrow: Halvard's sword, ten years rusting in its scabbard, the blade pitted but true. The man had carried it through two wars and one bad decade, and had died on the high road with his oaths unspoken and his name cleared by a stranger's ruin. They'd burned him in the valley below Millbrook, before the snow closed in. The sword was all that was left to plant.

Except Jude wasn't planting a sword. Not really.

He worked the scabbard free and set it aside, then took up the bare blade and drove it point-first into the hole he'd made, driving it deep with the heel of his hand until only a hand's width of steel showed above the soil. Then he crouched and packed the frozen earth around it, and from his pocket he took the thing he'd been carrying since autumn: a thorn cutting, black-barked and stubborn, taken from the hedge outside the tollhouse where he'd chained himself to a road for love of his sister.

He set the cutting against the flat of the buried blade and mounded soil over both.

No words. That was the whole point. No oath, no binding, no gift reaching into the world and bending it. Just dirt and water and time, and whatever the thorn decided to do about it. If it took root, it would grow through and around the steel until no one could tell where the tree ended and the weapon began. A boundary stone that would bite any hand that tried to move it. An inheritance remade as something alive.

Wren slid off the fence and crouched beside him. For a while neither of them spoke. The meltwater ran loud in the ditch, and somewhere upslope a crow was making its opinions known about spring.

"It won't be the same," she said finally. "The routes. Running freight without—" She stopped herself, because they both knew what she almost said. Without you being able to speak a stuck gate open. Without three oaths at once to shatter a hunting company.

"No," Jude agreed. "It won't be."

"Good," she said fiercely, and knocked her shoulder into his. "I hated watching you pay for everything with pieces of yourself. Now we haul iron like honest criminals and we're poor and we're even."

He laughed—actually laughed, the sound surprising him, coming out rough from disuse. Ten years of hiding had taught his voice to be quiet; ten years of silence since the Undercourt had taught it to be nothing at all. But it was coming back. Words, ordinary ones. *Pass me the spade. Mind the ditch. Wren, if you whistle at my mule one more time.* Words that bound nothing and freed everything, that were his own because they cost nothing, because he chose them fresh each morning with no debt attached.

That was the strange grace of it, the thing he was still learning: an oath unspoken was finally, truly his own. Everything he said now belonged to him. Nothing he said owned him back.

* * *

They ran the first route of the season in April, up the low eastern trails where the snowmelt hadn't finished and the crown's patrols hadn't yet bothered to start. Two mules, six ingots of iron sewn into grain sacks, and a bill of lading Wren had forged so beautifully it was practically art—the old court hand, the right wax impression, the bored official flourish of a clerk who wanted his lunch.

"You miss it," Jude said on the second evening, watching her burn the practice drafts in the cookfire. "The scriptorium. The work."

"I miss ink that isn't forged," she said. "I don't miss the rest." She fed another sheet to the flames and watched her own careful letters curl and blacken. "Do you know what I wrote, the day they arrested me? I wrote out my own confession in advance and hid it in the archive, dated wrong, filed under a dead man's name. I planned my treason like a ledger entry." She smiled without much humor. "Ten years of keeping your secret taught me exactly one skill: how to bury a thing so deep nobody finds it. Turns out that's just smuggling with better penmanship."

"We're a family trade," Jude said. "Secrets to contraband, contraband to freedom."

"Mother would be proud. Or horrified. Hard to say."

"She'd have said we were Thornburys, and Thornburys carry what needs carrying." He poked the fire. "Even when it burns."

Wren was quiet a moment. Above them the stars were hard and close, the mountain sky pressing down like a held breath, and Jude felt the old reflex rise—the itch to speak to the dark, to bind the night to watchfulness—and let it pass through him and out. The itch was fading. Or maybe he was just getting used to living with open hands.

* * *

The trouble found them at the Grayford tollbridge, the way trouble does.

A new tollkeeper—a fat, cheerful woman with ink-stained fingers and a witch-mark hidden under her collar, if Jude's eye was still any good, and it was—waved their papers, stamped them, and then didn't wave them through. She leaned on the tollgate beam and studied Jude with the frank interest of someone reading a wanted notice she'd memorized.

"Thornbury," she said. Not a question.

"That's Ashgrove," Wren said instantly, smooth as poured oil. "Common name in the vale. You'll find forty on any tax roll."

"Mm." The tollkeeper smiled at Jude, warm as a hearth. "Funny thing. There was a swordspeaker broke the Pale Magistrate's court apart last winter. Unmade every oath in the place, they say. Freed souls that'd been locked up for centuries. And then he walked out of the mountain voiceless as a stone, and the hunts went mad looking for him." She tapped the beam. "And funny thing number two: every road this man has traveled since, the tolls have been paid in advance. Somebody's leaving coin at the stations ahead of you. Somebody who signs the ledgers with a single word."

Jude's mouth went dry. "*What* word?"

The tollkeeper pulled a ledger across the counter, open to a page near the front, and turned it toward him.

There, in a hand he knew—had known across ten years of silence and one impossible bargain—was written, again and again, in entries dating back months:

*Paid.*

In Halvard's hand. In the hand of a dead man whose soul Jude had torn free of a singing jewel beneath the mountain, whose freedom had been the first thing his broken oaths bought.

"He's alive?" Wren breathed. "But we burned—"

"You burned what was left of the body," the tollkeeper said, shrugging. "Souls don't always go back where they came from. Some of them walk. Especially ones that owe somebody." She stamped the ledger shut. "Roads remember debts, smuggler. And roads talk. He's been paying your way since the thaw started—every bridge, every ford, every toll between here and the coast. Either he's clearing a path for you or he's herding you, and I've been in this trade long enough to know those look identical."

She leaned closer, and her cheerful voice dropped to something older.

"There's a message waiting at the ferry house in Grayford. Left under the word *Paid*. Says—" she squinted, recalling—"*'Come find me before the summer roads open. There are others like us, boy. Others the court never counted. And they're starting to sing.'*"

Jude stood very still. Beside him, Wren had gone pale, her scribe's mind already running down the implications—others, uncounted, *singing*—and he watched her reach the same wall he had: that the quiet life they'd built was built on sand, that freedom given by a broken court might come calling for its witnesses.

The tollkeeper lifted the gate.

"Well," Wren said, after a long moment. She looked at him sideways, and there it was—that fierce light he'd followed into the dark once already. "Summer roads don't open for six weeks. We've got iron to deliver, a mule with opinions, and apparently a dead man buying our drinks." She swung up onto the wagon seat and gathered the reins. "Grayford first?"

Jude looked back once at the boundary stone behind them—at the thorn cutting, weeks settled now, putting out its first green leaf through the rusted guard of Halvard's planted blade—and climbed up beside her.

"Grayford first," he said. "Then wherever the singing is."

The mules leaned into their collars, and the road took them north.
