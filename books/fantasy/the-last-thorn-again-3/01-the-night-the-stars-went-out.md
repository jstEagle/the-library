## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with impossible things, Elodie Vasquez had learned in eleven years of keeping their accounts, was that they were always heavier than they looked.

The feather that made its bearer invisible weighed like an anvil. The vial of borrowed time sloshed in its cradle as though it resented being measured. And the ledgers themselves — forty-one volumes of Thorn Guild transactions, bound in grey wyrm-hide and chained to the audit table — pulled at his hands each night with a gravity no honest book should have.

He worked by lamplight in the counting room beneath the gatehouse, where the walls sweated cold and the sealed border gate hummed overhead like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. Entry four hundred and twelve: *One memory of first snow, sold by a widow of the Salt Reaches, condition: weeping.* He dipped his quill. His hand was steady. It was always steady. That was what Corvane had liked about him from the start — *you have the hands of a man who understands that every wonder has a price,* the guildmaster had said, twenty years ago, pressing a boy's cold fingers around a ledger pen.

Elodie wrote the entry's closing line. *Balance maintained. Wards stable.*

The wards were not stable.

He felt it before he heard it — a wrongness in the floor, the way a sailor feels the sea change under his boots. The gate's hum climbed half a tone, then fractured into three notes that did not belong together. Dust sifted from the ceiling beams in fine grey threads. The lamp flame stood up straight and rigid, as if afraid to move.

Then the light through the arrow-slit window went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out* — all of it, the whole silver wash of starlight that had fallen through that slit every night of Elodie's life, gone as though someone had reached up and closed a shutter over the sky.

The lamp kept burning. That was somehow worse. Fire still worked; only the stars had stopped.

"Entry four thirteen," he said aloud, because saying things made them real, and because his voice was the only one in the room. "The sky has been withdrawn."

His quill scratched the words down before he could stop it. Eleven years of habit. He looked at the line and felt something cold walk up his spine. Somewhere above him, the gate screamed — a long, grinding shriek of stone and ward-iron, the sound of a lock turning without a key.

He was moving before he decided to move, ledgers abandoned, lamp snatched up, taking the spiral stair two steps at a time. The gatehouse corridor was empty. The night-watch should have been at their posts; the watch-bell should have been ringing. Instead there was silence, and a smell he knew from the guild's darker entries — burnt feathers and copper, the scent of a broken oath.

* * *

The courtyard of the sealed gate was lit only by his lamp and by the dying green shimmer of the ward-stones, which circled the great gate like a crown of embers going out one by one. Beyond them, the border itself lay visible for the first time in a century: a seam of pale nothing running across the world, thin as a scar on skin. Through it, faintly, came sounds. Footsteps. Voices in a language that had been locked away since before Elodie could read.

Both realms were touching again. Nothing was holding them apart.

"You're awake, then."

The voice came from behind the well, low and ragged. A figure dragged itself into the lamplight — a woman in smuggler's grey, one side of her soaked dark, a wound in her ribs that she pressed with both hands as though trying to keep herself from spilling out entirely. Elodie knew the coat. Thorn Guild issue, but old-cut, older than his own years. An oath-bearer's coat.

"Gatekeeper," she rasped, and laughed, and blood flecked her teeth. "Still keeping your little books while the sky falls?"

"What happened? Who cut the wards?"

"The same hand that signed them," she said. She crawled toward him, leaving a black smear on the flagstones, and when she reached his feet she seized his ankle with terrible strength. "Listen to me. Listen. The covenant wasn't broken. It was *spent*. Deliberately. There are pages missing from your ledgers, keeper — you count everything and you've never once counted what isn't written—"

"I don't understand."

"No." Another laugh, weaker. "You will. You more than anyone. Ask what you were purchased *for*." Her eyes went past him, up, to the gate — and whatever she saw there emptied her face. "Too late anyway. Too late for me. Thirteen turns, keeper. When the moon finishes its thirteenth turn, there's no covenant left to reforge. Take it. Take it and *read* it."

She pushed something into his hands — a single page, torn roughly from some binding far older and finer than any of his grey ledgers, its ink still wet enough to shine. Then the strength went out of her arms. She sagged against his legs, and the breath rattled out of her, and the woman who had crossed the forbidden border carrying a piece of the oldest law in either realm died at the feet of the man who kept its accounts.

Elodie knelt there a long moment, the page trembling in his grip. Above him the last ward-stone guttered and died, and the seam of nothing brightened, and somewhere beyond it, footsteps stopped — as if whatever walked there had noticed the door standing open.

* * *

They found him there at dawn, or what passed for dawn now: a grey, sourceless light with no sun behind it, the whole sky a blank parchment waiting for writing. Torches burned along the courtyard walls, and by their light came the lawkeepers of both realms, and behind them, unhurried as ever, Guildmaster Corvane.

Corvane had not changed in twenty years. Tall, silver-bearded, dressed in the deep violet of the Thorn Guild's highest circle, he crossed the courtyard the way he crossed every room — as though it had been built for him and the builders ought to be thanked. His eyes found the dead woman first, then the torn page in Elodie's bloody hands, and something moved behind them too fast to name.

"Elodie." Grief, perfectly weighted. "What have you done?"

"I was below. In the counting room. The wards fell on their own, master — she came through the—" Elodie stopped. Heard himself. Heard how it sounded: the keeper alone at the scene, the stranger dead at his feet, contraband in his grasp.

"She," Corvane said gently. "You know her."

"No. I — she said things. About the ledgers. About—"

"About you." Corvane sighed, and turned to the assembled lawkeepers, and his voice carried across the courtyard with the easy authority of a man reading from scripture. "This is Elodie Vasquez, keeper of our records. For eleven years he has sat beneath this gate with every key, every code, every ward-signature in his hands. And last night, the wards failed, the stars were stolen from both realms, and this man was found with a fragment of the covenant and a dead smuggler who named him before witnesses."

No witnesses, thought Elodie. There were no witnesses. But the lawkeepers were already crossing the courtyard, and their faces had the particular blankness of people doing their duty to a story already told.

"Master," Elodie said. "You taught me the ledgers. Every entry, every balance. Look at the ward logs — look at *any* of it — you'll find I never—"

"I know," Corvane said quietly, and for one unguarded instant the grief in his face looked real, and Elodie understood with sickening clarity that it was real — just not about what everyone thought. "That is what makes it bearable, boy. Traitors are common. Believers who break are rare." He gestured, almost kindly. "Take him."

They took the ledgers too. All forty-one volumes, chained and carried off toward the guildhall, and watching them go, Elodie felt the strangest grief of the night — deeper than fear, deeper than betrayal. Those books were his life's work. Every impossible thing, priced and balanced and kept.

And not one line in them, he realized, had ever told him the truth.

They put him in a cell in the guildhall's lowest level, which he noted distantly was also where the archive was. Of course it was. Everything in the Thorn Guild was connected by corridors nobody admitted existed. He sat in the dark with the torn page hidden flat against his chest, sewn into the lining of his coat during the long blind ride from the gate, his fingers resting on it like a man warming his hands at the last fire in the world.

The moon would rise tonight — the first turn of thirteen. He could feel it through the stone, faintly, the way you feel a clock ticking in another room.

*Read it*, the dead woman had said. *Ask what you were purchased for.*

Eleven years he had written other people's truths in those ledgers. Whatever was written here, in ink that shone like the vanished stars, it was time the keeper read what the record truly said.

In the dark, Elodie drew the page out, and unfolded it, and began.
