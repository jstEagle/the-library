## 3. Thirteen Turns of the Moon

*Elodie*

The moon was a nail-paring in the sky, and it was dying.

Twelve turns since the stars went out. Twelve turns of running through a borderland that had begun to fray at its seams — roads that looped back on themselves, rivers that flowed uphill at dusk, villages where the children had stopped dreaming entirely. Elodie felt every turn of the moon in his bones now, like a clock winding down inside his chest.

And he was not alone in the dark. He hadn't been for three nights.

"You're limping again," said the voice behind him, low and dry as old paper. Sera Quill, lawkeeper of the Ashen Court, sworn to drag him back in chains — walking three paces behind him because she refused to walk beside a fugitive, but walking nonetheless.

"I'm resting my leg."

"For the fourth hour?"

"The leg requires dedication."

She didn't laugh. She never laughed, though twice he'd caught something flicker at the corner of her mouth before duty strangled it. They moved along the ridge above the drowned village of Merrow's Bend, where the third oath-bearer was said to hide: Mother Vess, once the covenant's hand of mercy, now a recluse who traded in memories the way other women sold eggs.

Below them, lanterns moved through the ruins. Too many. Moving wrong — in pairs, flanking, the way Corvane's mercenaries swept ground they meant to own.

"They found her first," Elodie breathed.

"Then we go in loud," Sera said, drawing her blade. The steel sang a note that had no business existing, given the sky had been silent for a year. "Or we go in smart."

"Smart is louder than you think."

He pulled the torn page from his coat — the covenant fragment, the dead man's testament — and pressed it flat against the standing stone at the ridge's crest. The stone remembered what it was made to do. Light crawled up from beneath the moss like veins catching fire, and across the whole drowned valley, every shard of covenant-oath still bonded to earth answered: a web of pale gold threads stitching ruin to ruin, marking Vess's hiding place with a burning sigil.

It also marked *them*. Every mercenary in the valley turned toward the light.

"Worth it?" Sera asked, already moving.

"We'll know in a minute."

They went down into Merrow's Bend like a dropped torch.

* * *

*Sera*

Sera Quill had arrested forty-one people in her career. She had never once fought beside one.

That was the thought occupying the small, stubborn part of her mind not devoted to staying alive, as she put her shoulder through the door of the bell-tower and found Mother Vess surrounded by four of Corvane's men — and found Elodie already among them, fighting like a man reading a book only he could see, each movement answering an attack half a heartbeat before it came.

The ledger-keeper fought like prophecy. It unsettled her more than the moonless sky did.

She took the nearest mercenary from behind, hilt to temple, clean and quiet. The second turned; she let him commit, then broke his wrist against the wall and put him down. The third reached Elodie's blind side —

"Down," Sera snapped, and threw her knife past his ear. It took the man in the shoulder and pinned him to the doorframe, screaming.

Elodie straightened, breathing hard, and looked at her over the wreckage of the room. "You just saved me."

"I saved the arrest." She retrieved her knife. "You're worth more alive. The Court pays double for a traitor delivered breathing."

Mother Vess rose from behind the altar like smoke given a spine. She was older than the records said — older, Elodie would tell him later, than any record allowed — and when she saw the torn page in his hand, she made a sound like a hinge giving way after a century.

"So it's finally come due," she said. "I wondered which of us would live long enough to carry it."

"Then you'll come with us," Elodie said. "Two more bearers remain. The smith Oren Hale, and—"

"And the twelfth turn wanes, child." Vess touched his cheek with fingers cold as wellwater. "I can feel it counting. We have until the moon finishes dying. After that, no gathering will matter."

Outside, the sky pulsed — a slow, arrhythmic throb where stars should have hung, like a wound remembering it used to beat.

* * *

*Elodie*

They gathered the rest like sparks leaping a firebreak.

Oren Hale came out of the iron country swinging a hammer wrapped in covenant-chain, having burned his own forge rather than let Corvane's men take the oath-metal hidden in it. The last bearer was a surprise that took Elodie's knees out from under him: Tamsin Vale, the guild's own quartermaster, the woman who had taught fourteen-year-old Elodie how to balance a ledger — revealed now as the covenant's final sworn voice, hiding all these years one corridor away from the betrayal itself.

By the time the five of them stood on the guildhall's outer steps, the moon was a sliver, thin as a knife's edge, and the border between realms had worn so thin that both skies showed through at once — the ash-grey heavens of the far realm bleeding into their own like ink into water.

The guildhall doors were open.

"He wants you to come in," Sera said quietly. "This is a throat, Elodie. Not a doorway."

"I know." He gripped the torn page. "But throats are where the truth gets spoken."

They walked in together.

Corvane sat in the high seat of the guildhall — the seat Elodie had polished as an apprentice, the seat that had seemed to him then the very throne of honor. Around the hall stood thirty of his mercenaries, and bound kneeling in the center, beaten bloody, were a dozen guild-folk who had refused to swear to him. Old friends. Faces Elodie had eaten beside, argued beside, trusted.

"Ledger-keeper," Corvane said warmly, and the warmth was the worst thing in the room. "You've brought me the last oath-bearers gift-wrapped. I confess I expected more running."

"You knew I'd come." Elodie's voice didn't shake. He was proud of that. "You built everything so I'd come."

"I built everything so you'd *understand*." Corvane rose. "Do you truly believe I sabotaged the wards out of greed? Out of ambition? Sit down, boy, and let me read you your own entry."

He gestured, and a clerk — trembling, tear-streaked — laid the master ledger upon the table. Corvane opened it to a page near the beginning, older than any Elodie had catalogued, written in an ink that shimmered like starlight trapped in tar.

"*Transaction the First,*" Corvane read. "*One gate, failing. One covenant, fraying. Price paid: a child born of both realms, blood of either shore, to serve as the living seal.*"

The hall went very quiet.

"You weren't raised by the guild, Elodie," Corvane said gently. "You were *purchased* by it. The gate was built around your birth. Your cradle was set in the threshold-stone. Every ward, every seal, every binding that held two realms apart — they all anchored to you. And when the covenant frayed twelve turns ago, the old keepers chose to spend their anchor rather than renew it. To break the pact and let the realms bleed together rather than give up the power it gave them."

"No," Elodie whispered.

"I told them no too." Corvane spread his hands, a martyr's gesture. "So they named me traitor first. Everything you've run from this year — you ran from *their* sin, not mine. I only wanted to find another way. And there isn't one." His eyes glittered. "There was only ever you. You are the seal, boy. The gate doesn't need your loyalty. It needs your *blood*, your name, your memory — poured into the threshold until nothing of Elody Vasquez remains. That is the price of the stars. That is the price it has always been."

The torn page burned cold in Elodie's hand. Around him, the kneeling guild-folk lifted their faces. Mother Vess wept without sound. Tamsin Vale looked at him the way one looks at a ledger entry one wrote long ago and hoped never to reread.

And the moon began its final turn.

* * *

*Sera*

Later — much later, in years that might or might not exist anymore — Sera Quill would try to name the moment she broke her oath. She never managed it. There wasn't a moment. There was only a slow tipping, a year-long lean that became, in that hall, a fall.

Her oath was simple: the law of the Ashen Court, which held that borders were sacred and those who breached them were criminals. By that law, Elodie Vasquez was guilty. By that law, she should bind him and drag him home.

But she had seen the ledgers. She had helped him read them, in archive-candlelight, while telling herself she was only gathering evidence. And evidence, properly read, said this: the law itself was an entry in someone's ledger. The Court's authority rested on the same broken covenant, bought with the same stolen child.

Truth, then. Truth over oath. It was not a noble feeling. It felt like stepping off a cliff in the dark.

Corvane's men moved first — because Corvane, seeing her face, understood before anyone. "Quill," he said softly. "Don't."

She drew her blade anyway, and its strange ringing note filled the guildhall, and she stepped forward until she stood at Elodie's left shoulder, exactly where she'd refused to stand on every road they'd walked together.

"I'm placing myself under arrest," she announced to the hall, clear and carrying, "alongside the traitor Elodie Vasquez. Let the record show we are both guilty of the same crime: believing what the ledgers actually say."

For one heartbeat, silence.

Then Oren Hale raised his hammer. Then Tamsin Vale drew the quartermaster's keys she'd carried hidden for twenty years. Then Mother Vess spoke a word older than either realm, and the bound guild-folk tore free of their ropes, and the hall erupted.

Sera fought back to back with a man she'd hunted across a year and a broken sky, and if her heart hammered harder than battle justified, she filed that entry away for later — for a later that Elodie's white, resolved face told her might never come.

Because above them, through the shattered skylight, the moon slid into its last shadow, and Elodie Vasquez began to walk toward the guildhall's inner door — the one that opened onto the threshold-stone — as if he already knew the price, and had decided, somewhere in the year of his running, to pay it.

"Elodie," Sera called after him, and hated how it sounded — like begging. "What are you going to do?"

He turned. In the light of the dying moon, he almost smiled.

"What I was purchased for," he said. "I'm going home."

Behind the inner door, the threshold-stone began — faintly, patiently, like a heart waiting to be needed — to glow.
