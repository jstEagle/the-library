## 4. The Living Thorn

The guildhall smelled of burnt parchment and iron. Where Corvane had fallen, the ink of the covenant lay pooled across the mosaic floor like a wound that refused to close, black and glistening, drinking the light of the few candles still burning. The thirteenth moon hung in the shattered skylight above us, swollen and wrong, the last star in an empty sky.

Elodie knelt beside the spent pages. Twelve turns of the moon, twelve oaths gathered — the ferryman's bone-white sigil, the salt-witch's knotwork, the twins' mirrored marks — all of them inked into the covenant's spine, all of it useless now. The final page was blank. It had always been blank. That was the secret Corvane had died keeping: the covenant did not bind the realms to each other. It bound them to a person.

To him.

"The ink is spent," Sera said behind him. Her voice was steady, but her hands were not; he could see the tremor as she sheathed her lawkeeper's blade. She had broken her oath an hour ago, standing between Elodie and Corvane's last mercenary, and the breaking had changed something in her face. She looked lighter. She looked terrified. "Whatever you're thinking, Elodie, say it slowly."

He touched the pool of black ink. It was warm. Of course it was warm. It had been waiting for him since the night he was born, since the night two realms had signed their peace in the only currency both understood: a child who belonged to neither, and so to both.

*Transaction recorded,* his own ledger-keeping hand had written, years ago, without knowing what he transcribed. *One seal, living. Custody: the Guild.*

The gate at the border was three miles east, its arch stripped of wards, humming with a hunger he could feel in his teeth. He could feel it because it had been made from him. Or he from it. The distinction had stopped mattering somewhere around the twelfth turn.

"Say it," Sera said again.

"I'm going to give it what it wants." He stood. His legs held, which surprised him. "Blood. Name. Memory. All of it."

"No."

"You've read the page, Sera. You know what happens at moonrise if no one stands in the archway wearing the covenant. Both realms come apart. Not with fire. With *forgetting*. Every border, every law, every promise anyone ever kept across that gate — unraveled. Your realm forgets mine exists. Mine forgets yours. And everyone we gathered — the ferryman, the witch, the twins — they dissolve into the space between."

"There has to be another way."

"There isn't. I've checked every ledger in the archive twice." He almost smiled. "You know me. I check things twice."

Sera crossed the hall and took his face in her hands, the way no lawkeeper should touch a fugitive, the way no friend should touch a man already half-gone. "Then hear my counter-offer. You don't walk into that gate alone. I carry your book. I keep your record. Whatever's left of you on the other side of this — someone will remember it *properly*. Not as a myth. As an entry. Dated, witnessed, true."

"That's not how memory works."

"It's how *mine* works," she said. "I've been hunting you for twelve turns of a dead moon, Elodie Vasquez. Do you think I'll forget you just because the stars come back?"

Outside, the wind changed. The thirteenth turn had begun.

* * *

They walked the border road together, and the world walked with them in pieces. Trees along the verge stood half in leaf and half in ash, unable to choose a season. A river they crossed ran backward for ten paces, then forgot itself and ran forward again. In the fields, farmers who had traded with the other realm for generations stared at their own barns as if seeing them for the first time.

The forgetting had started. It would finish by moonrise.

At the crest of the last hill, the gate rose against the empty sky — black stone, older than either realm, its arch carved with thirteen hollows where the oath-bearers' sigils were meant to sit. Twelve now glimmered faintly, the gathered oaths he'd carried stitched into his skin since the guildhall, rising through his arms like light under frost. The thirteenth hollow waited at the keystone. Shaped like nothing. Shaped like a man.

Sera stopped at the boundary stone. Lawkeepers could not cross uninvited; some rules survived even the end of rules. "Elodie."

He turned. For a long moment neither of them spoke, because everything worth saying had already been entered into evidence.

"Your ledger," he said finally, drawing the worn book from his coat and pressing it into her hands. "Start a new page. Put today's date. Write: *Elodie Vasquez, keeper of the sealed gate, keeper of ledgers, born of two realms. Gave himself so both could keep their own.* Then sign it. Witnessed entries are binding. You taught me that."

"I taught you nothing. You read my reports when you thought I wasn't looking."

"I did everything thoroughly," he said. "It's the one thing I'm taking with me."

The moon touched the keystone.

He stepped into the arch.

* * *

It did not hurt, exactly. It *recorded* him.

The gate took his blood first — a thin line opening along his palm, and the black ink rising up the stones to meet it, page meeting pen. Then his name: he felt it lifted out of him syllable by syllable, *E-lo-die*, each one set into the masonry like a jewel into a setting, until the sound of it belonged to the border and not to the boy. Last came memory, and this was gentler than he feared. His mother's lullaby went first, given gladly, becoming the hum of the wards. Then Corvane's face, the man who raised him, the man who betrayed him — he let the gate have the whole tangle of it, love and ruin together, because a seal that keeps only the good promises fails at the bad ones. Then the guildhall. The archive. The smell of candle smoke and old vellum. The night the stars went out.

He kept one thing. Just one. Her voice saying *I'll remember you properly.* The gate tried to take it and he held it the way he had once held a ledger shut against the rain, and after a long moment, the gate — which was also him, which had always also been him — relented.

In the thirteenth hollow, light kindled.

Above the arch, the sky remembered itself. One star, small and hard, punched through the dark like a needle through cloth. Then another. Then a constellation he had catalogued a hundred times in the margins of shipping manifests, back when he believed impossible things were only inventory. They came back not all at once but one by one, each arrival marked by a sound like a page turning, and the two realms settled against each other along the border like two hands learning to hold.

On the boundary stone, Sera opened the ledger to a fresh page and wrote, in a lawkeeper's careful hand, while the stars came home:

*Entry the first of the new covenant. The gate stands. The gate is Elodie Vasquez. Witnessed and sworn.*

She signed it. Then she sat down on the cold stone with her back against the arch, inside the border's reach now, invited or simply permitted, and began recording everything else — the ferryman's oath, the witch's knot, the names of the fallen, the crimes of Corvane, justice kept outside the law because the law had forgotten how.

And deep in the stone, in the place where a keeper used to be, something listened to the scratch of her pen and found that it was enough. That it would always be enough, for as long as she kept writing, for as long as travelers crossed beneath him and traders swore their strange oaths and children asked why the thirteenth star burned brighter than the rest.

But far to the north, past the last lit farmstead, where the returned stars cast shadows in the wrong direction, something old turned over in its sleep — something the original covenant had never been meant to wake — and the newest entry in the ledger of the world had not yet been written.
