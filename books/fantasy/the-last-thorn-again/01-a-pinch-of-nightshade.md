## 1. A Pinch of Nightshade

The soup arrived at the king's table looking like forgiveness: pale gold, flecked with chervil, steam curling upward in a way that suggested comfort rather than consequence.

Cyrus Dravenmoor stood at her station along the east wall of the banquet hall, hands folded, face arranged into the pleasant blankness that had kept her alive for ten years. She was the crown's food-taster, which meant she was also the most important person in the room and the least noticed, a combination she had spent a decade perfecting the way other people perfected a lute or a knife-throw.

The under-steward carried the taster's bowl to her on a silver salver, moving with the bored efficiency of a man who had done this four hundred times and would do it four hundred more. He did not look at her face. Nobody looked at her face. That was the point.

She lifted the spoon.

The first taste was leek and cream, a whisper of nutmeg, salt from the eastern pans. Good soup. Honest soup. Her tongue catalogued it automatically, the way a customs officer's eye catalogued faces—leek, cream, nutmeg, salt—and then it found the thing that didn't belong.

Not in the flavor. In the texture underneath the flavor. A faint grit at the back of the throat, like sand in an oyster that hadn't gotten around to making a pearl yet.

Cyrus swallowed and did not react.

*White arsenic,* she thought. *Merchant grade, cut with flour to stretch it. Maybe half a grain in the whole tureen—not enough to kill outright, but enough over weeks to make a king sickly and pliable while his physicians chased their tails.*

That was almost elegant. That was almost professional. She'd have admired it if her own body weren't already busy doing what it always did: breaking the poison down, molecule by molecule, with the quiet mechanical indifference of a mill grinding grain. The arsenic would be gone from her blood before the under-steward reached the far wall.

But there was something else.

Beneath the arsenic, so faint she might have imagined it, was a taste she had no name for. It wasn't bitter or sweet or sour. It was *old*. It tasted the way the air tasted inside the mountain shrines her mother had taken her through as a child, before the old gods went silent and the shrines filled up with dust and tourists. It tasted like standing in a room where someone had just finished praying.

Her scars prickled—the long thin ones along her forearms, the ones she told physicians were from a childhood fever. They prickled the way they sometimes did before storms, except there was no storm coming. The sky outside the banquet hall windows was a fat, cloudless blue.

Cyrus took a second spoonful. Confirmed both poisons. Took a third, because a taster who flinched at two spoonfuls was a taster who got questions asked, and questions were the one thing she could not afford.

Then she set down the bowl, caught the under-steward's eye, and gave him the smallest possible nod. *Clear.*

The tureen went out. The king ate. The court laughed at somebody's joke about the ambassador from Vell and moved on to the fish course, which Cyrus also tasted, and which was merely over-salted.

Nobody died. Nobody knew. By tomorrow morning the king would feel slightly unwell and blame the wine, and by next week he'd feel slightly unwell again, and in three months some physician would diagnose a weakness of the humors and prescribe something useless, and the slow poisoning would continue unless—

Unless she did something about it.

Which was the problem, because Cyrus Dravenmoor had built her entire life on the principle of doing nothing about anything, visibly.

* * *

The kitchens at Greyhollow Palace ran on heat, noise, and hierarchy, and Cyrus moved through all three like smoke through a keyhole.

She'd learned the building the way smugglers learn a mountain: not the grand roads, but the gaps between them. The service stair behind the tapestry of the Battle of Redford, where the third step had been loose since the reign of the old king's grandmother and nobody had ever fixed it because fixing things wasn't anyone's job. The coal chute that connected the scullery to the outer ward, passable if you didn't mind smelling like a foundry. The gap in the pantry wall where a mason had cut a corner forty years ago and a clever apprentice had hidden a bottle of gin ever since, refilled by each generation of apprentices in turn like a sacred flame.

She knew these passages because her mother had taught her that buildings and mountains obeyed the same law: *the official road is the one they watch; everything else is yours.* Her mother had run goods through the Thornfell passes for twenty years—salt, silk, untaxed brandy, occasionally people—and had raised her daughter on maps drawn in recipe cards, routes disguised as instructions for plum cordial and spiced honey cakes. *Take the low road past the burnt oak until you reach the second stream. Fold in the walnuts. Do not tell your uncle.*

Cyrus had those cards still, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her trunk, and she had never once needed them in ten years of palace life, and she had never once been able to throw them away.

In the kitchen she found what she was looking for: the assistant cook named Pell, a soft-bodied young man with flour on his forearms and ambition in his eyes, currently shouting at a scullery maid about the consistency of a sauce.

"Pell." Her voice was pitched exactly loud enough. Not a whisper—that drew attention. Just a normal voice saying a normal thing to a person nobody bothered to watch. "The leek soup. Where did the leeks come from?"

Pell blinked at her. Tasters were furniture that talked, occasionally. "The palace gardens, same as always."

"And the cream?"

"The Darrow dairy, same as—" He stopped. Something flickered behind his eyes, the particular flicker of a man remembering a small irregularity he'd decided not to report. "Actually. New delivery boy this week. For the cream. Old Tam's nephew, he said. Had papers."

"Did you read them?"

"They were papers," Pell said, with the dignity of a man who had once read a paper and found it sufficient. "Sealed and everything."

"Where is he now?"

"Gone, presumably. Delivers and goes." Pell frowned, sensing—slowly, the way his sauces reduced—that the conversation had acquired a purpose. "Why? What's wrong with the soup?"

"Nothing," said Cyrus. "It's excellent. I only wanted to compliment whoever chose the cream."

She left before his frown could finish becoming suspicion. Behind her she heard him resume shouting about the sauce, reassured, and felt the familiar double pulse of her trade: satisfaction and contempt, delivered together, like a coin with two faces.

Half a grain of arsenic, introduced through the cream, dosed small enough to evade every physician in the capital. Which meant the poisoner wasn't a desperate kitchen hand or a jealous rival. It meant patience. It meant access. It meant someone who understood that the surest way to kill a king was to never quite kill him.

And beneath it, that older taste. That prayer-flavored wrongness.

Cyrus climbed the loose stair behind the battle tapestry and let herself think about the second thing, because the first thing was dangerous and the second thing was worse.

There were plants that grew only on the north faces of Thornfell, above the snow line, where the old gods' shrines used to stand before the silence. Her mother had called them godsbane and refused to carry them even for triple pay. *Nothing that grows where prayers used to land comes to any good,* she'd said, and then she'd died anyway, of a fever, ordinary as bread, and Cyrus had learned that the world did not reward caution so much as it simply failed to notice it.

The taste in the soup was not godsbane itself. But it was a cousin of it. Something grown in ground that remembered worship.

Someone was poisoning the king with arsenic and faith, together, the way you'd cut a drug with something to carry it deeper.

Cyrus sat down on the loose step in the dark behind the tapestry and allowed herself, for exactly sixty seconds, to feel afraid.

* * *

The fear passed, as it always did, into procedure.

Procedure said: identify the vector. The vector was the cream. The cream came through one gate at dawn, was signed for by one clerk, and was tasted by one woman whom nobody watched. Procedure said: she could intercept tomorrow's delivery, dilute the dose, buy the king months without anyone knowing a battle had been fought. Procedure said: do not report it, because reporting it meant becoming visible, and visible people in Greyhollow Palace had short, eventful lives.

Procedure did not say what to do about the fact that her arms were burning.

By evening the prickle in her scars had become a steady heat, low and constant, like a banked fire. She sat in her narrow room above the taster's quarters—a room chosen specifically for its forgettability, third door past the linen store, indistinguishable from nine others—and unwrapped the bandage she wore there out of habit more than need, and looked at the old marks.

They had been fading for years. Faint white lines, the kind you'd miss unless you were looking. Tonight they were pink. Tonight they looked *new*, and when she pressed a fingertip to the longest one, the skin beneath was warm as a held stone.

"That's not arsenic," she told the room quietly. "Arsenic doesn't do that."

The room, being a room, declined to answer.

She should sleep. She had a dawn delivery to intercept and a poisoner to outmaneuver and a king to keep alive without his knowledge, consent, or gratitude, and none of that worked well on no sleep. Instead she sat on the edge of her narrow bed and did the thing she did perhaps twice a year, which was to take out her mother's recipe cards and look at them.

Plum cordial. Spiced honey cake. Blackberry preserves. Twenty years of smuggling routes dressed as domestic instruction, and her mother's handwriting going shakier toward the end, and one card near the bottom of the stack that wasn't a recipe at all—just a single line in her mother's tightest, most careful script:

*If the fires wake, go to the First Hearth. You'll know why when you can't ask me.*

Cyrus had read that card a thousand times and filed it, every time, under *grief talking.* Mothers left strange sentences behind; it was practically a job requirement. But tonight her arms were burning and the soup had tasted like a shrine and somewhere below her, in the banquet hall, a king was eating poison laced with the memory of answered prayers.

She put the cards away. She blew out the candle. She lay down and stared at the ceiling and performed the arithmetic she performed every night, the one that kept the loneliness at a manageable distance:

*I am invisible. Invisible is safe. Safe is enough.*

It had been enough for ten years. She fell asleep repeating it, and if the words had begun to taste like the soup—like something true with something old buried underneath—she was too tired to name the flavor.

* * *

Dawn found her at the cream gate, wearing the grey apron of a scullery drudge she had borrowed and would return, watching the delivery cart rattle up the outer ward road.

The new delivery boy was already dismounting, papers in hand, and Cyrus noted three things in the time it took him to tie off the cart horse. First: his boots were too good for a dairy laborer, city-made, soft-soled. Second: he held the papers in his left hand the way you hold something you've rehearsed holding. Third: he glanced at the kitchen door twice in ten seconds, which was twice more than an honest man delivering cream ever glanced at anything.

She drifted closer, pushing an empty barrel that explained her presence to anyone who cared, which was no one.

"Mornin'," she said, in the flat market accent she kept folded away for occasions like this. "You're new."

"Tam's nephew." He didn't look at her. Furniture talking to furniture. "Got papers."

"So you said." She peered into the churns on the cart, made a show of checking seals. "Long haul from the Darrow valley. Roads bad after the rains?"

"Roads are roads." He handed the papers to the gate clerk, who stamped them without reading them, because the clerk had stamped them six mornings running and boredom is the great solvent of vigilance.

But Cyrus had read them yesterday, through the pantry-wall gap, while the clerk slept off his lunch. The seal was good. The handwriting was good. The route listed was wrong—it ran through the Kettle Gap, and the Kettle Gap had been closed since the spring floods, and every actual driver in the Darrow valley knew it, and only someone working from a map instead of a road would write it down.

Only someone who had never driven the route. Someone whose employer had bought a forgery from a forger who'd never left the capital.

She knew that map. Not this map specifically, but its species. Half the smugglers in Thornfell had died or hanged because of men who trusted maps over roads.

"Safe travels," she told the boy pleasantly, and drifted back toward her barrel, and began to plan.

The plan was simple, which was how she liked them. Tomorrow's cream would arrive poisoned again. She would taste it, nod it through, and then—through Pell, through a suggestion planted so lightly it would seem like Pell's own idea—see to it that the king's soup was made with yesterday's cream, held back for exactly this contingency. The king would eat clean. The poisoner would believe the dose was landing. And in a month, when the slow kill failed to progress, the poisoner would grow impatient, and impatience was the one mistake invisible people could count on visible people to make.

It was a good plan. It required nothing but her ordinary, unnoticed competence. It required her to remain exactly what she had spent ten years becoming: a woman no one saw, saving a life no one knew was threatened, in a palace that would forget her name within a minute of hearing it.

She was halfway back across the ward, planning the exact phrasing she'd plant in Pell's ear, when the burning in her arms changed.

It had been heat. Now it was a pull. Low in the scars, deep, directional, like a compass needle finding north—except the direction it found was up, and east, and very far away, toward a wall of blue mountains she could see from the palace towers on clear days and had not looked at in ten years.

Thornfell.

Cyrus stopped in the middle of the ward, a scullery drudge with an empty barrel, and looked at the mountains, and the mountains seemed—she would remember this afterward, and distrust the memory, and remember it anyway—to look back.

Somewhere in the kitchens behind her, a scullery maid was about to burn a stew. Somewhere in the city, a god that had been silent for thirty years was turning over in its sleep. And somewhere in her chest, beneath the arsenic immunity and the forged records and the ten careful years of being no one, something that had been waiting patiently since before she could walk sat up and paid attention.

Cyrus picked up her barrel and walked back into the palace, and did not run, because running was visible.

But her hands were shaking, and the last thought she had before the kitchen door swallowed her was not about the poisoner, or the king, or the plan.

It was her mother's voice, patient as a recipe: *You'll know why when you can't ask me.*

She was starting to know why.
