## 2. The Prayer That Answered

The kitchen at midnight smelled like every kitchen Cyrus had ever worked: tallow smoke, wet stone, onions gone sweet in a pot nobody remembered to cover. She knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat, which was to say intimately and without affection.

She should have gone to bed. The tasting had ended hours ago—the king alive, the poisoner still unidentified, Cyrus herself still a gray smear on the palace wall. A good invisible woman went to bed. A good invisible woman slept the sleep of someone who had done exactly as much as was required and not one drop more.

Instead she sat on an upturned crate in the corner behind the flour racks, where the lamplight gave out and the shadows took over, and she ate cold bread and listened.

She told herself it was habit. Ten years in kitchens taught you that night was when the truth came out—when the cook stopped performing for the steward and the scullery girls stopped pretending they weren't exhausted and everyone said what the day had been too bright for. Kitchens were confessionals with better smells. If something was wrong in the palace, it would be wrong here first, in the small ways: a delivery short, a guard paid off, a name mentioned once too casually.

And besides, she hadn't been able to stop thinking about the dish.

Arsenic she understood. Arsenic was honest, in its way. It wanted what it wanted, and if you knew its habits you could catch it sitting there in the sauce like a guest who'd arrived too early. But whatever had been folded into the arsenic—that other thing, bitter-green and old, old like grave dirt, old like a word you almost remembered—it had *answered* her tongue. Not reacted. Answered. Her scars had lit up under her skin like banked coals breathed upon, and for half a heartbeat she had tasted something that wasn't a flavor at all.

She'd spat the mouthful into the testing cloth before it could finish whatever it was beginning. She'd told herself the shaking afterward was adrenaline. Adrenaline was respectable. Adrenaline didn't require explanation.

But she couldn't stop listening for it.

The kitchen emptied by degrees. The cook left first, then the turnspit boys, then the last of the scullery hands trailing each other out toward the servants' stair, complaining about wages and a wedding none of them were invited to. By the second bell the room belonged to the coals and the mice and one girl who hadn't left.

Cyrus knew her by sight: Pell. Sixteen, maybe seventeen, red-knuckled from lye water, hair escaping her cap in pale wisps. She was the kind of girl palaces were built to overlook—small enough to disappear behind a serving tray, tired enough to be furniture. Cyrus had spent ten years perfecting that quality and recognized it instantly in others, the way a counterfeiter knows real coin.

Pell stood over a pot on the back hearth, and the pot was ruined.

Cyrus could smell it from her corner: scorched barley, burnt to the bottom of the iron, the kind of black that doesn't scrape out so much as surrender to. Pell stared down into it, and her shoulders did the thing shoulders do when crying is coming but being refused entry—one shudder, held, another shudder, held.

"It's for the infirmary," she said, to the pot or the room or no one. "Old Fenwick broke his hip and he won't eat anything else and I—" Her voice cracked and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I fell asleep. One bell. Just one bell."

She lifted the pot and looked into it the way you look into a grave you dug yourself.

And then she did the thing that changed everything.

She didn't pray. Not properly. Cyrus had heard proper prayers all her life—court prayers, polished and Latin-smooth, addressed upward through layers of clergy and incense to gods who had been silent for three generations and were spoken of in the past tense, politely, the way you speak of a grandfather who died before you were born. The old gods. The Hearth-Father, the Thorn-Mother, the Nine-Names-Below. Dead gods. Sleeping gods. Gods the church kept on the books the way you keep a deed to land you can't find anymore.

What Pell did was worse than praying. She just talked.

"You're not even real," she said, scrubbing at her face. "Mam said you weren't real and Mam was right about everything else. But if you were—if there's anyone—if *anything* is listening—" She swallowed. "He's going to die without his supper and it isn't fair. It isn't *fair*. Please. Please fix it. Please fix it, please fix it, please—"

Her voice dropped to a whisper, and then below a whisper, lips moving, and Cyrus might have looked away then, embarrassed for her, except—

Except Cyrus's scars woke up.

Not stung. Not burned the way they'd burned at the tasting, a warning flare along the old lines. This was different. This was her whole forearms lighting from within, both arms, the fine lattice of pale tissue she'd carried since childhood suddenly warm as fresh bread, warm as a hearthstone at dawn, and underneath the warmth a feeling she had no word for except that it was *attention*. Somewhere vast and far away and very, very close, something had turned its head.

The stew unmade itself.

There was no better word for it, and Cyrus would spend years searching for one and never find it. The burn reversed. That was the only way her mind could hold it: the black crust lifted off the iron bottom in curling sheets and unburned, darkening backward into brown, into gold, into the soft dun color of barley cooked right; the acrid smoke thinned and sank back down into the pot instead of rising; the smell of scorching folded itself away like a letter returned unread. In the space of three heartbeats the pot held supper again. Hot. Whole. Steaming gently, as though it had never known any other state.

Pell made a sound Cyrus had never heard a human make—a small, punched-out note of pure animal disbelief—and dropped to her knees on the greasy flagstones.

"No," she whispered. "No, no, no—"

She reached out one finger and touched the side of the pot and snatched it back, then touched it again, longer, laughing now, a thin wild thread of laughter with terror underneath it like wire. "Thank you," she babbled, to the air, to the hearth, to the dark above the rafters. "Thank you, thank you, I'm sorry, thank you—"

*You're welcome,* said the dark, in a voice that was not a voice.

It wasn't sound. That was the worst of it. It bypassed Cyrus's ears entirely and arrived somewhere behind her sternum, in the scarred country of her body, rendered directly into meaning the way heat arrives into skin. It wasn't words either, quite. It was more like the sensation of a door opening somewhere enormous, and everything on the other side leaning slightly closer to see.

Cyrus did not move. Moving was visible. She had built her entire life on the principle that moving was visible, and some deep animal part of her understood with total clarity that whatever had just answered Pell was currently *in the room*, the way weather is in a room through an open window, and that its attention was moving across the space like a slow searchlight.

It passed over the coals. Over the mice. Over Pell, kneeling in her spilled tears and joy.

Over Cyrus.

It stopped.

For one endless moment she felt herself seen—not her gray smock, not her practiced nothing-face, but the thing underneath, the architecture beneath the architecture, twenty years of small doses and deliberate dullness laid open like a house with the roof taken off. Something regarded her with an attention so complete it made the chancellor's spy network look like a child squinting through a keyhole.

Then, behind her sternum, gentle as a hand on a shoulder:

*Ah.*

One syllable. It contained recognition. It contained, horribly, *pleasure*. It contained the tone of a grandmother finding the grandchild she'd been told about finally standing in the doorway after a long journey, mud on her boots, grown taller than expected.

*There you are.*

Then it was gone—or withdrew, which felt different, felt less like leaving and more like stepping back through a door while continuing to watch—and the kitchen was only a kitchen again, tallow and stone and cooling steam, and Pell kneeling alone by a pot of rescued barley porridge.

Cyrus realized she was gripping the edge of the flour rack hard enough to leave marks in the wood. She let go. She checked her breathing the way she checked sauces: measured, controlled, brought back down to temperature.

*Think*, she ordered herself, because thinking was the ladder out of every pit she'd ever fallen into. *Inventory. What happened. What you saw.*

A burnt stew unmade itself. Fine. She'd seen sleight-of-hand performers do nearly as well, and grief made people unreliable witnesses, and she herself had inhaled a great deal of flour dust over the years.

Her scars had answered. Less fine. Her scars were hers, private, the one ledger she kept in her own body, and they had behaved tonight like instruments tuned to something she'd never consented to tune them to.

And the voice—*there you are*—that was not fine at all, because you could explain away a girl's hysteria and you could explain away a fever dream, but you could not explain away the specific, patient, recognizing warmth of being known by something that had been waiting for you since before you could walk.

*You'll know why when you can't ask me,* her mother had said.

Cyrus looked at her arms in the dim light. The scars lay quiet now, faint as ever, the map no one was meant to read. But she could still feel the place where the attention had rested on them, the way your skin remembers sunlight after you've come indoors.

The old gods were dead. Everyone knew the old gods were dead. Three generations of silence, churches repurposed, feast days reassigned to sensible civic occasions. The crown had spent a century making sure of it—or so the histories said, and Cyrus trusted the histories about as far as she could throw the archive building.

They weren't dead. They were waking. And whatever was waking had been expecting *her*.

Across the kitchen, Pell had gotten up off her knees. She ladled porridge into a covered bowl with shaking, careful hands, wrapped it in a cloth, and held it against her chest like something rescued from a fire. At the door she paused and looked back at the hearth—at the empty air above it—and Cyrus watched her decide something, watch the fear lose by a nose to wonder.

"Tomorrow," Pell whispered to the dark, shyly, like a girl talking to a boy across a market stall. "I'll—tomorrow I'll bring an offering. If you're there. If you want."

The dark said nothing. But the coals in the hearth flared, briefly, softly, the way coals flare when breathed on, and Pell smiled with her whole cracked heart and hurried out with her supper for a dying man.

Cyrus sat alone in the shadow behind the flour racks for a long time.

Here was the thing she could not say to anyone, could barely shape inside her own skull: for ten years she had been safe the way a stone at the bottom of a well is safe. Untouched, unseen, unwitnessed. She had called it survival. She had built it brick by brick out of blandness and routine and the disciplined erasure of everything in her that might catch a light.

And tonight, in ninety seconds, something had reached down the well and known her name.

She should have felt terror. She did feel terror—cold and professional, the operative's assessment already running: *witnesses, exposure, the chancellor, what happens to useful anomalies in this palace.* Veyra's people would hear about a kitchen miracle within a day. Veyra collected miracles the way other women collected jewelry.

But underneath the terror, in a place she didn't have locks for because no one had ever tried the door, was something worse.

Hope.

Terrible, unfamiliar, greedy hope. Hope like thirst. Because the voice had said *there you are*, and some starved animal part of Cyrus—ten years at the bottom of the well, ten years of being no one on purpose—had heard it and thought, with a longing that shamed her: *someone knows I exist.*

She put her head back against the flour rack and closed her eyes.

*Get hold of yourself.* Her mother's voice again, but this time not patient—sharp, the voice from the bad nights, the nights the recipe cards came out and the shutters got barred. *Listen to me. When the gods start answering, run the high roads and trust the low. And girl—whatever they promise you, count what it costs the person next to you.*

Cyrus opened her eyes. The kitchen was quiet. The pot sat clean and full on the hearth, steaming its ordinary steam, evidence of nothing.

Tomorrow, Chancellor Veyra would hear a rumor about a scullery maid. Tomorrow, the archive would need visiting—properly, thoroughly, because if someone had been feeding her poisons since childhood, someone had written it down, and paper was the one thing in this palace that never learned to keep quiet. Tomorrow she would need to learn everything there was to know about a dead god's answer and a living chancellor's appetite.

Tonight, she needed one thing first.

She rose, crossed the kitchen floor on silent feet, and stood before the great hearth where Pell had knelt. The coals had settled to a low red breathing. Above them the chimney rose into darkness, and beyond the darkness, she knew without knowing how she knew, the mountains waited—Thornfell, the smuggler roads stitched through its bones, the paths her mother had drawn on cards and made her memorize until the map lived behind her eyelids.

Paths she'd always assumed were for running from men.

Cyrus knelt. It felt absurd, kneeling in a palace kitchen in a gray smock, and she did it anyway. She had no prayer. She had never once in her life asked anything of anything. So she said the only true thing she had:

"My mother was Halloran. Of the Thorn roads." Her voice came out rough. "If you know what I am—you tell me what I'm supposed to *do* with it."

The fire did not answer.

But every scar on her body lit at once, warm and unmistakable, like a lamp carried toward her down a long dark road—and somewhere far above the palace, far above the city, in the high passes where the snow never melted, something ancient turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to smile.

Cyrus stayed kneeling until the coals dimmed.

Then she stood, brushed the ash from her knees, and went to find out what the archive knew about her.

Because whatever was coming for her was already awake—and it had known her name a long time before she did.
