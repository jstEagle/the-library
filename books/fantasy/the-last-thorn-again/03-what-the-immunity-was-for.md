## 3. What the Immunity Was For

The palace archive smelled of dust and old tallow, and beneath both—faint as a lie told well—the sour tang of rat poison.

Cyrus noticed it the way she noticed everything now: too much. Three days since the scullery maid's stew had unmade itself in a swirl of steam and answered prayer, three days since her own scars had lit up like banked coals under her skin, and every sense she owned had gone sharp enough to draw blood. She could taste the archive before she entered it. She could taste the rats that lived in it.

She slipped through the door Maera of the kitchens had left unlatched for the coal delivery—a door no one guarded because no one important ever went to the archive—and stood in the dark letting her eyes adjust.

Nobody stopped her. Nobody ever stopped her. That was the whole architecture of her life: a woman built so thoroughly unremarkable that guards looked through her, clerks forgot her name between one petition and the next, and the king himself, whose soup she tasted twice daily, would struggle to pick her face from a lineup of servants. Ten years of deliberate nothing. It was armor. It was also, she was beginning to suspect, a cage someone else had welded shut.

*Find out why*, she had told herself, lying awake while her scars throbbed in time with a heartbeat that wasn't entirely hers anymore. *You taste poisons for a living. Start with your own history.*

Her own hiring record. Twenty years old. Filed somewhere in this tomb of ledgers among forty thousand others.

She moved down the row of shelves, trailing one finger along the spines, counting alcoves by feel. The Household Registry occupied the third gallery, records sorted by reign, then department, then year. The Taster's Office fell under Household. The year she'd been hired—

She found the shelf. Found the box. Pulled it down and set it on the reading table by the window where moonlight came through in a gray blade, and then she stood there a moment with her hand flat on the lid, listening to her own pulse.

Twenty years ago she'd been nine years old, an orphan off the charity rolls of Saint Verren's, hired into the palace kitchens as a scullery girl. That much she remembered—or thought she remembered. The memory had the soft edges of something handled too often. A matron's kind voice. Papers signed. A cart ride through gates she'd never seen again.

*Read it first*, she told herself. *Grieve after.*

She opened the box.

* * *

The hiring record was the third document in the folder, and it was wrong.

Not forged—forgery she knew how to spot; half her job was spotting it, in invoices for imported spices and requisitions for cellar wine. No, this was authentic parchment, authentic seal, authentic clerk's hand. What was wrong was arithmetic.

*Hired: 14th of Thawmonth.* Her ninth birthday. Fine. But the endorsement at the bottom, the physician's certification required for any servant handling food, was dated six months *earlier*. Certified fit for tasting duty before she'd ever been hired for it.

Someone had decided she would be a taster half a year before anyone asked her to taste anything.

Cyrus read it three times. The candle of doubt in her chest guttered, steadied, refused to go out. Clerks made mistakes. Dates smudged. Ink bled. Except the ink hadn't bled, and the date wasn't smudged—it was written in a confident, careful hand, and beside it, in the margin, someone had added a single word in the same script:

*Sufficient.*

Sufficient for what?

Her mouth had gone dry in the way it did when she'd been dosed with something bitter, and she almost laughed at herself for it. Old habit. The body always braced first.

She kept reading.

Behind the hiring record were more papers, and they should not have existed. Tasters got one file: name, hire date, certification, and—if they survived long enough—a death notice. Cyrus Dravenmoor's folder held eleven documents across twenty years. Dosing schedules. Not the official ones, the ones posted in the Taster's Office listing which dishes required sampling—those were public, boring, safe. These were private. Handwritten. A regimen running parallel to her official duties like a second spine inside her spine.

*Nightshade, 2 grains, fortnightly, in winter wine.*
*Arsenic solution, 3 drops weekly, masked in almond.*
*Add wolfsbane extract upon menses, reduced dose.*
*Copper salts, quarterly.*
*Godsbane tincture — see attached — increase by one drop per season, do not exceed threshold without authorization.*

Attached was a diagram. Of *her*.

She recognized the outline instantly, though she'd never seen it drawn: the narrow shoulders, the crooked left pinkie she'd broken at twelve falling off the laundry sled. Someone had measured her. Someone had been measuring her since childhood—organs marked in faded ink, the liver shaded dark, the heart ringed twice, and along both forearms small crosses she understood only when she turned the page and found the key.

*Scar sites. Covenant channels. Observe for luminescence during administration.*

Covenant channels.

Her scars. The thin white lines she'd always assumed were kitchen accidents, burns from kettle rims, cuts from careless knives—she'd catalogued them herself over the years the way you catalogue any damage, with resignation and a certain grim pride. There were fourteen of them. She'd counted once, drunk on festival wine, tracing them by candlelight and wondering idly how such a careful person had accumulated so many wounds.

Fourteen crosses on the diagram. Fourteen scar sites.

The paper trembled in her hand. She set it down flat, pressed it with both palms, and made herself breathe the way she breathed through a suspected poison: slow, shallow, controlled. Don't fight the substance. Let it tell you what it is.

*Observe for luminescence.*

Three nights ago her scars had burned. Had glowed—she hadn't imagined it, hadn't dreamed it, the light had come off her skin in threads of gold like veins of ore in dark stone, and the maid's ruined stew had unmade itself, and some sleeping thing beneath her ribs had turned over and opened one eye.

She had thought it was the gods waking. Something happening *to* her.

It wasn't happening to her. It had been happening to her for twenty years. Drop by drop, grain by grain, season over season, someone had fed her a liturgy disguised as a medical regimen, and her body—the one thing she owned, the one country she ruled absolutely, the fortress she'd built her entire survival around—was not a fortress at all.

It was a kiln. And someone had been firing it, slowly, patiently, for two decades, shaping her into a vessel.

She sat down on the reading stool because her legs had opinions about standing.

* * *

Time passed strangely in the archive. She worked by moonlight and a stub of candle she allowed herself only in short, hooded bursts. Somewhere below, a watchman coughed. Somewhere above, the palace slept its heavy royal sleep, and the king she'd saved last week from arsenic laced with something older went on breathing because of her tongue, knowing nothing, thanking no one, not even knowing her name.

Good, she'd always thought. That's the point. That's the design.

Whose design? The question had changed shape. For ten years she'd assumed the answer was *mine*—her regimen, her discipline, her private project of immunity, begun the day she'd realized a food-taster who could be poisoned was just a slower kind of corpse. She'd been proud of it. Quietly, fiercely proud: *no one did this for me, I did it myself, my body is mine because I made it mine.*

She turned back to the first page of the dosing schedule and looked properly at the hand that wrote it.

She knew that hand.

Not personally—she'd never met its owner—but professionally, the way a locksmith knows a particular maker's filing. Chancellor Veyra's office issued memoranda in that script, or rather her chief clerks did, trained to a uniformity so exact it might as well have been a seal. Every schedule page carried it. Every annotation. *Sufficient.* *Increase by one drop per season.* *Observe for luminescence.*

Twenty years ago Veyra had been a junior minister. Young for it, brilliant, already feared. Already, apparently, building something.

And there—at the bottom of the folder, folded twice, older than all the rest—was the document that made the cold in Cyrus stop being metaphorical.

A letter. Private correspondence, never filed officially, tucked here by someone who wanted it findable but deniable. The handwriting was different: looser, hurried, a scholar's scrawl. The seal was broken but she recognized the impression—the triquetra of the old faith, hearth-flame and thorn and open hand. A god's seal, on a mortal's letter.

*To the Keeper of Records,* it began, *and through him to the Lady who requests our silence—*

*You ask whether the child can be prepared without her knowledge or consent. I will answer plainly, since plainness costs me nothing now that I am leaving the capital and its rewards behind. Yes. The covenant does not require consent to build a vessel, only to fill one. This is its mercy and its horror. You may shape the clay for thirty years, and the clay will never know it is being shaped.*

*But mark this, and mark it well: what you propose has been tried. The last bloodline of the First Hearth was not extinguished in the Purge, whatever the histories say—they were scattered and renamed, and the crown of that day hunted them precisely because a prepared heir is a loaded weapon. If the gods wake and find their fire housed in a soul that was forced rather than chosen, they will not thank the smith. They will remember the hammer.*

*I will have no part of it. Burn this if you are wise, madam. Or keep it, as evidence, against the day the child grows clever enough to come looking.*

*The day the child comes looking.*

Cyrus read it four times. On the fourth reading her eyes stopped working properly, and she discovered her face was wet, which was absurd, she didn't cry, crying was noise and noise was death in her profession—

*Scattered and renamed.*

Dravenmoor. She'd never known where the name came from; orphans got surnames like coats off a donation rack. But Ashvane—no, she didn't know that name yet, wouldn't know it for weeks—some part of her, the part that had been built without her permission, stirred at *First Hearth* like a dog hearing its true master's step on the stair.

They hadn't poisoned her to protect her. They hadn't even poisoned her to protect the king, or only incidentally, the way you oil a lock you happen to need. Every dose, every carefully calibrated torment of her childhood—the winters she'd spent sick and shaking in the attic room, convinced her own weakness was a personal failing; the fevers; the nights her blood felt like molten wire—every hour of it had been administered. Scheduled. *Increased by one drop per season.*

She had spent twenty years believing she was the author of her own survival.

She was the manuscript.

* * *

She heard the footsteps with ten minutes' warning—her hearing, too, had sharpened past usefulness into pain—and had the folder reboxed and the shelf straightened and herself folded into the shadow between two cabinets before the night archivist shuffled through with his lamp, muttered at the draft, and shuffled out again.

In the dark afterward, Cyrus did the thing she did best. She thought like a poisoner.

Veyra had built her. Fine. To what end? The letter said it: a vessel for the covenant's power, ready when the gods woke. Veyra couldn't have known they'd wake *now*—or could she? The waking had started weeks ago, small things, deniable things, and Cyrus had watched the chancellor at court watching everything with those pale patient eyes. If Veyra had known the harvest was coming, she'd have needed the vessel ripe.

Three drops of arsenic a week. Godsbane, increasing by one drop per season. *Do not exceed threshold without authorization.* Authorization from whom? From Veyra. Which meant Veyra could accelerate the ripening whenever she chose. Which meant Cyrus's next dose—tomorrow morning, the winter wine, two grains of nightshade—might be medicine, might be maintenance, or might be the beginning of the final pressing.

Or the regimen might simply continue, patient as erosion, until the vessel was needed and collected. Either way, she was livestock that had learned to read its own feed schedule.

There was a version of Cyrus Dravenmoor—an older version, the one she'd been yesterday—who would have gone back to her attic room, taken tomorrow's dose, said nothing, and begun the long quiet work of finding leverage. Invisibility was armor. Knowledge was a knife you kept sheathed until the throat presented itself.

But that Cyrus had trusted her body. This one knew better.

She thought about the maid kneeling in the scullery, praying to a dead god over burnt stew, and the stew obeying. She thought about the light in her own arms, gold as hearthfire, answering something she'd never invited. *The clay will never know it is being shaped.* Well. The clay knew now.

And the letter's warning rang in her like a struck bell: *If the gods wake and find their fire housed in a soul that was forced rather than chosen, they will not thank the smith. They will remember the hammer.*

Whoever filled the vessel mattered. Consent mattered—that was the whole grammar of the old covenant, freely given, freely bound. Which meant Veyra's plan had a flaw at its center, a flaw shaped exactly like a person: you could build the vessel, but you couldn't make it willing. Unless you lied to it for twenty years. Unless the lie held.

The lie was holding less well every day.

Cyrus slipped out through the coal door, crossed the frozen yard hugging the wall's blind side, and climbed to her attic by the drainpipe she'd been climbing since she was twelve—since she was *made* twelve, she corrected bitterly—and sat on her cot with her back to the cold stones and laid out her life like a taster's tray.

Option one: stay. Play the obedient vessel, dose herself on schedule, and hunt for leverage inside the machine. Safe. Slow. And every dose deepened their work in her blood.

Option two: run. Tonight, now, with nothing but the clothes she wore and the knowledge in her head. But run where? Veyra's reach covered the capital like frost covers a window.

Unless it didn't. Unless there were roads through the Thornfell mountains that appeared on no crown map, because she was the only living person who carried them—in her memory, yes, ten years of tasting had bought her the confidence of smugglers who thought she was nobody, but more than that, in ink. Her mother's recipe cards. The ones she'd been left with at the orphanage, the ones she'd always believed were just a dead woman's handwriting, cardamom and lamb and honey-cakes. She'd read them a thousand times and never once questioned why the margins were full of notes about passes and waypoints, trail markers and safe houses, in the same loving hand.

A recipe book that was also a road map. Left to a child of the First Hearth. *So she could always find her way home.*

Cyrus took the tin box from under the floorboard—antidotes, cash, and the worn recipe cards she'd carried through ten years of poison—and fanned them across her knees. Cardamom lamb. Honey-cakes. *Take the high path at Wolf's Tooth, the snow lies honest there.* Sage and barley broth. *When the cairns wear red wool, the toll is paid ahead.*

Home. The word landed in her chest like a coin dropped down a well, and she waited for the splash and heard none.

Below her window, torches moved in the outer yard. More than usual. She counted them with a professional eye and didn't like the total, and liked even less the direction they converged on: the administrative wing. The Chancellor's offices, burning midnight oil.

Or burning something else.

Cyrus blew out her candle, packed the tin, and pulled on her oldest coat—the one that made her look like exactly what everyone had always assumed she was: nothing, nobody, a servant of no consequence.

For ten years that assumption had kept her alive.

Tomorrow it would get her killed. She could feel it coming the way she felt arsenic on her tongue—a faint sweetness at the edge of things, too late to spit out, time only to swallow or to run.

She swung onto the windowsill, into the dark and the falling snow.

*Run*, then. But run toward something, for once, instead of away. The cards fluttered in her coat pocket like a heartbeat. Somewhere north, past the passes her mother had mapped in the margins of a honey-cake recipe, there was a hearth with her name on it.

It was time to go home and demand to know what they'd built her for.
