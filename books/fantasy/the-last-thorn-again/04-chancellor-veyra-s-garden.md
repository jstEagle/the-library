## 4. Chancellor Veyra's Garden

The greenhouse sat at the crown of the palace's oldest tower, behind three locked doors and a lie. The lie was the sign on the outermost door: *Botanical Annex — Apothecary Stores*. No one had questioned it in eleven years, because no one questioned anything Chancellor Veyra labeled. That was the first lesson of power she'd learned at her father's knee, back when his knee was all he could afford to offer her: people do not challenge the boring.

She unlocked the third door with a key she kept sewn into the lining of her left sleeve and stepped into air that smelled of iron and wet earth and something faintly sweet, like fruit going bad in a beautiful room.

Rows of raised beds stretched away under glass. In them grew the only crop in the capital that was harvested by fear: godsbane. Black-stemmed, silver-veined, its flowers closed even now, in the gray of early morning, as if the plant itself were holding its breath. There were forty-one varieties catalogued in the royal ledgers. Veyra grew sixty-three. The extra twenty-two existed nowhere on paper, because paper could be seized, and everything in this room existed for the day someone tried to seize it.

She took up the watering can — plain tin, unadorned, because a chancellor who owned gilded gardening tools deserved to be overthrown — and began at the north bed, working down the rows the way she did everything: systematically, without hurry, without waste.

"You sent for me before dawn," said Corvus from the doorway. Her spymaster did not enter greenhouses uninvited. He'd learned that too. "Either the realm is ending or you've finally taken up flowers."

"Both, possibly." She tipped water over a tray of seedlings whose leaves curled like question marks. "Tell me what you know about the kitchen girl."

A pause. Corvus was good, but he was not fast, and Veyra had spent a decade making sure he knew the difference between being good and being adequate. "Which kitchen girl?"

"The one who isn't a kitchen girl." She moved to the second bed. "The taster. Dravenmoor."

"The food-taster? What about her?" A beat, and then — there it is, Veyra thought, listening to him recalibrate — "Has something happened with the king's table?"

"Something happened with the king's table nine days ago, and you didn't notice, which is why we're having this conversation among the weeds instead of over breakfast." She set the can down. "The nightshade in the quail. You remember the report. Taster identifies it, king lives, everyone goes back to sleep."

"I read the report."

"You read the summary I wrote for you." Veyra crouched beside a plant in the corner bed, the oldest one, grown from cuttings whose provenance she had burned along with the man who'd sold them to her. Its leaves were darker than the others. Hungrier-looking. "Here's what the summary omitted. The nightshade was dressed. Someone had masked it with bittercress extract — an old technique, pre-Conquest, used by temple assassins who wanted their poisons to pass a taster's tongue. Nobody has used it in two hundred years because nobody remembers it. Except our taster remembered it. She flagged the dressing before she flagged the arsenic, which means she tasted the *craft* before she tasted the crime. Do you understand what that means?"

Corvus came a step inside the door. "That she's better trained than her file suggests."

"Her file says she was hired at fourteen by a provincial steward, tested once, passed, and forgotten." Veyra stood, wiping soil from her fingers onto her apron — she wore an apron in here, another small deliberate vulgarity, the chancellor on her knees in the dirt. "Her file also says her bloodwork shows no heavy-metal accumulation after ten years of controlled dosing. Her file says her liver enzymes are those of a woman half her age. Her file says—" she smiled, thin and private, "—her file says a great many things that a falsifier wrote twenty years ago, and the interesting question is not whether the file lies, Corvus. Files always lie. The interesting question is who paid for the lying, and what they were building."

"And you know the answer."

"I know most of the answer." She walked the center aisle slowly, letting him follow. "I inherited this project the way I inherited everything else — mid-collapse, underfunded, and with no documentation. My predecessor, Chancellor Aldric, kept his real ledger in his head, which was very secure right up until the stroke. But Aldric was a sentimentalist, and sentimentalists leave traces. Letters to physicians. Payments to a convent in Thornfell where a certain infant was born twenty-three years ago. A standing order, renewed annually, for godsbane root — purchased through the same smuggler routes that run the Thorn Road, which is why I know those routes better than any living soul outside the guilds."

"You're saying the crown has been poisoning the taster on purpose. For twenty years."

"Dosing," Veyra corrected. "Poisoning is what happens when you don't know what you're doing." She stopped at the old plant again. Up close, its silver veins had a pulse to them, almost — light moving beneath the surface, slow as sap. "There are records, fragments, from before the gods went quiet. The covenant era. And the records agree on one strange detail: the hearth-keepers, the mortal line that anchored the covenant, prepared themselves for the binding through years of ritual ingestion. Poisons, yes, but also ashes, consecrated salts, things I have no name for. They called it *making the vessel*. The body had to be rebuilt to hold what the covenant poured into it. Fire that would kill an ordinary person. Favor that would hollow one out."

Corvus had gone very still. He was a clever man; she could see the shape of it assembling behind his eyes, and she let him get there himself. People trusted conclusions they believed they'd reached.

"You think Dravenmoor is a hearth-keeper."

"I think Dravenmoor is the last one, and I think whoever arranged her hiring intended exactly that, and I intend to find out whether they succeeded." Veyra turned to face him fully. In here, without her chain of office, without the council chamber's distance, she looked like what she was underneath: a narrow, hard, tireless woman in her fifties with soil under her nails and murder in her ledgers. "And then nine days ago, the stew."

"The scullery maid."

"A dead god answered a burnt pot, Corvus. Embers rewound. Ash became onion. I have spoken to four witnesses myself, quietly, one at a time, and they all describe the same thing — the maid praying to the Hearthmother out of habit, nothing more, and the Hearthmother answering like a housewife stepping out of a hundred-year sleep to fix dinner." Veyra's voice stayed level. It always stayed level. That was the discipline her whole life was built around, the levee against everything underneath it. "Do you know what frightens me about that?"

"The king's safety—"

"No." She touched the dark-leaved plant, gently, the way one touches a sleeping animal whose dreams are unknown. "What frightens me is that it answered a prayer that asked for *nothing*. The girl didn't beg for a miracle. She just spoke to the dark like her grandmother taught her, and the dark spoke back. The gods aren't waking because someone summoned them. They're waking because they're ready. On their own schedule. For their own reasons." She withdrew her hand. "Power that arrives uninvited is power that comes with terms attached. And no one alive knows the terms."

"So we suppress it."

"We *manage* it." She resumed walking, faster now, momentum as argument. "Suppression is what Aldric tried, and look where it got us — a covenant broken so badly the silence itself became a wound. No. Here is what happens next. Effective immediately, Dravenmoor is promoted. Out of the kitchens, into the household guard of tasters — a new office, my creation, well paid, highly visible. We give her rank, salary, a suite near the east wing."

Corvus frowned. "You're rewarding her?"

"I'm *harvesting* her. Think, Corvus. If she is what I believe she is, then whatever is rebuilding her will accelerate now the gods are stirring — proximity, purpose, call it what you like. Every prayer answered in this city, every small miracle, will resonate through her like a struck bell. And if she is what I believe she is, then when the covenant reforges itself — and it will, these things always seek their shape — the anchor point will be wherever she stands." Veyra reached the far end of the greenhouse and laid her palm flat against the cold glass, looking down through the morning haze at the capital: ten thousand rooftops, the palace hearths already smoking, tiny and obedient. "So we choose where she stands. We put her at the center of the court, wrapped in crown wages and crown gratitude, and when the fire comes looking for its hearth, it will find the hearth already inside the walls. Inside the law. Inside *me*."

"And the crown holds the covenant."

"The crown holds the covenant." She said it simply, the way she said true things — no flourish, because flourish invited scrutiny. "One voice, one authority, accountable to the realm rather than to the whims of things older than language. You saw the old histories, same as I did. Gods answering prayers by drowning valleys. Blessings that curdled into curses because some priest mispronounced a word. Mortals spent four thousand years climbing out from under divine caprice, and I will not be the chancellor who hands the ladder back down." She turned from the window. "The waking gods want a door into the world. Fine. We'll build the door ourselves, put a lock on it, and keep the key in the treasury."

For a long moment Corvus said nothing. Then, carefully: "And if she won't cooperate? If this Dravenmoor has ideas of her own about what she's becoming?"

Veyra smiled again, and this time it reached her eyes, which made it worse.

"Then we'll have learned something valuable about vessels, won't we. Everything breaks. The trick is choosing what the pieces are worth." She picked up the watering can again. "Now. Three tasks. First, I want her followed — subtly, Corvus, she survived a decade of poison by noticing things; send your best and tell them to be furniture. Second, the convent records from Thornfell, the ones Aldric bought — I want every page on my desk within the week. Third—" she hesitated, the smallest hitch, gone almost before it arrived, "—find out what happened to her mother."

"Her mother?"

"The hiring record lists a father. It does not list a mother. Twenty years ago someone erased a woman from an official document, and people only erase mothers when the mother matters." Veyra tipped water over the dark-leaved plant, watching it drink, watching the veins brighten faintly, greedily, at the touch. "Everything in this garden grows toward something, Corvus. I want to know what direction she grows."

* * *

He left. The door sealed. The greenhouse settled into its held breath.

Veyra worked another hour among the beds — pruning, pinching aphids from the underside of leaves with a thumbnail, entering measurements in a ciphered notebook. This was the part of the day no one ever saw, the chancellor alone with her impossible agriculture, and she guarded it more jealously than the ledgers. Because the ledgers showed what she did. This showed why.

At the center of the room, apart from the beds, stood a stone basin, old, cracked, salvaged at ruinous expense from a demolished shrine in the provinces. Nothing grew in it. Nothing grew in it because nothing *could* — she had tried eleven species of godsbane in that basin and every one had died within a season, roots blackening as if scorched from below. The masons swore the stone was ordinary granite. Veyra had tested it herself. Ordinary granite did not stay cold in August.

She knelt at the basin now and pressed her palm to the stone, a habit she told herself was inspection and knew was something else.

Cold. Always cold. Cold like the memory of a hearth going out.

Her father had died of the gray fever when she was nine, in a village the old prayers had abandoned generations before. Her mother had prayed anyway — every night, on her knees, until her knees gave out — and the fever had taken her mother eighteen months later, still praying, still unanswered. Veyra had stood at the graveside at eleven years old and made a decision that had never once wavered: she would never kneel to anything that did not answer. And if something started answering — if the dark opened its eye and offered miracles to the desperate — then it would answer to the throne, through the throne, or it would not answer at all.

It was a clean creed. It had carried her from a pauper's plot to the chancellery in thirty years. It was entirely true.

Except.

Except that lately, kneeling here in the dark of the greenhouse with her hand on the cold stone, she had begun to dream. Small dreams. Harmless-seeming. A fire in a round stone room, and voices in it, and a seat — a fourth seat, empty, waiting — and every night the dream lasted longer, and every morning she woke with her heart slamming and her hand aching, and twice now she had found frost on the *inside* of her bedroom windows in high summer.

Gods did not haunt chancellors. Chancellors haunted history. That was the order of things.

But the basin was colder than it had been last month. She was nearly sure of it.

Veyra straightened, smoothed her apron, and allowed herself one full breath — the only indulgence of her day — before becoming the Chancellor of the Realm again, seamless and dry and utterly without visible fear.

On her desk, when she returned below, a courier's packet waited. Thornfell post, smuggler-marked, bought dearly. She broke the seal expecting convent pages.

Instead: a single line, unsigned, in a hand she did not recognize.

*Ask your predecessor's ghost what he promised the mother. Ask him what the child was promised in return. And ask yourself, Chancellor, whether the vessel can be held by hands that never asked to hold it.*

Veyra read it three times. Then she fed it to the candle and watched the promise of a stranger curl into ash, and reached for a pen to draft the promotion papers.

Outside her window, far to the north, thunder rolled over the mountains — though the sky was clear.
