## 5. Run the Thorn Road

The bell above the kitchen gate rang twice—once for fire, once for traitors—and Cyrus was out the scullery window before the second toll finished dying.

She'd rehearsed this for ten years without ever admitting it to herself. The bundle under the loose hearthstone: dry clothes, a knife, forty silver crowns sewn into a sash. The route through the tannery yards where the watch never patrolled because the smell offended their noses more than crime did. The gap in the east wall that a mason had quietly widened three kings ago and charged the crown for repairs it never got.

She moved through the city like water finding cracks.

Behind her, horns were sounding from the palace. *Poisoner*, they would be saying. *Heretic*. Veyra worked fast. Of course she did. You didn't become Chancellor of the Seals by letting inconvenient people finish their breakfasts.

Cyrus paused in the shadow of a shrine alcove—an old one, one of the dead gods' niches that had stood empty her whole life—and pressed two fingers to the stone. It was warm. It should not have been warm. She pulled her hand back as if from a stove and kept running.

*Later,* she told herself. *Grieve later. Think later.*

The recipe cards rode against her ribs in an oilcloth wrap, tied with string gone soft as cloth. Twenty-three of them, her mother's handwriting slanting across each one: measurements for stews, cures for chilblains, the exact sugar ratio for plum preserves. And beneath the recipes, inked faint enough that only someone who knew to look—a daughter who had traced every letter a hundred times by candlelight—there were lines. Paths. Numbers. A map of Thornfell written in the margins of a cook's life.

*The mountain remembers what the court forgets.* Her mother had said that once, laughing, flour on her knuckles. Cyrus had thought it was just something mothers said.

---

By full dark she was through the wall and into the pine dark beyond, and the city's bells had faded to a rumor behind her. She allowed herself one minute of standing still, chest heaving, listening to the enormous indifferent quiet of the foothills.

Then the grief arrived, right on schedule, exactly the way she'd known it would.

It came not as a wave but as a list. The narrow bed in the taster's quarters with its blanket worn thin at the corner where she worried it. The head cook, Brenna, who saved her the ends of the honey loaf because she'd noticed years ago that Cyrus liked them and never once made a fuss about it. The king himself, foolish and fond and entirely unaware he owed his life tonight to a woman whose name he could not have produced under torture. The small warm pride of competence. Ten years of being *no one*, which had felt like armor right up until the moment it felt like a coffin.

"They'll hang you in effigy by week's end," she said aloud to the pines. Her voice sounded strange, unpracticed. When had she last spoken with no one to overhear?

The trees didn't answer. But somewhere upslope, faint as a struck match, something hummed—a note with no throat behind it. The old god-stone warmth again, following her like a stray dog.

She walked faster.

---

The smuggler roads began where the king's road ended, which was to say at a collapsed bridge that wasn't actually collapsed. Cyrus knew the bridge. Her mother's cards called it *the Sleeping Giant*—a span the crown had condemned thirty years ago so that traffic would funnel through the tollgate at Miller's Ford. In truth, only the western third had been dropped. The rest stood sound over the gorge, reachable by a goat path on the far bank and a climb down roots that tested your faith in roots.

She crossed at moonrise, arms out, boots finding the stones her mother's numbers promised were there. Below her the river ran black and loud. Above her, the stars. And threaded through all of it now—she couldn't pretend otherwise—the faint burn of her scars, the ones that shouldn't exist, keeping time like a pulse counting toward something.

On the far side, she allowed herself a smile. It felt rusty and enormous.

*They taught me to taste poison,* she thought. *Nobody ever thought to teach me anything else. So I taught myself.*

Ten years of memorizing the smugglers' ledger while pretending to read nothing but menus. Ten years of noting which guards drank on duty, which clerks took bribes, which roads appeared on no official map because official maps were how empires strangled. They had made her invisible and she had used the invisibility like a crowbar.

The exhilaration hit her halfway up the goat path—sudden, indecent, joyful. Her legs burned and her lungs burned and she laughed, once, sharp, at the sheer audacity of being alive and free and hunted all at once.

Then she crested the rise, saw the firelight in the hollow below, and stopped laughing.

Campfires. Wagons. Mules picketed in a line. A caravan, camped precisely where her mother's cards said a caravan would camp on the third night of any month with a dark moon—because the tollgate guards drank hardest then, and the Sleeping Giant carried no tolls at all.

And caravans meant people. People meant questions. Questions meant the thing she had spent ten years avoiding: being seen.

Her stomach dropped through the mountain. There was no way around. The high pass was snowed in until midsummer; the low road was the low road. She needed these people the way a drowning woman needs a plank, and planks do not care whether you want company.

She went down into the light.

---

They heard her before she reached the fires—one sentry, then two, then a ring of knives that parted to show her the camp's center: a broad wagon, a woman sitting on its tailboard cracking walnuts one-handed, and around her a dozen faces ranging from hostile to professionally curious.

The woman was perhaps fifty, built like the wagon itself, with grey shot through black braids and a scar that split one eyebrow and continued, somehow, to look amused. She cracked another walnut, considered Cyrus from muddy boots to windburned face, and did not reach for a weapon.

"Well," she said. "A lone woman walking out of Thornfell at night with a knife and good boots. Either you're lost, you're running, or you're bait." She held up the walnut. "Which is it?"

"Running," Cyrus said. "Not bait. Lost is insulting."

A ripple of laughter went through the ring—not warm, but no longer hostile. The woman smiled with half her mouth.

"And what are you running from?"

"The Chancellor."

That killed the laughter. Knives shifted. The woman set down her walnuts with great care, as though the conversation had suddenly become fragile.

"That's a dangerous name to say out loud on my road."

"It's a dangerous name to say anywhere," Cyrus said. "I'm saying it anyway because I'm tired and I'd rather be honest than safe. Neither's been working out for me lately."

Silence. Then a wiry old man near the fire snorted. "Honesty. On the Thorn road. Mark the date."

"Hold." The woman raised a hand, and the camp settled. She studied Cyrus again, longer this time, and something in the study changed—it deepened, narrowed, became almost painful in its attention. "Say your name."

"You first."

"Sefa," the woman said. "These are my people. We run cloth south and salt north and we don't ask what else rides under the tarps. Your turn."

Cyrus weighed it. Every instinct she owned—the instincts that had kept her alive through arsenic and antimony and the slow patient politics of a royal kitchen—screamed at her to give a false name, a trade name, nothing. Be nobody. Be cargo. Be furniture.

But her scars were burning. Had been burning since the shrine alcove, since the stew unmade itself, since the archive and the falsified record and the word *vessel* written in a clerk's careful hand twenty years ago. Something in her had already decided that the armor was off. The body had voted before the mind convened.

"Cyrus," she said. "Just Cyrus."

Sefa went very still.

Then, slowly, she stood from the tailboard, and the whole camp seemed to stand with her, and she looked at Cyrus the way you look at a door you'd sealed years ago hearing a knock.

"Just Cyrus," Sefa repeated. "No family name?"

"I don't use one."

"No," Sefa said softly. "I don't imagine you would. What was your mother called? The one who wrote those cards—I can see the oilcloth wrap from here, girl, my mother carried hers the same way."

The night tilted. Cyrus's hand went to her ribs without permission.

"How could you possibly—"

"Because I know the wrap. Because I know the string. Because—" Sefa exhaled, and for the first time the amusement fell off her face entirely, leaving something older and rawer underneath. "Because thirty-one years ago a woman cooked for a caravan caught in the Whitefall freeze. Fed us for nine days off nothing but what she carried and what the mountain gave. Kept eleven of us alive who had no business living. My mother was one of them. I was six."

She stepped closer.

"She wouldn't take payment. Said the mountain doesn't charge and neither did she. Said if we ever wanted to settle it, find her daughter someday, walking the high paths, and give her whatever she needs and ask nothing back." Sefa's voice roughened. "I've paid that debt forward a dozen times over the years, hoping. Hoping like a fool. And now you walk into my fire with her map-wrap against your ribs and tell me your name is *just Cyrus*."

The camp had gone silent. Even the mules seemed to be listening.

Cyrus found she couldn't speak. Ten years of invisibility, and this stranger had seen through her in four sentences—from an oilcloth wrap, from a habit of carrying, from a debt her mother had scattered across the mountains like seed.

"She died eight years ago," Cyrus managed finally. "Winter fever. I was already at the palace by then. I never—" Her throat closed. She started over. "She never told me about the debt. About any of it. There's a lot she never told me."

"No," Sefa said gently. "There would be. Women like your mother and women like me—we keep our ledgers dark so our children stay light." She turned to the camp and her voice changed, snapped back into command like a whip finding its crack. "This one eats with us, rides with us, and stands behind us. Anyone has a question about that, bring it to me and I'll explain it slowly."

She looked back at Cyrus, and the half-smile returned, but there was steel under it now.

"You're running from Veyra, you said. Then hear me plain: the Thorn road is honest about one thing only—it's harder than whatever's chasing you. Can you keep pace? Can you hold silence when silence is wanted? Can you pull weight?"

"Yes," Cyrus said. "All three."

"Good." Sefa tossed her the last walnut. "Then welcome to the caravan, Just Cyrus. We move at first light, we sleep in shifts, and nobody asks anybody's business before the Grey Stair. After the Grey Stair—" she shrugged, cracking the walnut one-handed, precise as a trap springing, "—after the Grey Stair everybody's business tends to come out anyway. The mountain sees to it. Always has."

Cyrus caught the walnut. Sat down at the fire someone had silently made room beside. A bowl of barley soup appeared in her hands, hot and oversalted and the best thing she had ever tasted, and she ate it while the camp folded itself back around her—tentatively, the way a pack accepts a strange dog.

She should feel safe. Part of her did, shockingly, immediately, in a way she distrusted on principle.

But most of her was looking at the mountains rising black against the stars, higher and colder than anything the maps admitted to, and feeling her scars burn in a rhythm that was no longer quite her own pulse. Like something below the stone had noticed her crossing. Like something old had lifted its head.

*The mountain remembers,* her mother had said.

Apparently it remembered her, too.

She slept in shifts, as instructed, and dreamed of a hearth she had never seen, burning in a hall she had never entered, and a voice—deep, cracked, ancient, unmistakably alive—saying her family name like a key turning in a lock.

She woke before dawn with the taste of smoke and sulfur on her tongue, and Sefa standing over her, frowning at the eastern sky.

"Eat fast," the smuggler said. "We've got weather coming. And girl—" she hesitated, and for the first time since the fire, she looked uncertain. "There's a detour. Two days off the road, past the frozen lake. I owe somebody up there too. Somebody old." Her jaw worked. "You'll want to see this one yourself, I think. Though the gods know why I'm sure of it."

Cyrus sat up, heart hammering against the recipe cards, against the scars, against the name she hadn't spoken yet even to herself.

"Show me," she said.
