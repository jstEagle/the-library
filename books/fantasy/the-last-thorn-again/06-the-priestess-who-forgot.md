## 6. The Priestess Who Forgot

The shrine announced itself by smell before sight: cold stone and old incense, the ghost of myrrh that had burned so long in this place it had soaked into the mountain itself. Cyrus smelled it from a hundred paces up the switchback trail, threading between Sefa's mules, and her stomach did something complicated.

Temples meant people. People meant questions. She had spent eleven days on the Thorn Road being nobody—a caravan hand with chapped hands and no name worth remembering—and she'd gotten good at it again, fast. But her scars had been burning since they crossed the high pass, a slow heat under the skin of her forearms like sunstroke trapped in the flesh, and when she'd asked Sefa what lay ahead on this stretch of road, the smuggler had spat over the edge and said, "The Weeping Shrine. Nobody goes there. Priest went bad years ago. Gods left it."

The gods hadn't left anything lately. That was rather the problem.

"I'll scout ahead," Cyrus said.

Sefa looked at her sideways. In eleven days she had learned exactly three things about Cyrus: she could read a trail like a ledger, she never slept facing away from a stranger, and she didn't talk about herself. Sefa seemed to consider it a fair trade for someone who could spot an ambush two ridges early. "Scouts don't take long," she said, which was as close as she came to concern.

The shrine sat in a fold of the mountain where two cliffs leaned together like conspirators. It had been built to be found—the path was wide, the steps cut smooth—but it had also been built to be *kept*, and the keeping showed in details the moss hadn't eaten yet: iron rings for festival lanterns, a cistern fed by snowmelt, niches carved into the walls at shoulder height, hundreds of them, each one sized for a candle or an offering or a small clay vessel of somebody's grief.

The doors were open. That was wrong for a dead shrine. Dead shrines kept their doors shut against weather and goats and worse.

Cyrus stood in the doorway a long moment, letting her eyes adjust, one hand resting on the little knife she wore at the back of her belt—cooking blade, mostly, but a blade. Incense haze hung in the air, thin but present, and beneath it another smell she knew too well from the palace infirmary: the sour-sweet reek of a body fighting itself.

Someone was singing.

Not well. The voice cracked and restarted, cracked and restarted, the way a person sings when they're holding something down—when the song is a lid. It came from behind the altar, and it took Cyrus three full seconds to understand that the singer wasn't singing *to* anyone. She was singing *at* herself.

"You are Maera of the Hearth," the voice said, between cracks. "You are Maera of the Hearth. You keep the flame. You sweep the floor. You do not know anything else. You are Maera of the Hearth."

Cyrus came around the altar slowly.

The woman knelt on the bare stone, scrubbing it with a rag that had long since stopped moving water. Her hair had gone white at thirty-five, maybe forty, hard to tell—grief ages people unevenly, like weather. Her hands were raw to the wrist. And all down her forearms, in patterns that made Cyrus's own arms ache in sympathy, the skin was split and glowing—not bleeding, *glowing*, faint gold light seeping out of the cracks like heat escaping a kiln.

The woman looked up. Her eyes were the worst part. They were the eyes of someone who had done this many times: searched a stranger's face hopefully, then fearfully, then settled into polite blankness because hope was more dangerous than despair.

"Pilgrim?" she said. "I'm sorry, we're not— I'm sorry. Are you hurt? I can fetch water."

"Your arms," Cyrus said.

The woman glanced down at them as if noticing a stain on a skirt. "They itch," she said. "It passes." Then, quieter, with terrible practice: "It doesn't pass. It comes back. I'm sorry, pilgrim, I don't know why I said it passed." She pressed the rag flat against the stone and held it there as though pinning the world in place. "Who are you? No. Don't answer. If you answer, I'll remember meeting you, and then tonight—" Her breath caught. "Tonight is bad enough already."

*Then* the light flared. All down the ruined forearms the gold brightened, and the woman arched her back and made a sound Cyrus had only heard once before—from a man in the palace kitchens who'd drunk cleaning lye, the sound of a body being destroyed from inside by something it couldn't spit out.

Cyrus should have run. Every instinct honed across ten years of poison and invisibility said: *not your problem, not your name, walk away.* She took one step backward instead of three, and hated herself for even that much.

Then the woman spoke again, through her teeth, in a voice that was hers and also not:

"*She asks if healing means remembering.*"

The gold dimmed. The woman sagged forward onto her hands, breathing like a bellows. "Sorry," she whispered. "Sorry. It uses me sometimes. Talks through me. It's getting stronger, and I don't know what it wants, and I don't want to know, because wanting things is how you lose them—" She looked up at Cyrus again, and this time her gaze snagged, caught, held. "Your arms," she said. "You're marked. Under the sleeves. I can see it in how you hold them."

Cyrus said nothing.

"You're one of theirs." Not accusatory. Almost wistful. "Or you will be. Oh, hells. Oh, *hells*." She started laughing, wet and awful. "It sent me a marked one. It's been sending me nothing for six years, six years of silence, and now it sends me *company*."

* * *

Her name was Maera. Just Maera; she said the rest had been drunk away, and she said it lightly, the way you mention a coat given to charity. She wouldn't say more than that. She fed Cyrus barley soup that tasted of woodsmoke and stubbornness and sat across the fire from her with her bandaged arms folded like a penitent.

"The Lethe-water," Cyrus said, when the silence had gone on long enough. "There isn't any within fifty leagues of here."

"There's a spring under the altar." Maera poked the fire. "There always is. Every hearth sits on a spring, if you dig deep enough. That's the whole theology, really—fire above, forgetting below, and us in the middle trying not to fall in either direction." She smiled, and it was the smile of someone who had rehearsed being fine. "I drank deep. Most come to the spring wanting to forget a lover or a debt. I wanted to forget everything, and the water obliged. Six clean years."

"And now?"

Maera's hands tightened around her bowl. "Now it won't stay forgotten. It started three weeks ago—little things. The taste of incense. A name half-heard in a dream. Then the dreams got teeth." She pulled back one sleeve, unwinding the bandage with clinical detachment, and Cyrus made herself look: the skin beneath was fissured in fine gold lines, like a plate glazed and fired wrong. "The god I served is waking. And apparently what's mine is his, including what I threw away. He's taking it back piece by piece, and he's not gentle about it. Memory has weight, taster. Did you know that? When it comes back after the water, it comes back heavy. Like swallowing stones."

"How do you know I'm a taster?"

"You taste everything before you eat it. You've done it four times since we sat down—micro-habit, lips first, wait, then swallow. Court-trained. Somebody paid good money to make you suspicious of soup." Maera's mouth twitched. "Old habit of mine, reading people. Or maybe not old. Maybe it came back last week and I think it's old. That's the trouble with drinking the river—you stop knowing which parts of you are survivors and which are salvage."

Cyrus turned her bowl in her hands. Outside, the wind moved through the empty offering-niches and made a low moaning chord, like a crowd sighing in its sleep.

"What does it want?" she asked. "Your god. When it talks through you."

"It hasn't said. It just—" Maera frowned, groping. "It *pours*. It pours things into me and waits to see if I break. Fragments. A corridor. A voice saying *you knew the price*. Hands counting out coin. My own hands." She looked up sharply. "Why are you here, taster? Truly. Nobody climbs to a dead shrine by accident, and marked ones don't wander."

And because the truth was a kind of poison too, and Cyrus had spent ten years learning to dose it precisely, she gave Maera a measured sip. "I'm running. From the chancellor. From—something I found out about myself. There's a place called the First Hearth, and I think I'm supposed to go there, and I would very much prefer not to."

Something moved behind Maera's eyes—recognition trying to surface, hitting the water-logged wreckage of six forgotten years, sinking. "First Hearth," she repeated. Her glowing arms trembled. "That word has weight. Why does that word have *weight*—"

"Don't fight it," Cyrus said quickly, hearing the panic rise. "If it hurts, don't—"

"It always hurts." Maera laughed, short and bitter. "That's the thing nobody tells you about the gods coming back, taster. Everyone prays for miracles. Nobody prays for the bill."

* * *

Night came down cold off the glacier, and Cyrus woke to screaming.

She was on her feet with her knife drawn before her mind caught up—old training, the part of her that survived—and then she registered the source: the altar room, gold light strobing through the doorway like a forge seen through a keyhole.

Maera was on her knees before the spring, both arms plunged into it to the elbow, and the water around them steamed. Her head was thrown back. The voice coming out of her was layered now, two voices braided, hers and something vast speaking in the same throat:

"*Give it back. Give it BACK—*"

"No!" Maera tore her arms free and slammed them against the stone, as if she could shake the light loose like burning embers. "No, no, please—I gave it up, I *paid* for the giving, the water took it fair, that's the covenant, that's the *law*—" She caught sight of Cyrus in the doorway and something in her face collapsed into shame so total it was almost privacy. "Don't look. Please. Whatever you see—don't look at me while it happens."

Cyrus looked anyway. She owed the woman that much honesty, at least: witness without flinching.

The god poured. Cyrus watched it happen—watched Maera seize, watched images move behind her closed eyes the way storm-light moves behind curtains. Watched her whisper fragments aloud in a voice gone small and young: "*the corridor... the crown's messenger... he said it was mercy, he said if I didn't, there'd be a war...*" Watched her clap both ruined hands over her ears and scream at whatever was inside them: "*I DON'T WANT IT BACK!*"

And the god answered, through her own mouth, patient as erosion:

"*What you broke is yours to carry. We do not accept the river's mercy as payment. Nothing is forgotten. Nothing is forgiven until it is remembered.*"

Then it released her, all at once, the way a fist opens. Maera dropped face-down on the cold stone and lay there, steaming faintly, breathing in shudders.

Cyrus stood in the doorway with her knife still in her hand and understood, with a sick lurch, exactly what she was looking at.

A woman drowning upward. Dragged out of the merciful dark one memory at a time, each surfacing a wound, and no way to stop it that wasn't cruelty of a different kind. She could cut Maera's throat—mercy of the worst sort. She could find the spring and drink, and drown her own memories too, and she caught herself actually considering it, actually tasting the temptation on her tongue like sugar of annihilation: *never having learned what your hiring papers said, never having been built into a vessel, just a girl with a plain face and a dull job—*

No. She'd spent ten years curating what she knew. She was not going to start amputating it now.

Which left the third option. The one she'd walked up the mountain avoiding.

Healing meant forcing someone to remember. Or it meant standing next to them while they were forced, and making sure they didn't drown alone.

Cyrus sheathed the knife. She crossed the room, knelt in the spilled gold light, and did the one thing her whole hidden life had trained her for and her whole hidden life had starved: she put her hand on Maera's shoulder and stayed there.

"I'm not going anywhere," she said. It sounded absurdly formal, like words read off a card. She tried again. "Whatever it shows you next—I'll hold the bucket. You just bail."

Maera lifted her head. Her eyes were red-rimmed and exhausted and, underneath the ruin, desperately grateful, and she clearly hated being grateful. "You shouldn't," she rasped. "Anyone who stands near me ends up in its light. It'll start showing *you* things. It's already curious about you—I felt it sniffing at your blood the moment you walked in. Marked ones, it says. *Marked ones come home.*"

"I know what I'm marked for." Cyrus's jaw tightened. "Mostly. And I'm walking to it anyway, because the alternative is running forever, and I've done the math on running." She helped Maera sit up against the altar. "Come with us. Sefa's caravan goes south along the spine—we're passing under the Hearth itself in eight days. You can remember on the road. Loudly, painfully, with witnesses. Better than remembering here alone."

"Why would you want me?"

Because, Cyrus almost said, I know what it is to have your own body used as someone else's instrument without your consent. Because you're the first person in ten years who looked at my arms and saw the same scars. Because the gods are handing back everything we buried and neither of us gets to refuse the delivery.

What she said was: "Misery loves company, and I'm told I'm miserable company. We'll balance."

Maera stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, horribly, she began to laugh—the real laugh this time, cracked and rusty from disuse, doubling her over until she had to grip the altar. "Oh, *hells*," she gasped. "Six years of solemn penance and the gods send me a dry-voiced poison-taster to chaperone my apocalypse. That's—that's exactly the sort of joke They think is funny."

She wiped her eyes. Looked at her arms, glowing quietly in the dark like banked coals. Looked at the spring, black and patient under the altar.

"If I come," she said slowly, "it won't stop the pouring. It'll get worse. Closer to the Hearth, heavier. By the time we arrive, I may be nothing but memory wearing a body." She flexed her ruined hands, watching the gold seep. "And there's something else. Something it showed me just now, at the end, right before it let go—one image, clear as glass, and it *hurt*, taster, it hurt worse than all the others, which means it matters." She looked up, and her expression had changed: the wistfulness burned away, and underneath was something older and harder. A priestess's face. "A door. Under the mountain. And written over it, in the old script I'm apparently still literate in: *Only the forgotten may open what the forgotten closed.*"

The wind moaned through the offering-niches. Somewhere far below them, deeper than any spring, Cyrus felt—or imagined she felt—a vast attention shift its weight, like a sleeper rolling over toward morning.

"Pack your bag, priestess," Cyrus said. "We leave before dawn."
