## 7. Embers Below the Ice

The lake had a name once. Every place in Thornfell had a name once, and then the gods went quiet and the maps forgot, and now it was just the Ice to anyone who ran cargo through the high passes. Sefa had been calling it that for twenty years, and in twenty years she had never crossed it without stopping.

"You're detouring again," said Bren, her wheelman, watching her turn the lead wagon off the road. "Again. In winter."

"I'm detouring *always*," Sefa said. "It's not a detour if you planned it."

"It's a detour if it costs us two days."

"Then call it a pilgrimage." She flicked the reins and the mules leaned into the snow. "It costs less than what happens if we don't pay our respects."

Bren muttered something about superstition, which was rich coming from a man who wouldn't start a journey on a Friday or eat eggs on a Tuesday, but he held his tongue, because Bren was smart enough to know which of Sefa's rules were habits and which were debts. This one was a debt. The oldest one she carried.

Behind the wagons, the new passengers kept their heads down like good cargo. The taster woman — Cyrus — rode with the priestess who didn't remember being a priestess, both of them wrapped in wool and silence. Sefa had watched them for three days now. The taster watched everything and touched nothing, cataloguing the caravan's rhythms the way a thief catalogs a lock. The priestess flinched every time the wind made a sound like singing.

Sefa had hauled fugitives before. These two were different. These two came with weather attached. Since they'd joined, the old god-shrines along the road had started doing things — candle flames standing straight in crosswinds, a spring running sweet where it had run bitter for a decade. Small mercies, waking up like joints after a long sleep. And the taster woman's scars, when her sleeve slipped: thin silver lines across her forearms, glowing faintly at dusk, like banked coals remembering fire.

Sefa didn't ask. Asking was how you found out things you'd have to charge for.

* * *

The Ice lay in a bowl between two shoulders of mountain, flat and white and blind under the afternoon sun. Snowmelt rivers fed it in summer; in deep winter it froze clean through, down and down, and the cold came up out of it like something breathing slow.

Sefa stopped the caravan at the treeline, as always. You did not take iron wheels onto that ice. You walked the last quarter mile alone, as always, carrying what you'd brought.

This year she'd brought honey wine from the valley, a bolt of saffron-dyed silk, and a lump of raw amber the size of her fist. The same gifts, more or less, that she'd carried every winter since she was nineteen years old and stupid and dying.

"Wait here," she told Bren. "If I'm not back by dark—"

"I know. Burn the ledgers and run." He said it lightly. They both knew the ledgers were the closest thing Sefa had to a soul.

She shouldered the pack and stepped out onto the white.

The wind dropped the moment her boots left stone. It always did. As though the lake were listening for footsteps and preferred quiet ones. Halfway across, her breath hung in the air instead of scattering, and the light changed — the sun going low and gold too fast, the shadows of the peaks leaning in like an audience.

Sefa stopped at the center, over the deepest point, where in summer the water was black as a pupil.

"Ashvane," she said. Her voice cracked on the second syllable, the way it always did. "It's Sefa. Of the Thorn Road. I've come to trade."

For a long moment, nothing. Just the creak of ice settling, miles of it, a sound like a ship the size of a country changing its mind.

Then the ice beneath her feet lit from below.

Not sunlight. Not reflection. A glow rising through the frozen depths, amber-red, pulsing slow — slower than last year, Sefa noted, and dimmer, and her stomach tightened the way it did reading a ledger full of bad debts. She knelt, because kneeling was correct, and put her palm flat against the cold.

The ice melted under her hand in a perfect circle, and an eye looked back at her.

It was the size of a cartwheel, slit-pupiled, ringed in scales the color of cooled bronze gone green at the edges. It regarded her without blinking, because Ashvane had a second eyelid for that, horizontal, and it closed and opened like a slow breath. The eye was old. That was the thing no song prepared you for — not the size, not the fire, but the *age* in the look. Like being seen by a mountain that remembered your grandmother.

"Sefa of the Thorn Road," said a voice from everywhere — up through the ice, through her boots, through the bones of her skull. The voice had been magnificent once. Now it crackled at the edges, like a log splitting in a dying hearth. "You are late this year."

"The passes were watched. Chancellor's men on the south roads." Sefa kept her voice level. You didn't lie to a dragon — they heard heartbeats — but you could choose your truths carefully. "I came as soon as the way was clean."

"Liar," Ashvane said, almost fondly. "You came as soon as the way was profitable. But you came. That is more than most."

"What can I say. I'm sentimental about creditors."

A rumble moved through the ice, and it took Sefa a moment to understand it was laughter, and another moment to understand how much effort the laughter cost. The glow beneath them flickered like a lamp in wind. She had watched that light gutter year by year — bright as forge-fire when she was young, then ember-steady, and now this: a pulse, a pause, a pulse, each one weaker, like something counting down.

"How long?" she asked, before she could stop herself.

The eye narrowed. "Bold question for a debtor."

"I'm asking as a friend."

"You are asking as a smuggler who has just realized her smuggling route runs past a grave." The great eye drifted half-closed. "Longer than I feared. Shorter than I hoped. The fire does not replenish itself, little road-runner. Fire must be given purpose, or it eats its own house. My purpose broke forty years ago, and I have been burning my own walls ever since."

Sefa sat back on her heels on the ice, pack heavy on her shoulder, and felt the shape of the thing she'd come to do settle into place — and felt, underneath it, the unease she'd been carrying up this lake for twenty years. Because here was the truth of Sefa and Ashvane: she owed the dragon everything, and the dragon had never once named the price.

Nineteen years old, first winter running cargo solo, she'd taken the shortcut across this lake against every rule, and the ice had taken her wagon, her goods, her partner Joss, and very nearly her. She'd gone under. She remembered the cold as a kind of brightness, and she remembered deciding, with the strange calm of the drowning, that this was fair — that the mountain takes what the mountain takes.

And then the ice had broken *outward*, away from her, in a widening bloom, and something vast had risen beneath her and set her on the surface like a mother cat moving a kitten, and a voice had said: *Little thief. Your blood is warm. Warmth is currency here. What will you trade?*

She'd offered everything. Coin, cargo, her own life's labor. And the dragon had said: *Keep your coin. Keep your years. Trade me a favor, unnamed, to be called when I have need of one.*

Everyone knew better than to sign an unnamed favor. Everyone except a drowning girl with no options and a dragon who knew exactly what drowning girls would agree to.

Twenty years, and the favor had never been called. Twenty years, and Sefa had come back every winter with honey wine and saffron silk, not because the dragon wanted them — Ashvane ate sunlight and memory, not gifts — but because showing up was the only interest payment she could think of, and because some part of her believed that if she stayed generous long enough, the debt would stay dormant forever.

Now she looked down at the guttering light and understood, with cold clarity, that the favor was going to be called soon. Dying creatures settled accounts. And whatever a last keeper of covenant-fire needed from a smuggler, it would not be small, and it would not be cheap, and Sefa had built her whole life on knowing the price of things in advance.

"Open the pack," Ashvane said.

Sefa unpacked the gifts on the ice. The wine she poured into a depression that steamed and drank itself. The silk she spread; the amber she rolled forward until it slid through the melted circle and sank, swallowed without a ripple. The eye followed the amber longest. Dragons loved amber — trapped sunlight, old forests, time made solid.

"You bring me pretty things," Ashvane said. "Why?"

"Because you saved my life and I'm not allowed to repay you properly. So I bring bribes instead. It's how I apologize for still owing you."

"And do you still fear the debt?"

Sefa considered lying. The eye made it pointless. "Every day. An unnamed favor from something like you — I've lost sleep over it for twenty years. I've written wills around it. I've told my heirs—" She stopped. She had no heirs. "I've told myself stories about it."

"Good," said Ashvane. "Fear of an unmeasured debt is honest. It is the merchants who feel no such fear that I cannot abide." The glow pulsed, steadied, dimmed. "And yet you come. Every winter. Through watched passes. Why?"

Because the alternative was being someone who didn't come back, and Sefa had buried enough people to know that the world divided cleanly into those who returned to their debts and those who let them rot. She said none of this. "Habit," she said.

"Habit," Ashvane agreed, in the tone of one filing the lie under acceptable.

* * *

They traded, as they always did. News for news. Sefa gave the south: the chancellor's new edicts, the manhunt proclamations nailed to every gatepost, the temples shuttered in the capital, the prayers that had begun working again and the panic that followed. Ashvane gave the mountain: which passes would slide, where the ice-bridges had thinned, which valleys the wolves had abandoned — wolves always left a valley first when something older moved in.

At the end, as Sefa repacked the empty wine flask, the voice came again, quieter, and it was the quietness that frightened her.

"The travelers you carry. The scarred one and the forgetting one."

Sefa's hands went still. "What about them?"

"You know what they are. Or you suspect. You have suspected since the shrines began answering." The eye rose closer to the underside of the ice, filling her whole downward view, bronze and green and ancient. "I am going to ask you to bring them to me, Sefa of the Thorn Road. Tomorrow. At moonrise. To the far shore, where the stone shelf stands above the water."

"That wasn't the trade we agreed—"

"No. It is not the winter trade." The light beneath the ice gathered itself, dimmed, gathered. "It is the favor."

The words landed the way avalanches land — silently at first, with all the noise arriving after. Sefa knelt on the ice and felt twenty years of careful bookkeeping tear straight down the middle. All those winters of wine and silk, all the wills and contingency plans, all the comfortable assumption that the debt was a relic, a curiosity, a story to frighten apprentices — and here it was, called in full, on a frozen lake, in the voice of a dying god-beast.

"That's it?" she managed. "Bring them to the far shore?"

"For a creature of my age, arranging a meeting *is* the work. What passes at the shore will pass whether or not I lift a scale to make it possible." The eye half-closed. "But hear me, road-runner, because I will say this once, and I do not repeat myself for mortals: what sleeps in that woman's blood is the reason I am still alive, and the reason I am dying, and the only reason either of those facts matters. Bring her to the shore. After that, the debt between us is paid, and you will owe me nothing, ever, and you may spend the rest of your life free of me."

Free. The word should have tasted like relief. It tasted like the air before lightning.

"And if I refuse?" Sefa asked. "If I decide my passengers' business isn't mine to broker?"

The pause went on so long she thought the dragon might have died beneath the ice.

"Then I will die a few days sooner," Ashvane said at last, "and the covenant dies unkept, and the chancellor harvests what wakes in its place, and every road you run, little thief, becomes a road she owns. I will not threaten you. I have never threatened you. I am merely telling you what the world looks like tomorrow if you choose small today." A pulse of light, fainter than the last. "You asked how long I have. Ask yourself instead how long any of us have, once the keeping stops."

Sefa stood. Her knees cracked. Somewhere behind her, at the treeline, the caravan lamps were being lit, tiny and warm and impossibly far away.

"I'll bring them," she said. "Moonrise. Far shore."

"I know," said Ashvane. "You always pay."

The eye sank. The glow withdrew downward, down into the black deeps of the lake, a last red thread unspooling into darkness, and the ice sealed over her palm as though it had never been open, and the wind returned all at once like a door slamming.

Sefa stood alone at the center of the Ice for a while longer, listening to her own heartbeat slow.

* * *

Bren met her at the treeline with a cup of something hot and questions he was too well-trained to ask. Behind him, the campfires burned, and beside the nearest fire, wrapped in a borrowed blanket, the taster woman sat sharpening a knife she clearly didn't need, watching Sefa with those gray eyes that missed nothing.

"She spoke to you," Cyrus said quietly, when Sefa passed close.

It wasn't a question, and Sefa was too tired and too rattled to perform surprise. "You heard that from here?"

"I saw the ice glow." The knife whispered against the stone. "And I've been feeling something pulling at me since we entered the valley. Like a hook behind my ribs." Her scars, Sefa noticed, were faintly luminous in the firelight — brighter than they'd been an hour ago. Brighter, maybe, the closer she'd come to the lake. "Ashvane. That's the name. Isn't it."

Sefa set down her cup. Twenty years of running the Thorn Road had taught her every trick of deflection, and she reached for all of them, and found, to her irritation, that she didn't want to use a single one. There was something about this woman — this invisible woman who had spent a decade making herself nobody — that made lying feel like a waste of good craft.

"Aye," Sefa said. "Ashvane. Last keeper of the covenant's fire, if you believe the songs. Guttering toward the dark, if you believe my eyes." She crouched so they were level. "She's called in a debt I've owed her for twenty years. Tomorrow night. Moonrise. The far shore." She watched the taster's face carefully. "She wants you there. Both of you — you and the priestess. And I'd take it kindly if you didn't ask me why, because I don't know why, and not knowing has been sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone since sundown."

Cyrus looked past her, toward the lake, toward the darkening ice. The firelight caught the silver lines on her arms, and for just a moment they glowed like embers breathing.

"I think," the taster woman said slowly, "that I've been heading toward this shore my whole life. I just didn't know the address." She turned back, and there was something new in her face — not fear, exactly, though fear was in there somewhere, underneath. Something more like a woman hearing her own name called from a house she'd forgotten she owned. "Moonrise, then."

Sefa nodded and walked away before her expression could give anything else away.

In her tent, she took out her ledger — the real one, the one with the debts that mattered — and turned to the page she'd kept for twenty years. One line, in her youngest handwriting: *Ashvane, the Ice — one favor, unnamed.* She dipped her pen. Her hand shook, just slightly, and she hated it.

She struck the line through. Paid in full.

It should have felt like freedom. Instead she sat staring at the crossed-out entry, feeling lighter and poorer at once, the way you feel when you finally sell the family land and realize the land was holding you to the world. Outside, the wind came off the lake carrying a smell that had no business in midwinter: woodsmoke and honey and something older, something like sun-warmed stone.

And beneath the ice, faint but unmistakable even at this distance, the light pulsed once — twice — like a heart remembering its rhythm.

Like something waiting for moonrise.
