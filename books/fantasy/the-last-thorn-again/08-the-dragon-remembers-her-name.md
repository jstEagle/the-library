## 8. The Dragon Remembers Her Name

The lake beneath the ice was not water the color of anything. It was black, and it moved, and when Sefa led them out onto it at dawn—boots wrapped in felt so they made no sound—Cyrus understood that they were walking on the lid of something.

"Stay off the blue patches," Sefa said over her shoulder. She said it the way she said most things: like a woman discussing freight rates. "Blue means thin. Blue means you're a gift."

"A gift to what?" Maera whispered. The priestess had not slept well. None of them had, since the memories had begun crawling back into her like ants returning to a disturbed nest. She kept pressing her knuckles against her temple as though she could hold the door shut from inside.

Sefa glanced back, and for once her grin had no humor in it at all. "To what's owed."

Cyrus counted their steps. It was an old habit—count everything, measure everything, know your exits—and it steadied her the way counting always did. Forty paces onto the lake. Sixty. Beneath her boots the ice sang in long, low notes, a sound like a struck cable, and each note traveled outward and did not echo, because nothing out here gave it back.

The caravan had camped two miles south while Sefa brought only four of them: herself, Cyrus, Maera, and the boy Tam with the sled. The rest were told it was a salt run. Smugglers lied the way rivers ran downhill; Cyrus had stopped questioning it around the second day on the road.

"Why am I here?" Cyrus asked. "You said a trade."

"I did." Sefa stopped walking. Ahead of them the ice changed—there was no other word for it. The black turned smoky, then gold-flecked, as if someone had stirred embers into the frozen lake and let them drift. Heat shimmered above the surface in a place where their breath still fogged. "And I told you before we came up the mountain that I owed your mother a debt I couldn't pay in coin. This is part of how I'm paying it. What comes next isn't mine to arrange. It's yours. I just have to get you close enough."

"Close enough to *what*?"

Sefa didn't answer. Instead she took three more careful steps forward, knelt, and knocked on the ice. Twice, then once. A rhythm. A knock at a door.

The ice went silent.

Not quiet—silent. Every creak, every groan of settling frost across half a mile of frozen lake simply stopped, as though the whole sheet were holding its breath. Cyrus felt the silence in her teeth. Then, far below, something exhaled, and the exhale rose through forty feet of black water and ten feet of ice and came up under their feet as warmth.

The gold-flecked patch spread. Widened. Became a circle twenty feet across, glowing from beneath like a lamp behind horn.

"Off the circle," Sefa said calmly, stepping backward. "Everyone off. It doesn't like being stood on when it opens."

They scrambled clear. The glowing ice bulged upward, cracked in a spiral, and peeled open like the petals of some enormous frozen flower—and heat rolled out of it, dry and enormous, carrying a smell Cyrus knew from nowhere and recognized instantly: hot stone, old smoke, and underneath it something sweeter, like cedar burned in a temple. Steam geysered. Snow at the edges flashed to vapor.

And up through the hole, slow as a ship surfacing, came an eye.

It was the size of a cart wheel. It was the color of a banked fire—orange shot through with veins of grey ash—and it had a lid like a shield sliding aside, and lashes like charred reeds, and when it fixed on them Cyrus felt it the way she imagined a rabbit felt a hawk's shadow: total, inescapable attention. Her whole body wanted to run. Ten years of training held her still, because running from something this large accomplished nothing except dying tired.

"*Ashvane*," Sefa said, bowing—not deep, smuggler-deep, one hand over her heart. "I've come about the passage. And I've brought you something better than the usual salt and iron. I've brought you a question."

The eye blinked. When it opened again, both eyes had risen now, twin lanterns above the broken ice, and between them the lake's surface bowed downward as a vast skull pressed up against the underside of the world. Frost steamed off scales the color of slate and ember.

*"Passage,"* said the dragon.

Her voice did not come through the air so much as through the ground, through Cyrus's boot-soles, through the bones of her legs, up her spine and into the roots of her teeth. It was a woman's voice worn down to bedrock by centuries of disuse.

*"You want my sky. You always want my sky. And you bring me salt and iron and stories, and every year I am smaller, and every year your debts grow heavier."*

"The debt grows lighter," Sefa said steadily. "Because this time I'm paying interest." She stepped sideways—deliberately, Cyrus realized later, the way a dealer turns a card—and put her hand on Cyrus's shoulder and drew her forward. "Look at her."

The dragon looked.

There is no describing what it is to be looked at by a dragon. Cyrus had spent her entire adult life perfecting the art of not being looked at—the downcast eyes, the forgettable face, the body folded small as a kitchen chair. She had walked past the king himself and left less impression than his wine steward. She was, by craft and by long discipline, nobody.

Ashvane's gaze landed on her like a brand on hide.

*"No,"* the dragon said softly. *"No. Say it isn't—"*

She surged upward. The whole lake heaved; cracks shot out from the opening like black lightning; Tam fell on his sled and Sefa grabbed him by the collar without looking away from the water. Ice slabs the size of barn doors tilted and slid, grinding, and through the widening hole rose a head longer than the caravan's lead wagon—wedge-shaped, scarred along the jaw, horns worn blunt like old tools. Steam poured off her. Her breath washed over them, hot enough to make their eyes stream, smelling of that temple-cedar sweetness, and her nostrils flared once, twice—

And then Ashvane made a sound that Cyrus would hear for the rest of her life.

It began as a roar and broke apart halfway out of her throat into something else entirely—a cracked, keening note, high and ragged, the sound a creature makes when grief arrives decades late and finds the wound still open. It echoed off the mountain walls across the valley. Snow avalanched somewhere distant, a soft white thunder. Birds burst up from the treeline in a dark scatter.

*"Dravenmoor,"* the dragon breathed. *"You are Dravenmoor."*

The name hit Cyrus like cold water.

Not Draven. Not the false surname on her falsified hiring papers, the one the archive had shown her, the lie built over a lie. The full thing. The whole weight of it. She had never heard it spoken aloud in her life. Her mother had never said it. Her mother had burned the recipe cards' margins rather than say it.

"How do you know that name," Cyrus said. Her voice came out flat. Taster's voice. Steady hands, level tone, even when the dish is trying to kill you.

The great head lowered until one eye hung before her, near enough that she could see herself reflected in it—small, warped, standing very straight. Near enough that she could see the grey veins in the orange iris, and understand suddenly that those veins were not age.

They were ash. The dragon was going out, the way a fire goes out, vein by vein, ember by grey ember.

*"I kept the fire for your family,"* Ashvane said, *"before there was a crown in the lowlands. Before there were smugglers on these roads. Your line lit the First Hearth and I carried its flame, and when the covenant broke I carried the last coal of it out of the ruin in my own mouth so it could not be trampled."* The eye narrowed. *"I swore I would know the blood again if it ever came down my mountain. Do you know how long a promise keeps when the keeper is dying? Two hundred years, little heir. Two hundred years, and every winter colder, and every spring fewer prayers answered, and I thought—I *knew*—the line had guttered out with the covenant."*

"I'm not—" Cyrus started, and stopped, because it was a lie and they both knew it. She was not a taster named Cyrus Draven. She was a vessel somebody had been building since childhood, dosed ritual by ritual, poison by poison, shaped in secret toward a purpose written in a falsified record twenty years old. Her immunity wasn't luck. Her scars burned when gods woke. Her body had never been hers.

But hearing it in a dragon's voice was different from reading it in an archive. Reading was information. This was verdict.

"What do you want from me?" she asked.

*"Want?"* Ashvane laughed, and it was a terrible sound, like a landslide in a chimney. *"Child, I don't want. I *owe*. That is the shape of my whole wretched life—everything I love becomes a debt. Your ancestor gave freely and I swore freely, and oaths outlive their makers."* The head swung toward Sefa. *"You said you brought me a question. Ask it, thief, and be quick. Looking at her is like looking at the hearth of a house I burned down."*

Sefa cleared her throat, and for the first time since Cyrus had met her, the smuggler looked uncertain. "The Thorn Road's closed above the Split. Veyra's soldiers hold every pass, and there are hunters on the ice roads wearing crown badges and no crown manners. We need your sky. One flight, over the wall of the world, down to the shrine valleys. In exchange—" she hesitated— "in exchange for whatever favor you name. Unnamed. Open-handed. That's the offer."

*"An open debt,"* Ashvane murmured. *"From a smuggler. The world truly is ending."* Then the eye returned to Cyrus, and all the weariness in it sharpened into something else. Something that made Cyrus's skin prickle. *"No. Not yet. First there is a matter older than your passage."*

"Which is?"

*"Your name,"* said the dragon, *"was taken from you. Names can be stolen—your chancellors and your archivists are very good at theft—but a name given back by fire cannot be ungiven. Understand me, child. I am the last keeper of the covenant's flame. What I speak, the old law hears. If I call you what you are, you will never again walk through a room unseen. Kings will feel you enter. Gods will turn their heads. Every hunter in Thornfell will find that the trail of you has grown warm and bright and easy to follow."* The great jaw worked, as though the words themselves were heavy. *"So I ask first. Because consent is the whole of the old law, and I have watched what happens when it is skipped."*

The ice creaked around them. Somewhere under the lake, the black water shifted.

Cyrus stood very still.

This was the moment she should refuse. Every instinct she owned screamed it. Ten years of small doses and downcast eyes and deliberate mediocrity—ten years of building a self so dull that power itself overlooked her—and here was a creature offering to strip all of it away with one word, on a frozen lake, with the chancellor's hunters already on the roads below. Being seen was death. Being seen was the whole game she'd survived by refusing to play.

But.

But the scullery maid's stew had unmade itself, and her scars had sung, and the archive had told her that her invisibility was never hers either—that it too had been built into her, installed, a lock someone else had forged and fitted. She had spent a decade proud of armor she hadn't made, guarding a life that was itself a kind of cage. Survival bought at the cost of belonging. Her mother had died alone in a rented room under a false name, and Cyrus had called that wisdom.

Her mouth was dry. "If you say it," she heard herself ask, "will it hurt?"

*"Yes,"* Ashvane said gently. *"Everything true does, the first time."*

Maera made a small sound beside her. When Cyrus glanced over, the priestess's eyes were wide and wet, and her lips moved around words she couldn't quite catch—one of the returning memories surfacing like a drowned body. "*Say it*," Maera whispered. "Gods forgive me, I remember hearths. I remember the naming-words. *Say it.*"

Cyrus looked back at the dragon. At the grey veins spreading through the fire of her eyes. At the last keeper of a broken bargain, asking permission with the last of her strength.

"Do it," Cyrus said.

Ashvane drew breath. The whole lake inhaled with her—the water pulled, the ice flexed, the steam reversed and funneled inward—and Cyrus felt the pressure of that gathered breath against her chest like a hand. Then the dragon spoke, and it was not loud. It was worse than loud. It was precise.

*"Hear the old law, hear the flame that remembers: I name you Cyrus, daughter of Elira, of the line of Hearthkeepers. Dravenmoor of the First Hearth. Last thorn of the burning house. Rise up and be seen."*

The words went through her like arsenic and like antidote at once.

It hurt exactly as promised. It started in her scars—every one of them, the whole decade's pharmacology mapped across her body in small silver lines—and they lit, all together, white-hot, and the heat did not stay on her skin. It went *in*. It went into her blood and her marrow and whatever deep structure the poisons had been quietly rebuilding all these years, and Cyrus felt it find its fittings, socket after socket, a mechanism completing itself with a series of clicks she heard from inside her own bones. She did not scream. Tasters don't scream. But she dropped to one knee on the ice and her vision whited out and for a moment she was aware of nothing except the enormous, unbearable sensation of being *perceived*—not by the dragon, but by everything. The mountain. The sky. The sleeping things under the world, turning over in their sleep, noticing.

Then it passed, and she knelt in the steam, breathing hard, and she was different.

She couldn't have said how, at first. Then Tam, the boy, helped her up—and flinched. Not from her touch. From her *face*. He stared at her the way people stare at a bonfire in a field at night, and couldn't stop staring, and didn't know why. Sefa looked too, and the smuggler's famous composure cracked straight down the middle.

"You're—" Sefa started. Stopped. Tried again. "Ten minutes ago I'd have sworn on my mother's grave I could lose you in any market in any city in the world. Now I—" She rubbed her eyes. "Now you're the only thing on this lake. Like someone lit a lamp behind your ribs."

Cyrus raised her hands. They looked the same. They felt like they were humming.

"It's the name," Maera said hoarsely. Tears ran freely down her face and she seemed unaware of them. "A true name given by fire can't be hidden. Eyes will slide off everyone else in the room and land on you. Hunters will dream about you before they ever see you. Oh, child. Oh, you foolish, brave child. Do you understand what you've done? Veyra won't need records to find you now. The world will point at you like a compass needle."

"Good," Cyrus said.

They all looked at her. She looked back, and found that her hands had finally gone steady—not taster-steady, the trained stillness of a woman pretending not to be afraid, but something underneath that. Something that didn't shake because it had stopped pretending.

"They were going to find me anyway," she said. "Better they find someone who exists."

Above them, Ashvane exhaled—a long plume of smoke and sparks rising into the pale morning—and settled her chin on the broken edge of the ice like an old dog too tired to stand through the whole conversation. The motion opened her throat, and Cyrus saw it then, down in the dark of the dragon's chest: a single point of light behind the scales, small and stubborn, pulsing faintly. Guttering.

Like a candle in a draft. Like a coal in a dead hearth.

*"Well,"* Ashvane rumbled, and there was something almost like humor in it now, exhausted and rueful. *"That's done, and the sky knows your name, and there's no taking it back. So."* The great eye lifted to Sefa. *"About your passage, thief. I will fly you over the wall of the world. But I am dying—you can all see it, don't pretend otherwise—and a dying dragon does not make the crossing quickly or gently. And the route I must take passes directly over the shrine valleys."*

The dragon paused, and the pulse of light behind her breastbone flickered, and when she spoke again her voice had gone quiet enough that they leaned in to hear it over the lap of black water against broken ice.

*"Which means, little Dravenmoor, that before we fly, you should know what waits below us in those valleys. Because the priests there have been praying to me for years. And lately—" the grey veins in her eye seemed to darken, "—lately, something has been answering them in my voice. And it is not me."*
