## 9. Veyra Closes the Net

 Chancellor Veyra had always believed that a realm was a garden, and that most gardeners failed for the same reason: they loved what grew instead of loving what survived.

She stood in her greenhouse before dawn, sleeves rolled, hands black to the wrist with loam, and considered the row of godsbane before her. Nightshade-kin, but older. The leaves drank light and gave back nothing—a plant that photosynthesized silence. It had taken her nine years to cultivate it from three shriveled cuttings to this: forty healthy specimens, each one proof that divinity could be grown in dirt, pruned with shears, kept in its pot.

The old gods were waking. She had known it for eleven days, since the first report out of the kitchens—a stew unmaking itself, a scullery girl babbling about a god with no name. Most ministers would have called it hysteria. Veyra had called it a forecast.

Weather came. You did not argue with weather. You built roofs.

"You didn't sleep," said Corvus from the doorway.

Her spymaster never announced himself; he simply arrived, like damp. She did not turn from the godsbane. "Neither did you."

"I slept four hours on a chair like a gentleman." He came to stand beside her, a thin man with ink-stained fingers and the patient eyes of a man who had read every letter ever intercepted in the capital. "The taster's gone."

"I know she's gone."

"Then let me say the rest of it, because someone has to say it while you're talking to plants." He picked up her pruning knife, turned it over, set it down again. "Cyrus Dravenmoor left the palace two nights ago through the scullery gate. She took nothing but a knife, a cloak, and a packet of recipe cards. She bribed no one, killed no one, and vanished into Thornfell like smoke down a chimney. Either she's the luckiest runaway in history, or she knew exactly which roads would be watched and which wouldn't."

"She knew," Veyra said. "That's the point of her. That was always the point of her."

"Then you'll want to hear what the archive clerk found." He held out a folder, thin as a hymnal.

Veyra dried her hands slowly. She did not reach for it yet. There was a discipline to bad news: you let it wait until your hands were clean, so that whatever you did next could not be blamed on trembling.

Twenty years ago, a hiring ledger entry had been altered. The original ink scraped away with a blade fine enough to leave the parchment unscarred—someone very good, or someone very practiced. Beneath the forgery, restored by lamp and patience, was the true record: *Child of Hearth-line Dravenmoor. Regimen approved by Crown Charter. Administered per covenant protocol.*

Covenant protocol. Words that should have meant nothing, because the covenant was dead, because the gods were dead, because the First Hearth had gone cold before Veyra was born. And yet there they were, in a crown archive, attached to the name of a food-taster who had spent a decade swallowing poison on schedule and never once sickened.

"The regimen wasn't wages," Corvus said. "It was liturgy. Someone was building that woman the way you'd build a bridge—load-bearing, tested daily."

"Who signed the charter?"

"The chancellor who preceded your predecessor. The trail dies there. Burned, probably. But Veyra—" he hesitated, which he almost never did, "—there's a marginal note. In the old hand. It says: *the vessel must not know, lest knowing change the clay.*"

Veyra finally took the folder. She read the note twice. Somewhere behind her sternum, something cold settled into place, the way a keystone settles when the last stone is laid.

They had made her a vessel and left her ignorant so the ignorance would hold her shape. Every meal Cyrus Dravenmoor had tasted, every grain of arsenic she'd metabolized and shrugged off, had been a brick in a temple she couldn't see. And now the gods were waking, and the temple was walking out the door, and somewhere in the Thornfell mountains a half-made covenant was looking for its keystone.

"They're going to find her," Corvus said. "Or she's going to find them. Either way—"

"Either way we lose the only weapon in the realm that works on gods." Veyra set the folder down on her workbench, beside the shears. "Yes. I'd reached that part."

* * *

The council chamber smelled of beeswax and fear.

Veyra had requested the emergency session at the fourth bell, when the ministers would be rawest—woken early, fed late, already whispering about the stew incident, which had grown in the retelling until the scullery maid was a prophetess and the burnt stew a sign of the apocalypse. Fear, properly harvested, was the most renewable resource in any kingdom. She intended to harvest all of it.

She did not bring the folder. She brought one page, copied fair, and she laid it on the table where everyone could see the seal.

"Lords and ladies. Nine days ago, a dead god answered a prayer in this palace. Three days ago, our archives confirmed that the woman who tasted His Majesty's food—the woman who stood between him and death for ten years—was never a servant at all. She is the last heir of the Hearth-line. The bloodline bound into the old covenant. The bloodline the old gods answer first."

She let the murmur build, then cut it off with one raised hand. That was another lesson of gardening: things grew toward whatever light you allowed.

"The records show her poisoning regimen was a ritual preparation, sanctioned under charter twenty years ago, designed to make her body capable of carrying divine fire. Capable of carrying it *for whoever holds her.*" She paused. "And three days ago she fled to Thornfell. To the mountains. Where the old altars are."

Lord Aldery, Minister of Granaries, went gray. "You're saying she's been poisoned into sainthood."

"I'm saying she's been *prepared* into a weapon, my lord, and that whoever aimed her wanted her ignorant of it—which means we cannot know who else has claims on her. A vessel doesn't choose what pours into it. That is the entire horror of vessels." Veyra folded her hands. "I am not accusing her of treason. Treason requires intent, and everything we know suggests she genuinely does not understand what she is. But I am telling you that the old gods are waking, they are answering prayers without consent or contract, and their chosen instrument has just walked out of our custody and into their arms."

"And the king?" asked Lady Merren, ancient and sharp. "Does His Majesty know his soup-taster was a sacrament?"

"His Majesty knows the gods are returning, and that his grandfather's generation broke the bargain that put them to sleep, and that when gods wake angry they do not knock." Veyra let her voice drop, gentler, the tone she used for truths too heavy to shout. "Here is what frightens me, my lords. Not the woman. The woman ate poison for ten years and saved a king she never met. If she were ours—if we could sit her down and explain—she might even help us. But she isn't ours anymore. She's running toward the First Hearth with smugglers and worse, and every day she goes unclaimed, the covenant remakes itself around her whether we will it or not. Divine law, imposed without mortal consent. That is how realms die. Not in fire—in paperwork. In terms nobody read because the terms were written by something immortal."

Merren narrowed her eyes. "So what do you propose?"

"A manhunt," Veyra said simply. "Writs for her detention, not execution—let that be understood. We take her alive, we shelter her, we give her the choice her handlers never gave her. And if she refuses the crown's protection..." She spread her hands. "Then she has chosen the gods over the realm, and we treat her as what she has become."

Aldery shifted. "And if the people won't give her up? Half the city's praying to the old names now. They think the miracles are a mercy."

Veyra smiled, and it cost her nothing, because it wasn't cruel—it was tired. That was the trick of it. Cruelty was loud; conviction sounded exhausted, burdened, reasonable.

"Then we remind the city what mercy costs," she said. "A god answers a prayer today. Which prayer does it refuse tomorrow? Whose child does it overlook? We do not worship caprice in this realm. We replaced it once, with law, and law is why no farmer starves waiting for a sky-father's mood to lift. I am not hunting a woman, my lords. I am defending the idea that mortals get a vote."

They voted. Of course they voted. Eleven writs, sealed by noon.

* * *

By evening, the city had the story, and the story had teeth.

Veyra's criers worked from the gates inward, and the tale they told was precise, calibrated, almost gentle: *A woman raised in secret to carry the old gods' power has fled justice. She is not accused of a crime against persons. She is accused of a crime against the realm's freedom. Bring her to the chancellor's men unharmed. There is a reward. There is also, for those who harbor her, a reckoning.*

Corvus found her on the palace balcony at dusk, watching the city kindle below. Lantern by lantern, the capital lit itself, and somewhere in that glow were ten thousand conversations turning on Cyrus Dravenmoor's name.

"You called her a weapon in there," Corvus said. "In the streets, by now, she's a monster. The broadsheets have her drinking blood."

"The broadsheets have her drinking blood because frightened people draw monsters faster than they draw strangers. It buys us a week of urgency. When we have her in custody, I'll correct the record personally." 

"And if correcting it comes too late? If some bounty man decides dead things can't run?"

"Then I'll hang him myself," Veyra said, and meant it, and Corvus blinked, because she did mean it. "Listen to me. This is not vengeance. I have never met the woman. I don't hate her. I *pity* her—she was built in a cellar by men who are dust now, and she never agreed to any of it. That is precisely why she cannot be permitted to finish becoming whatever she's becoming. Her whole life was decided without consent. I will not add the gods' consent on top of it. Somebody in this kingdom has to say: the chain ends here. No more vessels. No more covenants written in other people's bodies."

Corvus was quiet a long moment. "You sound like a sermon."

"I sound like a gardener. Things grow toward whatever light you allow, Corvus. Right now the light is coming from the mountains, and it is *hungry*, and it will grow through us if we let it." She turned from the rail. "Send riders to every pass. Double the bounty on information. And pull the file on Sefa's caravan—she owes debts in Thornfell, and debts run along roads. If the taster reaches the First Hearth—"

"If she reaches the First Hearth," Corvus said, "what happens?"

Veyra looked at him, and for one unguarded moment the chancellor fell away and there was only a woman standing in the dark above a city full of lamps, holding the same terror every mortal had held since the first storm: *something enormous is paying attention to us, and it does not love us.*

"Then mortals stop mattering," she said. "That's what happens. Gods don't share, Corvus. They never have. That's why we put them to sleep."

She went inside to draft the second wave of writs. Behind her, the city burned bright against the coming dark, every window a small defiance, every flame an argument that warmth belonged to the people who made it—and far to the north, past the passes, past the ice, the mountains waited with the patience of things that had outlasted every garden ever planted over their graves.
