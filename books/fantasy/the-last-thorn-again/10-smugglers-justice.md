## 10. Smugglers' Justice

Sefa smelled the stranger before she saw him, which was the first wrong thing. On the Thorn roads you smelled everything—mule sweat and pitch and the sour bite of high-altitude pine—but a man who carried no smell at all, who'd scrubbed himself with ash and cold water to sit among people whose lives depended on noticing, that was a man with training.

She let him walk with the caravan for two days.

That was the test, mostly. Two days of shared fire, shared watch rotations, shared complaints about the weather and the mules and Doss's singing. The caravan had rules older than Sefa's grandmother: nobody asked where cargo came from, nobody asked where it went, and everybody pulled their weight. The stranger pulled his weight. He laughed at the right times. He even took a turn scraping the ice off the axles without being asked twice.

But he watched the girl.

Not the way men usually watched Cyrus Dravenmoor—which was not at all, which was the whole point of her, the way she could stand in a room and be furniture. The stranger watched her *carefully*. He counted her steps. He noted when she ate and what she refused. On the second night, when she'd gone down toward the stream alone, he'd shifted his bedroll three feet closer to the trail before anyone saw him do it.

Three feet. Toward an exit route. A hunter's habit.

So on the third morning, when they came down through the pass into the hollow below Grey Cairn—the old barrow with its guts carved out by smugglers three generations back, big enough to hold forty people and every illegal thing they carried—Sefa put her hand on his shoulder as he ducked under the lintel stone.

"Welcome to the trial," she said pleasantly.

He went very still. Good instincts. Bad position. Six of her crew had drifted behind him over the last hour, casual as clouds, and now the cairn mouth was blocked by Wren and her crossbow and Doss holding a mattock like he'd been born wanting to use it.

"I don't know what you mean," the stranger said.

"Course you don't." Sefa steered him inside, into the round chamber where the walls sweated mineral light and the old bones in the niches had been there so long they were just suggestions of people. "Here's how this works. We're not soldiers. We don't have cells or chains or any interest in hauling you anywhere. But we have a road, and the road is ours, and you came onto it hunting something. So we ask questions until we believe your answers, or we don't."

"And if I don't answer?"

"Then the mountain keeps what it catches." She said it lightly, because that was how you said terrible things on the Thorn roads, and because half of it was theater. Mostly half.

The stranger sat where they pointed him, in the center of the chamber, on a flat stone that generations of smugglers called the Answering Seat. He was good-looking in a forgettable way, brown hair, brown coat, a face built for wanted posters that never quite matched. His eyes moved constantly—not scared, Sefa noticed. Cataloguing. Exits, numbers, weapons.

"Name," said Wren.

"Corren Hale. Bounty charter out of Vellmark."

Honesty. Interesting. Liars usually started with lies.

"Chartered for whom?"

He hesitated, and the hesitation told Sefa more than the name would have. "Independent," he said finally. "I take postings from the magistrate boards. There's one up right now. Woman, twenty-five, thirty. Traveling under false papers. Wanted for poisoning."

The chamber went quiet enough that Sefa could hear the mountain drip.

"Poisoning," Wren repeated flatly.

"Of the king's table. They say she served the crown for years and turned her coat." Corren's gaze flicked—there and gone—toward the shadows at the chamber's edge, where Cyrus stood with her arms crossed, watching everything with that stillness of hers. "They say she's a heretic too. That the gods waking up is her doing."

"They say," Sefa echoed. She strolled a slow circle around the Answering Seat. "And who says? Because last I checked, the magistrate boards in Vellmark don't post heretics. Heresy charges come from the capital. From the chancellery." She stopped. "From Chancellor Veyra."

Corren said nothing, which was an answer.

"Here's my trouble, Corren Hale." Sefa crouched to his eye level, forearms on knees. "You've eaten our food for two days. You've sung—badly—with Doss. You know our faces, our number, our route. If I let you walk out of here with a bounty posting in your pocket, you sell us all. Not because you're evil. Because it's your job. Because somebody paid you and paying is what makes it real." She sighed. "So the question isn't whether you're a danger. You are. The question is what we do about it."

Wren raised the crossbow slightly. Doss spat. Old Piet, who'd been silent in the corner the whole time, said in his dust-dry voice, "The cairn's got room."

It wasn't a threat, exactly. It was worse than a threat. It was logistics.

And that was when Cyrus stepped out of the shadows.

"No."

One word, quiet, but it landed in the chamber like a dropped anvil. Everyone looked at her—including Corren, whose breath actually caught, because up close and lit by the mineral glow, with everyone's attention on her, she stopped being furniture. Sefa felt it herself, that odd vertigo of suddenly seeing someone who'd spent days being invisible.

"She's—" Corren started.

"I know what she is." Sefa didn't take her eyes off Cyrus. "Cargo speaks when spoken to. That's the rule."

"The rule is wrong." Cyrus's voice stayed level, but Sefa heard the effort under it, the sound of a woman prying open a door she'd kept locked her whole life. "You want to kill him because he might betray you. But you haven't asked him anything that matters."

"What matters?" Wren snapped. "He's a bounty man."

"You're all bounty men," Cyrus said. "Every one of you. You move stolen goods past starving farmers and call it freedom, and you'd gut anyone the law points at, because the law pointing means you get to feel righteous while you do it." She walked forward into the light, into the middle of everything, and Sefa watched her do it like watching a swimmer go off a cliff into deep water. "Ask him why he took the posting. Ask him if he read the charge sheet. Ask him if the woman described matches me, or if it matches fifty women, because that's how a hunt works—it doesn't need to find the right person, only a person."

Corren's jaw tightened. "The description's specific."

"It's specific enough to be wrong. Brown hair, scar on the left forearm." She pushed up her sleeve, showed the long pale line there. "I've had that since I was nine. I fell out of a plum tree. Shall we compare scars with every woman on the mountain?"

"Why should we believe you?" Doss rumbled.

"You shouldn't. Believing's not the point." Cyrus turned to face them all, and Sefa realized with a small cold shock that the girl was shaking—but standing anyway. "The point is what you do. He came to take a prisoner. Fine. That's his trade; I won't pretend trades are clean, mine least of all. But you're talking about killing a man in a hole in the ground because he's inconvenient. I've seen what happens to places that start doing that. It doesn't stop with him."

Silence again. Longer this time. Sefa looked around her crew—at Wren's white-knuckled crossbow, at Piet's unreadable eyes, at Doss studying the floor like the stones might vote—and understood that whatever happened next would decide what the caravan *was*, forever after. Every crew reached a moment like this. Hers was happening in a barrow full of dead ancestors with a stranger's life balanced on it.

"Piet," she said. "You've been quiet."

Piet shrugged. "Girl's not wrong. I've been on the other side of that arithmetic. Forty years back, a crew I ran with decided a boy was inconvenient." He scratched his jaw. "I still remember his boots. Don't remember his face. Just the boots. You don't forget being the kind of people who count a life as ballast."

"He'd sell us," Wren said. "Not might. Would."

"Maybe." Sefa stood. "Or maybe he takes the posting back to Vellmark and tells them the trail went cold in the high passes, because Corren Hale is a professional, and professionals know a dead bounty pays nothing." She looked down at him. "Is that true?"

Corren held her gaze. Sefa gave him credit; most men looked away by then. "If I walk out of here," he said slowly, "they'll know I found something. Charter-holders report progress. If I report nothing twice, they reassign, and the next one won't be careful like me."

"So report something," Cyrus said. "Report that you tracked her to a shrine near the ice lake and lost her in a storm. Report whatever's true enough to end the search." She paused. "Or don't. Walk out and keep hunting. But if you keep hunting, understand what you're hunting. Go on. Look at me. Really look."

He did. And Sefa watched something shift in his face—not belief, not yet, but the beginning of doubt, which on the Thorn roads was worth more than gold. Whatever he'd expected the poisoner-heretic of Veyra's posters to be, it wasn't a tired woman with a plum-tree scar asking him to be better than his job.

"Why do you care?" he asked her. "Two days ago none of these people knew you existed. Why spend yourself on me?"

Cyrus was quiet a moment. When she answered, her voice had something new in it—rough, unpracticed, like a muscle used for the first time.

"Because ten years ago, somebody decided I was inconvenient, and nobody in the room said no. I learned to survive by making sure I was never in the room at all." She glanced at Sefa, just briefly. "Then some fool smuggler dragged me into one. So. No."

*No.* One word carrying ten years of weight, set down between them like a stone on a cairn.

Sefa straightened up and clapped her hands once, loud in the hollow dark, and the sound broke the spell the way a good captain's ought to.

"Here's what happens," she announced. "Nobody dies today. Piet, take Corren's charter papers—we hold them until he's a day's walk clear of us, then he gets them back. Wren, give him food for the road, the good bread, not the stuff we feed Doss. And Corren Hale." She leaned in close. "You're going to carry a message for free. Tell whoever reads your reports that the Thorn roads have long memories and longer reach. Tell them a debt was owed to Halda Dravenmoor's daughter, and it's paid now, and we don't pay debts twice."

Corren went pale around the mouth. "Halda Dravenmoor," he repeated. "The map-woman. She ran the north passes twenty years ago. Every charter-holder learns that name—they teach it as a warning."

"They teach it as a *lesson*," Sefa corrected. "Different thing entirely."

They fed him, armed him with directions instead of a weapon, and walked him to the cairn mouth. At the lintel stone he stopped and turned back, and for a moment the professional mask slipped and there was just a tired man squinting into the grey morning.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the boards in Vellmark have gone strange lately. Postings appearing overnight, sealed orders, magistrates who won't say who's paying. Whatever's happening in the capital—" he glanced at Cyrus, then at the mountains beyond, where the sky above the high peaks had been flickering faintly green for three nights running, "—it's bigger than one woman running."

"We know," Sefa said.

"Do you? Because that light up there—" He pointed at the green shimmer. "Old-timers in Vellmark call it the gods' lanterns. Last time it burned like that, according to the archives, was the year before the Silence." He settled his pack. "Year before the dragons went missing and the prayers stopped answering. My grandmother said her grandmother wept for a week when the flames went out at the First Hearth."

Nobody spoke. Above the peaks, the green light pulsed once, slow, like breathing.

Corren nodded to them all—to Wren, to Piet, and last, longest, to Cyrus—and went down the trail toward the low country, and Sefa stood watching until the mist took him.

* * *

That evening the caravan camped in the lee of the cairn, fires banked low, and Sefa found Cyrus sitting apart on a flat rock, turning her mother's recipe cards over in her hands. The tattooed map-lines caught the firelight. Up close, Sefa could see the ink had changed—new lines surfacing along the old ones, faint as veins under skin, paths that hadn't been drawn twenty years ago.

"Your mother's map is redrawing itself," Sefa said, sitting down beside her.

"I noticed." Cyrus didn't look up. "It started after the dragon. After Ashvane said my name." She was quiet a moment. "You lied in there. About the debt being paid."

"I did?"

"Halda Dravenmoor saved your life. You told me. A debt like that doesn't get paid by feeding a bounty man bread." Now she looked up, and her eyes in the firelight were the eyes of someone who had spent a decade reading poisons in soup and had recently started reading people instead. "Why'd you really stand for me? Wren was right. Killing him was safer."

Sefa took her time answering. She watched the fire, and the green shimmer above the peaks, and thought about boots she couldn't forget either.

"When I was young," she said, "my mother told me the difference between cargo and kin. Cargo is what you carry because you're paid. Kin is what you carry because you can't not." She nudged Cyrus's shoulder with her own. "Two days ago you were cargo. Best-kept secret I ever hauled. Tonight you stood up in front of a loaded crossbow for a man who came to drag you to your death, because you couldn't not. That's not something I owe your mother. That's something I chose."

Cyrus stared at her. Sefa had the distinct pleasure of watching the words land somewhere the woman had clearly never been hit before—somewhere soft and long-disused, behind all the armor.

"Chosen," Cyrus repeated, like a foreign word.

"Don't make it weird," Sefa said gruffly, and threw a stick on the fire.

They sat in silence awhile, the two of them, while the caravan settled around them—Doss's snore starting up, Wren cleaning her crossbow, Piet humming something old. Overhead the green light pulsed, steady now, rhythmic, and Sefa found herself thinking it looked less like lanterns every hour and more like a heartbeat.

Then Wren came jogging up from the watch point, fast, no lamp.

"Sefa. Problem." She was breathing hard. "Rider coming up the switchbacks. Alone, but he's flying the chancellery colors—and behind him, maybe an hour back, torches. Twenty, thirty of them. Soldiers."

Sefa was on her feet before Wren finished. Beside her, Cyrus had gone very still, and when she spoke, her voice was the old voice—the invisible voice, the one that had survived ten years of court kitchens—but aimed now at something larger.

"The storm gate," she said. "There's a path my mother marked that runs above the ice lake. It's not on any chart. It climbs through the storm line itself." She was already rolling her recipe cards closed, already standing, and the green light above the peaks pulsed faster, like something answering. "We have one hour. And we'll need Ashvane."

Sefa looked at the thin thread of smoke rising far off from the frozen lake, where something ancient and guttering waited, and made the fastest bargain of her life.

"Wake the camp," she said. "We run the high road tonight."
