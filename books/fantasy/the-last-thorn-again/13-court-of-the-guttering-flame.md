## 13. Court of the Guttering Flame

I have carried ten thousand memories longer than your kingdoms have had names, and I will tell you a truth about them: they do not fade. They *settle*. Like ash in a hearth that has gone cold. You can stir them if you know where to rake.

The girl Cyrus Dravenmoor did not know where to rake. She walked into my dying mind the way a thief walks into a temple—softly, apologetically, one hand already raised in excuse—and she stumbled directly onto the coals I had guarded for three hundred years.

I could have turned her away. Understand that. A dragon's memory is a hoard, and hoards are defended. But I was guttering. My fire had burned down to a blue whisper in my chest, and there is a peculiar honesty that comes with dying: you stop guarding things and start *spending* them.

So when she touched the oldest ember and asked, in her small cautious voice, "What happened here?"—I opened the door.

*Come in, little heir,* I said, in the tongue that lives behind words. *Come see what your family built.*

* * *

She arrived standing in a courtyard of black stone under a sky the color of hammered copper, and I watched her do what humans always do first: check the exits. There were none. The courtyard was a ring, and the ring was a hearth, and the hearth was everywhere at once, because memory does not obey architecture.

"Is this real?" she asked me. She asked it the way she asked everything—as though the answer might be used against her later.

"It is truer than most things you've stood in," I told her. "It is remembered. Remembered things are the only ones that can't lie."

Three figures stood at the courtyard's center, and Cyrus went still in the way of prey animals, which is to say the way of survivors. I let her look.

The first was a woman in robes the grey of river fog, and even rendered in ember-light, even after three centuries, Maera would have known her own face. The priestess of the First Hearth, before the forgetting, before the bargain. Her hands were raised, palms open, holding nothing—which was the whole point of them.

The second was a man in travel-stained leathers, a road-speaker by his sash, scarred from ankle to jaw. He had no crown. That mattered. He had been offered crowns by four different realms and had refused all of them, on the grounds—his own words, carved into the First Hearth's lintel—that *a king can be bought, but a road cannot.*

And the third—

Cyrus made a sound low in her throat when she saw me. Not me as I am now, starved and ice-bound and blue-flamed. Me as I was: Ashvane the Hearthkeeper, wings like storm-fronts, fire banked in my chest not as a weapon but as a *promise*. The way a candle is a promise. The way a seed is.

"You," she whispered.

"I was younger," I said. "We all were. Watch."

* * *

The memory moved the way memory moves—not in scenes but in meanings, whole years folding into gestures.

I showed her the world before the covenant, because she needed to see it to understand anything that came after. Three realms, and none of them speaking. The gods above, answering prayers capriciously or not at all, pouring favor on one valley and drought on its neighbor for reasons no mortal could parse. The dragons below the mountains, hoarding our fire and our grudges, burning villages over slights decades old. And humanity in the middle, doing what humans do when the powers above and below treat them as game pieces: surviving alone, lying well, trusting nothing.

"The gods were not evil," I told her, as we watched a temple burn in a war started over an answered prayer. "Neither were we. We were *unbound*. Do you understand the difference? Evil intends. Unbound power merely falls, and it falls on whoever is underneath."

"And mortals were always underneath," Cyrus said. Not a question. She'd spent a decade underneath. She knew the view.

"Always underneath," I agreed. "Until three of them decided to stop asking politely."

* * *

Here is what the histories got wrong, and what Veyra's stolen archives got wrong, and what every crown that ever lusted after the covenant's power got wrong: the First Hearth was not forged. It was not won. It was not seized from anyone, and it could never have been.

It was *offered*.

The woman in grey—the priestess, Maera's predecessor in office if not in failure—had spent forty years walking between the realms, unarmed, unarmored, carrying nothing but a question. She asked it of the gods in their high temples and of the dragons in their deep halls and of kings in their palaces, and the question was always the same:

*What do you want that you cannot take?*

Most laughed at her. Some tried to kill her—I showed Cyrus the scar across the priestess's collarbone, given by a dragon who regretted it for the rest of his long life. But the question worked on her the way water works on stone, slowly, in the dark, until one day the god of the eastern harvest found himself unable to answer it and was so unsettled by this that he came down to the black courtyard himself to demand an explanation.

And she said: *You want to be trusted. You cannot take trust. It must be given.*

The god sat down on the courtyard stones like a tired farmer, and he did not leave for a year.

That was the beginning of the court. Not a court of law—a court of presence. Gods came, because for the first time in creation someone had named what they actually wanted. Dragons came, because the priestess asked us the same question and I, young and proud and secretly so lonely it felt like a sickness, discovered that what I wanted and could not take was *witness*. Someone to see the fire for what it was instead of what it destroyed.

"And the third realm?" Cyrus asked quietly. She already knew.

"You," I said. "Mortals. The road-speaker came last, and he wanted nothing for himself, which made him the most dangerous creature any of us had ever met."

* * *

I showed her the night of the making, because she needed to feel it in her body before she could understand it in her mind.

The courtyard full. Gods standing awkwardly among dragons, both of them uncertain around humans, all three kinds of power discovering that proximity itself was a kind of miracle no prayer had ever produced. No incense. No sacrifice. The priestess had forbidden both—*we are not feeding something, we are meeting it*—and so there was only a hearth, unlit, in the center of the ring.

The road-speaker spoke first. He did not make a speech; he made a list. Every broken promise the strong had ever made the weak, he named aloud, one by one, for hours, while gods and dragons stood and listened and some of them wept, because none of them had ever heard their failures spoken without flattery or fear attached.

Then the priestess spoke. She offered the terms: each realm keeps its nature. Fire stays fire. Divinity stays divine. Mortal law stays mortal law. None may consume another, none may command another, and the hearth between them burns only so long as all three tend it freely.

Then she turned to the gathered powers and said the words that Veyra has spent twenty years trying to find a loophole in, and that Cyrus's whole poisoned, hidden life had unknowingly prepared her to hear:

"This covenant is not made by consent once. It is made by consent *always*. Any hand that holds it unwillingly holds nothing. Any power taken rather than given is ash. Say yes tonight, and say yes tomorrow, and say yes on every day after—or walk away now, and we will part as strangers who met."

And I—Ashvane, young, proud, lonely—walked to the hearth and breathed my fire into it. Not commanded. Not bribed. Asked, and answered.

The gods followed. The mortals followed. And the flame that rose from that shared offering was not like any fire since—it was warm without consuming, bright without blinding, and it *asked questions back*, which is how you always know a living thing.

Cyrus stood inside the memory of that light, and I watched something happen in her face that I had seen once before, three hundred years ago, on the face of the road-speaker: the expression of a creature realizing that everything it had done to survive had also been preparation.

"They gave it freely," she said. "All of it. That's the mechanism. That's the *whole* mechanism."

"Now you see why the shard Veyra stole will do nothing but harm her."

"She thinks it's a battery."

"She thinks everything is a battery." I let the copper sky darken toward the remembering of the breaking, because mercy demanded I show her that too. "Watch what we did wrong, little heir. It matters more than what we did right."

* * *

The breaking took thirty years, which in the memory passed like weather.

It began small, the way all betrayals begin. A king asked the priestess's successor whether the covenant might be *interpreted* more favorably. A god grew tired of answering every prayer that deserved answer and began curating. A dragon—another dragon, not me, though I did not stop him, and I have had three hundred years to sit with that—took back a mountain he had pledged to a human village, on the grounds that consent given once should cover all future takings.

Consent once. That was the poison. Everyone had heard the priestess's terms and everyone had quietly amended them in their hearts: *yes today*, and assumed tomorrow was included. But the covenant was not a contract. It was a practice. It was a hearth, and hearths go out if you stop feeding them.

Maera's predecessor died, and her successor was bribed—your Maera, Cyrus, though she did not know yet what she would do, and neither did I—and the offerings thinned, and the gods withdrew to test whether mortals would notice, and mortals did not notice, because mortals were busy, because mortals were always busy, and one morning the hearth was cold and the silence where the gods had been was so complete that within a generation most people believed they had never existed at all.

"I noticed," Cyrus said. Her voice was very flat, the way voices get when they're holding a great weight motionless. "The scullery maid noticed. People kept praying anyway. For two hundred years, into a dead line, they kept praying."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you tell them? Why did the dragons stay silent?"

I showed her. The memory rose around us: the last session of the court, the empty seats, the handful of us remaining, and the decision we made in our shame. That we had failed our own covenant—we had stopped tending, stopped witnessing, retreated into our mountains to hoard our guilt along with our gold—and that creatures who break faith do not get to lecture others about faith. So we waited. Three hundred years, waiting for a mortal to ask again. Waiting for someone to come to the cold hearth and offer, freely, to rebuild.

"We were cowards," I said. "Do not dress it up for me. We broke it too, and then we hid, and hiding is how hoards begin. Power hoarded is power lost, Cyrus. I have watched it rot in my own chest for three centuries. Your chancellor is going to learn it the hard way, unless—"

"Unless someone offers instead." Cyrus looked down at her hands, those careful, steady, poisoner's hands, hands trained for invisibility, and I watched her understand that the covenant had never needed her bloodline. Bloodlines don't consent. Only people do.

"It cannot be reforged with the shard," I said, because I wanted it in her ears plainly, without poetry, the way you say things you need someone to carry through a storm. "Veyra can hold that stone in a palace hearth until the mountains wear down. It will burn cold. Worse than cold. A taken thing doesn't just fail to give warmth—it *draws* it. She will light it and her city will begin to freeze, one hearth at a time, starting with the poorest, because taken power feeds downward on exactly the people it was stolen from. And she will not repent. She will double the taking. That is what hoarders do. I know. I am one."

Cyrus was quiet for a long moment. In the memory, the copper sky was dissolving, the courtyard stones going soft at the edges, because I was spending the oldest of my embers now and the room was running out.

"When we reach the First Hearth," she said slowly, "it has to be offered again. All of it. By all of us. Freely."

"Not obedience. Not duty. Not even destiny." I put as much gentleness into it as a creature my size can manage. "Partnership. Someone must speak for mortal law. Someone must speak for the gods. And someone must stand witness for the lawless—the road-people, the smugglers, the ones who survive outside every covenant because every covenant excluded them. Three voices. One flame."

"And the voice for the gods," she said. "That's Maera."

"That is Maera, yes. If she consents. Everything hinges on consent, little heir. Even yours. Especially yours."

The courtyard was nearly gone now, unraveling into blue-dark, and Cyrus turned to me with the question I had been dreading, the one I had brought her here partly to answer and partly to avoid.

"Ashvane. When the flame takes all three voices—" she hesitated, then forced it out, because she was braver than her training, "—what does it cost?"

I could have lied. Dragons are excellent liars; it is one of our traditional arts. But I was spending my last true embers, and dying creatures owe the living honesty.

"Everything you're still withholding," I said. "That's the price. Whatever armor you have left, whatever corner of yourself you've kept unbargained—the flame knows, and it will ask for exactly that. For me it was my solitude. Three hundred years ago I gave up being a creature apart, and I have missed it every single day, and I would give it again. That is what freely given means. Not painless. Just *chosen*."

Her eyes widened slightly, and I saw her think of a vial, a final vial of antidotes, an old habit of safety she still carried against her heart—and I saw her decide not to think about it yet. Humans are allowed their pacing. It's one of their better qualities.

The memory collapsed. We were back in the ice-hollow under the frozen lake, my body a ruin of ribs and blue guttering light, Sefa's caravan camped above us with their fires burning small against the wind. Cyrus knelt in the snow beside my head, snowflakes melting an inch from my skin, refusing to touch me and refusing to leave.

"Veyra lit it already?" she asked. "Or she will?"

"She is preparing to," I rumbled. "And when she does, every hearth in the capital will begin to die, and she will blame the gods, and she will be believed, because fear is the easiest lie to sell. You have days, Cyrus Dravenmoor. Perhaps less."

She stood, and pulled her coat tight, and looked north up the mountain where the storm was building like a wall. Then she said something I did not expect, and that I have added to the short list of moments I would relive if dragons got second deaths.

"Then we climb tonight."

Above us, the wind changed. I felt it through the ice, through my failing blood, through senses older than language: a storm coming down off the peaks that was not entirely weather, and beneath it, far to the south, the cold spark of the stolen shard waking in its new setting, drawing warmth, drawing warmth, drawing warmth.

And beneath *that*—faint, furious, unmistakable—the sound of a god clearing its throat.

"Cyrus," I said. "Run."
