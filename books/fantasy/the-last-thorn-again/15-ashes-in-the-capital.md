## 15. Ashes in the Capital

The shard did not want to burn.

Veyra had known this, in the way she knew most things—early, quietly, and without telling anyone who might use the knowledge against her. She had known it when her agents brought it to her wrapped in wool and prayer-cloth, sweating through their gloves as though it were a sleeping snake. She had known it when she locked it in her strongest ironbox for three days and heard, each night, a sound like slow breathing from inside the lock.

She had hoped she was wrong.

 Chancellor Chancellor Chancellor. The word was a stone she could stand on. Chancellor Veyra Solenne, Keeper of the King's Peace, mistress of the greenhouses, the ledgers, the roads. Chancellor of a realm that had survived four hundred years precisely because it had learned to stop asking heaven for anything. She stood before the great hearth of the Hall of Audiences at midnight with the Hearthstone shard in her hands, and she told herself the cold in her fingers was only iron and winter.

"Light it," she said.

Her fire-priest—a nervous man named Aldous whose order she kept alive on purpose, the way one keeps a canary in a mine—wet his lips. "Chancellor, the rites require—"

"I know what the rites require. I've read them. All of them." She set the shard on the andiron herself, because if Aldous's trembling hands dropped it she would have to have him flogged, and floggings left questions. "You will speak the invocation. I will provide the flame."

"The flame should come from living wood, cut at dawn, by hands that—"

"Aldous."

He stopped talking. That was the trouble with men like Aldous: they mistook ritual for understanding. Ritual was just the paperwork of power. Veyra had spent twenty years reading the actual ledgers—the correspondence of dead kings, the suppressed reports from the border shrines, the confession of a priestess named Maera that three separate archivists had tried to bury and Veyra had personally dug up. She understood what the covenant was better than any priest alive. It was a contract. Contracts could be seized. Contracts could be enforced. Contracts could be *renegotiated*, by whoever held the collateral.

She lit the kindling herself, with her own hand-lamp, because there was a certain symmetry in it that she intended to enjoy later, once it worked.

The fire took. Orange, ordinary, cheerful. For a moment nothing happened, and Veyra allowed herself the first full breath she'd taken since the agents came down off Thornfell with their prize and their report—one taster escaped, one dragon sighted, casualties acceptable—and then the flame went out.

Not guttered. Not died down. Went *out*, all at once, like a snuffed candle, though the logs still sat there whole and uncharred, cool to within an inch of the coals.

And the cold came in.

It came in the way water comes into a hull that has found its flaw: everywhere at once, patient, total. Veyra felt it first in the hall itself—the air thickening, the torches along the walls dimming not in brightness but in *heat*, their flames shrinking toward blue. Then through the soles of her boots, up from the flagstones, as though the palace itself had become the outside of something.

Aldous made a sound like a man swallowing his own tongue. "Chancellor—"

"Be quiet." She watched the shard. It sat on the andiron untouched by any fire now, and it had begun, very faintly, to glow—but not with light. With the absence of it. A darkness that drew the eye the way a wound draws flies.

She had read every account of the original covenant-making. Three realms, one hearth, freely given. Freely given—that phrase again; it recurred in the old texts like a refrain, like a warning label on a bottle. She had assumed it was piety. Priestly embroidery. What contract in history had ever been *freely given*? Power flowed to those strong enough to hold it; that was the first law, older than gods, and everything else was commentary.

But the shard was teaching her the second law now, at midnight, in her own hall: power hoarded is power lost. Or perhaps worse than lost. Power hoarded becomes a debt, and debts collect interest.

The torches went out entirely. Somewhere above them, in the dark of the rafters, ice cracked across a window.

"It's drawing," Aldous whispered. He had his arms around himself. His breath frosted. "Chancellor, it's drawing heat. From the room. From—" he swallowed, "—from us."

Veyra looked down at her own hands. They were steady. Her hands were always steady; she had trained them the way other people trained hawks. But beneath the steadiness she could feel the cold working inward, a fine bright wire of it, threading up through her wrists toward the places where a body keeps its fires.

*Take it back,* said a voice that was not a voice, that arrived fully formed behind her eyes like a thought she'd been tricked into thinking. *Or feed it.*

She picked up the shard. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything. Through the wool wrapping she could feel it pulling—not greedily, not hungrily, but with the bottomless patience of a well. The warmth of her palms ran out of her like sand through open fingers.

"Into the box," she said. "Sealed. Triple-warded. Move."

* * *

By morning, the city had noticed.

Not the shard—no one below the fourth floor knew about the shard—but the cold. Frost on the inside of windows in the wealthier quarters where glass was good and fires were plentiful. Wells steaming. The bathhouses' water going tepid no matter how much fuel the stokers fed, and the stokers feeding more, and the price of coal beginning, by nine o'clock, to climb the way prices climb before a riot.

Veyra sat in her office above the Hall of Records with a brazier pulled so close to her desk it scorched the varnish, and read reports, and did not shiver, because Chancellor Veyra Solenne did not shiver.

"The granary district," said her spymaster. Corvane stood at the window with his back to her, which he only did when he didn't want her to see his face. "Three streets of it, frost-killed overnight. Not the crops—they're milled grain, there's nothing living to kill—but the *warmth*. Bakers say their ovens draw twice the fuel for half the rise. And the shrine quarter—"

"The shrine quarter is closed. Has been since the arrests."

"Yes, Chancellor. Closed. Which is why it's interesting that last night, witnesses report singing."

"Singing."

"Old hymns. In Old Tharnic, mostly. Coming from shuttered temples with no priests in them." Corvane turned. He was a careful man, Corvane, gray at the temples and gray in his methods, and right now he looked like a man reading a letter he wished were forged. "And the small mercies are failing. You know how it's been since the gods started waking—bread rising luckier, fevers breaking easier, lost children turning up at doorsteps. Little things. People called them blessings and thanked the crown for tolerating them." He paused. "They're stopping. Mid-stoppage. A fever breaks at noon and returns by dusk, worse. A lost child walks halfway home and stands in the road, weeping, having forgotten where home is. Whatever was answering prayers has stopped answering *halfway*."

"Because they're withdrawing," Veyra said. "Pulling back their attention. Consolidating."

"Or because someone took something of theirs," Corvane said, "and they're taking it back. With interest."

The brazier popped. Neither of them looked at it.

"Corvane." Veyra folded her hands on the ledger in front of her—the master ledger, the one no one else had ever seen complete, twenty years of her own handwriting tracking every rumor of waking divinity from the Thornfell shrines to the capital gutters. "Tell me what you think I should do."

It was a test, and they both knew it, and Corvane was too good a spymaster to pass it honestly. "I think," he said slowly, "that the city is frightened, and frightened cities look for someone to blame. I think that right now they blame the gods, or fate, or winter. I think that if the cold spreads—if the wells freeze, if the granaries spoil—we will have perhaps six days before they start looking for a nearer culprit." He met her eyes. "And I think you already have a culprit prepared, because you always do."

Of course she did. The taster. Cyrus Dravenmoor—or whatever name the woman had worn while poisoning a king's food for ten years under everyone's nose. Poisoner, thief, heretic; the warrants had gone out two weeks ago and the roads were seeded with bounty hunters. Veyra had built the accusation the way she built everything: load-bearing, elegant, true in every particular except the ones that mattered.

What she had not planned for was the possibility that the theft itself would fail to work.

She had accounted for resistance. She had accounted for the gods fighting back—openly, dramatically, plagues of frogs, voices in thunder, the theater of it. She had *not* accounted for this: a silence so total it ate heat. A withdrawal so complete that the world began forgetting how to be warm. It was not punishment. Punishment would have been almost a relief. This was something colder and more bureaucratic: the gods simply closing their accounts and leaving.

*Feed it,* the not-voice had said.

She had not fed it. She had sealed it in iron, and the iron was frosting over from the inside, and somewhere beneath her sternum a feeling lived that she refused, on principle, to name.

Fear. It was fear. Fine. She could work with fear. Fear was fuel like any other.

* * *

At midday she went down into the greenhouse.

It was the only place in the palace that still felt like hers. Rows of godsbane under glass, silver-leaved and sour-smelling, thriving in soil she'd had carried up from the mountain's shadow. The godsbane grew better lately. Everything else grew worse. She had noticed that too, and filed it away in the drawer of observations she did not yet understand.

She walked the rows with her hands clasped behind her back, and she let herself think, which was dangerous, and therefore necessary.

The plan had been clean. Seize the covenant's anchor before the gods finished waking; bind its power to the crown; end, forever, the age in which mortal lives hung on divine caprice. She had watched divine caprice kill people. She had been a child in the river districts the year the Fever of Whims took one house in five—had watched the priests shrug and call it the gods' will, had watched her mother pray until her knees broke and lose her anyway. Veyra had sworn, at eight years old, kneeling in ash, that no god would ever again be permitted to decide who lived in her country.

That was the creed. That was the whole creed, and she had never once deviated from it, and she clung to it now the way a drowning woman clings to a spar that is also, admittedly, dragging her under.

But the shard burned cold. The shard *drank*. And a thought had gotten into her head during that midnight hour in the great hall, a thought shaped like Maera's old confession, the one Veyra had dug out of the archive and used as a lever against the priestess's order for fifteen years: *we broke it because we believed we were saving them from a war.* The priestess had betrayed the covenant out of fear. Fear of what the gods wanted. And what had the gods wanted? Consent. Partnership. The one thing a chancellor of the crown could never give, because partnership meant the throne was no longer supreme.

So the gods had gone silent for sixty years. So the world had learned to live without them. So far, so manageable.

Except they weren't silent anymore. They were waking, and waking angry, and they were doing it *through people*—through scullery maids and burnt stews, through scars that burned, through a food-taster whose body had been rebuilt, brick by poisoned brick, into something that could carry what Veyra was trying to steal.

Veyra stopped at the end row of godsbane. The plants leaned toward her, or seemed to.

"You're afraid of me," she said aloud, to whatever was listening. The greenhouse was empty. The greenhouse was never empty. "Good. Be afraid. Fear is the beginning of negotiation."

Nothing answered. That was the trouble with gods: they never negotiated when you wanted them to, and always when you didn't.

* * *

By evening, the panic had a shape.

Corvane laid it out for her in the map room: pins and thread, the cartography of dread. Frost-lines radiating outward from the palace district—not evenly, not weather-like, but in *rings*, like ripples, like something at the center drinking. The bathhouse riots had started at four o'clock; two constables hurt; the Watch holding the doors but not the street. The coal merchants had begun hoarding their own stock. Three temples that had stayed shuttered through two weeks of arrests had thrown open their doors tonight—not the official priesthood, but crowds, hundreds, standing in the dark among the cold altars, praying without words, the way you shout at someone you believe has stopped listening.

"And here." Corvane's finger moved to the eastern quarter, to the tenements along the canal. "This is the worst of it. No frost rings—worse. Warmth draining straight out. Elderly dying in beds that were warm at supper. Infants. The physicians are calling it a wasting chill, but it doesn't behave like illness. It behaves like *hunger*."

"Quarantine the blocks. Move the vulnerable to the barracks halls—heated, provisioned."

"With what coal, Chancellor? The merchants won't release stock without royal guarantee, and the treasury clerk wants your signature on a requisition that would empty the winter reserve."

"Sign it provisionally under my seal. I'll answer for it."

Corvane wrote it down. Then he stood there, not writing anything else, not leaving, which was his way of saying something he couldn't put in a report.

"Say it," Veyra said.

"There's talk in the streets." His voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man handling a blade by the flat. "Talk that the crown woke something it shouldn't have. Talk that the arrests at the shrines started this—that the gods were sleeping peaceful until we dragged their priests out in chains, and now they're taking back every mercy we ever got from them, with interest." He finally looked up. "Six days, I said this morning. I'll amend that. The talk has legs. By week's end, they won't be blaming the gods anymore. They'll be blaming *you*. And they'll be looking for anyone who might fix it." A beat. "There's a story moving through the gutters. About a taster. A poison-taster who fled the palace the night the gods began answering. They're saying she wasn't fleeing arrest. They're saying she was *chosen*."

Veyra stood very still.

The brazier behind her had gone cold. She hadn't noticed when. The room's warmth was leaving through the walls, through the floor, drawn east and south and down, toward some center of appetite she preferred not to imagine, and she stood in the growing chill of her own map room and felt the exact moment the narrative slipped out of her hands.

For fifteen years she had written every story the capital told about itself. She had done it with informants and printers and well-placed rumors, patiently, the way water cuts stone. And now a story was writing itself—a story with a hero she had invented as a villain, a heroine born from her own warrant, running free in the mountains with a smuggler's caravan and a dying dragon, gathering to herself everything Veyra had tried to seize.

She could feel the old temptation rising, familiar as hunger: tighten the grip. More arrests. Louder decrees. Name the taster a witch in every square, triple the bounty, burn the gutter-stories and hang a few storytellers. Control the story harder.

It had worked for fifteen years.

It would not work now, and she knew it, and knowing it felt like the cold in her hands at the hearth: a knowledge arriving through the skin, past all her armor.

Because the people were not afraid of Cyrus Dravenmoor. They were *hoping* for her. Hope was the one thing no decree could confiscate. Veyra had spent her whole life confiscating things, and she recognized contraband when she saw it.

"Chancellor?" Corvane said.

"We change the approach." She turned to the map, to the frost-rings spreading from her own palace like a stain. "The shard stays sealed—Ironbox One, and double the guard, and no one speaks of it, ever. Pull the bounty hunters off the roads. All of them."

Corvane's eyebrows rose a fraction of an inch. In Corvane, that was astonishment.

"Not to cancel the hunt." She touched the map, far north, where the mountains rose into the white blankness at the top of the sheet. "To concentrate it. If the realm is going to tell stories about a chosen one crossing Thornfell to save it—then the chosen one will arrive at the First Hearth to find the crown already there, waiting, with the shard in hand and the high ground occupied. Let her run her errand. Let her spend her strength on the climb. We'll take the mountain's front door and hold it, and when she comes down out of the passes half-frozen with her smugglers and her mad priestess, she'll find that hope has a garrison."

"And if the mountain refuses us passage?"

"Then we make the mountain regret it." She said it lightly, and hated how it sounded even to her—like a line from one of the gutter stories, a villain's line, crisp and confident and doomed. She said it anyway. Doubling down was not a vice; it was a discipline. The alternative was to stand in this cooling room and admit that a scullery maid's burnt stew had known more about the architecture of heaven than twenty years of her study, and that admission was not available to her. Would never be available to her. There were things a chancellor could not afford to unlearn, and certainty was one of them.

"Send riders tonight," she said. "Full company to the Hearth Gate. And Corvane—" she caught him at the door, "—find out what the dragon costs to kill. Everything. I want the figure by morning."

After he left, she stood alone in the map room with the pins and thread and the spreading rings of frost, and she held her hands out over the dead brazier as though warmth were a thing she could command back into the world.

The coals stared up at her, gray and cold as ash.

Somewhere north, in the mountains, the true fire was climbing toward its hearth. And here in the capital, in the heart of the greatest power mortals had ever built, the cold breathed in, and settled, and waited to be fed.
