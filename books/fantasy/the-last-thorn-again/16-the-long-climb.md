## 16. The Long Climb

The storm found them at the tree line, the way storms always do in Thornfell—suddenly and with intent.

Sefa had run these roads for twenty years. She'd crossed passes in spring melt, in autumn fog, once in a hailstorm that had killed two mules and a man who wouldn't let go of his cargo. She knew every kind of weather the mountains could throw. This was different. This wasn't weather. This was the mountain deciding whether to let them pass.

"Wind's wrong," she shouted to Cyrus over the roar. They stood at the mouth of the old pilgrim stair, a switchback of stone so ancient the steps had worn into bowls. Above them the clouds churned low and green-black, and lightning moved *inside* them, sideways, like something swimming. "Storm should be coming out of the west. This is coming down off the Hearth."

Cyrus pulled her hood tighter. Even now, even after everything, Sefa half-expected not to see her—to lose her in a crowd, in shadow, in the space between one glance and the next. But since the dragon had spoken her name on the ice, Cyrus Dravenmoor was impossible to miss. Not louder. Not bigger. Just *present*, the way a lit lamp is present in a dark room. People's eyes went to her and stayed there, confused about why.

"The Hearth doesn't want us," Sefa said.

"The Hearth is testing us." Maera said it from behind, calm as a woman reading scripture, though her hands were shaking around her staff. The priestess hadn't slept in three days. Her memories were still arriving, wave after wave of them, forty years of things she'd drowned on purpose, and she bore each one like a woman being flogged by her own past. But she didn't complain. Sefa was starting to understand why the gods were restoring her instead of striking her dead. Guilt like that was worth more than piety. "It tested me once. I failed. It will test all of us now."

"Wonderful," Sefa muttered. "I love tests."

Behind them, filling the whole width of the stair, Ashvane dragged herself up through the snow.

The dragon could no longer fly. Sefa had known it for weeks—the way the great wings hung loose as wet canvas, the ember-light under her scales guttering like a lamp running dry. On flat ground Ashvane could still manage a lumbering walk, tail carving a trench behind her. On the stair she was a catastrophe, cracking stone, sliding, catching herself on her forelegs with breaths that sounded like a bellows full of gravel.

"We should have gone around," Sefa said, for the fourth time.

"There is no around." Cyrus looked back at the dragon, and something passed between them—not words, Sefa had stopped trying to follow those—and Ashvane lowered her head, exhaled steam into the snow, and began again. One claw. Then another. "She chose this road before we did."

That was the thing about dragons nobody told you: they were worse than smugglers about debts. Sefa had bargained passage across the frozen lake for a favor unnamed, and she'd thought she was the clever one in that negotiation. Now the favor was taking shape, and its shape was *this*—a dying fire-drake crawling up a mountain because a girl with poison-scarred hands needed to relight the world, and Sefa's caravan, her people, her gold, her roads, all of it dragged along in the wake.

"Move!" she bellowed at her crew. "Kess, take point! Dov, get that rope anchored before the wind does it for you! If any of you die up here I'm not carrying you down, I'm leaving you as a warning to others!"

They laughed. That was the trick, the only trick she knew. Keep them laughing and they keep moving.

They climbed.

* * *

By the second hour the cold had stopped being cold and become a fact of the world, like stone. Sefa's fingers went wooden inside her gloves. The rope between climbers iced over and had to be chipped free at every anchor. Twice the wind came off the ridge in walls hard enough to lift a grown man off his feet; both times it was Ashvane's body, wedged across the stair below them, that broke its force.

The third time, the wind took Tam.

He was the youngest, sixteen, all elbows and opinions, and the gust peeled him off the wall-side of the stair like a leaf off a roof. He screamed. The rope snapped taut, jerked Dov off balance, and for one long horrible moment the boy swung out over nothing, boots scrabbling air above a drop that ended somewhere next week.

Sefa was moving before she thought, which was how she'd survived twenty years—hands on the rope, boots braced against an anchor stone, hauling with her whole furious body while Dov screamed and Kess threw himself onto the line beside her. Too heavy. The ice, the angle, too heavy—

A hand closed over hers. Cyrus. Then Maera's, gnarled and strong. And then a weight settled over the whole company like a blanket, warm and impossible, and Sefa realized Ashvane had turned her head into the wind and was breathing out—a slow, controlled stream of dragon-warmth that melted the ice on the anchors, softened the snow where they braced their feet, wrapped the four of them on the rope in air that did not try to kill them.

Tam came over the edge gasping and crying and swearing all at once, and they hauled him home onto the stone, and Sefa sat down hard in the slush and laughed until it turned into something else.

"He owes me," she croaked.

"He owes all of us," Cyrus said. She was kneeling by the boy, checking his fingers for frostbite with a taster's precision—pressing, watching the color come back, counting. "But mostly he owes the mountain. Remember that. Everything up here is borrowed."

"You've changed," Sefa said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "You know that? Ten days ago you'd have been three steps ahead and invisible. Now you're hauling ropes."

Cyrus looked at her hands. The scars, the burn-marks, the calluses built by ten years of measured self-poisoning. "I was never here before," she said quietly. "Now I am."

Above them, thunder rolled down the pass like a door closing.

* * *

They sheltered that night in a hollow beneath an overhang that Maera called a *watch-rest*—a place where pilgrims had once slept on their way to the First Hearth, back when there were pilgrims. Someone had carved a hearth-spiral into the rock, worn nearly smooth. Maera knelt and touched it and wept without sound, remembering, Sefa supposed, whatever she'd paid to forget.

No fire. No fire this high, not with the storm hunting heat the way it seemed to be. They huddled together under oilcloths and shared dried meat and snow-water, and Ashvane curled herself around the hollow's mouth like a wall, her hide barely warm now, her breathing slow.

It was Ashvane who spoke first. Her voice filled the hollow without effort, low as landslide.

"The Hearth will not admit you as you are."

Everyone looked up.

"You carry too much," the dragon said. Her eye, huge and copper, reflected each of them small and bent. "Not supplies. Not weapons. The covenant was made by giving, freely given—that was its whole architecture, the reason it held for a thousand years and the reason it broke when one voice sold itself. Power hoarded is power lost. Power surrendered is power made. You cannot walk into that place clutching what you refuse to lose."

"I'm not giving up my axe," said Kess.

"No one wants your axe," Sefa said.

"The shard was stolen from us," Cyrus said slowly. "And Veyra can't use it. Because it has to be given."

"It must be remade by consent," Ashvane agreed. "And consent begins long before the hearth-gate. It begins here. Tonight. Each of you holds something you believe keeps you alive. Set one down."

Silence, except the wind.

Sefa looked at her own hands, and hated the dragon suddenly, completely, with the clean hatred of a woman caught.

Because Sefa knew exactly what she carried. She carried it in a belt-pouch against her skin where it never left her, had never left her in nineteen years, through knife-fights and betrayals and two marriages and the worst winter of the century. Gold. Not caravan gold, not trade coin—*hoard* gold. Her mother's rings melted down. The ransom from the Vellori job. Coin scraped from every close call, every lucky escape, every year she survived, converted immediately into metal and weight because weight could be counted and counted things could be trusted.

Her hoard. A smuggler's retirement. Her proof that survival was possible, that she would never again be the girl with nothing standing in a burned doorway.

"You first, dragon," she said roughly. "What are *you* giving up?"

Ashvane's breath came slow. "Everything. My flight. My strength. By tomorrow, perhaps my life. I gave my fire to the covenant when it was young and I have spent nineteen years watching it gutter. What remains to me I will spend on that stair." The copper eye blinked, slow as sunset. "I am not asking you for anything I have kept."

Maera moved first.

The old priestess reached into her robe and drew out a small clay flask, stoppered with wax, and Sefa felt her stomach turn before she understood why—because everyone in the caravan knew what river-water in a flask meant. Lethe-water. Forgetfulness. Maera had drunk from it once, deep, to drown her guilt, and she'd carried the rest ever since. Insurance. A door out, if the memories got too loud.

She set it on the stones between them.

"I drank to forget that I broke the world," Maera said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "I told myself if it came back—if the guilt came back—I could drink again. Deeper. Forever. That flask was my permission to be a coward for the rest of my life." She nudged it toward the center of their circle. "Take it. Throw it off the mountain. I don't trust myself to."

Nobody moved. Then Tam, the boy, still gray-lipped from the rope, picked it up, walked four paces into the screaming dark, and hurled it. They heard it crack on rock far below, very faintly, once.

Maera exhaled like a woman setting down a load she'd forgotten she carried. And Sefa saw it happen—the shaking in her hands stopped. Just stopped.

"One," said Ashvane.

All eyes came to Sefa.

"Oh, come *on*," she said.

* * *

She fought it for an hour. She wanted that noted, by history, by the gods, by anyone keeping records: Sefa of the Thorn roads fought it for a full hour, arguing that gold was practical, that a company needed resources, that the dragon's riddle-talk didn't apply to honest thieves, that she'd earned every coin with blood and near-death and she'd be damned if she'd throw her life's work off a cliff because a dying lizard quoted covenant law at her.

The others let her argue. Cyrus listened the way she listened to everything—completely, silently, like the listening itself was a gift—and when Sefa finally ran out of words, Cyrus said:

"What was it for?"

"What?"

"The gold. All of it. Nineteen years. What was it *for*?"

And Sefa opened her mouth to say *retirement, safety, the far south, a house*, and heard, in the space where the answer should have been, nothing. Wind. Just wind. Because the truth was the gold was never *for* anything. It was armor. The same as Cyrus's invisibility. The same as Maera's forgetting. Something you carried so the world could never touch you again, and something that, in the carrying, guaranteed the world never would.

"That's the trick of it," Sefa said finally, and her voice came out strange. "Survival. You win it, and win it, and one day you look up and realize you won yourself a box. Comfortable little box. Nothing gets in." She unbuckled the pouch. It weighed nothing and everything. "Nothing gets *out* either."

She stood, walked to the lip of the hollow, and stood there a long moment with the wind trying to take it from her—no. Not taking. *Asking.* That was the difference, and she felt it in her palms, in the weight. The storm wasn't stealing. It was waiting to receive.

"Mother," she said, to nobody, to the mountain, "look at your idiot daughter."

She threw the pouch. It vanished into the dark mid-flight. Somewhere below it would sit in the snow until spring, worthless as a stone, and some lucky fool would find a fortune, and Sefa found she was laughing—laughing hard, ugly, relieved laughter, the kind you can't fake.

Her belt felt like it weighed less than her bones. She sat back down lighter than she had been in nineteen years.

"Two," said Ashvane.

Everyone looked at Cyrus.

Cyrus already had it in her hand. Of course she did. Sefa should have known—should have guessed the moment the dragon started talking, because Cyrus Dravenmoor had been raised by poison the way other children were raised by prayer, and there was only one thing left of all that education that she still carried.

A vial. Small, glass, corked with wax, holding maybe six swallows of clear liquid. Sefa knew what it was by now; they all did. Broad-spectrum antidote. The final refinement of ten years of work, the one that could pull a body back from arsenic, nightshade, adder-venom, half the poisons of the civilized world. Cyrus had brewed it herself, in a smuggler's camp kettle, over three nights. It was, quite possibly, unique.

"It's my last one," Cyrus said. Stating it plainly, like a merchant declaring a price. "If I'm poisoned on the way to the Hearth—or after, if Veyra comes for me—this is the only thing that saves me. Without it I'm just a person. Anyone's poison kills me like it kills anyone else."

"That's insane," said Kess.

"Yes," Cyrus said. "It is. It's also the point." She turned the vial in her scarred fingers. "Ten years ago someone decided what my body was for. Built me for it, dose by dose, without asking. Every immunity I own was installed in me like bricks in a wall." She looked up, and the lamp-that-was-Cyrus burned steady. "I've spent my whole life armored. Against poison, against notice, against belonging. And the armor worked—it kept me alive. It just didn't keep me *anything else*." She stood. "The covenant runs on consent. Freely given. So here's mine. I give up my last defense. Whatever happens at that hearth happens to all of me, unarmored. That's the only offer I have that's worth making."

She walked to the edge. Paused—one heartbeat, and Sefa saw the fear on her, plain and human and enormous—and then opened her hand.

The wind took the vial the way the mountain takes everything: gently, completely, forever.

When Cyrus came back and sat down among them, something had shifted in the air of the hollow. Sefa couldn't name it. It was as if a knot in the world had loosened. Ashvane's ember-eye brightened, just slightly, just enough.

"Three," the dragon murmured. "Enough. The rest you'll surrender at the gate, or you won't enter. Sleep now. Tomorrow the stair ends and the true climb begins."

* * *

Sefa didn't sleep. She lay under the oilcloth listening to the storm hammer the mountain and watched her breath make ghosts in the freezing air, and did inventory, the old smuggler habit, except the inventory was different now.

Tam, snoring against Dov's shoulder, alive because five people had held a rope and a dragon had breathed warm. Kess grumbling in his sleep about his axe, bless him, loyal as gravity. Maera awake, as always, lips moving—praying, or remembering, or both, the guilt and the forgiveness sharing the same mouth. Cyrus sitting up against Ashvane's flank, in the last warmth the dragon had to give, staring at nothing with the expression of a woman rehearsing her own death and finding it acceptable.

And Sefa herself, poorer than she'd been since she was nine years old, and richer than she had ever been, lying in a hole in a mountain with people who had seen her throw away her armor and had not laughed.

*Belonging,* she thought. *So that's what it costs. Everything you were hiding behind.*

Dawn, when it came, was green-black and howling, and the storm had not weakened overnight but strengthened, as if the mountain had felt them set things down and grown angrier—or eager. They packed in silence. Ashvane rose last, with terrible effort, stone grinding under her claws, and turned her head up the stair toward the pass that led, at last, to the First Hearth.

Then she went rigid. Her head snapped around, nostrils flaring, and the guttering light under her scales flared once, hard, like a coal blown on.

"What?" Cyrus was on her feet instantly. "Ashvane—what is it?"

The dragon's voice came out flat and grim, smoke without fire.

"Smoke. Not ours." Her head turned, precise as a compass needle, toward the southern ridgeline—the direction of the capital, the direction of the low country, the direction everything bad came from. "Men. Many men. And iron, and torch-oil, and—" the great eye narrowed to a slit of molten copper "—godsbane. Someone has found our road."

Sefa was already reaching for her spyglass, already knowing, with the sick certainty of twenty years on these trails, what she would find when she swept the southern ridge: a line of orange lights moving through the pre-dawn murk, patient and ordered, climbing.

Soldiers. A net, drawn up the mountain behind them.

"They didn't follow our trail," Cyrus breathed. "There *was* no trail. Nobody knew this road except—"

She stopped.

Sefa watched the name arrive in her face like a knife going in, and felt it land in her own chest a heartbeat later, because she'd fed him, she'd bandaged him, she'd argued for his life in a cairn while everyone with sense voted the other way.

The bounty hunter. The one they'd spared.
