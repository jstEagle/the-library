## 17. Betrayal at the Hearth Gate

The storm broke an hour before dawn, and with it, something in Corren Hale's face.

Cyrus saw it from across the fire — or what was left of a fire, because they were above the tree line now and burning anything was a luxury Ashvane had forbidden. The dragon lay against the cliff wall like a landslide that had learned to breathe, her scales gone the dull gray of old iron, her eyes half-lidded coals. Maera sat closest to her, hands folded, murmuring prayers that had stopped sounding like practice and started sounding like conversation.

And Corren sat apart, as he always did since Smugglers' Justice, since Cyrus had stood between him and Sefa's knife and said *not this way*. He'd been quiet ever since. She'd mistaken the quiet for gratitude. Or at least for surrender.

She should have known better. Quiet men who owed you their lives were not grateful men. They were men doing arithmetic.

He wasn't at the fire when she woke properly. His bedroll was there, his pack, his boots — everything but the man. Cyrus went still in the way ten years of tasting other people's poison had taught her, reading absence the way she read a dish: what was missing told you more than what was present.

"Sefa," she said. "Where's Hale?"

Sefa looked up from greasing her crossbow strings. The look lasted one second too long. "Checking the switchbacks," she said. "I sent him."

"You didn't."

The caravan camp was a bowl of stone below the last ridge before the Hearth Gate — a notch in the mountain where, according to every song and every map tattooed on her mother's recipe cards, the first hearth of the world had been carried up out of the deep places and set into living rock. You could see it from here: a thin line of gold light along the saddle of the pass, steady as a pulse, unbothered by wind or snow. It had been burning for three days straight now, visible for the first time in living memory. Pilgrims would have come, once. Soldiers would come now.

"He took the east trail down," Cyrus said. "Toward Veyra's garrison at Coldmere." She was already pulling on her coat. "How long has he been gone?"

"Cyrus—"

"*How long.*"

"Two hours," Sefa admitted. "Maybe three. I thought—" Her jaw worked. "I thought he'd earned the benefit of the doubt. You bought him that."

Cyrus stared at the east trail, white and empty and full of nothing, and felt the arithmetic finish itself in her gut. Two hours downhill at a soldier's pace. Three hours back up with a company. Veyra would not send scouts; she'd send the net.

"We move now," she said. "Wake everyone. Leave everything we don't carry."

Ashvane opened one eye. The eye was the size of a shield and the color of a dying sunset, and when she spoke, the words came up through the ground rather than the air. *"You will not outrun them, small one. Not me. I am slow as a funeral now."*

"Then we get you to the gate before they close it." Cyrus looked up at the notch of gold. Half a mile of climbing. Maybe less. "Maera, can you walk?"

Maera rose without being asked. There was something in her face Cyrus hadn't seen before — not fear. Readiness. Like a woman who had been waiting twenty years to be caught and had finally stopped pretending otherwise.

"I can walk," Maera said. "I've walked toward worse things than this."

* * *

They made the ridge as the sun cleared the eastern peaks and lit the whole white world the color of blood and butter, and that was when Cyrus heard the horns.

Three notes, repeated. Not a hunting call. A cordon call. They were already in the pass behind them — Corren had sold them the route entire, every switchback, every blind curve, probably drawn it in charcoal on a table while officers nodded — and now the company was climbing through the notch with nowhere left to go except forward, toward the gate, which was not a gate at all anymore but a doorway of black stone set into the mountainside, older than any masonry Cyrus knew, its lintel carved with three interlocked rings: flame, wing, and hand.

The gold light pulsed behind it.

"They're coming up the switchbacks," Sefa reported from the rocks. She'd climbed onto a boulder with the ease of a woman who had spent her whole life on surfaces no sane person trusted. "Sixty. Maybe eighty. Cavalry can't form up here, so they'll come on foot. We've got maybe twenty minutes."

"Can we hold the doorway?"

"With what?" Sefa laughed, short and hard. "Four smugglers, a priestess, and a dragon who couldn't fight off a wet cat?"

*I can hear you,* Ashvane rumbled, dragging herself up the last stretch of slope. Each breath plumed steam. Each footfall left a print that would hold meltwater for a season. *And I could fight off a wet cat. Possibly two.*

Cyrus stood before the black doorway and pressed her palm flat against the stone. It was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything about this place hummed at the frequency of her own blood, the frequency Veyra had spent twenty years tuning her toward, dose by careful dose. Through the stone she felt the First Hearth on the other side — vast, patient, guttering. A fire that had burned alone for so long it had almost forgotten what company was.

"It knows us," Maera said quietly beside her. "It's been waiting."

"For me?"

"For someone. For anyone willing to knock." Maera's hands were trembling. Cyrus noticed, because noticing was her whole profession, and she realized the tremor wasn't cold. "Cyrus. If they take me—"

"No one is taking anyone."

"Hear me." Maera turned, and the morning light caught the faded brand on her collarbone — the mark of a priestess stripped of office, seared there thirty years ago by men who never learned what she'd actually done. "There's something I haven't told you. Something I remembered on the climb, fully, all of it, like a knife coming out sideways." Her voice didn't waver. That was the terrible part. "When the soldiers reach us, they'll be looking for a poisoner and a heretic. That's what Veyra's proclamations say. But if they find something better — if they find the woman who *broke the covenant* — they'll stop chasing you. Veyra can't resist it. Proof. Confession. A name for all the fear she's been selling."

"You're not making sense—"

"I am the reason your gods went silent." Maera said it plainly, the way you'd report weather. "Thirty-one years ago, the crown came to me in the temple at Vel Saroth and told me the gods were going to war over the covenant's succession, that millions would die in the fire of their quarrel, and that I — only I, because I held the priesthood's trust — could prevent it. All I had to do was unseal the Hearthstone shard and let the king's mages 'hold it in safekeeping.' I believed them. Gods, I *wanted* to believe them. I was young, and proud, and they flattered me with the weight of the world." She exhaled. "So I opened the seal. And the moment the shard left the hearth, the covenant failed, and the gods' voices went out of the world like a snuffed lamp, and I understood — too late — that there had never been a god-war coming. There had only been a theft. And I had held the door."

The wind moved across the ridge. Somewhere below, boots on scree, hundreds of them, a sound like rain that hated you.

"Why tell me now?" Cyrus whispered. "Why not on the lake, or the shrine, or any of the hundred nights—"

"Because now it's useful." Maera smiled, and it was a real smile, wrecked and calm. "A confession is only worth anything when there's someone to hear it. I drank the Lethe-water to escape this. The gods gave it back to me whether I wanted it or not — that was their mercy, cruel as everything else they do. But mercy isn't forgiveness. Forgiveness has a price, and the price is standing in the place where you fell and saying *this is mine*." She looked past Cyrus, at the black doorway, at the gold pulse beyond it. "I healed you at the shrine, girl. Don't argue, you know you did — I pulled the ritual poisons out of you when the waking gods showed me how. So let me heal myself the same way. Let me choose the consequence instead of running from it. It's the only prayer I have left that they'll actually answer."

Below them, the horns sounded again — closer now, a single sustained note, and then the vanguard crested the switchbacks.

* * *

They came in column, then fanned out across the bowl of stone with the drilled ease of troops who had done this in a dozen mountain passes. Gray cloaks, Veyra's colors, the crowned-tower sigil stitched at the shoulder. Their officer rode as far as a horse could go and then dismounted and walked forward alone, sword sheathed, hands open, the universal grammar of *we don't need violence here* — which, in Cyrus's experience, meant violence was already decided and merely awaiting paperwork.

"Cyrus Dravenmoor," the officer called up. He had a herald's voice, built for slopes and crowds. "By warrant of Chancellor Veyra, Lord Protector of the Realm's Faith, you are under arrest for poisoning, heresy, and trafficking with outlaw powers. Surrender quietly and the rest of your company walks free. That is the offer. It expires when my archers finish climbing."

Cyrus stepped forward to the edge of the rise, and every smuggler instinct in her screamed at the exposure of it — visible, named, *seen*, everything she'd spent a decade avoiding — and she stepped anyway. Ashvane's naming had cracked the armor. There was no putting it back on. She'd tried, these last weeks, hunching her shoulders, dropping her gaze, and people simply *looked at her now*, as though her name were written somewhere on her face.

"You have the wrong crime," she called back. "Ask your chancellor what she's really carrying in that box on her hip."

"The warrant is not a discussion."

"She's right, you know." Maera's voice, beside her, and then Maera was walking past her, down the slope, into the open, arms raised, hood thrown back. Cyrus lunged to grab her and Sefa caught her arm — gently, firmly, the grip of someone who had already understood and was grieving it in advance. "Let her," Sefa breathed. "Gods help me. *Let her.*"

Maera stopped ten paces from the officer, snow swirling around her hem, and her voice carried up the whole white bowl of the mountain, clear as temple bell.

"I am Maera of Vel Saroth," she said. "Daughter of the temple, keeper of the western flame, branded and defrocked — and none of you know why, because the record was burned. I will tell you why. Thirty-one years ago I broke the Covenant of the First Hearth. I unsealed the Hearthstone with my own hands and delivered it to the crown, believing I saved the world from a god-war. There was no god-war. There was only greed, and I was its instrument. Every famine winter since, every silent shrine, every unanswered deathbed prayer — that silence is mine. I bought it. I signed for it." She turned slowly, addressing the whole line of soldiers, letting each of them see her face, her brand, her tears freezing on her cheeks. "If your chancellor wants a criminal, here is the only one on this mountain whose crime matters. Take me. I surrender freely, and I will confess everything — names, dates, the sealed orders — before any court she likes. In exchange, these people walk."

The line of soldiers shifted. Cyrus watched certainty flicker in forty faces — because they had grown up in the silent years same as everyone, they had prayed to dead air same as everyone, and here was a woman telling them the silence had a name and a face and it was *hers*. Fear of the waking gods was Veyra's currency. But guilt — shared, confessed, human guilt — that was a different coin entirely, and it rang in a register soldiers weren't armored against.

The officer recovered fast. Too fast. "The priestess is also wanted," he said. "All parties will come down together. This changes nothing."

"It changes everything," Maera said softly, "and you know it. Because a confession given freely is testimony, Captain, and testimony survives even your chancellor's archives. Kill me and it dies with you. Arrest me gently and I talk for years." She spread her hands. "Your move. Choose like a man who prays."

For one long moment, the mountain held its breath.

Then the horns sounded again — but wrong, ragged, from two directions at once, and Cyrus spun toward the sound with her heart dropping through the floor of the world. Down the western trail, where no soldiers should be, gray cloaks were cresting the ridge. A second column. Corren hadn't just sold them the route. He'd sold them the route *twice*.

"They're flanking," Sefa snapped, crossbow up. "Cyrus, the doorway, *now* — we cannot be caught in the open—"

Arrows arced. Not aimed to kill — aimed to herd, driving them back from the slope toward the black stone, and Cyrus understood with cold clarity that Veyra didn't want corpses on this mountain. Veyra wanted her alive, intact, *ripe*. The vessel harvested whole.

Maera was already walking toward the soldiers, hands still raised, head high, choosing the consequence with every step, and Cyrus stood torn between the healer's fury — *you don't get to spend yourself, I didn't drag you out of that river to lose you here* — and something older and stranger rising in her chest: admiration, fierce and helpless, the way you admire a flame exactly because it burns.

"Go," Maera called back, without turning. "Knock on the door, Cyrus. Tell them the priestess paid her toll. Tell them—" her voice caught, just once, "—tell them Maera of Vel Saroth is *coming home*."

Soldiers closed around her like gray water. The last Cyrus saw of her was the brand at her collarbone, catching the sunrise.

Then Sefa's hand was on her shoulder, hauling her back toward the black doorway, Ashvane dragging herself up the final yards with a sound like a mountain grieving, arrows hissing past, and the gold light behind the ancient stone pulsing faster now, brighter, like a heart that had heard its name called after thirty-one years of silence.

The doorway stood open before them. Beyond it, stairs descended into fire.

Behind them, the whole armed weight of the crown came over the ridge.

Cyrus took her mother's recipe cards from her coat — the map, the legacy, the last thing she owned that had loved her — pressed them once to her lips, and stepped through.
