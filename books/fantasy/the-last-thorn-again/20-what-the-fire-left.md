## 20. What the Fire Left

The mountain stopped shaking on the third day after the reforge.

Maera felt it the way she felt everything now—through her whole body, through memories that had come back to her like knives and stayed like scars. The tremors had been Ashvane's dying breath moving through the stone of Thornfell, and when they ceased, Maera knew without being told that the dragon was gone.

She climbed down from the shrine ledge where she'd slept—or lain awake pretending to sleep—and made her way along the corridor of fused stone toward the First Hearth. The air changed as she walked. It always changed now. Before the covenant broke, before she broke it, the approach to a god-touched place had smelled of incense and cold water. This smelled of woodsmoke and bread. Someone was baking.

That alone would have told her the world had turned over. In forty years of service to silent gods, no sacred space had ever smelled like breakfast.

Cyrus sat cross-legged at the hearth's edge, feeding split logs into a flame that burned white-gold and utterly still, like a held breath that had finally decided to exhale. The Hearthstone shard—the one they hadn't lost, the fragment Sefa had pried from her own lucky charm belt—glowed in the fire's heart, no longer needed but not discarded either. Some things you kept because they remembered what you were.

"She went an hour before dawn," Cyrus said, without looking up. "I thought you'd want to know."

"I knew." Maera lowered herself onto the warm stone beside her. Her knees protested. Everything about her protested these days; resurrection of memory came with interest charges. "You can feel it too, can't you? Where she went."

"The hearth feels... emptier. But not dark." Cyrus turned a log with a bronze poker, and Maera watched the girl's hands—steady hands, poisoner's hands, covenant-bearer's hands now. "Like a room after someone leaves who loved it well."

"That's exactly right." Maera's throat tightened. "That is exactly the prayer I would have written for her, if anyone had asked me to write prayers anymore."

* * *

They buried nothing, because there was nothing left to bury. What remained of Ashvane lay in the First Hearth itself: a bed of embers that refused to cool, arranged in a shape that might have been accidental and was not—a long curve, a suggestion of wings folded, a dragon sleeping in fire instead of ash.

Sefa came up from the lower chambers mid-morning, smelling of snow and the cheap wine she drank when she was grieving and didn't want anyone to notice. She carried the dragon's gift: three scales, each the size of a dinner plate, shed naturally over the last century and hoarded in the ice caves like the rest of her treasure. One for each voice the covenant had accepted.

"Smuggler's custom," Sefa said gruffly, pressing a scale into Maera's arms. "You don't leave a dead partner's goods sitting unclaimed. Bad luck. Insulting, even."

"Ashvane wasn't my partner."

"She shielded you with her body when Veyra's soldiers fired those ballista bolts." Sefa's eyes were red-rimmed and hard. "In my trade, that makes her your partner whether you signed papers or not."

Maera looked down at the scale. Up close, its surface held patterns like frost ferns, like river deltas, like the branching paths of every prayer ever whispered by someone who wasn't sure anyone listened. She had spent twenty years telling herself that the silence of the gods had been mercy—that a god who answered could also demand, and she had seen enough of demands. Now she held proof in her arms that listening had never actually stopped. The gods had gone quiet the way a host goes quiet when guests break something precious in another room. Not absent. Appalled.

"Thank you," she said, and meant more than the scale.

"Don't thank me. Thank her." Sefa jerked her chin at the ember-bed. "And don't do anything stupid with that scale, priestess. I've got a reputation to consider."

She left the way smugglers left—suddenly, loudly, already complaining about the price of rope in the valley towns. Maera watched her go and felt the strange new weight of affection for people she had known less than a season. That was what belonging did, she supposed. It accumulated debts you were glad to owe.

* * *

The first prayer she wrote in thirty-nine years took four attempts.

Not because the words were hard. Because the words were easy, and that terrified her. Old habit wanted liturgy: formal invocations, the careful architecture of worship she'd been trained in before she'd traded it away for crown silver and a lie about preventing a war. But the flame in the First Hearth didn't want architecture. When she knelt with her formal phrases prepared, the fire dimmed, patient and faintly amused, like a grandmother waiting for a child to stop reciting and simply talk.

So on the fourth attempt, Maera set aside the scroll and spoke plainly.

"You let her die content," she said. "I need to know if that was kindness or if it was just what fire does when it finishes burning. I need to know if you forgave me, or if you simply decided I was more useful broken than destroyed. And I need—" Her voice caught. "I need to know if what I did can be mended or only ended. Those are different things. I've spent my whole life confusing them."

The flame rose. Not dramatically—not the theater of miracle she remembered from her novitiate, pillars of fire and voices in thunder. It rose the way a listener leans forward. And in the warmth against her face, Maera felt an answer arrive, and the answer was not words.

It was a question.

*Why do you assume forgiveness and use are opposites?*

She sat back on her heels, breath knocked out of her. The presence filled the chamber—not overwhelming, just total, the way morning light is total. And threaded through it, unmistakable, was Ashvane. Not returned, not speaking, but present the way a beloved melody is present in a room where someone once hummed it. The ember-bed brightened gently as the god's attention passed over it, the way you'd smooth a blanket over a sleeper.

"Oh," Maera said. "Oh, you're going to be like this."

The warmth flickered. If fire could be wry, this was wry.

"You're not going to give answers," she said slowly. "You're going to give better questions. Every time. For the rest of my life."

The flame did not deny it.

She laughed—a cracked, rusty sound, the laugh of a woman who had drunk Lethe-water to escape her own mind and had gotten all of it back and then some. It turned wet halfway through. She let it. Cyrus appeared in the doorway with an armful of kindling, took one look, and quietly withdrew, which Maera appreciated more than any sermon.

When she finished weeping, the fire was still there. Still leaning forward. Waiting, not for her to be finished with grief, but to see what she would build out of it.

* * *

They descended from the First Hearth on the sixth day, because holy mountains, Maera had learned, were like feasts: staying too long turned communion into indulgence. The company gathered at the gate where the betrayal had happened, where soldiers had seized the stones and Maera had stood before them and named her crime aloud for the first time in forty years.

Kell was there. The bounty hunter—converted witness, lawless voice, the third speaker the flame had accepted—stood apart from the others with his wrists wrapped where the ropes had chafed, watching Maera with the expression of a man still deciding whether he'd been saved or captured. She suspected he'd be deciding for years. She suspected that was healthy.

"You're really going back to it," he said. "Priesting."

"I'm going back to the work. Whether it's still called priesting depends on which village you ask."

"They'll remember what you did. The old ones will. They'll remember the year the gods went silent and whose name was on the bribes."

"Yes," Maera said. "They will."

"And you're going anyway."

"Kell." She smiled, and felt how strange the expression was on her face, like a coat kept in cedar too long. "Forty years ago I broke a covenant because I believed I was saving the world from a war between gods. I was wrong about the war. I was wrong about the saving. The only thing I was right about was that mortals shouldn't kneel to power that doesn't answer questions—and then I helped make sure no power answered anything at all." She touched the dragon scale slung across her back. "The least I can do is walk into every village that remembers and stand there while they decide what I'm worth. That's not penance. Penance would be easier. It's just the job."

He studied her a long moment. Then he nodded once, the way smugglers and bounty hunters nodded—which was to say, completely.

* * *

The descent through Thornfell was a pilgrimage nobody had planned.

Word moved ahead of them the way word always moved through the mountains—faster than horses, faster than sense. By the second day, people lined the switchbacks: herders, trappers, a whole caravan of dyers with their bolts strapped to mules. They didn't cheer. Maera had braced herself for cheering or stones and received instead something far harder to bear: attention. Faces turning toward her. Eyes that saw a disgraced priestess walking down out of the holy mountain with a dragon's scale on her back, and waited to learn what that meant.

At the shrine of the Twin Springs—where Cyrus and Sefa had found her weeks ago, half-mad with forgetting—a girl of maybe ten stood at the roadside holding a wooden bowl of spring water. She thrust it out as Maera passed, then lost her nerve and stared at her boots.

"For the blessing?" the girl mumbled. "Gran says the springs answered again. Gran says the gods are back. Is it true?"

Maera stopped. The company paused around her—Cyrus watchful, Sefa ostentatiously checking her mule's harness, Kell hovering like a man ready to catch whatever fell.

Here it was, then. The moment she'd been dreading since the hearth. Not the grand confession at the gate—that had been strategy, buying time, spending truth like coin. This was worse. This was a child.

"What's your name?"

"Maret."

"Maret." Maera crouched, joints cracking, until they were eye to eye. "The gods aren't back the way your gran means, probably. There won't be burning bushes. There won't be voices from the sky telling armies where to march. What there will be—" she touched the surface of the spring water in the bowl, and it shivered though her hand hadn't broken it, "—is attention. You'll pray, and instead of nothing, you'll get a question back. Something that makes you figure out what you actually want. The gods used to do that, before my time. It's why they were loved, and it's also why someone like me was able to convince everyone they were dangerous." She swallowed. "Someone like me was wrong."

The girl frowned with the tremendous seriousness of ten-year-olds. "A question back? Like what?"

Maera opened her mouth to say she didn't know—and stopped, because the bowl's water had stilled into a mirror, and in it she saw not her own face but the ember-bed high above, and rising through her chest came the warmth, the leaning-forward feeling, and the question arrived clear as a bell rung once:

*Maret asks if the gods have returned. What do you ask, Maera of the First Hearth?*

Her breath went out of her. The girl was watching her face, and whatever she saw there made her grip the bowl tighter.

"Ask her," Maret whispered. "Whatever it is. Ask her."

So Maera bowed her head over the spring water, in front of a child and a smuggler and a poisoner-turned-prophet and a bounty hunter learning to pray, and asked the question that had been sitting in her chest like a stone since the day she took the crown's silver.

"When I speak for you—at villages, at courts, wherever they'll listen—how do I keep from becoming what I was? How does anyone carry a god's voice without deciding it agrees with them?"

The water warmed under her fingers.

The answer, when it came, was so simple she nearly missed it: a sensation like a hand on the back of her neck, gentle, turning her—toward Cyrus. Toward Sefa arguing with her mule. Toward Kell, standing awkwardly in the road with his wrapped wrists.

*Never carry it alone.*

Maera laughed again, and this time it didn't crack. "Yes," she said to the water, to the mountain, to the long patience above them all. "Yes, I think that was always the whole secret, wasn't it?"

She blessed Maret's bowl with a splash of spring water and no liturgy whatsoever, and the girl ran off downhill shouting to her gran, and Maera stood in the road feeling lighter than she had in forty years and twice as accountable.

* * *

They reached the valley town of Lowmark on the ninth evening, and that was where the hook of the new world caught them all.

The inn was full—not with travelers but with locals, packed shoulder to shoulder, and when the company pushed through the door the room didn't hush the way rooms hushed for famous people. It hushed the way rooms hushed for weather.

An old man stood near the hearth, holding a letter, reading aloud in a shaky voice. "...and the king's own taster has founded a guild, stamped and chartered, for tasters and healers and road-speakers, sworn to serve any soul who pays—not just crowns. My cousin in the capital swears it's true. He watched the ceremony himself. And the chancellor—" here the old man's voice dropped, and the room leaned in, "—the chancellor's seat stands empty. Burned clean through, they say. Not her. Just her chair. Just the chair, and every record in her office, and the lock on her strongbox, melted to slag. Nothing else touched. The priests at the capital shrine say the fire left a message written in the ash, but they won't say what it says."

Maera looked at Cyrus across the crowded room. Cyrus looked back, and her face did something complicated—the look of a woman who had spent a decade perfecting invisibility and was now, apparently, guild-founding and legend-generating, and hadn't yet decided how she felt about being visible.

Sefa elbowed her way to the bar and came back with drinks and news. "Fire-messages. All down the valley. Every hearth that still burns true delivered something last night—one word each, they're saying. Different words for different houses." She set the cups down, and for once the smuggler's swagger was gone; she looked almost shy. "Ours said *debt paid*. Which, coming from a god, in my experience, is either very good or very bad news."

"And yours?" Maera asked.

Sefa turned her cup in her fingers. On the bottom, scorched into the glaze in letters of fine gold, a single word gleamed. She tilted it so they could read it.

*Grow.*

Silence held the table. Then Kell cleared his throat and turned over his own cup, and there it was in the glaze: *Witness.* Maera looked at hers. Hers said, in the same fine golden hand:

*Remember.*

They all looked at Cyrus. Cyrus, who had spent the whole descent saying she wanted nothing but a bath and a month of anonymity, stared at the bottom of her untouched cup for a long time. Whatever word the fire had left there, she covered it with her palm before anyone could see, and her expression, when she raised her head, held wonder and dread in equal measure—the look of a woman who had just learned the future had arrived unannounced, and was addressed to her by name.

"Well," said Sefa, raising her cup. "To questions that ask questions back."

They drank. Outside, the first stars came out over Thornfell, and in every hearth in Lowmark, the flames burned steady and attentive, leaning forward, waiting to hear what their people would ask next—and in the highest snowfield of the range, beneath the ashes of a frozen lake, something small and warm stirred in its shell, and began, patiently, to grow.
