## 21. A Seat at the Table

The throne room smelled of beeswax and lilies, which meant the king was nervous. Lilies were his mother's flower, and he ordered them brought in whenever he had to make a decision that frightened him.

Cyrus stood before the dais in a dress that fit her badly—new wool, stiff at the collar, dyed a deep hearth-red that made her feel like an accusation. Three months ago she would have worn grey. Grey was the color of furniture, of servants, of people whose names were written down only on pay ledgers. But grey had been a costume, and costumes, she was learning, could be taken off.

"Kneel," said Chancellor—no. Not chancellor anymore. Veyra's office had been dissolved, its seals melted down, its greenhouses turned over to the healers' guilds for growing actual medicine. The man who stood there now was Lord Protector Aldous Renn, a broad, careful man with ink-stained fingers who had spent the interregnum doing the unglamorous work of keeping the kingdom from eating itself.

She knelt. The marble was cold through the wool. Somewhere behind her, the court held its breath—the same court that had never once looked at her in ten years of tasting their food, now craning to see the Poisoner of Record, the Woman Who Reforged the Covenant, the Last Dravenmoor of the First Hearth. They had given her so many names in three months she sometimes forgot which one answered when she was called.

"Cyrus Dravenmoor," Renn read from a scroll, "for service beyond measure to crown and realm, the king is pleased to offer the title Duchess of the Thornfell Marches, Warden of the First Hearth, with lands, revenues, and a seat upon the Privy Council."

The words hung in the air like incense smoke. Cyrus kept her eyes on the middle step of the dais and thought about soup.

* * *

She had been back in the palace kitchens for six days before anyone worked up the courage to speak to her.

It was the scullery maid, Pell. Of course it was Pell—the girl whose burnt stew had unmade itself in front of Cyrus's eyes and started all of this. She was sixteen now, taller than Cyrus remembered, with flour on her forearms and the particular wary stillness of someone who had prayed once and been answered and hadn't decided yet whether that was a blessing or a wound.

"You're really going to eat here?" Pell asked, watching Cyrus ladle barley soup into the taster's silver bowl. "After everything."

"I'm really going to eat here." Cyrus blew across the spoon. Barley, leek, a little too much salt because the new cook was nervous. No arsenic. No nightshade. Nothing older than salt. She swallowed. "Someone has to make sure you lot aren't trying to poison him again. You put in far too much thyme last week. It was practically assault."

Pell laughed—a startled, bright sound—and then pressed her hand over her mouth as though laughing in front of Cyrus Dravenmoor might summon something.

"Pell." Cyrus set down the spoon. "What I did wasn't magic. Tasting. Anyone can learn it. Most people just don't want to spend ten years being invisible enough to do it."

"Invisible?" Pell frowned. "You weren't invisible. We all knew who you were. We just—" she hesitated, choosing the words the way you choose steps across ice, "—we thought you wanted to be left alone. So we left you alone."

Cyrus had carried many weights out of Thornfell: the shard's loss, Ashvane's death, Maera's confession, the memory of fire taking Veyra apart in a room full of witnesses. None of them had landed quite as hard as that sentence, delivered by a scullery girl over a pot of barley soup.

All those years she had believed her invisibility was a wall she built. It had been a door everyone else was politely holding shut.

* * *

"I must decline," Cyrus said now, to the throne room, to the king, to three hundred faces turning toward her like flowers toward an unexpected sun.

A murmur ran through the court. On the dais, King Aldric—who had been a tired middle-aged man when she started tasting for him and was a tired old one now, softened somewhat by the return of small mercies to the world—leaned forward on his throne.

"Decline?"

"The title, Your Majesty. And the lands. And the council seat." She rose from her knees, because this next part was easier standing. "I didn't cross Thornfell so that power could pool around me instead of around the crown. That was Veyra's mistake, not her method. I'd rather not inherit it."

"Then what," said the king, and there was something underneath the royal puzzlement that sounded almost like hope, "do you want?"

It was, Cyrus reflected, the first time in her life anyone in this room had asked her that question directly. Ten years of tasting. A hundred dishes a month. Not once: *what do you want?*

She had prepared an answer. She'd written it out three times and burned two drafts because they sounded either too proud or too apologetic. The third draft was folded in her pocket, going soft with the heat of her palm, and she found now that she didn't need it.

"A guild," she said. "Chartered, funded, answerable to the crown but staffed by its own. Tasters, first—trained ones, paid ones, people who learn poisons the way I did but with wages and days off and someone who notices if they don't come home." A ripple of uneasy laughter moved through the court; she let it. "Healers, second. Veyra's greenhouses are sitting empty and the waking gods have been generous with simples and charms, but generosity isn't training. And third—" she paused, because this was the part she'd rehearsed least, "—road-speakers."

"Road-speakers," repeated Renn, writing it down. The man wrote everything down. She liked him enormously for it.

"Smugglers," Cyrus translated. "Former smugglers. Sefa has agreed to serve as master of that branch, if the crown can stomach stamping the papers of people who've spent their lives dodging it." She heard Sefa shift behind her—she'd insisted the whole company attend, over Sefa's loud objections about palaces and *the smell of them*—and felt rather than saw the smuggler's grin. "The Thorn roads exist whether we name them or not. Better they belong to people who know every switchback, answering to a charter, than to whoever's willing to pay in the dark."

"And this guild," the king said slowly. "It would hold the First Hearth?"

"No one holds the First Hearth." The words came out of her with a weight that silenced the room, because they were not entirely hers—they were the covenant speaking through the scar tissue of her body, through ten years of small poisons metabolized into purpose. "That was the whole lesson. The flame answers consent, not command. The guild doesn't guard the Hearth. The guild *tends* it. There's a difference, and everyone in this room learned the difference three months ago, some of us more expensively than others."

Her eyes found the empty place near the eastern pillars where Veyra had stood. Where Veyra had *been*, until the fire finished its arithmetic. The stone there had been replaced; the masons had matched it well. You'd never know, unless you knew.

The king was quiet for a long moment. Lilies nodding in their vases. Courtiers not daring to breathe.

"Granted," Aldric said finally. "All of it. Chartered under your name, Cyrus Dravenmoor." He smiled, tired and genuine. "Though I confess I'll miss your discretion at my table."

"Your Majesty will eat better without me," Cyrus said. "I've spent ten years making sure your cooks erred on the side of bland."

* * *

The charter ceremony was smaller than the audience—Renn, the guild's first eleven members, and a table in the east wing with ink that took forever to dry.

The first eleven: four tasters recruited from kitchen staff, including Pell, who had signed her name with the fierce concentration of someone carving it into history. Three healers out of the charity houses, one of whom had been treating god-touched fevers since the waking and had opinions about the old liturgy that Maera, consulted by letter, had pronounced *heretical but useful*. Two road-speakers sent up from Thornfell with Sefa's seal already burned into their travel papers. One archivist, a dry-witted woman named Osla whom Cyrus had met while breaking into the palace archive and who had, it turned out, seen her do it and simply decided to finish cataloguing the falsified hiring records herself. And one former bounty hunter.

Corvus sat at the end of the table, uncomfortable in clean clothes, his broken arm healed crooked at the wrist because he refused healer's attention on principle. He had been the bounty hunter in the cairn. He had been the one who sold their route to Veyra's soldiers at the Hearth Gate. And he had been the one standing witness at the First Hearth when the flame took three voices—lawless, god-touched, and mortal—and remade the world's oldest bargain.

"I still think this is a trap," Corvus said, staring at the charter like it might bite.

"It's paper," Cyrus said.

"Paper's how they get you. Charters. Contracts. Confessions." He flexed his crooked wrist. "I sold you out. You gave me a *seat*. Nobody does that."

"Somebody did," Cyrus said. "Sefa pulled you out of a bounty cage once, years ago. She never told you. I asked her why, and she said, 'Because he looked cold.'" She slid the quill toward him. "That's the whole philosophy of the guild, right there. Somebody looked cold. So we fed them."

Corvus signed. His hand shook slightly. Osla witnessed it without comment, which was its own kindness.

When the last signature dried, Cyrus stood, and eleven people stood with her, and no one knelt to anyone, and she thought: *this*. This was the thing she'd been building toward without knowing it—not the covenant, not the fire, not even the naming. This moment where survival skills became shared ones. Where the people who'd learned to live in the cracks were handed the tools to build on open ground.

* * *

Three weeks later, the king requested her at his table one final time.

Not a formal occasion. Just supper, in the small dining room, the one with the bad draft from the north window that the masons kept failing to fix because, rumor said, the king liked the reminder that his palace leaked like any other house. A single place setting across from him. Soup tureen steaming between them.

"Nervous habit," Aldric said, gesturing at the tureen. "Indulge an old man. I haven't eaten a meal in ten years without watching for your little pause afterward—the one where you'd decide whether I was about to die."

"I didn't know you noticed the pause."

"I noticed everything about you, Master Dravenmoor. That was rather the problem. I was never allowed to say so." He served himself, then waited, hands folded, while she filled her bowl and tasted.

Barley again. Leeks. Salt, correctly measured this time—the new cooks had settled. A bay leaf, which was showing off. She swallowed, and set down the spoon, and performed for the last time the small ritual that had defined her adult life: the pause, the assessment, the verdict.

"You'll live," she said.

"What a strange thing," the king murmured, ladling more soup, "to be told by the person who saved your life eight times that you'll finally be allowed to just *have* it."

"Eight?"

"Nine, counting the arsenic in the wedding feast wine. I did the arithmetic after Thornfell. You identified nine attempts on my life and reported none of them through official channels." He looked at her over the rim of his bowl, and there was nothing regal in his expression at all, only a man's plain wonder. "You just quietly removed them and went back to standing in corners."

"Invisibility was armor," Cyrus said. "I told myself that for twenty years. It took a dragon dying on a frozen lake to show me the difference between armor and a coffin."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to stand in the middle of rooms and be extremely visible and probably terrible at it." She smiled. "The guild will send you a proper taster within the fortnight. Trained, chartered, salaried. Her name is Pell. She has an excellent palate and no idea yet how much she's worth. Be kind to her—she prayed to a dead god over a burnt stew and got answered, and she's still deciding what that means."

"I'll be kind," the king promised. Then, quieter: "The gods are quiet lately. Present, but quiet. Maera says that's how it should be."

"Maera says a great deal of things with enormous authority these days." Cyrus turned her spoon in the cooling soup. "She's earned it. But yes—quiet is right. Answers that ask questions back. Presence, not miracle. The covenant holds because nobody's trying to own it. That includes me." She looked up. "Especially me. Which is why I'm giving you this."

She reached into her coat and set something on the table between them: a small vial, stoppered with wax, its contents the color of dusk. Her final preparation. The antidote broad-spectrum, brewed by her own hand, refined over twenty years, the last piece of the arsenal that had kept her alive through a decade of deliberate poisoning.

"I don't need it anymore," she said. "My body isn't a weapon or a vessel or a vault anymore. It's just mine. And I find I don't want to carry armor into rooms where I'm actually invited."

The king picked up the vial carefully, as though it were a relic, which in a sense it was.

"What will you do with yourself, Cyrus Dravenmoor? Duchess of nothing, warden of an unlocked hearth, mistress of a guild of reformed criminals and scullery girls?"

"Teach," she said. "Taste, occasionally, when Pell wants a second opinion. Walk the Thorn roads in spring when Sefa lets me—I've been promised the northern passes are better in daylight." She stood, and he stood with her, an old king and a woman who had spent her life learning how not to be seen, both of them smiling at the absurdity of the view from here. "And mostly? Sit at tables. Unpoisoned ones. With people who know my name."

"That last part," the king said gravely, "is the bravest thing you've done yet."

* * *

The guildhall's kitchen smelled of bread and woodsmoke and, faintly, of the First Hearth itself—a warmth that had followed the company down from the mountain and never entirely left them, settling into walls and lungs like a memory the body refused to release.

Cyrus came down the stairs past the new charter plaque—Osla had insisted on engraving it, and had smuggled into the border flourishes a tiny thorn and a tiny flame, side by side—and found the evening shift already at work. Pell at the stove, teaching two recruits the bitter-green smell of oleander contamination. One of the road-speakers arguing cheerfully with Corvus about the fastest line through Wolf's Tooth Pass. Sefa perched on the big table, boots muddy from the day's run, reading a letter and grinning like a cat in a creamery.

"News from the lake?" Cyrus asked.

Sefa held up the letter. Her hand, usually so steady on a knife, shook slightly. "From Bren, the one I left watching Ashvane's ashes. Cyrus." She looked up, and Cyrus saw in her face something she had only seen once before—on a frozen lake, in the guttering amber eye of a dying dragon. Wonder, wearing fear's clothes. "The ash moved. Three nights ago. It's warm, Bren says. Warm in the middle of winter, warm enough that the ice won't close over it." She turned the letter around so Cyrus could read the last lines, written in a hand gone shaky with excitement:

*There's an egg in the ashes. It's singing.*

Cyrus stood very still in the doorway of her guildhall, in her borrowed light, surrounded by the sounds of people who knew her name—and felt the covenant turn over in its sleep, like a guest shifting closer to the fire.

"Well," she said at last, reaching for her coat. "Tell the road-speakers to ready the northern passes. And somebody wake Maera."

"Why? What do we do?"

Cyrus grinned—the unguarded, un-grey grin of a woman thoroughly, permanently seen.

"We go meet the future. It's arriving unannounced, and it's going to want breakfast."
