## 22. Smoke on the Wind

The pass had a new name now, and Sefa hated it.

They called it the Hearthgate Road, because of course they did, because the guild clerks in their warm offices down in Vale Corrin needed something tidy to stamp on their ledgers. *Hearthgate Road, toll-free by covenant law, open to all licensed traders.* Twenty years she'd run that stretch of stone under three different names—Kestrel Cut when the king's men were watching, Widow's Teeth when they weren't, and once, memorably, Don't —and now it had a name like a bakery.

She rode it openly anyway. That was the part that still felt like stealing.

Her guild badge hung off her left rein where any checkpoint officer could see it: a stamped copper thorn on green wax, the Dravenmoor seal pressed beneath it. *Road-speaker, third class.* Third class. She'd smuggled firewine past two separate wars and once talked her way out of a noose with nothing but a dead man's boots and a better lie, and some clerk had looked at her ledger of honest declarations for four years running and decided she was third class.

"Could've been first," Tam said beside her, reading her face the way he always did. "You didn't ask."

"Asking's for people who want things."

"You want things."

"I want the road clear and my cargo dry," Sefa said. "Everything else is weather."

But her thumb kept finding the badge as they climbed, worrying its edge like a loose tooth. Four years of open roads. Four years of declaring her goods at the gate and paying honest tolls and sleeping without one ear cocked for boots on gravel. She'd thought it would chafe forever, this legality. Instead it had settled over her like an old coat that turned out to fit.

The mountains hadn't changed, though. Thornfell kept its own counsel the way it always had—the same wind sawing through the pines, the same scree slopes that shed rocks at travelers like a dog shaking off rain, the same high silence above the treeline where you could feel how small you were. The covenant had returned the gods to the world, but the gods had never been in the mountains. The mountains were older than gods and would be here after, and they made sure you knew it.

They made camp that night below the ridge where everything had almost ended—the ambush ridge, though nobody called it that anymore except her, privately, the way you don't say a dead friend's name too loud. Tam got the fire going. Olla sang half a song about a fisherman's wife before falling asleep mid-verse, which was traditional now; the caravan had rules about these things, unspoken ones, and one of them was that Olla never finished a song after sundown or the road went sour. Superstition. Sefa let them keep it. A caravan needed its rituals the way a body needed salt.

She took first watch, which was also traditional, because she'd never learned to sleep early and had stopped pretending otherwise around her fortieth winter.

That was when she smelled the smoke.

Not woodsmoke. Not peat. This was mineral smoke, hot-stone smoke, the smell of a forge burning something that wasn't iron—and there was only one place in these mountains that smell came from, and only one reason it should be rising out of a valley that had been cold for four years.

Sefa stood very still and let the old instincts come up out of their grave.

*Ashvane's lake* lay two valleys east, past the spine of rock the old maps called the Serpent's Tooth. It had been a lake once—a frozen one, black ice thick enough to walk a wagon onto, and beneath it, in a hollow of warm stone, the last dragon of the covenant had been dying for longer than anyone could count. Sefa had traded with her. Favors for passage, passage for favors, a debt unnamed and therefore unpayable and therefore perfect. Then Cyrus Dravenmoor had walked into the dragon's memory and come back changed, and Ashvane had spent what was left of herself shielding two women from a chancellor's soldiers, and afterward there had been no more dragon. There had been a lake with a hole melted in its heart, and ash floating on the water like grey snow, and Sefa had stood on the shore and felt the exact shape of the word *unpayable* change meaning forever.

The ember had gone into the First Hearth. Everyone said so. Maera had said so, and Maera was a priestess again and priestesses were supposed to know.

Sefa woke Tam with a boot to the shin, gentle as she knew how.

"Cargo check," she said. "Now."

He blinked up at her, read her face, and didn't argue. Good lad. She'd have shot him otherwise, or at least told him she would have.

* * *

They reached the Serpent's Tooth at grey dawn, and Sefa made the others wait at the crest while she went down alone, because some doors you knock on yourself.

The valley had not recovered. Nothing grew in the burn scar, and the meltwater ran thin and strange over ground that had been scoured to bare rock. But the lake itself—

The lake was gone.

In its place lay a basin of pale glassy stone, fused smooth, wide as a threshing floor, and at its center rose a low mound of ash like a cairn somebody had built and then apologized for. And from that mound, thin as thread, rose the smoke.

Sefa walked out onto the fused stone. Her boots clicked. Somewhere far below the surface—far, far below—something ticked and shifted, thermal breath, a heartbeat measured in hours instead of moments. The heat came up through her soles gentle as a cat leaning against your leg.

"Ashvane?" she said, and hated how young her voice sounded.

The smoke curled. Did not answer. Dragons answered when they chose, and Ashvane had chosen her last words long ago, standing between soldiers and the woman she'd named heir to a covenant older than kingdoms. Whatever warmth remained down there was just warmth. Residue. A banked fire keeping itself out of habit.

Sefa shouldered her pack and started to turn away, and that was when the ash moved.

Not much. A shift, a slump, the way snow slides off a branch. But enough. She was across the basin before she'd decided to move, kneeling in the warm grit, digging with both hands like a woman digging a child out of a collapse—and she was, in every way that mattered. Because there, cradled in a hollow of fused stone that someone or something had shaped with deliberate care, sat an egg.

It was the size of a bread loaf and the color of banked coals, dark veined with orange, and it was warm. Warm the way the basin floor was warm, yes—but warmer at its crown, where a fine crack had begun to spider outward, patient as frost forming on a window.

"Oh," Sefa said. Just that. One syllable, all she had.

She looked around the empty valley as if someone might be standing there to explain. Nobody was. The mountains kept their counsel. But the shape of the hollow, the care of it, the way the egg sat exactly at the point where the deepest heat rose—it had been placed. Ashvane had gone into the Hearthfire and left this behind the way a merchant leaves a sealed letter with a trusted partner: *hold this until I can't, and then it's yours.*

Unpayable debts, Sefa thought. You spend your whole life arranging them so nobody ever has to settle up. And then the road goes and settles them anyway.

* * *

She didn't touch the egg. That was the first rule of anything ancient and warm—you don't grab, you offer. So she built a windbreak of stones, unpacked her own cloak, and lined the hollow with wool, and then she sat back on her heels and had the longest think of her life, right there on a dead dragon's grave, while the sun came up over the Serpent's Tooth and her caravan waited impatiently on the ridge.

Options. She was good at options.

Take the egg to the capital. No. The capital had clerks, and clerks had ledgers, and the first thing written in any ledger about a dragon egg would be *asset*. She'd spent four years learning what the covenant meant—she'd watched Veyra unmade by a fire she tried to own, from a hillside two miles off, close enough to feel the heat and far enough to survive it—and the whole lesson, the entire blasted lesson, was that power held is power lost.

Take it to the guild. Same problem with better manners.

Leave it. The valley was warm, protected, hidden. The egg had survived four years without her; it might manage forty more. Except the crack was spreading *now*, today, and whatever hatched would hatch hungry and small and alone in a burn scar with nothing to eat, and Sefa had buried too many friends to watch a child starve out of respect for its mother's privacy.

Which left one option, and it was the one her feet had already decided, because her feet had always been wiser than her head.

Vale Corrin was six days' ride. Cyrus Dravenmoor lived outside it now—not in the palace, never again in the palace, but in a stone house near the tasters' guildhall with a garden full of godsbane she grew for medicine instead of murder, and a door that opened for anyone hungry. Sefa had eaten at that door maybe thirty times. Every time, Cyrus had asked about the roads, and every time, Sefa had answered honestly, which was still the strangest habit she'd ever acquired.

Cyrus would know what to do. Or, more likely, Cyrus would refuse to decide alone—which was its own kind of knowing, and the thing about her that Sefa had come to love most and admit least.

* * *

They broke camp within the hour. Sefa wrapped the egg in her cloak and then in oiled canvas and then, after a moment's consideration, in her spare shirt, because the thing had started making a sound—a faint, rhythmic tapping, like a knuckle on a door—and she found she couldn't bear the idea of it traveling cold.

Tam rode beside her and did not ask questions for eleven miles, which showed restraint, and then asked all of them at once, which did not.

"It's an egg, Tam."

"I can see it's an egg."

"It's a dragon egg."

"Yes," Tam said. "I can see that it's a dragon egg, Sefa, I'm asking why we're carrying it like a sack of onions toward the largest concentration of government officials south of the mountains."

"We're not carrying it to officials. We're carrying it to Cyrus."

"That's worse. She's the official-est official there is. They wanted to give her a title. There was talk of a statue."

"And she declined the title and threatened to melt down the statue commission's budget into soup for the poor, which I witnessed personally." Sefa adjusted the bundle in front of her saddle. Under the canvas, the tapping had quickened, eager, insistent. "There's a difference between holding power and being powerful, Tam. Cyrus knows it. That chancellor never did. That's the whole story. That's the entire war we all survived."

Tam chewed on that for a mile. "So we're taking a dragon to dinner."

"We're taking a dragon to family," Sefa said, and heard how it sounded the instant it left her mouth, and decided not to take it back.

* * *

Six days. The tapping became scratching became, on the fifth night, a muffled chirp that sent Olla into hysterics and the mules into a ditch. On the sixth day, Vale Corrin rose out of the valley haze all red roofs and chimney smoke, and the gate guards waved Sefa's caravan through without a second glance—one glance, a nod at the thorn-and-hearth badge, a bored gesture onward—and Sefa thought, not for the first time, that the strangest thing the covenant had given her wasn't safety. It was boredom. Blessed, uneventful, lawful boredom.

Cyrus was in the garden.

Of course she was. She was always in the garden these days, sleeves rolled, hands in the dirt, looking less like the woman who'd reforged a divine covenant and more like exactly what she'd been all along: someone good at keeping things alive. Maera sat on the step shelling peas, silver-haired and serene, wearing her priesthood like a coat she'd finally grown into. Two apprentices weeded rows of nightshade under strict instruction. A kettle steamed on the outdoor hearth, because there was always a kettle, because there was always room.

Sefa dismounted, lifted the bundle down, and stood in the gateway holding it like an offering.

Cyrus looked up. Looked at Sefa's face. Looked at the bundle. And Sefa watched ten kinds of understanding cross that clever, careful face in the space of a breath—the last one being recognition, because the tapping had stopped, and in the sudden quiet a small voice from inside the canvas made a sound no bird had ever made: a thin, questioning note, like a bell struck once in an empty temple.

Maera dropped her peas.

"The lake," Cyrus said, standing slowly, dirt falling from her hands. "Sefa. What did you find?"

"Warm ashes," Sefa said. "And a guest."

She peeled back the canvas. The egg lay in its nest of wool, cracks webbing its crown now, and as the sunlight touched it the shell glowed from within, coal-orange, pulsing gently—breathing, or nearly. The little voice sounded again, closer, and this time Cyrus's scars lit in answer, faint gold lines along her forearms, the covenant recognizing its own bloodline's newest member before anyone else could.

"She left it for us," Cyrus said quietly. Not a question. Her hand hovered over the shell without touching. "All those years, guttering in the dark under the ice. She was waiting for the fire to come back so there'd be something worth hatching into."

"The fire came back," Sefa said. "Your fire."

"Our fire." Cyrus's correction was automatic now, worn smooth with use. She glanced at Maera, at the apprentices frozen mid-weed, at Sefa—at the whole improbable company of survivors gathered in a vegetable garden—and something in her face was the same look she'd worn stepping out of the dragon's memory four years ago: terror and wonder sharing a chair.

The shell split with a sound like river ice letting go in spring.

What emerged was small and damp and furious and blinking, wings like wet leaves, eyes like twin embers—and it looked, without hesitation or doubt, directly at Cyrus Dravenmoor, last of the First Hearth, and chirped the questioning note again. *Here? Is it here? Is this the world?*

"Yeah," Sefa said softly, answering for all of them, because someone had to and she'd always been quickest on the draw. "This is it, little one. Welcome in. Mind the peas."

The dragonet sneezed a spark that singed a row of lettuce, and Maera laughed until she cried, and Cyrus knelt in the dirt of her garden with ash-colored scales pressing against her palms, and Sefa leaned against the gatepost with her arms crossed and her guild badge catching the light, and thought:

Four years of open roads, honest tolls, stamped papers, boring, beautiful legality—and the future had still arrived unannounced, cracking out of its shell in a cabbage patch, hungry and loud and already home.

Some guests, she decided, you just stop being surprised by.

The dragonet opened its mouth and breathed its first true flame—a candle-flame, wobbling, magnificent—and lit the kitchen fire from twenty feet away, and somewhere far to the north, deep beneath the Serpent's Tooth, Sefa would have sworn she felt the mountain sigh.
