## 1. The Ledger Opens Both Ways

There are three rules in the Vellum Guild's inventory room, and I have broken all of them at least once.

Rule one: never taste the stock. Rule two: never read what isn't assigned to you. Rule three: if a memory whispers your name, report it to the guildmaster immediately and then take a long walk in the cold until you stop hearing it.

I was following none of them tonight, which is more or less my natural state.

The inventory room sits three floors above the counting hall, in the oldest part of the guildhouse, where the walls are stone instead of plaster because plaster doesn't hold memories well — they seep out of it like damp. Shelves upon shelves of vials in wax-sealed glass, each one labeled in ledger-script so fine it takes a jeweler's eye: *One grief, bottled, widow of Harrow Lane, vintage eleven years.* *Childhood, ages four through fourteen, seller deceased, buyer discreet.* *The smell of rain on a particular street in a city that no longer exists.*

That last one had been traded three times this year. People are strange about what they miss.

My name is Dominic Whitlock, and for nine years I have been the guild's keeper of impossible trades — the person who counts what cannot be counted, weighs what has no weight, and writes it all down in ledgers that would make an archivist weep and an auditor commit crimes. I am very good at my work. This is not a boast; it is a confession. The better I am at cataloguing other people's losses, the less time I have to catalogue my own.

Tonight's inventory was routine. That should have been my first warning. Routine nights are when the guild likes to test me — slip something odd into the manifest and see how long I take to notice. Tonight the manifest listed forty-one items. Forty were ordinary by our standards, which is to say horrifying by anyone else's.

Item thirty-one was a mother's love for a son who'd died at sea, sold back to her twice already by different brokers, and I made a note in the margin: *verify provenance; someone is selling this woman her own heart at retail.*

Item forty was a decade of someone's childhood — the whole thing, ages six through sixteen, decanted into a single grey vial that hummed faintly when I held it up to the lamp. Childhoods always hum. Griefs sit heavy and silent, like stones. Loves are warm to the touch, embarrassingly so, and I handle them with gloves because once, early on, I touched one bare-handed and spent the rest of the day missing a family I'd never had. But childhoods hum. They haven't decided yet what they want to be.

This one hummed off-key. I turned it under the lamp, checked the seal, checked the label. The seller's name had been scraped away with a knife, carefully, the way you'd peel a label off a bottle you intended to refill. Someone didn't want their childhood traced back to them.

*Noted,* I wrote. *Flagged for Corvane.*

And then there was item forty-one.

It wasn't a vial. It was a book.

Now, books come through the inventory room maybe once a season, and they're usually worthless — diaries whose owners sold everything except paper, or grimoires with the useful pages torn out. Guild policy says unclaimed books go to the binding room and become ledgers themselves, which has always struck me as a small cruelty dressed as efficiency. A book that held somebody's thoughts gets its spine cracked and its soul ruled into columns.

This book was old. Not guild-old, not even building-old. Old the way rivers are old. The leather had gone the color of dried blood and soft as skin, and there was no title on the cover, just a stamp I didn't recognize: a thorned branch, closed in a circle.

I should have sent it down to binding. I want that on the record, wherever records go after we're done making them. I *should have.*

Instead I opened it.

The first hundred pages were prophecy — or what passes for it in our trade, which is to say handwriting so confident it dares you to disbelieve it. Dates running backward past the founding of the guild, past the reign of the Thorn King, past anything I could name. Entries in at least four hands, maybe five, some of them centuries apart, all describing the same events with the same maddening vagueness: *the snow comes early, the crown remembers, the thief opens the door.*

Prophecy is a scam. I've inventoried enough of it to know. It works the way memory-brokers work: you say something vague enough, and every reader finds their own face in it. *A crowned sleeper shall wake.* Fine. Every king who ever napped fulfilled that one.

But the entries got stranger as the dates approached the present. Less riddle, more record. An entry from sixty years ago described, in flat prose, a fire in the eastern quarter — date, hour, number of dead — written fifty years before the fire happened. I checked. I know the fire. We hold three memories of it in the vaults. The numbers matched exactly.

I told myself it was coincidence. I am a professional at telling myself things.

Then I reached the last written page, and the last entry, and my hands did something embarrassing before my mind caught up. They went still. Completely still, the way a rabbit goes still, the way you go still when you realize the floor you've trusted for years has been hollow all along.

The entry was dated tonight.

Not approximately. Not in the vague prophetic way where *the seventh night of the waning moon* could mean any winter in any century. The full date, written out in the guild's own house calendar, the one Corvane keeps locked in his office — the calendar only four people alive can read, and I'm one of them because I invented half its shorthand.

Tonight's date. In a book older than the guildhouse.

And beneath the date, in ink that was still — I pressed my thumb to it before I could stop myself, and it smudged, and I felt something in my chest turn over like a key — beneath the date, in the same hand:

*Dominic Whitlock opens the door.*

My own name. My exact name, spelled the way I spell it, which is wrong according to every style manual in the city and right according to me. Nobody spells it that way. Nobody but me.

I sat down on the stool. I don't remember deciding to sit. The room was very quiet in the way the inventory room gets quiet after midnight, when the vials settle and stop humming and it feels less like a warehouse and more like a chapel where everyone's prayers are stored alphabetically.

Here is the thing you need to understand about being a keeper of records: you develop a theology. Mine runs something like this — nothing happens until it's written down. Events are chaos; records are order. The world is raw material and the ledger is the world made honest. For nine years I have believed, in the quiet place under my ribs where belief actually lives, that I stand downstream of history. Things happen. Then I write them down. The pen comes second. The pen *always* comes second.

And now here was a page where the pen had come first.

Somebody had written tonight down before tonight happened. Somebody had written *me* down before I sat on this stool holding this book, and they'd known the shorthand, and they'd known the spelling, and the ink was wet.

Wet. After a century.

Unless the century was the lie. Unless the book wasn't old at all but made to look old, planted in tonight's manifest by someone who wanted me to find it — Corvane, testing me again, though he's never tested me with anything this good, this cruel. Or unless the date wasn't tonight but a night like tonight, a hundred years ago, and some long-dead clerk named Whitlock — no. No, I checked the spelling again. Twice. Three times. My hands were not steady and I checked anyway.

*A door opens both ways,* said a smaller note beneath the main line, in yet another hand, cramped and hurried, as if written by someone running out of time or light. *What enters may leave. What leaves may enter. Keeper, count yourself among the stock.*

Count yourself among the stock.

I looked at the shelves around me. Four thousand vials, give or take, each one a piece of somebody — a grief, a love, a childhood, a single afternoon in a garden that meant more to its owner than the owner ever knew until it was sold. And somewhere in this room, mixed in among them, flagged in my own neat hand as item forty-one or shelved under some innocent label, was a memory that belonged to me. Had always belonged to me. Was waiting for me the way the page had waited, with my name on it and wet ink.

Or — and this thought arrived late and cold, sliding in under the door of my mind like winter — or the note meant something worse. Count yourself among the stock. As in: *you are already counted.* As in: somewhere in a ledger older than the guild, Dominic Whitlock is not the keeper but the kept. An entry. An item. Inventory.

The lamp guttered. It does that; drafty room, bad oil. I've worked through a thousand gutterings without looking up.

Tonight I looked up, and for half a heartbeat the shadows between the shelves arranged themselves into rows, and the rows into aisles, and the aisles went down — down farther than three floors, down past the counting hall, past the cellars, into the dark under the guildhouse where I have never been and where, I realized with the particular nausea of a man reading his own obituary, something was keeping accounts of its own.

Then the lamp steadied, and the room was just a room, and I was just a tired man with ink on his thumb and a book in his lap that knew him better than he knew himself.

I did what any sensible professional would do.

I stole it.

Not because I'm brave — I'm not, courage is for people who can afford the consequences — but because of rule two. Never read what isn't assigned to you. The corollary, which every keeper learns in their first year, is: *never let anyone learn what you've read.* A sealed memory missing from the vaults is a scandal. A prophecy naming me, sitting open on my desk at dawn? That's a confession. That's a gallows with my name on the placard.

So I closed the book, wrapped it in the oilcloth I use for leaking vials, and put it inside my coat, against my chest, where it lay warm as a stolen heart. I finished the manifest — forty entries, all accounted for, item forty-one logged as *book, valueless, to binding* — and I forged my supervisor's initials in the margin, which took me four seconds and which I have never before done in nine years of scrupulous, boring, blameless honesty.

Funny, the things you learn about yourself at midnight.

At the door of the inventory room I stopped. Habit. Always check the corridor, always lock the shelves. But standing there with my hand on the latch, I became aware of a sound I'd been ignoring for the better part of an hour — so deep and so regular I'd filed it under *the building settling*, where the guild files everything it doesn't want to think about.

A slow pulse. From below. Once every dozen breaths.

I have lived in this guildhouse for nine years. I have inventoried its ghosts and priced its silences. I know every sound it makes, and I am telling you it has never made that sound, or rather — I have never *heard* it, and those are different things, and I suspect the difference is the entire point of the book against my chest.

The pulse came again. Steady. Patient. Like something counting.

Like something keeping a ledger of its own, and waiting, with the calm of an entry written a century in advance, for the keeper to finally read his name.

* * *

I took the long stairs down, the servants' route, because the main stair passes Corvane's office and Corvane's lights were burning. They're always burning. The man sleeps the way a knife sleeps.

In the entrance hall, under the great seal of the guild — a quill crossed with a key, both of them, I noticed for the first time in nine years, growing out of a thorned branch — Maren was coming off the night shift, coat still dusted with street-snow. She's a collector, one of the best: goes out into the cold with a smile and a contract and comes back carrying pieces of people in her satchel. She nodded to me. I nodded back. Two professionals, exchanging nothing, which is the warmest greeting the guild allows.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Inventory," I said.

"Anything interesting?"

"No," I said, and felt the book press against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and hated how easily the lie came, and hated more that it came *smoothly*. Nine years of scrupulous honesty, undone in one sentence. Or maybe not undone. Maybe just — opened. A door opening both ways.

"Go home, Whitlock," she said, and walked out into the snow.

Snow. I stood in the doorway after she'd gone and watched it fall — early this year, too early, fat unhurried flakes that hadn't fallen when I'd come in at dusk. The weather-wardens had promised a mild month. The weather-wardens are usually right.

Behind me, three floors up and then far, far below that, the pulse beat on. Slow. Content. The rhythm of a sleeper who has dreamed the same dream for a hundred years, and whose dream, tonight, has just grown a new detail.

Me.

I pulled my collar up, stepped into the falling snow, and went home to decide whether I was a thief now, or had been one all along, and — the question underneath both, the one with wet ink — what exactly I had agreed to, a century ago, when somebody wrote down my name.

I did not sleep. But that, I'm afraid, is the next chapter's problem.

Mine was just beginning.
