## 4. A Child Made of Tomorrows

The guildhouse keeps me in the room with the good wallpaper.

I know it's the good wallpaper because Sister Vess told me, back when she still talked to me like I was going to stay. It's got little gold leaves on it, climbing up and up toward a ceiling I've never touched, and if you stare at one leaf long enough it stops looking like a leaf and starts looking like a hand. I counted them once, all four hundred and twelve of them, on a night when sleeping felt like drowning. Now when I can't sleep I don't count them anymore. Counting makes them move.

My name is Wren. I'm eight years old, which everyone says like it explains something. *She's only eight, she couldn't have known.* But knowing things is the whole entire reason they keep me, so I think that argument is broken and nobody wants to fix it.

Here is how it works, in case you're wondering, because everybody wonders and nobody asks:

I dream tomorrow. Not all of it — mostly just the sharp parts. The bad bits come through clearest, like how you can hear someone crying through a wall even when you can't hear them talking. A spilled cup of tea. A lie told in a stairwell. And sometimes — the times that matter — somebody dying.

That last kind started two years ago, when I was six and didn't know what blood looked like coming out of a person instead of a pigeon. The guild found out because I told a collector named Hobbs that his brother shouldn't take the river road on Thursday. Hobbs laughed and ruffled my hair and his brother took the river road anyway, and after Thursday, Hobbs stopped laughing at everything forever. After that, the guild gave me this room with the good wallpaper and a bell to ring when I dreamed, and they wrote down everything I said in ledgers with black covers.

They call me their little weatherglass. I heard Master Corvane say it once, to a fat man from the shipping houses. *Our little weatherglass, never wrong about storms.* He said it warm, like I was a thing he was proud of owning.

I've decided that when I'm big, I'm going to be something nobody owns. I haven't figured out what yet. Maybe a cat.

* * *

Tonight the dreaming comes early, before the candle's even burned down to its collar.

I'm walking in snow. That part's normal; lots of my dreams have snow lately, more than usual, snow falling up and sideways and once — this is the strange one — snow that had already fallen, lying in drifts inside rooms, inside people's mouths while they talked. But tonight there are thorns too. Thorns growing up through the snow like the snow is just a blanket somebody laid over a garden, and underneath it everything is waking. The thorns are black at the bottom and red at the tips, and they're not reaching for anything. They're reaching for *me*. And beneath the thorns, beneath the snow, under all of it, there's a heartbeat. Not mine. Bigger than mine. Slow, like a drum at the end of a long parade, the kind of drum you feel in your teeth.

And a voice, way down under everything, that doesn't use words. It just says *soon* the way heat says *hot*.

I wake up with my hands balled in the blanket and the bell right there on its little table where it always is, and I don't ring it.

That's the first time ever I haven't rung it. I want to be honest about that in case anybody reads this someday, though nobody will, because who would read an eight-year-old's ledger? Still. Honesty matters. Dominic says so.

I sit with my knees up to my chin and I look at the bell and I don't touch it, and the reason is sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone: every time before, when I dreamed thorns or blood or a face going gray, ringing the bell meant somebody died anyway. The bell doesn't stop anything. The bell just lets the guild write it down ahead of time, neat and tidy, in black books. Once I asked Sister Vess why we bothered writing down deaths we already knew about, and she said, *so the families can be billed for the mourning cloth in advance,* and then she saw my face and said, *it keeps the city orderly, little wren.*

Orderly. Like the city's a cupboard and I'm the one who tells it which jar is going to break.

So tonight I hold the stone in my chest and I don't ring, and I wait for morning, and I wait for the one person I want to tell.

* * *

Dominic comes at midmorning, the way they always do, carrying their ledger and their tiredness like two heavy bags, one in each hand.

Dominic isn't like the others. The collectors look at me and see a tool that talks. Sister Vess looks at me and sees a line item. Master Corvane looks at me and sees — I don't know what he sees, but it makes my skin crawl, like being looked at by a mirror that likes what it sees too much. But Dominic looks at me and sees a kid. I can tell because they flinch when I say true things. Tools don't make people flinch. Kids do.

Today Dominic sits down on the ugly chair — the guild gives visitors ugly chairs on purpose, Hobbs told me once, so they leave faster — and opens their ledger and says, "Inventory day. Tell me what you've got."

That's our game. Officially Dominic is supposed to write down my dreams in the black book. Instead, Dominic writes them in their own ledger, in their own shorthand, and I've peeked enough times to know the shorthand says things like *W. dreamed of rain again, no action needed* when really I dreamed of a funeral with forty umbrellas and nobody crying. Dominic lies to the guild in ink. It might be my favorite thing anyone has ever done.

"I dreamed about thorns," I say.

Dominic's quill stops. Just for a blink. But I watch faces the way other kids watch birds, and I caught it. "Thorns," they repeat. "Garden dreams. Very seasonal."

"Through snow," I say. "Blooming up through snow. Black ones with red tips. And there was a heartbeat under everything, big and slow, and it said soon." I hug my knees. "I've dreamed the thorns before. Three times. Maybe four."

The quill is very still now. "When, exactly?"

"The night before Hobbs's brother took the river road. The night before old Master Ellery fell down his own stairs even though he'd climbed them ten thousand times. And the night before—" I stop, because the third one is the one nobody likes. "The night before the fire in the scribes' wing. The one where the seven copyists died and they told everyone it was a lamp left burning."

Dominic doesn't say anything for a long moment. Ugly chair creaks. Somewhere below us, the guildhouse does its daytime noises — doors, coins, voices being polite at each other.

"You're telling me," Dominic says finally, very carefully, the way people carry something breakable across a slippery floor, "that you dreamed of thorns before each of those deaths."

"Not thorns exactly, the first times. Blood in snow, that first one. Then roots cracking stone, for Master Ellery. Then last year, branches through a window." I count on my fingers because counting helps me be brave. "But it's the same dream wearing different clothes. And every single time, within three days, somebody dies. Somebody big, usually. Important-dead, not ordinary-dead."

"And now thorns through snow, and a heartbeat saying *soon*."

"Yep." I pop the word like a bubble, because if I don't make it small it'll be as big as it actually is. "Also I didn't ring the bell."

Dominic looks up sharply. "Why not?"

Because here's the thing about telling the truth: once you start it's like pulling a thread on a sweater, and I am about to pull the whole sleeve off. "Because last month I dreamed a woman dropping her baby down a well. I rang the bell at dawn. They sent collectors. They watched her street all day. And at dusk she did it anyway, and afterward Master Corvane asked me — he smiled the whole time — he asked me whether the dream changed because we knew, or whether knowing was always part of the dream." My voice goes wobbly and I hate it. "I don't know the answer. I think about it every night. If seeing it is what made it happen, then my bell kills people. And if seeing it didn't matter, then my bell is just — a bill. A bill sent early."

The silence after that is long and terrible and warm, somehow, both at once.

Then Dominic gets up from the ugly chair and crosses the room and sits down next to me on the window seat, close but not touching, the way they always get it exactly right, and says, "Wren. Listen to me. You are not the storm. You're the girl who hears the thunder. Those are different things, and the difference matters, and anyone who tells you otherwise is trying to hand you a weight that belongs to them."

"That's a nice speech."

"It's not a speech, it's a fact. I deal in facts professionally." But their mouth does the thing it does when they're joking to keep from doing something else. "You didn't ring the bell. Good. Who else knows about the dream?"

"Just you." I pick at a loose thread on my sleeve. "Are you going to write it down?"

Dominic looks at their ledger for a long moment. Then they open it to a fresh page, and I watch over their arm as they write, slow and deliberate: *Rain again today. Nothing worth noting.*

"That's a lie," I say.

"That's mercy," Dominic says. "In this building, it's the same work done carefully." They snap the ledger shut. "Thorns through snow. A heartbeat. *Soon.* Don't say any of it to anyone else — not Vess, not Hobbs, especially not Corvane. Can you keep a secret?"

"I keep yours," I point out. "I know about the thing in your coat pocket with the wax seal you keep touching like it's a sore tooth. I've known for six days. You check on it every time you pass a mirror, because mirrors are how you check if anyone's behind you. You think you're sneaky but you're just quiet."

For the second time since I've known them, Dominic Whitlock, keeper of the guild's impossible inventory, looks genuinely frightened. Not of me. For me, maybe. Or for themselves, with me standing in the blast radius. Both, probably. People are usually both.

"Wren," they say slowly, "what exactly do you see when you look at me?"

"Nobody's ever asked me that." Which is true, and it stings worse now that I've said it out loud. Everyone asks what I see about *other* people. Nobody wonders what I see about me. "I see somebody who steals for a living and hates it, and is good at it and hates that too. I see somebody whose name got written somewhere it shouldn't have been, and it scared you so bad you went gray around the mouth." Dominic's hand moves toward their pocket and stops. "See? Sore tooth. And I see—" I hesitate, because this part is new, arrived just this morning along with the taste of snow, "—somebody who's going to have a choice soon. A big one. About giving something back instead of taking. And whichever way you choose, it's going to be the most important thing that happens in this city for a hundred years."

The clock in the corridor strikes eleven, each note landing like a coin on a counter.

"Dominic?" I say, when they still haven't moved. "Your face is doing the gray thing again."

"It does that." They stand up, smooth their coat, and become almost-normal again, except their eyes, which are moving fast behind the calm like somebody reading a page quicker than they'd like. "New rule. From today, when you wake from a dream — any dream — you tell me first. Before the bell, before Vess, before anyone. Me first. Can you do that?"

"Me first," I agree. "What do I get?"

Dominic blinks. "You want payment?"

"Everyone in this building trades. You taught me that. Dreams for secrets, secrets for safety." I shrug, casual as anything, heart hammering. "I want to learn letters. Real ones, not just my name. So I can read the ledgers myself someday. All of them."

Something crosses Dominic's face then that I don't have a word for yet — I'm eight, my word-hoard has holes in it — but it looked like grief and pride having an argument. "Deal," they say. "First lesson tomorrow. Bring your sharpest questions."

"They're all sharp."

"I'd expect nothing less." At the door they pause, hand on the frame, back to me. "Wren. The thorn dreams. If you have another one — tonight, tomorrow, any time — you come find me immediately. Day or night. Even if it's nothing. Especially if it's nothing."

"Why? What happens if it's not nothing?"

Dominic doesn't turn around. "Then I suspect the heartbeat under the snow starts counting days instead of saying *soon*."

They slip out, quiet as their own rumor, and I'm alone with the good wallpaper and the bell I didn't ring.

* * *

That night I dream the thorns again, and this time they're closer, and this time the snow isn't falling — it's rising, drifting upward past the windows of the guildhouse, past the tower, past everything, like the whole world is a snow globe somebody has picked up and shaken and is now holding very, very still.

And under the heartbeat, under the *soon*, there's a new sound. Footsteps. Heavy ones, unhurried, coming up through the floors of the world.

And threaded through it all, faint as smoke, I hear Master Corvane's voice, saying words I don't understand yet but will:

*"Wake him. Wake him, and let winter do the rest."*

I don't ring the bell. I put on my shoes, instead, and go find Dominic in the dark.
