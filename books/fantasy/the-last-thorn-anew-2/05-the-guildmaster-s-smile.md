## 5. The Guildmaster's Smile

There are three ways a keeper of records learns he's about to die.

The first is the obvious one: someone tells him. This almost never happens. The Vellum Guild prefers its endings unannounced, the way it prefers everything—tidy, deniable, filed under miscellaneous.

The second is arithmetic. You count the collectors who don't look at you in the corridor. You notice that your name has been quietly removed from tomorrow's duty roster, or that the ink on your access chits has been re-cut with a fresher seal than the one you signed. Numbers don't lie. That's why I love them and why they terrify me; I have spent my whole career making them lie for other people, and I know exactly how easy it is.

The third way is the worst one. The third way is tea.

Guildmaster Corvane sent for me at half past eleven at night, which was wrong in itself. Corvane kept merchant's hours and slept like a man who had never once owed anyone an apology. A summons at half past eleven meant either catastrophe or ceremony, and in my experience the two were usually the same thing wearing different coats.

The note was written in his own hand. I knew his hand the way you know a scar—slightly too elegant, every letter leaning forward like it was eavesdropping. *Come up, Whitlock. Bring nothing. Tell no one.*

I stood in my cell of a room above the counting floor and did the second kind of arithmetic while I dressed. It was four days since I'd taken the sealed memory off the restricted shelf. Four days since I'd looked through its keyhole of glass and seen what the guild has been keeping warm beneath the city all these years. Four days since I'd hidden it somewhere no collector would think to search, because no collector would believe anyone would be stupid enough to put it there.

Four days was generous. Corvane was not a generous man. If he'd only just noticed, it wasn't because he was slow. It was because he'd wanted to watch what I did with it first.

That thought followed me down the stairs like a debt collector.

* * *

The guildhouse had been a nobleman's palace once, back when noblemen still bothered to live in Grayhollow instead of merely owning it from a distance. Corvane had taken the top floor and remade it into something between a study and a reliquary. Ledgers everywhere, of course—the man worshipped ledgers the way older generations had worshipped saints—but also curiosities: a clockwork orrery with too many planets, a taxidermied owl whose eyes had been replaced with polished obsidian, a sword on the wall that I would have bet my pension had been taken off a corpse.

Corvane himself sat by the fire with a decanter and two glasses, and I want to be honest here, because honesty is a discipline I practice in private: for one full second, seeing him relaxed and golden in the firelight, I thought, *maybe it's fine.* He had that gift. It was the single most dangerous thing about him, more dangerous than his network of collectors or his vaults or his private army of ledger-clerks with knives under their cuffs. Corvane could make you feel, in his presence, that everything was going to be all right, and that he was the only reasonable adult left in the room.

He was sixty-some years old and looked forty, which is the sort of thing people said about him as a compliment. I'd always found it unsettling. Men who age slowly are usually spending something to do it.

"Dominic." He poured before I'd even sat. "Sit. You look terrible."

"I've been working nights."

"You always work nights. You look worse than usual." He handed me the glass. Good amber. The kind we sold by the drop to clients who wanted to remember being happy at somebody's wedding. "Tell me about the inventory."

"It's in the report."

"The verbal report. For me. Personally. Now." He smiled. I catalogued the smile the way I catalogue everything—date, provenance, condition—and noted that it reached his eyes exactly as far as it needed to and not one hair further.

So I gave him the inventory. Bottled grief, three lots. A decade of a cooper's childhood, sold by the man himself to pay his daughter's debts, poor bastard—weeping while he signed, thanking us after. Two years of a widow's marriage, purchased by her son who couldn't bear to watch her grieve and couldn't bear to lose the grief either, so he bought it and left her the love without the mourning. Impossible trades, ordinary Tuesday. I recited figures. Corvane listened with his head tilted, and I understood, distantly, that this was a performance we were both giving, and that mine was the weaker one, and that he knew it, and was letting me finish anyway out of something like courtesy. A cat lets the mouse reach the wall.

"And item," I said, "the restricted shelf reconciled. Nothing outstanding."

"Nothing outstanding," Corvane repeated softly.

Here is where I should tell you what I decided in that moment. I decided to lie. Not well—I'm a good liar, but lying to Corvane was like lying to a mirror—and not comfortably. But there was a version of this evening where I confessed, where I put the sealed memory on his desk and said *what is sleeping under our feet, Guildmaster*, and lived or died on his answer.

I didn't take it. Partly because I'd already seen what happens to people who bring Corvane questions instead of answers—they get filed. And partly, if I'm truthful with myself, which I try to be once a day whether I want to or not, partly because some small stubborn animal in my chest had looked through that glass seal at the thorn-crowned figure beneath the city and made a decision my brain hadn't approved yet.

"Nothing outstanding," I confirmed, and drank my stolen happiness, and waited to see if the trap would close.

Corvane turned his glass in his fingers. The fire cracked. Somewhere below us, the counting floor ticked and rustled with clerks working the late shift, hundreds of lives reduced to entries.

"Remarkable," he said. "Truly. Because my reconciliation says otherwise."

* * *

I want to describe his smile properly, because it matters, because I spent the rest of that night and several nights afterward taking it apart like a lock.

It wasn't a cruel smile. That's what everyone gets wrong about men like Corvane. Cruelty is crude; cruelty shows its teeth. His smile was *delighted*. He looked at me across the fire with the open pleasure of a patron watching a protégé finally perform, and he said:

"Lot ninety-one. Restricted shelf. Sealed memory, provenance withheld, client unnamed, delivery scheduled—" he consulted a card on the arm of his chair that he did not need to consult, "—tomorrow night. And it isn't where the shelf says it is. Hasn't been for four days."

The room got very quiet, or I did. One of those.

"Now," Corvane went on, pleasantly, "there are three explanations. One: a clerical error, which would embarrass us both, because you don't make clerical errors, Dominic, it's why I keep you. Two: theft, which would sadden me, because then I'd have to feed you to the collectors, and I genuinely—" and here he pressed a hand to his chest with such sincerity that I nearly applauded, "—genuinely like you. Or three."

He let the silence sit. He was good at silence. Most powerful men fill it because they're afraid of it. Corvane wore it like a coat.

"Three," I said, because he was going to make me.

"Three," Corvane agreed, "you took it *on purpose*, knowing what it was, having read enough of the deep ledgers to guess what it was—which means you're the only person under this roof besides me who understands what we actually keep down there—and you hid it somewhere clever, and you've spent four days deciding what kind of person you're going to be about it."

My mouth was dry. The amber hadn't helped. "And which explanation do you prefer?"

"Oh, the third, obviously. The first two bore me." He set down his glass and leaned forward, and the firelight did something theatrical with his face, and I realized I was being given a show, that the whole room was staged, the owl, the sword, the two glasses—he'd planned this seating arrangement the way he planned everything, down to the angle of the light. "You want to know the funny part? The genuinely funny part? I've been waiting years for someone to steal it. I'd begun to despair. Do you know how hard it is to find a thief with a conscience anymore? They're extinct. Consciences slow people down. And then along comes my best ledger-keeper, who reads prophecy for fun, who finds his own name in a book a hundred years old—yes, Dominic, I know about the ledger, I've known since the day you found it, you checked it out through the archive and the archive reports to me, everything reports to me—and rather than run, or drink himself stupid, or sell it back to me, he goes down to the restricted shelf and takes the one thing he shouldn't."

"You knew what the prophecy said."

"Of course I knew what the prophecy said. I've read it more times than you have. I read it before you were born." He said it lightly, but the words landed heavy, and I felt the floor tilt a degree, the particular vertigo of learning that your discovery was never a discovery, that you were a line in someone else's ledger long before you ever held a pen. "*On the night the frost comes early, the keeper will take what must be taken,*" Corvane recited, from memory, in the flat tone of a man quoting scripture he'd stopped believing in and started using. "*And the thief shall be the key, for the door knows its own.*" He spread his hands. "You took the memory off that shelf in under an hour, past wards you shouldn't have been able to pass. The door knew its own. I confess I had my doubts about the poetry, but the old books are annoyingly reliable."

"Why?" My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Why let me steal it? Why not just hand it to me and skip the theater?"

"Because it had to be taken, not given. The seals respond to intent. A gifted memory opens like a gift. A stolen one opens like a wound." He shrugged, elegant, horrible. "Also, I wanted to see if you'd run. Thieves who run are useless. Thieves who stay and do their jobs for four days while their heart tries to climb out of their throat—" he raised his glass to me, "—those are rarer than prophecy."

The trap closed, then. Except it didn't feel like a trap. That was the sickening part. It felt like being *seen*. Four days of dread and double-checking my hiding place and rehearsing innocent expressions in front of the washbasin, and Corvane had watched all of it from the top of the house like weather, and now he was offering me a chair by the fire.

"What's in it?" I asked. "The sealed memory. What does the client want delivered?"

Corvane's smile changed. Widened by a fraction. And for the first time that evening, the delight in it touched something underneath that wasn't delight at all, and I understood that everything before had been the performance and this was the man.

"A war," he said.

* * *

He talked for a long time after that. I'll give him this: he was honest, in the way that men are honest when they're certain no one can stop them. He told me about the figure beneath the city—not the king's history, I'd get that later, from ruins and murals and Aldric's ruined face, but the mechanics, the plumbing of the miracle. A hundred years of deliveries. Curated memories, sifted and softened, piped down through the root-vaults like warm milk to keep a god dreaming gently. Grief without the guilt. Love without the loss. The guild fed him beautiful lies so he'd sleep sweetly, and the world stayed unfrozen, and the guild got rich brokering the surplus.

"But dreams curdle," Corvane said, poking the fire. "Even gentle ones. He's starting to stir—you've seen the signs, the cold snap last month, the frost on the inside of windows. He wants something real. And if we don't give him something real, he'll help himself, and what he'll help himself to is everything." He turned to me, earnest as a priest. "A war, Dominic. Fed to him deliberately. Controlled. A war he can taste and savor and turn over in his sleep until it satisfies him for another century. We choose the war. We write it. We give the beast a dream of battle instead of letting it wake hungry. It's mercy, really. It's the kindest possible arithmetic."

"You'd start a war to prevent a waking."

"I'd *write* a war. There's a difference. Borders will move. Some cities will burn. But the world keeps turning, and he keeps sleeping, and the guild keeps the ledger balanced. Whereas the alternative—" he gestured vaguely upward, at the ceiling, at the sky beyond it, at the long winter that had once swallowed a generation, "—whereas the alternative is everyone remembers everything, all at once, forever, in the dark."

And here is the part I'm least proud of, the part I've never said aloud to Aldric or Wren, though I suspect Wren guessed: for a moment—a real moment, a counted, weighed, ledgered moment—I saw the sense in it. I'm good at arithmetic. That's my curse. And his arithmetic was clean. Feed the sleeping monster a nightmare of our choosing, spend ten thousand strangers' suffering to save a million. Every keeper of records meets that equation eventually. Most of us just get better at not looking at it.

What broke the spell wasn't morality. Morality is slow. What broke it was smaller and sharper: I thought of the cooper weeping while he signed away his childhood, thanking us afterward because we'd taught him gratitude was the price of survival. I thought of how Corvane had smiled while explaining it. And I realized the smile was the same one he was wearing now, and that Corvane had probably shown this exact arithmetic to someone else, a century ago, the man who'd first started feeding the king, and that the chain of reasonable men stretched back into the dark, each one certain, each one charming, each one balancing the ledger with other people's lives and calling it mercy.

"So," I said. "Partnership."

"Partnership." He slid a folded paper across the table between us. Terms, drafted in his own leaning-forward hand. My name at the bottom, awaiting signature, the date filled in—tonight's date, the date from the prophecy, the date that had been waiting a hundred years for me like a hook waits for a fish. "You open the delivery. No one else can—the seals took to you. You pour the war-memory down into the dream yourself, and afterward, a seat beside mine. The guild has been a warehouse, Dominic. With you, it becomes a throne."

I picked up the pen. I even dipped it. And I looked at his face—that warm, patient, reasonable face—and asked him the question I actually needed answered, the one that had been sitting in my chest since the restricted shelf:

"When he stirs, Guildmaster. When the dream curdles and he reaches for something real and we've fed him a hundred years of soft lies—whose memories does he take first?"

Corvane didn't miss a beat. That was the terrible thing. He'd already done the math; I could see it behind his eyes, tidy and filed.

"Whoever's closest," he said kindly. "Which is why I need a thief. Which is why the prophecy needs a thief. Someone who can go down there, if it ever comes to that, and take from the vault what's needed. Quickly. Quietly. Before anyone notices anything missing." He tapped the paper. "You're the contingency, Dominic. You've always been the contingency. That's what the door knows its own *means*."

And there it was. The whole shape of it, laid out in firelight: he hadn't summoned me because I'd stolen from him. He'd let me steal because someday soon he'd need someone to steal *for* him—someone the vault trusted, someone expendable, someone whose name was already written in an old book so that his betrayal would come pre-forgiven, pre-ordained, a plot point instead of a crime. The prophecy wasn't a warning. It was a job posting.

I signed.

Let me say that plainly, because the next chapters of my life only make sense if you understand it: I picked up the pen and I signed my name to Corvane's terms, in my own careful keeper's hand, while planning, even as the ink dried, exactly how I was going to rob him blind.

He beamed at me. He shook my hand. His grip was warm and dry and strong, a merchant's grip, a man who'd never broken a bone in his life because other people's bones did the breaking.

"Tomorrow night, then," Corvane said, walking me to the door, pouring a farewell glance of pure paternal pride over his shoulder. "Delivery at midnight. Wear something respectable—it's a coronation of sorts." At the threshold he paused, and his voice dropped into something almost tender. "Oh—and Dominic. Your little knight. Ser Aldric. He recognized the seal on what you took. Old order's mark. He came to me this morning, very troubled, asking questions."

The floor dropped out of my stomach, quiet as snowfall.

"I told him nothing, naturally. I never do." Corvane smiled his smile, all the way to the eyes this time, which was somehow far worse. "But a partner likes to know where all his pieces are standing. Do watch that one. Sentimental men make excellent thieves and dreadful allies."

I walked down through the sleeping guildhouse with his handshake still on my skin and his arithmetic still crawling in my ears, and I did the only arithmetic that mattered now, fast, in the dark: one knight, possibly turned; one stolen memory, hidden in a place I suddenly needed to move it from; one child who dreamed of thorns in the snow; and one midnight delivery, less than a day away, that would either drown a sleeping king in a manufactured war or start the thaw of everything.

The prophecy had named me because Corvane needed a thief.

Fine. Then the prophecy was about to learn what thieves are actually for.
