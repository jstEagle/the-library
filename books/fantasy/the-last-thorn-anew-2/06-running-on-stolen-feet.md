## 6. Running on Stolen Feet

I have been chased before. I want that on the record, in a fair hand, witnessed by two reputable liars: Ser Aldric Vane has been chased before. Chased by debt collectors in three cities, by the husbands of women whose names I never learned properly, by a dog in Marrowgate that held a grudge with what I can only describe as theological conviction. Being chased is a profession's hazard when your profession is leaving places quickly.

But I had never been chased by my own face before.

That was the trouble with the collectors Corvane sent after us. They did not carry portraits. They carried memories — Dominic's memories, or rather the guild's copies of strangers' memories of Dominic, bottled and decanted into hired heads until half the undercity could pick that thief's profile out of a crowd. I watched it happen at the Saltmarket steps, not an hour after we fled the guildhouse: a collector pressed two fingers to a fishmonger's temple, and the woman blinked twice and then turned and pointed directly at Dominic as though her own mother had told her where to look.

"That," said Wren, "is going to be a problem."

Wren says things like that. Calmly. The way other children say *it's raining*.

"Yes," said Dominic. "It is."

"Also there are six more behind us."

There were.

* * *

The undercity beneath Vellum Street was built by masons who believed in over-engineering and by smugglers who believed in their improvement. Aqueducts became culverts; culverts became cellars; cellars became streets with no name above ground, lit by lamps that burned on rendered tallow and the occasional desperate prayer. It smelled of wet stone and old fat and, faintly, everywhere, of the river that had been asked to forget too much.

We ran through it in the manner of people who had no plan but had, crucially, momentum.

Dominic ran like a ledger being carried off a burning shelf — precise, economical, every footfall accounted for. I ran like a soldier, which is to say badly for long distances and adequately for short ones, and I was discovering that tonight was composed entirely of short ones. And Wren ran like water finding its level, slipping between crates and knees and elbows as though the crowd had agreed in advance to make room for her, which, knowing Wren, it may have.

"They'll cut us off at the Tannery Gate," Dominic said, breathless, not slowing. "Collectors work in pairs. One drives, one waits."

"You know this how?"

"I wrote their rotation schedules for eleven years."

Of course they had. Of course the guild's memory-thief had also been its bookkeeper, because apparently one crime per person was considered greedy at Vellum House. Eleven years of schedules sat in Dominic's head like scripture, and scripture, I have learned, is just a map of where the bodies are buried, written by the burial committee.

"Tannery Gate has a grate," I said. "Rusted shut since — "

"Since you checked it last year while drunk," said Dominic, "on guild coin, which I invoiced, which means I know about it, which means it's our best route. Congratulations, knight. Your disgrace is finally useful."

I have been called many things in my life. *Useful* was new. I found, to my considerable irritation, that I liked it.

* * *

We made the gate. The grate was rusted exactly as thoroughly as my hangover remembered, and Dominic got it open in forty seconds with a pry bar stolen from a wall bracket, muttering the whole time about maintenance budgets, which is the sort of thing a person says when fear has worn grooves so deep in them that even terror comes out organized.

Beyond the grate the tunnels narrowed, then widened without warning into a cistern vast enough that our footsteps came back to us late, like witnesses who'd taken bribes. The dark down there was total except for Wren, who had insisted on carrying a candle stub she'd lifted from somewhere, and who now held it up and squinted into the black water below the walkway as if reading something.

"There's a boat," she said.

"There is not a boat," I said.

"There's a boat. Under the ledge. Left side." She tilted her head. "Somebody hid it. Somebody who was scared." A pause. "They're dead now. But the boat's still good."

I looked at Dominic. Dominic looked at me. Somewhere in that shared glance was the entire negotiation of the next ten years: *do we believe the eight-year-old, or do we stand here in the dark being reasonable?*

We took the boat. There was, in fact, a boat. It floated. I am not going to explain how Wren knew. I have stopped asking, mostly because she answers, and the answers are worse than the questions.

We poled out onto the black water, and for the first time since the guildhouse doors slammed behind us, nothing was chasing us audibly.

"Well," I said. "That went well."

"You're joking," said Dominic.

"I'm establishing morale. Someone has to. You've spent the whole flight cataloguing our failures in alphabetical order, and the child has predicted four separate deaths, and I've realized I left my cloak in a tavern I can no longer afford to drink in. Morale, keeper. It doesn't keep itself."

Dominic stared at me across the boat with the expression of a person reevaluating every decision that led to sharing a vessel with me. Then — and I would swear this on any relic you care to name, false ones included — the corner of their mouth moved.

It was not quite a smile. It was the memory of a smile, filed under *pending*, awaiting further review. But it was aimed at me, and it was the first honest thing I'd seen on that face since I'd known them, and I thought: *there you are.*

Then the candle went out, because of course it did, and we spent the next hour poling blind through a sewer while Wren narrated hazards from memory she hadn't earned yet.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. First there was laughter. Let me tell you about the laughter, because everything after it was bought with it.

* * *

It happened in the narrows, where the cistern feeds into the older tunnels — pre-imperial brickwork, arched like ribs, the kind of passage where your voice comes back wrong. We'd grounded the boat against a collapsed stair and were walking again, single file, hands on the damp walls, when Wren tripped over what she announced was "a root but probably a skeleton" and grabbed my arm, and I, in the reflexive gallantry of a man who once trained for twenty years to be decorative, caught her, overcorrected, and put my boot directly through the last intact plank on the walkway.

The plank gave. I did not fall, precisely. I performed a controlled descent, which is what knights call falling when there are witnesses.

And Wren laughed.

Not a small laugh. Not a child's polite giggle. A full-bodied, honking, graceless laugh, the laugh of someone who has perhaps never had cause to use the equipment before and is delighted to find it works. She laughed so hard she had to sit down on the wet stone, and Dominic, who up to that moment I believe had been physically incapable of producing sound unrelated to complaint, made a noise — a snort, really, quickly strangled, but a snort — and then Wren laughed harder because Dominic had snorted, and I stood there in the dark with one leg through a floor, watching two fugitives come apart with laughter in a sewer, and I felt something crack in my chest that had been sealed since the chapterhouse burned.

I laughed too. God help me, I laughed until my ribs ached, sitting in filth with a hole in the world under me and hunters above us wearing my companion's face, and it was the best minute of my life in fifteen years.

"We're going to die down here," Dominic said finally, wiping their eyes, "and our last act will be laughing at Aldric falling in a sewer."

"Falling with dignity," I corrected.

"You screamed."

"A battle cry."

"It was very high."

"It startled the darkness, keeper. That's tactical."

Wren wiped her eyes and looked at the two of us with an expression I didn't understand at the time. I understand it now. It was the look of a child taking inventory — the same instinct Dominic had, the same ledgers, kept in a different hand. She was counting us. Deciding, perhaps, whether we were worth the tomorrows she spent so freely.

Apparently we passed. She reached up, took one of my hands and one of Dominic's, and walked between us like that for a hundred yards, swinging our arms, humming something tuneless, as though the undercity were a market street and the collectors a rumor.

Neither of us let go. I noticed that neither of us let go.

* * *

We surfaced near the old fulling yards, past midnight, in a district where the guild's writ ran thin because the wool-dust rots memory faster than the collectors can harvest it. Even the lamplight seemed forgetful there. It was as good a place as any to stop running, and better than most, because stopping required believing we could.

Dominic crouched against a wall, doing what they always did: writing. A scrap of paper, a nub of charcoal, mapping our route in that cramped beautiful hand, annotating collector positions from eleven years of schedules, building us an escape out of pure record-keeping.

"They'll widen the search by dawn," they said. "Corvane won't send collectors after us forever, though. He'll send something else once he realizes collectors are chasing a thief who knows all their routes."

"What's worse than collectors?"

"Me," Dominic said simply. "He'll send someone with my methods. That's what I'd do." They looked up, and the lamplight caught the exhaustion under their eyes, and for a moment the wry ledger-keeper vanished and I saw what lived underneath: a person who had read their own name in a death-book and run anyway, because running was the only prayer they knew. "Aldric. Why are you helping me? Truly. You owe me nothing. You owed the guild less."

I considered lying. I'm good at it; it was my order's second language.

"The seal on your stolen memory," I said instead. "The thorn-and-crown. That was ours. The Order of the Briar. I knelt before that seal when I was nine years old and swore a vow I've spent thirty years pretending I never heard." My voice did something unsteady, and I let it, because Wren was watching and I wanted her to see a man tell the truth at least once in her vicinity. "Whatever the guild stole from my order, whatever that seal opens — I need to be there when it's answered. Not for justice. Justice is for courts. For truth. Courts couldn't give me that when they stripped my spurs, and they can't give it to me now."

Wren nodded gravely, as though I'd said something wise, which is either touching or alarming depending on the day.

"And you?" I asked Dominic. "Why run? You could have given the memory back. Corvane might have forgiven — "

"No," Dominic said. Flat. Immediate. "No, he wouldn't have, and more importantly he wouldn't have needed to. He wants me compliant, not forgiven. There's a difference, and the difference is a cage." They folded the charcoal sketch away. "Besides. I've kept everyone else's secrets for eleven years. Time somebody kept mine."

"Which is?"

Dominic smiled — the real one this time, brief and crooked and gone.

"That I don't know yet what kind of person I am when I'm not property. Ask me at dawn."

* * *

Dawn, when it came, was the color of dirty pewter, seeping down through the grates of the fulling yard like something poured. We had slept in shifts — Wren curled against my cloak-less side, Dominic sitting upright with the stolen memory clutched to their chest like a coal they were afraid would go out.

I woke to Dominic shaking my shoulder, and their face told me everything before their mouth caught up.

"They found the boat," they whispered. "And there's a boy on the wall above us. Twelve, maybe. Collector-touched — his pupils are blown wide, he's holding a borrowed face in his head right now." Dominic swallowed. "It's my face, Aldric. A child up there is looking for my face, and he's about to look down here."

Above us, small boots scraped on the yard wall.

Wren opened her eyes. She looked at the wall, then at Dominic, then at me, and she said, with the terrible calm I was already learning to dread:

"He hasn't seen us yet. But someone will. Before midday." Her small fingers closed around Dominic's sleeve. "Unless we're somewhere the snow can't follow."

I looked up. Through the yard's broken roof, past the pewter light, the morning sky hung gray and heavy and wrong — too cold for the season, too still, like a held breath.

Somewhere beneath our feet, something ancient rolled over in its sleep.

And the first flakes began to fall.
