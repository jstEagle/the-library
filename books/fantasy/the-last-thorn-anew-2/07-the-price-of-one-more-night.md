## 7. The Price of One More Night

The hospice smelled of candle smoke and old soup, which Wren decided was better than the guildhouse, which smelled of ink and lies. Everything in the world smelled like something, if you paid attention. That was one of the few things she liked about her gift — she'd already smelled all these candles before she lit them.

She sat cross-legged on a cot in a room meant for dying people, which felt rude, so she whispered an apology to the empty bed across from her. The woman who'd occupied it had gone somewhere else this morning, and nobody would tell Wren where. Grown-ups did that. They told you everything was fine in voices that had holes in them.

Dominic was pacing again. Wren had learned to count their steps. Eleven paces to the shuttered window, eleven back. Aldric sat against the wall with his sword across his knees like it was a cat he didn't trust, and every time Dominic turned at the end of a lap, Aldric's eyes tracked them with the tired patience of a man watching weather.

"They'll check the hospices," Dominic said for the fourth time. "It's what I would do. It's exactly what I would do."

"Then we should be flattered," Aldric said. "You have excellent taste in hiding places."

"It's not funny."

"I wasn't laughing." But the corner of his mouth did something, just slightly, and Wren saw it because she always saw things a half-second before they happened, and she'd seen that mouth-twitch coming since supper.

Wren pulled her knees up to her chin. She knew something the other two didn't know yet, and knowing things before other people knew them was usually the worst part of being her. Usually she could keep quiet. Keeping quiet was a skill, like whistling through grass or pretending to sleep when Sister Hallow came down the dormitory row with her lantern. But this thing kept pressing at her teeth like a word trying to get out.

Tomorrow morning, three collectors were going to find them here.

She'd dreamed it twice. Once as a flash while washing her face — water in the basin going suddenly still, showing her a door opening and boots and a voice saying *check the beds*. And once properly, in sleep: thorns on snow again, but closer now, thorns growing up between floorboards, and above her three shadows wearing guild grey, and one of them carrying a ledger with her name in it.

*Wren. Age eight. Asset. Recoverable.*

That last word she'd read right off the collector's clipboard in the dream, which was cheating, probably, but dreams didn't have rules, that was the whole point of them.

"Dominic," she said.

Eleven paces stopped mid-stride. "Mm."

"The collectors come tomorrow. Morning. Three of them. One has a list and my name is on it under 'asset, recoverable.'"

The silence that followed had a shape. Wren felt it press against her ears like deep water.

"How do you know?" Aldric asked, very carefully, the way you ask someone if they've touched a hot stove.

"Because I know." She unfolded herself and stood on the cot so she could look at both of them at once, which was the only way grown people listened properly — from slightly above. "You always ask how I know and then I say because I know and then you make faces. So this time just believe me first and make your faces after."

Dominic's face made itself. It was the face they made when they were doing arithmetic they hated. Wren had watched that face enough times now to read it: *three of us, two exits, one child, no coin, no allies, and the whole city learning our features by heart.*

"There's a back stair," Dominic said slowly. "Behind the laundry. It goes down to the canal gates."

"It's watched," Wren said. "Not tomorrow morning. Tomorrow afternoon, by a fat man eating an apple. But there's another way." And here was the pressing word, finally out past her teeth. "Sister Verity owes me."

Aldric frowned. "The matron?"

"She doesn't owe me *yet*." Wren hopped off the cot. "But she will tonight, and then she'll walk us out through the dead-room door at first light, and the collectors won't think to look there because nobody visits the dead on purpose."

"That's grim," said Dominic.

"It's true, though. Grim and true hold hands a lot."

* * *

Sister Verity ran the hospice the way a lighthouse runs a rocky coast — steadily, without affection, and absolutely refusing to let anyone crash. She was sixty if she was a day, with hands like tree bark and eyes that had catalogued every kind of crying there was and found most of it repetitive.

She listened to Dominic explain — badly, with too many words — that they needed passage out at dawn and couldn't pay, and she looked at each of them over her spectacles like a woman counting spoons.

"And why would I risk my house for runaways?" she asked.

"Because you're kind," said Dominic.

"I'm not kind. Kindness is expensive. I'm *dutiful*, which is cheaper." She turned to Wren. "And you, little prophet. What do you say? You've been staring at me for ten minutes like I'm a sum you're working out."

Wren took a breath. This was the part she'd seen coming since the washbasin, and she'd rehearsed it lying awake while Dominic paced, and she still hadn't found a version that didn't make her chest hurt.

"I can show you something," she said. "Something that hasn't happened yet. Something about your brother."

Verity went very still. Tree-bark hands went flat on the table.

"Tomas died eleven years ago," she said.

"I know. I'm sorry. But he didn't die alone in the snow on the Kettle Road like everyone thinks. He made it to the shepherd's hut at Harrow Fell. He got a fire going. He wrote your name in the frost on the window because he wanted the last thing he did to be calling for you." Wren's voice wobbled and she steadied it, because this part mattered. "He wasn't scared, at the end. He was warm for a little while, and thinking of you."

The silence stretched so long that Wren heard the candle flame eat its wick. Then Verity sat down, slowly, like her legs had been unplugged.

"How could you possibly—"

"Because I remember futures. Not pasts. Futures." Wren swallowed. "I haven't seen it yet. Not all of it. I've only seen the edge of it. But I can go deeper, and when I do, it'll be true — truer than anything anyone ever told you, because you'll *have* it. You'll have him writing your name. You'll carry it instead of the not-knowing."

Verity's eyes were bright and terrible. "What does it cost you, child?"

Here it was. The pressing word. The one she'd bitten down on all night.

"A year," Wren said. "One year of my tomorrows. When I pull a future out whole and hand it over, I don't get to live inside it anymore. It becomes yours. A year of me — gone before I get there. I'll wake up eight and skip straight to nine, more or less, and there'll be a hole shaped like a year where I should have been." She shrugged, aiming for casual and hearing the miss. "Everybody skips bits of childhood. Mine are just organized."

"No," said Dominic.

They said it fast, too fast, like catching a dropped glass. Wren had known they would. She'd seen this conversation too, three different ways, and in two of them Dominic said no and in one of them Dominic said nothing at all, which was worse.

"You don't get a vote," Wren told them. "It's mine to spend."

"You're *eight*."

"I'm eight and I'm the only one of us who can see tomorrow, so maybe that makes me the treasurer." She crossed her arms. "You spent things too. In the vault. You told me. You took memories out of people's heads and put them in bottles and sold them. Everyone in the guild spends somebody. I'm just spending *me*, which makes me the only honest merchant in the building."

"That's different—"

"It's exactly the same except kinder." She softened, because Dominic's face had gone somewhere bad, somewhere gray. "Hey. Look at me. I'm not breaking. I'm buying something. There's a difference, even if it doesn't feel like it. You buy bread, the bread's gone, but you're not hungry. That's not a wound. That's a trade."

Dominic opened their mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. "What do you even buy with a year?"

"Three things." Wren held up fingers, counting, because lists helped. "One: Sister Verity walks us out the dead-room door at dawn, past the collectors, and swears the hospice never saw us. Two: she gives us dry cloaks and a name of a ferryman downstream who hates the guild. Three—" and here her own voice went small, "—she forgets she ever met us after a week, because that's part of what I'm taking. The memory of us. So when they question her, there's nothing to find. We were never here. She keeps Tomas. We stop existing to her. Fair price."

"That is not fair," Dominic said hoarsely. "None of that is fair. You're a child."

"I'm a child with a *ledger*," Wren said, "and I balance it myself, thank you."

* * *

They did it that night, in the chapel annex, because Verity wanted it done while her courage was warm.

Aldric stood watch at the door, sword drawn out of habit, though Wren had already seen that no one came. Dominic sat in the corner with their head in their hands, radiating a fury so quiet it was almost polite.

Wren knelt on the stone floor across from Verity and took the old woman's bark-rough hands in hers.

"It might tickle," she warned. "Or it might feel like falling asleep in the sun. Nobody's ever told me. I've never given a big one away before. Only little bits, to the guild, when they tested me."

"What do I do?" Verity asked.

"Just hold on and don't let go, even if you see something sad. Especially if you see something sad."

Wren closed her eyes and went down into the future the way you dive into cold water — all at once, because hesitating at the edge only makes it colder.

There. The Kettle Road. Snow like wet wool. A man with a familiar jaw walking into the white, stubborn, cursing the weather with a vocabulary he'd have apologized for in front of his sister. The hut at Harrow Fell. The matches, damp, three of them, the third catching. Firelight on frost. A finger tracing letters in the ice of the window pane. *V-E-R-I-T-Y.* His breath fogging and clearing, fogging and clearing, slower each time, and his face — his face was not afraid. His face was thinking of a girl who used to steal plums off the high shelf and blame the cat.

Wren gathered it up — all of it, the road and the hut and the fire and the finger and the love underneath like a banked coal — and she gathered everything around it, a full year of her own unwritten days, mornings she would never taste, a loose tooth due to fall out in spring, a summer she'd have spent learning to whistle, rain on a roof somewhere she'd never sleep — and she pressed it all together until it had weight, until it was a single smooth stone of *time*, and she set it into Verity's hands the way you set a bird onto open air.

It cost exactly what she'd said it would. She felt the year leave her the way you feel a tooth leave your gum — a strange lightness, a gap the tongue keeps returning to.

When she opened her eyes, Verity was weeping without any noise at all, tears running into the seams of her face, holding her hands against her chest like they held something breakable. Which they did.

"He was warm," the old woman whispered. "He was warm, and he wrote my name."

"Yes," Wren said. Her own voice sounded far away. The chapel seemed very large and slightly tilted, and there was a hole in her now, year-shaped, and she probed it gently the way you probe a missing tooth. It didn't hurt. It just echoed.

"You marvelous, foolish child," Verity said, and crushed her into a hug that smelled of candle smoke and old soup, and Wren decided it was worth it, mostly, except for the loose tooth. She'd been looking forward to the loose tooth.

* * *

Dominic didn't speak to her until they were alone in the laundry room afterward, folding the dry cloaks Verity had left, and when they finally spoke, it came out ragged.

"You should have told me first."

"I did tell you first. Right before."

"*Before deciding.* You're eight. You don't get to make trades like this alone. That's — that's what I do, Wren, I weigh costs, I keep ledgers, that's my whole cursed life, and you just — you just handed over a year of yourself like it was pocket change—"

"It wasn't pocket change." Wren climbed onto the folding table so she could sit level with them, feet swinging, because arguing worked better eye-to-eye and she refused to argue upward. "It was a whole year and I knew what it was and I chose it anyway. That's what makes it different from the guild. The guild takes. I *spent*. Do you see? If you take something from someone, they're smaller after. If you spend something, you bought something. I bought us one more night. Maybe two. I bought Verity her brother back. I bought us out the dead-room door." She shrugged. "Survival's worth its price. That's just arithmetic."

"Arithmetic." Dominic laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle. "You sound like me. That's horrifying. You're supposed to sound like a child."

"I am a child. A child who's watched you do sums for a week." She reached out and patted their arm, twice, firmly, the way you pat a horse you mean to calm. "You're angry because you can't pay my debt for me. I know. But some prices only come in one currency, and mine's the only mint that makes it."

Dominic stared at her for a long moment. Their eyes were shiny in the lamplight, and Wren pretended not to notice, because noticing out loud would have embarrassed them both.

"When this is over," Dominic said finally, "when the king is dealt with and Corvane is dealt with and the guild is dealt with — I'm going to find a way to give it back. Every day. I swear it on the new ledger I'm going to write someday."

"You can't promise tomorrows. You're not a prophet."

"No," Dominic said. "But I'm a thief. And I've never met a lock I liked."

Despite everything — the hole in her, the tilted chapel-feeling, the tooth she'd never wiggle — Wren grinned.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin, and Sister Verity walked them through the cold room where the dead lay under sheets, out a door that opened on the canal, and the three of them slipped into the mist like threads pulled loose from cloth.

At the gate, Verity caught Wren's sleeve one last time. Her eyes were red-rimmed and steady.

"One year," she said. "I'll find a way to repay a year."

"You already did," Wren said. "You carried us through."

Then they were moving along the towpath, three small figures in the early fog, and behind them the hospice bell tolled once for someone newly dead, and ahead of them the river waited, and somewhere downstream a ferryman who hated the guild was waking to a morning he didn't yet know would change everything.

Because Wren had seen him too — in the flash of the trade, in the edges of the year she'd given away. A broad man with a scarred barge and a hatred of the Vellum Guild carved deep as a figurehead, and beside him, sitting on a crate with her ankles crossed, a woman in traveling leathers whom Wren recognized from a hundred of the guild's delivery manifests.

*Maren,* the future whispered. *Collector. Carrier of stolen grief. She turns.*

Wren smiled into the mist, at the hole in her and the road ahead, and said nothing yet.

Some tomorrows you spend. Some you save.

This one, she thought, might be worth keeping.
