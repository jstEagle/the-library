## 8. Where the Old War Sleeps

I had sworn never to go back to Greyhollow. Most men swear such things about a woman or a debt. I swore it about a building.

The chapterhouse of the Order of the Winter Vigil sat three days' ride north of the city, up a road the maps had quietly stopped naming, as if cartography itself had agreed to forget us. We went on foot, because horses attract eyes and eyes attract collectors, and Corvane's collectors were already sniffing after us like hounds that had been given our scent in the form of stolen memories — Dominic's own face, sold back against them, which is a cruelty I have decided not to think about too closely lest it keep me up at night for the rest of what remains of my life.

Three days of walking with an ex-thief who talked when nervous, a child who dreamed true, and me, who had nothing left to be but honest, which was the most uncomfortable condition I had ever occupied.

"You're quiet," Dominic said on the second evening, as we made camp in the shell of a shepherd's hut. "Quieter than usual, I mean. You've got a specific quiet going. I inventory quiets. Occupational hazard."

"It's the chapterhouse," I said.

"What about it?"

"I burned it."

Dominic looked at me over the little fire we'd allowed ourselves, one flame's worth, no more. Wren was asleep against my cloak, which she had claimed on the first night without negotiation, in the manner of eight-year-olds everywhere who understand instinctively that large sad men exist primarily to be slept upon.

"Burned it," Dominic repeated. "Your own order's house."

"Officially, heretics burned it. Unofficially—" I turned my hands toward the fire. They are not good hands. They never were. "Unofficially, I carried the lantern."

Wren stirred but did not wake. Small mercies.

* * *

Let me tell you about the Order of the Winter Vigil, since no one else will, since we paid good money to be struck from every record, which is a strange purchase for an order whose entire purpose was remembering.

We were founded nine hundred years ago to guard a truth so dangerous that our founding charter does not name it. It refers only to *the Patient Below* and instructs each generation of knights to ensure, by whatever means necessary, that He continues to sleep. That was the whole of our sacred duty. Not slaying dragons, not championing widows — though we did some of that, for cover, and I was rather good at it — but standing watch over a sleeping king whom the world believed dead, and making certain he stayed that way.

You will ask: why not kill him? Every novice asked. I asked, kneeling in the cold of the novitiate, all nineteen years of me bristling with righteousness. And old Ser Garett, who trained me, put his hand on my shoulder and said the sentence that would eventually ruin everything: *Because, boy, you cannot kill a man who has agreed to hold your worst day for you.*

I didn't understand then. By the time I understood, I was holding the lantern.

* * *

Greyhollow announced itself the way grief announces itself: gradually, then all at once. First a broken boundary stone bearing our sigil — a thorn circled by snow — then the orchards, gone wild and sour, then the walls themselves rising out of the hillside like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down to die and been permitted to finish.

"Oh," Wren said softly.

She had never seen it. She'd been born forty years after the burning. But children who remember futures sometimes remember pasts they were never in, and she stood before those blackened gates with the expression of someone greeting a relative at a funeral — recognition and dread sharing the same face.

"You knew this place," she said to me. It wasn't a question. With Wren, questions are mostly decorative.

"I was knighted here."

"Did it hurt? When it burned?"

"Yes."

She nodded, satisfied, and walked through the gate ahead of us, because children have no sense of haunted ground and every sense of adventure, and God help the ghost that tried to come between Wren and a thing to climb on.

Dominic fell into step beside me. My ex-thief — I say *ex* generously; once a ledger-keeper, always a ledger-keeper — had gone quiet in the way I was learning to read. The professional quiet. The cataloguing quiet.

"The vaults under the guildhouse hummed," they said. "Memories do that, when there's enough of them. Did you know that? A hundred lives stored together make a sound like bees. A thousand, like weather." A pause. "This place doesn't hum. This place has been *empty* a long time."

"That was the point of the burning," I said. "Everything portable was removed or destroyed. Everything that couldn't be destroyed—"

"—was painted," Wren called back from inside the dark of the gatehouse, her voice echoing oddly. "Aldric. There are pictures down here. Lots of pictures. Come see what your friends did."

And there it was. The reason I had sworn never to return.

* * *

The great hall had lost its roof, so daylight fell through the ribs of charred beams in long grey columns, lighting dust motes that drifted like slow snow. Along the walls, where fire had spared them out of what I can only assume was irony, ran the murals of the Winter Vigil. Nine hundred years of them. Painted by brothers whose names were struck from record even as their brushwork survived. Fire eats wood and paper and flesh. It is strangely respectful of pigment.

I had walked this hall a thousand times. I had memorized these images as a novice, recited their meaning at matins. But I had never looked at them the way I looked at them now — standing beside a thief who had seen the inside of the guild's deepest lie and a child who dreamed in prophecy — and I found, to my considerable horror, that I had spent thirty years *looking without seeing*, which is the specialty of every institution I have ever served.

The murals began, as they always begin, with winter.

Not a picturesque winter. Not the gentle snowfall of festival cards. The mural showed a world encased — rivers frozen to their beds, trees split by cold, a village of tiny figures huddled around a fire no larger than a coin. Above them, rendered in whites and blues so pale the figures seemed painted in exhaustion itself, stretched a sky that went on forever. The Long Winter. Two hundred years of it, according to the histories everyone agrees on and no one remembers correctly.

Beside it, the second panel: the king.

He was painted young. That detail struck me now as it never had before — I had grown up with the Thorn King rendered as a monster, thorns and shadow, the bogeyman of a hundred cautionary tales. But the brothers who painted this wall, closer to the truth in time and perhaps in sympathy, had shown a young man in a crown of living briar, kneeling in the snow among the dying villagers. His hands were open. Empty. Held out.

"He looks sad," Wren said. She had gone still, which for Wren was remarkable enough that I noticed it over my own dread. "Why does he look sad? Monsters aren't sad."

"Continue reading," I said, because my voice wasn't working properly and needed a moment.

The third panel. The bargain.

Here was the image at the heart of everything — the image the Order existed to guard, the image I had helped bury under ash and official history. The young king stood at the center of the panel, arms spread, and from the four corners of the painting came the people. Farmers, queens, soldiers, children, lepers, priests. Each one reached toward him, and where they touched him, thin lines of gold flowed from their heads into his crown of thorns. The painter had labeled each stream, in script small enough to require a novice's patience: *grief*. *rage*. *shame*. *the memory of the drowned son*. *the night of the fire*. *what was done at Harrow Ford*. *guilt*. *guilt*. *guilt*.

"He took them," Dominic said. Their voice had gone very flat, the way it goes when the ledger-keeper in them is trying to do arithmetic faster than the person in them can grieve. "He didn't steal them. Look — they're *giving*. Nobody's chained. There's no coercion in the composition. It's a voluntary transfer. He offered, and they accepted, and—"

"And he ended the winter," I finished. "That's the fourth panel. Look."

The fourth panel showed spring. Actual, botanical, unambiguous spring — blossoms, green fields, a sun with a human face (the brothers had a weakness for anthropomorphizing celestial bodies; we all have our heresies). The villagers danced. The king stood at the edge of the celebration, apart, heavier now, his thorned crown grown dense and dark with all he carried, watching the happiness he had purchased with his own interior life.

"A mercy," Dominic whispered. "It was a mercy. He took every painful memory in the realm so people could bear to live again, and it worked, and the winter broke, and—"

"And he never gave them back," I said. "Fifth panel."

The fifth panel was the one the Church expunged, the one that made the Order heretical, the one I burned this hall to bury. It showed the same village, generations later, flourishing and green. In its center stood the king — unchanged, still young, still crowned — and he was holding out his hands, offering to return what he held. The streams of gold now flowed the other direction. And the people — the painter had made this unmissable, had made it the visual thesis of the entire wall — the people were turning away. Covering their faces. Parents pulling children behind their skirts. One figure had raised a stone.

Below the mural, in the brothers' careful hand, a single line of text:

*He asked to set down his burden. We would not take it back, for to feel our griefs again was worse than to let another carry them forever. Thus we made him monstrous, for a monster may be feared, but a benefactor must be thanked, and we could not bear to thank him.*

The hall was very quiet. Dust drifted through the light like the snow in the first panel.

"That's not right," Wren said finally, with the absolute moral authority of an eight-year-old confronting injustice. "That's *mean*. He helped them."

"He helped them," I agreed.

"So why's he the bad guy?"

"Because," said Dominic, and there was something terrible in how gently they said it, "if he's not the bad guy, then we're the ones who owe him. And nobody wants to owe the man who's been carrying their worst day for nine hundred years. Easier to call him a monster. Monsters don't invoice."

I looked at my ex-thief — at the keeper of ledgers who understood, better than any knight of my order ever had, that debts are the most dangerous things in the world precisely because everyone owes some and no one wishes to pay.

* * *

We walked the rest of the hall slowly. Panels six through eleven told the rest: the growing weight of the king, the darkness thickening in his crown, the first tremors of his sleep. Panel ten showed the founding of the Vellum Guild — this was the detail that made Dominic sit down abruptly on a fallen beam, all the color leaving their face — showing robed figures carrying sealed vessels into the earth, feeding the sleeper curated dreams, chosen memories, pleasant ones, gentle ones, *anesthesia*.

"They weren't preserving him," Dominic said. "They were keeping him sedated. A hundred years of carefully selected dreaming so he wouldn't wake up and remember what he's holding. So he wouldn't remember that we—" They stopped. Started again. "So the realm wouldn't have to answer the question in panel five."

"And Corvane means to wake him," I said. "With war. With the worst memories of all. Feed a sleeping king a banquet of slaughter and see what rises."

Wren had stopped before the final panel. The twelfth. It was the newest — the paint still held a depth the others had faded from — and I remembered, with a lurch, that it had been commissioned the year I took my vows. Painted by old Ser Garett himself, who was a better knight than a painter, which is saying something unkind about both.

It showed the future. Or a future. The king awake, crowned in thorns gone black as iron, standing over a city in snow while thorns bloomed through cobbles and doorways and the mouths of statues. Beneath it, Garett had written the Vigil's final charge, the one we were founded on and failed:

*A king who holds every grief will one day wish to share it. Guard his sleep, or guard your children. You will not be given time to do both.*

"I've seen this," Wren said. Her voice was very small. "Not the painting. The *thing*. Thorns through snow. Through doors. I dream it, and then—" She looked up at me, and her eyes were far too old for her face, and I would have taken every wound of my disgrace doubled over just to give her back one year of being simply eight. "Someone dies, Aldric. After I dream it, someone dies. I dreamed it two nights ago. Before the snow started."

The snow. The false winter already beginning in the city behind us, falling out of season because somewhere beneath the streets, Corvane was pouring war into a sleeping god's ear.

Dominic rose from the beam. When they spoke, the wry ledger-keeper's voice was gone, replaced by something harder and cleaner that I suspected very few people had ever heard.

"Then we have less time than I thought. Aldric — your order guarded this truth for nine hundred years. Tell me you kept more than paintings. Tell me there's a record. The real one. What he carries, where it's weakest, what wakes him, what lets him *rest*."

I looked at the mural of the king holding out his hands to people who refused them. Then I looked at the thief and the child — the two people the prophecy had tangled into my ruin, or my redemption; the record, as Dominic might say, had been kept before either of us was consulted.

"There's a crypt beneath the reliquary," I said. "My master sealed it the night before the burning. He gave me the key and told me that if I ever came back here, it would mean the world had run out of time." I drew the iron key from around my neck, where it had hung for thirty years, warm against my chest through every cold night I pretended it meant nothing. "I had hoped, frankly, never to find out if he was right."

Behind the reliquary altar, behind stone I had sworn an oath to leave shut, something waited that my order had died rather than let the world read.

I turned the key.
