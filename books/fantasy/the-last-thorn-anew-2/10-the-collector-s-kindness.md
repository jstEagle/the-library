## 10. The Collector's Kindness

My name is Maren Voss, and I have spent nineteen years taking things that don't belong to me from people who can't afford to lose them. The Guild calls this "collection." I call it what it is on the days when I'm honest with myself, which are fewer than they used to be.

Tonight was supposed to be simple. Corvane's writ had come down at dusk — three names, three faces, three bounties payable in memories of the collector's choosing. A thief named Whitlock. A disgraced knight whose name I didn't need to look up because everyone in this city knew it by heart by now. And a child.

A *child*.

I'd told myself a great many comfortable things over nineteen years. That the people we harvested had signed willingly. That memory was surplus, like hair or fingernails, and grew back. That grief was better stored in a vault than carried in a chest. But there is no comfortable sentence in any language that ends with "and then you hunt down an eight-year-old girl through the snow," so I stopped telling myself things somewhere around the second bridge and just walked.

The snow was wrong. Everyone in Vharen could feel it — snow in the harvest month, fat flakes falling without wind, without cold enough to deserve them, as if the sky itself had been given a memory of winter and decided to act on it. The lamplighters worked with their collars up and their faces turned down. Nobody looked up. People who look up in this city see things they have to spend money to forget.

I found her at the tannery steps, of all places. Curled into the lee of a drainage arch where the warm water ran out, which was clever of her, because collectors work the cold routes. Clever, and tired, and asleep — actually asleep, the way only children sleep, with the total surrender of someone who has never once had to be the last one awake in a dangerous room. Her breath made a small fog against the stone. She had mud on her cheek and a dried tear-track through it, and she was holding something clutched to her chest like a talisman: a strip of ribbon, blue gone grey, tied in a bow that had been retied so many times the fabric was fraying into thread.

I stood over her with my writ in my pocket and my hand on the sleep-knife at my belt, and I thought: this is the moment. Nineteen years, and this is the moment you finally see yourself.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about being a collector. We don't carry our own memories much anymore. We trade them away — a bad year here, a dead friend there, whatever hurts too much to hold — and the Guild pays handsomely, and we walk around light as birds and twice as hollow. But there are rules about what a collector may sell. Certain memories are considered *load-bearing*, Corvane says. Essential to function. You keep those whether you want to or not.

Mine is my daughter.

Her name was Liss. She would be twenty-two now, if she were anything now, which she is not. She died at nine — fever, the kind that comes up the river in bad summers — and for eleven years I carried that grief like a stone in my mouth, and then one day I sat across a desk from a pleasant Guild clerk and sold him everything except the load-bearing part. What remained was small. A single afternoon. Liss at seven, sitting cross-legged on our kitchen floor, teaching herself to tie a bow with a strip of blue ribbon, tongue between her teeth, failing forty times, and on the forty-first time getting it, and looking up at me with a face like a struck bell — *look, Mama, look what I did* —

That was the load-bearing memory. That was what I kept when I sold my daughter's laugh, her funeral, her name-day, her whole short life except one ribbon.

And Dominic Whitlock stole it from me four months ago.

* * *

You should understand how that works, because it isn't what people think. When your vault is robbed, you don't feel the absence. The whole obscenity of memory-theft is that it doesn't leave a hole; it leaves a smooth place. You know you had a daughter the way you know a fact from a book — Liss, born in the Year of the Grey Tide, died at nine — but the *knowing-with-your-body* is gone. I woke the morning after the Whitlock job and reached for the ribbon afternoon and found nothing, and worse than nothing: a shape where something used to be, the way a bed stays warm after its sleeper leaves.

I reported it. Of course I did. I'm not a rebel; I'm a functionary with a knife. And the Guild investigated, and shrugged, and told me losses happen, and offered me a voucher toward a replacement memory of comparable emotional weight, which is a sentence I want you to sit with. *Comparable emotional weight.* As if grief were grain, measurable by the bushel, fungible across all persons.

I took the voucher. I never redeemed it. Some part of me understood even then that no other memory could ever weigh the same, because the weight wasn't in the memory. It was in the fact that I'd been there. That it had happened to *me*. You cannot purchase provenance.

So yes. I had reasons to hate Dominic Whitlock. I had a writ authorizing me to do considerably more than hate them. And I stood on the tannery steps in the wrong snow, over a sleeping child who had nothing to do with any of it, holding my hand six inches from a sleep-knife, and I asked myself the question I'd been dodging for nineteen years:

What exactly did I think I was collecting?

* * *

She woke the way city children wake — all at once, no drift, eyes open and already calculating. She looked at my face. She looked at my belt. She looked at the alley behind me, measuring distances I doubted she could make, and then she did the thing that broke me, the thing I will remember when everything else I've bought and sold has rotted out of my head:

She straightened her spine, lifted her chin, and said, in a voice pitched carefully flat, "You're here for me. It's fine. Don't hurt anyone else to do it."

Eight years old. Offering terms of surrender. Negotiating the price of other people's safety with the only coin she had, which was herself.

"Nobody's here to hurt anybody," I said, which was true, and also a lie, because the writ said *retrieve alive*, and everyone in the trade knows what *alive* means once the retrieval goes badly. "Wren, isn't it? The dreamer."

Something moved behind her eyes at that — not fear exactly. Recognition. The particular wariness of a child who has been treated as a resource long enough to know what adults want from her before they ask.

"You're a collector," she said. "You take remembering."

"I collect debts," I said, and heard how it sounded, and winced. Nineteen years of that line. *Just settling accounts.* The Guild's catechism, worn smooth as a prayer bead from handling.

"Whose?" she asked.

And that — that stupid, innocent, surgical question — is what undid me. Because I opened my mouth to say the debtors' names, the writ's names, and what came out instead, into the falling snow, to a child on a tannery step, was:

"A little girl's. Nine years old. Blue ribbon."

Wren went very still. Children go still differently than adults; adults freeze like animals, all tension, but children go still like water, like they've turned glassy and you can suddenly see all the way down. She looked at me for a long moment. Then she said, with the terrible gentleness children reserve for adults they've decided are wounded:

"You lost somebody."

"My daughter."

"And someone took the remembering of her." Not a question. She'd seen the inside of the Guild's inventory long enough to know the shapes of theft. "Dominic took it?"

"Dominic took it."

"Was it good?" she asked. "The remembering. Was it a good one?"

And I don't know why I answered her honestly. Habit, maybe. Or the snow, which kept falling in that wrong, patient way, as though the whole world had slowed down to listen. Or the fact that she was the first person in four months who had asked me about the loss rather than the paperwork of the loss.

"It was the best one," I said. "It was the only one worth keeping. She was learning to tie a bow. It took her forty-one tries. She counted every failure out loud like she was keeping score against the universe." My voice did something I didn't authorize. "Forty-one tries. And then she got it, and she looked at me like I should be proud of the universe for finally cooperating."

Wren nodded slowly, the way old women nod, the way oracles nod. Then she said the thing I will never sell, never trade, never let any clerk in any vault lay a finger on:

"She's still yours, you know. Even if the remembering is gone. The remembering isn't the daughter. It's just where you kept her."

I am fifty-one years old. I have taken memories from two hundred and six people — I know the number because the ledger knows it, because the ledger keeps count even when I can't bear to — and no one, not a priest, not a clerk, not the Guildmaster himself in his velvet reasonableness, ever said anything to me as true as what that child said on the tannery steps while the wrong snow came down.

The remembering isn't the daughter. It's just where you kept her.

* * *

Here is what I did, and I'll thank you not to admire it, because there was nothing admirable in it. There was only a woman standing in front of a locked door she had finally noticed she'd been leaning against her whole life.

I crouched down to her level, which is something collectors are trained never to do — stay above, stay distant, stay a shape rather than a face — and I said, "The others. Whitlock and the knight. Where are they?"

"Why?"

"Because if I can find them first, the ones coming behind me won't." I let her see my face properly. Let her read whatever was written there. "I'm not going to pretend I'm kind. I came here tonight to do an ugly thing and I'd have done it if you'd been anyone else's child. But you're not. You're mine, in the sense that every child in this city is mine — I've been taking pieces of its people for twenty years, and that makes the whole city my debt." I paused. "Also, in a different sense, I think you might be the only person who can tell me where I left myself."

She studied me. Eight years old, and she studied me the way a jeweler studies a stone for flaws, and whatever she saw — whatever futures flicker behind those eyes, because I'd heard the stories, everyone in the Guild had heard the stories about the little prophet they kept like a lamp — whatever she saw made her nod.

"The hospice on Candle Lane," she said. "Saint Verity's. They're hiding with the dying, because collectors won't go near deathbeds. Too many strong memories, too much grief in the air. It muddies the instruments."

"That was smart of them."

"That was Dominic being scared," she said, with the faintest smile, the first I'd seen on her. "Scared people are usually smart. It's the brave ones you have to watch."

"And you're going back to them now."

"I'm going back to them now," she agreed. Then, tilting her head: "Are you going to tell Corvane where we are?"

I thought about it. Honestly, carefully, the way I hadn't thought about anything in years — turning the question over like a stone, looking at what lived underneath it. If I reported nothing, another collector would find Saint Verity's within days; Candle Lane was the obvious hole, and Corvane had thirty collectors and I had one conscience, and conscience is a poor match for arithmetic.

"No," I said. "I'm going to tell him I caught you and lost you. Which will be true. I'll say you slipped the sleep-knife — you're small, it happens, children run cold, the dose misjudges. He'll believe it because he wants to believe his instruments are imperfect rather than his people disloyal. Corvane has never once believed in disloyalty. It interferes with his self-portrait."

"That's a lie," Wren observed.

"It's a kindness shaped like a lie," I said. "In my trade, that's the best craftsmanship available."

* * *

I walked her to Candle Lane. I know how that sounds — the hunter escorting the prey to cover — but I wanted her inside walls I trusted, and I trusted Saint Verity's because I'd collected there often enough to know the sisters kept their doors heavy and their tongues heavier. At the corner where the lane bent and the hospice lamp showed yellow through the snow, she stopped.

"There's a thing you should know," she said. "About Dominic."

"I'm listening."

"They kept a lot of memories this summer. More than they could use, more than they could sell. Aldric asked them why once, and they said—" she frowned, reaching for the exact words, and I realized she remembered things *exactly*, verbatim, the way the rest of us only wish we could — "they said, 'Somebody in this city is owed these, and I intend to find out who.'"

The snow came down between us. Somewhere across the rooftops, a bell rang the hour, and the sound fell soft and wrong, muffled by weather that shouldn't exist.

"One of them was mine," I said. "The ribbon afternoon. Wasn't it."

"Yes," said Wren simply. "They didn't steal it to keep. They stole it because it was in the vault wrong — I dreamed that, a while ago. A shelf full of names that weren't paid for. Things taken without a debtor's signature." She looked up at me. "I don't fully understand it yet. I understand it a little more every night. But I think Dominic wasn't robbing people. I think Dominic was doing receipts."

"Receipts," I repeated.

"Checking who owes whom." She shrugged, a small sharp motion, entirely adult. "That's what I dreamed, anyway. Dreams aren't orders. They're just... early."

She turned and went up the lane, small feet printing neat holes in the new snow, and I stood at the corner like a fool and watched until the hospice door swallowed her. Then I took out the writ, and I looked at the third name on it — *WREN, age eight, retrieve intact* — and I did something I had never done in nineteen years of service.

I tore it in half. Then halves again, until the pieces were too small to read, and fed them to the storm drain, where all the honest rivers go.

* * *

Corvane received me at midnight in his office above the counting floor, all warmth and lamplight and that smile he keeps polished like silverware. "Maren. My steadiest hand. Tell me about the girl."

"Caught sight of her near the tanneries," I said. "Lost her in the warrens under Cooper's Row. She's quick, and she's being sheltered — someone local, someone who knows the crawl-spaces. Give me two days and a map of the undercity drains, and she's yours."

He studied me. Corvane studies everyone; it's his hobby, his craft, his religion. I met his eyes with nineteen years of unremarkable competence arranged across my face like armor, and I thought about Liss tying her bow, forty-one tries, *look, Mama* — except that I couldn't think it, not really, not with the body's knowing, only the book's knowing, and the smooth place where the afternoon used to live ached like a tooth in a jaw that's been pulled.

"You seem different tonight," Corvane said mildly. "Lighter."

"Do I?" I said. "Perhaps the snow."

"Perhaps." He smiled his counting-house smile and waved me off. "Two days, Maren. The king's dreams are getting restless, and restless dreams require... staff."

I bowed. I climbed the stairs out of the counting house into the falling snow, and I stood a moment in the street with my collar up, and I made myself a promise with the only collateral I had left, which was nothing, which was everything:

Before this winter finished falling, I was going to walk back into that vault, past every clerk and every lock, and I was going to find one small memory of a girl and a blue ribbon and forty-one tries. And if Dominic Whitlock was the thief who'd taken it, then fine. Let them be a thief. I'd been one for nineteen years, and I'd finally learned the trade's real lesson, the one they don't print in the catechism:

Some things are stolen in order to be returned.

I turned toward Candle Lane, and the snow kept falling, and ahead of me — I swear this, though no one will believe it — ahead of me, in the yellow window-light of Saint Verity's, I saw a shadow move that was too big for a child and walked with the deliberate weight of a man in armor, opening a door.

The hunted, gathering. And I, the hunter, walking toward them through the snow with empty hands and a full heart, about to offer the only bounty I had left:

Everything I knew about how the machine works from the inside.
