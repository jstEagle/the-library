## 11. Snow Begins to Fall

I want it on the record — my record, the one nobody audits but me — that I knew something was wrong before I saw a single flake. The pigeons told me. All of them. At once.

They lifted off the rooftops above the Tannery Rows in one gray sheet, wheeled twice over the chimneys, and then landed again as if embarrassed by the whole performance. Pigeons don't do that. Pigeons are the guild's only honest competitors: they take what they want and feel nothing about it. When they start acting like an omen, you pay attention.

"Earthquake," said Aldric, who had been sleeping sitting up against the wall with his sword across his knees like a man who'd made peace with looking ridiculous.

"It's not an earthquake."

"Flood, then."

"Aldric. Look at the sky."

He looked. I watched him look, and I watched the exact moment his face did the thing faces do when they understand a thing their body already knew. The clouds had come down. Not rain-clouds, not the fat gray bellies we get off the harbor in autumn — these were lower than that, pressed against the rooftops like a ceiling being lowered into a room where people are still eating dinner. And they were the wrong color. White at the edges. The white of old scar tissue. The white of a page that's been written on and scrubbed clean a hundred times.

"It's midsummer," Aldric said, in the tone of a man informing the sky of its contractual obligations.

"I know."

"It snowed the year the order fell. Just like this. Out of nothing." His hand found his sword hilt without any decision from him. "Corvane's started feeding him."

That was the part I hadn't wanted to say out loud, because saying it makes it a fact, and facts have weight, and I am a man whose entire profession is knowing how much things weigh. But there it was. Somewhere beneath our feet — beneath the tanneries and the wine cellars and the ossuary and all the respectable strata of Vellum City — Guildmaster Corvane was pouring war into the dreams of a king who should have been allowed to keep sleeping until the world forgot his name.

And the sky was starting to remember instead.

* * *

We moved before full dark. That was Wren's idea, or rather it was Wren's certainty, which is a different and more alarming currency.

"The snow comes at the bells," she said, sitting cross-legged on the floor of our bolt-hole with her chin in her hands. "Midnight bells. I've seen it three times now. It comes when the bells ring, and it doesn't melt, and by morning the whole city's white, and everyone comes out of their houses and stands in it and some of them start crying and some of them start singing and both groups are wrong."

"Wrong how?" Aldric asked.

"They think it means something good or something bad." She shrugged, eight years old and ancient about it. "It just means he's turning over in his sleep. Like when you dream someone's chasing you and your leg kicks."

I wrote that down. Habit. Then I looked at what I'd written — *he turns over; the sky bleeds* — and decided some entries don't belong in ledgers, even private ones, and crossed it out so hard I nearly tore the page.

The hospice had been kind to us for six days. Sister Ovelia asked no questions, which in this city is either charity or a business model, and fed us barley soup and let Aldric fix her leaking roof in exchange for not charging us for the fixing. But hospices fill up fast in a panic, and by evening the panic had arrived ahead of the weather.

You could hear it in the streets. Not screaming — screaming comes later, screaming is the second movement. This was the murmur. Doors opening and closing. People standing in doorways looking up. A woman two streets over singing an old harvest song badly and loudly, the kind of song people sing when they've decided the world is ending and they might as well be embarrassing about it. And underneath it, threading through every alley: the old words. You couldn't see them but you could feel them moving through the crowd like lice through a seam.

*Thorn King. Thorn King. He kept the winter once, he'll keep it again.*

"He'll *save* them," Aldric said bitterly, listening. "That's the poison of it. Half the city remembers the long winter as salvation. Their grandparents knelt in the snow and were grateful."

"And the other half?"

"The other half remembers whose memories he took to make it. Mine included." He said it flatly, the way men say things they've said to themselves ten thousand times. "My grandmother lost sixty years. Everything from her wedding day onward, gone between one winter and the next. She used to look at my grandfather like a stranger who was very polite to her. Sixty years of marriage, and she thanked him for his kindness the way you thank an innkeeper."

I didn't say anything. What was there? *Sorry the mercy that saved the world ate your family* isn't a sentence that improves anyone's evening.

But here is the thing about old hatreds resurfacing: they don't come back alone. They bring their cousins. By the time we reached the Shambles — taking the long way, the wet way, under the aqueduct where the smell covers everything — we could hear the first real trouble. Glass breaking somewhere near Copperlane. A shout of *"Thorn-taker!"* answered by a shout of *"Winter-bringer!"*, which is the sort of theological dispute this city settles with bricks.

Two mobs, then. Or one mob with two opinions, which is worse, because it means everyone has a side pre-loaded and waiting. A century of peace built on top of a sealed vault, and Corvane had cracked the seal, and everything underneath — every grudge, every grief, every grandmother who looked at her husband like an innkeeper — was coming up through the crack like water through a floorboard.

"We need to be underground before midnight," I said.

"I know," Aldric said.

"Not the root-vaults yet. The cisterns. We watch the snow come down from somewhere it can't bury us, and tomorrow—"

"Dominic." Wren tugged my sleeve. She wasn't looking at me. She was looking at the gap between two rooftops down the street, where the sky showed through like a slit in a curtain.

"What?"

"It's early," she said. "This time it's early."

* * *

The first flake came down at half past eleven, twenty-nine minutes ahead of schedule, and I will confess to you — ledger-keeper to whoever's reading — that I flinched. A grown man, trained thief, veteran of the guild's deepest corridors, flinching at a snowflake the size of a fingernail paring.

Because it didn't melt on Aldric's shoulder. It sat there. It sat there while he brushed at it and missed, and it sat there while Wren reached up and touched it and pulled her finger back like she'd expected it to be hot. It sat there glowing faintly, the way paper glows when you hold it near a candle but haven't caught it yet.

"That's not weather," Aldric said.

"No," I said. "That's memory."

I know memory when I see it falling. I've bottled enough of it. Grief falls exactly like that — slow, unhurried, certain of its welcome. And this snow was made of the same stuff the vaults are made of, the same pale stuff that hums in the deep shelves, except no one had catalogued it, no one had sealed it, it was simply *loose*, spilling upward out of the ground and then falling back down because that's what loose things do.

More flakes came. Then dozens. Then the sky stopped pretending and started snowing in earnest, great slow deliberate flakes, each one carrying its little glow, and the whole street began to fill with a light that had no source and cast no shadows. It was beautiful. I want to be honest about that. It was the most beautiful thing I'd seen since the inside of the vault, and it was beautiful the way a knife is beautiful, and I stood in it hating myself for finding it lovely.

The singing woman stopped singing.

Down the street, a man came out of his house, walked to the middle of the road, held up his hands, and caught snowflakes, and began to weep — big helpless sobs, the sobs of a man remembering something. And I thought: *that's it, that's exactly it.* The king was dreaming harder, turning over, and his dreams were leaking up through the roots of the city, and whatever leaked got into people. One flake of a dead war on your tongue and suddenly you're crying for a battlefield you never stood on, or a grandmother you never met, or a winter you were spared.

"Inside," Aldric said. "Now. Both of you."

"The cistern entrance is four streets—"

"Four streets in *this*?" He gestured at the falling light. "Wren, take his hand. Don't touch the snow if you can help it. Don't let it land on your tongue."

"Why?" Wren asked, in the way children ask why — genuinely, dreadfully, wanting the real answer.

"Because it's someone's," I said. "Every flake. It's someone's something, and it hasn't been given back, and it's looking for a head to live in."

She took my hand. Her fingers were cold. Eight years old, and she'd spent a year of her own tomorrows buying us one night in a hospice, and now the sky itself was spending memories like a drunk lord spends coin, and I was holding her hand tighter than she probably appreciated.

We ran.

* * *

I want to describe the streets, because someday — if there is a someday, if there are ledgers after this one — someone will ask what it was like, the night the new winter started, and I refuse to let the official version be the only version.

The official version will say *panic*. Panic is a lazy word. What it actually was:

A lamplighter on Bellows Street who wouldn't leave his post, lighting lamps that lit themselves in the snow-glow, because forty years of habit is a religion and he wasn't going to abandon it on its holiest night.

An old soldier sitting on the steps of the Chantry of Staves, methodically taking off his boots, because — he explained to no one, to the air — "if it's coming back, I meet it barefoot, the way I left it last time."

Two women dragging a third between them, the third shouting in a language none of them spoke, because a flake had melted on her lips and given her someone else's mother tongue along with someone else's terror.

Children. Children running everywhere, delighted, catching snowflakes on mittens, and every adult who saw them doing the arithmetic I was doing — *how much can a child hold, what does a flake of a hundred-year-old grief do to someone who's only had eight years of anything* — and snatching them indoors with a violence indistinguishable from love.

And everywhere, growing louder under the snowfall, the name. Spoken in doorways, spat in gutters, whispered in queues outside shuttered shops. *Thorn King.* Some voices praying it. Some voices cursing it. All of them getting louder, because fear is a choir and it always finds the key.

At the mouth of Copperlane we passed a shopfront with its window smashed in, and inside, three men were burning furniture in the hearth of a family that wasn't theirs, and painted across the broken glass in something that was probably wine and hopefully was wine: **HE KEPT US SAFE ONCE. LET HIM KEEP US AGAIN.**

"There it is," Aldric said quietly. "There's the war. Before the war. That's how it starts — not with armies, with paint."

"Corvane planned this." My breath fogged, though the air wasn't cold-cold yet, just wrong-cold. "He feeds the king war-memories, the king leaks, the city remembers the winter, half the city decides they *want* him awake — and then Corvane doesn't need to wake him at all. The city wakes him. Corvane just holds the door."

"You sound impressed."

"I sound nauseous. There's overlap."

Wren laughed. One short, cracked bark of a laugh, there in the middle of the falling end of the world, and I felt it land somewhere under my ribs and stay there. Because that's the other thing I want on the record: in the middle of it, she laughed, and it wasn't madness. It was the laugh of a person who has seen the future several times and noticed that people in it are still people, still making jokes, still holding hands too tight. If she could laugh at it, I reasoned, then perhaps it was survivable. This is not sound reasoning. It is the reasoning that keeps people walking.

* * *

We made the cistern entrance with minutes to spare — a grate behind the tanner's drying-yard, rusted open since before I was born because no one has ever wanted to go down there voluntarily, which makes it excellent for people like us.

Below, the water channels ran black and quiet, and the sound of the city went soft and distant, and for the first time in hours I unclenched everything below my jaw.

Then Wren said, "Dominic."

"Mm."

"It's not stopping up there. The snow. In my dreams it stops by morning and melts in a day. This time—" She hugged her knees. In the dark I could just see her eyes, wide and steady, the eyes of someone reading a book the rest of us haven't been issued. "This time it doesn't stop. This time it gets heavier every night until the roofs start breaking. Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless somebody goes down and does the thing you're going to do." She said it simply, the way she says everything, as if prophecy were a chore list. "I don't know what the thing is. I've tried to see it. It's blurry. Futures where you're alive are always blurry."

"Flattering."

"It's true! Clear futures are the ones where nothing changes. You're very change-y." She yawned, enormous, eight years old again for exactly one second, and leaned against my arm. "Also I dreamed the knight cries later. No offense."

"Some taken," Aldric said from the darkness, and there — there it was again, that fragile laughter, the three of us passing warmth around like a flask in a snowstorm, which, I supposed, was precisely what we were doing.

I sat with my back against wet stone and listened to the city overhead: the murmuring, the singing, the breaking glass, the name repeated like a heartbeat. Above us, snow made of stolen lives settled on a city that didn't know it was being robbed, and somewhere far below all of it, deeper than the cisterns, deeper than shame, something crowned in thorns rolled over in its sleep and dreamed of war.

Corvane had the vault keys. Corvane had the feed. Corvane had a city talking itself into kneeling.

What did we have? A disgraced knight, a child made of tomorrows, and a thief with a head full of other people's secrets — including, now, the location of the old chapterhouse, where Aldric swore we'd find the truth written on the walls in paint older than the guild.

The snow would not stop. Every night heavier. Until someone went down and gave the sleeper back everything he was owed.

I took out my little book, and by the light of a single snowflake that had drifted down the grate and refused to die on the stone beside me, I wrote one line:

*Day one. It's falling. We go down.*

Then I closed the book, because even I have limits, and the flake glowed on beside me like a small patient witness, waiting — as everything in this city waited now — for someone to finally give it back.
