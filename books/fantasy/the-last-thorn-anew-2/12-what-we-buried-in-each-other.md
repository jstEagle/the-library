## 12. What We Buried in Each Other

The safehouse was a former counting house with a collapsed roof and opinions about damp. Rain came through in three places, each drip landing on something that had once been valuable — a ledger desk, a brass scale, the skull of whatever the Vellum Guild had written off decades ago and never bothered to remove. I sat with my back against a pillar older than my order and listened to Dominic argue with a lock.

"You could pick it," they said, not looking up from the mechanism, "if your hands weren't the size of candlesticks."

"My hands won tournaments," I said.

"Your hands lost an entire chapterhouse."

There it was. The joke I'd let them make twice before, because a man who flinches at every wound teaches people where all of them are. But tonight the words landed differently, because Wren was asleep upstairs with her boots still on, because the collectors had passed our door twice since dusk, and because I had spent the whole walk here rehearsing a sentence I'd promised myself I would never say aloud in any lifetime, including the ones where I was drunk, and there had been several.

I said it anyway.

"I helped bury him."

Dominic's picks stopped moving. In the silence, the roof dripped its judgment: one drop on the scale, one on the ledger desk, one, patiently, on the skull.

"The Thorn King," I said, because apparently once you open a grave you might as well describe what's in it. "A generation ago. I carried the shovel."

* * *

You should understand something about the Order of the Briar Vigilant before you judge me, though I suspect you'll judge me anyway; everyone does eventually, and honestly the waiting is worse than the verdict.

We were founded to remember. That's all. Not to fight, not to guard relics or crowns — to *remember*. When the Long Winter ended four hundred years ago, when the Thorn King took every grief in the realm into himself and lay down beneath what would become this city to hold it, someone had to keep the account. Someone had to know he was there. Our whole sacred charge, carved above the doors of our chapterhouse in letters deep enough to survive fire, was this: **He sleeps. Feed him nothing. Tell no one.**

We were very good at telling no one. We were less good, as it turned out, at feeding him nothing.

"Wait," Dominic said. They'd given up on the lock entirely now and were sitting cross-legged on the floor facing me, which is either a courtesy or a tactical position, depending on the thief. "The guild feeds him memories. You told me that was Corvane's corruption. A hundred years of rot."

"It was a hundred years of *policy*," I said. "Corruption implies somebody broke a rule. Nobody broke anything. The rule changed, quietly, and the men who changed it wrote the new rule into the old book so that future brothers would think it had always been mercy." I rubbed my face. My beard had gone greyer since the flight began; I could feel it happening somehow, which is not a thing beards do, but grief is inventive. "Here is what happened. The king took the realm's pain to end the winter. And then he held it. Forever. And the brothers who came after — sensible men, kind men — asked the obvious question. He's suffering down there, holding a nation's worth of horror in his sleep. Can we ease it? And the answer, discovered by trial and error over two centuries, was yes. You can give a sleeping god a memory. He'll dream it. It blots out his own for a while."

"So the guild didn't invent the feeding."

"The guild inherited it like a family inherits a debt," I said. "Or a disease. The Vigilant started it. We told ourselves it was compassion. We curated what we gave him — gentle things, mostly. Weddings. Harvests. Children laughing. A century of the nicest moments we could buy, poured down into the dark so the man beneath us wouldn't drown in what he swallowed for our sake." I laughed, and it came out wrong, the way laughs do when they've been kept too long in a cellar. "Do you see the arithmetic? Every sweet memory we fed him was one more year we didn't have to look at what we owed. We weren't comforting him. We were comforting ourselves. He was our conscience, and we drugged our conscience nightly."

Dominic said nothing. Thieves are good at that. It's how they get you to hand things over.

* * *

Let me tell you about the night it ended, because you've earned the whole ugly shape of it, and because I have carried this alone through thirty years and it has grown teeth.

I was young. Knight-errant of the Briar, third circle, full of the certainty that comes free with youth and is billed later. I was assigned to the vault rotation — the deep watch, the root-vaults, the place beneath the city where the oldest brothers went to administer the feedings. I want to be clear that I believed in it. I knelt at the edge of that vast darkness with a vial of some farmer's golden afternoon in my hands and I felt *holy*.

And then, one night, I saw the ledgers.

Not ours. Ours were honest, if honest is the word for a lie maintained with excellent penmanship. These were the selection ledgers — the ones that decided *which* memories got purchased, and from whom. And I noticed, because I was young and hadn't yet learned that noticing is a dangerous hobby, that certain categories never appeared. No plagues. No massacres. Nothing from the war years. Nothing painful, ever, except—

Except once. One entry. A single painful memory, logged and sealed and marked for delivery, sixty years back, and beside it in faded ink a note in the Grandmaster's own hand: *For balance. He must not forget what he is.*

"For balance," I said aloud, now, in the ruined counting house, and Dominic flinched at my tone. "That's the phrase. That's the phrase I've heard in my head for thirty years. Because I understood it instantly, the way you understand a blade coming at you. The pain wasn't forbidden because it was cruel. It was forbidden because it was *dangerous*. Pain wakes him. Grief wakes him. If he ever truly felt what he swallowed — all of it, undiluted, uncurated — he would wake up. And a waking king holding a nation's agony is either a savior or a detonation, and nobody in four hundred years has wanted to find out which."

"So they kept him asleep," Dominic said slowly, "by keeping him happy."

"They kept him asleep by lying to him," I said. "Every night. For four hundred years. We turned the greatest act of sacrifice in history into a sedated patient, and called the sedation mercy, and built a city on top of him, and charged rent."

* * *

Here is the part I have never said to anyone. Here is the part I let my order die to protect.

I reported what I found. Of course I did. Young Aldric, third circle, marched straight to the Grandmaster with the selection ledger under his arm and righteousness in his mouth like a lit torch. I remember the exact expression on the old man's face when I laid it on his table. Not anger. Not fear. *Relief.* Like a man who has been waiting forty years for someone to finally say it out loud so he could stop pretending he hadn't.

"Do you know why the rule says *tell no one*?" he asked me.

"To protect the king," I said.

"No," he said. "To protect the world. From the truth. Think, boy. What happens if the realm learns their king isn't dead — that he lies beneath them, awake enough to dream, drowning in everything they gave away? Half the realm will want to dig him up and worship him. Half will want to dig him up and finish him. And the other half — the ones who gave him their grief willingly, whose dead children's last hours are *in him*, being soothed by wedding dreams — those people will learn that their sacrifice has been pickled in syrup and stored. There will be war. There will be schism. There will be a thousand little Corvanes, each certain they alone know what the king needs." He pushed the ledger back across the table. "The lie is load-bearing, Aldric. You don't pull a stone out of an arch to inspect it while standing underneath."

"And you agreed," Dominic said. Their voice was very quiet. "Young and righteous as you were. You agreed."

"Not that night," I said. "That night I called him a coward. I threatened to go to the crown. I did everything a hero does right before the story teaches him the lesson." I looked at my hands. Candlestick hands. Tournament hands. Shovel hands. "And then he showed me the second ledger. The one with the names. Every brother who had ever tried to speak. Not killed — the Vigilant doesn't kill its own, we were too pious even for that. *Disappeared.* Reassigned to frontier posts until they died of weather and loneliness. Families moved without explanation. Names struck from the rolls so thoroughly that their own mothers' memories of them were later purchased by the guild and filed away." I swallowed. "My predecessor in the deep watch tried exactly what I threatened. His name is in that ledger. So is his daughter's. She's alive somewhere. She doesn't remember she ever had a father. The guild sold that memory to a client who wanted to know what unconditional love felt like, and they bought it from a nine-year-old girl who couldn't afford winter boots, and the transaction was *legal*."

The rain dripped. On the scale, on the desk, on the skull.

"So I made the trade," I said. "The one every coward makes and calls it wisdom. I stayed silent. I rose in the ranks. I became the man who signed the reassignments, who struck the names, who smiled at young idealists in the refectory and thought, *not you too, please, not you too, just eat your soup.* I told myself I was containing the damage. Managing the secret. Keeping the arch standing." My voice did something embarrassing on the last part and I let it, because I had decided, somewhere around the word *ledger*, that tonight I was going to be finished with dignity. "And when the order was finally destroyed — when the crown came for us, when they burned the chapterhouse and scattered the brothers and struck the Briar from every roll in the kingdom — I ran. I could have fought. I could have testified. I knew where every body was buried, literally, and I said nothing, because saying something meant pulling the stone out of the arch, and I had spent twenty years learning to love the arch more than the people holding it up."

* * *

Silence. The good kind or the bad kind, impossible to tell. Dominic sat very still, and I remembered that stillness from interrogations I'd witnessed — the stillness of someone deciding whether you're worth the knife or the rope.

Say something, I begged silently. Condemn me. It's easier. Condemnation I know how to carry; it's forgiveness that weighs nothing and therefore crushes you completely.

"You knew," Dominic said at last. "When you found my stolen memory in the vault. When you recognized the seal. You already knew what was down there. You'd known for thirty years."

"Yes."

"And you didn't report me. You didn't run. You joined *me*." They stood, paced to the window, stood in the gap in the shutters where the streetlight came through in a bar across their face. "Why?"

"Because you stole the wrong thing," I said. "Because the seal on that vial was my order's — the Briar's mark, on a guild delivery, meaning the new management was using my dead brothers' insignia to wrap their poison. And because—" I stopped. Started again. Honesty is a muscle and mine was cramping badly. "Because when I looked at you, kneeling in that vault with a stolen grief in your hands like it was an egg you were afraid to crack, I saw a younger me. Except you were doing the brave thing. You were actually doing it. And I thought: perhaps the debt can be paid by proxy. Perhaps if I help you, some fraction of the weight transfers." I laughed again, that cellar laugh. "It's a childish theology. Knights are sentimental. It's the armor."

"That's not why," Dominic said. They turned. The light-bar crossed their eyes. "You could have paid a debt anywhere. You followed me because of Wren."

The rain chose that moment to intensify, hammering the collapsed roof like applause from a hostile audience.

"Yes," I admitted. "Partly. A child who remembers futures, kept in a cage by the same machine that cages a king — I'm not subtle, Whitlock, I never have been. The symbolism walked up and bit me. But also—" and here I made the final withdrawal from whatever account of self-respect I had left— "also because I have spent thirty years protecting a lie, and I am *tired*, and I would rather spend whatever remains of my life protecting three people and the truth, even if the truth kills us. Even if it kills *him*." I said that last part carefully. "Because that's the question, isn't it. The one you've been circling since the murals. Whether giving him back everything — waking him with the full weight, killing him with honesty — is mercy or murder."

"Is it?" Dominic asked. "Murder?"

"He took the realm's pain so we wouldn't have to carry it," I said. "And we answered by making sure he never had to feel it either. We took even his sacrifice from him. We left him nothing — not the burden, not the release, not even the *knowledge* of what he'd done. Four hundred years of anesthesia." I shook my head. "No. Giving it back isn't murder. Murder is what we've been doing all along, one gentle memory at a time. Giving it back is the first honest thing anyone will have said to him since he lay down."

Dominic was quiet a long moment. Then they crossed the room, picked up the lockpicks, went back to the stubborn lock, and said, without looking at me:

"I robbed the guild for eleven years. Do you know why I started?"

I hadn't expected that. "Money?"

"Everyone assumes money." The first pin clicked. "I started because my mother's death was taken. Sold. I found out at nineteen that the last time she said my name existed as a product, catalogued, priced, available to any stranger with coin. And I couldn't buy it back — too expensive — so I decided that if grief was going to be a commodity, I'd become the best thief in the business and steal *everyone's* back. All of it. Empty the vaults. Give people themselves." Another click. "Naive. Obviously. You can't steal back ten thousand memories a year with one pair of candlestick-adjacent hands. So I became a ledger-keeper instead. Told myself I was mapping the vault for someday. Told myself a lot of things." The lock surrendered with a small, contemptuous click. "My point is — I know what it is to maintain a lie because the truth feels too heavy to lift. I did it for eleven years. The difference between us is a few decades and a suit of armor."

"Is that forgiveness?" I asked. "Because I should warn you, I'll need it itemized. I'm told I respond well to ledgers."

Dominic glanced back at me, and for the first time since the flight began there was something in their face that wasn't fear or calculation. It was almost warm. Almost.

"It's not forgiveness," they said. "Forgiveness is for when someone wronged *you*. What Corvane and the guild did wronged everyone. What you did—" they shrugged— "you kept the arch standing because you thought the sky would fall. Turns out the sky was already falling, and the arch was holding it *up*. So no, Ser Aldric. This isn't forgiveness." They tossed me the broken lock's key ring, and I caught it, because some habits survive disgrace. "This is a co-conspirator deciding you're useful. Try not to make me regret it."

"That," I said, "is the kindest thing anyone has said to me in thirty years."

"I know," they said. "That's the saddest thing I've ever heard."

* * *

Wren came downstairs an hour later, drawn by the smell of the terrible soup I'd made on the brazier — I cook the way I pray, with sincerity and poor technique — and she looked at the two of us sitting shoulder to shoulder in the wreckage like soldiers after a battle nobody witnessed, and she said:

"You told him."

"Told who what?" I managed.

"You told him the thing," she said, climbing onto the ledger desk and dangling her feet. Her voice went sing-songy, the way it did when the tomorrows leaked through. "The shovel thing. Now the dream will change. It was thorns in snow before. Tonight it's different." She frowned, working at it, a child trying to describe the inside of a clock. "Tonight there's a market. Stalls. Candles. People buying back pieces of themselves, and crying happy." She looked up at us both. "And snow. Still snow. But warmer snow, somehow. Is warmer snow a thing?"

Dominic and I exchanged a glance over her head — the glance of two liars recognizing, simultaneously, that the child had just described something none of us had dared to imagine yet.

"It could be," Dominic said slowly. "If someone built it."

"Markets aren't hard," I offered. "I've sacked several."

"You *what*—"

"Later. Over soup. Wren — how much snow? How soon?"

She closed her eyes. When she opened them again, they were frightened, and I understood that the fright wasn't for herself.

"Soon," she said. "Before the next moon. And underneath it — underneath the market-dream — there's a door opening. Down deep. Something big turning over in its sleep." She hugged her knees. "He's starting to taste it. Whatever Corvane's feeding him. He's starting to *wake up angry*."

Outside, far off toward the guild quarter, a bell began to ring — not the hour bell, not the alarm bell, but the deep bell under the Vellum house, the one I'd been told as a young knight was purely ceremonial.

Neither of us believed that anymore.

"We'll need money," Dominic said, already reaching for their coat. "Real coin, and lots of it, and fast, and legally — well. Legally adjacent." A pause. A grim little smile. "Aldric. How do you feel about markets?"

I pulled on my own coat, and for the first time in thirty years, the weight I carried shifted — not lighter, never lighter, but *shared*, which is the only miracle I've ever personally verified.

"Warm snow," I said. "All right. Let's go build one."
