## 13. The Market of Impossible Things

The market lived under the old tannery district, where the smell of the river covered what needed covering and nobody asked where your goods came from. They called it the Gullet — a crooked lane of stalls and lanterns that had no right to exist, since half its merchants were selling things that shouldn't exist at all.

I'd been to markets before. I'd been *taken* to one, back when I was small enough that the Guild's minders still bothered pretending I was a ward instead of an asset. But I'd never been on this side of a stall. Never been the one with something in my hands that somebody wanted.

"You're doing the face again," Aldric said.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you're about to be sick into your own boots."

"That's just my face."

"It's not," Wren said. "Your real face is nicer."

Aldric snorted. Dominic shot us both a look over the crate we were using as a counter — the particular look of someone who has decided that complaining will take more energy than it's worth, and is filing our faces away for later.

We'd set up between a woman who sold bottled fog (actual fog; she swore it was from the mountains, though it smelled like the river) and a man whose entire stock was left shoes. The Gullet didn't do signage. You knew what a stall sold by who was crying in front of it.

That was the thing about memories. That was the thing nobody told you, because the Vellum Guild made very sure nobody said it out loud: grief was the only currency that never devalued.

* * *

Our first customer came before the lanterns were even properly lit. A cooper, by his hands — big knuckles, wood-shaving scars — who stood in front of our stall for a long time without speaking, working his jaw like he was chewing on words too tough to swallow.

Dominic waited him out. I watched them do it, and I understood for the first time that waiting was a skill, same as lockpicking or lying. Most people couldn't stand silence. They filled it. And whatever they filled it with was usually the truth.

"My wife," the cooper said finally. "Died two winters back. Fever."

"I'm sorry," Dominic said, and meant it. That was another skill. You could tell meant-sorry from polite-sorry the way you could tell a live coal from a painted one.

"There was a — the Guild took things from her. When she was sick. Memories, they called it *easing her passage*, but they paid me for them so I signed." He said *signed* like the word tasted of ash. "I've got the receipts. I never could afford to buy them back. Then I heard —" He stopped. Started again. "There's talk. Under the tanneries. Talk of someone selling things *back*."

Dominic slid the ledger across the crate. Not the Guild's ledger — ours. Three days old, four entries, written in Dominic's small precise hand.

"We don't charge what they charged," Dominic said. "Mostly we charge what you can pay. Sometimes that's nothing. Tell me about her first."

And here's what I learned that night, standing in the cold with thorn-dreams still itching behind my eyes: people don't want their memories back the way you'd think. The cooper didn't say *give me the day she died*. He said *there was a morning, she laughed at something I said, I can't remember anymore what I said, only that she laughed, and I've lost it, I've lost the sound*.

He wanted one laugh. Out of eleven years of marriage, one laugh.

Dominic found it. Of course they did — they'd spent their whole life in ledgers, and a memory receipt is just a ledger entry wearing different clothes. Twenty minutes of cross-referencing while the cooper stood there wringing his enormous gentle hands, and then Dominic held up a little glass vial with a ribbon of gold light coiled inside it like a sleeping eel.

"This is hers laughing," Dominic said. "You said something about a goat. I don't know why a goat. It'll come back when you take it."

"How much?"

"What have you got?"

He had six coppers and a carved wooden bird he'd made her. Dominic took three coppers and the bird.

"The bird's worth more than the memory," Aldric muttered afterward.

"No it isn't," Dominic said. "It's exactly worth it. Write it down, Wren."

So I wrote it down. *One laugh, returned. Paid: 3 coppers, one wooden bird.* It felt strange under my hand, writing in a ledger that gave things away. Like learning to write with my other hand.

* * *

By midnight we had a line.

Not a long one — word travels slow in winter, and slower when the thing traveling is hope, because hope weighs more than rumor — but a line. A widow. Two brothers who'd sold their childhoods separately years ago and wanted to remember each other small again. An old soldier who didn't want a happy memory at all; he wanted the worst day of his life back, because his dead friend was in it, and he said a man ought to carry his own dead.

Aldric handled him. I don't know what Aldric said — I saw the soldier put his hand on the knight's shoulder, hard, once, like a blow that was also a blessing, and then he walked off straighter than he came.

"He didn't pay," I said.

"He paid enough," Aldric said. "Thirty years ago he pulled me out of a burning chapel. Tonight I settled the account."

"You never mentioned a burning chapel."

"Aldric doesn't mention anything," Dominic said, sorting vials. "It's how he stores information. In sealed vaults."

"I contain multitudes," Aldric said gravely. "All of them classified."

I laughed. I heard myself laugh — really laugh, the kind that comes out before you decide to let it — and something in my chest went strange and warm, because I counted. Without meaning to, I counted: this was the third time in two days. Before the guildhouse, before Dominic and the knight and the running, I could go weeks. Laughter was a thing I spent carefully, like everything else I had.

But tonight it kept coming loose. When Dominic got into a haggling match with the fog-woman over stall space and won by convincing her the fog was *her own childhood fog*, returned at last. When Aldric tried to eat a meat pie from a vendor who turned out to be selling pies made of actual mystery, and his face went through four separate expressions before landing somewhere near philosophical acceptance. When a tiny grandmother pressed a honey-cake into my hands and said I had "the fattest cheeks of any oracle she'd ever seen," which, fair.

Warmth, in winter. That's what the Gullet gave us that night, and not from any brazier.

* * *

It was late — the deep-late, when the lanterns burn low and the honest thieves go home — when the woman came who changed the shape of the night.

She was young. Younger than she moved. She stood at the edge of our light for a long time, and I knew before she spoke, because I know the way grief walks when it's fresh. It doesn't walk yet. It stands.

"They said you sell things back," she said.

"If we've got them," Dominic said gently. "What are you missing?"

She laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd ever heard, that laugh. "Everything. Almost. My daughter was ill. The fever-kind. The Guild offered to take her pain. All of it. Every bad hour, every ache — they'd carry it for her, ease her through." Her hands were shaking. "They didn't say pain was stitched to everything else. They didn't say they'd take the whole cloth. She died smiling, ser. She died *smiling* because they'd taken every memory that ever hurt her, and there wasn't one left that remembered me."

Nobody said anything. Even the market seemed quieter around our stall, the way a room goes quiet when someone is praying.

"She's in the vaults," the woman said. "Her name was Liss. She'd be nine now if —" She stopped. Pressed her knuckles against her mouth. "Do you have her? Could you find her? I don't need all of it. One memory. Any one. I just want — I want to be remembered by her, once, before I —"

"Yes," I said.

Everyone looked at me. Including Dominic. Especially Dominic.

"Wren," Dominic said, in the voice they used for *careful*.

"She's in the root-vaults," I said. "I dreamed the shelves. I dreamed them twice. Little jars, rows and rows, and one row that hums different, and I know — I *know* I could find her. Children keep together down there. I don't know how I know. I just do."

"That's the plan," Aldric said quietly. "The whole reason we're earning coin tonight. To get *down*. Not to shop."

"One jar," the woman whispered. "Please."

Dominic looked at her for a long moment. I watched them weigh it — I'm good at watching Dominic weigh things, it looks like nothing happening behind the eyes while the whole world gets rearranged — and then they turned to me, and whatever they were going to say, they swallowed it.

"Give me your name," Dominic said. "Not for the ledger. For the promise."

"Maren," the woman said. "Maren Ostwick."

*Maren.* I wrote it in my head, in the place where I kept the important tomorrows. Some part of me — the part that dreamed of thorns blooming through snow — stirred and went quiet again, like a dog hearing a far-off whistle. I didn't understand it yet. I wrote the name down anyway.

"When this is done," Dominic told her, "come back here. Three nights from now, this stall, this hour. If we're alive, you'll have your daughter's memory. If we're not—" They paused. "Then the debt passes to whoever finds our bodies, which will be a great comfort to them."

The woman almost smiled. Almost. "You're strange people."

"We're a guild," Dominic said. "Of sorts."

* * *

We packed up in the gray hours before dawn, counting coins that weren't really the point. We'd made enough. More than enough — the bribes for the undercity gate-wardens, the forged transit seals Aldric's contact promised, the little toll-gifts you bring to people who live in the dark places and resent the sun-folk. The way into the vaults was bought.

But sitting on the crates while Aldric doused the lanterns, I thought about what we'd actually earned, and it wasn't coin.

Twenty-two mercies. That's what I wrote in the ledger, at the bottom, because Dominic had gone to argue with the fog-woman and someone had to close the accounts. *Twenty-two mercies sold. Twenty-two mercies returned.* And next to it, because I was eight and honest and couldn't help myself, I drew a small picture of a bird.

The Guild had a ledger too. I'd seen it — the great master-book in Corvane's office, chained shut, fat with a hundred years of taking. Everything in it flowed one direction. In. Always in. Take the grief, take the joy, take the child who dreams wrong, take take take, and feed it all down into the dark under the city to keep a dead king dreaming sweet.

Ours flowed the other way.

I sat there in the cold with my numb fingers and my honey-cake crumbs and understood something so simple it felt enormous: the difference between the Guild and us wasn't magic. Wasn't skill. Wasn't even goodness, exactly, because Aldric still lied like breathing and Dominic still kept secrets stacked in their chest like cordwood and I still saw futures I didn't share.

The difference was the direction the river ran.

Take, or give back. That was the whole of it. A hundred years of the Guild, and it fit on one side of a scale, and one night under the tanneries tipped it.

"You're glowing," Aldric said, dropping down beside me. Literally glowing, a little — my gift did that sometimes when I was full of a feeling too big to hold. He pretended not to notice, which was his way of being kind.

"Thinking."

"Dangerous. Dominic does that too. Look where it got us."

"Fugitive," I agreed. "Hiding in a black market. Selling stolen goods back to their owners."

"Crime of the century," Aldric said solemnly. "When they hang us, make sure my plaque reads *returned a laugh to a grieving cooper*."

I grinned at him. And then the grin slipped off my face, because the dream came — not the thorn dream, not tonight, but the *other* kind, the quick cold flash, the one that always arrived sideways like a knife thrown from a room I hadn't entered yet:

*A lantern burning low. Maren's face, wet-eyed, holding a small jar to her chest like a second heart. And behind her, at the mouth of the Gullet, lantern-light glinting off a collector's badge — and a voice saying, kindly, terribly kindly:* "You've been helpful, mistress. Now you're going to tell me where they sleep."

The vision let go of me. I was gripping Aldric's arm hard enough to leave marks.

"Wren?"

"Maren's in trouble," I said. "Or she will be. Or — Aldric, she *finds* us. She finds us and she saves us and it costs her everything, I saw it, I saw the badge —"

I stopped, because Aldric had gone very still, and beyond him, coming up the lane through the dying lantern-light, was Maren herself. Out of breath. White-faced. Running toward our empty stall with her hand thrust out, and in it, catching the last light —

A collector's badge.

"I followed you," she gasped. "From the hospice. From the beginning. I'm — oh, gods, I don't have much time — I'm one of them, I hunt for the Guild, I've been hunting *you* —"

She doubled over, hands on her knees, and when she looked up at us, her eyes were full of tears and terror and something that looked desperately like hope.

"— and I need to tell you what Corvane is planning. Before the snow comes."
