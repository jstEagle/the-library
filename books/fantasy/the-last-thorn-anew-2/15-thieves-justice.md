## 15. Thieves' Justice

I have been a collector for the Vellum Guild for nineteen years, and in that time I have learned that the most dangerous thing a person can carry is not a knife or a secret but someone else's grief. You hold it long enough, sealed in its little glass vial, warm against your palm like a sleeping bird, and it starts to make arguments. *Take me home,* it says. *I belong to someone. I was pried out of them with tongs and sold to the highest bidder, and you are the hand that did the prying.*

Most collectors learn to stop listening. That's the first thing they teach you at the guildhouse: the memory is inventory. The memory is coin. The memory is never, ever yours.

I stopped listening nineteen years ago. I just got very good at pretending I still heard the lessons.

* * *

The girl was waiting by the cistern gate in Hollowmere, exactly where the tip said she'd be, and she was so small that my hands shook before I even reached her.

Wren. Eight years old, maybe nine — children who spend their futures don't always keep track. The Guild's ledger listed her as an asset, which is what we call people when we're ashamed of calling them property. She'd dreamed something Corvane wanted, so Corvane wanted her, and I had been given the collection order because I was good at finding people and better at not asking what happened after.

She saw me coming from thirty feet off. Of course she did. Children who remember futures see everything coming; that's the whole cruelty of them.

"You're Maren," she said. "You carry the blue vial on Thursdays."

I stopped walking. "That's not information you were given."

"It's information I remembered." She hugged her knees tighter against the cistern wall. "You take it to the house on Candlemaker's Row. You knock twice, then once, then twice again. And every Thursday you stand in the doorway a little longer before you leave." She looked up at me with those eyes that had seen too many tomorrows to bother being afraid of this one. "You're hoping someone will ask you to stay."

The vial in my coat pocket — the one I'd carried eleven years now, bought back off a private buyer at scandalous expense because I could not bear knowing it sat in some rich man's cabinet gathering dust — the vial seemed suddenly very heavy. My daughter's laugh. Six seconds of it, the only piece of her the fever left unclaimed, and I had paid a collector's entire pension to hold it where I could reach it.

"Corvane sent you," Wren said, when I didn't answer.

"Corvane sent me."

"And you're deciding right now whether to do it." She tilted her head. "I've seen both versions. In one of them, I'm in a room with no windows by tonight. In the other—" she frowned, the way children frown when the future does something untidy, "—in the other one you give me bread and I get to be cold somewhere safer."

"What happens in the second one?"

"I don't know yet. It keeps changing." She held out her small hands, palms up, like she was asking alms or offering them. "It changed when you touched the vial this morning. You cried into your tea so nobody would hear. That's when it changed."

I am forty-six years old. I have hunted debtors through three districts and talked a knife-wielding widow out of her husband's bottled courage without spilling a drop of my own. I have done everything the Guild asked and told myself the ledger balanced because someone, somewhere, had to keep accounts.

I took out the vial instead of the binding cord. I took out the bread instead of the writ.

"Eat," I said, and my voice cracked like ice on a pond. "Then we're going somewhere warmer, and you're going to tell me where I can find a thief, a knight, and enough trouble to hang us all."

* * *

They nearly killed me at the door of the hospice on Vell Street, which I suppose is fair. Dominic Whitlock came out of the shadows with a letter opener held like they'd been taught to hold worse, and Ser Aldric — and there's a name I'd only heard in cautionary tales, the knight who let his order burn — put himself between me and the stairwell with a rusted sword and the posture of a man who has decided to die inconveniently.

"It's the collector," Wren announced from behind my knees, in the tone other children used to announce supper. "But the good version."

Dominic did not lower the letter opener. "There's no good version of a collector."

"There are," Wren said, "but they're rare. Like comets."

"Maren Solenne," I said, and gave my name the way you surrender a weapon. "Nineteen years with the Guild. I was sent to collect the girl. Instead I'm here to collect you — or rather, to warn you, which pays considerably worse."

"Warn us of what?" Aldric rumbled. Up close he was less a fallen knight than a wall that had learned to walk, all grey stubble and old scars and eyes that catalogued exits.

"Corvane knows where you are. Not tonight — he's letting you settle, letting you think you've outrun him. He wants you comfortable before he closes the trap." I pulled off my gloves, finger by finger, because it felt like the honest thing to do, baring the collector's hands. "And I know why. I've seen the delivery manifests. I've carried three of them myself this month, down into the root-vaults. War-memories, Dominic. Battle-grief. Siege-terror. A hundred years of the worst things men have done to each other, curated and concentrated, and every last drop going to the same client."

The silence in that hospice hallway was the kind that has weight.

"The king," Dominic said quietly. It wasn't a question. They'd seen him; everyone in that room knew they'd seen him. "He's feeding the king war."

"He's waking him with war. There's a difference, and the difference is snow." I looked at the window, where the light had gone strange and pale all week, though the calendar swore it was harvest-tide. "My mother lived through the last long winter. Forty years of it. She said the snow didn't fall from clouds. It fell from *decisions*."

* * *

We gathered around a hospice table that evening — the thief, the knight, the child, and the traitor-collector, four people who by every law of the city should have turned each other in — and I laid out what I'd stolen from Corvane's private office while delivering his correspondence, because a collector learns locks the way priests learn psalms.

A schedule. A sequence. Dates stretching back a century in the Guild's own hand: *memory delivered, dream sustained, sleeper pacified.* And beneath it, fresh ink, Corvane's personal cipher: the escalation. Grief first. Then terror. Then, on the night the prophecy named, the whole vault's worth of war poured into a sleeping mind until it woke believing the world had never made peace at all.

"He doesn't want a tyrant," Aldric said slowly, turning the pages with hands that trembled only slightly, which for him I suspected was screaming. "He wants a savior. A strong king to end all suffering. I've met men like this. They're worse than tyrants, because tyrants know they're damned."

"He thinks he's merciful," I agreed. "That's the horror of him. I've carried his orders for years and he signs them with a little flourish, like a love letter."

Dominic stared at the schedule for a long time. When they spoke, their voice had gone flat and precise, the voice of a ledger-keeper doing arithmetic nobody should have to do. "The feed can't simply be cut. Wren tried — the dreams bleed through anyway, slower, but they bleed. And if the king wakes half-fed, half-mad, with a century of nightmares and nothing to balance them—"

"The last winter," Aldric said, "but worse."

"So we don't cut the feed." Dominic looked up, and I saw it happen — saw the thought arrive in their face like weather. "We do something else. We give him everything else. All of it. Every memory the Guild has ever taken — the grief, yes, but also the weddings and the births and the forgivenesses. A hundred years of the whole human ledger, poured back at once. Not waking him to war. Drowning him in everything, so he can finally *finish*, and finally rest."

Nobody spoke. The hospice clock ticked like a patient heart.

"That's not a rescue," Aldric said at last. "That's an execution. You'd be killing a king."

"No," said Wren, softly, from her seat by the fire. She'd been quiet so long I'd almost forgotten her, which I suspect she found restful, given how loud the future usually was. "That's mercy. I've dreamed it. He *wants* to die. He's been trying to for a hundred years and we won't let him." She looked at Dominic. "You're not stealing his life. You're stealing his death. Nobody's ever stolen anything kinder."

*Steal his death.* I watched those words land on each of us in turn, and I watched each of us — knight, thief, collector — flinch and then, horribly, nod.

"Well," said Aldric, rubbing his jaw. "I've sworn oaths to two sovereigns and broken both. Breaking a third law seems almost traditional at this point."

"This isn't law-breaking," Dominic murmured, with the ghost of a smile, the first I'd seen on them. "Law-breaking is when there's a law against it. No court in the city has ever imagined this crime. We'd be inventing it."

"Then we invent it well," I said, and heard my own voice steady in a way it hadn't since my daughter died. "Because I know the root-vaults. Nineteen years, I've walked those corridors. I know which doors the wardens watch and which ones they've forgotten. I know the delivery schedules, the shift changes, the blind spots. Corvane built his plan on the backs of collectors like me, and he never once thought to wonder what we carry." I spread my hands on the table — the collector's hands, the tongs-and-vials hands, and for the first time in nineteen years they didn't feel like someone else's. "You need a way in. I am a way in."

Dominic studied me for a long moment. Ledger-keepers weigh everything; it's their nature and their curse. Whatever arithmetic they performed on my face, it came out in my favor.

"Allies, then," Dominic said. "Not employees. Not assets. Allies. Equal shares of the risk and equal shares of whatever comes after." A pause. "If there is an after."

"If there is," Wren said cheerfully, "I've seen a little of it. There's a rose bush involved. I don't understand it yet either."

* * *

We planned until the candles drowned themselves. I drew the root-vaults from memory on the back of a delivery manifest — the long spiral stair, the humming shelves, the warden posts, the one ancient freight-lift wide enough for six and slow enough to be a coffin. Aldric mapped the chapterhouse ruins above, the old ways his order had sealed a generation ago, ways the Guild forgot existed because forgetting is our whole trade. Wren contributed warnings — "not the left stair, someone falls, I haven't seen who" — delivered in the matter-of-fact tone of a child reporting tomorrow's weather.

Somewhere past midnight, Dominic caught my eye across the table.

"Why?" they asked. No accusation in it. Genuine curiosity, the question of a person who keeps ledgers and needs the entry explained. "You had a life inside the Guild. A pension. A place. Why throw it into this?"

I reached into my coat and set the blue vial on the table between us. Inside, six seconds of laughter shimmered like a trapped summer.

"Because I spent eleven years buying back a piece of someone the Guild helped take," I said. "Eleven years, and I got six seconds. And Corvane sits above a hundred years of everyone's everything, and he means to spend it all making a king believe in war." I closed my hand around the vial. "I know what it costs, being kept from your own memories. I would not wish it on a god. Especially not one who's begging to be let go."

Dominic nodded slowly, and something passed between us that had no name in any ledger I've kept — recognition, maybe, between two people who had spent their lives handling other people's hearts and finally decided to give one back.

"Then it's settled," Aldric said, rising, and for a moment the ruined knight stood like something out of the old murals, tall and grim and lit from within by terrible purpose. "No crown sanctions this. no court will judge it, no law shields us. Four thieves, one dead king, and the greatest theft ever attempted." He almost smiled. "Let posterity call it what it is."

"Justice," Wren said.

"Thieves' justice," Dominic corrected, and this time the smile was real.

I blew out the candles. Outside the hospice window, the first flakes of the false winter drifted down through the lamplight, soft as ash, patient as snow.

Three days, by my count. Three days until Corvane's next delivery — the largest consignment of war-memory ever assembled, already crated in the vaults below, waiting for the descent.

Which meant we had three days to break into the most guarded place beneath the city and steal a death.

I had carried grief for nineteen years. I found, tucking the blue vial close against my heart, that I could carry this too.
