## 18. Everything We Carried

I have spent my life opening things that were not meant for me — letters, locks, ledgers, skulls. I thought myself an expert on what happens next: the small violence of it, the guilt that arrives on schedule like a collector. But nothing in the Vellum Guild's inventory prepared me for opening a king.

The root-vaults hummed around us like a hive of sleeping bees, except the honey was grief and the hive was a hundred years deep. Shelves upon shelves of sealed memories spiraled down into dark, each one warm to the touch. Each one addressed, in the Guild's neat clerk-hand, *To His Majesty, With Compliments.*

A century of compliments.

"Is he awake?" Wren whispered. She was pressed against my side, her hand cold in mine. Maren stood watch at the stair with Aldric, sword drawn, though we both knew steel would mean nothing down here.

"He's dreaming," I said. "Corvane made sure of that."

Because that was the horror of it, once I'd finally read the whole ledger — every page, every cipher, every careful margin-note in Corvane's own hand. The Thorn King had ended the last long winter by drinking down the realm's pain. Every grief, every terror, every memory too heavy for a human spine. He'd carried them all so no one else had to. And then the Guild — his own creation, his own mercy turned machine — had decided he should never wake up from it. So they fed him curated dreams. Gentle ones. A mother's lullaby here, a summer festival there, skimmed off the top of ordinary lives and poured into a sleeping mouth.

He never got to give anything back. He never even got to finish grieving. He'd swallowed a war's worth of sorrow and been put to bed with someone else's pleasant memories, forever.

And now Corvane wanted to feed him a new war instead. Wake him angry, aim him like a weapon at a world that had forgotten to be grateful.

"Dominic." Aldric's voice, low, from the stair. "Boots above. Many."

* * *

Corvane came down the last spiral alone, which was somehow worse than if he'd brought an army. He looked tired, actually tired, and I hated that I noticed it — hated that some treacherous part of me still remembered twenty years of being his best thief, his cleverest find, the orphan he'd taught to read ledgers and people both.

"You could have had everything," he said mildly, as though we were discussing inventory over tea. "I offered you a seat beside a god."

"You offered me a chair next to a hostage."

"A king, Dominic. The man who took the winter so we wouldn't have to." His voice warmed with genuine feeling, which was the most dangerous thing about him. "Every suffering since is because we let him sleep instead of serving him. Give him the world again, properly this time, and there will be no more grief anyone has to carry alone. That's not tyranny. That's arithmetic."

"It's theft," I said. "You'd know. You invented it."

Behind him, at the top of the stair, collectors filled the archway like crows on a fence. And between two of them, held up by the collar like a sack of turnips — Wren.

No. Wait. I blinked. Wren stood at my elbow, her hand still in mine. The girl in the collectors' grip was —

"Let me go," the other Wren said, calm as Sunday. "Please. You'll want to see this part."

Maren moved before any of us understood. Her knife was out and at the lead collector's throat, and she said, very quietly, "You carried my daughter's memory for eleven years. You know what I know now." And the collectors, gods bless their haunted hearts, hesitated — because every one of them was carrying something they'd taken from someone who missed it, and compassion moves through a hunting party faster than you'd think.

Wren slipped free. Both of her — the real one squeezing my fingers, and whatever the other one was, walking straight past Corvane to the dream-feed apparatus: a great brass throat of pipes and valves, gulping memory-seals down into the dark where the king slept.

"What are you?" Corvane demanded.

"I'm tomorrow," she said, "and you're yesterday, and I'm so tired of both of us." She wrenched the main valve.

The dream-feed died with a sound like a sigh.

* * *

For a moment, nothing. The humming stopped. The warmth went out of the shelves.

Then, from far below, through miles of root and stone, something vast rolled over in its sleep.

"No!" Corvane lunged for the valve, and Aldric caught him — the disgraced knight and the guildmaster, grappling like common drunks among the casks of stolen childhoods. Collectors surged forward. Maren met them, and Wren grabbed my sleeve and shouted, "Dominic! Now! Before he wakes up *hungry*!"

She was right. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. A waking Thorn King with a century of ungrieved pain and a head full of Corvane's war-dreams would not rise merciful. He would rise the way a debt comes due.

Unless someone paid it first.

I had planned this, in the way I plan everything — obsessively, at night, with diagrams. Pour the memories back. All of them. Not curated. Not gentle. Everything the vault held, returned at once, the way it should have been returned a hundred years ago. Let him grieve it all in one breath and be done. Let him finally die a man instead of living forever as a warehouse.

What I hadn't planned was how it would feel to stand at the master ledger-desk at the vault's heart, break its seal, and open every shelf in the room simultaneously.

"Dominic," Aldric gasped, still wrestling Corvane. "Don't—"

"Somebody has to keep the books," I said. "It's the only thing I'm good at."

I opened the ledger.

* * *

They didn't pour into him. They poured through *me* first.

Of course they did. I was the keeper; the records knew their clerk. For one endless moment I was everyone the Guild had ever robbed. I was a widow's thirty years of loving a man who snored. I was a soldier's last look at a sunrise. I was a child's terror of a cellar, a baker's pride in bread, a traitor's guilt, a midwife's joy, ten thousand funerals and forty thousand first kisses and every small shame anyone ever hoped to bury.

I felt all of it, and I did not break, because here is the secret they never tell you about carrying other people's burdens: they weigh less when you're setting them down than when you picked them up.

I gave it all to him.

Down through the roots it went, a river of everything, and the Thorn King woke the way a drowning man surfaces — convulsing, gasping, *feeling*. I felt his vast attention close around the flood like hands around water. I felt him try to sort it, order it, do what kings do with burdens: carry them.

"Don't carry it," I told him, my voice cracking across the dark. "It was never yours to keep. It was only yours to hold until we learned to hold our own. You can put it down now. You've carried it long enough."

And the Thorn King — thorn-crowned, ancient, half-made of winter — began to weep.

Not the weeping of a god. The weeping of a man. A hundred years of tears compressed into moments, grief after grief after grief passing through him like weather, and with each one he grew smaller. Not lesser. *Smaller.* The way a mountain becomes a hill becomes a grave-marker becomes a stone becomes a man. The thorns receded. The frost fell away like a shed cloak.

Corvane stopped fighting. He stood staring down into the dark, and the flood of truth rising from the vault touched him too — every lie he'd told himself about mercy and arithmetic coming home at once, itemized. I watched a lifetime of self-deception get audited in real time. He didn't scream. He just... folded, the way a ledger folds shut, and sat down among the casks with his head in his hands, a man unmade by honest bookkeeping.

The Thorn King knelt — a king, kneeling, to a thief, and I suppose neither of us saw the irony until later. Or maybe we did. Maybe irony was the last thing either of us had left.

"You kept the records," he said. His voice was almost human now. Almost *tired*.

"Someone had to."

"You gave them back." Wonder, in it. Genuine wonder. "No one ever thought to simply... give them back."

"We were busy inventing reasons not to."

He smiled. It was a good smile, worn smooth by centuries. "Thank you," he said, and meant it, and then he lay back against the roots that had held him, and closed his eyes, and this time there was nothing underneath the sleep but sleep.

He died the way snow stops falling: gradually, and then completely, and then it was spring.

* * *

Silence, afterward. Real silence, the kind the vault hadn't known in a century. The shelves stood empty around us, thousands of little doors hanging open like the mouths of birds.

Maren lowered her knife. The collectors, one by one, set down their seals and sat down too — hollowed out, blinking, men and women suddenly unemployed from their own worst selves. One of them started crying and couldn't stop, and nobody judged him, because we'd all been carrying something.

Aldric helped Corvane to his feet. I expected rage. Instead the old knight just said, "Come upstairs. There's work to do, and you're going to do it honestly or not at all."

Wren found my hand again in the dark.

"Did you see?" she asked. "At the end?"

"See what?"

"The other me. From the dreams." She smiled up at me, and there were tear-tracks on her face that I don't think were all hers. "She wasn't a ghost, Dominic. She was a receipt. Everything spent gets paid back eventually. Even futures." She squeezed my fingers. "Even ours."

Above us, faintly, through a hundred feet of stone and root, I heard the city bells begin to ring — not alarm, not yet. Something older than alarm. Something that sounded almost like morning.

I took out my pen.

Time, at last, to write down what actually happened.
