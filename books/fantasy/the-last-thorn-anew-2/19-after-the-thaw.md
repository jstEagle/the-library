## 19. After the Thaw

I woke up because the snow stopped.

Not gradually, the way snow is supposed to stop, all trailing off and apologetic. It stopped the way a door slams shut. One moment I could hear it hissing against the shutters of Maren's sister's attic — that dry, hungry sound it had made for nine days, like the world was being filed away flake by flake — and then nothing. Just silence, thick as wool, and a light through the gaps in the boards that was the wrong color entirely.

The right color. That's what I mean. Sunlight. Actual sunlight, gold instead of grey, falling across my face like it had been waiting its turn.

I lay there for a while not moving, because for nine days I had learned not to trust anything that felt good. Good feelings were how Corvane got people. Good feelings were bait. But nobody came up the ladder. Nobody's boots rang on the stair. And after a while I heard Aldric downstairs arguing with a kettle, which meant the kettle was winning, which meant it was an ordinary morning, or as close to one as we were ever going to get again.

I went down.

Dominic was at the window with a cup of something they were pretending to drink. They looked terrible in the way they always looked terrible after doing something enormous, which is to say: neat as a pin, hair combed, every button done, eyes like two holes burned in paper. When you spend a hundred years of stolen lives in one pour, I suppose your body copes by ironing itself flat.

"It stopped," I said.

"It stopped," Dominic said.

"Is he—"

"Yes." They said it quietly, without looking away from the street. "He's dead. Properly dead this time. He thanked me, Wren. Do you know what a king thanking a thief sounds like? Like a bell underwater."

I didn't say anything, because I'd dreamed him dying, back before any of this started — thorns blooming through snow, going brown, going soft, letting go of the ground. Dreams are funny things. You can carry them around for years thinking they're just weather inside your head, and then one day they turn out to be a map.

Aldric set down three cups of tea that were mostly kettle-scrapings and sat with us, and none of us talked, because some silences are the good kind, the kind you've earned. Outside, the light kept coming.

* * *

By midmorning the city had noticed.

We went out because Aldric insisted we needed bread and because I insisted I needed to see it, and Dominic didn't insist on anything, which from them is agreement. The streets were ankle-deep in melting snow, and the whole city was out in it like ants whose hill somebody kicked over, except happy-kicked, if that's a thing. People stood in doorways blinking at the sun like it owed them money. Children were making the first slushballs of the season. A fishmonger was shouting about prices in a voice so cheerful it was basically singing.

And everybody was saying the same thing, in different words, in the way crowds do:

*"Overnight,"* a woman told her neighbor. *"It broke overnight, just like that."*

*"My mother woke up remembering her wedding day,"* a man said outside the chandlery, and he wasn't complaining about it either, which confused me until I understood. *"She hasn't sung since I was small."*

*"They're saying the Thorn King's woken up."*

*"They're saying he's dead."*

*"They're saying the guild did it."*

*"They're saying the guild's been robbed blind."*

That last one was the one that mattered, though nobody knew how much yet. We heard it first from a lamplighter on Tanner's Row, who told Aldric — because everyone tells Aldric things, he has that kind of face, like a locked door people trust anyway — that the Vellum Guildhouse had its doors standing open since dawn, and no guards, and lights burning in rooms where lights never burned.

So of course we went.

* * *

The guildhouse looked wrong the way a stage looks wrong when the play's over and they've left the sets standing. I'd only ever seen it from outside, and only ever quickly, the way you look at a dog you think might be the biting kind. From the inside — well. There wasn't much inside anymore.

The vaults under the main hall had been opened. Every shelf. Every case. Every little lead-and-glass reliquary where the guild kept its inventory of other people's lives, labeled in that thin clerk's hand I'd spent eight months watching Dominic practice and un-practice: *Grief, bottled, vintage unspecified. Childhood, ages four through fourteen, slight water damage. Courage, one measure, owner deceased.* All of it gone. Not stolen — the locks weren't broken, the cases weren't scratched. *Emptied.* Carefully. Reverently, almost. Somebody had carried out ten thousand memories in the night and left the shelves dusted behind them.

"They'll be found," Dominic said, running a finger along a bare shelf like they were reading braille. "Every one of them. On doorsteps. In coat pockets. Under pillows. I mapped it all out on the walk here." They glanced at me, and for half a second the paper-burn holes in their eyes crinkled, which from Dominic is a grin. "The widow on Candlemaker's Lane gets her husband's last year. The cooper on Bell Street gets back the daughter who moved away and never wrote. There's a cobbler who's been missing his own name since '61. He'll wake up tomorrow knowing exactly who he is, and he'll never know why, and that's the point."

"The point being?" Aldric asked, though I think he knew.

"That you can rob a whole city," Dominic said, "or you can give it back, and either way nobody sees your face. I've spent my life proving the first half." They looked around the emptied vault, at the humming absence of it, the way the air felt lighter, like the building itself had put down a weight. "Seemed time to prove the second."

There were clerks drifting through the halls, pale as candles, touching empty shelves. One of them, a young man with ink-stained cuffs, saw the three of us standing there — a disgraced knight, a memory-thief, and an eight-year-old girl, which must have looked like the world's least threatening invasion — and he didn't raise any alarm. He just said, "Did you feel it? This morning. When it broke." And when we nodded, he said, "I've worked here eleven years and I never once wondered what we were keeping all this *for*. Isn't that something? Eleven years." Then he went back to touching the shelves, gently, like they were sleeping animals.

People keep asking me if I remember everything from those days. Mostly yes. Some things I sold, and those are gone, and I've made my peace with the arithmetic. But I remember that young clerk, because he was the first person I ever saw understand the guild and grieve it in the same breath, and I would spend a lot of the next few months learning exactly how that felt.

* * *

Here's the thing about getting a year of your future taken out of you: you don't feel the hole. That's the trick of it, the cruelty and the kindness both. You just have a year-shaped space where a year should go, and everyone else walks around inside their own years, and you nod along, and sometimes someone says "remember when" and you say "of course" and hope they don't test you.

Wren, age nine, whoever she turned out to be — I haven't met her yet. She's out there somewhere being lived by me, or not, depending on how the spending went. I bought us that night in the hospice with her, and I'd do it again, and also I'd like five minutes alone with her to explain myself, and also I never want her to know. All three of those at once. Being a person is complicated; I recommend it anyway.

What I'm telling you is that when the thaw came, I couldn't feel it the way everyone else could. Everyone kept saying the city felt lighter, and I believed them, but it was like believing in the far side of a wall. Until the evening, three days later, when we were eating stew in Maren's sister's kitchen and an old woman knocked on the door.

She had a parcel wrapped in oilcloth. Inside was a small glass case, guild-made, and inside that was a memory, and the label read: *For the house of Maren Collier. With thanks.*

Maren hadn't come to the kitchen that night — she'd gone back to the collectors' barracks to resign in whatever way collectors resign, probably loudly — but her sister knew her handwriting on the address and went white as porridge. Because Maren had a daughter once. Before the guild. Before the collecting. A little girl called Sef who died of the lung-rot at six, and Maren had sold her grief over her to the guild because she couldn't afford to keep carrying it, and that was the trade that made her a collector in the first place: sell the guild your worst day, and they'll hire you to take everyone else's.

The old woman said a stranger had pressed it into her hands at the market and asked her to deliver it, paid her a copper for her trouble, and walked off toward the river. She didn't describe the stranger. I checked her face very carefully anyway, and there was nothing there to check, which is its own answer.

Maren came home at dark. She held the case in both hands for a long time without opening it, and the kitchen got very quiet, and even the fire seemed to lean in. Then she opened it, and the memory rose up out of the glass like steam, and it went into her, and Maren Collier — who I had only ever seen with a collector's face, flat and patient and tired — put her hand over her mouth and made a sound I hope I never learn to describe properly.

Afterward she sat down and said, "Sef laughed at everything. I'd forgotten that. Not forgotten-forgotten. It was more like it was in a drawer." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, businesslike. "Whoever did this — and don't tell me you don't know, the three of you have guilt written on you like a shop sign — whoever did this gave me back my daughter laughing. So." She looked at each of us in turn, and last and longest at Dominic. "The guild will come apart now. Corvane's gone, the vaults are empty, the ledgers are ash — don't look surprised, someone burned the archive wing, and it wasn't ghosts. There are forty collectors with no work and a hundred clerks with no purpose and a city full of people who just found out memories can be given back instead of taken." She folded her arms. "Somebody ought to do something about that. Somebody with experience."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Aldric, who has the tactical instincts of a siege engine and the subtlety of one too, said, "You're volunteering us."

"I'm volunteering *somebody*," Maren said. "I simply happen to be looking at them."

* * *

Later that night I climbed back up to the attic, because I do my best thinking in attics, it's the slope of the ceiling, it makes thoughts sit still. Below me I could hear Dominic and Aldric and Maren talking in low voices about charters and oaths and whether a guild could legally give things away, which is the sort of question that sounds boring and turns out to be the most important question anyone's ever asked, and I listened to it the way you listen to rain: not the words, the sound of it. The sound of people building something.

And I thought about the year I'd spent. Somewhere out there, future-me was living it — or wasn't — and I decided, lying there under the sloped ceiling with the thaw dripping off the eaves like the whole sky was counting, that I wasn't sad about it. Scared, maybe. Sad is for things you've lost. This I'd *given*, and there's a difference, and the difference is everything, and it took a guild full of thieves and a knight with no order and a king made of thorns to teach me that.

But here's the part I haven't told anyone yet, the part I'm telling you now because you're a page and pages can't interrupt.

When the old woman handed over the parcel, when the memory-case changed hands between us, I touched it — just brushed it, barely, the way I do — and for half a heartbeat I was *inside* the delivery, inside the stranger who'd carried it through the market crowd.

Grey cloak. Hood up. Hands I knew better than my own, because I've watched them write ledgers by candlelight for months.

But that's not the thing. Dominic delivering memories door to door, fine, obvious, I'd have bet the rest of my tomorrows on it.

The thing is what I felt in that half-second of borrowed skin. The stranger was *happy*. Not pleased-with-themselves happy, not smug. Happy the way you're happy when a fever breaks. And underneath the happiness, threaded through it like wire, was something I'd never felt in anyone before, and it took me until the eaves stopped dripping to name it.

Relief. Enormous, bottomless relief — the relief of someone putting down a weight they'd carried so long they'd mistaken it for a spine.

Except. Here's the thing. Here's the thing that kept me awake until the birds started.

In that half-second, the stranger was counting. Not coins. Memories. I felt the tally turning over behind their eyes, item by item, the way Dominic counts everything, and the tally wasn't finished. Ten thousand delivered, give or take. And at the bottom of the count, one entry left, marked and set aside, the way you save the best bite for last.

One memory. Unlabeled. Undelivered.

And I felt, clear as a bell underwater, whose it was.

Mine.

Tomorrow morning, I'm going downstairs early, before anyone else is up, and I'm going to find out why.
