## 21. What Wren Chose to Keep

The new guildhouse smelled different than the old one. That was the first thing I noticed, standing in the doorway of the memory-room with my hands in my pockets like a girl who had nowhere better to be and all the time in the world to not be there.

The old guildhouse — Corvane's guildhouse, the Vellum Guild as it had been for a hundred years — had smelled of vellum and sealing wax and underneath it something sweetish and wrong, like fruit going bad in a locked room. I'd never noticed it when I lived there. You don't notice the smell of a cage when you've never been outside one. But now I knew what freedom smelled like: woodsmoke and Aldric's terrible cooking and the rose Dominic planted that was already, stubbornly, trying to bloom out of season.

This new place smelled of all of that, plus ink. Fresh ink. The ledgers here were written by hand every day, in ordinary ink, about ordinary trades. *One hour of a mother's lullaby, returned to a weaver who'd sold it in a famine year. One summer afternoon, given freely to a man dying of the coughing sickness so he could leave his granddaughter something warm.* Nobody sealed anything down in the deep dark anymore. Everything sat on open shelves, labeled honestly, waiting to go home.

And me? I was standing in front of the shelf where my own year was kept.

They'd recovered most of it, after. When the vaults flooded back into the world — when Dominic poured a century of stolen lives into the Thorn King and let him finally, finally die — some of what came loose came loose in pieces. Grief came home to the grieving. Love found its lovers. And somewhere in the great unraveling, a small glass vial with my name on it had surfaced, holding everything I'd paid at the hospice to buy us one more night.

A year of my future memories. Gone before they ever happened, spent like a coin tossed to a ferryman.

Except now here it was, sitting on an open shelf in honest light, and Maren was saying, "You only have to say yes," and Dominic was saying nothing at all, because Dominic had learned when a choice belonged to somebody else, and Ser Aldric was pretending very hard to inventory a shelf across the room where there was absolutely nothing left to inventory.

"You understand what's being offered," Maren said. She always talked to me like I was a person, which I appreciated, even when she talked to me like a person at a funeral. "The restoration is clean. Better than clean — it's been resting in the king's light, if you believe Aldric's theories, and I don't, but it's whole. Every day of that year. You'd wake up tomorrow remembering things that haven't happened yet."

"That's the part that worries me," I said.

"It should worry you. Prophecy isn't a gift, Wren. It's a debt." Maren's face did something complicated. She carried her daughter's memory still — she'd chosen to keep that one, the hard way, the honest way — and I think sometimes she looked at me and saw both of us at once. "You'd know your own death coming. Most people can't carry that."

"I already know things come true," I said. "That's never been the problem."

Dominic looked over then. Just looked. They were good at that — asking a question without spending any words on it. It used to annoy me. Now it felt like being handed a chair when my legs were tired.

"The problem," I said, because they'd asked, "is that I liked who I was when I didn't have it back."

* * *

Let me explain, because everyone kept looking at me like I was eight years old and confused, instead of eight years old and exactly right.

Before all this — before the ledger and the thorns and the night the snow fell wrong — I was the guild's little oracle. They kept me fed and dressed and called it kindness, and in exchange I dreamed their futures for them and never got told what the futures were *for*. I dreamed thorns through snow and woke up in a cold sweat and some collector wrote it down and filed it under *assets*, and nobody ever asked me whether I wanted to be an asset. I had flashes. I had no story. A flash is just lightning; a story is the storm, and the storm is what tells you where you're standing.

Then came the hospice, and the price, and the year I poured out of myself like water from a jug. And here is the thing nobody warned me about, maybe because nobody had ever done it on purpose before: when I gave that year away, I didn't just lose days. I lost the shape of them. And into that empty shape, the journey poured.

I remembered Dominic teaching me to pick locks with two hairpins and infinite patience, and how they'd laughed — actually laughed, that awful rusty-hinge laugh of theirs — when I picked the third one faster than they had. I remembered Aldric carrying me through the flooded tunnels with his armor off because he said metal sang too loud, and his heart hammering under my ear the whole way, steady as a drum in a march he refused to admit he was marching. I remembered the market of impossible things, selling mercies to strangers, and the old woman who cried when she heard her dead husband's voice again and pressed a copper into my hand like I'd made him from scratch. I remembered the fire. Our fire. The anniversary night, the rose in the ground, the page of prophecy curling into ash, and all of us around it like a family someone had assembled out of spare parts and sheer stubbornness.

None of those things happened inside the year I spent. But grief doesn't check dates. The hollow where my year should have been filled up with everything that mattered, the way a river fills a channel dug for some other purpose entirely. I was a girl with a hole in her, and the hole turned out to be the exact size of my life.

If I took the year back — every restored morning, every predicted rain, every foreseen goodbye — the shape would fill in properly. Neatly. And all that other stuff would be pushed to the edges, the way proper furniture pushes a blanket fort out of a parlor. I'd be whole again, people said. Complete. I'd stop flinching at doorways. I'd stop waking up counting who was still alive.

I'd also stop knowing, bone-deep, why I counted.

* * *

"So what do you want to keep?" Maren asked, when I finally said it out loud. Not argued it — said it. There's a difference, and Maren was the first adult in my life who seemed to know it.

We'd moved to the table by the window, where the light came in honest and grey. Dominic sat across from me with the vial between us, turning it slowly, watching the memory catch the light like oil on water. Aldric had given up pretending and just stood behind my chair, which was his way of hovering without admitting to hovering. Knights are terrible at retirement.

"All of it?" Maren pressed gently. "Or parts?"

"Parts," I said. Then, because that wasn't quite true either: "One part."

"One."

"The journey." I put my hand flat on the table, next to but not touching the vial. "Everything from the night I ran until the morning the winter broke. Not the year I spent — I don't want the year back, I don't want to know what rains were coming or which friendships were doomed. I want the part I made while I was paying. The walking. The hiding. The laughing in the tunnels when Aldric slipped on the ice and swore in three languages. The market. The fire." I looked up at Dominic. "I want to keep choosing it."

Dominic went very still, the way they did when something landed deeper than expected. "Wren," they said carefully, "the year is yours. All of it. Nobody here thinks less of you for taking it back. Honestly, half of us would sleep better."

"I know." I grinned at them, because I'd learned grinning from watching them lie, and mine was better. "But you also taught me that a ledger only means something if the entries are true. And the true entry reads: *spent willingly.* If I take it back, the entry changes. It becomes *recovered,* and recovered sounds like something that happened to me. Spent is something I did." I tapped the table. "I did it to save you. Both of you. I'm not erasing the receipt."

Aldric made a sound behind me that might have been a laugh strangled by honor. "She negotiates like the guildmaster's ghost," he muttered.

"Worse," Dominic said. "Corvane never had a point."

So we did it there, at the window table, with Maren's careful hands and the honest grey light. She drew the thread of the journey-memories out of the vial — they'd knitted themselves to the year somehow, tangled in like roots through soil, and pulling them free took patience and a needle-thin focus and twice made Aldric pace to the window and back — and she laid them out in a ribbon of pale gold that hummed when I breathed near it.

"Last chance," she said. "Once these settle, they stay."

"Settle them," I said.

It didn't hurt. That surprised me. I'd thought getting memories back would feel like being refilled, and instead it felt like being recognized. Like coming home to a house where someone had kept the fire lit. The tunnels, the hairpins, the swearing in three languages, the old woman's copper coin warm in my palm, the fire and the ash and the rose — all of it settled back into me, not filling the hole but lining it, the way a good boot lines a foot that's walked far enough to deserve the wear.

The rest — the year itself, the unlived mornings, the foresights and forebodings — Maren resealed and set on an open shelf, labeled in her neat hand: *Whitlock, W. One year, unspent. Held in trust. Owner's choice.*

Not destroyed. Not hidden. Just... waiting. Because I'm eight, and eight is young, and maybe someday I'll walk past that shelf and change my mind, and the new guild — our guild, the one that gives — will hand it over without a single question asked. That's the difference between keeping and hoarding. Keeping leaves the door open.

* * *

That evening Aldric burned dinner, which was traditional, and we ate it anyway, which was also traditional. Dominic dealt out the evening's returns list — three memories to go home tomorrow, all small ones, all precious — and I sat on the floor with my back against Aldric's knee and felt my head full of a journey I'd gotten to keep.

"Do you regret it?" I asked Dominic, apropos of everything. "Giving the king all of it at once? A hundred years of everybody's worst days?"

Dominic considered the fire for a long moment. "He thanked us," they said finally. "Nobody thanks you for giving back their grief. But he did. So no. I regret plenty of things. Not that."

"And me?" I pressed, because I was feeling brave and full. "Do you regret me? What I spent?"

Dominic looked at me across the fire, and for once didn't reach for a wry answer. "You bought us the night we needed," they said. "I'd have paid it back with interest if you'd let me. Since you won't—" the rusty hinge creaked, almost a smile, "—I suppose I'll just have to keep you instead. Consider yourself an asset of the new guild."

"Filed under assets," I said solemnly. "*Kept, not taken.*"

Aldric snorted. Outside, the first real snow of the season was falling — ordinary snow, honest snow, snow that would melt in a week and come back next winter like snow was supposed to. And in my kept-and-chosen head, I could see the whole journey laid out bright, every tunnel and every mercy and every terrible meal, mine because I'd paid for it, mine because I'd said *keep*.

Tomorrow, Maren had said, we'd begin the winter returns — dozens of memories, a whole season of giving. And there was a name on tomorrow's list I hadn't seen yet, in handwriting I hadn't learned yet, for a trade that hadn't been dreamed yet.

But that was tomorrow's entry. Tonight, the ledger was true, and I was the one holding the pen-shaped end of it, and the fire was warm, and I chose this.
