## 22. The Last Thorn, Uprooted

A year ago tonight, I opened a ledger and found my own name waiting for me like a knife left on a pillow.

I'd like to tell you I've made peace with that. I haven't, exactly. I've made something adjacent to peace — the way you make a neighbor of a wolf, or a habit of a limp. Some nights I still wake at the hour I found that page and lie there listening to the city breathe, half-expecting to hear my name scratched into some new record somewhere, written by a hand that got there before mine.

But tonight is not one of those nights. Tonight I have dirt under my fingernails and rose hips in my pocket and three people I love arguing about soup behind me, and if that isn't peace, it's close enough that I've stopped checking the difference.

* * *

The ground where the root-vaults stood doesn't look like much now. That's the thing nobody tells you about cataclysm — how thoroughly it tidies up after itself. The vaults collapsed in on themselves the night the king died, all those centuries of stored memory exhaling at once, and the guildhouse above them settled down like an old man into a chair he's decided to die in. What's left is a sunken courtyard behind the ruined east wing: cracked flagstones, weeds with ambitions, and soil so rich it's almost obscene. A hundred years of grief makes excellent compost. There's a joke in there. Wren would make it. I'm choosing dignity instead, which she tells me is very funny of me.

We could have rebuilt on the old foundations. Corvane's vaults had been stripped bare — every memory returned, every shelf emptied by our own hands, anonymously, in the small hours while the city slept through the first morning of its thaw — but the stones remembered what they'd held. Aldric said you can't scrub a place like that clean; you can only decide what it means going forward. So we decided. We let the old guildhouse stand as a ruin, a warning with moss on it, and we built the new guild two streets over, above a bakery, because Maren insisted that any honest trade should smell like bread.

But this courtyard — this we kept. Not as a memorial. As a garden.

"You're brooding," Wren announced from atop the low wall, where she perched the way she always did, knees to her chest, watching me work the soil. She's nine now. She carries her year of spent tomorrows lightly, the way you carry a scar you chose — not proudly, exactly, but without apology. "You do your brooding face. The one where your eyebrows are doing arithmetic."

"My eyebrows are resting."

"Your eyebrows never rest. They keep the ledgers even when you sleep." She hopped down and squatted beside the hole I'd dug, inspecting it with the gravity of a foreman. "It's crooked."

"It's *organic*."

"It's crooked *organically*." She reached into my pocket without asking, because eight years of being property teaches you nothing about boundaries, and pulled out the rose hip, turning it over in her fingers. It was a hard little thing, red-brown, unremarkable. Most people walked past rose hips all winter without a second glance. Most people had no idea what slept inside one. "You really think it'll grow?"

"I think thorns grew here for a thousand years out of pure spite," I said. "I think a rose has decent odds."

She considered this, then nodded once, satisfied, and handed it back like returning a sword.

Behind us, the argument about soup had resolved itself into the argument about whether Aldric was allowed to put salt in the soup, which was a proxy war in a conflict nearly as old as our friendship. He emerged from the ruin's doorway carrying the pot himself, which was his way of winning, and set it near the fire pit we'd built from the fallen lintel of the old vault door. There was a poetry to that I appreciated and he pretended not to notice. Ser Aldric Vane, last knight of an order that had died keeping a terrible secret, now cooked dinner for thieves and children over the bones of the thing he'd helped bury. If his penance had a shape, it was ladle-shaped, and he bore it without complaint.

Mostly without complaint.

"The salt," he said to me, by way of greeting, "is a matter between a man and his soup."

"It is," I agreed. "And between him and everyone who has to eat it."

He gave me the look — the one that used to be knightly disapproval and had weathered, over the months, into something more like fondness with armor on. Then he sat down heavily on the wall and looked out over the courtyard, at the hole, at the fire, at the sky going the deep bruised purple of early spring evenings, and something in his shoulders came down an inch. It came down an inch every time he saw the sky now. A year ago the first snow of the new long winter had fallen out of season, and none of us had been sure the world would ever see green again. Old habits of dread take longer to thaw than rivers do.

"Maren's late," he said.

"She's bringing the wine."

"Then she's not late. She's *staged*."

* * *

I lit the fire while the light failed. Small fire. Deliberately small — big enough to warm hands, too small to read faces across, sized exactly for the four of us and no one else. I'd learned that from Wren, actually, the importance of right-sized things. A child who remembers futures learns early which moments deserve their full weight, and she had taught me, without ever meaning to, that most of life's real ceremonies happen around fires the size of cupped palms.

From my coat I took the page.

You know the one. You've heard enough of this story to know. The page from the century-old prophetic ledger, the one that had borne tonight's date and beneath it, in faded iron-gall ink, a single line: *Dominic Whitlock, keeper of the ledger both ways.* For a year it had lived in a drawer in my desk at the new guildhouse, because I couldn't burn it and I couldn't file it and I couldn't quite throw away the only object that had ever known me before I knew myself. I'd taken it out sometimes at night and read it like a letter from a stranger who understood me completely.

Tonight was its anniversary. Tonight, a year ago, Corvane had stood in his office with his terrible open hand extended, offering me a seat beside a waking god, and I had almost believed him — that's the part I don't say aloud often, the almost. He hadn't been a monster. That was the horror of him. He'd been a kind man with a catastrophic idea, the way so many monsters are, and when the truth of a century flooded back into the sleeping king below us and unmade him, I'd felt something worse than triumph. I'd felt grief. For the version of events where he'd been right. For the version of me who might have sat down next to him.

Wren watched me hold the page over the fire without lighting it. She didn't say hurry up. She just waited, the way she'd learned to wait, with her whole attention, like each moment was a guest worth receiving.

"Any last words?" Aldric asked dryly. "For the record?"

"It *was* the record," I said. "That was rather the problem."

I turned the page in my fingers one more time. The ink had faded further over the year, as if prophecy, having served its purpose, was content to become paper again. My name was still legible. Barely. Enough.

Here's what I'd finally understood, sometime around midwinter, shoveling snow off the new guildhouse steps while Maren complained about her boots: the page had never been a sentence. I'd read it as fate — *this will happen to you* — because that's what a keeper of records does, reads entries as verdicts. But prophecies are written in the grammar of possibility, and the difference between *will* and *may* is the whole width of a human life. The ledger had opened both ways, that first night, and I'd spent a year learning what that meant. It meant the record was never finished. It meant someone was always holding the pen.

I touched the corner of the page to the flame.

It caught reluctantly — old paper, treated against damp, the guild spared no expense on things meant to last — and then it went up all at once, curling gold at the edges, and I held it until the heat kissed my fingertips and let go. Ash lifted. A few flakes drifted back down and landed in the fresh-dug soil, which seemed fitting. Prophecy makes good compost too.

"Feel different?" Wren asked.

I took inventory, the way I still do, out of habit: hands steady, chest loose, no name itching anywhere under my skin. "Lighter."

"That's the wine," said Maren's voice from the doorway, "because I haven't given it to you yet."

* * *

She came across the courtyard with a bottle in each hand and the walk of a woman who had stopped pretending to be anything other than what she was, which was the finest collector the guild had ever produced, retired. She'd kept exactly one stolen memory after everything ended — the one of her daughter, the one she'd carried for years without knowing whose grief she was holding — and she'd returned it to herself in front of witnesses, which is how the new guild handles such things now. Everything done in daylight. Everything answerable. She says the transparency rules are insufferable. She also wrote half of them.

"Vane," she said, handing him a bottle, "you've let her sit on the wall again. Her boots are filthy."

"My boots are *sovereign*," said Wren.

"There it is," Aldric murmured. "The foreign policy of an eight-year-old."

"Nine," said Wren, with the weariness of the chronically miscounted.

Maren settled beside me on the wall and we passed the bottles around, and for a while nobody said anything important, which is the truest sign of company worth keeping. The fire ticked. Somewhere beyond the ruin, the city went about its evening — cart wheels, a dog with opinions, the bakery's ovens banking down for the night two streets over, filling the dark with the smell of tomorrow's bread. A year ago tonight, this city had been three days from tearing itself apart over memories of a war it barely remembered. Tonight it was arguing about grain tariffs. I have never loved a civic dispute more.

"Entry for the new ledger?" Maren asked eventually. It had become a ritual of hers, needling me. "Let's hear it. *Item: one rose bush, planted over the grave of a god. Condition: hopeful.*"

"*Provenance: stolen,*" I said. "*Like everything worth having.*"

The hip I'd planted wasn't stolen, actually — that was the joke underneath the joke. I'd grown it myself, from cuttings Maren had brought back from her daughter's village, from a hedge that had survived the long winter the first time, a hundred years ago. A plant that had already outlived one frozen age and come up flowering anyway. If anything in this world knew how to wait out winters and keep its thorns about it, it was that bloodline.

Aldric refilled cups. Wren narrated the fire like a sporting event. And when the coals had settled into that deep orange quiet, I knelt at the edge of the hole and set the rose hip in the earth.

It's a strange thing, planting. You dig a small grave and call it a beginning. You put a thing in the dark and trust the dark to give it back changed. I thought, as I covered it, of the king — of the vast thorn-crowned figure in the root-vaults, fed a curated dream for a century so that his sleep would be gentle and his waking would be convenient for other men. Of the moment we broke the feed and poured everything back into him at once: every grief, every guilt, every love the guild had siphoned away to keep him dreaming. He had wept. I will carry that sound to whatever grave I'm bound for — a sound like a mountain remembering it had once been rain. *Thank you,* he'd said, in a voice that moved the dust of centuries. *Now I can stop.* And he had died the way the merciful die, which is gratefully, and the false winter had broken within the hour, and the world had woken lighter without knowing why.

He had taken every painful memory in the realm to end the first long winter, and never given them back, because giving them back meant feeling them. I understood him better than I wanted to. Keeping is easy. Returning costs. But the cost, we'd learned — all of us, the thief and the knight and the child and the collector — is the only currency that buys anything real.

"There," Wren said softly, when I'd tamped the soil flat. She was kneeling beside me now, her small hands pressing the earth down too, solemn as a coronation. "Now it knows where home is."

"Do roses know that?"

"Everything knows that. That's why ghosts stay. That's why the king stayed." She dusted off her knees. "Things stay where they're loved wrong until somebody loves them right, and then they can leave."

I looked at her. Nine years old, carrying a year of purchased tomorrows, saying things like that around a mouthful of bread crust. The guild had kept her in a room and mined her dreams, and she had come out of it with more grace than any of us deserved, and if you told me she was the reason we'd won — well. You wouldn't be wrong. But she'd tell you differently. She'd tell you it was the soup, or the salt, or the day Maren let her go, or the night Aldric knelt to a dying king and asked pardon and received it. Found families are like that. Everyone remembers being saved by everyone else, and the arithmetic never balances, and thank the gods for it.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down to a red heart and the stars were out in their full indifferent splendor, Maren and Aldric argued companionably about whether the courtyard should have benches (she was pro; he maintained that ruins should offer nowhere comfortable to linger, on principle), and Wren fell asleep against Aldric's shoulder mid-sentence, the way children do, abandoning her own story like a coat she'd get later. He didn't move. He sat very still, the way he always did when she slept on him, a man guarding something with his whole body and pretending it was nothing.

I stayed by the grave a moment longer.

Tomorrow I'd go back to the guildhouse above the bakery, and there would be the usual queue — the widow wanting to return her husband's anger so she could keep his tenderness, the old soldier trading his nightmares for someone's wedding day, all the impossible trades we broker now, honestly, in daylight, with receipts. Tomorrow Wren would sit at the front desk because she refused to do anything else, telling frightened strangers which of their memories were worth keeping, and she'd be right, because she usually is. Tomorrow Aldric would train the new escorts not just in arms but in mercy, and Maren would audit our books with terrifying gentleness, and somewhere a stranger would receive back a piece of themselves they'd forgotten they'd lost, and weep, and never know whose hands had carried it home.

But tonight there was just the fire, and the planted dark, and my family breathing around it.

I thought about writing it all down — the new ledger entry, the one Maren had asked for. *Anniversary of the page. Burned the prophecy. Planted the thorn's opposite.* I'd write it tomorrow, with proper ink, because some things should be recorded carefully. But the true entry, the one that would never fit in any book, was simpler:

*Nothing happened tonight that was written beforehand. Nobody's name arrived ahead of them. We were merely here, together, warm, and it was enough, and it was ours.*

The rose will come up in the spring. Thorns and all. Let it.

Some winters, you learn, aren't weather at all — they're just the time it takes to plant what you mean to keep.
