## 1. Charms and Cinders

The pigeons told Ines Galloway everything worth knowing in Vellamarch, and that morning they told her the sky was wrong.

*Grey smoke from the east, grey smoke from the west,* they muttered from their perches along the fishmonger's awning, preening and shifting. *Grey smoke where there should be no smoke at all.*

She kept her eyes down and her hands busy, threading a copper wire through a hagstone while she listened, because a woman who stared at birds was a woman who drew attention, and attention was the one coin Ines had spent twelve years learning never to carry.

"Charms for the lovelorn," she called instead, her voice pitched to the bright, harmless register of a market huckster. "Charms against bad milk and worse husbands. Charms to keep the moths from your wool and your mother-in-law from your door."

They came, as they always came. The charm trade in Vellamarch ran on hope and superstition in equal measure, and Ines sold both by the handful. A knotwork bracelet to ease a birth—knots were only knots, but the midwife swore by them, and the swearing did most of the work. A sachet of dried lavender and salt against nightmares. A painted pebble for luck at cards, which worked exactly as often as any other pebble.

What she could not sell, what she had never once sold in twelve years behind this stall, was the truth: that when she pressed a stone into a farmer's palm and murmured over it, the mice in the granary wall heard every word, and if she asked them kindly—and sometimes she did, quietly, for a crust of bread or a promise—they would leave his stores alone until spring.

That gift had no price tag. It had a pyre.

She felt the rats before she saw them, a prickling along the baseboards of the world. They came up through the drain grating near her feet as the morning crowd thickened, three of them, lean and quick-eyed, and they sat on their haunches and looked at her with the frank hunger of beggars who know a soft touch when they've found one.

*The tall ones in black came through the Shambles before dawn,* said the boldest, whiskers trembling. *They carried fire in little glass bottles. They marked doors.*

Ines's hands did not stop moving. Twelve years of practice lived in her hands. She wound the wire, smiled at a woman buying a fertility charm, took the coppers, made change, and only then, low under the noise of the market, said: "Marked whose doors?"

*The ones that smell of old magic. Yours smells of nothing.* The rat's nose wrinkled. *Almost nothing.*

*Almost* was a word Ines did not like. She fed them anyway—a heel of yesterday's bread broken small—and sent them off with a warning to spread through the walls like spilled water: tell everyone, tell everyone, keep out of sight today.

Then she stood, brushed flour dust from her apron, and tried to look like a woman with nothing more dangerous in her than a jar of dried nettle.

* * *

Rell came at noon, as Rell always came.

Captain of Witch-Hunters now, boots polished to a mirror, silver badge at her throat shaped like a thorn crossed with a sword—the crown's new emblem, the queen's new emblem, stamped on every gallows notice in the city since spring. But Ines still saw, beneath all that iron discipline, the girl she'd grown up with in the river villages: the girl who used to wade into the shallows after her, laughing, calling her *Galloway the goose-girl* because Ines could always call the geese home without shouting.

Ines had not called anything home in twelve years. And she had not spoken more than ten words to Rell since the day the first Hunter wagons rolled into Vellamarch and the burning started.

"Lady Merchant." Rell stopped at her stall, gloved hand resting lightly on the wood, and began the inspection that was really something else entirely. Her eyes went over the charms one by one—hagstones, knotwork, lavender, painted pebbles—with the unhurried thoroughness of a woman reading a letter twice. "New stock?"

"Same stock. Luck doesn't change much."

"No." Rell picked up a hagstone, turned it, set it down. Her fingers lingered a half-second too long on the knotwork bracelet, and Ines watched her thumb trace the pattern and remembered—hated remembering—that Rell's grandmother had tied knots like these, back when knots were just knots and nobody burned for thread. "No, it doesn't. Though I'm told some of it does."

"The market's full of liars, Captain. I only sell them rope and stones."

Something moved behind Rell's eyes—not suspicion exactly; suspicion would have been simpler. It was the look of a woman standing at a door she has decided not to open, and holding the handle anyway, just to feel its weight.

"The queen's edict extends to charms," Rell said quietly. "Harmless trickery is tolerated. Anything else is treason."

"Then arrest me for my prices."

For a moment—one breath, two—Rell almost smiled. It was an old smile, half-buried, like a path grown over. Then it went, and she tapped the stall once with two fingers, a farewell or a warning, and walked on down the row to inspect the tinker's cart.

Ines let out a breath she felt in her ribs.

Above her, a pigeon dropped onto the awning strut and cocked its head. *She knows you,* it observed.

"She knows who I used to be," Ines murmured. "There's a difference."

But her hands had gone cold around the copper wire, and all afternoon the word *almost* sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

They torched the market quarter at dusk.

There was no warning siren, no herald, none of the grim theater the Hunts usually staged. Just a whistle from the east end, and then the glass bottles came arcing over the rooftops trailing orange ribbons, and the Shambles went up like dry wheat. Ines heard it before she understood it—the roar, not of flame, but of a crowd becoming a stampede—and then the light found her stall, red and hungry, and she was moving.

She grabbed the strongbox. She left everything else—the lavender, the hagstones, eleven years of careful, invisible commerce—to the flames. Around her the market screamed itself inside out: a pie-seller beating sparks from her apron, a horse tearing free of its traces, Hunters in black moving through the smoke with hooked poles and torches, unhurried, methodical, terrible. Somewhere a voice was reciting names. She didn't stay to hear whether hers was among them.

Her cellar was four streets back, down a stair behind a chandler's shop, a rented hole she'd kept for exactly this kind of night—dark, windowless, smelling of tallow and old rain. She bolted the door, lit no lamp, and sat in the dark with her knees drawn up, listening to the city burn above her and telling herself: *they're burning the stalls, not the sellers; trickery is tolerated; you are nothing; you are almost nothing.*

She might have believed it, too, if not for the sound at the door.

Not a knock. A scraping. A long, dragging weight pulling itself across the threshold boards, followed by breathing—wet, labored, wrong. Ines pressed herself flat against the far wall. The bolt rattled once. Twice. Then the door shuddered and gave, and what came through it into her cellar was a wolf.

A real wolf—grey as river fog, big as a calf—and it was dying. She could see it in the way its flanks heaved, in the dark blood sheeting its foreleg, in the arrow shaft broken off high in its shoulder. It should not have gotten past the city gates. It should not have survived the streets. Yet here it was, and when it lifted its head and looked at her, its eyes were not animal eyes. They were old, and they were certain, and they knew her.

"*Speaker.*"

The word arrived not in her ears but somewhere deeper, the way beast-speech always came, and Ines's whole body flinched from it like a hand pulled from a flame. Twelve years. Twelve years of silence, of *almost nothing*, and the first wild voice to find her in all that time found her bleeding on her floor.

"I'm not—" she started, uselessly. "I can't help you. If they find you here—"

"They already found me." The wolf dragged itself closer, leaving a smear of blood on her clean floor, and Ines hated herself for noticing the floor, hated the part of her still calculating, still hiding. "*Listen.* There is no time. They are not hunting speakers tonight. They are hunting *this*."

It raised its wounded foreleg, and Ines saw then what had been pressed into the matted fur above its paw: a seed. Small as an acorn, dark as a coal—but not dead coal, *living* coal, veined underneath with a slow ember-glow that pulsed like a heartbeat. When it touched the air of the cellar, the candle stubs on her shelf guttered though there was no draft, and the rats in the walls went silent as graves.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"The last of the Thornheart. What the kingdom thought it burned. What the queen dreams of swallowing whole." The wolf's breathing was shortening, each breath a torn thing. "*Take it. Carry it beyond the mountains, to the ruined grove where the old oaks fell. Plant it before the solstice, and the wild law wakes again—or the queen takes it, and nothing wild ever lives free in this land again.*"

"I don't—I'm not the one you want. I sell painted pebbles. I'm no one."

"You are Ines Galloway," the wolf said, "who spoke to the geese at Millbrook ford, and the geese still speak your name."

The world tilted. That was a lifetime ago, a child's life, a village that had learned to look away from her family and then away from her. No one living should have known it. No one living *did*.

The wolf lowered its great head and nudged the seed against her folded hands, pressing, insistent, and the ember-veins flared hot against her palms—not painfully, but with a heat like recognition, like a key finding its lock. She felt it settle into her grip as if it had always been meant to rest there.

"*Carry it,*" the wolf breathed. "*Speaker. Ines.*"

And then, with her name in its mouth—her true name, the one no one in Vellamarch had ever used—the light went out of those old eyes, and the wolf was done.

Ines knelt in the tallow-smelling dark beside a body too large to hide and a seed that burned like a held secret, and above her the market quarter crackled and roared, and somewhere in that roar, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots on the stair outside her door.

Twelve years of being almost nothing.

The bolt rattled.
