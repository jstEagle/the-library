## 2. What the Wolves Remember

Ines ran through the burning dark with a seed that beat against her ribs like a second heart.

She had gone out the cellar's coal chute, down the reeking alley where she'd once bought eels and stolen apples as a girl of thirteen, and out through the tannery gate before the Witch-Hunters' fire had finished eating the market quarter. Behind her, Vellamarch glowed like a forge. The smoke tasted of charred cedar and something sweeter underneath—burnt feathers, burnt fur, things she refused to name. The charms in her satchel clinked and rattled: knotted red thread, river-stones drilled with holes, dried foxglove in waxed paper twists. Twelve years of harmless trickery. Twelve years of being nobody.

The Thornheart made that impossible now. She could feel it through her shirt, warm as a sleeping bird, and it pulled—not toward the city gates everyone would watch, but north, along the drainage culvert, toward the fen road where the mud swallowed hoofbeats and the willows grew thick enough to hide a woman and her guilt.

The wolf's last words kept returning to her. *Take it to the grove beyond the mountains. Before the solstice. They are not hunting witches, Ines Galloway. They are hunting the seed.*

She had asked it—she could still hear herself asking, voice cracking like a girl's—*how do you know my name?*

*The beasts never forgot you,* it had said. *You were the one who forgot.*

Now, wading hip-deep through fen water with reeds whispering over her head, she understood what it meant. The pull of the seed was not direction alone. It was attention. Every living thing along the fen road turned toward her as she passed—a heron standing too long in the shallows, its yellow eye fixed; a family of otters surfacing to watch her go, whiskered heads lined up like spectators. And beneath their watching, fainter but unmistakable, came their voices. Not words, exactly. More like weather made meaning. The heron's cold patience. The otters' bright, bubbling curiosity.

*You're awake,* the water seemed to say. *Why have you been asleep so long?*

"Leave me alone," she whispered, and hated how small it sounded.

The Thornheart pulsed, almost amused.

* * *

She walked through the night and the day after it, following the pull north by feel, because the seed knew the way better than any map. The fen gave way to heathland, the heathland to old forest that the king's foresters had been forbidden to cut since before Ines was born—the Wildwood, they called it still, though nothing wild remained in it, or so the city said. The city was wrong.

On the second evening, deep among oaks older than the kingdom itself, the wolves came for her.

She heard them before she saw them—not the hunting silence of wolves stalking prey, but deliberate noise. Pawfalls on leaf litter, heavy and unhurried. A low chorus of breath. When they emerged from the bracken there were seven of them, grey and scarred and enormous, and the largest came forward until she could see the pale seam of an old spear-wound ridged across his shoulder.

He looked at her the way Rell used to look at suspects. Like someone taking inventory of everything true.

*So,* he said, and this time it was words, whole and clear, filling her skull like cold water. *The thorn-bearer walks again.*

"I'm not—" Ines stopped. Her mouth had opened around the reflexive denial, the charm-seller's smile, the *I'm just a tradeswoman, sir, I sell luck and nothing else.* But the wolf's eyes held hers, and the lie died somewhere in her throat, unmourned. "I'm carrying it," she said instead. "I don't know if I'm worthy of it."

The pack stirred. A younger wolf growled something sharp, and the eldest answered with a flick of his ear.

*Twelve years,* the old wolf said. *Twelve years we watched you sell your paper charms and speak to pigeons about bread crumbs and pretend, pretend, pretend. Do you know what the hunt burned in Vellamarch? Not witches. There have been no witches worth the name for a generation. They burned the rat-speakers of Tanner's Row. The woman who sang the gulls away from the fishing nets. A child who talked to horses. All the small listeners, all the quiet friends. Because every one of them might hear what we remember.*

"What do you remember?" Her voice shook.

*The old war. The wild law. The grove that made kings kneel and rivers change their minds. We remember when the Thornheart was a tree, and the tree was the law, and the law said: no crown devours the land that feeds it.* He stepped closer, close enough that she could smell musk and blood and crushed ferns. *Your queen remembers too. That is why she burns the listeners first. Silence the witnesses, then take the last seed, and eat the law entire.*

"Why tell me? Why not take it from me?"

*Because the seed chose you, fool girl. It has been choosing you since you were small enough to sit in a badger's sett and gossip. It does not want your hands. It wants your truth.* His lips lifted from yellowed teeth, not quite a snarl. *Say it. Say what you are. To us. To yourself.*

The forest held its breath. Even the wind stopped moving through the oaks.

Ines Galloway, who had spent twelve years perfecting the art of being no one, stood in the last wild wood in the kingdom and found that the words came easier than she'd feared—as if they'd been waiting behind her teeth all along, patient as seeds.

"I speak with beasts," she said. "It isn't trickery. It was never trickery. I've been hiding it because hiding kept me alive, and I am done hiding." Her hand went to her chest, to the warmth beneath. "And I'm taking the Thornheart to the grove if it kills me."

The old wolf exhaled, a great huff of breath that ruffled her hair.

*Better,* he said. *Late, but better.* Then, softer, with something that in a human face would have been grief: *We knew your grandmother, little speaker. She planted half this wood with her own hands. You walk like her.*

He turned, and the pack parted to let her pass, and their voices followed her into the dusk—not threats now, but a litany, low and rhythmic as a blessing:

*Run true, thorn-bearer. Run true.*

* * *

They caught up with her at the river crossing on the fourth morning.

She heard the dogs first—and flinched, because the dogs' voices were wrong, flattened, the voices of animals whose speech had been burned out of them by hunter-craft, leaving only obedience. Then the horses. Then, cutting through it all, a voice she knew.

"Ines Galloway!"

Rell stood at the near bank with six riders fanned out behind her, rain streaming off her black-and-silver cloak, her crossbow lowered but very much present. Twelve years hadn't softened her: the same square jaw, the same burn scar crawling up her neck from the market fire that had taken her family's shop and given her to the crown instead. The same eyes that had once shared stolen plums with a charm-girl on the church steps.

"You're hard to find," Rell called across the water. "That's a strange thing, for a woman with no gifts."

"Maybe I'm just quick on my feet."

"The market quarter burned, Ines. Forty stalls. Eleven dead." Rell urged her horse to the water's edge. "And a wolf was seen dying outside a charm-seller's cellar. A wolf with no wounds on it that our hunters could find. Just—" she hesitated, and for a moment her certainty cracked, showing something raw beneath, "—just old age. Or grief. As if it had finished something."

Ines stood waist-deep in the crossing, the current pulling at her, the Thornheart pulsing against her heart like a warning. *Lie*, it seemed to say. *Or don't. But choose.*

"You think I did something," Ines said.

"I think eleven people are ash and you left the city the same night without selling anything." Rell's voice stayed level, captain-flat, but her gloved hands tightened on the reins. "I've known you since we were children stealing apples off the bishop's cart. I've read your file. There's nothing in it. Nothing. No one is that empty, Ines."

The dogs whined. One of them, a brindled bitch with kind brown eyes, looked at Ines and made a sound that wasn't quite a bark—grief, flattened and wordless, the ghost of a voice that had once been able to say more. *I'm sorry,* the sound said. *I'm sorry. I can't say it properly anymore.*

Ines's chest ached.

"Rell." She used the old name, the childhood one, and saw the captain flinch. "Whatever you think I am—you're right to suspect me. And whatever you're ordered to do about it—" she looked past Rell, at the riders, at the silent dogs, at the burned-out flatness where animal voices should be, "—you should ask yourself who gave those orders, and why."

"Give me a reason not to bring you in."

"The reason is standing behind you wearing a crown."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The river ran between them, brown and swift, carrying leaves from the Wildwood south toward the burning city. Rell's crossbow stayed down. Something worked behind her eyes—loyalty grinding against memory, duty against doubt—and Ines watched her oldest friend choose, for now, not to choose.

"Cross, then," Rell said finally, quietly. "But know this: the queen wants whoever holds what that wolf carried. Wants them alive. And Captain's orders only stretch so far before they become someone else's." She raised two fingers, and the riders wheeled away along the bank, giving Ines the crossing as if by accident. "Run north, Ines. Pray you outrun what comes after me."

Ines crossed the river without another word, and climbed the far bank, and did not look back—but she heard the dogs one last time, a single high sound, almost like speech.

Almost like *run true*.

Beyond the river, the mountains rose white-capped against the solstice sky, and the Thornheart pulled toward them, eager now, urgent as a heartbeat nearing home. Four days until the solstice. Three days of climbing.

Behind her, far to the south, Rell turned her horse back toward the queen's garden, and Ines prayed to every listening thing that her friend would arrive too late to stop being innocent.
