## 3. The Queen's Hungry Garden

The throne room of Vellamarch smelled of lilies and something beneath the lilies, something sweet gone wrong, like fruit rotting inside a locked chest. Rell had never been able to name it. She had stopped trying. Some smells a soldier learned to breathe around, the way she'd learned to breathe around blood.

Queen Sabrah received her kneeling, which was itself unusual. Captains of the hunt were not often granted the honor, and Rell knew what it cost the queen to give it—knew, too, that honors were currency in this court, and every coin spent bought something.

"Captain Rell." The queen's voice was warm the way a hearth is warm when the house around it is burning. "You burned the market quarter."

"The witch escaped into the eastern hills, Majesty."

"A charm-seller." Sabrah rose from the throne and descended the three steps, her skirts whispering like dry leaves. "Twelve years selling love-knots and fever-charms to fishwives, and my hunters burn a quarter of my city for her. Tell me why I should believe she is worth this."

Rell kept her eyes on the queen's slippers, on the gold thread worked there in patterns she had always assumed were decorative. Vines, she saw now. Thorned ones. They climbed the hem and vanished up beneath the silk.

"Because the wolf died speaking her name, Majesty. Because the beasts are moving toward her instead of away. And because—" Rell paused, weighing truth against survival, as she had weighed them every day since the market burned. "Because whatever she carries, everything wild in this kingdom already knows about it."

The queen smiled. It was the worst thing about her, that smile—genuine, delighted, the smile of a woman being handed exactly what she had asked for.

"Yes," Sabrah said softly. "They do."

She turned and walked, expecting to be followed, and so Rell followed, through an arch hung with white linen, down a corridor that grew warmer with each step though no braziers burned there. The sweetness thickened. Beneath it now she caught the other smell clearly: earth, wet and black, and under the earth, iron.

The garden door stood open.

Rell had served the crown eleven years. She had burned havens in the fen country and strung up hedge-witches from the border oaks. She had told herself, all that while, that the hunt took *things*—charms, relics, the occasional creature too dangerous to let live—and that the things went into vaults, or were destroyed. She had never once been invited to see where they went.

The garden was long and low-ceilinged, dug into the palace foundations, lit by lamps of green glass that made everything look drowned. Beds of dark soil ran its length. In them, planted like roses, like vegetables, like anything a gardener might tend—

Rell stopped walking.

A stag's antlers, still attached to the skull, sprouted from one bed, but they were gray as ash and crumbling at the tines. Beside them, a harp of rowan-wood had taken root and withered in the same gesture, its strings fused into bark. Further down, a cloak that must have been woven of river-light lay pooled and dull as old canvas. A lantern with a flame that did not flicker because it was no longer burning, only remembering that it once had. Feathers. Scales. A child's shoe that hummed faintly when the air moved over it, a sound like a mother's voice heard through a wall.

Every magical thing the hunt had ever seized. Not destroyed. *Planted.* And dead. All of it dead, arranged in rows like a graveyard that pretended to be an orchard.

"You see," the queen said, drifting between the beds, trailing her fingers above the soil without touching it, "magic cannot be killed. Everyone believes it can—the old kings believed it, the witches believed it, your hunters believe it. But magic is appetite, Captain. It can only be eaten."

Rell's mouth had gone dry. "Majesty?"

"When we take a thing of power, I do not lock it away. I give it to the garden, and the garden gives its strength to me." Sabrah turned, and in the green lamplight her eyes were the color of the glass, the color of pond water over something sunk. "I have eaten a hundred small magics, Rell. A thousand. Do you know what I have learned? That they were crumbs. Every haven we burned, every witch we hanged—they were keeping me alive on scraps. There is only one true meal left in this kingdom."

The Thornheart.

Rell thought of the wolf dying in a cellar that smelled of wax and cheap incense, pressing a burning seed into the hands of a charm-seller who talked to rats. Thought of the way the beasts of Vellamarch had gone silent the night the market burned—not frightened. *Attentive.*

"And when you have it," Rell said, and was proud that her voice stayed level, "what will you be?"

The queen laughed—a real laugh, bright and terrible. "More. Only more. Mortality is a cage, Captain, and I have spent thirty years picking its lock one stolen wonder at a time. With the Thornheart, I will not need to pick it. I will walk out through the bars, and nothing born of the wild law will ever again be able to refuse me. The wolves will lie down. The rivers will run where I say. The old world will kneel to the new one, and the new one will be *me*."

She came close then, close enough that Rell could smell the lilies again, and beneath them the rot, and beneath the rot—she understood this suddenly, wholly—the smell of turned earth waiting for seed.

"Bring her to me alive," Sabrah said. "Unharmed. The seed will come out of her easier if her hands are whole. Fail me, and I will plant *you* here, and you will spend eternity humming in a jar beside the rest of them. Do you understand?"

"I understand, Majesty."

* * *

That night Rell sat in her quarters above the barracks and could not light a candle. Her hands would not perform the ordinary business of striking flint; they only shook.

Eleven years. Eleven years of warrants signed in her name, of pyres whose smoke she could still taste when the wind came off the fens, and she had told herself the same story every time: that the hunt contained the dangerous things, that containment was mercy, that someone had to hold the leash. She had built a life on that story the way you build a wall—one stone at a time, each stone small enough not to look at closely.

And the stones had been going into the queen's *garden*. Into her body. Every witch Rell had dragged to the pyre had been a course in a feast. Every relic seized had been a bite. She had not held the leash. She had carried the platter.

And Ines.

Rell closed her eyes and was seventeen again, in the year before the purges, before either of them had learned what the world does to girls with secrets. Ines Galloway, all elbows and laughter, sitting on the granary roof sharing stolen plums, whispering—only once, only to her—that the pigeons told her which carts carried grain and which carried soldiers, and laughing at her own confession until Rell laughed too, because it was Ines, and it was impossible, and impossible things were safe to laugh at in those days.

Then the first hunts began. Then Ines's family was questioned. Then Ines vanished into the city like water into sand, and Rell put on the crown's gray coat, and neither of them ever spoke of plums or pigeons again.

At the stall, three days before the burning, Ines had looked up at her with twelve years of careful blankness, and Rell had looked back, and both of them had pretended not to recognize the other. Two women standing on opposite sides of a counter built entirely of silence.

*Bring her to me alive.*

Rell got up, found the flint, lit the candle. Its small flame steadied her hands more than any oath ever had. On her desk lay the campaign map of the eastern hills, marked with the hunt's projected routes, and beside it the reports: sightings along the Weirwater, a shepherd who swore his dogs had turned north singing, a drover who claimed the crows had formed a line over the high pass like a procession.

Everything wild was guiding Ines Galloway toward the ruined grove beyond the mountains. Everything wild wanted the Thornheart planted.

Rell dipped her pen. She thought of the queen's smile, the rows of dead wonders humming in their beds, the thorned vines stitched up beneath royal silk. She thought of a girl on a granary roof with plum juice on her chin, telling the truth about pigeons and being loved for it.

She wrote her report to the crown: *Subject sighted fleeing south along the Weirwater road. Hunt converging on the marsh crossings.*

It was a lie. The Weirwater led nowhere but drowning-country, days from anywhere, and Rell would send two companies of hunters there to wade and curse and find nothing but eels.

Ines would go north, into the hills, toward the grove. Alone, if Rell had anything to do with it.

The pen hovered. One more line, and Rell wrote it knowing it might hang her: *No further sign of the beast. Recommend the northern passes remain unpatrolled until the southern pursuit resolves.*

She sanded the page, sealed it, and set it aside for the dawn courier. Then she sat very still in the candlelight and listened to herself become, quietly and irreversibly, a traitor.

It felt, she discovered, less like falling than like setting something down—a weight she had carried so long she had mistaken it for her own spine.

Outside her window, far off in the dark hills, a wolf began to sing. Not a hunting cry. A road-song, the kind packs used to sing to travelers they meant to guard.

Rell listened until it faded, then began, by candlelight, to forge her second false report.
