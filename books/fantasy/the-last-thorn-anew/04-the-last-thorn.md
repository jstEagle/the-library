## 4. The Last Thorn

The grove had been waiting so long it had forgotten how to be a grove.

Ines came over the last ridge at dusk on solstice eve, her boots split, her hands raw from three days of climbing, and found only a circle of black stumps in a bowl of grey ash. No grass. No moss. Even the stones looked starved. The wind moved through the dead ring and made no sound, as if the air itself had given up trying to speak here.

But the seed knew.

It woke against her breastbone like a second heart, beating light through the wool of her shirt. She had carried it pressed there since the river, wrapped in linen and prayer, and every mile it had grown heavier—not with weight but with *want*, pulling at her like a child tugging a sleeve. Now it pulled downward, toward the ash, and Ines understood that she had brought it home to a grave.

She knelt in the center of the ring where the oldest stump stood, wider than a wagon, its rings long since rotted to lace. Her fingers sank into soil that crumbled like old bread. Nothing lived in it. Nothing would.

"Come on," she whispered, and dug.

The hole was shallow before her arms began to shake. She set the seed into it—smaller now than when the wolf had pressed it into her palms, dimmer, as though twelve days of running had spent its fire—and waited for something to happen. A green shoot. A shudder in the ground. Anything.

Nothing happened.

The seed lay in its little grave like an eye that refused to open.

"No," Ines said. "No, you don't get to do this. Not after all of it." She scooped ash back over it anyway, patted it down, sat back on her heels, and stared. "I carried you out of Vellamarch. I lied to a Hunter-Captain. I traded my coat to a bear and my dignity to a mule who wanted compliments in rhyming couplets. You will *grow*."

The seed did not grow.

What answered instead was not the seed but the ground itself—a voice older than any beast she'd ever spoken with, older than speech, rising up through her knees like cold water. It did not use words. It used memory: the grove green and roaring with life; a thousand throats of leaf and feather singing one law; and beneath it all the simple, brutal arithmetic of wild things—*nothing is given. Everything is traded.*

Ines laughed, a cracked and terrible sound. "A gift," she said. "You want a willing gift."

The silence agreed with her.

She thought of the queen's hunters then, because she could not help it. Somewhere down the mountain, torchlight was snaking up the switchback trails—she had seen it from the ridge at noon, patient as a noose being paid out. Rell among them. Rell, who had let her cross the river with a look that said more than either of them dared speak aloud. Minutes, maybe an hour. Perhaps less.

And the seed wanted a gift. Not blood—the wolf's blood had soaked into it already and done nothing but warm it. Not suffering. It wanted what the queen could never give, what the hunt had spent twelve years burning out of the world: something freely offered, something that cost the giver everything and was given *anyway*.

Ines looked at her own two hands, scarred from charms-wire and cellar work, hands that had spoken to rats and pigeons and dying wolves for thirty years. Twelve years hiding it. Twelve years pretending the whisper in the marketplace was wind, that the pigeons wheeling over her stall were only birds. She had buried the gift so deep she'd almost convinced herself it wasn't hers.

It was the only thing she had ever truly owned.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you clever, cruel old law."

She laid both palms flat on the ash above the seed and closed her eyes.

She expected it to hurt. What came instead was worse—it came gently, the way sleep comes, the way warmth leaves a body into winter air. The gift rose out of her like breath in cold weather, visible for one impossible moment as a silver thread running from her chest down through the ash, into the dark, into the seed.

And as it went, the world went quiet.

Not silent—she had been deaf before, in small ways, all her life, muffling herself. This was different. This was the whole of it leaving. She felt the last of it pass through her like water through open fingers: the market-rats' gossip, the pigeons' gossipier gossip, the mule's rhymes, the bear's gruff courtesy, the wolf's dying words—*Galloway. Take it where it was planted. Finish what we started.* All of it draining away into the earth, and the earth drinking, drinking—

The seed caught fire.

Not flame—greenness. Light the color of new leaves burst up out of the ash and Ines threw her arm across her eyes and felt rather than saw the grove answer. Roots moved underground like a waking giant turning over. The black stumps split and budded and *unfurled*, branches unrolling like fists opening after centuries, and the bowl of ash filled with grass that grew as fast as falling rain, knee-high, waist-high, silver-seeded and singing. Saplings shot up between the ancients. Flowers opened in the dark. The whole ruined ring roared back to life around her with a sound she would remember all her life even though she could no longer name it—the sound of the old wild law coming home.

And it didn't stop at the grove.

She felt it go—felt it in her bones, in the last echo of what she had been—as the green poured off the mountains like a tide, racing down the valleys, under the rivers, across the plains toward Vellamarch. Toward the queen's garden with its withered husks and stolen power. She couldn't hear what happened there anymore. But she could imagine it: every caged enchantment bursting its pot, every devoured thing reclaiming itself, Queen Sabrah's hoarded might draining away through her fingers like the gift had drained through Ines's—because the old law kept accounts, and the accounts had just come due.

Ines knelt in the living grass and wept, and did not know if it was grief or relief, and supposed it was both, and let it be both.

* * *

They found her at moonrise, sitting in the grass with her empty hands in her lap.

Rell came first, alone, as Ines had known she would. The captain walked out of the dark into the grove and stopped as though she'd struck glass—stopped at the edge of the new green, where the ash ended and the impossible began. Behind her, farther down the slope, torches wavered. Hunters' voices, uncertain. None of them stepped past their captain.

Rell looked at the grove a long time. At the trees that hadn't existed at sundown. At Ines, kneeling in the center like a woman at a graveside, which was not far wrong.

"It's true, then," Rell said. Her voice was rough. "All of it. The seed. The—" She gestured, and her hand shook. "Sabrah woke this evening with nothing. Her guards say she screamed at her garden like a beggar screaming at an empty purse. Half the hunt has already deserted. They're saying the wild law is back, that it *knows names*."

"The old law always knew names," Ines said. "That was the problem." Her voice sounded strange to her—flat, ordinary, the voice of a woman with no more in her than any other woman. She found she liked it.

Rell came closer, boots dragging in the deep grass, and stood over her. Ines saw her take inventory, the way captains do: the raw hands, the hollow face, the absence where the gift had been. Rell had hunted witches for six years. She knew what a drained one looked like.

"You gave it up," Rell said quietly. "All of it."

"It wasn't mine to keep. It was only ever borrowed. From them." Ines nodded at the trees, the night, the small rustling lives she could no longer hear. "Everything speaks, Rell. It always did. We just stopped listening."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Twelve years hung between them—two girls sharing stolen plums behind the charm-stalls, one of them learning too early to hide what she was, the other learning too late what the crown she served really hungered for.

"I have orders," Rell said at last. "Bring you alive. Bring the seed alive." She looked at the great tree that had grown from the stump, its new branches full of moonlight, and something in her jaw set. "There's no one left to bring anything to."

She turned and raised her voice down the slope. "The grove is dead! Nothing here but ash and a madwoman praying!" A pause. "We're going down!"

The torches withdrew, reluctant, arguing, dwindling. Rell did not move until the last light had gone below the ridge.

Then she crouched, briefly, so her eyes were level with Ines's. Up close she looked ten years older than she had at the river crossing.

"I'm going to burn the hunt's records," she said. "All of them. Every name we took, every family. And then I'm going to make sure no one ever builds another garden like hers." A ghost of the old grin, the plum-stealing grin, surfaced and sank again. "You'll hear about it. Well—you won't. But you'll know."

"Rell." Ines reached out and gripped her wrist, once, hard. "Thank you isn't big enough. So instead: live well. It's the only payment the old law ever asked for."

Rell laughed—a broken, wet laugh—and squeezed her hand back, and stood, and walked out of the grove without looking back, because looking back was for people who still believed in endings.

* * *

In spring, in a village whose name meant nothing to anyone important, a woman with grey coming in early at her temples rented a cottage with good south-facing soil.

She planted beans and onions, marigolds against the beetles, a rowan tree at the gate because some habits were older than memory. She could not hear the bees anymore, or the sparrows squabbling in the hedge, or the fox that raided her henhouse every third night. When the fox came she shouted and waved a broom like any other woman, and felt a strange, clean joy in how ordinary it was.

Neighbors said her vegetables grew better than anyone's. She told them it was the manure.

On midsummer's eve, the rowan at her gate flowered out of season, white and heavy as snow, and every bird within a mile gathered in its branches at sunset and sang—not to her, never to her again, but near her, around her, a wall of small fierce voices filling the dusk.

Ines stood at her gate with dirt under her nails and listened the way people listen—with nothing but her ears—and understood every word.
