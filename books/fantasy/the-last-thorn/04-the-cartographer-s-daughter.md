## 4. The Cartographer's Daughter

The summons came at the hour when candles gutter and honest work pretends to be sleep.

Isolde Vane was awake anyway. She was always awake at that hour — a habit inherited from her father, who claimed the truest maps were drawn between the last bell of night and the first grey thought of morning, when the world's edges were softest and most willing to confess. She sat in her narrow room above the Enforcers' Wing with her boots off and her sword across her knees, not because she expected trouble but because the weight of it helped her think, the way a paperweight holds down a page that wants to tell the truth.

The knock, when it came, was three measured strikes. Guild rhythm. *Come, but know that you are summoned.*

She opened the door on a junior clerk she did not know, a boy with ink-stained cuffs and eyes that kept sliding past her shoulder as though the corridor itself might be eavesdropping. He handed her a slip of vellum sealed with the Guild's sigil — an open hand holding a closed eye — and said only: "The Master of Ledgers requests you. Immediately." Then he was gone down the stair, footsteps quick as a man leaving a room before the furniture accuses him.

Isolde dressed without hurry. Hurry, her father had taught her, was what amateurs called preparation. But her hands were not quite steady when she pinned her enforcer's badge at her throat, and she noticed this, and disliked herself for noticing.

The Guildhouse at night was a different animal from the Guildhouse by day. By day it was bureaucracy made stone — clerks hurrying with ledgers, appraisers carrying bottled weather, the great Rotunda ringing with the commerce of small impossibilities. By night it breathed. She could feel it breathing as she crossed the Cloister of Records, past shelves that held borrowed years in stoppered jars, their contents shifting like silt in dark water. Past the vault where they kept the one honest lie, which no one was permitted to open twice. The building knew something had gone wrong. Buildings always knew first.

The Master of Ledgers received her in his private office, a room she had entered perhaps six times in nine years of service. It smelled of cedar oil and old vellum and, beneath both, faintly of iron — the smell of blood that has been written into a contract and dried there. Aldous Wren sat behind a desk older than the city, his hands folded atop a single folder, and Isolde understood immediately that everything else in the room was furniture.

"Vane." His voice was dry as a ledger line. "You are familiar with the name Okonkwo."

Not a question. She gave it the respect of silence.

"A cartographer. Third-rank clerk, Registry of Impossible Geography. Meticulous. Quiet. Unambitious in the way that makes men dangerous — they see things the ambitious train themselves not to." He opened the folder a hand's width. "Last night a stranger died in the map room of this Guildhouse. Before he died, he placed an object into Clerk Okonkwo's hands. An object that does not appear in any catalogue we hold."

"And you believe Okonkwo took it."

"I believe," Wren said, with the patience of a man explaining arithmetic to a child, "that the object is missing, the stranger is dead, and the clerk has not been seen since the third bell. I believe these three facts are related. Belief is not my office. Recovery is yours."

He slid the folder across the desk. Inside was a single sheet: Rafael Okonkwo's service record, thin as a sparrow's bones. Fifteen years of flawless entries. Corrections rendered in a hand so precise it looked typeset. Not one reprimand. Not one commendation either — a man who had made himself invisible to the ledgers, which, Isolde reflected, looking at that neat grey record, was its own kind of ambition.

"Terms?" she asked.

"Recover the object. Bring Okonkwo to the Rotunda for questioning." Wren's folded hands tightened, just once, around nothing. "Alive, Vane. I want him able to answer questions. Dead men are famously uncooperative."

"And if he resists?"

Something moved behind the Master's eyes — not warmth, exactly, but the memory of warmth, like light through smoked glass. "Then remind him who wrote the rules he has spent fifteen years obeying. You will find, I think, that he listens. Clerks always listen. It is their whole education."

* * *

She read the file again in her room, by candlelight, because she did not trust the Guildhouse walls at night and because reading it once had not been enough to make her feel anything she could name.

*Rafael Okonkwo.* She knew the name the way she knew the names of certain stars — not because she had studied them but because someone she loved had pointed at them once and told her stories.

Her father. Edmun Vane, Senior Cartographer, dead eleven years now, commemorated in a plaque by the Rotunda stairs that no one read. Isolde had grown up inside his legends: the survey of the Glasswater coast drawn during a storm so complete the ink froze in the nib; the mapping of the Sorrowdeep caves by sound alone, blindfolded for nine days after the dark took his sight and returned it; the story she had loved best, the one about the young clerk with the beautiful careful hands who had found an error in one of Edmun Vane's master charts — a river drawn a mile east of where it ran — and had corrected it in red ink without a word, and how her father had laughed until he cried and framed the correction rather than the chart.

"That boy will either save the Guild or bury it," her father had said. "There is no third road for a man who corrects rivers in red ink."

She had met Rafael Okonkwo four times in her life. Once at her father's funeral, standing apart from the mourners, holding his cap like a man who had never learned where hands went at funerals. Twice over disputed surveys, professional and cool, his voice level while hers sharpened. And once —

Once, three years ago, in the map room, late, over a chart neither of them had business studying. A border dispute, officially. Unofficially, an hour that had begun with two professionals leaning over the same table and ended with her back against the map cabinet and his mouth against her throat and both of them pretending afterward, with tremendous discipline, that it had never happened, that the heat between them had been a drafting error, noted and corrected.

She had filed it under *never again* and had taken it out maybe every week since.

And now the Guild wanted her to hunt him.

Isolde set the file down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. *Divided loyalty* was a phrase from tactical manuals, clean as a compass rose. In practice it felt like this: her body remembering the exact temperature of his skin under her palms while her badge lay cold against her throat; her father's voice saying *he'll bury the Guild* while Wren's voice said *bring him alive*; and underneath it all, quieter and worse, the question she could not stop asking —

*What kind of man runs?*

Rafael Okonkwo did not run. That was the thing. Fifteen years of perfect entries proved it. If he had run, then whatever he carried was worth more to him than everything he had spent fifteen years building — and Isolde Vane, raised on her father's legends, knew better than anyone alive that men who valued things more than the Guild were either traitors or prophets, and the Guild had hanged both.

Unless he hadn't run at all. Unless he was dead in a ditch somewhere with his beautiful careful hands broken, and the stranger's death was murder, and the Guild's too-quick arrival at the map room meant something darker than diligence.

She hated how much she wanted that to be true. Hated that some traitorous part of her was already composing arguments for his innocence, already drafting the report that would keep him breathing. Hated that when she imagined finding him — cornered, defiant, those grey eyes lifting to hers across whatever ruined room they'd meet in — her first feeling was not duty.

It was relief.

* * *

Dawn found her in the Registry of Impossible Geography, where the night clerks let her pass with the wary deference owed to enforcers. Rafael's desk stood in the third row, and it was the neatest desk in the room — papers squared, pens capped, everything in its place except the one thing that mattered.

But desks confessed to those who knew how to interrogate them. Her father had taught her that too. She knelt and looked across the surface at eye level, the way you read a landscape.

The inkwell sat slightly nearer the window than a right-handed man would leave it. A chair castor had scuffed a half-circle toward the door — he'd risen fast, faster than habit. And on the blotter, so faint it might have been imagination, a ghost of pressure: the rectangular shadow of something folded, something that had lain there long enough to leave its shape in the felt.

Folded. Small. Portable. A map.

Isolde rose slowly. Around her the Registry murmured with its morning sounds — nibs scratching, ledgers turning, the great clock counting out hours toward some appointment the Guild would regret — and she stood in the middle of it all with her father's training whispering in her ear and her heart behaving like a traitor.

Northwest, she decided. Out of the city a fugitive goes by the oldest roads, the ones predating the Guild's tolls, because new roads have memories and old roads have mercy. It was what her father would have done. It was, if she was honest, what she would do.

She left the Guildhouse within the hour, riding hard, telling herself the pace was pursuit.

By dusk she had found what the fugitive had left behind: signs subtle enough that anyone but a cartographer's daughter would have ridden past them. A hoofprint in clay still holding rainwater. A hedge cut wrong, branches broken outward. And once, caught on a bramble at knee height, a thread of grey wool that matched, exactly, the clerk's coats.

He was close. A day ahead, perhaps less. She could feel him ahead of her the way a chart feels the coastline it approaches — every sign a sounding, every broken twig a depth-marking, drawing her onward through the gathering dark toward a border she could not yet name.

At a crossroads shrine, its old god worn faceless, she stopped to water her horse and saw the fresh chalk mark on the stone. A cartographer's mark. A bearing, and a distance, and beneath them a small symbol she knew from her childhood — her father's own shorthand, the mark he used for *land that remembers.*

Isolde stared at it for a long moment, her breath steaming in the cold.

Then she drew her sword halfway, checked the edge, and slid it home — and rode north, into the dark, toward the man she had been ordered to bring back alive, praying to a god whose face had been erased that she would find him before the Guild's other hunters did.

Because she had read the assignment twice, and both times, in the terms, Wren had said *alive* — but the escort assigned to follow her at a discreet distance had been given no such instruction.

She had checked.
