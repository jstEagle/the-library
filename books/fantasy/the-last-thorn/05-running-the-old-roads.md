## 5. Running the Old Roads

The roads out of Corvane were supposed to be safe.

That was the whole point of them, Rafael had always understood — the Guild's highways ran straight and lantern-lit from the capital to the provinces, each mile-stone stamped with the sigil of the Ledger, each wayhouse licensed, inspected, recorded. On a Guild road you could not be robbed, because robbery was a thing that happened to people who existed in the ledgers, and on a Guild road every traveller existed. He had believed this the way he believed in the weight of paper, which is to say completely, right up until the moment he understood that the same machinery that guaranteed your existence could revoke it.

So he did not take the Guild road north. He took the old one.

It began behind the tannery district, where the cobbles gave up pretending and became packed earth, then became something older than either — a rutted track worn into the land by feet that had been walking it before anyone thought to write down where they were going. The surveyors' maps called it nothing. That was precisely why he trusted it. A road with no name on any ledger was a road no ledger could find him on.

He walked through the last grey hour before dawn with the map folded against his chest, inside his coat, inside the oilcloth, inside the lie of himself. *Clerk Third Class Rafael Okonkwo, absent without leave.* The words would already be entered somewhere by now, he imagined, in Isolde Vane's precise hand or one very like it. He tried not to think of her face when she read them. He failed, mostly.

The country changed as he went. Not gradually — that was the strange part. Between one hillcrest and the next, the hedgerows stopped being hedgerows and became hedges, thick and knotted and older than agriculture, and the birdsong shifted key, and there were moments — several of them, more than he could dismiss — when the light seemed to arrive a half-second after the sun should have sent it. As if the land here were remembering how to be looked at, and doing it badly.

Or as if it had been looked away from for so long that it had forgotten its own timing.

He stopped at a stream to drink and caught himself speaking aloud to the map. "I know," he said, absurdly. "I know."

*You do not,* said the margin, in ink that had not been there yesterday. It had developed opinions the way a fever develops — suddenly, and all at once, and always worse at night. *But you are walking in the right direction, which is more than most of your kind have managed.*

"Where does the old road end?"

*It doesn't. Roads remember longer than borders do.*

Rafael wiped his hands on his coat and kept walking, and told himself that talking to an inked page was a symptom of exhaustion and not of anything else, and knew, in the meticulous ledger-keeping corner of his mind that had never once lied to him, that he was entering a false record.

* * *

The ambush, when it came, was almost a relief, because at least it obeyed rules he understood.

They took him at the ford below Hallowmere, where the track dipped between two shoulders of hill and the water ran shallow over red stone. One moment he was picking his way across the stepping-stones; the next there were figures on both banks — four, five, six of them, rising out of the bracken like the land simply deciding to have people in it — and a bowstring drew back with a sound like a held breath being released.

"Don't," said a woman's voice, flat and bored. "The clerk runs poorly. You'll miss and I'll have to laugh."

Rafael stood very still in the middle of the stream, boots filling with cold water, hands raised in the universal grammar of surrender. His heart was performing some frantic arithmetic — escape routes, odds, the exact distance to the far bank — and arriving at zero every time.

"I'm not—" he started.

"You're carrying it." The speaker came down the bank: a broad man past fifty, grey-stubbled, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who had been in a great many fights and found them tedious. A scar ran from his left ear to the hinge of his jaw, old and white and deliberate-looking, the kind of mark that had been earned once and then worn ever since as a lesson. "We've watched you since the tannery gate. You walk like a man carrying something heavier than his coat allows. And you flinched at the mile-stone outside Corvane — the one with the Ledger's mark scraped off. Everyone else walks past it. You *flinched*."

"You've been following me since the city?"

"We've been waiting on this road for eleven years," the man said. "You happened to come down it. Sit." He nodded at a flat stone at the water's edge, and around him the others relaxed by degrees — not lowering their weapons, exactly, but ceasing to aim them, which among certain people is a courtesy. "My name is Brannoc. This is Sefa, Dagny, the twins who won't give you their names because they think it's funny, and Merrick, who's asleep standing up and would thank you not to mention it. We're Thornlanders."

Rafael sat, because his legs made the decision independently. "There are no Thornlanders."

"No," Brannoc agreed pleasantly. "Officially. There are no Thornlands either. There's no war, there's no treaty, there's no debt, and there are no two hundred thousand people who used to live between the Ashwater and the Grey Fells. Would you like some tea? We've got tea. It's terrible. Everything we own is terrible; that's what dispossession looks like when you get up close."

Someone pressed a tin cup into Rafael's hands. It was terrible. He drank it anyway, because his body had decided that whatever was happening now, warmth could only improve it.

"The map," Brannoc said. "Show me."

Every instinct Rafael owned — the Guild's instincts, installed over ten years like good joinery — screamed at him to deny it. *Catalogue reference T-0. Both copies destroyed. No such realm exists.* He opened his mouth to say something careful and deniable, and instead heard himself say: "Why?"

Brannoc smiled, and it transformed his face into something younger and much sadder. "Because that's the first honest question a guildsman has asked me in thirty years, and I'd like to reward it before I stop trusting you again." He held out his hand, palm up, patient as stone. "Show me the map, clerk."

And Rafael — meticulous, careful, quietly lonely Rafael Okonkwo, whose entire life had been built on the principle that records must be protected from the world — reached inside his coat, unwrapped the oilcloth, and put the most dangerous object in the world into a stranger's scarred hand.

* * *

Brannoc unrolled the chart across his knees and did not speak for a long time.

The fire had been built small and screened, against the light of it reaching the ridge line. Around it, the exiles had gone quiet in a way that was not peace. Sefa had closed her eyes. One of the twins — Rafael still couldn't tell them apart — had turned his face away, the way men do when they don't want to be seen feeling things.

"It's my grandmother's hand," Brannoc said at last. His thumb moved along the western edge, where the ink lay densest. "The coast. She surveyed it in a coracle, in weather that should have killed her, because the Guild's charts had already begun to leave us off and someone had to keep the shape of the place alive somewhere." He looked up. "Where did you get it?"

"A man brought it to the guildhouse. He was dying. He said—" Rafael stopped. Saying it aloud, to these people, felt like setting a match to something. "He said, *the line is drawn through us.* Then the Guild enforcers arrived within minutes. Too fast. Like they'd known."

"They did know." Brannoc rolled the map with a gentleness that contradicted everything else about his hands. "The Master of Ledgers has hunted every copy of every chart of our country for three hundred years. Not because the maps are valuable. Because they're *evidence*. A map is a witness, clerk. It says: this was here. It says: you cannot unmake a thing merely by refusing to write it down." He fed a stick to the fire. "Your grandfather's name was Edmun Okonkwo."

The name landed in Rafael's chest like a dropped stone.

"He was a surveyor," Brannoc went on, watching him. "Third rank, Guild of Impossible Things, attached to the Boundary Commission of the final year of the war. There's a roll of names in the treaty archive — the Commission's signatories, the men who held the pens. Okonkwo appears four times. Site surveys. Line verification." He pronounced each word carefully, laying them out like items on a table. "Do you know what the treaty paid for peace with? Not gold. Gold would have been cheap. It paid with *names* — ours, struck from the rolls so our descendants couldn't inherit citizenship anywhere. With memories — the war itself, edited out of the public record until children born after it were taught their grandparents had lived in empty hills. And with the land itself, declared legally nonexistent, so that no border could be defended, because a border requires a thing on either side of it, and we had been reclassified as nothing."

"That can't be done," Rafael said. It came out almost pleading. "A realm can't be — you can't strike a country from—"

"You did," Brannoc said. "*Your* guild did. Your grandfather's pen did. And it has worked beautifully for three centuries, except for one small problem." He tapped the rolled map against his knee. "The land didn't agree. Land is stubborn. Land remembers. Every year the Thornlands press a little harder against that treaty line, and every year the Guild spends more ink pretending otherwise, and the pretending gets more expensive, and now—" he spread his hands, taking in the dark hills, the wrong-timed light, the hedge that had been a hedgerow that morning, "—now it's failing. The erased country is coming back whether the ledgers approve or not. And the Master of Ledgers needs that map destroyed because it's the only document left that shows the world what the truth looks like while it happens."

The fire cracked. Somewhere upslope, an owl called — or something calling itself an owl.

"Why tell me this?" Rafael asked. "You could take the map and let me go. Or not let me go. Why explain?"

"Because of your surname," Brannoc said simply. "Because you're the grandson of a pen-holder, and I want to look into your face while you decide what that makes you. Because the Guild will offer you forgiveness — they'll offer you your little life back, warm and clean and catalogued — and I want there to be at least one voice in your ear that offered you the truth instead." He leaned forward, firelight pooling in the furrows of his scarred face. "Your grandfather drew the line through living land, Rafael Okonkwo. Through villages. Through a school at Marrowford where the children were at their lessons when the Commission's instruments passed over. The line is drawn through us. The dying man told you true."

Rafael looked down at his own hands, ink-stained, steady, his grandfather's hands, holding a terrible cup of tea beside a stranger's fire, and felt the vertigo open beneath him like a shaft.

All his life, the Guild had been the fixed point — the ledgers, the rules, the great machine that made things true by writing them down. And these people, these non-existent people, were the enemy, the anomaly, the error to be corrected.

Except they weren't lying to him.

In ten years inside the Guild of Impossible Things, no one had ever laid the whole truth in front of him at once. They rationed truth like a famine good. These exiles had handed it over entire, freely, to a stranger with an enemy's name — because they wanted him to *choose*.

"The enforcers will have tracked me," he said quietly. "By tomorrow, maybe sooner. There's one they send for things that matter. Her name is Isolde Vane."

"We know the name," Brannoc said. Something moved behind his eyes — recognition, and under it, carefully buried, grief. "Vane. Yes. Another pen-family." He rose, joints cracking, and looked north up the dark road, toward hills that officially were not there.

"Sleep, clerk," he said. "Tomorrow you'll want to be rested. Because at dawn we move — and by nightfall, if the maps are right, the Guild's enforcers will have found the ford."

He paused at the edge of the firelight.

"And you'll have to decide which side of the line you're standing on when they do."
