## 6. The Treaty of Blood-Debt

The fire was built the way Brannoc had been taught to build fires before he could hold a knife: low, ringed with stone, smoke folded back into itself so that no watcher on any ridge would see it. Old habits. Dead-country habits. He crouched at its edge and fed it a knot of blackthorn, and watched the flame take the wood the way it had once taken whole villages — greedily, without apology.

Behind him, the camp was settling into its night-sounds. Merren was humming to the child, some half-remembered lullaby in a language only four people left alive still spoke. Dagny was sharpening a blade that did not need sharpening, because grief needed somewhere to go and hers had always gone through whetstones. And by the far side of the fire sat the cartographer's son, Rafael Okonkwo, turning his stolen birthright over in his hands as though it might bite him.

It would, eventually. Maps like that one bit everyone who touched them.

Brannoc had seen the moment of recognition earlier — the flicker in the clerk's careful eyes when Brannoc had spoken his grandfather's name. *Okonkwo.* The name that lived in the treaty rolls in ink so old it had turned brown, the color of dried blood, which was fitting, because that is what the ink had been made from.

The boy — no, not a boy, a grown man with tired eyes and ink-stained fingers, but *boy* to Brannoc the way all Guild-born were boys — the man flinched whenever one of them moved too fast. He had run three days on roads that predated every kingdom the Guild now claimed to have drawn into being, and he looked like it. But he had not run from them when they'd surrounded him. That was worth something. Or it was worth nothing. Brannoc had not yet decided.

"Tell me," Rafael said quietly, into the silence. "You said you knew my grandfather. You said—" He stopped. Started again, more carefully, the way a man picks his way across rotten bridge-planking. "You said the line was drawn through you."

Brannoc turned the blackthorn knot over in the fire. The thorns caught light like small accusations.

"Sit properly," he said. "This is a telling, not a conversation. There are rules."

Rafael hesitated, then shifted to sit cross-legged, facing the fire, the map across his knees. Brannoc almost laughed. The clerk actually knew the old forms. Someone in that marble mausoleum of a guildhouse had taught him courtesy, even if they hadn't taught him history.

"My grandmother told me this story," Brannoc began, "when I was seven years old, the age at which Thornland children were traditionally taught what had been done to them. She told it every year after, on the eve of the Long Dark, and she made me repeat it back word for word until I could say it in my sleep. Not because she loved the sound of her own voice. Because a story that isn't carried dies, and a country that isn't remembered was never erased at all — it was merely misplaced." He looked up. "So. Let me carry it to you, cartographer's blood, and see what you do with the weight."

* * *

"Before the Guild drew us off the world," Brannoc said, "there were the Thornlands, and the Thornlands were alive."

He said it plainly, without ornament, because ornament was a Guild disease and he refused to catch it.

"Not alive the way your guildmasters use the word, when they sell bottled storms to merchants and call a river *awakened* because they've bound a spirit into its source. Alive the way a body is alive. The land had veins — we walked them, we called them the green-roads — and it had breath, and it had a memory longer than any ledger. Every hill in the Thornlands knew its own name before anyone named it. The forest kept our dead the way other countries keep their kings: close, honored, consulted."

"The war came out of the east in the days of my grandmother's grandmother's grandmother. You'll have heard of it, if the Guild let anything survive in its histories. They will have called it the Brushfire War, or the Unpleasantness, or some other small word for a thing that burned for ninety years. We called it the Devouring, because that is what it did. It ate everything. Armies marched into the green-roads and the roads swallowed them, because the land defended itself — why wouldn't it? Would you lie down for men who burned your forests and salted your springs?"

"And the war did not end. Understand that first, before anything else I tell you tonight. It did not end. It was *priced*."

He fed the fire another branch. In the flames' shifting light, the scars across his face and forearm moved like living things.

"There came a winter when neither side could lift a sword. Too many dead. Too much ash. And into that exhausted quiet came the Guild of Impossible Things, carrying ledgers instead of weapons, which is how they have always fought their wars — late, and on paper. They offered terms. Both sides, bleeding out, said yes before they read them. Reading is a luxury of the un-desperate."

"The treaty had a price clause. One price clause. The Guild's negotiators called it the *Settlement of Accounts*, and it sounded like bookkeeping, because everything the Guild does sounds like bookkeeping right up until it opens a vein." Brannoc's voice went flat and hard, the voice of recitation, the words worn smooth by a hundred tellings. "*In consideration of peace perpetual, the parties hereto surrender unto the Guild the following accounts receivable: the names of the inhabitants of the Thornlands; the memories pertaining thereto held by all persons beyond the line; and the territory lying within the bounds hereafter described.*"

"Inhabitants," Merren murmured behind him, to the child. "Territory. As though we were stock in a warehouse."

"They took our names first," Brannoc went on. "Every one. Entered them into a ledger and then struck them through, and the striking-through was the magic, if magic is the word for it — and it isn't, but your language has no honest word, so it must serve. A name struck from a ledger is a name struck from mouths. Our neighbors woke one morning unable to pronounce us. Then they woke unable to remember us. Then there were no neighbors, only empty hills where villages had been, and maps with blank spaces where the Thornlands had been, and Guild clerks writing *terra incognita* in the margins as though the land had never borne wheat and children and songs."

"Some of us survived it. Do not ask me how — the how is ugly and long. Enough that a few hundred slipped the strike-through, hiding in the deep green-roads where the ledgers couldn't reach, and enough that our names survived in one place the Guild never thought to check: in the land itself. The Thornlands remember us even now. It is the only parent we have left."

"And the border." His jaw tightened. "Here is the part your grandfather did, cartographer. Here is the part you inherited tonight whether you wanted it or not."

* * *

The fire had burned down to coals. No one had slept. Rafael sat exactly where he had been sitting for hours, the map forgotten in his lap, and Brannoc gave him credit for that — many Guild men would have looked away by now, or argued, or reached for the comfortable lies of their training. This one just listened, white-faced, like a man taking medicine.

"The border had to be drawn," Brannoc said, quieter now. This part of the telling always went quieter. "That was the shape of the peace. A line, agreed by both sides, separating what remained of the armies. A surveyor's task. Simple."

"But the Thornlands were alive. I told you. Veins. Breath. Memory. A border drawn across a living country is not a line on paper — it is a wound in flesh. The Guild's own surveyors knew it. Three of them refused. Their names are in your guildhouse records, marked *resigned*. Two of them died within the year, badly. The third was your grandfather."

Rafael's head came up. "Okonkwo didn't refuse."

"No." Brannoc let the word sit in the fire-smoke between them. "Okonkwo held the pen."

"He was the finest line-hand of his generation — that is not praise, it is report. When the others refused, the Guild gave him the pen, because he could draw a straight line through anything, they said. Even a scream." Brannoc leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the firelight filled the hollows of his scarred face and he looked carved rather than born. "And he drew it. Forty leagues of border in a single season, and where the line crossed the green-roads — the land's own veins — he crossed them cleanly. Neat intersections. Beautiful drafting. I have seen the original. It is in your Vault of Precedents, catalogued under *Treaty Instruments*, shelf nine, and it is the most obscene object ever made by human hands."

"Every place the line crossed a vein, the land bled. Not water. Not sap. Blood — the land's own, thick and slow and ancient. And the treaty required payment for the peace, and the payment was taken *at the line*, drop by drop, year by year, a standing debt against a standing wound. Blood-debt. That is the true name of your Guild's great peace. They did not end the war. They mortgaged it. And the interest is paid in a currency only one country on earth possesses."

He stood then, slowly, joints cracking from cold and old breaks, and crossed to Rafael, and held out his scarred hand.

"Your grandfather signed. Your father served the Guild that enforced it. And you spent your life keeping the ledgers that record our erasure as *completed transactions*." His hand stayed open, steady. "I do not blame you. Blame requires choice, and you were never given one — that is the genius of the thing they built. But the map you're holding chose you anyway. Ask yourself why a dying stranger ran through a burning city to put it in the hands of an Okonkwo. Ask yourself what the land remembers about your family's debt."

Rafael did not take the hand. He looked down at the map instead, and Brannoc saw him see it — truly see it, perhaps for the first time. The marginalia had begun to move. New script was surfacing along the western edge, fine and urgent, ink rising through the parchment like breath fogging glass:

*They are coming. The enforcers crossed the river at dusk. The woman Vane leads them.*

Brannoc read it upside down, and something old and grim settled into his chest like a stone into a well.

"Well," he said. "It seems the telling is over, cartographer. The land has heard enough, and so have I — and now we all learn what kind of Okonkwo you are. Because come morning, the Guild will be on us. And among them is a woman whose father also signed the treaty."
