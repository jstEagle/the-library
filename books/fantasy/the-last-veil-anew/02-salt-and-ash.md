## 2. Salt and Ash

Greyhollow smelled the way it always did: salt from the flats to the south, ash from the tanneries to the north, and between them the sour undertone of a town that had learned to survive by not asking questions. Sable had come down out of the Thornfells with three mules, eleven bolts of mountain silk no customs officer would ever see, and a headache that had ridden behind their eyes for two days straight.

The storm was still in them. Not on them—in them. That was the wrongness of it. Eight years of weather had taught Sable the difference between rain that fell *on* a body and weather that lived under the skin, and what had come down off the pass with them was the second kind. A low pressure sitting behind the breastbone. A pressure that answered.

They put it out of their mind the way they put everything out of their mind: methodically, and with practice.

The mules complained as Sable led them through the Salt Gate at midmorning, hooves ringing hollow on old paving stones worn to the color of bone. Greyhollow sat where the mountains gave up and the salt marshes began—a border town in every sense, loyal to no crown long enough for any crown to bother taxing it properly. It was the kind of place where a person could sell anything if they didn't ask where it came from, and buy anything if they didn't ask where it was going. Sable had been coming here since their third winter of exile. The gate guards knew them by a different name. The harbor-fence knew them by another one still. Only Maren Voss, who kept the counting house above the chandlery, knew them by no name at all—she simply called them *the courier*, which was the safest name anyone had ever given Sable Fairweather.

"Eleven bolts," Maren said when Sable laid them out on her scarred oak table, peeling back the oilcloth. Her fingers moved over the weave without touching it, reading thread-count like scripture. "Thornfell loom-work. You're carrying this through the pass yourself now?"

"I carry everything through the pass myself."

"Mm." She held a bolt up to the window light. The silk caught it and threw faint colors across her face, blue and green, like shallow water. "Prices are soft this season. Church traffic's scared the southern buyers off the roads."

Sable kept their face still. "Church traffic."

Maren set the bolt down. She was sixty, built like the door of a strongroom, and she had watched more smugglers hang than Sable cared to count. She never wasted a look. When she gave Sable one now, long and level over the top of her spectacles, Sable felt it land like a hand on the shoulder.

"You haven't heard," she said.

"I've been in the high passes since the thaw."

"That would explain it." She rolled the silk with quick, practiced turns. "Inquisitors came up the coast road three weeks ago. New Dawn, gray cloaks, the full liturgy. They're not here for us—they don't care about salt or silk. They're asking after weather."

"Weather." Sable let the word sit there, flat and stupid, while their heart performed a slow, cold maneuver behind their ribs.

"Answered prayers." Maren said it the way she said all church language—with the flat distaste of a woman who had buried a husband under a faith that had already moved on to newer gods. "Shepherds up in the high valleys swearing their prayers came true. Rain where rain was asked for. A chapel in Hollowmere that stood untouched when the avalanche took everything around it. Some girl's fever breaking the same night her mother prayed till her knees bled. Coincidence stacks up, and the New Dawn doesn't believe in coincidence. They believe in competition."

*Rain where rain was asked for.*

The memory arrived unbidden, unwelcome: eight days ago, halfway up the Widow's Stair, hail hammering down so precisely around Sable's campsite that the fire hadn't even guttered. A circle of ice with Sable at its center. They'd told themselves it was wind shear. They'd told themselves a great many things.

"How many inquisitors?" Sable asked, and was pleased at how bored their voice sounded.

"Four that I know of. Maybe six. And they brought clerks." Maren said *clerks* the way other people said *rats*. "Men with ledgers. They've been buying copies of every weather report the toll stations file. Every storm log going back five years. Paying good coin, too, which tells you how serious they are."

Five years. Sable's storms were small things, careful things—diverted squalls, a fog bank borrowed for cover, the occasional blizzard nudged a day east so a client could cross a pass alive. Small enough to hide in the noise of the mountains. But ledgers were patient. Ledgers added.

"I'll take nine crowns a bolt," Maren said, mercifully returning to business, "and not a copper more, because I have to move it quietly now, thanks be to whatever god you people haven't managed to annoy yet."

"Nine is robbery."

"Nine is survival. Take it or carry it back up your mountain."

Sable took it. Ninety-nine crowns in mixed coin, heavy enough to feel real, real enough to feel temporary. Outside Maren's window, Greyhollow went about its morning: fishwives crying the last of the spring catch, a funeral bell tolling from the Chapel of the Dawn—the new one, white-stoned, built directly over the bones of some older shrine whose name nobody remembered. Sable looked at it too long. The bell seemed to look back.

*The mountain feels watchful,* they'd thought on the pass. Now the town did too. Or perhaps it was only Sable, carrying watchfulness around like contraband, finding it everywhere they looked.

* * *

They spent the afternoon doing what they always did in Greyhollow: being unremarkable. They bought lamp oil and dried apples and a new whetstone. They drank one cup of bad cider at the Drowned Lantern and listened to two conversations at once, which was one more than necessary. They reshoed the lead mule and argued the farrier down from an outrageous price by pointing out, gently, that the man's own forge-weather vane had been installed backward and had been lying to him about the wind for a year. Small kindnesses, small cruelties. The currency of staying invisible.

It was almost evening when the gray cloaks came into the Lantern.

There were two of them, and they entered the way water enters a hull—not loudly, but with the certainty of eventual sinking. Gray wool, silver dawnburst sigils at the collar, boots that had been cleaned recently enough to matter. One was young, fresh-faced, carrying a ledger under his arm like a weapon he hadn't been issued yet. The other was older, lean, with the patient eyes of a man who had learned that most truths surrender eventually if you just keep asking.

The common room quieted the way rooms do—not silence, exactly, but a lowering of volume, a collective decision to become furniture.

"Good evening," the elder inquisitor said to the room at large, warmly, terribly. "Please, continue. We're only travelers, like yourselves."

Nobody continued anything.

He ordered tea. He paid for it with exact change. Then he and his young colleague settled at a corner table, opened the ledger, and began, with terrible gentleness, to interview the room.

"The toll station at Kestrel Ford logs a storm on the twenty-third of last month," the young one read aloud, to a drover nursing his supper three tables away. "A localized hail event. Unusual precision, the clerk wrote. Can you speak to that, master drover? You'd have been on that road."

"Hail comes where hail wants," the drover said.

"It does," the inquisitor agreed pleasantly. "And yet this hail seems to have wanted very particular things. It spared a campsite. It spared a traveler." He turned a page. "We're compiling accounts of answered prayer in the high passes. Nothing sinister. The Dawn rejoices when the faithful are heard. We only wish to give thanks properly—and to understand, of course. Understanding is devotion's first duty." He smiled. It was a genuinely nice smile. "Did you pray on that road, master drover? On the twenty-third?"

The drover stared into his stew. "Can't say I did."

"Then you've nothing to fear." The smile again. "Only the guilty fear questions."

*That isn't true,* Sable thought, into their second cup of cider, keeping their face angled toward the window and their hood up. *Everyone fears questions. Questions are how the innocent get measured for rope.*

They should leave. Every instinct Sable had refined over eight years said so—leave the cider, leave the coin, go out the kitchen door past the boy scrubbing pots, take the marsh path north, and be gone before these men ever memorized their face. It was the smart play. It was the play they'd made a hundred times, the play that had kept them alive while braver and better people died loudly.

Instead Sable sat, and drank, and listened, because some traitorous part of them needed to hear it all.

For an hour, the inquisitors worked the room like a tide, patient and unhurried, and the room gave up its sediment grain by grain. Yes, the rains in Hollowmere had been strange. Yes, old Tam Wicker swore his wife's grave-frost had melted overnight after he'd begged the old gods—begged *anything*—for one warm day to dig her plot. No, nobody had seen anything, heard anything, done anything. Greyhollow was a town of people who had survived by knowing nothing, and they performed their ignorance beautifully.

But the ledger grew anyway. That was the horror of it. Every denial went into the book alongside every half-admission, and the book did not care about the difference. The book only wanted shapes. Patterns. *Weather anomalies correlated with settlements along the high passes; correlation with sites of pre-Dawn worship; correlation with—*

"With exiles," the young inquisitor said suddenly, and Sable's blood went to slush.

He'd turned back several pages, hunting something. "Master Quill, here—" he tapped the ledger, "—writes that the Hollowmere rains coincided with the sighting of a traveler. Alone, hooded, heading north. And there's a similar account from Kestrel Ford. And one from the shrine at Cold Bracken, where the shepherd says a stranger passed the night, and where the following week the chapel bell rang with no hand near it." He looked around the room, bright-eyed, earnest, horrifying. "A stranger. Alone, always alone, moving the passes. Does anyone recall such a traveler? Perhaps someone who keeps to themselves. Someone who came down from the mountains lately."

Silence. Beautiful, complicit silence. Even the pot-boy stopped scrubbing.

The elder inquisitor lifted his tea, unhurried, and spoke over the rim of it. "My colleague is enthusiastic. Forgive him. What we seek is simpler than a stranger." His eyes moved around the room, mild as milk. "We seek a storm-caller. An exile of the order that once called itself the Wardens of the Veil—though I'm told they've scattered names like leaves. If such a person passed through here, they are not necessarily guilty of anything." A pause, delicately calibrated. "But someone has been answering prayers in those mountains. And prayers, friends, are not answered by accident. Either God has returned to the high places—or something else is wearing His voice. And either way, the Dawn must know which."

He set down his cup. "Whoever helps us will be remembered generously. Whoever shelters such a one—" he spread his hands, regretful, almost sorrowful, "—will be remembered at all. There is a difference, I'm afraid, between the two kinds of remembering."

Sable looked at their cider. Counted four breaths. Kept their shoulders rounded, their hands work-worn-looking around the cup, their whole body a dull anonymous thing in the corner of a dull anonymous room. Inside, behind the ribs, the pressure that had ridden down from the pass stirred once, like a sleeper turning over, and Sable clamped down on it with eight years of practiced nothing.

*Something else is wearing His voice.* The words followed Sable out of the Lantern an hour later, when the inquisitors finally rose, nodded graciously to the room's studied blankness, and left into the salt-smelling dusk. They followed Sable up the chandlery stairs, through the door Maren unlocked for them, into the narrow rented room where they counted their ninety-nine crowns twice and slept badly with a knife under one hand and the window cracked toward the mountains.

*Either God has returned—or something else is wearing His voice.*

Both options ended with gray cloaks climbing into the Thornfells. Both options ended with ledgers, and patience, and the kind of remembering that came with fire.

* * *

Maren found them at dawn, at the marsh gate, checking mule harnesses in the gray light. She carried a wrapped parcel of bread and cheese and a look that Sable trusted even less than the bread.

"I told you prices were soft," she said. "I didn't tell you everything."

"No," Sable agreed. "You didn't."

"There was a man asking after you specifically." Maren handed over the parcel, and her hand did not hurry, but it did not linger either. "Not by name. By description. Storm-caller, exile, eight years gone, moves cargo through the Widow's Stair. He offered my doorman ten crowns to confirm whether such a courier had come through the Salt Gate this week."

"And your doorman?"

"Took the money and told him nothing, because I pay twelve." Maren adjusted her shawl against the marsh wind. "He's still in town, this asker. Sitting at the Lantern every evening with his tea, watching the road down from the pass like a man fishing. Patient sort. The dangerous kind of patient—the kind that thinks he's kind."

Sable tightened a buckle that didn't need tightening. Their fingers were steady. They were proud of that, in a distant, useless way.

"You should go north," Maren said. "Through the marshes, around, take the coast. Let the passes rest a season."

"The passes are my living."

"Your living won't mean much if you're answering questions in a Dawn chapel with hot irons nearby." Maren looked up at them then, and beneath all her iron practicality there was something old and tired and almost fond. "I've known you eight years, courier. I know you don't sleep indoors during storms. I know you whistle when you think no one's listening, and that your whistling sounds like weather coming. I never asked. Asking's bad for trade. But I'll say this once, free of charge, because you've never once cheated me: whatever's up in those mountains that's got the church sniffing—whatever it is—it knows your name. Weather like that remembers who it belongs to."

Sable said nothing. The marsh wind came off the flats carrying salt and the far-off reek of the tanneries, and somewhere behind it, faint as a memory, the smell of the high snowfields.

"One more thing," Maren said, turning to go. "The asker. Your doorman caught a detail, since he was being paid for details. The man's papers named him an inquisitor, but his accent—" she shrugged. "Coastal. Lowland. But he used one word wrong, just once, and my doorman's mother was a mountain woman. He said *veil-breaker*. Said it like a curse. Like a word the order uses for its own dead."

She left Sable standing at the marsh gate with the parcel in their hands and the dawn coming up bruise-colored behind the Thornfells.

*Veil-breaker.* The order had a hundred titles for its exiles—oath-lost, storm-blind, the unbound—but that one was older than all of them, and it belonged to the founding years, to the wars, to the sealed places. Sable hadn't heard it spoken aloud since the day they'd been stripped of their grey robe on the freezing terrace at Veilhold, while the mountains watched and said nothing.

Sable swung up onto the lead mule and turned north, toward the pass, toward the mountains that felt watchful—because the cargo waiting for them there was worth more than safety, and because running the coast meant letting the ledgers win by default, and because, though they'd have cut out their tongue before admitting it, some part of them needed to climb back into that watchful silence and find out what, exactly, had been answering.

Behind them, Greyhollow woke. Somewhere in it, a man with coastal vowels and a patient manner finished his morning tea and made a small, neat mark in a small, neat ledger.

Ahead, the Widow's Stair waited under a sky so clear it looked rehearsed.

By noon, the first clouds had begun to gather over the high peaks—gathering, Sable noticed with a chill that had nothing to do with altitude, in a perfect circle, with the pass at its center.

Like something listening.

Like something waiting for them to come home.
