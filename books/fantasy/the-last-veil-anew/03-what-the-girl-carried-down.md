## 3. What the Girl Carried Down

The storm had followed Sable down from the high passes for three days, patient as a debt collector.

They had tried to outwalk it, cutting east along the scree fields where the Thornfells shed their broken teeth into the valley of the Vane River, but the clouds simply poured over the ridgeline behind them and settled in, gray and heavy as wet wool. By the second morning it had begun to snow—too early for true winter, too steady to be anything innocent. The flakes came down without wind, which was the wrong way for snow to fall in these mountains. Snow here always came sideways, driven by gusts that could strip paint from a wagon. This snow fell straight, deliberate, like something being set down carefully rather than thrown.

Sable did not like it.

Eight years in the Thornfells had taught them to read weather the way other people read faces, and this weather was wearing an expression. There was intention in it. The storm moved when they moved, paused when they paused, and twice now—at the ford below Kettle Gap, and again on the switchbacks above the burned waystation—it had opened a corridor of clear sky exactly where Sable needed one, then closed again like a door drawn shut behind them.

*Exhaustion,* they told themselves, for the hundredth time since Greyhollow. *You're tired, you're spooked, you're hearing your own guilt in the wind.* The inquisitors had been asking about answered prayers, about impossible weather, about exiled storm-callers. Of course the mountains felt watchful now. A man who knew he was hunted saw hunters in every shadow, and Sable had spent eight years being very good at seeing things that were not there.

But they had also spent eight years being very good at knowing the difference.

* * *

The shrine appeared out of the snowfall like something remembered rather than seen.

Sable knew it—or had known it, once. It sat in a fold of the mountain above the old pilgrim road, a low stone structure with a roof of overlapping slate shingles gone green with moss, built so long ago that its foundations had begun to sink back into the hillside, so that it seemed less built than grown. Pilgrims had stopped coming before Sable's grandmother was born. The new faith had no use for roadside shrines; their cathedrals were white stone and stained glass, their god spoke through bishops and burning. Whatever nameless thing this little house had been raised for had been forgotten by everyone except the mountain itself.

Sable used it sometimes. A smuggler learned every hollow and overhang in their territory, every place to wait out a patrol or stash a crate or sleep rough without freezing. The shrine had a dry corner behind the fallen altar slab where a body could curl up out of the wind, and in eight years Sable had never found anyone else's leavings there. The mountain kept its dead places to itself.

Which was why, when Sable crested the rise and saw the shrine sitting in the featureless white with its doorway dark and its threshold swept clean of snow—not drifted against, not buried, *clean*, as if the snow itself declined to cross it—every hair on their arms stood up beneath their oilskin coat.

They stood in the falling snow and considered walking on.

It would have been easy. It would have been the sensible thing, the survival thing, the thing the last eight years had been training them for: see nothing, know nothing, owe nothing. The road forked fifty paces below the shrine. The lower path led down toward the river valley and warmth and distance from whatever waited in that dark doorway. Nobody would ever know Sable Fairweather had passed within shouting distance of a mystery and chosen not to shout at it.

Their boots crunched forward anyway.

*Damn it,* they thought, without much conviction. Eight years of discipline, undone by a swept threshold. But that was the thing about thresholds—someone had crossed this one recently, someone small enough and desperate enough that even the weather had decided to make an exception, and Sable had been that person once, half-dead on a stranger's road, and some debt like that didn't stay paid just because you'd forgotten who held the note.

The interior smelled of cold stone and old incense, a sweetness gone to dust but still stubbornly present, soaked into the walls by centuries of offerings. Sable's eyes adjusted slowly. Light came through gaps in the slate roof in pale gray columns, each one full of drifting motes and slow-falling snow that had found its way in and lost its urgency, settling onto the floor like guests who'd forgotten why they came.

Behind the fallen altar slab, in the dry corner, something breathed.

Sable heard it because they had spent eight years learning to hear breathing—the difference between a sleeping man and a feigning one, between exhaustion and injury, between fear held tight and fear about to break loose. This breathing was shallow and fast and trying very hard to be silent, which meant whoever owned it had been hiding from something for long enough to have learned the habit badly.

"I can hear you," Sable said quietly, in the tone they used for skittish mules and frightened clients. Not threatening. Just present. "I'm not church. I'm nobody."

The breathing stopped entirely, which was worse.

Sable crouched, keeping their hands visible, and leaned to look past the altar slab. In the corner, wedged into the angle where wall met floor, was a child.

A girl, maybe nine years old, maybe ten—it was hard to tell under the grime and the hollows. Dark hair cut short and raggedly, as if with a knife. She wore a nightdress under a man's coat three sizes too large, sleeves rolled and rolled and rolled again, and her feet were wrapped in rags that had been white once. Frost had gotten into the wrapping. Her lips had the bluish tinge that Sable knew from bad winters and worse roads, and her eyes—

Her eyes were open, fixed on Sable, and they were the color of a sky full of rain that hasn't decided yet.

"Please," she said. Her voice was a ruin, scraped down to almost nothing. "Don't tell them."

*Them.* Sable's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. They thought of the inquisitors in Greyhollow, their clean gray cloaks and their patient questions, the ledger with its careful entries. *Someone is asking specifically about exiles.*

"Who's looking for you?" Sable asked, though they already knew, and the girl must have heard the knowledge in their voice, because she didn't answer. She just pulled the oversized coat tighter and looked at Sable with the particular steadiness of someone who has already run out of options and is now only deciding how to meet the end of them.

She was shivering so hard the frost in her foot-wraps crackled.

"Gods' teeth," Sable muttered, and the curse came out sounding like a prayer, which was fitting, because everything in this shrine suddenly felt like the inside of a bell.

*Leave her,* said the voice of eight years' discipline, calm and reasonable as an accountant. *She's church business. She'll be found or she won't. You have cargo waiting in Lowmark and a reputation for discretion that took eight years to build and ten minutes to lose if anyone sees you carrying a fugitive child out of a haunted shrine. You survived Greyhollow by walking away. Walk away now.*

Sable unshouldered their pack instead.

They worked fast, hands moving on old habit—blanket out first, the good wool one, then the flint and a handful of dried peat from the shrine's ancient woodpile, because even abandoned holy places kept their stores if you knew where the piety had rotted through. The girl watched all of it without moving, without speaking, tracking Sable's hands with those rain-colored eyes like a wild thing deciding whether the trap was kinder than the cold.

"Eat," Sable said, pressing a strip of dried venison into one small blue-tinged hand. The fingers wouldn't close around it. Sable folded them closed themselves, and—

Static leapt.

It cracked off Sable's fingertips into the girl's skin with a snap loud enough to echo off the stone, a blue-white arc that left the smell of lightning in the air and made them both flinch back. Sable stared at their own hand. Their hand stared back, ordinary, callused, faintly smoking at the tips.

That shouldn't happen. Static happened in wool and dry air, happened to everyone, was nothing, was *explainable*—but Sable had lived inside their own skin for thirty-one years and knew what lived there, what they had starved and buried and refused for eight years, and what had just jumped between them and this child had come from *both* directions. It had leapt *to* Sable. Something in the girl had reached for the storm in them the way a drowning thing reaches for light.

And then Sable heard the voice.

Not aloud. Not in either ear. It arrived the way pressure arrives before a storm—a change in the air, a weight behind the eardrums, a certainty that the sky had leaned close enough to whisper:

***oh***

Just that. One syllable, vast and wondering, spoken with the tone of someone finding a thing long lost in a pocket—a key, a coin, a name. It passed through Sable like weather through an open window, stirring everything, taking nothing, and was gone.

The girl was staring at them. Her lips had parted. The frost-blue had receded from her mouth, and her eyes had gone wide and wet and terrified in an entirely new way.

"You heard her too," she whispered.

Sable's mouth was very dry. "Heard who?"

"The lady in the storm." The girl's voice cracked. "She's been singing to me my whole life. Mama said I made her up. But—" She pointed a shaking finger at Sable's still-faintly-smoking hand. "She knows you. She said—she said *there you are*." The child's face crumpled. "I don't want to be special. Special is why they burned our house."

Outside, far down the valley, a sound carried through the falling snow: dogs. Many dogs, baying in that disciplined rhythm Sable knew from border patrols and bounty runs, and beneath them the thin brass note of a horn blown twice—the signal for *quarry sighted, close the net.*

The girl flinched at the sound like a struck animal and began, silently, to cry—not sobbing, just leaking, tears freezing on her cheeks while her body shook with cold and her eyes stayed locked on Sable's face, waiting to learn whether she had been found or rescued.

Sable looked at her. At the burned house she'd mentioned and the burned faith she represented and the burned world that was coming up that valley behind a pack of dogs. They looked at their own hand, which had finally, after eight years of obedient silence, touched something that answered.

*Walk away,* said the accountant voice, one last time, and for the first time it sounded afraid.

"Can you stand?" Sable asked.

The girl nodded. She couldn't—when she tried, her legs folded like wet paper—but she got one arm over Sable's shoulders, and together, in the falling snow that had begun, very softly, to fall harder on the southern approaches to the shrine and nowhere else, they went out the back, down the goat track Sable alone knew, into the deep fold of the mountain.

Behind them, the dogs bayed at an empty shrine. And somewhere above the clouds, in a voice like distant thunder remembering how to speak, something sang.
