## 4. The Inquisitor's Patience

Inquisitor Malachi Hale believed that the soul, like a house, could be read by its drafts.

He had learned this from his mother, a seamstress in the salt-fens of Corvale, who could stand at a doorway and tell him which rooms of a house were lived in and which were shut, which windows leaked, which hearths were tended and which had gone cold. *Warm air tells the truth*, she used to say, pressing her chapped hand to his cheek. *Cold air lies.* He had been nine years old, and he had thought it was only something mothers said. It had taken him twenty years and four hundred interrogations to understand that she had handed him the whole of his vocation in a single sentence, wrapped in kitchen-warmth like a coin in bread.

Now he stood at the doorway of the waystation inn at Greyhollow's western gate, and he listened to the drafts.

The common room was warm in the way mountain rooms are warm—woodsmoke and wet wool and the sour breath of too many bodies sharing a small fire—but beneath it ran other currents. Fear, sharp as struck flint, coming off the innkeeper every time Hale turned toward her. A duller fear, banked like a covered ember, from the drover nursing ale by the window. And under all of it, so faint he might have imagined it: grief. Someone in this room had lost someone recently, or was about to.

He did not ask about it yet. Asking was the last instrument in the case, not the first. First came patience; then observation; then, if the soul required it, the question that opened the door all the way.

"Brother Inquisitor." The innkeeper set down his bowl of barley soup with hands that trembled only slightly, and for that steadiness alone Hale marked her as braver than most. "I hope it's to your liking. We don't get much call for—"

"It is generous," Hale said, and meant it. Hunger made men cruel judges of soup, and he refused to be a cruel judge on an empty stomach. He bowed his head briefly over the bowl, the old gesture of the New Dawn—the dawn received, the day begun—and when he looked up, her shoulders had come down half an inch. Good. Terror closed throats. Comfort opened them, eventually, like frost leaving a latch.

He ate slowly. Around him, the room performed its careful pantomime of ordinary evening: dice rattling without enthusiasm, a fiddle tuned but not played, conversation pitched just low enough to be deniable. They had been doing this for three days now, ever since he arrived in their grey stone town with his grey cloak and his two grey-horsed attendants, asking questions no one wanted to answer.

*Have you prayed lately? Have your prayers been answered?*

It seemed such a gentle question. It was, in fact, the most dangerous one the Church of the New Dawn possessed, and Hale wielded it with the respect dangerous things deserve.

* * *

He worked by lamplight in the small room they'd given him above the stable, the one with the window facing the pass. On the desk before him lay the reports, and he arranged them not in the order they'd arrived but in the order they'd happened, because truth kept chronology even when men did not.

Item: the shepherd Aldric Voss, of the high summer pastures above Kettle Gap, who swore before two witnesses and a shrine-lamp that on the ninth night of the harvest month, lost in fog with his flock scattering, he had cried out to *anything that could hear him*—the old formula, the desperate formula, the one the Church tolerated precisely because it never worked—and that the fog had parted in a straight line, a road through the cloud, precise as a mason's cut, leading him home. Hale had interviewed Voss himself, three days ago, sitting across from him in a room much like this one. The man's fear had been real, and so had something else underneath it, something that took Hale a long time to name. When he finally did, he wrote it in the margin of his report and immediately regretted the word:

*Gratitude.*

Not the gratitude of a man who has stumbled lucky out of danger. The gratitude of a man who believes he has been *noticed*. Who has spent his whole life shouting into a silence and heard, once, unmistakably, a voice say *this way*, and cannot unknow it, and does not entirely want to.

Item: the storm of the twelfth, recorded by the toll-keeper at Serpent's Stair, who kept meticulous weather logs as toll-keepers do, since weather was money in the passes. A squall cell, sudden and localized, that had struck the smugglers' road—the road that officially did not exist—with a precision the toll-keeper described, in writing, as *"uncanny, brother, I'll not soften it, thirty years I've watched these mountains and I have never seen lightning choose."*

*Choose.* Hale underlined the word twice, then drew a line through both underlinings, because a report that betrayed eagerness was a report that invited correction from superiors eager to correct.

Item: the avalanche at Hollowmere Pass, fourteen days past, which had buried a church convoy carrying tithed grain. Six dead. And one survivor found alive at the center of the slide, in a pocket of air that should not have existed, sheltered beneath a slab that should have crushed her—a girl of perhaps ten years, unmarked, unfrozen, though the night around her wreckage had dropped cold enough to kill two grown men huddled in the same rubble not ten feet away. She had walked out of the slide at dawn, barefoot on ice, and asked the first person she saw, very politely, whether they knew the way to somewhere warm.

Hale had the transcript of her recovery interview. He had read it eleven times.

*"Where is your family, child?"*

*"They didn't make it under."* A pause. *"Something held the roof up for me. I heard it breathing."*

*"What did it sound like?"*

*"Like my grandmother. But my grandmother died before I was born."*

And then, the line that Hale had copied out separately, on its own card, and carried now in the inner pocket of his coat where it lay against his chest like a coal that would not cool:

*"It said I shouldn't be afraid. It said it remembered being afraid, and it hated making anyone feel that way."*

He touched the card through the fabric of his coat, an unconscious gesture, the way a man touches a wound to check if it still hurts. It still hurt. Because here was the fracture running through the bedrock of his certainty, and he could not mortar over it no matter how many times he tried: the voice in the child's account was *kind*.

It should not have been kind. Everything Hale had been taught, everything the Church of the New Dawn had bled to establish across three generations, insisted that whatever lingered from the age of the old gods was a residue, a haunting, a hunger wearing the mask of divinity. The Dawn Doctrine was plain: the gods of the elder world had not died in the Sundering War but *broken*, shattered into fragments too vast to die and too damaged to live, and those fragments endured now only as parasites endure, in borrowed warmth, in stolen breath. Every miracle of the old faith was a lure; every answered prayer a hook; every wonder a mouth opening.

He believed this. He wanted the record to show, always, that Malachi Hale believed it—not from convenience, not from career, but from conviction earned in blood. He had seen what the fragments did when they found purchase. He had stood in the village of Ashenford eleven years ago, after the thing in the well had spent a summer answering prayers, healing cattle, mending roofs, drawing the faithful close and closer until the whole village had knelt together at the wellhead on the autumn equinox, and he had seen what remained of them afterward, kneeling still, smiling still, their devotion hollowed out like gourds with the meat scraped away. Forty-one people. He had known three of them by name before the end. He had promised the youngest, a boy called Tam who had mended his bootlace once on the road, that he would save him.

He had arrived four days late.

So yes. He believed. Believing was the load-bearing wall of everything he was, and a man does not tap his own load-bearing walls.

But the voice in the child's account was kind, and kindness was expensive, and parasites did not pay for what they took.

* * *

On the fourth evening, the drafts told him the room was ready.

He felt it as he came down the stairs—the quality of attention in the common room had changed, the way snowpack changes before it slides. The drover by the window was watching him openly now instead of pretending not to. The innkeeper's fear had shifted registers: less dread of the question, more dread of the answer she was deciding whether to give. People did not decide for days. People decided in moments, and then spent days working up the courage of the decision.

Hale ordered tea he did not want, sat where the fire's light reached him fully—he never questioned anyone from shadow; shadow was the tool of men with something to hide, and he hid nothing, and he wanted them to see that—and waited.

She came to him near midnight, when the last dice had gone quiet and her husband had gone up to bed. She stood beside his table wringing her apron, and she spoke without preamble, the way people speak when they have rehearsed past the point of rehearsal and must simply fall forward.

"My sister lives up past the Stair. Her boy took fever at the turn of the month. Fever like drowning, brother, like the breath going out of him and not coming back, and she prayed—to the Dawn, to the Vigilant Flame, she's devout, we're all devout—and nothing. Nothing. And then in the worst hour of it, near morning, she says she just... stopped praying proper. Stopped praying *right*. And she said the old words. The ones you're not supposed to. Just to say she'd tried everything." The apron twisted. "Her boy woke at dawn. Whole. The physician couldn't account for it. He wrote 'spontaneous remission' in his book and wouldn't meet her eye."

"And she reported this?" Hale asked gently. "To her parish priest?"

The look she gave him was almost pitying. "Brother. She has a son. What would you have her report?"

The honest answer—that a soul healed by an unclean power was a soul mortgaged to it, that the debt always came due, that he had seen the interest collected in Ashenford—rose in his throat and stopped there. Because her question had a second edge, and the second edge was aimed at him: *what would you have her report, knowing what the Church does with reports?* There had been the boy in Marrowdale, two years back, whose answered prayer had ended with him on a pyre while his mother screamed herself mute. Hale had authorized the pyre. He had stood witness, as doctrine required. He had told himself the fire was mercy—better cleansed now than hollowed later—and some nights that belief held him, and some nights it lay on his chest like a stone, and he had learned to sleep anyway, because a man must sleep.

"I would have her report the truth," he said at last, "and I would see her protected for it. That is why I am here, and not someone crueller."

It was not entirely a lie. That was the trap he built for himself daily and stepped into nightly: he was not someone crueller, and he knew exactly what the crueler ones did with the reports his diligence gathered. His thoroughness fed the pyres whether he lit them or not. He had made his peace with this the way one makes peace with winter—by not looking directly at it.

"The storm-caller," the innkeeper said, barely above the fire's crackle, and Hale went very still, the stillness of a heron before the strike. "You've been asking about exiles. About ones who went into the mountains. There was talk, years back—before my time keeping this house—of one from the Stormsworn order who wouldn't do what the order wanted. Who vanished up into the Thornfells rather than—" She faltered. "Rather than drown a village, they say. Rather than use the storms on people."

"Who says?"

"Smugglers say. And smugglers say true, because they've no reason to lie to each other." She straightened, decision complete now, terror transmuted into the strange clean courage of the committed. "There's a guide works these roads. Eight years, maybe more. Nobody knows their true name. They take people over passes no one else can cross, and they always know the weather, and—" her voice dropped further, and here at last came the thing she had actually come to say, the pearl at the center of all her fear, "—and last month, brother, a party came down the Stair swearing that when the lightning fell on the ridge ahead of them, it fell in a pattern. Like writing. Like a warning, telling them the bridge below was gone. They lived. Everyone in that party lived. And they paid their guide double and kissed the ground at the bottom, and they won't speak of it to priests, but they'll speak of it drunk, and I listen when drunk men talk."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind moved along the eaves, and Hale listened to it with his mother's ears: a draft from the high country, cold and clean, carrying something that was not quite weather.

"You've done right to tell me," he said quietly. "You will be remembered in the Vigil."

She nodded, unconsoled, and went up to bed. And Hale sat alone by the fire with his untouched tea cooling, and laid out the shape of it in his mind the way he laid out reports: a storm that chose. A child the cold refused. Prayers answered along the smugglers' roads of the Thornfells, precisely where an exiled storm-caller had walked unseen for eight years.

The doctrine offered one reading: a fragment of the broken gods had found a vessel, a living lock grown careless, and was using it. The fragment must be located, the vessel purified, the door sealed. This was clean. This was the work. This saved souls—saved them the way a surgeon saves, by cutting.

But another reading sat in the dark behind the first, patient as winter: that the old words were being answered by something that warned travelers off dead bridges, that held roofs over children, that spoke in grandmothers' voices and hated causing fear. And if that reading were true—if any part of it were true—then the Church had spent three generations burning the innocent, and he, Malachi Hale, had fed the fires with his beautiful meticulous certainty.

He took out the card with the child's words on it and read it again by the firelight. *It said it remembered being afraid, and it hated making anyone feel that way.*

"Tomorrow," he said aloud to the empty room, to the draft from the high country, to whatever listened in the wind the way he listened in rooms. "Tomorrow I ride for the Stair."

He slept badly, and dreamed of a house full of open doors, and in every doorway stood his mother, listening.
