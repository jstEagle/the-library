## 5. Knights Don't Knock

The waystation smelled of pine smoke and old wax, and for the first time in eight days, Sable let themselves believe nothing was coming.

They had earned the belief honestly. The pass behind them was closed by rockfall—natural, they'd checked twice—and the road ahead wound down through country where the church's riders moved in packs of four and never left the low valleys before midmorning. The girl slept by the hearth with her boots still on, curled like a comma at the end of a sentence no one had finished. Every few hours her breathing stuttered, and the fire guttered, and Sable pretended not to notice that these two things happened together.

Wren had been with them three days now. Long enough for the frost to leave her cheeks. Not long enough for either of them to say out loud what the static between their hands had meant, or the voice—not a voice, a pressure, a *presence*—that had poured through Sable's bones like meltwater when they'd touched. They had agreed without agreeing to say nothing. It was the most honest arrangement either of them had made in years.

Sable sat sharpening knives they did not need sharpened. It was what they did instead of thinking. The whetstone sang its small gray song, and outside the wind worried the shutters the way a dog worries a bone it can't crack, and the mountains stood around the little building like an audience waiting for someone to make a mistake.

Knights didn't knock. That was the first thing you learned about knights, if you'd spent your exile learning things: they kicked doors, or they rode past them, or they burned them with everyone inside. A knock was a courtesy of people who expected to be welcomed, and no knight had ever expected welcome from anything Sable Fairweather kept behind a door.

So when the knock came—three measured raps, knuckle on oak, almost polite—Sable's knife stopped mid-stroke and every hair on their arms stood up under the wool.

*You're imagining it,* said the part of them that had survived eight years by being very good at imagining things weren't happening.

The knock came again. Same rhythm. Patient. The patience was worse than force would have been.

Sable set down the knife, crossed the room in four silent steps, and put one eye to the gap in the shutter.

A horse, gray as old dishwater, steaming in the cold. And beside it, standing in the falling light with snow gathering on her pauldrons like a second armor, a woman in mail that had been good once and was now merely true—dented, patched, the white surcoat over it dyed a flat travel-brown that fooled nobody who knew what to look for. She carried no lance. Her sword hung sheathed, the crosspiece wrapped in leather so it wouldn't flash. Her helm was off, tucked under one arm, and her hair was cropped short and going iron-gray at the temples, and her face—

Sable knew the face. Not personally. From woodcuts, from the scandal-sheets that circulated through Greyhollow's taverns the way water circulated through a sponge. *Ser Odessa Vane. Oathbreaker. The Knight Who Knelt to No One.* Stripped of rank two winters back for refusing an order the church had never admitted giving, court-martialed in absentia because she hadn't stayed to be court-martialed, her name scraped off the rolls at Vespergate with ceremony and salt.

She was looking directly at the shutter. At Sable's eye.

"Fairweather," she said. Her voice carried through the plank wall with the ease of someone used to making herself heard across a battlefield. "I know you're in there. I've been tracking you since Greyhollow, and I'm tired, and my horse is more tired, and I could do this the loud way but I'd rather not frighten your guest."

Sable went cold from the scalp down. *Your guest.* She knew about Wren.

"I have food," the knight added. "And a letter I was ordered to burn, which is why I haven't."

There was a long silence in which Sable considered, seriously and at length, the back window, the goat track behind the waystation, and the six-hour head start they might buy before dark. But the girl was sleeping by the fire, exhausted down to the marrow, and the rockfall had closed the other direction, and some animal instinct older than strategy said: *this one isn't lying.* Exiles recognized the smell of each other. It was something like wet stone and something like burnt bread and something like the specific loneliness of people who had once belonged to institutions.

Sable unbarred the door.

* * *

She filled the doorway the way weather fills a valley. Up close she was bigger than the woodcuts, broader through the shoulders, with a scar that ran from her left ear to the hinge of her jaw like a seam someone had sewn carelessly. She stood in the entry and did not come in until Sable stepped aside—an odd courtesy, or perhaps just the habit of a woman who had learned that entering rooms uninvited got people killed.

"Vane," she said, though they both knew Sable knew.

"Sable." They gave only the name. Names were currency; surnames cost extra.

Her eyes went around the room the way a soldier's eyes go around a room—exits, weapons, sightlines—and then settled on the sleeping girl, and something happened in Odessa Vane's face. It was small. Most people would have missed it. It was the look of someone finding a thing they'd traveled a long way to find and wishing they hadn't.

"She's the one," Vane said quietly. "From the shrine in the Hollow Vale. The child who lived."

"You've been tracking *her*?"

"I've been tracking the weather." She unbuckled her sword belt—slowly, deliberately, letting Sable watch every movement—and leaned it against the wall far from her hand. Then she pulled off her gauntlets, finger by finger, and set them on top. "And the weather kept leading me to you."

Sable didn't like how that sentence felt in their chest. "Sit," they said, because their mother's manners had outlived everything else, "and don't touch anything."

The knight sat. She took up half the bench. She produced from her saddlebag a cloth bundle and unwrapped it with the reverence of a priest handling relics: hard cheese, black bread, dried apples, a twist of salt. Real food. Army food, once, but real. Sable realized they were staring and made themselves stop.

"Eat," Vane said, pushing it across the table. "I'll talk while you chew. People argue less with full mouths."

"That's a terrible generalization."

"It's served me well in negotiations." For a moment—half a moment—something dry and almost warm flickered in her face, there and gone, like sun through moving cloud. Then it was gone, and she was all business again, and Sable understood that the warmth was a door she had bricked over and only forgot herself near, sometimes.

"The order came down from the Chancery of the New Dawn six weeks ago," Vane said. "Sealed. Eyes-of-the-Grand-Inquisitor-only. I was one of nine knights trusted to carry copies to the regional chapters." She paused. Sable chewed. Outside, the wind changed key.

"Directive Seven-Lambda," she went on. "Regarding persons designated *godseed*. You'll want to know what the word means before I show you the paper, so: it means children. Children in whom the old faith's... remnant... has taken root. The Chancery has known about them for longer than the public has known the word. There are records. Generations of records. Births tracked, baptisms falsified, households watched. And Directive Seven-Lambda is the end of all that watching." Her voice did not change at all. That was the horror of it—the flatness, the discipline holding a lid down over something enormous. "It orders extermination. All seed-bearers. Not interrogation, not conversion, not quarantine. Extermination. With a schedule. By season, by region, with quotas reviewed quarterly."

The fire popped. Wren stirred in her sleep and murmured something, and the flames bent sideways as if listening, and neither adult looked at each other, and both of them noticed.

"And you carried this," Sable said finally. "You delivered it."

"I delivered seven copies." She turned her cup—it was Sable's cup, she'd helped herself, another soldier's presumption—in a slow circle on the table. "To seven chapters. I watched men read it. Do you know what they did? They thanked me. They thanked me for the clarity of it. One of them wept with gratitude, because he'd been begging his superiors for permission for years and thought he was being ignored, and here was proof the church loved him after all." She looked up. Her eyes were the color of slate in rain. "The eighth copy was for the chapter at Cairnmoor. I was three days out when I understood I would rather be stripped of everything a second time than hand it over. So I burned it. And then I kept riding, because burning it wasn't enough—I needed someone who could read what its existence meant."

"Why me?" Sable asked, though a cold part of them already suspected, and hated the suspicion.

"Because you're a storm-caller." Vane said it plainly, no accusation in it, just fact laid on the table like the cheese. "Exiled, hidden, deniable. Because impossible weather has been following you through the Thornfells for two months like a loyal hound. Because prayers are being answered in the high passes and the answers sound like thunder." She reached into her breastplate and drew out a packet—oilcloth, then waxed linen, then a letter bearing a broken seal, its wax stamped with the rising-sun sigil of the New Dawn, cracked clean down the middle. "Because I need passage through mountains only you know, and I am prepared to pay for it with this, and with everything else I have, which is not much and mostly regret."

Sable looked at the letter for a long time. The firelight moved on the broken seal. Somewhere in the walls, the waystation's old timbers ticked and shifted, settling against the cold.

"If I read that," Sable said slowly, "I stop being a smuggler and become a witness. Witnesses get summoned. Summoned people get found."

"Yes," said Odessa Vane. "That's rather the point. I'm done carrying things for people who burn children. I'd like, for once in my life, to carry something toward the fire instead of away from it. Or—" and here her voice cracked, just barely, a fault line showing through plaster, "—or to carry it somewhere the fire can't reach. I don't much care which anymore. I just can't carry it alone."

Wren was awake.

Neither of them had heard her sit up—she moved like the small creatures she'd learned to be among—but she was sitting against the hearthstone with her knees drawn up, watching them with those too-old eyes, and the fire had gone strange and still, burning straight up like a candle flame in a sealed room.

"Godseed," she repeated. She said the word carefully, tasting it, the way you taste a fruit you've been told is poison. "Is that what I am? Is that what the word means?"

The silence stretched. Sable found they could not speak. It was Odessa Vane—oathbreaker, door-burner, the Knight Who Knelt to No One—who slid off the bench, crossed the room, and knelt in front of the child with the slow care of someone approaching a spooked animal, and said:

"It means you're exactly what they're afraid of. Which means, if you ask me, that you're worth more than everything they have." She held out the letter. "Do you want to see it? It's yours, really. More than anyone's."

Wren looked at Sable. Sable looked at Wren. Something passed between them—the static again, faint, a whisper of charge in the air like the minute before summer lightning—and Sable understood suddenly, with a lurch, that whatever was inside the girl had been listening the whole time. Had maybe always been listening. Had led the weather to them like a hound, just as Vane said.

"No," Wren said softly, pushing the letter back. "But keep it safe. Keep it where they can never print another one." And then, smaller: "Where are we going?"

Sable opened their mouth to say *nowhere yet*, or *somewhere safe*, or any of the lies guides tell frightened passengers.

What came out instead was: "South. Down the old burial road. There's someone there who maps places that shouldn't exist—and if anyone knows what's hunting you, it'll be her."

Outside, far off across the black ridgeline, thunder rolled. Clear sky. No clouds. Just the mountain, clearing its throat—or answering—and Sable closed their eyes and felt, unmistakably now, the watchfulness of the high passes settle over the waystation like a hand laid gently on a shoulder.

Three days ahead, Juniper Bell. Two days behind, whoever Vane's desertion would send looking.

And somewhere in between, the storm, waiting to be asked.
