## 6. Voices in the Rain

Wren had never told anyone about the voice, because telling was how you got caught.

She learned that early—earlier than she could name. There were rules in her village, unwritten but iron: don't sing the songs your grandmother sang unless they came from the hymnal; don't hum when you're happy, because humming turns to melody and melody turns to old words; and above all, if you hear something inside you answer when you pray to nothing at all, keep it behind your teeth until it starves.

Wren had kept it behind her teeth for eleven years.

It hadn't starved.

It wasn't a voice like her mother's voice, or the priest's voice, or even like the wind's voice, which was a real thing you could point to. It was more like a lullaby she'd been hearing since before she was born—as if someone had sung to her in the dark of her mother's body and simply never stopped. It didn't use words most of the time. Words were too small for it. Mostly it was a feeling, warm and low, like standing near an oven in winter, like being carried when you thought no one knew you were tired.

*Little one,* it would say, when it bothered with language at all. *Little one, grow.*

And Wren had grown, hating it, loving it, terrified of both.

Now she sat in the corner of Sable Fairweather's waystation with her knees pulled up to her chin, watching the storm come down off the Thornfells like a gray wall falling over, and she could feel the lullaby rising to meet it.

The waystation was one room, mostly, built into the shoulder of the mountain so that half of it was stone and half of it was timber and all of it smelled like woodsmoke and old rope and whatever Sable boiled in the black pot on the stove. Wren had been there three days. Three days since she'd run through the shrine door with the riders' torches behind her and found not a hiding place but a person—a tall, sharp-faced smuggler with storm-gray eyes who had looked at her like she was a problem and then, without saying why, had decided to be her problem anyway.

Three days since Sable had touched her hand to pull her out of the snow, and the static had jumped between them, blue-white, singing—and Wren had heard the lullaby stutter, startled, like a child caught eavesdropping.

Sable had snatched their hand back as if burned. They hadn't spoken of it since. They'd fed her barley soup, given her the blanket that smelled of cedar, pointed at the corner, and said, "Sleep. Don't touch anything. Don't go outside." Then they'd spent two days pretending she wasn't there, which was a kindness of its own kind, because pretending meant not asking, and not asking meant not knowing, and not knowing meant she couldn't be turned in.

But tonight the storm was coming, and Wren could feel the thing inside her turning toward it like a flower toward light, and pretending was going to get very hard.

* * *

She watched Sable work while the first rain hit the roof.

They moved like water finding its level—checking shutters, banking the fire, hauling the shuttered lanterns in from the eaves. Everything done twice, everything done without wasted motion. Eight years alone in the mountains, Wren thought. That's what eight years alone looks like. Every habit sharpened into survival.

"You should eat," Sable said, without looking at her. They always seemed to know where she was in the room. "There's bread."

"I'm not hungry."

"Liar. You're growing. Growing people are always hungry. Eat."

So she ate, because the bread was good and because obeying felt safer than arguing. The lullaby hummed under her ribs, pleased, patient. Outside, thunder rolled somewhere far away, deep and long, like furniture dragged across the sky by giants.

"Does it bother you?" she asked, before she could stop herself.

"Does what bother me?"

"The storm. Being stuck inside during it."

Sable paused, a log halfway into the stove, and gave her a look Wren couldn't read—something guarded moving behind the gray eyes like a fish under ice. "Why would it bother me?"

"Because you're—" She stopped. Bit it back. Too late.

"A storm-caller," Sable said flatly. "Because you think I'm a storm-caller."

"Everyone says the exiled ones hide in the passes. Everyone says—"

"Everyone says a lot of things." The log went in. Sparks climbed. Sable closed the stove door harder than necessary. "I'm a smuggler, girl. I move cargo and I move people who pay. I don't call storms. Storms come whether I want them or not. That's the whole truth of weather."

But Wren had seen the static jump between their skin and hers. She had heard—through the god inside her, which heard everything, which noticed things Wren didn't have ears for—the mountain itself lean toward this sour, silent, careful person like a dog leaning toward the hand that used to feed it.

She didn't say any of that either. She was good at not saying things. She'd had practice.

The rain thickened. The wind found the gaps in the walls and played them like reed pipes, low mournful notes. Wren wrapped the cedar-smelling blanket around her shoulders and tried to make herself small.

*Little one,* said the lullaby, warmer now, closer. *Listen. Listen. He is coming home.*

*Who?* she asked it, inside, the way you ask when you've stopped expecting answers.

*The one who holds our doors.* A pause, and then, gently, almost sadly: *The one who has forgotten he is holding them.*

Wren pressed her face against her knees. She hated when it talked like that. Hated it the way you hate a fever—because it lived in you and you couldn't put it down.

* * *

She woke to thunder directly overhead.

Not rolling—cracking, splitting, so close it felt like the mountain had snapped a bone. Rain hammered the roof in fists. The fire had burned to embers and the room was lit only by the dull red glow and the white flicker through the shutter seams, and for a moment Wren didn't know where she was, only that the lullaby inside her had gone taut as a bowstring.

*He is listening,* it sang. *He is listening, little one, listen with us—*

"No," Wren whispered into the blanket. "No, please, not here, not him—"

Across the room, Sable sat up in their cot.

Wren saw it happen, and afterward she would turn it over and over in her mind like a stone with a vein of gold in it, trying to see every side. Sable Fairweather, who did everything deliberately, who checked shutters twice and spoke in half-sentences and never once in three days had made a sound in sleep—sat up straight as a struck bell, eyes open, unblinking, fixed on the window.

And when the thunder cracked again, Sable's mouth opened and a voice came out that was not Sable's.

It was a woman's voice. Or it was a man's. Or it was both, braided, the way a chord is many notes and still one sound. It was old the way mountains are old, and it filled the little waystation like water fills a cup, and every hair on Wren's arms stood up because she knew it—she had known it since before she was born.

*"Little lock,"* the voice said, through Sable's lips, gentle as snowfall. *"We do not mean to frighten. We mean only to ask."*

Sable's hands gripped the edge of the cot hard enough that Wren heard the wood creak. Their face was slack, terrified, present—the rest of them taken, the eyes still their own. That was the worst part, Wren thought. The eyes stayed. Whatever else borrowed the mouth, Sable was still in there, watching from behind their own face.

"Stop," Sable rasped, between thunderclaps, in their own rough voice. "Get out. Get—I didn't say—you can't just—"

*"You never said no,"* the chorded voice answered, and there was something in it that might have been apology. *"Eight years, little lock. We knocked softly every season. You never once said no. You only never said yes. So we waited, as we have always waited. We are very old. We know how to wait."*

"Wait for *what?*" Sable's voice cracked on it.

The lightning flashed, and through the seams of the shutters the whole world went white, and in that white Wren felt the god inside her rise up like a tide answering the moon, and the chorded voice coming out of Sable softened, and softened, and became—for three heartbeats, four—the lullaby. The exact lullaby. The one from before birth. Sung aloud, in a stranger's throat, in a smuggler's waystation, in a storm.

*"To be let go,"* it said. *"Nothing more. We ask so little, and we have asked it so long."*

Then it was gone.

The thunder rolled away eastward, grumbling, reluctant, like a guest leaving. The rain settled into an ordinary drumming. Sable sagged forward onto their knees on the cot, breathing hard, shaking, both hands pressed flat against their skull as if to hold it together by force.

The embers ticked. The wind sighed through the wall-gaps.

Wren realized she was standing. She didn't remember standing. She was across the room, barefoot on cold boards, the blanket fallen behind her, and she was looking down at Sable Fairweather kneeling in the ruins of their invisibility, and the god under her ribs was singing so loudly and so tenderly that her own chest hurt with it.

"Don't look at me," Sable said. Their voice came out shredded. "Don't—go back to your corner. Go back to—"

"What did it ask you?" Wren whispered.

Sable's head came up slowly. In the ember-light, their eyes were wet and furious and lost. "You heard it."

"I've heard it my whole life." The confession fell out of her like a tooth, painless and terrible, gone before she could catch it. "Not—not like that, not out loud, it doesn't talk like that, it sings. It's a lullaby. I thought everyone heard it until I was four and I hummed it in front of the priest and my mother went white as bone and after that I never—" Her breath hitched. "It sings to me. It's been singing to me since before I was born. And tonight it sang through *you*, and it was the same song, it's the same song, it's—"

"Say it," Sable said quietly. Not a command. Almost a plea. "Say what it is. Because I need to hear someone else say it, because if it's only me, then I'm mad, and if I'm mad then none of this—"

"It's a god." Wren's voice shook. "An old one. One of the buried ones. It lives in me. It's lived in me since the beginning and it never once asked for worship, it never asked for anything except—"

"Growth," Sable said. "*Little one, grow.*"

Wren stared. "How do you know that?"

"Because that's what it just said to *me.*" Sable laughed, and it was the worst sound Wren had ever heard, a laugh with no floor under it. "Eight years I hid. Eight years I strangled every spark of it, never called, never reached, never prayed, never *anything*, because I swore I'd rather die than be what they wanted me to be. And the whole time—" They pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "The whole time something was knocking politely at my door asking permission, and I thought it was just memory. Just the old ache. I thought it was homesickness."

Outside, the rain gentled. Somewhere far off, thunder muttered one last word and fell silent.

"The static," Wren said. "When you touched my hand. At the shrine."

"When I touched your hand," Sable agreed, lowering their arms, "something recognized me. Something *old* recognized me. And I told myself it was exhaustion. I'm very good at telling myself things." They looked at her then—really looked, the first time in three days, the first time since the snow—and their expression shifted through fear and wonder and landed somewhere raw. "It said it was waiting. Eight years. What was it waiting for, Wren?"

"For you to say yes."

"To what?"

"That's what it asked tonight. Through you." She hugged the blanket around herself, though she wasn't cold; the god ran warm, always warm, oven-warm, hearth-warm. "It said it wants to be let go. That's all. It's been bound so long and it only wants to be let go, and it can't leave without—" She faltered, because the next part frightened her worse than the riders ever had. "Without a storm-caller's consent. It said that once. When I was seven. It said *only a lock may open the door, little one, and you are not the lock, but you will lead me to it.*"

The silence stretched thin as wire.

Sable rose slowly, joints cracking, and crossed to the window, and put one eye to the seam in the shutters, watching the rain fall silver through the last of the light. Their shoulders were rigid.

"They bred us for it," they said at last, barely audible. "That's the story they tell the novices, or half of it—that storm-callers were made to hold back the wild weather, to guard the passes. But it was never the weather we were holding." A breath. "Was it."

"I don't know what you were holding."

"No," Sable said softly. "Neither did I. Until tonight."

They stood there a long moment, the smuggler and the child, in a waystation full of smoke and rain-sound, each carrying a piece of a truth neither wanted whole. Then Sable turned, and their face had closed again—but differently now, Wren thought. Not locked. Latched. Like a door deciding, carefully, whether to open.

"You should sleep," they said. "Both of us should sleep."

"And tomorrow?"

Sable hesitated at the edge of the ember-light. "Tomorrow I ride down to Greyhollow. There's someone there who maps roads that don't exist, and if anyone knows what a burial line looks like drawn in ink, it's her." They rubbed their hands together, as if the static still clung to them. "Sleep, Wren. And if the lullaby comes back—"

"It'll come back."

"—then listen to the words this time. All of them. Because I have a feeling," Sable said grimly, "that it's been telling us exactly what it wants for years, and we've been too busy being afraid to write it down."

They banked the embers and lay back down, and within minutes their breathing slowed into something like sleep, though Wren doubted it was truly sleep, and doubted they'd have much of it tonight either.

She curled in her corner with the cedar blanket pulled to her nose, and the storm moved off eastward, and the god inside her settled, satisfied, warm.

*Soon,* it sang, soft as ash falling. *Soon, little one. He is nearly ready to answer.*

And far below them, down in Greyhollow where the lamps were being lit against the early dark, Wren somehow knew without knowing how that a man in a gray coat was lighting a candle of his own, and setting it beside a map, and drawing a slow circle around the high passes.
