## 8. A Family Assembled from Scraps

**Sable**

The whiteout came down the mountain like a held breath finally released.

One moment the pass was merely grey and grumbling; the next, the world ended at arm's length. Snow moved sideways, upward, in spirals that obeyed no wind Sable could name. They had seen a thousand storms in eight years on the Thornfell roads, and this one felt less like weather than like a curtain drawn across the world so that whatever happened behind it would go unwitnessed.

"Waystation's close," Sable shouted into the roar. "Hand on my pack. Nobody lets go."

Four figures roped together with smugglers' cord and prayer. Odessa Vane brought up the rear, one gauntleted fist knotted in Wren's hood, the girl tucked against her back like contraband of the most precious kind. Juniper Bell stumbled between them, map case strapped to her chest, her lips blue, muttering what might have been curses or coordinates.

Sable had been planning to travel alone.

That was the thought that kept surfacing as they fought toward shelter, absurd and insistent: three days ago it had been just them and the mountain, invisible, unremarked, safe. Now they were dragging a fugitive child, an exiled knight, and a heretic cartographer through a blizzard toward a waystation that could hold maybe six people and had never once held company Sable chose.

The waystation materialized out of the white like something remembered rather than seen — a squat stone bothy wedged against the cliffside, door hanging crooked, chimney breathing smoke because Sable had banked the fire before leaving and some small mercy had kept it alive.

They tumbled inside in a clatter of snow and steel and swearing. Sable got the door barred, got the fire fed, got everyone stripped of the outer crust of ice before anyone stopped shaking enough to speak.

"We're going to die here," Juniper announced cheerfully from the floor, where she lay in a puddle of meltwater, staring at the ceiling beams. "Frozen in a smuggler's hovel, surrounded by criminals. I want it noted I predicted this."

"Noted," said Odessa, wringing out her cloak with a creak of leather.

"You can't note things when you're dead. There'll be no one left to note."

"I'll note it," Wren said quietly, from the corner where she'd curled herself as small as possible. "I have good handwriting."

Juniper sat up. Looked at the girl. Looked at Sable. "I like her better than either of you, and I've known her four hours."

Sable didn't answer. They were watching the storm through the shutter slit, watching the white devour the road, and feeling — beneath the dread they'd carried since Greyhollow, since the word *godseed* first crossed their path — something dangerously adjacent to warmth.

* * *

**Wren**

The room smelled like onions and woodsmoke and wet wool, and Wren thought it might be the best smell in the world.

She knew better than to think that. Good smells went away. Good rooms went away. She'd learned that in the church nursery, and again in the farmhouse, and again and again until learning it had worn a groove in her. But her body believed in the smell anyway. Her body had gone soft and heavy by the fire, and when Sable put a tin bowl of stew in her hands — actual stew, with barley in it, with fat floating on top like little coins — she held it in both palms and let the heat soak into her fingers before she dared eat.

"Eat, don't worship it," Sable said, settling across from her with their own bowl.

"It's better than the temple food."

"The bar is underground," Odessa muttered.

"The bar is subterranean," Juniper corrected, mouth full. "There's a difference. Subterranean implies effort."

Wren ate. The god in her blood was quiet tonight, or nearly quiet — just its usual hum under everything, like a hearth in another room. Some nights it sang. Some nights it pressed against the inside of her skin like a moth against glass. Tonight it only listened, and she wondered if it liked the stew too, if gods tasted things secondhand through the children who carried them, and whether that counted as cruelty or kindness.

"What's it like?" Juniper asked suddenly. "The thing you carry. If you don't mind me asking. I mind asking, but not enough not to."

Wren looked into her bowl. The adults had been careful around the question for days, circling it the way you circle a snare. But Juniper asked things flat-out, like reading coordinates, and somehow that made it answerable.

"It's like having a song stuck in your head," she said slowly, trying to make it true. "Except the song is bigger than your head. And sometimes it hums along when I'm scared. And it knows all the words to songs nobody taught me." She turned her spoon over. "It's mostly lonely, I think. It's been alone a long time."

Silence around the fire. Odessa's hand had gone still on her whetstone. Even Juniper wasn't chewing.

"Lonely how?" Sable asked, very carefully.

"Like someone standing outside a window in winter, watching people be warm inside. Not angry about it. Just... waiting to be let in. It keeps saying thank you when I let it talk. Isn't that strange? A god saying thank you."

Nobody answered. Wren watched Sable look at the fire with an expression she'd started to recognize — the face of someone doing arithmetic they hated.

Then Odessa cleared her throat and said, "When I was nine, I stole a pie off a windowsill in Corvane and was chased by a goose for a quarter mile," and just like that the room loosened, laughter cracking through it like thin ice giving way to water underneath, and Wren understood that the knight had done it on purpose, and loved her a little for it.

* * *

**Odessa**

Odessa Vane had spent eleven years learning exactly how much noise armor made, and she catalogued the sounds of the bothy the way other soldiers catalogued terrain: Sable moving quietly among their stores, always quiet, quiet the way prey animals are quiet; Juniper's scratch-scratch of charcoal on paper even now, even half-frozen; Wren's breathing, deep and even, finally the breathing of a sleeping child instead of a hunted one.

The girl slept curled against the far wall with her boots still on. Odessa hadn't taken them off for her. She'd wanted to. She'd stood there with her hands out and then remembered whose hands they were — hands that had held ranks and swords and sealed letters marked *burn upon reading* — and decided the girl could keep her boots.

"You're doing the thing again," Juniper said without looking up from her chart.

"What thing."

"The staring-at-the-child-like-you're-keeping-a-vigil thing. You know saints do that? In the old paintings. Vigil-keepers. Very popular subject before the new church burned most of the painters."

"I'm keeping watch."

"On what? The storm?"

"Yes."

Juniper set down her charcoal. In the firelight her ink-stained fingers looked almost clean. "Vane. Odessa. Can I say a thing you'll pretend not to hear?"

"No."

"You keep sitting where you can see the door and the girl at the same time. Every time. You angle your bedroll so it covers both. You gave her the driest blanket and told her it was the least damp, which is a lie, because you took the damp one." Juniper tapped the chart between them — the burial lines, the cluster-marks of children, the terrible elegant geometry of it. "You were ordered to burn that letter. Instead you walked it through two hundred miles of mountains to give it to the one person who might do something. That's not desertion, that's—" She waved a hand vaguely at the fire, the storm, the sleeping girl. "That's the other thing."

"There is no other thing."

"There's always an other thing. I draw maps for a living. Everything is somewhere."

Odessa said nothing. Outside, the wind screamed against the stones, and inside, Wren murmured in her sleep — a syllable or two of something old, something that made the firelight bend slightly, though Odessa would tell no one she'd noticed.

"I was good at being a knight," she said at last, because the storm made confession cheap and Juniper already knew anyway. "That's the worst part. I wasn't failed out. I was *good*. And I burned that letter because for the first time in eleven years, being good meant doing evil, and I couldn't—"

She stopped. The words wouldn't come out shaped right.

"Yeah," Juniper said softly. "I know. Go to sleep, vigil-keeper. I'll watch the door."

* * *

**Sable**

Sable woke before dawn out of habit and found the storm still raging and Wren already awake, sitting cross-legged by the dead fire, feeding it splinters with the concentration of a surgeon.

"You should sleep more," Sable said.

"I slept." Wren glanced over. In the grey light her eyes looked older than ten years, older than any child's. "Did you dream?"

"Why?"

"You talked in your sleep." Wren poked the fire. "Not words. Weather-talk. Like thunder learning to be a voice. The god does that too, sometimes. Talks in its sleep." She looked up. "Are you like me?"

The question landed in Sable's chest and stayed there, sharp-edged.

"No," they said. "I'm nothing like you. I'm the opposite of you. My order built the locks. People like me are the locks."

"People like you are people," Wren said, with the devastating simplicity of childhood, and went back to feeding the fire.

Sable busied themselves with breakfast — oats, dried apples, the last of the honey scraped from a clay pot — and tried not to notice what they were doing, which was measuring. Four bowls' worth, plus extra for whoever woke hungriest. Water melted and stored. Wood stacked to last past tomorrow, in case the storm lasted past tomorrow. In case there was a past-tomorrow with these people in it.

In case there was a spring.

They caught themselves at it, honey spoon halfway to the pot, and stood very still. Eight years of thinking in singles. One portion, one bedroll, one exit planned from every room. Now their hands were automatically calculating rations for four and their mind had drifted — treacherously, absurdly — to spring, to the low passes opening, to whether Wren would need new boots by then (she would), to whether Odessa could be persuaded to teach the girl to hold a staff properly, to whether Juniper's maps might show a road south where no one asked questions, where—

Where they could keep them. Where the scraps could stay stitched together.

"Don't," Sable whispered to themselves, and the wind outside rose as if agreeing, and they hated how much the sound felt like counsel.

But they made four bowls anyway. Five, counting seconds.

* * *

**Juniper**

By midday the storm showed no interest in ending, so Juniper spread her charts on the floor and did what she always did with unbearable things: she drew them.

"Come look," she said, and they came — Sable wary, Odessa wary-er, Wren immediately, because children haven't yet learned to distrust invitations.

"This is the Thornfells as the church maps them." Roads, passes, garrisons, shrines of the New Dawn rendered in neat ecclesiastical script. Then she laid the second sheet beside it — her own work, illegal in nine dioceses, beautiful everywhere. The same mountains, but threaded with lines no official map admitted: the old roads, the buried processional ways, the places where the land remembered being holy.

"And this," she said, tapping red marks scattered along the deepest line like beads on a string, "is every seed-bearer report I've confirmed. Every child the whispers mention. Look where they fall."

"They fall on the line," Wren said.

"On the burial line. Exactly. Every one." Juniper looked up at Sable. "They weren't hidden randomly after the war. They were seeded along the bindings — living anchors, spaced like stitches in a wound. Which means the church isn't hunting children at random either. Someone in the New Dawn has worked out the pattern. They're not burning souls. They're cutting stitches."

The fire popped. Wind moaned through the chimney gap, a long minor note.

"That boy they executed in Greyhollow," Odessa said quietly. "Last autumn. Everyone said he was a heretic. He was a stitch."

"And every stitch cut weakens what's underneath," Juniper said. "And what's underneath has been bound for three hundred years, and bindings under strain snap rather than fade. I've mapped avalanches my whole life, Vane. I know what a slope sounds like right before it goes."

Wren reached out and touched the red mark nearest the center of the line — the mark nearest, Juniper realized with a chill, to this very valley — and the candle flames all leaned toward her finger at once, gently, like flowers toward sun.

"Sorry," Wren said, snatching her hand back. "He does that when he's curious."

"*He*," repeated Sable.

"He doesn't like 'it.'" The girl shrugged, small and apologetic. "He says names matter more than anything. He's been waiting a long time for his."

* * *

**Wren**

Supper was barley porridge with the honey, and Juniper told a story about mapping a lake that turned out to be the back of a sleeping river-god, which was probably a lie but a wonderful one, and Odessa corrected the story twice with military precision, and Sable laughed.

Wren noticed it because she'd never heard it before — a short, rough sound, startled out of them like a bird flushed from cover, quickly suppressed. But it had happened. The lock-keeper, the exile, the person who watched doors and exits and never relaxed: Sable had laughed.

Later, while the others argued about whether stars had opinions (Juniper: yes; Odessa: absolutely not; Sable: refusing to participate), Wren sat with her bowl and felt the warmth of it all settle into her like the stew had, and thought about the word she'd learned in the church nursery for people who belonged to each other.

*Family.* They'd used it for the church. It had never fit right, like a coat sewn for someone else.

This fit. This badly lit, freezing, arguing, honey-sweet room fit, these four broken people stitched together by accident and storm, and Wren — who had lost everything twice over, who carried a god like a coal in her chest — thought: *maybe broken things are the only things that know how to hold each other.*

She laughed then, out loud, at Juniper's impression of a church prelate falling off a horse, a real laugh, bright and surprising, and the whole room turned to look at her, and the fire flared up gladly in the hearth as if it too had been listening.

"What?" Wren said, cheeks going hot.

"Nothing," Sable said, and their voice was strange and soft. "Nothing at all."

* * *

**Sable**

Night again. The storm raged on, three days old now and showing no sign of mercy, and Sable stood at the shutter slit listening to it and hearing — as they had been hearing for weeks, and admitting for none of them — voices in it.

Not madness. Not exhaustion. Voices, plural, layered like a choir heard through a wall, patient and vast and *waiting*, and underneath them, fainter, something that might have been Wren's god humming along.

Behind them, the bothy breathed in sleep: Odessa's disciplined rhythm, Juniper's snore, Wren's small sighs. Sable had checked the door twice and the stores once and caught themselves, again, counting toward spring. Boots for Wren. Juniper's cough seen to in Greyhollow's lower town, where the doctors asked no questions. A route south. A future, sketched in the margins of a smuggler's mind like a road on one of Juniper's impossible maps.

*Don't,* they told themselves again, and again the wind rose, and this time it carried something else in it — faint, from the east, threading through the storm's white roar:

Bells. Church bells, ringing in a pattern Sable knew from Greyhollow, from executions.

Far away, downslope, in the world below the snow — something was happening to the saints.

Sable stood a long time at the slit, hand on the cold stone, listening to the bells dissolve into the storm, and did not wake the others, and did not sleep at all.
