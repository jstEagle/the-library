## 12. Riders on the Ridge

Odessa Vane had spent eleven years learning how to be still.

It was not a skill they taught in the knightly orders. They taught you to charge, to hold, to die beautifully with your banner snapping behind you. Stillness was for sieges, and sieges were for other people. No one had told her that exile was mostly waiting—that the whole shape of a discarded life was standing at a window, or a treeline, or a doorway, listening for hoofbeats that might mean salvation or ruin and being unable to tell the difference until too late.

She stood now among the black pines above the temple entrance, snow to her knees, sword loose in its scabbard, and listened.

Below her, half-hidden by a shoulder of granite, the others were still moving through the buried dark. She could not see them—had not seen them since Sable led the descent into the earth three days past—but she could feel them the way you feel a fire burning in another room. Wren's laughter had gotten into her ribs somehow, lodged there like a splinter. Juniper's muttering over her impossible maps. Even Sable's silence had become something she knew how to read: the difference between *leave me alone* and *stay near.*

Three days. The godseed children's burial lines ran deep, Juniper had said, and the temple ran deeper still. Three days of Odessa keeping watch alone on the ridge with a bow she barely knew how to use, a sword she knew too well, and a letter she should have burned months ago folded against her heart like a second, heavier breastplate.

The wind came up the valley in long slow breaths, carrying the smell of pine resin and cold stone and, beneath it, something older—the mineral exhalation of the mountain itself, as if the rock were breathing around whatever slept below. Odessa had stopped pretending she couldn't feel it. Whatever the old gods were, whatever Sable carried in their storm-touched blood, this place was *alive*, and being alive meant being watched.

She almost missed the sound, because it arrived dressed as weather.

A raven called from the eastern crag. Then another. Then a third, and the third call was wrong—not the ragged croak of birds arguing over carrion but a clean, deliberate note, repeated twice, and Odessa's spine went cold before her mind caught up, because she had heard that signal eleven years ago on the marches of Vharen, when she'd worn white-and-gold and answered to a different name.

Church riders. Eastern approach. At least a patrol.

She did not run. Running made noise, and noise made dust, and dust made questions. Instead she sank into the snow shadow of the pines and counted, the way her first sergeant had taught her, back when she was fifteen and believed the Church of the New Dawn hung the sun in the sky each morning out of pure benevolence.

One hundred. Two hundred. On two hundred and forty, they crested the ridgeline to the east.

Sixteen riders. Her stomach dropped through the mountain. Sixteen was not a patrol; sixteen was a company detachment, which meant someone had done arithmetic—which meant Inquisitor Hale had finally finished his patient, terrible sums. They rode in wedge formation through the deep snow, bannerman at the center, and even at this distance she could see the sun-disc sigils catching the flat grey light, bright as open wounds against the white.

At their head rode a man in grey without insignia, and she knew him without knowing him, the way you know a blade by the way it catches light. Hale himself. He would not have come for anything less than certainty.

*They've found the entrance,* she thought, and the thought arrived with terrible calm, the calm that had always come to her in the last moments before a fight, the calm that her superiors had once praised as composure and that she now recognized as simply the body deciding, ahead of the mind, what it was willing to lose.

Because here was the arithmetic no one had trained her for: sixteen riders, one disgraced knight, and four people in a hole in the ground who had become, without her permission and against every vow she still technically owed, her family.

Odessa drew her sword.

Not yet. Not to fight—to check. The blade came out whisper-quiet, and she turned it in the thin light. It had been her father's, bought back from a pawnbroker in Greyhollow with money she'd earned hauling freight, and it was good steel despite everything, despite the disgrace, despite the letter. Along the fuller, someone had scratched a small mark she'd never asked about: a seven-pointed star, old style, the kind the smiths put on blades before the war. Before the binding. Before the church taught everyone to forget.

She slid it home and began to move down the ridge.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, fit inside a single breath: make them look at her. Make them look at her for as long as a person could look at one thing while four other things slipped away behind them.

She chose the ground the way she'd been taught to choose it at the Academy of the White Banner—chose the narrow throat where the trail bent between the rockfall and the drop-off, chose the angle where sixteenth-century cavalry doctrine said you never sent riders in column because the front ranks died stupidly. She wedged herself into the rocks above the bend, pulled her hood forward, and became the thing the mountains had made her: a smear of grey and shadow that might be stone until it moved.

The riders came on slower than she expected. That was Hale, she decided. A man who rushed was a man who hadn't verified. He would send scouts first, and he did—two of them, dismounting at the bend, picking along the trail with drawn swords and the particular hunched care of men who had been warned.

Odessa waited until they passed directly beneath her.

Then she stepped off the rockfall.

She landed on the first scout with both boots and took his momentum into a roll, and the world became very simple, the way it always did: elbow, edge, distance. The second scout got his sword halfway up before hers found his wrist, and he sat down hard in the snow clutching it and making a sound like a kicked dog. She didn't kill him. She wanted to be clear about that later, whoever later belonged to—she did not kill either of them, and she told herself the reason was tactical, that screaming drew the rest faster, and only admitted on the third repetition that the real reason was a boy named Tam who had knelt in a square in Greyhollow and forgiven a man in a grey cassock, and how that forgiveness had gone into her like a splinter of ice and never melted.

The horses screamed. Of course they did. Sixteen riders wheeled at the bend, and the wedge collapsed into the ugly confusion of mounted men trying to fight uphill in chest-deep snow, and Odessa Vane, formerly Knight-Captain of the Third Banner, formerly Ser Odessa, formerly nothing at all, gave them exactly what the doctrine said they feared most.

She fought downhill.

Gravity is a weapon. So is terror. She came down the slope in great sliding leaps, snow exploding around her boots, and hit the flank of the formation where the horses were already panicking, and for ninety seconds—she would count them later, ninety seconds exactly—the bend belonged to her. Two riders unhorsed. A bannerman dragged from the saddle by the wrist. Her father's sword singing that old seven-pointed song, and somewhere under the roar of her own blood she heard herself laughing, high and wild, and hated the sound because it was the sound of the woman she used to be, the one who had loved this, who had been *made* to love this, and who had learned better too late to matter.

Then the ninety seconds ended, because ninety seconds is all a single body gets.

A crossbow bolt took her through the meat of the left shoulder, low, punching through and out, and the cold that followed was so total she mistook it for death for one full heartbeat. Her sword arm kept working—muscle does not consult pain—but her feet betrayed her on the next leap, and the snow took her sideways, and suddenly there were hands on her, many hands, and a knee on her sternum, and the sky above was full of wheeling ravens and sun-disc banners and one grey figure walking down through the press of riders with the unhurried gait of a man arriving slightly early rather than slightly late.

"Enough," Inquisitor Hale said, and the riders enoughed.

Odessa spat blood into the snow. It steamed. "That all of you? Felt like more."

Hale crouched beside her. Up close he was unremarkable—mid-forties, ink-stained fingers, eyes the brown of old tea—with the terrible ordinariness of men who do monstrous things between meals. He studied her face with the attention of a scholar reading a difficult passage.

"Ser Odessa Vane," he said. "Third Banner. Court-martialed for insubordination and desertion in the field at Redford Bridge. Struck from the rolls." He recited it gently, like a lullaby. "You were declared dead in absentia two winters ago. Your mother received a sealed letter of condolence."

"My mother received a bill for the seal wax," Odessa said. "The church doesn't waste kindness."

Something flickered across his face—not anger. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing an argument he has already had with himself.

"You are holding a pass," he said, "alone, with a wound opening in your shoulder, against a full detachment. There is no strategic sense in it. You are buying time for someone." His gaze moved past her, up the slope, toward the rockfall, toward the shoulder of granite, toward the crack in the world that three people and a child had climbed down into. "For whom?"

"The snow," Odessa said. "I'm very fond of the snow."

He sighed, and stood, and gestured, and two riders hauled her upright, and the shoulder tore in a way that turned her vision white at the edges. She bit down on the scream and gave them the tail end of it only, a grunt, and Hale tilted his head at the sound with clinical interest.

"Bind the wound," he ordered. "She bleeds out, we learn nothing." He turned back to her, and now his voice dropped, quiet, almost private, the voice he must have used in confessionals before the war took those too. "Ser Vane. I will tell you something true, because I find that truth given freely is often returned in kind. We are looking for a child."

The mountain held its breath. Odessa held hers longer.

"A girl," Hale continued, "who survived a night in the Thornfells that killed three grown men of my own escort. A girl whose prayers were answered before any storm-caller was within fifty miles. A girl who is, if the texts I have begun to verify are correct—" and there it was again, that hairline fracture in his certainty, visible for only a moment, *begun to verify*, as though the church's word were not scripture but merely a claim— "carrying something older than my faith. And there are people in these mountains who believe she deserves to live."

"There are people in these mountains," Odessa said through her teeth, "who know she will."

Hale looked at her for a long moment. Snow settled on both of them alike, indifferent, democratic. Somewhere far below them, muffled by forty feet of stone, Odessa imagined she could hear Wren laughing, and she fixed on that sound the way a drowning sailor fixes on a star, except the star was imaginary and the drowning was not.

"Take her to the camp," Hale said at last, turning away. "The good tent. And Ser Vane—" he paused, half-turned, snow gathering on his grey shoulders until he looked like a cairn marking some dead traveler's grave— "your rank precedes you. The Commander of the Northern Reach will want you alive and intact. Consider that whatever mercy you believe I lack."

"I don't believe you lack mercy," Odessa said.

His eyebrows rose, just slightly.

"I think you've buried yours somewhere," she said, "and you're starting to forget which pass you left it in. I've dug in a lot of passes lately. I could draw you a map."

For one strange instant, the corner of his mouth moved—as close to a smile as a man like that could get, and perhaps it wasn't amusement at all but grief wearing amusement's coat—and then he walked away up the trail, and the riders closed around her, and the ravens went up off the crags in a black ragged sheet.

* * *

They bound her shoulder with church linen, which struck her as funny in the way exhaustion makes everything funny, and put her in a tent with a brazier and a guard who could not have been more than nineteen and kept looking at her sword where it lay wrapped in canvas on the ground between them, confiscated but close, close enough that they both kept glancing at it like a third prisoner.

The boy-guard cleared his throat four times before he spoke.

"They say you held the pass at Redford Bridge for a night and a day," he said. "Before. Before the court-martial. They say you held it when the order came to fall back, because there were villagers still crossing behind you."

Odessa looked at him. Young. Sun-disc stitched fresh on his collar, thread still bright. Hands that had maybe held a sword in anger once, maybe never.

"They say wrong," she said. "I held it because I was slow. Because I kept finding one more villager. If I'd been fast, I'd have obeyed the order, and I'd probably be a commander now instead of a corpse on the rolls." She leaned her head back against the tent pole and let the brazier's heat soak into the ruined shoulder. "Being slow saved more lives than being good ever did. Write that down somewhere."

The boy didn't write anything down. But he shifted his chair, very slightly, so that his back was to the tent flap and his face was to her, and after a while, in the voice of someone confessing to a shrine, he said: "In Greyhollow. The boy they burned. My cousin was in the crowd. She says he forgave the Inquisitor. Out loud. In a voice that—" He stopped. Started again. "Is it true? About the voices?"

Outside, the wind had begun to rise, coming down off the peaks in long slow breaths, and beneath it—so faint the guard clearly couldn't hear it, so faint she might have dreamed it—Odessa caught something else. A vibration in the ground, in the frame of the tent, in her own broken shoulder. Deep. Patient. Like thunder that had decided to wait.

Like a door, very far below, beginning to open.

"It's true," Odessa said, and reached, slowly, with her good hand, toward the canvas-wrapped sword.
