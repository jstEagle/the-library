## 13. The God Who Asks

The temple breathed.

That was the only way Wren could describe it, though she had never told anyone, because it was the kind of thing that made people look at you sideways—the way the church riders had looked at her before she ran, the way her mother had looked at her in the last year, when the lullaby got louder and Wren started humming songs no one had taught her. The temple breathed. Not wind through broken colonnades, not draft through collapsed corridors. It *breathed*, slow and vast and patient, the way a sleeping animal breathes, and Wren felt it against her skin like warm air from an open oven door, except there were no ovens down here, nothing burned, nothing cooked. Only stone that remembered being something else.

She sat cross-legged on the floor of what Juniper called the antechamber of voices, because Juniper named everything, even things that should have gone nameless. The walls around her were carved with figures so old the chisel-marks had softened into suggestions: tall shapes with storm-cloud crowns, kneeling shapes with their hands open, and between them lines that spiraled inward like water finding a drain. Sable's lantern sat on the floor between them, turned low, throwing their shadows huge against the carvings so that the kneeling figures seemed to kneel toward Wren specifically, which she knew was silly, and which did not make it feel less true.

Sable stood at the edge of the light with their arms crossed, wearing the face they wore when they didn't want to be here. Wren was getting good at reading Sable's faces. There was the smuggling face, all flat competence and narrowed eyes. There was the tired face, which was most faces now, deepened by the long winter pressing down on the mountain above them. And there was this one—jaw set, shoulders drawn up near the ears, the whole body angled toward the exit like a cat pretending it hadn't decided to leave.

"You don't have to stay," Wren said.

"I'm staying." Sable said it too fast, the way people said things they'd already argued themselves out of three times. "Juniper's mapping the lower galleries. Odessa's watching the entrance. Which means I'm watching you, which means I'm staying."

"You're scared."

"I'm *cautious*." But Sable's mouth twitched, almost a smile, quickly put away. "There's a difference. Scared is when your legs decide without asking you. Cautious is when your legs ask first and you tell them no."

"What do your legs say?"

"My legs are traitors." Sable crouched down across from her, lantern-light catching the gray streaks in their dark hair, the scar bisecting one eyebrow. Up close, in the low gold light, they looked less like the fearsome smuggler of Greyhollow rumor and more like what they actually were: someone exhausted who kept getting up anyway. "Wren. Last time we came down here, you spoke in a voice that wasn't yours. You told me my name—my real name, the one nobody's used in eight years—in a language I haven't heard since I was expelled. Do you remember that?"

"No."

"I didn't think so." Sable rubbed their face. "So we're going to do this carefully. You sit there. I sit here. If anything feels wrong—if it feels like drowning, like falling, like *burning*—you squeeze my hand and I pull you out. Understood? Not if it feels strange. Strange is Tuesday, now. Wrong."

"Strange is Tuesday," Wren agreed solemnly, and this time Sable's smile lasted a full second before it went away again.

She liked Sable best in these moments, when the armor slipped. She would never say that aloud either, because Wren had learned early that loving people out loud was how you lost them. Her mother had loved her out loud every day of her life right up until the morning she pressed a bread roll and a copper coin into Wren's hands and said *run north, don't stop, don't pray where anyone can hear you,* and Wren had run north and hadn't stopped and had prayed nowhere, and it hadn't mattered, because the thing inside her didn't need her prayers. It sang whether she prayed or not.

*It doesn't need your prayers,* she thought at it now, the way she'd learned to think at it, a door cracked open just enough. *It doesn't even want them. So what do you want?*

The temple breathed in.

And the lullaby changed.

* * *

It had always been a lullaby. That was the first thing she needed Sable to understand, and the hardest thing to explain, because everyone expected gods to sound like thunder and commandments. Hers sounded like her mother humming over a washtub, like the rhythm of walking, like rain on a roof you trusted. She had heard it since before she could remember hearing anything else. When she was very small she'd thought everyone had a song inside them and hers was simply louder than most. When she was six, the priest in her village had laid his palm on her forehead during blessing-day and gone white as flour and called her *vessel* in a voice full of horror, and after that she understood that whatever lived in her was not supposed to be there, was in fact the worst thing a person could be, worse than thief, worse than liar, worse than dead—and that the church burned vessels, publicly, on feast days, so the faithful could watch the smoke and feel clean.

So she had hidden it. She had learned to hum other songs over the lullaby, loud ones, ugly ones, tavern songs with words she wasn't supposed to know. She had learned to flinch away from blessings. And all that time, underneath the tavern songs, the lullaby went on, patient as water, never angry, never demanding. Never once—not in nine years—had it asked her for anything.

Until the temple. Until now.

The change was not dramatic. That was the terrible part, or the beautiful part; she couldn't decide which. The lullaby simply slowed, the way a speaker slows when they step up to a podium, the way her mother's voice had slowed that last morning when the singing was done and the saying-goodbye began. And then, for the first time in her life, it was not a song at all.

It was a voice. And it used her mouth.

"Sable Fairweather," said Wren, in a voice that was hers and deeper than hers, layered like a chord, "the binding weakens, and I am permitted at last to speak plainly, and I find I hardly know how. Forgive me. It has been a very long time since anyone asked me a question."

Wren felt herself go distant—present but peripheral, like standing behind a window watching someone use her hands. It didn't hurt. That was the other thing no one believed. The god had never once hurt her. The church had hurt her, the fear had hurt her, the running and the hunger and the frozen nights had hurt her. The voice had only ever been company.

Sable had gone very still across the lantern. Their face had done something complicated—she saw it even through her distance—fear and recognition fighting for the same ground.

"You know my name," Sable said carefully.

"We gave your order its names. Before your order forgot us. Before they built their locks and called the locks a calling." The voice—Wren's mouth—sighed, and the sigh moved through the temple, stirred dust from ledges that had not been disturbed in centuries. "I will be brief. Briefness is a mercy and I have little practice at it. Hear me, storm-child."

"I'm listening."

"The faith that burns these children believes we are conquerors. That we seed ourselves in mortal blood to bide our time, gather strength, and rise again to finish the war we lost. Your order believes it. The inquisitors believe it. Perhaps some of my siblings believe it, buried as they are; I cannot speak for the drowned ones or the buried ones, only for myself." A pause, and Wren felt it considering, feeling for the shape of honest words the way a blind person feels for furniture. "I am not a conqueror, Sable Fairweather. I am a prisoner. And I am tired."

"Tired," Sable repeated flatly.

"Tired." Something almost like laughter moved through Wren's chest, brief and rusted. "You expected thunder. You expected a demand for altars and blood and the burning of the new temples. Nine hundred years ago, yes—that was who I was, or partly who I was; we were not gentle, we were *gods*, and gentleness came later, if it came at all. But nine hundred years in the dark changes a thing. Ask your knight what eight years of exile made of her. Ask yourself. Now multiply it by thirty lifetimes." The voice grew quieter. "I do not want worship. Worship is a wall with a door in it, and I have been behind the wall so long the door has grown shut. I do not want war. War is how we came to be locked beneath your mountains, and I remember the ending of that war better than any living soul, and I would rather unmake myself than see it again."

"Then what," Sable said, "do you want?"

Wren felt the question land. Felt it land in the god the way a hand lands on a shoulder—startling, almost unbearable, wanted so badly it hurt. And she understood, suddenly, with a child's terrible clarity, why the voice had waited so long to speak plainly. Not because it couldn't. Because no one had asked.

"To be let go."

* * *

The word hung in the antechamber. The lantern flame leaned as if listening.

"That's it?" Sable's voice cracked on the second word. "Nine hundred years, all of this—" they gestured at the carvings, the breathing walls, the children burned by the hundreds across two generations "—and you want *to be let go?*"

"It is not a small thing." The god's patience had an oceanic quality; Wren floated inside it like a leaf in still water. "Your kind lets birds out of cages and calls it nothing. But consider what I am. I am weather that learned to think. I am the long rain and the thaw-wind and the storm that ends a drought, and I was bound into stone by your order's founders because I ended a different kind of drought—a war-drought, a blood-drought—with too much rain. They were right to bind me. I will grant them that much. We were doing harm. We had forgotten how to stop." Another pause, longer. "But a lock that holds forever is not justice, Sable Fairweather. It is only a lock. And your order bred its storm-callers to be the locks' keepers—to pour your lives into the binding, generation upon generation, holding the door shut with your storms. That is what you are. Not a weapon. A *hinge*. Did they tell you that, when they exiled you? Or did they let you believe you were only a failure?"

Wren watched Sable absorb it, watched it move through them like a swallowed stone. Eight years of shame rearranging itself into something else. She wanted to reach across the lantern and touch their hand but her body was not entirely hers to move, and besides, she suspected that touching Sable right then would break something delicate that needed to finish breaking on its own.

"They let me believe I was a coward," Sable said finally. Very quietly. "For refusing to drown Correnvale. For refusing to turn my storm on a village that wouldn't pay the order's tithe."

"And you were not a coward. You were a hinge refusing to swing the wrong direction. It is the only refusal your order truly fears." The voice softened further, until it was nearly the lullaby again, nearly the mother-hum, and Wren realized the god was choosing its tone deliberately, gently, the way you approach a frightened animal. "Now hear the rest, because it is the rest that matters. I cannot leave on my own. The binding is a knot tied in weather, and only weather can untie it—but not weather commanded. Weather *consenting*. The knot knows the difference. It was designed to know the difference, by people who feared exactly what I could do with a coerced storm-caller." A breath; the temple drew it in with her. "A storm-caller must open the door willingly. Must choose it. No one can force you, no god, no order, no inquisitor. That is the flaw in every plan ever made about you, Sable Fairweather, including the plans of the gods. You are the one unownable thing in this world. And that is why they exiled you rather than killed you, though I doubt they knew it. A lock that cannot be forced must be persuaded—and you were sent into the mountains to be worn down by loneliness until persuasion became possible."

The silence afterward was enormous.

Wren felt Sable's heartbeat through the floor, fast, then slowing, then fast again. Felt the exact moment the understanding arrived, the way she imagined a man feels the moment he realizes the tide has turned beneath his boat.

"They've been asking," Sable said hoarsely. "All these years. The answered prayers. The storms that carried voices. That wasn't—I wasn't channeling you. You were—"

"Asking permission. Politely. For years." Yes. Weariness and wonder braided together in the old voice. "Every prayer that fell unanswered onto deaf stone, I caught what I could. Every storm you cursed in passing, I shaped a little closer to kindness, hoping you would notice, hoping you would understand: *not take me, not use me—free me.* But permission cannot be hinted. It must be offered with open eyes, by someone who knows the whole of what they are offering. So I waited. And then this child walked into my mountain carrying me in her blood, and I heard her heart, and I heard your footsteps behind her, and I thought: perhaps. Perhaps now. Perhaps this stubborn, frightened, exiled hinge."

"Why me?" Sable's voice was barely there. "There are other callers. The order—"

"The order will seal the doors and call it holiness. I have watched them do it for nine hundred years." The voice turned, then, and Wren felt it settle on her—not heavy, never heavy, but *attentive*, the way sunlight is attentive through a window. "And there is this child, and others like her, scattered along our burial lines like seeds after a harvest. Every year the bindings thin, and every year more of us press against the doors, and every year the church burns another child whose only crime is being the shape of a keyhole. I cannot free myself. I cannot free them. But a storm-caller who consents—" the voice faltered, actually faltered, and Wren marveled at it, a god stumbling over hope, "—a storm-caller who consents does not merely open my door. They open all of them. The knots are kin. What releases one releases the pattern. You would not be letting out a prisoner, Sable Fairweather. You would be ending a prison."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I return to waiting. I have gotten good at waiting. I will not haunt you, or punish you, or press against your sleep. That is the promise of a thing that remembers being a god and is trying, very hard, to become something better." The voice gentled to almost nothing. "But I will tell you what refusal costs, because you deserve the whole truth, and the whole truth is this: the church will burn Wren regardless. The order will seal this place regardless. Refusal saves no one. It only keeps your exile comfortable a little longer. I do not say that to coerce you. I say it because coercion is the old way, and I am finished with the old way, and if I am to die as a prisoner I would at least die having spoken honestly to the person who was kind to a cold child in a ruined shrine."

* * *

Then the presence withdrew—not gone, Wren could always feel it, a warmth under her ribs like a banked fire, but *withdrawn*, folding itself back down into the quiet it had kept for nine centuries—and Wren was wholly in her own body again, blinking in the lantern light, her cheeks wet without her noticing, her hands shaking in her lap.

Across the circle of light, Sable sat exactly where they had been, but they were not the same person who had sat down. Wren watched them stare at the carved figures on the wall—at the kneeling shapes with open hands—and saw them seeing the carvings properly for the first time. Not worshippers. Not prisoners dragging chains. Supplicants, maybe. Or witnesses. People recording, in the only way stone remembers, that something vast had knelt down to their level and asked.

"Eight years," Sable said, to no one, to the walls, maybe to the god. "Eight years I thought the mountains were watching me. Waiting for me to slip. I thought it was the order's spies. I thought I was paranoid." A short, disbelieving laugh with no humor in it. "It was you. It was all of you. Asking. The whole time, *asking*."

Wren wiped her face with her sleeve. "Are you angry?"

"I don't know what I am." Sable dragged both hands through their hair. "I spent eight years building a life out of hiding. Smuggling. Keeping my head down. Keeping my magic so buried I sometimes convinced myself it was dead. And the entire time, the thing I was hiding from was knocking politely at the door, hoping I'd answer." They looked at her then, and their expression was the one Wren had come to trust most and fear most—the one that meant Sable was about to say something true. "Do you know what they'll do, if I say yes? Not the god. The *people*. Hale, the order, the church. If I open those doors, every army in the world comes down on this mountain."

"Yes."

"And if I say no, they burn you anyway, and seal me in here with a god, and call the whole tragedy holy." Sable laughed again, that same hollow note. "Some choice. Some *hinge*."

"You could run," Wren offered. "You're good at it. You taught me all the passes."

Something flickered across Sable's face—temptation, quick and human and shameful—and then it passed, and what was left underneath was steadier than anything Wren had seen in them since the shrine. Since the static that leapt between their skin and hers, since the voice that wasn't theirs.

"If I run," Sable said slowly, "I take you with me, and we hide, and in ten years you're me—flinching at blessings, burying your own name, teaching some other foundling kid how to disappear. And the god waits. And the church burns somebody else's child next winter. And the winter after that." They looked down at their own hands, turning them over in the lantern light, hands that had called storms and steered contraband and pulled a half-frozen girl out of the snow. "The god said I'm the only one who can answer. It wasn't bragging. It was apologizing."

"Sable—"

"Don't." Softly. "Don't tell me it'll be all right. Don't tell me I'm brave. Just—" they stopped, and Wren saw them wrestle with something, and lose, and let it out anyway. "Just stay close tomorrow. Whatever I decide. Stay where I can see you."

"Okay," Wren whispered.

They doused the lantern and climbed back up through the breathing dark, past the spiral carvings, past galleries where Juniper's lamp glowed faintly as she measured the impossible geometry, up toward the grey daylight at the entrance where Odessa stood her watch with her sword across her knees. And all the while Wren listened to the warmth under her ribs, the banked fire, the patience of nine hundred years distilled into a single held breath.

Because far above them, carried thin and clear down through the stone, came a sound that did not belong to the mountain's silence at all.

Hooves. Many of them. And under the hooves, faint but unmistakable, a hymn—the marching cadence of the Church of the New Dawn, sung by a hundred throats in perfect, joyless unison.

Sable stopped on the temple steps, head lifted, face going pale and then very calm—the calm of someone who has just run out of time to be afraid.

"Well," they said quietly, to Wren, to the god, to the mountains that had asked so patiently for so long. "I suppose they've answered the question of *when*."
