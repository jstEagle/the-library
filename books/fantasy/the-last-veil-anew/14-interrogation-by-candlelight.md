## 14. Interrogation by Candlelight

The cell beneath the Greyhollow garrison had once been a wine cellar. Hale had chosen it deliberately, over the objections of the local garrison commander, who had wanted the prisoner held in the standard cells above—iron-barred, visible, a warning to the town. But Hale did not want Odessa Vane displayed. He wanted her understood.

He brought two candles instead of the single guttering stub the guards would have left her. He set them on the small table between them, adjusted their wicks with a pair of brass snips he carried for exactly this purpose, and waited until the light steadied before he spoke.

"Ser Vane."

"Ex-Inquisitor." She sat across from him, wrists unchained—he had ordered that too, over louder objections—and her posture was the thing that struck him first, as it always struck him when he encountered knights who had fallen out of their stories. She did not slouch. She did not fidget. She sat as though the chair were a saddle and the cellar a field she was surveying, and there was something in her stillness that was not defiance. Defiance he knew how to answer. This was something closer to patience.

"Inquisitor Hale," she said, "you're younger than I expected."

"You were expecting someone."

"I was expecting whoever they sent to break me." Her mouth curved, without much humor in it. "You don't look like a breaker."

"No one does, at first." He opened the folder he'd carried down—the interrogation folio, its pages soft-edged from handling—and laid his pen beside it with care. "I should tell you how I intend to proceed, so you can judge for yourself whether I am what you expect. I will ask you questions. You may refuse to answer any of them. Refusal will be noted, but it will not be punished. There is no rack in this room. There are no irons on your wrists. There is only—" he gestured, slightly, at the candles, the table, the two of them, "—this."

Odessa Vane studied him the way she might study terrain before committing to a crossing.

"That's a new method," she said.

"It's an old one. It has simply fallen out of fashion." He dipped his pen. "Fashion favors speed. Speed favors error. I have found that the truth and the convenient confession arrive dressed alike, and only time tells you which is which. So we will take time. The garrison commander believes I'm being wasteful. Perhaps I am."

"Why am I alive?"

It was the question he had known would come, and he respected her for asking it plainly rather than circling toward it through an hour of maneuvering. He set down his pen.

"Because of your rank," he said. "Because you were captured bearing arms against the Church's riders, which makes your case unusual. Because the commander above us wants you tried publicly, as an example." He paused. "And because I requested it."

"You requested it."

"I request many things. Not all are granted. This one was, because my record buys me a certain latitude, and because no one else wants the trouble of you."

"And what do you want, Inquisitor?"

"The truth," Hale said. "Which is the only commodity I have never been able to buy, bribe, or torture into reliable supply."

* * *

They talked for four hours the first night.

He began where he always began—not with the crime but with the person. Where she was born. What her father did. Whether she had brothers or sisters. She answered these early questions with the flat courtesy of a soldier giving a report, and he noted the answers without pressing, because the early questions were never really about the answers. They were about rhythm. Every liar has a rhythm to their lies, a cadence that smooths itself out, that rehearses. And every honest person has a different rhythm, rougher, less tidy, punctuated by the small hesitations of someone actually retrieving memory rather than constructing it.

By the second hour, Odessa Vane's rhythm told him something he did not expect.

She wasn't performing anything at all.

He had interrogated smugglers who wept magnificently and meant none of it. He had interrogated heretics whose defiance was a costume they wore so long they'd forgotten they could take it off. He had interrogated a woman in Vail's Crossing who confessed to crimes committed by her dead sister out of grief so tangled she herself believed it. In fifteen years, he could count on one hand the subjects who had simply—answered.

"You were dismissed from the Order of the Silver Vigil," he said, turning to the relevant page. "The formal charge was dereliction of duty during the evacuation of Harrow's Mill. The informal charge, according to your commander's letter, was insubordination of a more personal character. Tell me about Harrow's Mill."

For the first time, she looked away from him. Not evasively. It was the look of a person turning toward a wound to check whether it had finally stopped bleeding.

"You've read the official account," she said.

"I've read the account written by men who needed the account to justify themselves. That is why I'm asking you."

Something shifted in her face. He watched it happen and marked it: not the hardening he would have expected from a knight asked to relive disgrace, but a loosening, almost—a weariness so old it had worn smooth, like a stone in a riverbed.

"Harrow's Mill was a tax collection that went wrong," she said. "Eleven families. The collector decided that since the grain levy couldn't be paid in grain, it would be paid in example. My captain agreed. My orders were to hold the mill bridge while the collector's men made the point." Her jaw worked once. "There was a girl on the bridge. Eight years old, maybe. She wouldn't move. She kept saying her mother was still in the granary. And the collector's man raised his whip, and I—" 

She stopped.

"Yes," Hale said quietly.

"I put her behind me and I didn't move either. For three hours. Until my own captain came down and broke my sword over his knee in front of everyone." She looked back up, and her eyes in the candlelight were dry and terribly clear. "That's the whole story. Eleven years ago. I've been hiring out my sword to caravans ever since, because it turns out a knight who won't hold a bridge for a tax collector is exactly as useful as you'd think."

Hale wrote nothing for a long moment. Then he wrote, slowly, carefully, the words that mattered: *account corroborated in spirit by prior testimony of miller's widow, 1147; discrepancies minor.*

Because that was the thing. He had read the widow's deposition years ago, filed and forgotten in a regional archive. A woman who had described, in halting terms, a knight who stood on a bridge all afternoon with a child sheltered behind her, and who had prayed for that knight afterward for longer than the woman could explain.

Two accounts. Eleven years apart. One from a disgraced soldier telling her story to an inquisitor by candlelight, one from a grieving woman telling hers to a parish priest. They matched.

People lied to inquisitors. That was the whole texture of his profession, the weave of it. But the lies people told were always *useful* lies—lies that shortened sentences, deflected blame, elevated the teller. No one constructed a lie that cost them everything and gained them nothing, and then maintained it flawlessly for eleven years across two unrelated testimonies.

"Thank you," he said, and meant it, and watched surprise flicker across her face—genuine surprise, another data point.

* * *

On the third night, he brought the boy into the room without meaning to.

He had not planned to speak of the execution. It sat in him like a splinter driven too deep to work loose with tools, and his professional discipline—the great, load-bearing discipline of his life—was to set such things aside until they could be examined under proper conditions. But somewhere past the second hour, after questions about routes and riders and the temple entrance in the mountains, Odessa Vane looked at him across the candles and said:

"You were there. At the burning in Greyhollow. I heard about it even down in the cells."

"You heard correctly."

"They say the boy forgave you."

"They say a great many things."

"I'm not asking what they say." Her voice didn't rise. That was the unnerving quality of the woman; she wielded quiet like other men used force. "I'm asking what happened. You sit here night after night asking me for truth like it's coin you're owed. Here's a chance to spend some of your own."

Hale set down his pen.

He was aware, distantly, that this was a boundary. Interrogators did not trade. Interrogators did not open themselves to the subject, because the subject would use whatever was given, and rightly so—it was the one weapon available to the powerless. Fifteen years of practice said: deflect. Return to the questions. Note the attempt at reversal in the folio.

But the candle flames stood very still between them, and the boy's voice was in the room already, had been in the room for nine days, older than the church, older than the doctrine, patient as weather, saying *it's alright, I know you're afraid, I would be afraid too.*

"He was fourteen," Hale said.

He hadn't intended to speak. The words arrived as if they had been waiting behind his teeth for over a week, pressed there, swelling.

"His name was Tam. Tam Aldous. His mother sold eels at the river market. When they bound him to the stake, he was shaking—I remember noting it, professionally, that he was shaking, and thinking it proved the fear of the flame, which is what the doctrine says fear proves." Hale's hands were quite still on the table. He had trained them to be still. "And then he looked at me. Not at the crowd. At me. Because I was the one holding the writ, and I suppose a fourteen-year-old understands that the man holding the paper is the one the paper happens through."

"What did he say?"

"He said—" Hale found, to his considerable alarm, that he needed a moment. He took it. The candles hissed softly. "He said, 'It's alright. I know you're afraid. I would be afraid too.' And then he said, 'You're trying to keep the dark out. I understand. But the dark isn't what you think it is.'"

The silence afterward had weight, like snow on a roof.

"I gave the signal," Hale said. "I have given it eleven times in my career. Each one was justified by evidence I verified, confessions I confirmed, threats I assessed. This one was justified by a report from a frightened village and a child who survived a night in the high passes that killed two grown men, which the doctrine says is proof of possession." He looked up. "He was not possessed, Ser Vane. I have heard possessed voices. I have cast the rite of discernment myself, forty times, and I know its signs. What came out of that boy on the platform was not a demon wearing a child. It was—" and here he reached, for the first time in his career, for a word outside his training, "—it was something *old*, and it was *kind*, and I burned it anyway."

Odessa Vane did not offer comfort. He noted that too, and was grateful. Comfort would have been a lie between them; what passed instead was something truer—she simply nodded, once, the way soldiers acknowledge a casualty report.

"You believe the doctrine protects children," she said. "That's why you do the work. I've met enough of your kind to see it. You're not a cruel man doing cruelty. You're a careful man doing what you believe is mercy."

"Yes."

"And now a burned boy has told you the dark isn't what you think it is, and you're sitting in a wine cellar every night asking a disgraced knight questions you already know the answers to, and I'd wager—" she leaned forward slightly, into the light, "—I'd wager you're not building a case against me at all. Are you, Inquisitor? You're building a case against the writ."

Hale did not answer. Which was, in the grammar of that room, an answer.

* * *

Afterward, alone in his quarters in the garrison attic, he did what he had never done in fifteen years of service.

He opened the folios.

Not the interrogation folio—his private copies. Reports. Depositions. Weather records from the Thornfell passes going back eight years, which he had requested on the fourth day and received, after delays, with a note from the provincial archive suggesting that such requests were irregular. Irregular. Yes. He had begun to collect irregularities the way other men collected sins.

The doctrine of the New Dawn held that the godseed was corruption: fragments of the defeated old gods lodged in mortal blood, growing, metastasizing, destined to burst forth as calamity unless excised in childhood while the seed was small and the host weak. Every text agreed. Every homily repeated it. He had preached it himself, twice, in smaller postings, and had been good at it.

But doctrine was a map, and Hale had spent his life learning the difference between maps and ground.

He laid the ground out on his desk by candlelight and walked it.

Item: the seed-bearers documented in church records over sixty years—two hundred and forty-one executions, each meticulously recorded, each justified in the margin by the same phrase, *imminent calamity averted.* He pulled each margin note and set them side by side. In two hundred and forty-one cases, not one recorded calamity had followed. Not a flood. Not a blight. Not a madness in the fields. Two hundred and forty-one imminent catastrophes, averted by flame, and the world had gone on exactly as though nothing had been averted at all.

A physician who lanced two hundred boils and cured no disease would be called a fraud. Why had no one asked the question?

Item: the weather reports. Unseasonal storms in the Thornfells, clustering over eight years, increasing in frequency and precision over the last six months. Storms that struck ridgelines with the accuracy of artillery. Storms that spared specific travelers. He had assumed sorcery, and sorcery it might be—but sorcery aimed at what end? To protect a fugitive child through a killing frost? The old gods of doctrine were engines of ruin. Nothing in the texts accounted for a power that spent itself keeping a little girl warm.

Item: the directive. The sealed order Odessa Vane had been commanded to burn—the extermination order, the word *godseed* appearing in an official document for the first time in the history of the Church, not as metaphor but as category. He had read it seven times now. And each time, the same detail snagged him like a nail in cloth: the directive did not instruct the faithful to *test* the children first. Everywhere else in canon law, everywhere, discernment preceded judgment—the rite, the verification, the weighing. This order skipped the rite entirely. As if the authors already knew what the tests would find. As if the finding had been made long ago, and quietly, and the conclusion drawn not from scripture but from something else.

Fear, perhaps. Or guilt.

Or knowledge.

Hale sat back. The candle had burned low. Outside the narrow window, Greyhollow lay under a sky the color of slate, and far to the north, invisible beyond the ridge, the mountains stood wrapped in cloud that had not moved in three days—cloud that gathered without wind and held without breaking, like a breath taken and kept.

He thought: *a storm-caller.* The reports spoke of one, exiled, unnamed. The order of storm-callers had refused to cooperate with the inquiry, citing ancient privileges, and the provincial archdeacon had let the refusal stand, which was itself strange—orders did not refuse the New Dawn and survive the refusing.

He thought: *what if the doctrine is the heresy?*

The thought arrived whole, blasphemous, enormous, and he did not flinch from it, because flinching was not in him. He wrote it down. That was his discipline, his one true gift: he wrote down even the thoughts that damned him, because a truth unrecorded was a truth that could be killed, and he had burned enough true things for one life.

At the bottom of the page, beneath the question, he added a second line, smaller:

*If the seeds are souls and not sicknesses, then I am the greatest destroyer this faith has produced, and I must know before I act again.*

Then he blotted the ink, closed the folios, and pinched out the candles—one, then the other—sitting a moment in the dark with the boy's voice, the bridge, the widow's prayer, the unmoving northern cloud.

In the morning, he would request travel papers. Officially, to inspect the mountain shrines for contamination. Unofficially—

Unofficially, he was going to find the storm-caller. And he was going to bring the writ with him, and he was going to hold it up against the ground and see, at last, which of them was lying: the map, or the world.
