## 17. What Wren Decided

Wren had learned to count her heartbeats the way other children counted sheep.

It was something she'd started in the church nursery at Vael's Crossing, back when she was too small to know why the sisters watched her with that particular softness, that pity dressed up as tenderness. She'd lie on her cot in the dark and count—one, two, three—listening for the other sound underneath. The hum. The lullaby. The voice that lived below her ribs like a second heart made of thunder.

One hundred beats. That was how long it took before the humming started, most nights. Lately it was faster. Lately it was thirty. Sometimes twenty.

Tonight, crouched in the cold dark of the waystation's root cellar, listening through the floorboards to voices above, she didn't need to count at all. The god was already awake. It had been awake since Sable came down the stairs an hour ago, snow in their hair, their face gray as old ash, and said words Wren wasn't supposed to hear.

*They've offered a pardon. For me. All I have to do is give them you.*

Wren pressed her palms flat against the earthen wall and breathed slowly. Above her, the murmur of argument continued—Sable's low voice, Odessa's clipped one, Juniper coughing between sentences. The sickness had gotten into Juniper's chest like water into a boat, and every day the coughing went a little deeper, and every day Sable's face got a little grayer, and every day the storm outside screamed a little louder against the shutters like something enormous pacing just beyond the walls.

She had heard everything now. She hadn't meant to. But cellars carry sound the way rivers carry leaves, and over the past weeks she'd learned the architecture of these people's secrets: Odessa's nightmares, named and numbered; Juniper's fever-talk about roads that fold; and Sable—Sable, who talked to the mountain when they thought no one was listening, who apologized to storms, who touched the doorframe of the shrine where they'd found her every morning like they were checking whether it still forgave them.

And tonight: *a pardon. In exchange for the girl.*

She should have been afraid. Instead what she felt was terribly, achingly clear—the same clarity she'd felt at seven years old, when Sister Maribel had held her hands across the table and said, *child, you are a vessel of holy purpose,* and Wren had understood, even then, that *purpose* was a word grown-ups used when they wanted to take something from you and call it a gift.

The god stirred beneath her sternum. Not words. Not yet. Just warmth, like a hand laid flat against the inside of her skin.

*I know,* she told it silently. *I'm thinking.*

Because here was the arithmetic no one upstairs would say out loud, though all of them carried it behind their eyes like a debt: four people, starving, in a waystation the church would find eventually because the passes were sealed but not forever, and one of those people was sick enough to die without medicine, and one of them—Sable—was being asked to trade away their soul to save the rest. And all of it, every ounce of the suffering, every hour of the hiding, every flinch at hoofbeats on the ridge—it all traced back to her. To what slept in her blood.

Juniper had called her a seed once, gently, drawing lines on a map. *You grow where they were buried.* But Wren had done her own counting since then, in the dark of the root cellar, and she knew what seeds were for. Seeds were for feeding things. Seeds got planted or eaten, and either way the seed itself did not get a vote.

Well.

She was done being a thing that happened to other people.

* * *

She waited until the house went quiet. Until Odessa's boots stopped crossing overhead, until even the wind seemed to pause for breath between gusts. Then she climbed the ladder slow, each rung silent under her careful feet—she'd practiced this, she realized, she'd been practicing leaving for days without knowing it—and slipped into the main room.

The fire was banked to embers. Sable lay on the pallet nearest the door, not sleeping—she could tell by the quality of their stillness, the way sleepers sagged and watchers held. Wren stood over them in the dark and looked at their face for a long time. The scar through the eyebrow. The lines around the mouth that hadn't been there eight years ago, according to Odessa, who told stories like she was paying off a debt.

Sable had found her half-frozen in a ruined shrine and hadn't asked what she was carrying. Had fed her first and questioned never. Had heard a god speak out of their own mouth during a storm and hadn't run, hadn't sold her, hadn't even flinched away from her afterward—just sat down heavily and said, *well, that's going to take some getting used to.*

People said the world was cruel. Wren had mostly found it indifferent. But Sable Fairweather was neither. Sable was kind in a place that punished kindness, and that was the rarest weather there was.

*I'm sorry,* she thought at them, fierce and quiet. *I'm sorry you'll be angry. Be angry alive. That's all I want. Everyone gets to be alive and angry, and I go, and it stops.*

She took the bread she'd saved from supper—half a loaf, hidden in her coat for three days, because she'd planned this even while hating herself for planning it—and she let herself out the door into the screaming white.

* * *

The blizzard took her name the moment she stepped into it.

That was the first thing. The wind peeled it away—*Wren*—and left her just a body moving through blindness, snow hitting her face like thrown gravel. The second thing was the cold, which was not like cold in stories. It did not bite. It *considered* her, patient and vast, and began unhurriedly taking her apart: fingers first, then ears, then the breath that turned to knives in her throat.

She had counted on the treeline being close. Half a mile, maybe less, the dark rank of pines where the mountain dropped toward the valley road. She'd seen it from the window a hundred times. But the storm erased distance entirely; the world shrank to a circle three feet wide, lit by nothing, filled with sideways snow, and the treeline might have been ten steps away or ten miles.

Doesn't matter, she told herself. Walk anyway. The valley road led down. Down led to Greyhollow. Greyhollow led to the church, and the church led to an end of it—an end to Juniper's rattling cough, an end to Sable's gray face, an end to Odessa standing watch all night with her sword across her knees like a woman guarding a grave.

The god was singing.

It had begun somewhere in the first hundred steps—a low tone beneath her heartbeat, rising through her chest into her throat, and she realized with distant surprise that her lips were moving, that she was humming along, that the sound was keeping her warm the way nothing else could. The lullaby. The one she'd heard since before she could remember. Except now it had changed key. Now it sounded less like a cradle song and more like—

Like pleading.

*Don't,* she whispered into the wind, and didn't know if she meant the god or the storm or herself. *This is the good version. This is the one where nobody else bleeds.*

Her foot caught a buried stone and she went down hard, knee-first, and the snow received her almost tenderly, almost like an invitation. For a moment she stayed there, cheek against the drift, and the exhaustion rose up in her like floodwater. So tired. Eight years old in the nursery. Nine years old running. Ten years old learning which shrines had loose floors and which priests had heavy hands. Eleven, twelve, thirteen—every year a door closing behind her, every kindness rationed, every night the lullaby and the counting and the watching for riders.

Maybe she could just rest here. Five minutes. The snow was soft. The god would keep her warm. The god had always kept her warm, that was the horrible secret of it—that the thing inside her loved her more purely than anything in the world ever had, and its love was the exact shape of her doom.

*Get up,* she told herself.

She didn't.

*Wren.*

Not her own voice. Not the god's either—or both, tangled together, coming from everywhere, coming from the storm itself. Her head snapped up, snow crusting her lashes, and through the white curtain she saw a shape. A tall shape, bent against the wind, arms wide, and around it the blizzard *bent*, the way iron filings bend toward a lodestone, the flakes curving into a spiral that left a pocket of terrible, impossible calm.

Sable.

They stood in the eye of their own making, and their eyes were blazing—not with anger, exactly. With something worse. With the look of someone who has just understood exactly how much they stand to lose.

"You—" Wren's voice cracked. "How did you—"

"The mountain told me." Sable's voice carried strangely, doubled somehow, as if two throats spoke in unison. "Or I told it. I don't—I can't tell anymore, and it doesn't matter." They came closer, step by deliberate step, and the calm followed them. "You heard. You heard everything."

Wren's chin came up. Even freezing, even kneeling, she made herself meet those eyes. "Then you know I'm right."

"I know you're thirteen years old and you decided your life was worth less than ours." Sable crouched, bringing their face level with hers, and this close she could see the frost forming on their lashes, could see that they were shaking—but not from cold. "Do you know what Odessa said when I woke her? She didn't ask where you went. She said, *the treeline.* Like she knew. Like we all knew you'd been teaching yourself to leave."

"Someone has to," Wren said, and the words tore coming out. "Nobody else dies for me! Don't you understand? Everything bad that happens near me happens *because of me*. The riders, the boy in Greyhollow, Juniper getting sick, you having to choose—" Her voice broke apart entirely. "I'm not worth it. I'm a seed. Seeds feed things."

For a long moment Sable said nothing. The storm howled around the bubble of calm, furious, excluded. And then Sable reached out—slowly, giving her time to pull away—and took her frozen hands in theirs.

Their skin was warm. Impossible, furnace-warm, crackling faintly at the edges with light like summer lightning trapped under the surface.

"When I was young," Sable said, "my order trained us to believe we were weapons. Tools. Things that happened to villages instead of people. And the day I refused, do you know what my mentor told me?" Their grip tightened. "He said, *you are nothing but what we made you.* And he was wrong, Wren. He was wrong, and it took me eight years and a frozen girl in a broken shrine to understand how wrong. You are not what they planted in you. You are not what anyone calls you. You are the person who shared her bread with a stranger who was pretending not to be hungry. You are the one who laughs at Juniper's terrible jokes. You are—" their voice caught, actually caught— "you are the reason I remembered I used to be somebody."

"The pardon—"

"Is worthless," Sable said flatly. "I'd be trading the only family I have for a roof and a leash. There is no version of me that gives you to them. There is no version of me that survives it." They pulled her up, and she came, legs numb, into the circle of their warmth. "So here's what happens instead. We stop hiding. All the way. No more cellars, no more silence, no more deciding alone in the dark whose life weighs more. If they want you, they come through the storm. Through *me*."

"But the binding, the temple, the order—they'll—"

"Let them come," Sable said, and the words rolled out of them like thunder rolling off a peak, and Wren felt the god inside her surge toward that voice like a tide answering the moon. "Let them all come."

The storm broke open.

Not ended—*opened*, the clouds parting above them like a curtain drawn by a giant hand, and moonlight poured down into the whirl of snow, and every flake within a hundred yards hung suspended, glittering, motionless, a whole sky holding its breath. In the silence Wren heard it clearly for the first time: not one voice in the storm but hundreds, thousands, layered like choir upon choir, murmuring at the edge of hearing, and every single one of them was saying the same word.

*Sable. Sable. Sable.*

"Oh," Wren breathed. "They're not using you. They never were. They've been *waiting* for you."

Sable stood in the falling-still snow with their face turned up to the parted sky, and tears froze on their cheeks, and when they spoke their voice was very quiet and very steady. "Eight years I hid. Eight years I made myself small so no one would see what I am." They looked down at her, and smiled, and it was the saddest and most joyful thing Wren had ever seen. "You walked into a blizzard to save us. The least I can do is stop being small."

The snow resumed, gentle now, drifting straight down like blessing. Sable shrugged off their own coat and wrapped it around her, and lifted her—they were stronger than they looked, or the storm helped, or both—and turned back toward the waystation's faint ember-glow.

"We go home," Sable said. "We wake the others. And tomorrow, we make for the temple—because if the order is coming to seal it, and the church is coming to burn it, then someone had better be there to open it first."

Wren, half-asleep against their shoulder, felt the god settle inside her like a child finally comforted. And beneath the lullaby, clear as a bell in the new-fallen quiet, she heard it say its first true word aloud, in a voice like deep places and old rain:

*At last.*

Behind them, far down the valley, torchlight flickered along the road—many torches, moving in formation, climbing toward the pass.

The order had come home early.
