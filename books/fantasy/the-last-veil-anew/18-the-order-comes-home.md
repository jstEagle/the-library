## 18. The Order Comes Home

They came at dawn, the way the order always came—as if arriving were a kind of judgment.

Sable felt them before they saw them. The air over the eastern ridge went taut and clean, scrubbed of weather, every gust smoothed into obedience, and that wrongness woke Sable out of a shallow sleep in the temple antechamber with their heart already pounding. Eight years since they had felt another storm-caller work, and still their body knew it instantly, the way a scarred hand knows cold iron. Someone was holding back the wind.

"Wren." They crossed the cold stone to where the girl slept curled against Odessa's bedroll, her breath fogging in the blue dark. "Wren, wake up. They're here."

She surfaced slowly, blinking, hair a wild halo. "Who's here?"

"The people who taught me everything I know about hiding." Sable was already moving, pulling on boots, gathering the maps Juniper had spread across the floor the night before—the burial lines, the temple's drowned halls, all of it inked in her careful, heretical hand. "And everything I know about being found."

Juniper was awake too, of course; she seemed to sleep like a cat, one ear always open. She stood at the broken doorway with a spyglass pressed to her eye, her breath steaming around it. When Sable reached her side, she handed the glass over without a word.

Below the temple's ruined stair, down where the mountain shoulder dropped into the white valley, a line of figures climbed through the snowfield. Twelve of them. No—fourteen. They moved in the order's old wedge formation, the one used for entering contested ground, and even at this distance Sable could see the frost-light of their working: a bubble of held calm traveling with them, snow falling straight as plumb lines inside it. Their grey-and-silver cloaks were the color of the storm itself, and at the wedge's point walked a figure Sable would have known anywhere, dead or alive, in this world or any other.

Master Ilenne Corvane. First Voice of the Storm-Sworn. The woman who had once lifted seven-year-old Sable onto a granite outcrop in the teeth of a gale and said, *listen, child—the thunder is only the sky learning your name.*

"She brought a full circle," Juniper murmured. "Twelve callers plus herself. That's not an expedition. That's a rite."

"What kind of rite?"

Juniper lowered the spyglass and looked at Sable, and there was something almost gentle in her face, which frightened Sable more than the riders below. "The kind that needs a circle."

* * *

The others gathered in the antechamber while Sable watched the column climb. Odessa stood by the inner door with her sword drawn—not pointed at anyone, just held, the way a man holds a rope in high wind—and Wren sat on the altar step with her knees pulled up, very small, very still.

"They'll want the temple," Wren said. It wasn't a question. She'd been hearing things for days now, as the binding beneath them thinned; sometimes she spoke in a voice that wasn't quite hers, patient and vast, and none of them remarked on it anymore because remarking didn't help. "They've always wanted it sealed. That's what the order is *for*, isn't it? You told me. Storm-callers are locks."

"I told you what I believed when I was young and stupid," Sable said.

"You told me what you learned when you were old enough to read your order's founding texts," Juniper corrected mildly, rolling her maps. "Which makes it true, however stupid."

Odessa shifted. "How long do we have?"

Sable did the arithmetic without wanting to. A full circle climbing from the valley floor, holding a calm-bubble the whole way—that was costly work, especially at altitude in deep winter. An hour, perhaps less. "Enough time to run, if we take the lower galleries and come out past the scree field. Not enough to outrun them in open country. They can walk on the wind, Odessa. We can't."

"Then we don't run." Odessa hefted her sword. "I've held worse doors than this one."

"With twelve storm-callers above you?" Sable heard their own voice crack with something between fury and fear. "You'd last four minutes, and then they'd be down here anyway, and you'd have died for nothing."

"For something," Odessa said quietly. "For time. There's a difference, and you know it, because you're the one who taught me the difference."

That stopped Sable's mouth. It was true—they had said it, months ago, on the road out of Greyhollow, when Odessa had asked why she should bother fighting for people who'd stripped her name. *Everything worth holding is held briefly,* Sable had said. *You fight for the time it buys.* They hadn't known then how soon they'd be fed their own words.

Wren stood up. She was wearing the coat Sable had cut down for her, sleeves rolled twice, and she looked, in the grey light leaking through the shattered roof, suddenly much older than her nine years. "I could go out to them," she said. "If I give myself up, maybe they won't seal it. Maybe they only need me gone."

"No," three voices said at once, and the unison of it startled a ghost of a laugh out of her, there and gone.

"They will seal it," Juniper said, gently but without mercy, because Juniper never lied to children or anyone else. "Whatever else they intend, the sealing comes first. That's the doctrine. The lock matters more than anything walking above it—including the locksmiths themselves. Your order wrote that into its bones, Sable. Am I wrong?"

Sable looked at the floor of the antechamber, at the faint spiral carved there ten centuries ago, worn nearly smooth by pilgrims' feet before the war and then abandoned. Below them, somewhere in the drowned dark, lay the god's sleeping hall, and Wren's god with it, asking politely, patiently, for the one thing only Sable could give.

"No," Sable said. "You're not wrong."

* * *

They came up the stair as the sun cleared the ridge, fourteen figures out of legend, and the snow around their path fell in perfect silence, not a flake drifting, everything ordered. Sable stood alone at the top of the temple steps with their hands empty and visible, the way you greet someone you expect to kill you, and waited.

Ilenne Corvane had not aged the way other people aged. Her hair had gone fully silver in the eight years since Sable's exile, but her back was straight as a lightning-struck pine and her eyes were the same pale grey as the horizon before a squall. She stopped six paces away, exactly the distance courtesy required, exactly the distance a caller needed for nothing at all, and studied Sable the way she had once studied failed students: thoroughly, sadly, without hatred.

"Sable Fairweather."

"Master Corvane."

"That name again." Something flickered across her mouth—not quite a smile. "I gave you a new one, the day I signed your exile. Did you ever use it?"

"Never."

"Mm." She turned her face toward the ruined temple behind Sable, taking in the collapsed roof, the frost-rimed pillars, the spiral carved in the threshold. "Eight years, and this is where you washed up. Do you know what I felt when the reports began? Answered prayers in the Thornfells. Weather with intentions. I told myself: some poor hedge-witch playing at divinity. And then I told myself: or a seed-bearer. And then, on the third night, I woke at the hour I used to wake to check your dormitory, and I knew. Because only one person I have ever taught leaves weather that looks like *feeling*."

"You taught me to hide it."

"I taught you to hide it so well that no one would ever find you." Her voice stayed level, but Sable heard the fracture under it, fine as a hairline crack in ice. "And instead you've spent the winter broadcasting to half the continent. Prayers answered in the passes. Voices in storms. Do you have any idea what you've done?"

"Yes," Sable said. "I'm starting to."

Behind Ilenne, the circle had spread into its working formation, twelve callers fanning along the terrace with their hands loose at their sides, and Sable felt the pressure of them the way you feel a coming change in pressure before a storm—ears popping, old scars aching. Odessa emerged into the doorway at Sable's back, sword bare, and one of the callers—a boy Sable vaguely remembered, Tamasin or Tammas, who'd wept at his first calling—flinched at the sight of steel.

"Stand down, Knight Vane," Ilenne said without turning. "Yes, I know you. Your order's shame travels faster than you do. You will not die today if you choose not to. That offer has an expiry."

"Everything does," Odessa said, and did not move.

Ilenne finally looked back at Sable, and now she let the mask slip a little, and what was underneath was worse than the mask: grief. Genuine, exhausted, parental grief. "Come down off this mountain with me. Bring the girl. Leave the rest—leave the maps, leave the knight, leave whatever you think is sleeping under these stones. Come home. The tribunal will convene. I will speak for you. Eight years of exile, Sable—eight years of penance already served. You refused them once, and look what your refusal bought: a smuggler's grave waiting for you in a snowdrift. Say the word now, kneel tonight, swear the oaths with a whole heart, and by spring you will stand in the Hall of Winds again with your name restored and your hands clean. Redemption, Sable. It is not too late. It is never too late. That is the entire point of us."

The words landed like warm rain on frozen ground, and Sable hated how much of them soaked in. *Home.* The Hall of Winds with its singing rafters. Bread at the long tables. Belonging, offered freely, after eight years of buying shelter night by night with secrets.

"You want the temple sealed," Sable said.

"The temple will be sealed regardless. That is not negotiable, and you know why—it is the oldest law we have, older than my office, older than the church you're so busy despising. The gods were bound because they burned the world, Sable. Our founders watched the sky catch fire. We are the jailers, and jailers do not negotiate with prisoners, no matter how politely the prisoners ask."

"And the children?" Sable stepped aside, deliberately, letting Ilenne see Wren standing small and pale in the doorway. "The jailers' oldest law—does it say what happens to the children who carry the seeds? Because the church has a law too, Master. I've seen the directive. Burn every seed-bearer. And I notice your circle climbed this mountain with the church's riders camped two valleys east, and nobody seems troubled by the company."

Something moved behind Ilenne's eyes—fast, suppressed, like a fish under ice. "The church's excesses are the church's sin."

"Their excess." Sable laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked with static, and every member of the circle heard it and stiffened, because that sound did not belong in human throats. "You'll seal the temple with everyone inside it. Say it plainly. I want to hear you say it."

Ilenne was silent for a long moment. Snow fell straight as nails around them both.

"The sealing cannot distinguish," she said at last. "It never could. That is its mercy and its horror, and our founders accepted both, because the alternative was the end of the world. If the girl is inside when the lock turns—" Her jaw tightened. "Then the girl is inside when the lock turns. I am sorry. I have carried harder sorrows than this one, and so will you."

"There it is," Sable said softly. "Redemption through obedience. That's the offer. Kneel, swear, and help you bury a living child under a mountain—and call it sorrow, call it mercy, call it the oldest law. Eight years ago I stood in front of the tribunal and they asked me to drown a village for the war effort, and I said the storm doesn't ask the water whom to love. Do you remember what you told me then?"

"I remember everything I said to you."

"You said: *a caller who refuses to be a weapon is a wall, and walls get torn down.*" Sable's voice shook now, and they let it. "You were right about one thing, Master. I am a wall. I've been a wall for eight years—for Wren, for Odessa, for every discarded thing that made it behind me. And I'm telling you what I told them: this wall does not move."

Ilenne closed her eyes. When she opened them, the grief had been packed away somewhere deep, and what remained was the First Voice of the Storm-Sworn, and she raised one hand, and the held calm over the mountain tightened like a fist.

"Then I mourn you twice," she said. "Circle—to positions. Seal at moonrise."

* * *

They retreated into the temple's throat, and the great doors—bronze and black oak, older than any kingdom—groaned shut behind them on hinges that had not moved in centuries, and Sable leaned against them breathing hard while dust sifted down through the torchlight.

"Well," Juniper said, from the darkness, where she was already unrolling maps by candle stub. "That went about as well as expected."

"How can you joke?"

"Because the alternative is screaming, and I did my screaming in Greyhollow." Her quill scratched. "Moonrise gives us five hours. The sealing needs the full circle in position around the crown of the temple—they'll build the lock from above, which means the deepest galleries stay open longest. If there's anything down there worth finding, we find it before moonrise."

"Or," Odessa said, "we go up and meet them. Fourteen against four, but they'll hesitate to kill their own."

"They won't hesitate," Sable said. "That's the thing about redemption through obedience, Odessa. It means the obedient have already decided we're the price." They pushed off the doors and looked at Wren, sitting on the altar step again, hugging her knees, watching Sable with those too-old eyes. Inside her, Sable could feel it now whenever they got close—the god's attention, patient as bedrock, listening.

"It's asking again," Wren said. "Isn't it. I can feel it pressing on the back of my teeth. It wants to talk to you."

"Let it wait."

"It says there isn't time to let it wait." Wren unfolded, stood. In the candlelight her shadow on the wall was enormous, winged somehow, though she was only a small girl in a borrowed coat. "It says the lock they're building isn't like the old one. The old one left room for asking. This one won't ask anything, ever again. It says if they finish it, everything under this mountain goes quiet forever—and so does everything the seeds are."

The words settled over the chamber like snowfall.

"So," Juniper said into the silence, capping her ink with a click that sounded terribly loud. "Five hours until moonrise. A god that needs a storm-caller's yes. And a woman upstairs who taught you everything you know about saying no." She looked up at Sable, and her eyes were bright and terrible and kind. "What's it going to be, Fairweather?"

Far above, muffled by bronze and stone, the circle began to sing—and the mountain, deep in its drowned halls, held its breath.
