## 21. After the Thunder

Wren counted the days by the sound of Sable's breathing.

It had become a kind of weather itself, that breathing—slow thunder when the fever climbed, a fine drizzle when it eased, and once, in the worst hour of the third night, a silence so complete that Wren had pressed her ear against Sable's ribs and listened for the storm inside them, willing it to keep its rhythm. *Keep going,* she'd thought, not knowing who she was asking. Sable. The god. The mountain. All of them. *Keep going.*

The waystation smelled of pine resin and melted snow and the sweetish rot of Juniper's fever-sweats, which had finally broken two days after the lightning came down off the ridge and did whatever it had done to the world. Odessa kept the fire fed like a woman standing a watch she refused to name. Nobody spoke about what they had seen. Not because it was forbidden, but because there were no words yet shaped to hold it.

Sable lay in the narrow bed beneath the window, and Wren watched them, because watching was all she could do, and because she could not stop.

They had changed. Anyone could see it if they knew how to look. Sable's hair, black as wet slate before, now carried streaks of white that moved—not grey, not age-white, but *moving*, faint as heat-shimmer, so that when the light caught it right, their head seemed wrapped in slow smoke. Their skin had gone cool and dry as air before rain. And sometimes, when Wren took their hand to change the poultice on the burn-scars that laddered their palms, Sable's fingers would twitch—once, twice—in time with something far away.

"They're listening," Juniper said from her corner pallet, voice still rough as gravel. She had her charts spread over her knees even now, too weak to rise but never too weak to work, her ink-stained fingers pinning down a map of the Thornfells that no longer matched the world. "The weather. I think they're listening to it."

"Can you listen to weather?"

"I can't hear a god forgive me either, child, and yet here we all are." Juniper tapped the map with one cracked fingernail. "Look. Every pass I ever charted has shifted. Not on paper—*underneath*. Like the mountains rearranged their bones while we weren't looking." Her eyes went soft and strange. "The old roads are waking up. Roads I drew as guesses. Roads my grandmother drew as guesses. They're becoming real."

Wren didn't understand maps, but she understood being changed. She had carried a voice inside her since before she could remember; she knew what it was to have your body be a house with someone else living in it. Now Sable's body was a house with the whole sky living in it.

On the fifth day, Sable woke.

Not gently. They came up out of sleep like a diver breaking surface, gasping, hands clawing at the blanket, eyes wild and unfocused—and then they stopped, mid-breath, and went utterly still, and Wren saw them *listen*. Their whole face tilted, the way hers did when the lullaby came.

"Sable?" she whispered.

"The wind's turning," Sable said hoarsely. "Northeast. There's snow on it. Light. It'll reach us by—" they frowned, tasting the words, tasting the *wind*—"by dusk. An inch, maybe two. It won't stay."

Odessa dropped the log she was carrying.

"You're reading it," Juniper breathed. She tried to sit up and failed, laughed at herself, gave up with the grace of a woman who had made peace with her own limits years ago. "You're not guessing anymore. You're *reading*."

"It's very loud," Sable said. There was wonder in it, and exhaustion, and underneath both something Wren recognized as fear wearing its bravest coat. "Everything is loud. The pressure over the western ridge. The river under the ice, talking to itself. It's like—" They searched for it. "Like walking into a room where every instrument is playing at once, and you're expected to know which song is yours."

Wren crossed the room and sat on the edge of the bed, careful of the bruises, careful of everything. Up close she could see the white moving through Sable's hair, could smell the ozone that clung to them now the way woodsmoke had once.

"Does it hurt?" she asked.

"No," Sable said, and then, honestly: "I don't know yet. Ask me in a year." Their hand found hers—the scarred palm against her small fingers, the static long gone quiet between them, or maybe just gone home. "How long?"

"Five days. You almost—" Wren couldn't say it. Her throat closed around it like a fist.

"She counted your breaths," Odessa said from the hearth, gruff, not looking at any of them. "Every hour. Out loud, some nights. Drove me mad."

"Someone had to keep time," Wren said, chin up, and Sable smiled—a real smile, cracked and tired—and squeezed her hand, and outside, right on schedule, the first flakes of dusk-snow began to fall, soft and unhurried, exactly as promised.

* * *

The news came slowly, the way news comes to mountains: carried by travelers, by rumor, by the rare brave soul willing to cross a pass in early winter for the price of bread and gossip.

The Church of the New Dawn was tearing itself apart.

The first traveler was a wool merchant with frostbitten ears who talked mostly to hear himself speak. He said the Cathedral in Greyhollow had emptied overnight—that half its congregation had walked out during the morning liturgy when a young deacon stood up during the homily and shouted that he had *heard it*, he had heard the voices in the thunder, and he would not kneel to men who burned children. He said the deacon had been arrested, and then the guards sent to arrest him had knelt instead, and then it had gotten complicated, and the merchant used the word *complicated* the way other men used *bloodbath*.

The second traveler was quieter. A woman with a soldier's bearing and no soldier's gear, traveling alone, who ate her stew in silence until Wren, unable to help herself, asked if she'd come through Greyhollow.

"I came through the square," the woman said. "In the autumn they burned a boy there. Everyone remembers. What they don't remember—" her jaw worked—"is that some of us remember wrong. Or we remembered right, and lied to ourselves about it. Now there's a storm in every dream, and in the dreams the boy is speaking, and he isn't angry. That's the worst part. He forgives us, and we can't bear it." She looked at Wren then, really looked, and something in her face crumpled and reassembled into resolve. "You're one of them. A seed-bearer."

Wren's whole body went cold. Beside her, Odessa's chair creaked.

But the woman only bowed her head. "I wanted to see one. Before—" She stopped. Started again. "There are others coming. Not soldiers. Mothers. Farmers. People whose children hum songs nobody taught them. We're going to need to know how to do this. How to let you be *people*." She stood, left coin on the table though Odessa waved it off, and paused at the door. "The church says the gods are demons wearing dead names. But I heard a demon forgive an inquisitor once, in a burning square, and demons don't do that."

She left. The snow had stopped falling an hour before she arrived, and started again the moment she walked out—as if the sky were keeping appointments now, keeping *courtesy*, and Wren hugged that thought to herself in the dark like a coal.

* * *

Sable got better the way rivers thaw: invisibly, and then all at once.

By the second week they could stand at the window for an hour at a time, one hand flat against the glass, murmuring. Wren learned to recognize the conversations. High thin clouds meant gossip. Fog meant Sable was arguing with the valley about something. Once, when a hawk rode a thermal past the window, Sable laughed out loud—startled, delighted—and wouldn't explain why except to say, "The updrafts are *gossips*."

The god in Wren had gone quiet since the temple. Not gone—she could feel it the way you feel a sleeping sibling beside you, warm and present—but quiet, resting, its long imprisonment ended, its voice given back to the thunder where it belonged. Some nights she missed it. That felt like a betrayal, missing it, but Juniper said missing it was proof it had been family and not a parasite, and Wren decided to believe her.

One evening Sable sat across from her at the table and said, "Show me the lullaby."

Wren froze. "Why?"

"Because it was never just yours. It was theirs, sung through you. And I heard pieces of it in the storm, but I want to hear it whole. From you. If you're willing." Sable's voice was gentle, careful, the way they handled anything that had been dropped before. "No one will ever make you sing it again if you don't want to."

So Wren sang it. Small and wavering at first, then stronger—the melody older than any language, the one she'd known since before birth, the lullaby that had been a god's whole heart folded small enough to hide in a child's blood. And as she sang, the fire settled. The wind outside gentled. Snow stopped mid-fall beyond the window and hung there, glittering, patient, an entire sky holding its breath to listen.

When she finished, the snow resumed, and Sable had tears on their face, and neither of them mentioned it.

"That's yours now too," Wren said, surprising herself. "A piece of it. You earned it."

"Wren Fairweather—" Sable began, and stopped, because she hadn't taken their name, not formally, nothing written or witnessed, and yet somehow the surname had arrived one day and stayed, unremarked, the way true things arrive.

"What?" she said, grinning.

"Nothing. Eat your stew."

* * *

Odessa received a letter in the fourth week.

It came by official rider, sealed with the sigil of the Crown's chancery rather than the Church's sunburst, and Odessa read it standing in the yard with the wind pulling at her cropped grey hair, and then she read it again, and then she folded it with great care and put it in the stove.

"Well?" Juniper asked.

"Pardon," Odessa said. "Restoration of rank. Command of a border garrison. All charges struck from the record, in light of recent... clarifications." Her voice was flat as slate. "They misspelled my name."

"And?"

"And nothing. It's not a pardon. It's a receipt. They're acknowledging they lost something and offering to buy it back cheap." She poked the burning letter with an iron poker, watching the seal curl. "I was a knight of a kingdom that ordered children burned. Whatever I am now, I'm not theirs to restore."

Juniper nodded slowly. "And if they come begging properly? If the new church—whatever grows out of these ruins—asks your help building something clean?"

"Then I'll consider it," Odessa said. "As a free woman. Taking wages." She looked over at the window, where Wren was teaching Sable a clapping game and Sable was losing catastrophically, laughing, their white-streaked hair catching the light. "Besides. I've already got a posting. Unpaid. Terrible rations. Two mapmakers and a weather-witch and a girl who hums old songs." She turned back to the stove. "Best commission I ever held."

* * *

Winter loosened its grip the way a fist opens: finger by finger, reluctantly, each one aches.

By late thaw the passes were walkable, and travelers came in earnest, and word came with them, arriving like spring water—finding every channel, filling every hollow. The Church of the New Dawn had not fallen; it had *split*, cleanly as a river around a stone. One half doubled down on ash and fire, shrinking, bitter, led by hardliners who called the storms blasphemy. The other half called itself the Church of the Listening Sky, and its first doctrine, scrawled on cathedral doors in a dozen cities, was five words long:

*The gods may speak for themselves.*

Seed-bearers stepped into daylight in ones and twos. Wren heard the stories and collected them like stones: a boy in the riverlands who made candles flame blue when he laughed. Twin girls in the south whose shared dreams had predicted three floods, saving a whole town. A blind old man who had carried his godseed unnoticed for seventy years and now, in his final season, spoke with a voice that made bishops weep. Some were welcomed. Some were feared. A few places, shamefully, still raised torches—but fewer every week, and always now there were neighbors standing in front of the doors, saying *no, not this one, not here*.

The world wasn't remade. Wren understood that. It was being remade, gently, at its edges, the way thaw remakes a frozen field—one warm afternoon at a time, one stubborn green shoot at a time. Nobody had won a war. Something better had happened: enough people had simply stopped believing the lie, all at once, the way ice stops being ice.

* * *

On a bright morning in early spring, Wren found Sable on the ridge above the waystation, standing in the wind with their arms loose at their sides, doing nothing that looked like anything.

She climbed up and stood beside them. Below, the valley was turning green at its seams, meltwater braiding silver through the brown.

"What are they saying?" she asked.

Sable considered. "Today? The mountain's pleased about the melt. The river's showing off—it found a new channel under the east bank and it wants everyone to know. There's a storm over the far coast, three hundred miles away, and it's..." They paused, tilting their head, listening to weather the way she had once listened to a god. "Homesick. Storms get homesick. Who knew."

"Do you miss it?" she asked. "Being hidden. Being nobody."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. Wind moved through their white-streaked hair, and Wren would have sworn the wind was being gentle, deliberately gentle, the way you touch something healing.

"Every exile dreams of going home," Sable said at last. "I just never knew my home was going to turn out to be the sky." They glanced down at her, wry and tired and more alive than Wren had ever seen them. "Ask me again in a year."

"You keep saying that."

"I keep meaning it." They held out their hand, and she took it, static-free now, just warm skin and old scars. "Come on. Juniper's redrawn the eastern passes and swears there's a road down there that doesn't exist yet. Odessa says that's nonsense. Odessa also said the temple was a legend, so my money's on Juniper. Someone should go settle it."

"Together?" Wren asked. Just to hear it said.

"Together," Sable said. "That's rather the point of a road."

They started down the slope, side by side, and behind them the wind rose softly off the ridge and followed—all the way down, all the way home, like something that had finally learned where it belonged.
