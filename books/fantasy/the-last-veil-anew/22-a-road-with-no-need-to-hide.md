## 22. A Road With No Need to Hide

The thaw came late that year, and it came all at once.

Sable woke on the first morning of true spring to the sound of water everywhere—the Thornfells shedding their winter in a thousand small voices, meltwater ticking off the eaves of the waystation, running bright in the ditches, dripping from the pines like the whole mountain had decided to talk at once. For eight years Sable had heard weather as threat or concealment: storm as cover, fog as mercy, wind as warning to run. Now they lay still beneath the wool blanket and listened to the snowmelt the way other people listened to music, and understood, not for the first time since the storm that had remade them, that the world had been speaking this entire time. They simply hadn't known how to hear.

*Good morning,* said the mountain, in the long slow grammar of stone and water. *You slept. Good.*

"Morning," Sable said aloud, to no one, and got up.

Wren was already awake. She was always already awake now—had been since midwinter, when the god in her blood had finished whatever quiet work it did and settled into her like a coal banked for the night rather than a fire threatening the house. She sat cross-legged on the table in the main room, hair still sleep-tangled, teaching Odessa's warhorse—now more plow horse than anything—to take an apple from her palm without nipping. The horse was losing the negotiation with tremendous dignity.

"You snore," Wren announced, without turning around.

"I do not."

"You do. Like the mountain before an avalanche. Juniper says so too."

"Juniper," Sable said, "says many things."

Juniper Bell was indeed saying things at that very moment, audible from the corner of the room where she'd spread her charts across two barrels and the floor: "—no, it has to be *Greyhollow Vale*, it can't stay Hollow if there are forty families farming it now, maps are supposed to tell you what a place *is*, not what it was—" A pause, the scratch of her pen. "Odessa, is *Vane's Rest* too much? It's too much, isn't it. I'll call it Vane's Crossing. Crossings are humbler."

From the loft came Odessa's voice, dry as old leather: "Name it whatever you like. I'm not going to argue with a woman who once redrew a river because she didn't like its bend."

"The river thanked me," Juniper said serenely.

It should have felt impossible, this room. Sable stood in the doorway with the cold morning air at their back and the smell of yesterday's bread and today's woodsmoke in front of them, and let themselves feel the impossibility of it fully, the way you press your tongue to a healed wound just to confirm it doesn't hurt anymore. Eight years of rooms entered alone and left alone. Eight years of sleeping with one boot on. And now: a girl who laughed, a knight who argued about place names from a loft, a cartographer committing small acts of heresy against geography itself—and Sable, standing in the middle of it, wanted by no one, hunted by nothing, expected nowhere and welcome everywhere along this stretch of road.

Belonging earned is a strange thing to hold. Belonging granted, Sable had learned long ago, could be revoked with a word—a sentence of exile read out under a grey sky while your own order watched you walk away. But this, this thing built from blizzards and shared bread and a girl carried out of a killing cold, this had no grantor. No one could take it back, because no one had given it. They had made it themselves, out of scraps, the way you make anything worth having.

"All right," Sable said. "Who's coming down to Greyhollow with me, and who's staying to defend the fortress?"

"I am," said Wren, instantly, raising her hand so hard the apple rolled away and the horse finally won.

"Someone has to guard the maps," Juniper said, which meant she wanted to stay and keep working.

"Then it's me and you, little one." Sable pulled their coat from its peg—an ordinary coat now, unremarkable, no longer cut to hide their shape or shadow their face. "We've got pilgrims to guide."

* * *

The road down from the waystation had changed, though not in any way a stranger would notice. The switchbacks were the same. The scree slopes were the same, the black pines, the cairns Sable had stacked over the years to mark the safe line through the rockfall country. What had changed was that Sable walked it in the open, at midmorning, in plain view of the valley below, and no part of them flinched.

For eight years they had moved like smoke through these passes—by night when possible, by gray dawn when not, hood up, name buried, magic locked behind teeth so tight they'd given themselves headaches that lasted seasons. They had learned invisibility the way other people learned instruments: practice, practice, until the hiding became the self. And now here they were walking in sunlight beside a chattering child, and every few minutes Sable caught themselves doing it—the old reflexive glance, the old calculation of sightlines—and every few minutes catching the catch itself, and letting it go, and feeling something in their chest unknot a little further each time.

"—and then Hale said—" Wren stopped herself, grinned sideways. "No, wait. You have to hear it right. So Juniper asks him, very politely, if the Church of the New Dawn has ever considered that their god might be *lonely*, and Hale gets this face, like—"

"Like a man swallowing a bee," Sable supplied.

"*Exactly.* Like he agrees and hates that he agrees." Wren skipped twice to keep pace on the steep section. "Do you think he'll actually do it? Change the church from inside?"

"He burned his orders in front of his superiors and walked out of the conclave with nothing but his book and his faith," Sable said. "He's either going to change the church or die trying. Possibly both." They paused. "I think he'd say that's what faith is for."

Wren considered this with the enormous seriousness she gave to questions of gods and institutions, and then abandoned it in favor of a question about whether hawks could get bored. They walked. The meltwater ran beside the road, loud and glad, and somewhere above them a hawk—bored or not—rode the thermals in great lazy circles, and the mountain watched them go down toward the valley, and its watching was kind.

That was the strangest inheritance of the storm, Sable thought. Before, the Thornfells had been a place that hid you, indifferent at best. Now every ridge and river knew their name—not *Sable Fairweather, smuggler, exile, oath-breaker to an order that bred its children into locks*, but simply Sable, the one who opened the door, the one who said yes. The land had opinions about them now. Mostly it approved. Occasionally, passing a slope where the binding had cracked and the rock was still settling into new shapes, it grumbled, and Sable murmured an apology to it the way you'd apologize to an old person whose house you'd rearranged, and the grumbling subsided.

They crested the last rise above Greyhollow near noon, and the town spread out below them in the spring light—smoke rising straight from forty chimneys, the market square already loud, the new fields beyond it dark and wet and waiting for seed. Fields that hadn't existed three years ago. Families that had come because word travels, even in the mountains, especially now: *the passes are open, the storms speak, nobody burns anybody in Greyhollow Vale.*

At the edge of town, where the pilgrim road met the market street, a knot of travelers waited by the old shrine—rebuilt now, whitewashed, both gods' symbols carved side by side over the door, which would have gotten everyone in it executed not long ago. Five of them: a merchant couple with a mule loaded heavy, a young man with the hollow cheeks of someone who'd walked far, an older woman leaning on a staff, and a boy of maybe twelve holding a birdcage with a finch in it, for reasons Sable chose not to ask about.

"Sable Fairweather?" the older woman said, squinting. "They said at the inn you guide people over Thornfell Pass."

"They said right." Sable looked them over the way they always did—the mule's shoes, the young man's boots, the boy's coat—old habit, assessing load and liability. Then, new habit: they smiled. "Pass is clear to the waystation. Snowline's retreating fast. Three days' walk, four if we're gentle with the mule. I'll take you up myself."

"And it's true?" the merchant asked, lowering his voice. "What they say. That the storms—the thunder, that it talks—"

"It talks," Sable said. "Mostly it complains about erosion. If you're polite to the mountain, the mountain is polite back." They hoisted the mule's lead. "Keep your prayers short and your gratitude sincere and you'll be fine."

And as the little caravan started up the road, Wren darting ahead to walk backward in front of the boy and interrogate him about the finch, Sable Fairweather walked in the middle of it, visible, named, employed, and felt—settling over them like sun after the long winter, warm and almost frightening in its simplicity—unhidden.

Eight years. Eight years of being a rumor, a shadow, a cautionary tale told in storm-caller halls about what happened to those who refused. And all that time, the whole time, they had thought exile was a wall around them. It had never been a wall. It had only ever been a door held shut from the inside, and all it had ever taken to open it was the terrible, simple act of stepping through.

* * *

Three days later they came back down with empty-handed pilgrims and full pockets, and the waystation smelled of roasting onions and Juniper's ink, and Odessa was arguing with the chimney.

"The draft's wrong," she said, when Sable came in shaking snowmelt off their shoulders—spring snow, brief and theatrical, already melting. "It smokes when the wind's from the east."

"So turn your chair."

"I don't turn my *chair* for a chimney. The chimney should know better." She said it deadpan, but Sable caught it underneath—the joke under the joke, the knight who had spent her whole life serving institutions that were supposed to know better and hadn't, rebuilding her principles one household grievance at a time. She'd grown a vegetable garden in the fall. Nobody had commented on this. Everybody had noticed.

"Where's Wren?"

"River. With the god." Odessa shrugged. "She goes most evenings now. Says it likes to watch the ice go out."

Sable found her there, an hour before sunset, sitting on the flat grey boulder above the shallows with her boots off and her feet in water that was still cold enough to ache. The river ran high with melt, loud, carrying the last pans of ice downstream in stately procession. Wren wasn't talking. Her eyes were half closed, and around her the air did a thing Sable had learned to recognize: it went attentive. Not charged, not dangerous—attentive, the way a room attends when someone wise begins to speak.

Sable sat down on the boulder without asking. There was no need, anymore, to ask.

"What's it saying?" they asked quietly.

Wren tilted her head, listening to something beneath the river's voice. "Not saying. Showing, maybe. It's remembering when this valley was all ice. Before the war. Before the burying." She frowned a little, working at it. "It says it used to sing the glaciers to sleep. That's why they called it the Quiet God—it sang things *down*. Storms. Wars. People." She glanced up at Sable, and in the slanting light her eyes held a depth that was hers and also, gently, not. "It says it's sorry it took so long to ask you properly. It says it was afraid of being refused again."

Sable looked out at the river. Their chest did the complicated thing it always did now when the old gods apologized to *them*, as if they were the injured party and not the descendants of jailers. "Tell it," they said, "that refusing was all anyone ever did to it, so it had reason to be afraid. And tell it—" they had to stop, start again— "tell it we're not done. There are more burial lines on Juniper's maps. More children waking up with lullabies in their heads. Somebody's going to have to help them learn to listen without being afraid, and somebody's going to have to teach the world not to worship them or burn them but just—" they gestured at the river, the mountains, the whole listening evening— "*hear* them. We're not done."

Wren listened. Nodded. Then, relaying with the scrupulousness of a child delivering a message exactly as received: "It says thank you. And it says—" a pause, and a grin broke across her face, sudden and bright and eleven years old— "it says Juniper's new name for the pass is wrong and she should ask the mountain instead of guessing."

"Oh no," Sable said. "Oh, that's going to start a war."

"It says wars are its least favorite thing."

"Well, it can come tell her that itself."

As if summoned—or perhaps simply punctual—Juniper appeared on the path above the river, sleeves inked to the elbow, calling down: "There you are! Odessa says dinner, and also the chimney and I have reached an understanding involving a longer flue, and also—Sable, there's a rider coming up the valley road. Alone. Grey cloak." A beat. "Storm-caller grey."

The warmth went out of the evening so fast Sable could taste it. Old reflexes rose like floodwater: the calculating glance, the counting of exits, the hand that remembered how to close around lightning. Beside them Wren had gone still, and the river seemed to lean closer.

But the rider came on slowly, openly, reins loose, and as they neared the bridge Sable saw the white streak in the dark hair first, and then the lined face, and then the eyes—grey as the cloak, grey as everything that order wore—watching Sable with an expression Sable had last seen on the day of their exile, and had never expected to see again: hope, badly disguised as duty.

"Maren," Sable said.

"Little Fairweather." Maren Solenne, first voice of the Stormcaller Order, who had read out their exile and had wept while doing it, dismounted at the foot of the bridge. She looked older. She looked lighter. In her hands she held a folded document with the order's seal broken—broken deliberately, the halves folded outward so the mark showed face-up, which among storm-callers meant only one thing: *dissolved, renounced, no longer binding.*

"We heard the storm," she said simply. "All of us, in the hall at Cairn Vaunt, the night you gave them their voices back. Every lock we ever set came open, and do you know what we discovered, Sable? We discovered we had forgotten what our own magic was *for*." She held out the broken-sealed paper like an offering, or an apology, or perhaps those had always been the same thing. "The elders are gone. The ones who built the locks. Those of us who are left—we want to learn what you learned in the temple. We want to ask the gods properly, the way you do." Her voice roughened. "We want to stop being jailers. And we don't know how. And someone told me—you'll laugh—that someone told me there's a smuggler in the Thornfells who guides lost travelers home."

Behind Sable, Wren giggled. Up the hill, Juniper had already produced fresh paper. Somewhere in the waystation, Odessa was pretending not to listen and failing completely, and the chimney smoked cheerfully in the wrong direction, and the mountain, patient and kind, leaned in to hear the answer.

Sable took the paper. Held the whole broken weight of their old life in two hands, light as ash.

"Well," they said, and found they were smiling. "First lesson's easy. You start by listening."

The river carried the last of the ice out of the mountains, singing all the way down.
