## 2. A Proclamation in Every Square

The bells began before dawn.

Isandro heard them from the third floor of the Exchange, where the ledger room's narrow window caught the gray light coming off the harbor. Six bells, then silence, then six again—not the harbor's rhythm, not the guild's. This was the municipal pattern, the one rung only for royal decree, and it had a sound like a stone dropped into still water: one clean announcement, and then everything rippling outward from it.

They set down their pen mid-figure. The ink pooled at the nib, a small black eye staring up at them.

Below, in the Lantern Square, a crier was unrolling something.

Isandro did not go to the window. That was the first discipline of hiding—*do not look when everyone else looks*—and they had practiced it for ten years until it lived in their spine. Instead they sat very still at their desk, surrounded by columns of impossible trade: six vials of bottled echo from the drowned chapel of Saint Verene; a shadow borrowed against interest from a man in Corvane who would never see spring otherwise; one last summer, sold twice, which was either fraud or genius depending on who survived the refund.

Ordinary work. Safe work. Ink that stayed where it was put.

But the bells kept ringing, and through the floorboards Isandro could feel the building changing around them—the particular hush of many people listening. The Exchange employed two hundred souls across its floors and cellars: appraisers who could weigh grief by the grain, couriers with hollow bones, clerks like Sefa who could read a sealed letter without breaking its wax. Every one of them was listening now. Every one of them had something to lose.

*A proclamation in every square,* they thought. *That means every square has someone it applies to.*

The crier's voice carried up even through glass and stone. It was trained to carry—the crown paid for good lungs—and it landed in the ledger room like a thrown knife:

"By order of His Majesty Aldric the Third, Keeper of the Veil: all persons bearing gifts beyond natural law shall present themselves for registration within thirty days. Registration is mercy. Refusal is treason. The hunt begins on the first frost."

Then the bells again. Then, distantly, other squares taking it up, other criers, other bells, until the whole city was a bowl of sound and Isandro sat inside it feeling the dread settle into their bones the way cold settles into stone—slowly, thoroughly, all the way to the foundation.

*Registration is mercy.* They turned the phrase over the way an appraiser turns a suspected forgery. It had the right weight. It had the right shine. And it was worth exactly nothing.

They knew what registration meant because they had helped write the ledgers that explained it, years ago, in the careful euphemism the Exchange used for everything ugly. *Relocation services. Custodial transfer. Inventory of persons of interest.* A registered gift-bearer became a line item. A line item became a wagon. A wagon went east, toward the King's Road, toward the pale towers visible from the highest windows on clear days, and nothing that went there ever sent word back.

Ten years ago, when the purges under Aldric the Second had been cruder, Isandro had watched from a rooftop as storm-callers were marched through the streets with iron collars humming dampening-songs. They had been nineteen, newly fled from the coastal provinces where their gift had drowned a village and killed their mother, and they had sworn an oath over their own shaking hands: *never again will the sky answer when I am afraid.*

So they had buried it. Layer by layer, year by year—a ledger-keeper's discipline applied to the soul. They had taught themselves to feel a thunderhead building and think only of the laundry left out. They had stood in downpours and felt nothing but wet. Or so they had told themselves. The gift hadn't died. It had gone down deep, like a coal banked under ash, and this morning, hearing the crier say *gift-bearers* in that flat official voice, Isandro felt it turn over in its sleep.

Their hand had gone white on the pen. Ink dripped onto the page, blooming across a column of figures for a client who had traded his father's lullabies for courage.

"Vasquez." Sefa's voice at the door, low. She was holding two cups of chicory and neither of them was for anyone else. "You're not at the window."

"No."

"Good." She came in anyway and shut the door with her heel, which she never did. Sefa was a broad, calm woman with ink stains to the elbow and a gift she treated like a party trick—reading sealed letters—which had always struck Isandro as either tremendous wisdom or tremendous recklessness. This morning her face said neither. Her face said fear, carefully folded into quarters. "It's everywhere. Not just the capital. My cousin wrote from Marrow's Ford last week—proclamation posted before the harvest fair. They've been planning this since summer."

"The criers are coordinated." Isandro took the cup because refusing it would be memorable. "Thirty days is generous."

"It's arithmetic." Sefa sat on the corner of the desk, which was also against the rules, and drank. "Thirty days is how long it takes a family to decide whether to report you themselves for the reward or risk being charged with harboring. It's not mercy. It's a deadline on love."

Isandro looked at her. Ten years of working three desks apart, and they had never once discussed what each of them was. It was the courtesy of the hunted: you didn't make another person complicit by knowing them. But the word *love* sat in the room between them now, undeniable, and Isandro heard themselves say, quietly, "Do you have somewhere?"

"I have a cousin with a fishing boat and no imagination." Safa smiled without any joy in it. "And I have a job I'm not going to abandon, because the Exchange protects its people. Odile said so herself, at Midwinter. *Under this roof, no hand reaches for you.* You remember."

Isandro remembered. They remembered standing in the great hall among two hundred others while Odile Ferran said it in her silver voice, and they remembered believing it, because wanting to believe it had been the whole point.

*The guild's neutrality is a story,* something said in them, cold and clear, like water finding a crack. *A story you have been telling yourself so you could sleep.*

They didn't answer Sefa. They said, "We should get back to work," and hated how easily it came, how ten years of practice made cowardice sound like prudence.

* * *

At midmorning, the mistress summoned the senior staff.

Odile Ferran received them in the Map Room, which was apt, because the Map Room contained the only complete chart of the city's hidden ways—the smuggler stairs, the priest-holes, the drowned gates of the old quarter—and because Odile liked to conduct difficult meetings beneath things that reminded people of escape routes she controlled.

She stood at the head of the long table with her hands flat on the wood, and Isandro took their usual place near the end, between Master Corvin the appraiser and a courier named Pell whose gift—breathing underwater—was the worst-kept secret in the building.

"You've heard the proclamation." Odile's voice filled the room without rising. She was perhaps sixty, gray as seafoam, with eyes the exact color of money counted in the dark. "I'll say this once, and then we return to work.

"The Veiled Exchange trades in impossibilities. We do not trade in politics. Whatever stands between the crown and the gifted is not our war. Our clients come to us precisely because we are nobody's ally and everybody's servant. That neutrality has kept this roof standing through three reigns, two sieges, and the Fire of Lowmarket, and it will keep it standing through this."

She let her gaze travel the table. Isandro watched it pass over faces—Corvin, whose gift of finding lost things had once located a dead king's conscience; Pell, who was gripping the table edge; young Mistress Anwe, who could unpick locks with a whisper and was seventeen years old.

"Some of you may be wondering about the registration edict." Odile's tone did not change at all. "I will address it plainly so that rumor doesn't do it for me. The Exchange does not register its members. The Exchange does not surrender its members. The Exchange does not *discuss* its members, inside these walls or out. What any of you do with your private lives and private natures is your own affair, conducted discreetly, as all our affairs are conducted. Discretion," she said, and the word landed like a coin on a counter, "has kept us alive. Continue practicing it. Return to your desks."

There was a pause in which no one moved, because moving first would mean something.

Then Corvin asked—he was old enough to dare—"Mistress. If hunters come to the door?"

"Hunters," Odile said, "come to doors that open." And she lifted her hands from the table, and the meeting was over.

Isandro filed out with the others, and it should have been comforting—that firm voice, that wall of discretion drawn around them. For ten years they had needed exactly this: an institution that would bury their name under commerce and paperwork, that understood hiding as a professional discipline. Odile Ferran had given them a decade of safe nights.

So why, walking back down the corridor with the proclamation bells still faintly ringing in their skull, did the comfort taste like the memory-wipe draughts they cataloged in drawer nine? Sweet on the tongue. Hollow underneath. Something taken out and replaced with smoothness.

Because, they admitted, climbing the stairs, because of the arithmetic. *Discretion has kept us alive.* Not justice. Not defiance. Alive. Odile hadn't promised to stand between the hunters and her people. She'd promised that the doors wouldn't open—which was a different thing entirely if the hunters already had names. Names could be sold. Names were, in fact, the Exchange's oldest commodity.

Isandro reached their desk and found Sefa already back at hers, and across the aisle Pell, and beyond him forty clerks bending over their ledgers as though the world outside hadn't just redrawn itself. The pens scratched. The ink dried. The machinery of impossible commerce ground on, indifferent, and Isandro understood with terrible clarity that this too was the design: keep everyone working, keep everyone quiet, keep everyone's nose in the columns so no one looked up long enough to ask what the roof was actually built on.

They pulled a fresh ledger toward them and wrote the date at the top of the page in their small, disciplined hand, and their hand was steady, and that steadiness frightened them more than trembling would have.

* * *

The crack came at evening, as cracks do, in a place no one was watching.

Isandro stayed late—it was quarter-day, and the eastern accounts wanted balancing—and by the time they finished, the ledger room was empty and the lamps burned low. They carried the day's folios down to the intake vault for sealing, because procedure was another kind of armor, and the vault clerk had gone home, and so Isandro did the sealing themselves: wax, signet, shelf, done. Ten minutes' work. Routine.

Except one parcel in the incoming tray had been logged but not yet shelved, and its manifest slip had fluttered loose, and Isandro—bending to retrieve it—saw the entry in the intake book that lay open beside the tray.

*Item: one (1) covenant-stone fragment, provenance elderkin, sealed per standing instruction, consignee: O.F., personal. Payment received in part: three (3) names, verified.*

Three names. Verified.

Isandro stood in the lamplit vault with the manifest in their hand and the cold coming up through the stone floor, and they read it again, and again, as though repetition might change what *verified* meant. Someone had paid the Exchange for a sealed covenant-stone—elderkin-work, the kind of thing that appeared in maybe one trade a generation—with names instead of coin. Registered names, presumably; *verified* implied a registry, and the only new registry in the world belonged to the Veilhunt.

The hunt had found a door into the guild. And the door had opened inward.

They told themselves a dozen stories on the stairs back up. A clerical error. A trap laid for someone else. Odile buying the names to destroy them, to protect her people—yes, that one had a shape that fit, that was almost comfortable—

But comfortable things had stopped fitting this morning, and the story wouldn't hold. Because Odile had stood in front of the senior staff and promised the doors would not open, and Isandro had spent ten years learning to hear what powerful people said by listening to what they carefully didn't. She hadn't said *no hunter will touch my people.* She hadn't said *the Exchange will fight.* She had said *discretion*, which was a merchant's word, and merchants knew that everything was for sale at the right price—even safety, even people, especially people, because people were the one commodity that appreciated in a panic.

In the ledger room, the lamps had guttered low. Isandro sat down at their desk in the dark and did not light another.

Somewhere below, behind a seal bearing Odile's own mark, a piece of the old pact between realms sat in the dark, bought with human names. Isandro didn't know yet what a covenant-stone was, not truly—only that the word *covenant* had appeared in the oldest folios, in the restricted drawers they weren't cleared to open, always redacted, always *per standing instruction*, as if the guild had agreed generations ago to stop asking questions about its own foundations.

*Amnesiac by design,* whispered the thought, and Isandro pushed it away, because it was too large for tonight, because tonight was for smaller fears.

Tonight was for the fact that somewhere in this building, possibly above them in the sleeping quarters, possibly at the next desk, someone had handed three human beings to the crown as payment. And tomorrow morning those three names would still be here—Sefa laughing over her chicory, Pell arguing with Corvin about the tide tables, young Anwe whistling to locks—and Isandro would have to look at all of them and wonder which name was already written in someone's margin, priced, verified, waiting for the first frost.

Outside, far off over the harbor, thunder muttered. Just weather. Ordinary weather. Isandro sat unmoving in the dark and felt the old answering pressure rise helplessly in their chest, the gift pressing up against its ten-year grave like a hand against a coffin lid, and they pressed it back down, and won, the way they always won.

But as they finally blew out the lamps and locked the folios away, they caught sight of the intake book's entry still open in memory—*three names, verified*—and understood that the question was no longer whether the Veilhunt would reach inside the Exchange.

Someone had already shown it the way.
