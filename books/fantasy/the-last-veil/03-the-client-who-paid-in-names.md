## 3. The Client Who Paid in Names

The rain came to Corvenne that morning the way bad news always did—sideways, without warning, and soaking through everything before anyone thought to close a window.

Isandro felt it before they heard it. A pressure behind the sternum, faint as a tuning fork struck in another room, and their hands went still over the ledger they'd been balancing since dawn. *Entry 447: one bottled echo, vendor's price forty crowns, buyer's price one hundred ten.* The ink dried slowly in the damp. Somewhere above the Exchange's counting-house, three stories of slate roof were shedding water into gutters that had not been cleaned in Isandro's decade of service, and the whole building groaned like an old ship settling into harbor.

*Not yours,* they told themselves, as they had every day for ten years. *Not your weather.*

They pressed their palms flat against the desk until the pressure passed. The wood was scarred with generations of clerks' initials and one deep burn from a candle that had fallen during the Night of Ashes—before Isandro's time, though Odile Ferran still flinched whenever anyone mentioned it. Isandro had learned to read the Exchange the way other people read faces: by its scars.

The counting-house occupied the middle floor of the Veiled Exchange proper—a narrow building squeezed between a chandler's and a shuttered theater on Ropemaker's Lane, unremarkable except for the fact that its cellar descended farther than any cellar in the city had a right to. Above them, the guild's front rooms sold what passed for ordinary wares: imported spices, secondhand jewelry, maps of coastlines that did not appear on crown charts. Below them, behind three locked doors and a door that was not a door at all but a folded space bought at considerable expense, lay the true inventory.

Bottled echoes. Borrowed shadows. One last summer, decanted into glass and guaranteed to smell of cut grass and river water for exactly as long as the buyer could bear it.

And, as of last winter, a single memory-wipe—the rarest thing on the shelves, older than Isandro's employment, older possibly than Odile herself.

"Vasquez." Pellam's head appeared around the stairwell door, his voice pitched low the way everyone in the building had learned to pitch things lately. "Mistress wants you upstairs. Client for the vault."

Isandro's pen stopped mid-numeral. "The wipe?"

"The wipe." Pellam's eyes flicked toward the window, toward the rain, then away again with the studied carelessness of a man who had decided not to have thoughts. He was fifty if he was a day, bald as an egg, and he had worked the Exchange for thirty years without once asking what any of it was for. "Buyer's already in the green room. Come up through the gallery so you're not seen crossing the lane."

*So you're not seen.* Three weeks ago those words would have meant nothing more than ordinary discretion. Now, after two mornings of criers in every square proclaiming the Veilhunt, after the proclamation nailed to the chandler's door with its list of requirements and its threat of seizure, the words had teeth. All gift-bearers must present themselves for registry within the month. All who harbored gift-bearers would be held accountable. All trades in gifts, glimmers, and workings were suspended pending royal inspection—

Except, apparently, this one.

Isandro capped the inkwell, squared the ledger, and followed Pellam up through the back stairs.

* * *

The green room had been a merchant's parlor once, before the Exchange absorbed half the block. Now it was paneled in dark wood hung with cloth of the color of pond water, lit by a single lamp turned down so low the flame seemed embarrassed to be burning. The client sat with their back to the light.

Hooded. Of course hooded. Half the Exchange's clients wore hoods, and Isandro had long ago stopped trying to read faces—they read hands instead, and posture, and the way a person counted out coin. This one's hands were gloved in good kid leather, fine-stitched, the kind that cost more than Pellam made in a season. They sat very straight. Not nervous. Waiting, the way a blade waits in its sheath.

"Mistress Ferran is delayed," Isandro said, taking the clerk's position at the side table—present but peripheral, the furniture of the transaction. "I'll conduct the preliminaries, if that suits."

"It suits." The voice was a surprise: smooth, unhurried, genderless by intention. Someone who had practiced sounding like no one. "You are the keeper of the ledgers?"

"I keep ledgers, yes."

"Then you will want to record the terms." From within the cloak, a gloved hand produced a purse and set it on the table with a click heavier than coin. "Half the price in sovereigns, mint-stamped, untraceable. The balance—" a pause, deliberate as a held breath "—in kind."

Odile arrived then, sweeping through the far door with her shawl pinned high and her expression arranged into its professional mask: courteous, impenetrable, faintly bored. She had been beautiful once, Isandro thought, in the way that fortresses are beautiful. Now she was mostly angles and arithmetic.

"You're punctual," she said to the client, which from Odile was nearly warm. She glanced at Isandro. "Vasquez will witness. Proceed."

The client unfolded from the chair. Under the cloak they were tall, narrow-shouldered, economical in movement. "The item. Your catalog describes it as a full excision. Not suppression—excision. The memory removed entire, leaving no residue, no dream-echo, no haunting. I require confirmation."

"You've read the catalog," Odile said. "You know what we claim."

"I know what catalogs claim. I'm asking what *you* guarantee."

For a moment the two of them regarded each other across the lamplight, and Isandro felt something prickle along the base of their skull—that old storm-sense, the one they'd spent a decade drowning in routine, stirring like a dog that hears its name in another room. There was a working on this client. Not a gift, exactly. Something worn, like armor. Something that smelled, if workings could be said to smell, of cold iron and clean rooms.

"The guarantee is this," Odile said. "The item has been tested twice in the last century. Both subjects retained no knowledge of the excised material. Neither dreamed of it. Neither grieved it, because neither knew there was anything to grieve. That is the horror of it and also the mercy, depending on which end of the transaction you stand."

"And the cost to the wielder?"

"There is no wielder. It works itself. That is why it is singular." Odile let the silence stretch. "Once it is used, it is used. You understand you're buying the only one."

"I understand that better than you know." The client turned—or tilted, rather, the hood angling toward Isandro without quite revealing anything beneath it. "Your clerk will record the payment in kind. Names, as agreed. Seven of them. Each verified, each current, each worth more than the gold."

From the folds of the cloak came a slip of paper, folded twice, sealed with plain black wax. No sigil. Odile took it without touching the seal, weighed it in her palm as though names had mass—and perhaps, in the Exchange's economy, they did—and slid it into her sleeve unread.

"We'll verify before delivery," she said. "Standard term. Forty-eight hours."

"Forty-eight hours is acceptable. But Mistress Ferran—" the smooth voice dropped half a register, and the room seemed to lean in "—verify quickly. The market for such names is about to become very crowded. And very dangerous to hold."

Something passed between them then, some frequency below hearing, and Isandro watched Odile Ferran—the woman who had stared down smugglers, tax collectors, and once, according to legend, a delegation from the palace itself—watched her go very still, the way small animals go still.

"Delivery Thursday," she said. "Midnight. Use the chandler's entrance."

The client inclined the hood a fraction. "A pleasure doing business with the Exchange. They say you deal in impossible things neutrally. I do hope neutrality continues to serve you." The gloved hand lifted in something too shallow to be called a bow. "Clerk. See that the entry is precise. Precision protects everyone."

Then the door closed behind them, and the lamp flame stood up straight again, and Isandro realized they had been holding their breath long enough for it to ache.

* * *

"Names," Isandro said, once the client's footsteps had faded down the gallery stairs. "Payment in names. For a wipe. Whose memories does this person intend to erase, that they need seven names to afford it?"

"That," Odile said, "is none of the Exchange's concern."

"It's entirely our concern. We're selling the instrument."

"We're selling an *antique*, Vasquez, to a buyer who met the price. What they do with it afterward is the same as what a man does with a knife he bought from a cutler." Odile crossed to the window and stood looking out at the rain without opening the curtains. Her reflection in the dark glass was ghost-pale and doubled. "Besides. Names are the oldest currency this house accepts. Older than coin. You'd know that if you'd read the founding charters."

"I've read the founding charters. They're duller than sermons and twice as evasive."

That earned her—the earned *him*, Isandro corrected; ten years of careful grammar—a glance in the reflection, dry as August. "Careful."

"I'm always careful." Isandro hesitated, and the hesitation was a mistake, because it let the next question out before judgment could catch it. "The names. Will you show me? For the ledger, I mean. If I'm to record payment received in kind, I should verify—"

"No."

The word came fast, flat, final. Odile turned from the window, and for just a moment her face was not the merchant's mask but something older and wearier underneath, like paint peeling from a wall to show older paint.

"The ledger entry records *payment in names, seven, verified per standard terms*. Nothing more. You don't see the names, I don't see the names until verification, and no one else sees them at all. That's how names stay safe, Vasquez. By never letting them gather in one pair of eyes." She gathered her shawl. "Thursday, midnight. You'll attend the delivery. Bring the transfer-writs and say nothing unless spoken to."

She paused at the door.

"And Vasquez. Whatever you think you felt in that room—whatever your instincts tell you about that buyer—you'll keep it behind your teeth. The Exchange survives on not knowing. It's the only reason houses like ours are permitted to exist at all."

Then she was gone, and Isandro stood alone in the green room with the lamp and the rain and the shape of a question they couldn't stop turning over.

*Neutrality continues to serve you.* The client had said it like a man checking whether a floorboard would hold his weight.

* * *

Two days later, cross-checking the quarterly accounts against the registry rolls—tedium Isandro had volunteered for precisely because tedium kept the gift asleep—something caught.

It was the kind of thing only a ledger-keeper would catch, the kind of thing ten years of disciplined boredom had trained Isandro's eye for: a discrepancy of overlap. The city's new registry of gift-bearers had been posted publicly, as proclamations were, nailed to the customs house and copied by criers. Most people averted their eyes from it the way they averted their eyes from executions. But Isandro had memorized it in a single pass, all ninety-four names, because a ledger-keeper's memory was the one part of the old gift that had never needed suppressing.

And now, reconstructing from memory the fragments Odile had let slip—not the names themselves, she had shown Isandro nothing, but the *shape* of the transaction, the timing, the client's words about crowded markets and dangerous holdings—Isandro laid the pieces against the registry rolls and felt the guilt arrive first, cold and complete, before suspicion even knocked.

Because here was the thing Isandro could not unknow: the Exchange had taken payment in seven names. Verified, current names. And the only market where names had suddenly become currency—the only market in living memory—was the Veilhunt. Registered gift-bearers were being taken. Unregistered ones were being hunted harder. Either way, a name on that list was a commodity with a price, and someone had just paid that price to the Veiled Exchange, and the Veiled Exchange had accepted.

Three of the names surfaced in Isandro's memory with terrible clarity, because Isandro knew them. Had known them across ledgers and years.

Marisol Tenne, registered the second day of the hunt—a hedge-healer from Saltrow whose gift was so mild she could coax fevers down a day early. She had traded with the Exchange for years. Bottled warmth, once, for her arthritic mother.

Devrel Osk, a lantern-tender whose light burned without oil. Registered under protest, Isandro had heard, dragged into it by his own family's fear.

And Sefa.

Sefa Ilves, who sat four desks down in the counting-house, who hummed when she balanced columns, whose small bright gift—she could make a locked door forget it was locked, gently, apologetically—had seemed too harmless to matter to anyone. Sefa, who had registered because Odile had instructed every gifted member of the household staff to register, saying it was safer, saying the Exchange's name would shield them, saying *we are neutral, no one touches what belongs to us*.

Isandro sat very still in the lamplit counting-house while the rain resumed against the windows, and the guilt curdled, thickened, turned slowly over into something with edges.

Because Isandro had been there. In the green room. Witnessing, silent, precise. Recording the terms of a transaction that had just put Sefa's name—Marisol's name, Devrel's name—in the hands of a hooded stranger who spoke of markets and danger like a man discussing weather. And whatever the Exchange intended to do with that payment—sell the names onward, trade them to the hunters, hold them hostage against inspection—there was no version of the arithmetic in which the names' owners survived it untouched.

*We are neutral.* Odile's voice in Isandro's memory, calm as still water.

But neutral parties didn't take payment in flesh. Neutral parties didn't sell the coordinates of the vulnerable to whoever could afford them. Neutrality, Isandro thought with a clarity that felt like the first crack of ice on a river, was a story the Exchange told itself so it could sleep. And stories, in Isandro's long experience, were the most expensive things in the world to buy back.

Outside, thunder rolled—far off, out over the harbor, harmless, someone else's storm. Isandro's hands had gone white-knuckled on the desk without their noticing, and the lamp flame leaned sideways in a draft that wasn't there.

They made themself breathe. Made themself write the ledger entry, neat and precise, exactly as instructed: *Item delivered against payment: sovereigns, mint-stamped; and names, seven, verified.* The lie of omission sat on the page like a stone in a shoe.

Thursday. Midnight. The chandler's entrance.

Two days to decide what a ledger-keeper who kept other people's secrets owed to the people those secrets were about—and whether the Exchange's silence, once broken, could ever be mended at all.

Down the corridor, faint through the wall, Isandro heard Sefa humming as she balanced her columns.

The sound followed Isandro home through the rain, and would not leave.
