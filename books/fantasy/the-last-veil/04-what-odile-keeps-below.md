## 4. What Odile Keeps Below

The vault beneath the Veiled Exchange had no door.

That was the first thing Isandro had learned in their second week of employment, ten years ago, when they'd asked a senior clerk where the deep holdings were kept and received a look of polite horror, as if they'd asked where the guild buried its dead. *Below,* the clerk had said. *Where things go when we stop wanting to remember them.* And that had been the whole of the answer.

For a decade, Isandro had honored the arrangement without thinking about it. Trades came down from above on brass dumbwaiters; payments went back up wrapped in oilcloth and wax. The vault was Odile's country, not theirs. A ledger-keeper cataloged what passed through their hands and asked nothing else. That was the covenant of the Exchange—unspoken, unexamined, and, like all covenants Isandro had ever known, built on someone's silence.

But Sefa had been dragged out of her cot four nights ago, and the silence afterward had teeth.

Isandro stood now in the counting room at the hour when even the tallow candles seemed embarrassed to burn—past midnight, before the sweepers came—with Odile's ring of keys heavy in their pocket. Stolen an hour earlier from the hook beside her chair while she sat upstairs drinking her nightly glass of plum wine and staring at nothing. She always drank alone. She always stared at the same spot on the far wall, at nothing, as if reading something written there in letters too small for anyone else to see.

*She sold three names.* The hooded client's payment ledger was still hidden under Isandro's floorboard, and every time Isandro closed their eyes they saw the entries: *three registered gift-bearers, tendered in part payment.* Names Isandro recognized. Names with faces. One of them—a lantern-eyed boy named Corvin who bought bottled echoes twice a season—had been seventeen years old.

The guild's neutrality was a story. Isandro had known it since the proclamation criers filled the squares with the word *Veilhunt*, but knowing it as abstraction and knowing it as arithmetic were different things. Three names was arithmetic. So Isandro had done the one thing ten years of careful invisibility forbade: they'd stopped asking nothing.

The stairwell behind the wine cellar went down farther than any building in Saltreign had a right to be tall. Isandro counted the steps out of habit—one hundred forty, then the stairs simply continued past the point where the city's foundations should have ended—and noticed, somewhere around step two hundred, that the air had changed. Warmer. With a faint mineral smell, like the inside of a struck flint. Like the air after lightning.

Their skin prickled. Ten years of burying the storm-calling hadn't dulled its senses; it only meant Isandro felt the world's electricity the way a scar feels rain. And something below them was humming with it.

At the bottom of the stairs stood a wall.

Not stone. Not brick. It looked, in the light of Isandro's shuttered lantern, like a slab of grey glass grown into the surrounding rock, seamless except for a shallow depression at chest height—the size and shape of a hand. Old carvings ran around the edges of the slab, worn nearly smooth, but Isandro's lantern caught them at an angle and the shadows sharpened: spirals, and branching lines like rivers or roots or lightning, and figures—tall figures, wrong-proportioned, standing beside smaller ones, their hands joined.

Isandro's heart was doing something embarrassing. They made themselves breathe slowly, the way they did when thunder rolled too close over the harbor. Then they pressed their palm into the depression.

The slab drank the heat of their hand. For one long moment, nothing. Then, deep in the rock, something turned over—a sound like a lock the size of a cathedral—and the grey glass thinned to translucence and parted along a seam Isandro couldn't see even while watching it happen.

Cold clean air flowed out, carrying the smell of ozone and old paper.

* * *

The vault was not a room. It was a street.

That was Isandro's second thought, and it refused to leave. Rows of shelving stretched away into darkness on either side of a central aisle paved with the same grey glass-stone, and the shelves rose higher than any warehouse rack in Saltreign, and the things on them were not bottles of echoes or jars of borrowed shadow. Those trades—the ones Isandro cataloged daily, the ones that gave the Veiled Exchange its name and its respectable margins—those were upstairs trades. Street-level wonders. This was something else.

Here lay objects that made Isandro's buried gift stir like a dog hearing its name spoken in another house.

A bell in a lead cradle that rang, faintly, though no wind reached this deep. A crown of black iron on a velvet pad, missing half its points, each stump capped with a plug of grey stone identical to the door. An hourglass whose sand fell upward, patiently, grain by grain. And everywhere, everywhere, ledgers—not like the trade-books upstairs but vast, bound in hide Isandro chose not to identify, their spines stamped with the same spiral-and-branching-lines sigil.

Isandro walked the aisle with their lantern held high and their breath coming short, and the hum in the walls grew until they could feel it in their molars. It wasn't random. It had rhythm. It had *phrasing.* Anyone else might have called it resonance, some property of the stone. But Isandro had spent their childhood learning the language of storms, and this was a voice holding one long, exhausted note.

The oldest shelf was nearest the far wall. Of course it was. Isandro followed the hum to its source and found a case of lead-lined glass, and inside the case—

They stopped breathing.

A fragment of stone the size of a loaf of bread, jagged on one side as if torn from something vastly larger, smooth-faced on the other, and carved across the smooth face with the spiral sigil. Storm-light moved inside it. Not reflected—*moved*, slow coils of blue-white radiance turning beneath the carved lines like weather seen from very high up. It was humming. It was the source. Everything else in the vault resonated against it the way a harp string answers a sung note.

Isandro knew what it was before they knew how they knew. Some inheritance older than exile, older than the hiding, older than the ledger-desk. They pressed their hand flat against the glass and whispered the word their mother had used only once, at night, when she thought the children slept:

"Covenant-stone."

Behind them, a floorboard creaked that had never creaked before, because there were no floorboards, because this place was older than floors—

"I wondered which of my clerks would finally come down here."

Odile Ferran stood at the mouth of the aisle with a lamp in one hand and no weapon in the other, wearing her nightclothes under a wool dressing gown, her iron-grey hair loose for perhaps the first time in Isandro's decade of service. She didn't look angry. She looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour.

"Odile." Isandro's voice came out steadier than they deserved. "Three names. You took three names in payment."

"I know what I took." She set her lamp on a shelf, next to the upward-falling hourglass, and the small domesticity of it was obscene. "I've been waiting four nights for you to work it out. I'd begun to worry I'd overpaid for your cleverness all these years."

"Corvin is seventeen."

"Corvin is alive, which is more than he'd be if I'd refused the buyer and drawn attention to the guild's registers." She said it flatly, without apology, and walked past Isandro toward the covenant-stone as though visiting an old acquaintance. "You want to understand. Good. Look around you, Vasquez. Really look. What do you think this place is?"

"A vault."

"It's a *keeperhouse.*" Her fingers hovered over the lead-glass case without touching it. "The Veiled Exchange wasn't founded to trade in impossible things. Trading was the cover. The *purpose*—" she tapped the case, once, gently, the way one taps a sleeping person's shoulder, "—was this. We were wardens. The guild's founders swore to hold the fragments of the pact between the human realms and the elderkin realms after it was broken, so that no one power could gather them and claim the magic of both worlds for itself. Neutral ground. That was the whole design. Every trade, every ledger, every ridiculous bottle of somebody's last summer—all of it existed so the guild would have a reason to exist, and hands in every city, and no master but itself."

Isandro looked at the shelves. At the bell ringing without wind. At the crown of black iron with its stumps plugged with grey stone. "The crown," they said slowly. "Those plugs are covenant-stone."

"Now you're reading the collection properly." Something bitter crossed Odile's face. "Two generations ago, the king's grandfather decided neutrality was an inconvenience. He sent men—not soldiers, *clerks*, men with warrants and wax seals—to collect. And the Exchange survived the only way it knew how." She turned to face Isandro fully, and her eyes in the lamplight were the coldest thing in the cold room. "It forgot. Deliberately. The founders' heirs underwent a rite—elderkin work, paid for dearly—that burned the true purpose out of every record and every mind. The guild remembers that it trades. It does not remember what the trading protects. Each mistress learns the truth the night she takes office, and carries it alone, and prays never to need it."

"And you needed it." Isandro's mouth had gone dry. "When the Veilhunt began."

"The hunt isn't a hunt, girl. It's a harvest." Odile's voice dropped. "Someone above us broke the last seal on the old pact-work, and the veil between realms is thinning in ways that frighten things on both sides of it. The crown wants every gift-bearer registered before winter because registered gifts can be *taken*. And the Memory-Warden who runs the hunt—he's not collecting strays. He's collecting the pieces he needs to finish what his grandfather started."

The words landed in Isandro like stones dropped down a well. *The Memory-Warden.* The hooded buyer's description matched every rumor: faceless, soft-spoken, paying in names. "So you sold me to buy time."

"I sold your name—which is false, which I helped you build, which leads nowhere—to keep him reading our ledgers instead of burning them. There is a difference between surrendering a name and surrendering a person. I have spent thirty years learning that difference." She said it without flinching, and Isandro hated how much of it sounded like wisdom, and hated more that wisdom and betrayal could wear the same coat.

"You erased yourselves," Isandro said. "The whole guild. On purpose."

"To survive."

"That's not survival. That's just a slower way of being taken." Isandro's hand was still against the glass, and the storm-light in the fragment had begun to turn faster, brighter, answering something in their blood that they had spent ten years strangling. The hum climbed. Somewhere down the dark aisles, the bell in its lead cradle rang louder, clear as an alarm. "You kept the pact's pieces and forgot why. You kept me and forgot what I am. This whole guild is a memory-tithe you collected from yourselves."

For a long moment Odile said nothing. The light in the stone turned. Turned. And Isandro saw—saw plainly, in the woman's tired face—that she had already run this argument against herself a thousand nights, alone, over plum wine, staring at a wall.

"Yes," Odile said quietly. "And now the tithe has come due, and neither of us knows the terms." She lifted the lamp. Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of rock, came a sound Isandro knew from the worst dreams of their exile: boots. Many boots. On the counting-room floor.

"They're here early," Odile said, and for the first time since Isandro had known her, the mistress of the Veiled Exchange looked afraid.
