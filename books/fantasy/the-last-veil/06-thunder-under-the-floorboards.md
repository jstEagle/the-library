## 6. Thunder Under the Floorboards

The rain began at dusk, and Isandro counted it like a debt.

Twelve drops against the ledger-room window before the first distant roll of thunder answered. They had always been in conversation, the sky and they—though Isandro had spent ten years pretending to be deaf to it. Now, with Sefa's cot lying empty three floors below and her ink pot still uncapped on the shared desk, every drop felt like a finger tapping on glass.

*You did nothing,* said the silence between raindrops.

*There was nothing you could have done,* said the older voice, the careful one, the one that had kept them alive.

Both voices were liars, probably. Isandro dipped a pen and copied out a column of figures for a trade that had never happened: six hours of borrowed courage, sold to a merchant's son too frightened to ask his father's blessing. The Exchange traded in everything except honesty.

The door below slammed.

Not the usual slam of clerks coming in from the wet—this was iron hitting oak, and it was followed by another, and another, a rhythm marching up through the building's bones. Isandro set down the pen with a steadiness they did not feel and slid the covenant-ledger from its hiding place beneath the false bottom of their desk drawer, where it had lived for two days wrapped in oilcloth like something dead. Maybe it was. Maybe carrying it was already killing them.

Boots on the stairs now. Many boots.

*Hunters.*

Isandro's body understood before their mind did. It had been trained for this the way a hare is trained for hawks—not by teachers but by ten years of flinching. They snuffed the lamp, pressed the ledger inside their coat against their ribs, and moved to the back wall where the tall window overlooked the cistern alley. Their hands were shaking. That was fine. Hands could shake as long as the rest stayed quiet.

Three floors down, Odile Ferran's voice rose, silk stretched over steel: "Gentlemen. The Veiled Exchange is chartered neutral ground. Whatever names you imagine you'll find here—"

"We don't imagine names, Mistress Ferran." The second voice was dry as old paper, and Isandro knew it from the proclamation squares: Captain Aurel Vane of the Veilhunters. "We read them. We're very good readers."

A chair scraped. Something ceramic broke.

Isandro put their palm flat against the window frame and breathed the way they'd taught themselves to breathe: shallow, dull, a bellows with no fire in it. *I am a ledger-keeper. I count other people's miracles and own none.* The mantra had held for a decade. It had held through Sefa screaming—

It did not hold through the sound that came next: a crackle, faint and wrong, like frost spreading across a pond, and then Vane's voice again, closer, almost bored. "Upstairs. All floors."

The boots resumed, and this time they were climbing toward Isandro.

---

They went over the windowsill into the rain because staying was death, and discovered halfway down the drainpipe that their body had made a decision their mind hadn't approved: the storm was listening.

It happened in pieces, each one small enough to deny. The rain thickened around the alley until the hunters' lantern-light below turned to smears of gold in gray wool. A gust shouldered open a gap between rooftops so Isandro could see the drainage grate at the alley's end—the one that fed the flooded undercity, the one no hunter would bother with on a night like this. Coincidences. Weather. Nothing more.

Then a hunter stepped into the alley mouth with a crossbow, and looked up, and Isandro felt his gaze find them the way a hook finds a fish—and the sky lost its temper.

Lightning came down the length of the street without any thunder announcing it, close enough that the air turned white and tasted of copper. It struck nothing. It struck *beside*, splitting a chimney stack forty feet from where Isandro clung, and the roar of it was not the sky's roar at all—it was theirs, torn out of a chest they'd kept locked since they were nineteen years old. Every window in the Exchange's rear face blew inward or outward depending on which lie of pressure the moment preferred. Glass fell like sleet. Rain, caught mid-fall, bent sideways—a curtain of water snapping horizontal, hanging there for one impossible heartbeat like a banner—and then the world remembered its rules and let go.

Isandro dropped the last twelve feet, hit the cobbles rolling, and ran.

Behind them: shouting, the captain's voice cutting through it, level as a plumb line—"There. *There*, you idiots, stop gawking at the sky"—and the slap-slap of pursuing feet on wet stone. Ahead: the drainage grate, rust-eaten, half-drowned.

Isandro wrenched it free with fingers that sparked when they touched the iron. Little blue threads crawled up their knuckles. *Later,* they told the sparks. *Behave later.* And then they were through, into the dark, dragging the grate shut behind them as boots rounded the alley mouth and a bolt meant for their spine shattered instead against stone.

The undercity took them like water takes a coin.

---

For a long time there was only falling, sliding, catching themselves on walls slick with a decade of city filth, the covenant-ledger a hard heartbeat against their ribs. Old Vessia had been built on top of older Vessia; everyone knew that. What the ledgers had hinted—and what the flooded quarter proved—was that the oldest Vessia had been built on something else entirely. Isandro splashed down staircases that descended into black canals. They passed archways carved with spirals they recognized now, having seen their twin on the covenant-stone fragment locked in Odile's vault. Elderkin work. The Exchange hadn't just *kept* things all these centuries. It had kept *secrets about itself*, and Isandro had filed its paperwork without ever reading the first page.

Their boots found a raised walkway—an old processional path, maybe, running along the top of a drowned boulevard—and they stopped there, chest heaving, and let themselves shake.

Because it wasn't fear anymore. That was the terrible part. Somewhere between the lightning strike and the sideways rain, the fear had burned off like morning fog, and what remained underneath was *hunger*. Ten years of rationing, and the gift had come back in one night like a starved animal finally thrown a side of meat—and it wasn't finished. Isandro could feel it ranging through them, testing the fences, humming in the marrow the way it used to hum when they were a child on the Saltreach coast calling little thunderstorms out of clear noon skies just to watch their mother laugh.

*Mother.* The word arrived uninvited and left a bruise.

"Hello," Isandro said aloud, to nobody, to the dark water, to the thing under their skin. Their voice echoed down the drowned boulevard and came back changed, freighted with a low rolling undertone that no human throat should have produced. The canal surface shivered with rings. Far off, thunder answered—once, softly, like an old friend knocking on a door they'd bricked over.

"Not yet," Isandro whispered. "We're still running."

But they were smiling. They could feel it, feral and unfamiliar, and they couldn't stop it.

---

They walked for hours by the light of their own fingertips. That was new, too—or rather, very old. As a child they'd glowed faintly during storms, and their mother had called it *the sky kissing you back*. As an adult they'd learned to smother even that. Now the smothering was gone, burned away with everything else, and pale violet light leaked between their knuckles and lit the elderkin spirals on the passing walls—which, impossibly, lit in answer. Spiral after spiral woke as Isandro passed, a soft blue-green chase of illumination running ahead of them down the boulevard like lamps being lit by an unseen servant.

*The city knows me.* Or the city knew the gift, which amounted to the same thing, and both thoughts were terrifying, and both were wonderful, and Isandro was so tired of being only terrified.

They stopped when the walkway ended at a wide chamber where five canals met, a drowned crossing crowned by the skeletal ribs of some collapsed dome. And stopped again, harder, because someone had strung lanterns among the bones.

Small ones. Handmade. Oil in teacups, a stub of tallow on a raft of cork. Someone lived here.

"You're bleeding light all over my ceiling," said a voice above, "and every rat in Vessia can see you coming."

Isandro looked up. Perched on a beam near the broken dome, half in shadow, sat a figure with a smuggler's layered coat and a face that might once have been handsome before someone had rearranged parts of it—nose flattened, scar through one brow, a grin that didn't reach the eyes because the eyes were busy doing arithmetic. Behind the figure, half-hidden in the ribbing of the dome, small shapes shifted. Children? No—one child, and the shapes were crates.

"The hunters are sweeping the upper city," the smuggler went on, conversational, while one hand drifted—casually, badly—toward a crossbow racked within reach. "Which means either you're very lucky, very stupid, or very expensive. You've got the look of expensive. So before I decide whether to rob you or sell you, why don't you tell me how a ledger-clerk"—a nod at the ink stains still on Isandro's cuffs, visible even by guttering lantern-light—"comes down my canal glowing like a festival lamp?"

"I stole something," Isandro said. Their voice was wrecked from smoke and running, but it held. "Something the crown will burn cities to get back. And I need to leave this city tonight."

The grin sharpened. "Now *that's* the opening line of a profitable evening." The smuggler swung down from the beam, landing light as a cat despite the bulk of the coat, and offered a hand calloused from rope-work. "Rook. This is my canal. Those are my crates. The child behind them is Tam, who is currently deciding whether to run, and I'd take it kindly if you didn't spark at us while she decides, because she startles."

From behind the crates, a thin voice: "She doesn't startle." A girl of perhaps nine edged into the lantern light—sharp-faced, sharp-eyed, hair cropped short with what looked like a hunting knife's help. She stared at Isandro with the frank appraisal of someone appraising weather. "You're the one the sky broke a window for."

"You heard that?"

"Heard it," Tam said. "Heard it coming, too. Half a bell before it happened, all the doors in the upper city rattled like they were scared." She tilted her head. "Doors talk to me. They were talking about you all evening." A pause, and then, with the merciless precision of children: "Your name is loud. Even though you tried to make it quiet."

Rook's arithmetic-eyes flicked from the child to Isandro and back. Whatever sum he'd been running, he seemed to arrive at a total. "Well," he said. "That settles the robbery question. Nobody robs a storm." He jerked his chin toward a side tunnel where, incredibly, the bow of a small flat-bottomed skiff nosed out of the dark. "Get in the boat, ledger-keeper, and show me what you stole that's worth burning cities. If it's worth what you think it's worth, I know some people north of the walls who've been waiting ten years for exactly this kind of bad news."

Isandro climbed into the skiff, the covenant-ledger pressing warm against their ribs—warmer than oilcloth and paper had any right to be, as if it, too, had been waiting. Outside, far above, thunder rolled across the city they'd lived in invisibly for a decade, and for the first time Isandro didn't flinch from the sound.

Tam settled at the stern beside Rook, hugged her knees, and watched Isandro with those weather-appraising eyes.

"Doors say the hunters aren't giving up," she announced. "Doors say there's a man with dry-paper eyes who reads names, and he's already reading yours."

* * *

The skiff slipped north through the drowned dark while the city above hunted, and Isandro sat in the bilge water with the stolen covenant-ledger open across their knees, reading by their own light, page after page of a truth the crown had killed to bury—until, near dawn, the tunnel mouth opened onto grey river fog and the far-off silhouette of the northern hills, and Rook cut the oars and said quietly:

"Well, then. I hope you're ready to meet the people this book belongs to."
