## 7. The Drowned Market

The undercity had been drowning for a hundred years, and it had made its peace with it.

Isandro waded through water the color of old pewter, the covenant-ledger wrapped in oilcloth and strapped against their chest like a second heart. Above them—two levels up, past rotted staircases and collapsed arcades—the hunters were still sweeping the Exchange district. They could hear it even down here: boots on stone, muffled by floodwater into something rhythmic and patient, like a pulse searching for a vein.

*Keep moving. Keep quiet. Keep breathing.*

The old disciplines held, mostly. But every few steps, the storm under Isandro's skin shifted in its sleep, and they had to press a palm flat against their own ribs as if holding shut a door.

The flooded quarter had been the fish market, once. Isandro knew the city's history from ledgers, not memory—the Exchange kept records of everything that could be priced, including neighborhoods. Half of Vessa's lower ring had sunk after the Veilbreak, when the crown dammed the rivers to feed the new works of magic and let the old quarters drink themselves to death. The merchants had left. The poor had stayed, building walkways between rooftops, stringing lanterns across streets that were now canals. Boats where carts used to be. Children who swam before they walked.

Isandro had never come here in ten years of hiding. There was nothing to hide *from* down here except everything: too many eyes, too little order, no ledgers at all.

Tonight there was nowhere else.

* * *

They found the drowned market by its smell—brine and woodsmoke and something sweet underneath, fried dough or candied root, stubborn comfort food cooked over driftwood fires. Lanterns hung from awnings that had once shaded stalls selling eels and oysters; now they lit a floating bazaar of salvage and rumor. A woman sold rain-catchers shaped like silver flowers. A man with river-pale hands traded in maps, his stall a raft of tarred planks. Two children raced coracles through the lanes, shrieking, and nobody told them to be quiet.

Noise was safety here. Noise was camouflage.

Isandro pulled the hood lower and bought a bowl of stew from a grandmother who looked them over exactly once and decided, correctly, that they were running from something. She charged double and added extra bread.

"You're dripping," she said.

"It's raining."

"Upstairs." She nodded at the ceiling of fog above them. "Down here we call it Tuesday."

Isandro almost smiled. It hurt, like using a stiff joint. They took the bowl to the edge of the market, found a half-sunken doorway with a dry step, and sat where they could watch both the crowd and the dark water behind them.

The ledger pressed warm against their chest. Or they imagined it did. Since the escape—since the window had shattered outward at a touch and the rain had bent sideways to cover their running—they'd felt it constantly, that low hum beneath the breastbone, the gift pacing its cage like something that had finally remembered it was not, in fact, a pet.

*You could have taken Sefa with you.* The thought arrived on schedule. It arrived every hour now. *You knew her cot was three doors from yours. You heard the hunters coming.*

*I didn't know. I couldn't have—*

*You could have tried.*

Isandro ate the stew without tasting it.

That was when the child found them.

* * *

She couldn't have been more than ten, barefoot, hair cropped short and unevenly, wearing a man's coat cut down with a fish knife. She appeared out of the crowd the way cats appear—not approaching so much as simply being nearby, then nearer, then suddenly sitting on the step beside Isandro's doorway as if she'd been invited years ago.

"You came down the Ossuary stairs," she said.

Isandro went still. "I don't know what you mean."

"You came down the Ossuary stairs," she repeated, with the patience of someone correcting a slow student, "at the wrong time. The third step groans. Everyone knows the third step groans. You stepped on it anyway because you were listening to the water instead of the stairs." She picked something out of the air near her ear—an invisible thread—and frowned at it. "And your coat smells like ink and lightning."

Every muscle in Isandro's body locked. Ten years of discipline said *stand up, walk away, vanish*. Ten years of discipline also said *do not run from things that are watching you*, and this small creature was most definitely watching, though her eyes—gray, unblinking, unsettlingly adult—were aimed slightly past Isandro rather than at them. As if reading a page written in the air.

"What do you hear?" Isandro asked quietly. It wasn't the question they meant to ask. It was the question that mattered.

The girl shrugged one shoulder. "Doors. Mostly doors. Doors talk before they open—you just have to be listening. That door—" she pointed at a shuttered boathouse across the lane "—wants to open tomorrow. It's excited about it. Your door—" now she pointed directly at Isandro's chest, at the space where the ledger lay hidden, and Isandro's blood went cold "—has been talking since you sat down. It doesn't stop talking. It's very rude."

"The ledger doesn't—"

"Not the book." The girl tilted her head. "Well. Also the book. But mostly *you*."

For a long moment the market noise washed over them both—the haggling, the fire crackle, the lap of black water against stone. Somewhere two levels up, faint as a dream, boots still pulsed their patient rhythm.

"My name's Tam," the girl said. "Rook says I'm not supposed to talk to strangers, but Rook says lots of things. Rook says you're going to be trouble. Rook says that like it's a bad thing, but Rook collects trouble, so I don't think it counts."

"Where is Rook?"

Tam pointed with her chin at the far end of the market, where the largest structure stood—a grounded river-barge, half-beached on the roof of a submerged granary, converted into something between a warehouse and a fortress. Lanterns burned at its bow like eyes. Figures moved along its deck rail.

"He wants to see you," Tam said. Then, before Isandro could ask how she possibly knew that: "He's been looking at you for an hour. He thinks he's subtle. He isn't. He's a boat person. Boat people are never subtle."

* * *

Rook turned out to be a woman somewhere past fifty, built like the barges she lived on—wide, weathered, immovable—and she received Isandro on her deck with a crossbow resting across her knees, which she made no effort to pretend was decorative.

"So," she said. "Ink-and-lightning walks into my market smelling of the Exchange and fresh crime. Sit down before you fall down. You've been bleeding from somewhere—I can tell by how you're standing."

"I'm not bleeding."

"Inside-bleeding." She waved vaguely at Isandro's whole torso. "Sit."

Isandro sat. The deck was dry, which felt obscene after hours of wading. Around them, Rook's crew worked in easy silence—a dozen figures coiling rope, checking hulls, loading crates whose contents clinked in ways crates shouldn't. Smugglers. And given what else Isandro had seen in this market—given the way several of Rook's people bore the particular stillness of those keeping something caged behind their ribs—more than smugglers.

"Movers," Rook said, answering the question Isandro hadn't asked. "That's the polite word. We move things the crown has decided belong to it. Sometimes cargo. Sometimes people." Her eyes, pale as river glass, settled on Isandro and stayed. "People with gifts, mostly. We've moved forty-one out of Vessa since the proclamation. Forty-one registered names walking north instead of into a hunter's wagon. You'd have been forty-two if you'd come to me three days ago."

"And now?"

"Now you're the one every hunter in the city is drawing pictures of." She reached beside her chair, lifted a folded broadsheet off a crate, and tossed it onto the deck between them. Isandro looked down at their own face. A charcoal rendering, good enough to be dangerous—the sharp jaw, the ink-stained fingers, the eyes the artist had somehow gotten exactly right, wide with a terror Isandro hadn't known showed. Beneath it, in the crown's crisp print:

**VEILBREAKER. STORM-CALLER. LAST SEEN FLOODING THE EXCHANGE DISTRICT. REWARD: LAND AND TITLE.**

"They think you broke the Veil," Rook said. "Not the metaphorical one. The actual working. Something happened up there tonight that cracked the wards on six blocks, and every witness says it started with rain falling upward around a clerk from the Exchange."

"The wards were already failing. I saw—"

"I don't care what you saw." Rook said it without heat, the way one states the tide schedule. "I care what the Warden believes. And the King's Memory-Warden believes you're either the most dangerous fugitive in a generation or the bait in somebody's trap, and he's sent three companies down into the undercity to find out which." She leaned forward. "So. Ledger-keeper. You stole something from the Exchange vault tonight. I can smell the vault-seal wax from here. The question that decides whether you leave my deck walking or swimming is: what did you take, and why should I burn my whole operation getting it out of the city?"

Isandro's hand moved toward the oilcloth bundle and stopped. Every instinct honed over a decade said *reveal nothing, trust no one, stay small, stay hidden.* Those instincts had kept them alive. Those instincts had also kept them sitting on a cot three doors from Sefa's while she screamed.

"The Exchange keeps a ledger older than the guild," Isandro said. "It records a covenant. Between the human realms and the elderkin. The source of every gift—including mine. The crown didn't banish magic. The crown *broke a treaty*, and it's been hunting witnesses ever since." They unwrapped the oilcloth enough to show the corner of the first page: the twin sigils, sun and root, bound by a line of script in no living language. "This is proof."

Rook didn't reach for it. She studied Isandro instead, long and hard, the way she might study a hull for rot.

"Do you know what happens to people who carry proof in this country?" she asked. "They drown. Usually politely. Usually with a title and a pension, right up until the day the Warden remembers they exist." She spat over the rail. "My mother carried proof. A song, actually—one verse of the elderkin tongue her grandmother taught her. Just a verse. They took her for it when I was nine. I watched the wagon go. So don't sit on my deck and offer me paper like paper changes anything."

"Then why move the gift-bearers at all? If nothing changes anything?"

Rook's jaw tightened. For a moment she looked older than fifty—she looked ancient, river-old, carved out of all the floods this city had survived.

"Because the ones I move get to keep *choosing*," she said. "That's all anybody gets. That's the whole prize. Choice." She looked away, toward the dark mouth of the canal where the water ran out of the undercity toward the sea. "There's a route north. Old salt roads, then the high passes. Past the tollhouse. To the Standing Stones, if the stories are true and the Stones still stand under whatever ice the crown's winter has put on them. I've got eleven souls leaving tomorrow night. Fishers. A hedge-healer. Two kids who glow in the dark when they forget to stop." Her eyes came back to Isandro, flat and measuring. "I wasn't going to wait for a twelfth. Especially not one with the entire hunt on their neck."

"But?"

"But you're the only person in this city who's actually *read* whatever's in that book." Rook tapped the oilcloth bundle with the toe of her boot. "Forty-one people, and I'm sending them north on stories and hope. You're offering me something better than hope, and I hate it, because hope floats and proof sinks." She stood, joints cracking like rigging in wind. "Here's my price. You come with us, you earn your place. You row when I say row, you fight when I say fight, you keep that book closed unless I say otherwise, and if the hunters close on us, you will *not* use whatever's humming inside you unless I give the word—because a storm-caller lighting up the night sky is a beacon, and I don't ferry beacons."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you finish your stew, and you're a stranger who was never here, and I'll be sorry about it later, quietly, the way I'm sorry about everyone I couldn't move." She extended a hand, calloused as dock rope. "Last chance to swim."

Isandro looked at the hand. Looked past it, to where Tam had appeared at the deck rail—nobody had seen her climb aboard; nobody ever would—watching them both with those gray, page-reading eyes.

"She hears doors," Isandro said. "Before they open."

"Best ears in Vessa," Rook said. "Waste of a gift on a child who won't stop talking, but the gods have a sense of humor."

"No. It's not a waste." Isandro took the offered hand. Rook's grip was iron wrapped in leather. "Ten years ago, someone with ears like hers would have saved my friend's life."

Something flickered across Rook's face—not softness exactly, but recognition, the look of one ledger-keeper acknowledging another's entry. She hauled Isandro to their feet.

"Welcome to the crew, Ink-and-Lightning. Sleep in the hold. We sail on tomorrow's dark tide."

* * *

The hold smelled of tar, salt pork, and lamp oil. Isandro lay in a hammock strung between crates, the covenant-ledger open on their chest, a stub of candle burning on a barrel head. Around them the barge creaked and settled, and beyond the hull the undercity murmured its hundred-year drowning.

They should sleep. Tomorrow they'd need everything—strength, nerve, the buried storm held tight on Rook's strict terms. Instead Isandro read, tracing the dead script with one fingertip, matching fragments against marginalia in their own hand from another life: *the Standing Stones, anchor-point; the Warden's line, oath-breaker's heir; the tollhouse takes what cannot be refused.*

*Takes what cannot be refused.*

A memory rose unbidden—Sefa's laugh, the way she'd hummed while copying columns, the way she'd said *you're the only one here who talks to me like a person, Vasquez*—and Isandro had to set the candle aside and breathe through it, slow, controlled, counting the creaks of the hull.

A soft thump. Tam dropped from the hatch ladder without using the rungs, landed soundless, and stood at the foot of the hammock, studying the upside-down pages.

"That word," she said, pointing at the script. "The spiky one."

"You can read elderkin?"

"No. But it's *loud*." Tam frowned at the page, and for the first time since the Ossuary stairs, she looked less like an unnervingly old child and more like exactly what she was: ten years old, barefoot, afraid of something she couldn't name. "All the words in that book are loud. But that one's loud like a door that's been locked a long, long time." She glanced up, gray eyes catching the candlelight. "And it's started knocking."

Outside, far above and far away, thunder rolled across Vessa—and Isandro, who had spent ten years teaching themselves not to listen, listened.

It answered.

The ledger lay open on their chest, and by candlelight, in a hold full of sleeping strangers, Isandro Vasquez began the work of learning what they had stolen—and what it intended to cost.
