## 8. Ledgers of the Broken Pact

The candle had burned to a stub by the time Isandro found the cipher's spine.

They had been at the stolen ledger for three days—three days in Rook's smuggler's den beneath the Drowned Market, where the ceiling wept brackish water into copper basins and the air tasted of salt, lamp-oil, and secrets too long kept. The book itself was a monster: a folio bound in grey hide that was not, Isandro had slowly come to accept, any hide they could name. Its pages were vellum once white as frost, now stained amber at every edge, as though the whole thing remembered being near fire. The script inside shifted. That was the first cruelty of it. Read it in lamplight and the letters lay flat and dead; read it by storm-light, or by the pale glow of the covenant-stone fragment wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of Isandro's pack, and the ink woke.

So Isandro read by stone-light, night after night, while the drowned city dripped around them.

The key came on the third night, and it was insultingly simple. The Exchange taught its clerks that the oldest ledgers were organized not by date but by debt—who owed whom, and what had been pledged against it. Isandro had spent ten years recording trades in bottled echoes and borrowed shadows, and somewhere in that decade of careful, invisible work, the grammar of obligation had sunk so deep into them that they no longer saw it as a language. They simply thought in it.

Which meant that when they stopped reading the ledger as a history and started reading it as an account, everything fell open like a door left unlatched.

*Pledged by the Crown of Vhalen to the Elderkin Courts of the Far Shore:* the entry began, in a hand so old the letters still carried the curve of a quill cut from a feather Isandro didn't recognize. *One voice from each realm, joined in witness. One keeper of the standing stones. One tithe of memory, freely given, renewed each turning of the year. In exchange: passage between realms, the mending of what is torn, and the sharing of the deep magic among all peoples who keep the terms.*

*Status:* —and here Isandro's breath caught— *BROKEN.*

Not lapsed. Not lost. Broken, with a clerk's cold precision, dated sixty-one years before Isandro's birth, and initialed with a sigil Isandro knew from a thousand proclamations nailed to a thousand walls: the crowned tower of House Aldemar. The royal seal. The mark that hung over every courthouse, every registry office, every Veilhunter garrison in the kingdom.

Isandro set the ledger down very carefully, the way one sets down something that might go off.

* * *

Rook found her at dawn, or what passed for dawn down here—a grey seepage through the grates overhead, enough to make the water-stains on the ceiling visible. She'd been up for two days straight herself, running messages through the flooded quarter, moving three families out along the sunken aqueduct before the hunters' next sweep, and she came down the ladder expecting to find the ledger-keeper asleep over their work.

Instead Isandro sat exactly where she'd left them, in the same posture, hands flat on the table on either side of the open book, eyes red-rimmed and fixed on nothing.

"You look," Rook said, "like someone who's just read their own death warrant."

"I've read everyone's." Isandro's voice came out scraped raw. "Rook. Sit down."

She sat. She was a compact woman built out of rope scars and bad decisions, with hair cropped short enough that Tam once said you could use her head as a whetstone, and she had survived eleven years of smuggling gift-bearers out of Vhalen by never letting anyone tell her something twice. "Talk."

"The covenant between the realms. The one everyone says was a myth, or a war, or a wound that healed wrong." Isandro turned the ledger toward her, one finger resting under the entry. "It was real. It was working. For four hundred years it worked—the elderkin shared their magic across the veil, humans gave memory-tithes to keep it fed, and there were keepers at the Standing Stones who held the whole thing together like a keel holds a ship upright."

"Everyone knows that story. It's a story for children."

"It's a story the crown let children keep because the truth needed hiding." Isandro's finger moved down the page. "Sixty-one years ago, King Aldric the Second broke it. Deliberately. Look—the entries after this aren't pledges anymore. They're seizures. He sent soldiers to the Stones. Killed the keepers. Took the covenant-stone apart and hid the fragments in vaults—and Odile Ferran has one of them under the Exchange, which means the guild has known, or half-known, since before I was born." Their voice cracked and they pushed through it. "He didn't destroy the magic, Rook. You can't destroy it. He *dammed* it. Diverted it. Every gift born in this kingdom since—the storm-callers, the door-hearers, the shadow-borrowers—we're leaks in his dam. We're what slips through the cracks he made when he decided magic belonged to the throne and no one else."

Rook looked at the page for a long moment. Then at Isandro. "And the Veilhunt?"

"Housekeeping." Isandro laughed, and the sound was terrible—there was nothing funny anywhere near it. "The dam's failing. Too many leaks. So the crown registers us, catalogs us, and takes us—takes our gifts, or takes our lives, whichever's cheaper—and feeds us into the machinery Aldric built. We're not criminals. We're *inventory*."

* * *

They read the rest of it together, all that day, taking turns because neither of them trusted their voice alone.

The ledger's middle sections were the worst. Isandro had catalogued impossible trades for ten years and thought themselves unshockable—one last summer sold to a dying woman; a grandmother's laughter decanted into glass; a soldier's courage rented back to him at interest—but the crown's accounting made the Exchange look like a charity stall. There were pages of tithe-records: villages assessed annually for memory, weighed like grain, the eldest recollections harvested first because they yielded the most. Whole towns that had forgotten their own founding, their own names, why their roads curved the way they did—curved, Isandro realized with a sick lurch, around ley-lines the crown didn't want anyone walking. There were requisition orders for gift-born children, ages listed alongside weights and values, transported north to a place the ledger only ever called *the Reservoir*, and Isandro had to stop reading then, had to stand at the edge of the flooded street with cold water lapping their boots and breathe until the shaking stopped, while Rook stood beside them saying nothing, jaw tight as a knotted line.

And there—buried in the final section, written in a newer hand, a hand Isandro recognized with a jolt as belonging to someone trained exactly as they had been trained, a fellow clerk of the Exchange, decades dead or disappeared—there was the arithmetic of the long winter.

*The covenant was the veil's loom,* the dead clerk had written. *Break the weave and the fabric does not fail at once; it fails at the edges first. The winters will lengthen. The veil will thin and then harden, sealing both realms apart forever, and all that was shared will be hoarded until the hoard runs dry. The crown believes it can hold back the dark with what it has stolen. The crown is wrong. I have seen the projections. I have run the numbers.*

*I have run the numbers, and we are already bankrupt.*

* * *

Tam found them that evening.

The child came down the ladder the way Tam did everything—sideways, fast, silent as spilled mercury—and stopped dead at the bottom, reading the room the way Tam read rooms, which was to say the way other people read weather. Something had changed in the den. The two adults sat surrounded by loose pages like survivors of a shipwreck, and the air had a pressure to it that even a door-hearer couldn't miss.

"You found something," Tam said.

"We found everything," Rook said. Her voice was level, but Tam heard doors before they opened, and Rook heard the particular creak of a woman deciding something enormous. "Sit down, sprout. You're going to want to hear this, and then you're going to want to hit something, and I need you to do your hitting quietly."

So Rook told it—not the way Isandro had told it, in the grammar of ledgers and obligations, but in the grammar of the streets. She told it like a con: the biggest mark in the world was the crown itself, and the crown had been running the longest con in history, selling the whole kingdom a lie about where its magic came from and who it belonged to. She told it about the Reservoir, and watched Tam's small face go carefully, terribly blank. She told it about the long winter—that it wasn't weather, it was a bill coming due, and the people who ran up the debt had decided everyone else would pay it.

When she finished, Tam sat very still for a moment. Then, in a voice pitched low and hard, far older than nine years old:

"So the hunters aren't the disease. They're the symptom."

Isandro looked at the child—really looked—and felt something shift in their chest that had nothing to do with storms. "That's right."

"And the cure's at the Standing Stones."

"That's right."

"Then what are we doing sitting in a hole full of water?" Tam stood up. "Every day we're here, another family gets registered. Another kid goes north. I hear the doors, Isandro. I've been hearing them all week—doors opening all over the city, and hunters on the other side of every one. We're not safe down here. Nobody's safe anywhere. So either that book tells us how to fix it, or it's just kindling."

* * *

Later, when Tam had gone up to sleep wedged in the rafters as always, Rook poured two fingers of plum brandy into tin cups and pushed one across the table at Isandro.

"I've moved thirty-one people out of this city in eleven years," she said. "Thirty-one. And I told myself it mattered, and it did matter, and it was a thimble bailing a sinking ship, and I knew that too, and I did it anyway because a thimble is better than nothing." She drank. "You're telling me there's a shipwright's plan sitting on this table."

"I'm telling you the covenant can be reforged. At the Stones, before the winter seals the veil. It needs voices from both realms and a keeper willing to anchor it." Isandro didn't say *with their memories*. Some part of them was already doing that arithmetic, the clerk's arithmetic, tallying costs in a column only they could see. "It needs time we may not have, a road north through the start of winter, and every gift-bearer we can gather. And it needs us to survive long enough to try, knowing the crown will throw everything it has at stopping us."

"Because if it works, the throne loses everything."

"If it works, the throne becomes just another house. No dammed magic. No tithe. No hunt. Every stolen thing flows back to where it came from." Isandro wrapped their hands around the cup, not drinking. "I spent ten years making myself invisible, Rook. Erasing myself line by line, because staying hidden was the price of staying alive. And the whole time, I was keeping books for a guild that helped build this. And now I'm holding the proof that my exile wasn't cowardice—it was *recruitment*. The crown wanted people like me frightened and scattered and ashamed, because frightened, scattered, ashamed people don't compare notes. Don't realize there are thousands of us. Don't realize that what they call our curse is just the magic they stole, finding its way home through us."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above, through the grates, the drowned city went on dripping, indifferent, and beyond it the real city lay under early snow with criers in every square reading names.

"So what happens now?" Rook asked. "Legally speaking. If we take this to a magistrate—"

"A magistrate appointed by the crown that wrote this ledger? We'd be executed twice. Once for treason and once for irony."

Rook grinned then, sharp as a hook. "Good. Just checking we agree on jurisdiction." She leaned forward, forearms on the table, and Isandro saw in her face the thing she usually kept banked behind the smuggler's pragmatism: a fury with a decade of thimbles behind it. "Here's what I know. The law belongs to the men who broke the pact. Which means whatever we do next, we do outside it. Justice starts where the writ ends—I learned that running cargo past customs boats, and I guess tonight you learned it reading." She tapped the ledger's grey cover. "So. You're the clerk. Give me a manifest. Who do we need, what do we need, how fast do we move?"

Isandro looked down at the ledger—to the last legible entry in the dead clerk's hand, the one they hadn't yet said aloud, the one that had been sitting in their chest all day like swallowed ice:

*If any soul reads this: the reforging requires a keeper. The last keepers died at the Stones, and the crown has made certain no one remembers the rite entire. But the rite survives in fragments—in the guilds, in the elderkin tollhouses, in the memories of anyone old enough to have sung the songs without knowing what they sang. Gather the fragments. Gather the gifted. Go north. And know that the crown's hunters have the same map you do, and fewer scruples, and a head start measured in generations.*

"There are fragments of the rite scattered across the north," Isandro said slowly. "Tollhouses. Old songs. Maybe people. The ledger names three places before the writing fails—an elderkin tollhouse at the border marshes, a town called Greyhollow where someone kept singing, and—" they paused, checked the page again, though they'd already memorized it, "—a name. Aurel Vane. A hunter the clerk trusted, sixty years ago. His descendants hold the hunters' route-maps to the Stones."

Rook raised an eyebrow. "You want to ask a Veilhunter for directions."

"I want," Isandro said, and heard their own voice steady and strange, like the air before lightning, "to stop asking permission from anyone."

Outside, far above the grates, thunder rolled in off the sea—early, unnatural, the sky itself remembering something. Isandro closed their eyes and felt it answer in their blood like a name called across water.

Three days later, they would learn that Captain Aurel Vane had been given their description, and that his company had entered the Drowned Market at dusk.

But that night, there was only the ledger, the brandy, the beginning of a list.
