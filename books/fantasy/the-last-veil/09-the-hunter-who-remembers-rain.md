## 9. The Hunter Who Remembers Rain

The rain had followed them for three days, and Captain Aurel Vane had stopped believing it was coincidence.

He rode at the head of six hunters through the pine country north of the drowned market, where the road dissolved into cart-tracks and the trees grew close enough to whisper. Behind him, his hunters rode in silence, as they always did. The Veilhunt's Third Company did not sing on the march. They did not joke. They carried writs sealed in black wax and crossbows wrapped in oilcloth against the wet, and they told themselves—every one of them had to tell themselves something—that what they hunted were not people but breaches. Leaks in the veil between realms. Dangerous weather wearing human faces.

Aurel had been telling himself that for eleven years.

It had mostly worked.

But the rain bothered him. Not because it fell—rain fell in the pine marches half the year—but because of how it moved. It came down in long silver slants that bent around their column like water parting for a stone, soaking the ditches on either side of the road while leaving the hunters dry. He'd noticed it an hour past dawn. None of the others had. None of the others ever noticed anything until it was written in a report and stamped with the crown's seal.

*Storm-caller,* he thought, and the word sat in his chest like a swallowed coal. *A real one.*

The ledger-thief they tracked had left signs a blind man could read: hoofprints in the soft verge, a burned-out waystation where someone had slept and paid the keeper too generously, and at every village, the same story delivered by trembling mouths—a tall stranger with ink-stained fingers who asked strange questions about old pacts and older stones, who flinched when thunder rolled though the sky stayed clear.

A storm-caller who flinched from thunder. That detail had lodged in Aurel like a splinter. You did not flinch from your own voice unless you had spent years strangling it.

He knew what years of strangling looked like. He wore his own around his neck, though no one could see it: a thin white scar beneath his jaw where he'd cut himself shaving the morning after his mother made him forget.

* * *

They caught the scent of woodsmoke near dusk, rising off a hollow where the cart-track bent toward a derelict mill. The mill had died with the river that used to feed it; now only its wheel frame stood, black ribs against the gray sky, and beside it a shepherd's hut with light leaking through shutter-cracks like something trying not to be seen.

Aurel raised a fist. The column halted.

"Six inside or out?" asked Sergeant Hallow, a broad woman whose face had gone smooth as river stone from years of this work. She said *inside or out* the way other soldiers said *dead or alive*, and she meant the same thing.

"One fire," Aurel said. "Small. Maybe four."

"The ledger-thief?"

"Possibly." He dismounted, boots sinking into wet needles. "We take them quiet. No writs read aloud until we have them bound. If any of them carry a gift, we do not give it room to wake."

Hallow studied him a moment longer than protocol required. She was good at her work in the worst way—good enough to notice things. "You've been strange since the flooded quarter, Captain. Since you let the undercity rat run rather than flood it proper."

"I didn't let anyone run. I lost them."

"You stood in knee-deep water watching a door close," Hallow said, "and you didn't order it broken down. I counted the seconds. There were nine of them."

Aurel buckled his sword-belt tighter and did not answer. Nine seconds. He remembered all nine. He remembered standing in that flooded street with his heart hammering like a fist on a locked door, remembering—absurdly, unbearably—a summer afternoon twenty years gone, a boy on a rooftop, rain bending sideways around them both, laughing.

He had filed that memory away where he filed everything the crown had taught him to bury. But it kept digging itself back up.

"Form on me," he said. "Quiet approach. We go in on my mark."

* * *

The hut's single door hung crooked on leather hinges, and Tam—the child, though none of them had known it yet—heard them coming before Aurel reached the wall.

Later, piecing together the ambush that wasn't quite an ambush, Aurel would understand that the smuggler had posted the child at the window precisely because the child heard doors before they opened. But in the moment there was only this: he laid two fingers on the door to ease it inward without sound, and from inside, a voice—high, unafraid, almost bored—said:

"It's already open. You're slow."

Then the hut erupted into motion. A table went over. The smuggler—Rook, the reports called her, a name like a knife thrown into a sentence—came through the shattered window in a spray of glass and shadow, moving wrong, moving *dark*, her outline smearing like wet ink. The big runner swung a wagon-iron at Hallow's head. And through the back doorway, silhouetted against the last gray light, went the ledger-thief: tall, narrow, ink-stained hands catching on the doorframe, turning once—

Once was enough.

Their eyes met across the ruined room. Aurel saw a face he had seen described in a dozen reports and never once imagined would look like *that*—not monstrous, not leaking power, just tired. Just a person running out of places to be invisible. And behind those eyes, behind the fear, something else flickered. Recognition, maybe. Or the hope of it. The look of someone searching a stranger's face for a reason to stop running.

The storm-light answered before Aurel could.

It rose off the thief's skin like heat-shimmer, blue-white threads crawling up their forearms, and the air pressure dropped so fast his ears popped. Outside, the rain—which had bent around their column all day, courteous as a servant—stopped falling entirely and simply *hung*, ten thousand drops suspended in the dark like stars nailed to nothing.

Every hunter froze. Even Rook paused halfway over the fence, her shadow-smear rippling.

Aurel alone stepped forward into that held breath, hand raised—not a command, not a spell, just a hand, palm out, the way you'd gentle a horse.

"Don't," he said quietly. To the thief, to the storm, to both at once. "You don't have to show me. I'm not here to see it."

The suspended rain trembled.

*What are you doing,* screamed the voice of eleven years of discipline, of writs and wax seals and the Memory-Warden's soft, patient voice explaining that gifts were wounds and hunters were knives that closed them. *What are you DOING—*

And beneath that voice, older, buried under scar tissue and oath and forgetting-powder sweetened with honey so a seven-year-old would swallow it, another voice spoke. A woman's voice, rough with smoke and affection, saying *the rain likes you, little wren, see how it comes to your hand? See how it knows you?*

He had grown up in the fen-country, in a village the maps gave a name and then forgot to keep. Next door—or near enough; in the fens everything was next door if you walked—there had lived a storm-caller. Old Maera, the village said, though she hadn't seemed old to him, only weathered, with lightning-gray in her hair and a laugh like far-off thunder. She'd taught him to whistle through cupped hands. She'd shown him how rain bent around people it loved, and how he should never tell anyone, because the world had gotten hungry for people like her.

Then the king's men came with their ledgers and their scales and their little paper envelopes of forgetfulness, and Maera went into the dark of a caged wagon singing, and Aurel woke the next morning with honey on his tongue and a scar he couldn't explain and a grief so vast it had no name attached, no face, no reason—just a hole shaped like a person, hollowed into him by law.

For years he'd thought the hole was a wound. Then he'd become a hunter, and learned to call it duty, and the calling had worked the way a lid works on a well.

Until the flooded quarter. Until nine seconds of watching a door close. Until now, standing in hanging rain, looking at a storm-caller who flinched from thunder, and feeling the lid tear clean off the well.

*Maera.* Her name surfaced whole, intact, obscene in its survival—they had taken his memory of her and somehow left him the shape of loving her—and Aurel Vane understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly what kind of machine he had spent eleven years being. Not a knife closing wounds. A knife *making* them. Hollowing children so thoroughly that even their griefs forgot their names.

The rain began to fall again. Softly. Around him, not on him.

"Captain!" Hallow, crossbow up, bolt trained on the fleeing thief. "Your order!"

The thief stood in the doorway still, storm-light guttering along their arms, watching him with those tired eyes. Waiting to see what he was. Both of them waiting to see what he was.

Aurel looked at Hallow's drawn bow, at the six hunters who trusted him, at the child in the window with its uncanny listening face, at the smuggler frozen mid-flight, at the thief with the weight of a stolen covenant-ledger somewhere under their coat—and he heard himself say, in the flat command-voice of Third Company:

"Hold."

Then, louder, turning, letting the storm-light see his empty hands:

"Contact signal! Full company converging on our position—east ridge, two minutes! Scatter pattern! *Go!*"

His hunters moved on instinct, eleven years of drilled obedience overriding sense—breaking apart into the trees toward a signal that did not exist, toward reinforcements that were not coming, because their captain's voice carried authority the way storms carried rain, everywhere at once and impossible to argue with.

In the four seconds that bought, Aurel pointed west, into the deepest dark of the pines.

"There's a game trail. It crosses the ford below the falls—the water will kill your tracks and theirs." His voice cracked on the last word, and he let it. "Run. Don't stop before the border tollhouse. And—" He faltered, hating himself, needing it anyway. "The ledger. When you learn what's in it. Make it worth this."

The thief stared at him. Rain slid down their face like the sky was doing their weeping for them.

"Why?" they asked. Just that. One word, hoarse, and it undid him more than any accusation could have.

Because I remember a woman I was made to forget, he did not say. Because the hole in me has a shape and your gift fits it. Because someone has to start keeping a different kind of ledger.

"Because I counted nine seconds," is what he said instead, "and I can't take them back. Go."

They went—thief, smuggler, child, runner, swallowed by the pines—and Aurel Vane stood alone in a shepherd's yard with hanging rain beginning, at last, to fall on his shoulders like a judgment, and waited for his hunters to return and find out what he had done.

* * *

He confessed within the hour. That was the part no one expected, least of all himself.

He could have lied. The scatter-order was defensible as a tactical error; the trail-west was deniable as misjudgment; the four seconds could dissolve into confusion. He was a captain with a spotless record and friends in the Second Company. He might have survived it entire.

Instead he sat in the mill by their rekindled fire, wrapped in a blanket he didn't need, and told Hallow and the five others the truth in a level voice: that he had recognized the thief's gift and let it walk. That he had bought its escape with a false alarm. That he was a traitor to the crown and to Third Company, and they should bind him now and drag him south, and he would not resist.

The silence afterward lasted a long time. Rain ticked against the mill's dead wheel. Somewhere in the dark, the ford ran loud over stones, carrying away all tracks.

"He gave us the east ridge," muttered young Pell, the newest, staring into the fire as though it had betrayed him too. "I ran toward the east ridge. I *shouted*. I—" He stopped, jaw working.

"You obeyed your captain," Hallow said. Her river-stone face gave nothing away, but she hadn't reached for her irons either, and that omission rang louder than any accusation. "That's the crime here, isn't it. All of ours. We obeyed him right into treason, and we didn't question it, because questioning isn't what we do." She looked up, and her eyes found Aurel's across the fire, and for the first time in eleven years he saw the river-stone crack. "Why, Captain? Give me one true thing. Not for the record. For me."

Aurel turned his hands toward the flames. Beneath his jaw, the old scar ached the way it always ached before weather changed.

"When I was a boy," he said, "there was a storm-caller who lived near us. I don't remember her. Do you understand? I know there was a her—I know the shape of her, the way you know furniture in a dark room by bruising yourself on it. The crown took her out of my head when I was seven and left the space where she'd been, and I built my whole life around not falling into that space. Eleven years of hunting people like her, telling myself I was sealing breaches." He laughed, and it came out broken. "Tonight I watched a storm-caller flinch from thunder, and I finally understood what we do. We don't close wounds. We make them. We make them in children, and we teach the children to call the scars duty."

Pell had gone pale. Another hunter, Vess, had her face in her hands.

"They'll hang you," Hallow said at last. Not cruelly. Like a surveyor marking a boundary. "The Warden doesn't forgive. And he doesn't forget—which is the one mercy he denies everyone else."

"Yes."

"And us? Those of us who stood in this mill and heard a captain confess treason and did nothing?" Her mouth twisted. "There's a word for what we are now, too."

"I know," Aurel said. "I'm sorry. I should have lied to you. It would have been kinder."

"No," said Vess, from behind her hands, in a voice sanded raw. "No, it wouldn't. My sister registered last month, Captain. In good faith. Because the proclamation promised registration meant protection." She lowered her hands, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "Her village hasn't sent word in three weeks. So don't apologize for showing me what the seal on those writs actually covers. Apologize for taking eleven years to show me."

The fire popped. Downstream, the falls kept their endless counsel.

Aurel looked at each of them in turn—six hunters of Third Company, sitting in a dead mill with a living secret—and understood that something had refrorged itself in this room tonight, some small and desperate covenant, unwitnessed except by rain and flame. Whatever happened next—whatever the Warden's justice eventually cost them—they were no longer the same company that had ridden out of the city behind black-wax writs.

"We report a hard contact," Hallow said finally, in the crisp cadence of a report being drafted aloud. "Target party engaged, dispersed using a storm-gift of significant force. Pursuit continues north toward the Standing Stones." She met Aurel's eyes. "Which gives the crown a direction. And gives *them* a warning, if anyone they trust hears it first."

"The tollhouse," Aurel said quietly. "Wren's crossing. If they're wise, they'll pass through it—and Wren collects memories at the border. True ones. Freely given." His throat tightened. "Tell them, if you ever speak to them, that the price is real, and worth paying, and that they shouldn't flinch from it the way I did for eleven years."

"And what do we do," asked Pell, very young, very lost, "while the pursuit continues north?"

Aurel rose. Through the mill's broken roof, the clouds had begun to tear, and beyond them the first hard stars of the long winter glittered like nails.

"Now," he said, "we hunt them. Properly. Loudly, badly, always a day behind." He pulled his cloak tight against the cold that was coming down from the ice-fields, a cold with intent behind it. "Because in a fortnight the Warden himself rides north with the full host, and if he reaches those Stones before they do—if the man who broke the covenant reaches the place where it could be remade—then every memory any of us ever lost stays lost forever. Including hers."

He didn't say *Maera's*. He didn't have to. Hallow looked at him a moment longer, then nodded once, and began kicking dirt over the fire.

Above the dead mill, the rain thickened, and turned, without ceremony, to snow.
