## 10. North Through the Falling White

Tam heard the door of winter before it opened.

It wasn't a sound like other doors—not hinges, not wood, not the creak of stone on stone. It was a long low groan under the world, the kind of groan a house makes when something heavy settles somewhere it shouldn't. Tam had been hearing doors all their life: the innkeeper's back door swinging wide three seconds before the tax collectors walked in, the cellar hatch in Rook's safehouse breathing open a heartbeat before the raid came. Doors spoke to Tam the way rivers spoke to drowned men.

This one was the biggest door Tam had ever heard, and it was opening over the whole sky.

"Rook." They shook him awake in the grey dark before dawn, their breath fogging in the ruined chapel where the company had sheltered. "The cold's coming faster than you said. I can hear it."

Rook blinked up from his bedroll, beard frosted at the edges, and for a moment—just a moment—the smuggler's easy grin didn't come. That was how Tam knew it was bad. Rook grinned at everything. He'd grinned when the hunters burned the ferry behind them. He'd grinned when the pass ahead turned out to be crawling with crown scouts. The grin was armor, and Tam had learned to read what lived underneath it.

"How fast?" he asked.

"Like it's running."

* * *

They broke camp by lamplight, eleven of them moving through a world gone the color of old bone. The unnatural winter had come down off the northern peaks like something poured rather than fallen—snow that fell sideways, snow that fell upward, snow that hung in the air unmoving as if the sky itself had forgotten what it was supposed to be doing. The company moved north through it in single file along a drover's road half-swallowed by drifts, toward the Standing Stones, toward the only hope anyone had bothered to name out loud since fleeing the city.

Isandro Vasquez walked third in the line.

Tam watched them from fifth position, counting steps the way they counted everything, because counting was a kind of door too and numbers never lied to you the way people did. Isandro walked with their shoulders hunched and their hood drawn and their eyes fixed on the middle distance, the way they'd walked every day since the flooded market, since the window that shattered and the rain that bent sideways and the ledger stolen out from under the whole weight of the Veiled Exchange. Ten years hidden. Ten years pretending to be nothing but ink-stained fingers and a bent spine over account books.

Ten years, and now they were supposed to reforge a covenant older than the crown, and Tam was supposed to trust them with it.

Tam did not count themselves among the trusting.

The company was a strange arithmetic. Rook at the front, reading the road like a ledger of his own. His runners: Dossa, broad and quiet; Pell, who whistled through her teeth when she was nervous and hadn't stopped whistling in four days; Wick, young and quick-talking, who could move through a crowd like water through a grate. Then the sisters Marisol and Aya, gift-bearers out of the river districts, who kept each other warm by keeping each other talking. Old Brecht, whose gift was small—he could make a candle burn twice as long—and who apologized for it constantly, as though being barely magical were a moral failing. Captain Aurel Vane walked near the rear, hunter's cloak traded for plain wool, still carrying himself like a man expecting to be arrested by his own shadow. And Odile Ferran, mistress of the Veiled Exchange, marched between two guards made of rope and suspicion, her wrists bound, her chin up, her silence more eloquent than any confession she'd given.

And Tam. Twelve years old, smallest in the line, hearing doors no one else could hear, useful in exactly the way a key is useful—which is to say, carried and needed and never once asked how the carrying felt.

"You should ride," Rook said, falling back beside them sometime past midmorning. He nodded at the mule that hauled their food. "Your feet'll freeze otherwise."

"I can hear better walking."

"You can hear fine from a mule."

"I can hear *her* fine from here." Tam jerked their chin at Odile without looking at her. "She talks in her sleep. Did you know that? She counts. Names and numbers. Like she's doing accounts even with her eyes shut."

Rook's grin flickered and held. "Everyone keeps ledgers, little door-hearer. Some just keep them in different places." He ruffled Tam's hood, which Tam permitted because it was cold and the gesture was warm, and moved forward again down the line.

That was the thing about Rook everyone got wrong, Tam thought. People saw the grin and the swagger and the way he called every disaster an *opportunity*, and they decided he was soft. But Rook had pulled six gift-bearers out of the city while the Veilhunt closed around it like a fist. Rook had looked at Isandro—a stranger bleeding storm-light and panic—and said *you're with me now, so walk*. Rook collected people the way some men collect debts: relentlessly, and with total commitment.

Tam just wished collecting people were the same as keeping them alive.

* * *

The snow changed in the afternoon.

Tam felt it before anyone else—the door groaning wider—and then Pell felt it, stopping mid-whistle so abruptly that the silence rang. By the time the others noticed, the flakes had gone from fat and drifting to small and hard and horizontal, driven on a wind that hadn't existed ten minutes earlier. The temperature dropped like a trapdoor opening.

"Off the road!" Rook bellowed. "Rock shelf, two hundred paces east—move, *move*!"

They moved. The wind came behind them like a living thing, shoving, shrieking, packing snow into the folds of their clothes and the seams of their boots. Tam ran with their head down and their ears full of roaring, and somewhere in that roaring they lost the count of their own footsteps for the first time in days, and that frightened them more than the cold did.

They reached the shelf—a lean of granite jutting from the hillside, deep enough to shelter a dozen bodies pressed together—and they counted themselves in out of the white. Rook. Dossa. Pell. Wick. Marisol. Aya. Brecht. Vane. Odile, roped, dragged stumbling. Isandro, last but two—

Tam counted again.

"Wick," Tam said. "Where's Wick?"

The white outside had become a wall. You couldn't see ten feet into it. The wind screamed through the gap of the shelf like breath forced through a keyhole, and beyond that screaming, faintly, humanly, there was another sound. A voice. Calling.

"*Here!* I'm—*here!*" 

"He fell back at the bend," Dossa shouted. "I thought—I thought he was right behind me—"

Rook was already moving, wrapping his scarf across his face, and Vane caught his arm. "You go out there, you're both dead. Wait for the squall to break. It has to break."

"It might not break." That was Isandro. Tam watched them come to the mouth of the shelf, snow crusting their lashes, staring out into the blindness. "This isn't weather. This is the veil fraying. The winter's not natural—it doesn't have to follow rules. It could hold for hours. Days."

Wick's voice again, thinner now, farther. "*Please.*"

And here was the thing Tam would turn over and over for the rest of their life, the thing they'd examine from every angle the way you worry a loose tooth: Isandro stood at the edge of that blinding white, and Isandro hesitated.

Not long. Later, in kinder hours, Tam would admit it wasn't long at all—three breaths, maybe four, the space of a man or woman or whoever Isandro was deciding whether to spend ten years of buried self on a gamble. Three breaths in which Isandro's hands rose slightly, twitched, curled closed. Three breaths in which the storm-caller who had shattered a window with a slip of temper stood at the lip of a killing wind and *didn't call anything at all*.

Then Rook tore free of Vane's grip and went into the white, rope tied to his waist, Dossa paying it out behind him, and the decision stopped belonging to Isandro at all.

* * *

They found Wick forty minutes later, or what the storm left of him.

Rook carried him back wrapped in his own coat, and nobody needed to say anything, because the shape of what Rook carried told its own story. Wick had been nineteen. He'd talked constantly, mostly about food he intended to eat when the war—or whatever this was—was over. He'd once taught Tam a trick for picking locks using two hairpins and a lie about being a locksmith's nephew. He'd been quick-talking and quick-moving and quick, in the end, to fall behind on a road none of them should have been on.

They laid him against the back wall of the shelf. There was no ground soft enough to dig and no fire hot enough to make a difference, so they covered him with snow, which Brecht said quietly was the old way, the mountain way, and that the cold that killed you also kept you. Nobody found much comfort in it.

Rook sat apart, staring at nothing, and his face had no grin on it at all. Tam had never seen that before, and hoped never to see it again, and knew they would.

Pell didn't whistle anymore. She sat with her knees drawn up and her teeth clenched so hard Tam could hear them grind beneath the wind.

And Tam—Tam went looking for someone to blame, because grief is a fire and blame is fuel and twelve-year-olds are not known for freezing to death gracefully. Tam walked the length of the shelf, past the huddled shapes, past Odile watching them with those unreadable merchant's eyes, past Vane standing sentry at the mouth of the shelter like a man guarding a wound.

Tam stopped in front of Isandro.

Isandro sat with their back to the stone, hands open on their knees, palms up, the way Tam had seen them sit twice before—in the drowned market, in the chapel—like someone listening to something very far away. Their eyes were red-rimmed. Whether from wind or weeping, Tam couldn't tell and didn't care.

"You waited."

Isandro looked up.

"At the bend. When Wick fell behind. You *waited*." Tam's voice cracked and they hated it and pushed through anyway. "You stood there and you did your—your *listening*, with your hands like that, like you were deciding if he was worth it. If we were worth it. You've got a storm inside you, everybody knows it, we've all *seen* it, and you stood there and let Rook do the dangerous part because you've spent ten years being nobody and you don't know how to stop!"

"Tam—" Rook's voice, hoarse, from across the shelf.

"No! Everyone keeps saying we need them, the great storm-caller, the covenant-breaker's fixer, the one who's going to save us all at the Stones—and Wick is *dead*, and where were the storms? Where was any of it?" Tam's breath came in ragged white bursts. "You want us to trust you? Belonging isn't given. My mam taught me that. You earn it. Every day. And you were three breaths too slow today, and three breaths is a boy's whole life."

The words landed. Tam watched them land—watched Isandro flinch as though each one struck separately, watched their open hands close slowly into fists against their knees. Around the shelf the company had gone still. Even the wind seemed to lean in through the gap to listen.

"I'm sorry," Isandro said. It was barely above the wind. "You're right. I hesitated, and hesitation has a price, and Wick paid it. I won't tell you it was complicated, or that I was afraid, or any of the true things that don't matter. I hesitated. If I'd moved when you did, he might be alive." A breath. "I will carry it. I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to watch me, from here to the Stones, and decide afterward whether I've earned anything at all."

Tam stood there shaking, waiting for the anger to break like a fever, and it didn't—it settled instead, banked itself down into coals that would keep, that would last the whole journey north. Good, some cold practical part of Tam thought. Anger was warmer than grief. Anger could walk.

"You'll carry it," Tam said. "Good. Carry it where I can see it."

They turned away and went and sat next to Pell, and put their shoulder against hers, and neither of them said anything, and that was its own kind of door—grief opening onto grief—and they sat in the doorway together until the light failed.

* * *

In the deepest hour of the night, when the squall finally broke and the stars came out savage and clear above the snowfield, Tam woke to voices.

Rook and Vane, at the mouth of the shelf, heads together over a scrap of map. Tam crept closer on frozen feet, because doors whispered to them even in conversations, and this conversation had the creak of a hinge about it.

"—the pass is done," Rook was saying. "Snowed to the eyebrows. We cut west through the tollhouse valley."

"The tollhouse." Vane said it flatly. "You mean the elderkin gate."

"Same thing."

"That's not a route, that's a price tag. Everything that goes through Wren's crossing pays. And the coin up there isn't money." Vane tapped his temple. "It's memory. True memory, freely given—that's the toll, that's always been the toll. Half the reason the crown abandoned that road two generations back is that too many soldiers came home not knowing why they'd marched."

Rook was quiet a moment. Snow ticked off the rocks above them.

"We don't have a choice," he said finally. "Behind us is the hunt and the winter both. Over the pass is death. Through the tollhouse is—we pay, and we grieve, and we keep moving north. That's the whole plan, Vane. It's been the whole plan since the city gates. We just haven't had to say the ugly part out loud yet."

"And Isandro?"

"What about them?"

"They're going to have to give something at that gate too. Something real. And after tonight—" Vane glanced back into the darkness of the shelf, toward where Tam sat listening, toward where Isandro slept alone at the far wall, apart from the others by common unspoken consent. "After tonight, I'm not sure there's much of them left to give."

Tam slid back into the shadows before either of them turned, heart hammering, and lay down between Pell and Dossa and stared at the star-hung gap in the rock overhead.

A toll paid in memories. Tam turned the idea over the way they turned keys in locks. Tomorrow they would walk into the elderkin's valley, and everyone they loved would have to hand over a piece of themselves to get through, and Tam tried to imagine which piece they'd choose and found they couldn't—that every memory they owned felt load-bearing, felt like a beam holding up the whole house of who they were.

Above them, the unnatural winter gathered itself for another assault, and the great door under the world groaned wider, and somewhere north, half-buried under centuries of ice, the Standing Stones waited with their own price already written.

Tam pulled Pell's blanket over both of them, and counted their own heartbeats until sleep took them, and dreamed of a gatekeeper with autumn eyes saying, gently, *and what will you give me, little door-hearer?*
