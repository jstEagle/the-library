## 14. Vane's Choice

Aurel Vane had been a hunter for eleven years, and in all that time he had learned one truth above the rest: the quarry always ran. It didn't matter how clever they were, how desperate, how blessed with gifts that could crack the sky—they ran, because running was what fear taught. His whole craft was built on it. Fear made people predictable. Fear drew straight lines through wilderness, and Aurel had spent his career walking those lines faster than anyone.

But Isandro Vasquez did not run. Not anymore.

Aurel stood at the treeline above the old shepherd's road and watched their camp through the falling snow—six figures around a low fire, huddled beneath an outcropping of granite, and he counted them twice because the count kept refusing to make sense. The smuggler Rook. The child who heard doors. The woman Odile Ferran, wrists bound, sitting apart like a stone someone had carried too far. Two runners, hollow-cheeked from the pass. And Isandro, standing at the edge of the firelight with their face turned up to the sky, as if listening for something only they could hear.

Seven days since the frozen pass. Seven days since Aurel had knelt in the wreckage of an avalanche with his squad buried behind him and let the storm-caller walk away. Seven days of knowing exactly what that made him.

*Traitor*, said the voice that wore his father's cadence. *You were raised better.*

His father had been a mason in Lowmark, back when Lowmark still had a name. Back before the tithe-wagons came through every autumn and took the town's memories in measured scoops—the year of the mill fire, the names of the dead, the song his mother sang while kneading bread—until the town stood whole and clean and empty, its people smiling at strangers who had once been neighbors. Aurel had been nine. He remembered being angry about something and not remembering what. He remembered his mother's hands and not her face. He remembered rain on the roof of a house he could no longer find in the ruins of his own mind, and a voice calling to him through that rain—a girl's voice, or a boy's, laughing, saying *come see, come see, it's learning our names*—

He had carried that fragment for twenty years like a splinter worked too deep to dig out. Storm-light. Laughter. A name almost spoken.

Then, seven days ago, on a shattered ridge, he had looked into Isandro Vasquez's eyes across thirty feet of smoking snow and known. Not proven. Not reasoned. *Known*, the way you know your own hand in the dark. The storm-caller who had leveled the pass was the storm-child of Lowmark, the one whose gift had frightened the tithe-clerks, the one whose name had been scraped out of every ledger and every head—including, apparently, his own—and the crown hadn't just erased a person. It had erased him *from* other people. It had reached into a child's memory and torn out the sound of his friend's laughter, and left the hole shaped like it, so that for twenty years Aurel had walked around inside himself bumping against the edges of something missing.

The hunters' creed said gifts were rot, that the Veilhunt pruned the kingdom's garden. The creed had never explained why pruning required forgetting. You did not forget a weed you'd pulled. You forgot a person you were ashamed of.

Aurel pulled his hood lower and started down the slope toward the fire.

* * *

Tam heard him first—he knew that by the way the child's head snapped toward the treeline a full minute before anyone else moved, small body going rigid, hand finding Rook's sleeve. Then Rook was on her feet, knife out, and the runners were scrambling, and Odile Ferran lifted her bound hands in something that might have been mockery or blessing.

"Captain Vane," Rook called across the snow, conversational as a market greeting, blade steady. "Come to finish what the mountain couldn't? Or just lost?"

"I'm alone."

"You're alone *now*." She flicked the knife point toward the dark behind him. "Your lot travels in packs of six and leaves one to circle wide. So where's the circler?"

"There is no circler." He stopped ten paces out, well short of the firelight's edge, and spread his arms—not surrender, exactly. Inventory. Letting them see he carried no crossbow, no net-chains, nothing but the long hunter's coat and the satchel slung across his chest. "I left my squad at the pass. They're digging out two hunters I buried. After that, they'll report that Captain Vane pursued the fugitives north alone and was lost to the weather. By morning, no one will be looking for any of you. By spring, no one will remember there was ever a hunt this far north."

Odile laughed softly, a dry rustle. "They'll remember you, though. Memory-Warden keeps excellent books."

"Yes," Aurel said. "He does." And that was the heart of it, wasn't it—the reason he had walked away from his own fire three days ago and followed these tracks through the white silence instead. Somewhere south, in a tower of perfect records, his name was already being written into the column of the taken. There had never really been a choice about which side of the ledger he ended up on. Only about whether he arrived empty-handed.

Isandro stepped forward, and the firelight caught their face—the same face from the ridge, wind-scoured, exhausted, watchful. "Why?" they asked. No accusation in it. That was somehow worse than accusation. Just the question itself, laid open between them like a card turned face-up.

Because he owed them an answer, he gave the true one. "There was a storm-caller in my town. When I was young. Before the tithe came." He watched Isandro go very still. "My mother paid the tithe gladly, you understand. Everyone did, after the Warden's men explained what the gifts had cost the last generation. Plague-towns. Burned harvests. A border war nobody could name. We were told forgetting was mercy. I believed it for twenty years." His breath fogged and hung. "Then I cornered a storm-caller on a ridge and saw the lightning answer them like a dog hearing its master's whistle, and I thought: *that's what I forgot.* Not a danger. A person. My crown took a person out of me and told me the space where they'd been was safety."

The snow fell between them. Somewhere upslope, a branch cracked under its burden of white and let go.

"You're Vane," Isandro said quietly. "Lowmark. You used to dare the river ice in late winter, before it was safe. You fell through once and I pulled you out and we swore we'd never tell, and then the clerks came in the autumn and—" They stopped. Their jaw tightened. "I don't have the rest. They took mine too. Different pieces, maybe. Or all of it."

"All of it," Aurel said. "All of it except—" He touched his own temple, absurdly, as if the fragment lived somewhere he could point to. "Rain. Laughing. *It's learning our names.* That's all I kept. Twenty years, that's what I had. I thought it was nothing. I thought everyone had some little broken thing like that rattling around."

Rook had not lowered the knife. "Touching," she said. "Truly. Now here's my question, Captain. Last time we met, you put a bolt within a hand's width of Tam's head. The time before, you burned the drowned market stairs to trap us. I don't care what you forgot. I care what you've done. So talk fast, and talk useful, because the second you stop being useful, Isandro's conscience stops protecting you. Mine doesn't have one."

"Rook," Isandro said.

"No. He gets to hear it." She stepped forward, boots crunching, and now the knife did move, tracking him with the lazy precision of a woman who had gutted men for less than sentiment. "You want in? Say what you're offering. All of it. Don't dress it."

Aurel held her gaze and opened the satchel.

"The route-books," he said, and drew them out: five volumes, oilskin-wrapped, their spines stamped with the crowned eye of the Hunt. "Every path the hunters use through the northern ranges. The way-stations—thirty-one of them, stocked, most unwatched since the Warden recalled men south for the winter offensive. The cairn-codes for signaling safe passage. The schedules of the supply trains on the King's Road until thaw." He set them down in the snow between them, deliberately, and stepped back. "And this." A single folded paper, soft with handling. "The Warden's standing orders. His own hand. Read the third paragraph."

Rook glanced at Isandro. Isandro crossed the distance, picked up the paper, and read. Aurel watched their face change—the small contraction around the eyes, the slow settling of the shoulders, like weight being accepted.

"Read it aloud," Odile said from her place by the rocks. "Some of us are owed the theater."

Isandro's voice came flat and cold off the ice. "*Upon recovery of the covenant-ledger and the apprehension of the asset designated Vasquez, I., remnant assets are to be liquidated without record. II., the Stones are to be collapsed by powder charge regardless of outcome. III., the captain commanding the northern pursuit is authorized to be expended if he proves unreliable, per precedent of his father's file.*" Isandro looked up. "Your father was a hunter?"

"My father was a tithe-clerk who found out what the tithe was actually for," Aurel said. "He asked questions in the wrong season. The Warden's predecessor wrote him off the same way—*unreliable, expendable.* It's practically a family crest. I found my father's file in the Warden's archive two years ago and I've been carrying it next to my heart ever since, waiting for it to mean something." He looked at the route-books lying in the snow. "Now it does. Or it's nothing. That part isn't mine to decide anymore."

* * *

They argued for an hour. He sat outside the ring of firelight and listened to it, because eavesdropping was honest work and he'd earned none of their privacy.

Rook wanted him dead. She made the case cleanly, without heat: a hunter was a hunter, trust was a resource scarcer than food, and the moment Vane's usefulness ended—or the moment he got scared, or ambitious, or homesick—he would sell them all for a pardon, because that was what hunters did, and she had watched hunters do it to her people for fifteen years. "You want to know what the covenant starts with?" Aurel heard her say, voice pitched low and hard. "Not stones. Not words. Survivors. And I keep survivors alive by not betting them on reformed wolves."

The runner whose brother had died on the pass said less, but what he said was worse: "Let him carry the packs. Let him walk at the back. If he runs, we lose nothing but a pair of legs."

Tam said nothing at all, which, from Tam, was its own verdict. The child simply watched him across the fire with those door-hearing eyes, and Aurel had the uncomfortable sense of being listened to the way you listen at a wall—for what moved behind it.

And then Isandro's voice, quiet under all of it. "When the Warden comes—and he's coming, that order says so—we will need to know where he'll strike from, and we will need to strike first, and everything we need for that is in those books. But that's not why." A pause, snow ticking down through pine branches. "Do you remember the tollhouse? What I paid?"

"Heard about it."

"A memory of my mother. Freely given. That's the price the old pact asks—real coin, given real and free. Not extracted. Not traded under threat. Given." Another pause, longer. "Vane is giving us the thing that will most likely get him killed, freely, and asking for nothing but a place to stand when the bill comes due. If that's not the same coin, I don't know what is. The covenant begins with people, Rook. It has to. Otherwise it's just stones and words again, and look what the last ones bought us."

Silence. The wind moved through the pines. Aurel found, to his surprise, that his hands were shaking—not with cold. He pressed them flat against his knees and waited to be sentenced.

Rook emerged from the firelight alone, knife still visible, and stood over the route-books. For a long moment she looked down at them as though they might bite. Then she crouched, picked one up, thumbed it open, and read three lines of her own smugglers' code mirrored back at her in a hunter's hand—routes her people had bled to learn, written out plain.

"If you betray us," she said, not looking up, "I won't kill you quickly. And I won't do it myself. I'll give you to Tam to decide, and that child has more grief than years, and no mercy worth the name. Understand me?"

"I understand you."

"And you sleep downwind. Where I can hear you breathe." She stood, tucked the book inside her coat, and jerked her chin toward the fire. "Well? Sit down before you freeze, Captain. You're no use to anyone as a corpse, whatever else you are."

* * *

Later, when the others slept, Isandro came and sat beside him at the edge of the outcrop, where the valley fell away northward—a vast white bowl under the stars, and far off, just visible where the glacier met the sky, a jagged line of black teeth rising through the ice.

The Standing Stones. Three days, if the weather held. It wouldn't.

"They'll be waiting there," Aurel said. "The Warden doesn't leave a charge set and walk away. Powder in the glacier roots, a full company in the passes, and every route-book I gave you already read, annotated, and countered. He's had those codes for years. Anything I know, he knows I know."

"I know."

"Then you understand what I'm actually offering. Not maps. Those are half-spoiled the moment they touch your hands." He turned, and the old fragment rose in him—rain, laughter, a name almost spoken—and he let it, finally, finish itself. "What I'm offering is that I grew up inside the Hunt. I know how the Warden thinks because men like him raised me to think it. Every trap he sets, I can guess the shape of it, because I would have set the same one. That's the coin. Spoiled maps and a traitor's instincts. It's not much."

Isandro was quiet for a while. Snow gathered on their shoulders and neither of them brushed it off.

"When you pulled me out of the river," they said at last, "you cried the whole way home. Did you know that? You pretended you hadn't, and I pretended I hadn't seen, and we never spoke of it. I'd forgotten I remembered that. It came back tonight, watching you argue with Rook. Some things they missed." A faint, tired smile. "Maybe the tithe couldn't reach everything. Maybe that's what we're doing up here—collecting what it missed."

Far below and behind them, south along the road they had traveled, a light kindled in the darkness. Small. Steady. Wrong—it burned without flickering in the wind, a flame too disciplined to be natural, and Aurel felt the hair rise on his neck with a certainty older than his training.

Signal-fire. Cairn-code. *Target sighted, moving north.*

Three days, he had thought. He looked at Isandro, and Isandro was already looking at the light, and neither of them said what both of them understood:

The Warden wasn't coming to the Stones.

He was already on the road.
