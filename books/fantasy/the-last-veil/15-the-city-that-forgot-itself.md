## 15. The City That Forgot Itself

Rook smelled the town before they saw it.

Not rot—rot he knew, rot was honest work and an honest stink. This was something else. Something like a house left shut through a long summer, all its air gone stale with waiting. Cold ash. Unwashed wool. The particular sweetness of bread that had risen and risen in its bowl until it collapsed, because no one had come to bake it.

"Brayhollow," Vane said from the front of the column, consulting one of his stolen route-maps. "Market town. Four thousand souls, give or take. We can resupply here—grain, tallow, maybe horses."

Rook grunted and said nothing. He'd learned, in twenty years of moving people who didn't want to be moved, that the map and the ground were two different countries. The ground always told the truth first.

They came down out of the pine scrub at midmorning, eight of them walking single file on the frozen road: himself, Isandro wrapped in a coat three sizes too big, Tam riding on Rook's shoulders because the girl's legs were short and her patience shorter. Behind them, the others—Dalla with her frost-scarred hands, Merric limping on a splinted ankle, the twins Ossa and Pell who hadn't spoken since the pass, since the snow took Bren. Bren, whose name Rook still caught himself counting when he counted heads. Eight now. It should have been nine.

The road curved along a dry streambed, past a shrine with a stone saint whose face had been chiseled off—not weathered, *chiseled*, deliberate as a signature—and then Brayhollow opened up below them in its shallow bowl of fields.

Rook stopped walking.

From this distance the town looked fine. Chimneys smoking, thatched roofs white with frost, geese in the stubble fields. Ordinary. Alive. But no smoke rose from the chimneys in *columns*; it just leaked, directionless, the way smoke rises from a fire nobody is tending. And there were no people in the fields. There were geese, and crows, and a dog trotting the fence line with purpose—but no people.

"There," Tam said, pointing with her whole arm, the way she did. She heard doors before they opened, but she saw everything else before anyone. "There's someone standing in that doorway. They've been standing there a long time."

"How do you know?" Dalla asked.

"'Cause the door doesn't want to be open anymore." Tam frowned. "It wants to close. Nobody's letting it."

They went down into Brayhollow anyway. Rook told himself it was for the grain. He told himself a lot of things in those days, and most of them were lies he kept in good repair, the way you keep a fence around a well so nobody falls in.

* * *

The first man they met stood in the market square, feeding pigeons out of a sack of seed, and he smiled at them with the open, guileless smile of a child greeting strangers.

"You'll want to mind the geese," he said. "They bite."

"Thank you," Rook said carefully. "Friend, we're looking for the miller. We'd trade for flour."

"Miller." The man turned the word over like a coin whose face he didn't recognize. His eyes were brown and clear and utterly empty, a well with no bottom and no water. "I think—I think there's a building where milling happens? I walk past it. I've walked past it every day." He looked troubled suddenly, a cloud crossing his face and then sliding off, unable to find purchase. "Every day. I know that much. Every day. But I couldn't tell you what happens inside, or what my father did, or—" He laughed then, light and easy, and the laugh was worse than the emptiness. "Isn't that funny? I know the *word* father. I just don't have one."

Tam had gone very still on Rook's shoulders. He could feel her small hands tighten in his hair.

"What happened here?" Isandro asked quietly. They'd been quiet all morning, more than usual even for them—since the tollhouse, since the memory Wren had taken, since Isandro woke each day reaching for a face they could no longer picture. Rook watched them ask the question the way you'd touch a wound to see if it was yours.

"The tithe," the man said brightly. "The king's tithe! Everyone gave. It was a festival day, I think—or it would have been, if anyone could remember what we were celebrating." He held up the seed sack. "I remember seeds. I remember that birds are hungry in winter. Those things stayed." He leaned in, confiding, delighted. "Most things went. Isn't it wonderful? No debts. No griefs. I had a wife, I'm fairly sure—the ring tells me so—but I can't feel sad about her, and isn't that a mercy?"

He showed them the ring. It was too big for him. Someone had tied a cord through it so it wouldn't slip off, and the cord was worn soft with years, and the knot had been retied many times by hands that loved him.

Isandro made a sound low in their throat, and Rook put a hand on their shoulder and steered them away, because he knew what was coming next in Isandro's face, and there was nothing in Brayhollow that could answer it.

* * *

The tithe-house stood at the square's center, and it explained everything, the way the crown's works always explained everything while explaining nothing at all.

It was a handsome building, new timber, royal blue painted on the lintels. Inside, they found the apparatus: a great copper vessel like an inverted bell, ringed with veins of pale glass that still faintly glowed, fed by pipes running down into the earth. Vane stood before it a long time without speaking. When he finally did, his voice had the flat quality Rook had learned to fear in soldiers.

"A Tithe Engine. Third generation, by the fittings." He touched the copper and pulled his hand back as if scalded. "I trained at the Citadel. They taught us these were for *collection*—that gift-bearers' excess magic was drawn off and stored for the realm's defense. A tax. A civic duty." His jaw worked. "This one isn't tuned for gifts. Look at the intake valves. It doesn't take magic. It takes *memory*. Everything the covenant bound—who your mother was, what your name means, why the shrine saint's face was struck off—everything that makes a history a history. Poured out and piped south to the capital."

"For what?" Dalla asked.

Vane didn't answer. Rook answered for him, because Rook had spent ten years reading ledgers, both kinds, the kind written in ink and the kind written in blood.

"For fuel," he said. "Memory burns hot in the old workings. You want to break winter's back, you need heat, and the cleanest heat in the world is a people's whole past, poured into the fire all at once." He looked around at the glowing veins, at the neat brass gauges, at the royal blue paint. "The Memory-Warden isn't hunting gift-bearers because he fears us. He's hunting us because we're kindling that hasn't been stacked yet. Gift-bearers remember *harder*, see. Our memories are worth more per head."

Merric spat on the floor. Even that small defiance sounded thin in here.

"And the town?" Ossa asked—one of the twins, voice cracked from disuse. "Why empty a whole town? What could four thousand lives buy?"

Vane walked to the far wall, where a ledger lay open on a stand, entries in a clerk's precise hand. He read for a moment. Then he closed it gently, the way you'd close a coffin lid.

"This quarter's quota," he said. "Brayhollow was drawn down to pay for the winter garrisons on the northern passes. Their history bought six weeks of warmth for soldiers who'll never know whose grandmother paid for it." He looked up, and for the first time since Rook had known him, Captain Aurel Vane looked old. "I signed requisitions. Not this exact engine, but ones like it. I thought I was feeding lamps."

* * *

They walked the town after that, because leaving felt impossible and staying felt worse, and horror, Rook had found, mostly consisted of not being able to do either.

They found the school with its lessons still chalked on the slates—*the river flows north, count by twos, the king's peace is the land's peace*—and no one who remembered learning them. They found a woman sweeping a doorstep that was already clean, over and over, because the sweeping was all that remained of her. They found a tavern full of men playing cards with perfect cheer, and when Rook asked what the stakes were, they all laughed and one said, "Whatever we had, friend. Whatever we had."

They found the churchyard, and this was where Tam finally broke.

She stood among the leaning stones reading names aloud—reading them the way she did everything, fiercely, with her whole body—and no one came to claim a single one of them. "Edmure Lask," she said. "Who was Edmure Lask? Somebody was Edmure Lask's daughter, somebody misses him, somebody knows what he looked like when he laughed—" Her voice climbed. "Somebody *buries* him! Somebody comes every year!"

No somebody came. The snow lay unbroken on every grave, smooth as fresh paper, and the names waited under it with no one left alive who could read them.

Isandro knelt beside her in the snow. Rook hung back—he wasn't good at this part, the comfort part, never had been—but he heard what Isandro said, low and steady, the words of someone paying a debt they understood from the inside.

"I lost my mother's face eleven days ago. I know how it feels. Like a room in you that's still warm but has no door anymore." A pause. "But listen to me, Tam. Listen. The difference between us and Brayhollow is that we *know*. We know what's missing. That's a kind of keeping. As long as we remember that we forgot, the forgetting doesn't win. So we say the names. All of them. Even the ones that hurt. Especially those."

Tam wiped her nose on her sleeve and looked up at the graveyard, at the whole silent town. "Then we say them all," she said. "All day if we have to."

And gods help him, they did. They spent the afternoon going stone to stone, all seven of them plus the child, reading names into the cold air—Edmure Lask, Maren Lask, Odger Fen, Sable Fen, the twins' voices growing stronger with each one, Merric's ankle forgotten, Dalla's scarred hands tracing the carved letters like braille—and Rook stood in the middle of it with snow in his beard and understood, with terrible clarity, what he was looking at.

Not a tragedy. A *preview*. This was the long winter's promise made flesh: not ice, not famine, but this—a kingdom of warm rooms and full bellies and not one soul in them who knew why they were singing, or whom the song was for. The Warden didn't need to kill anyone. He only needed everyone to forget, and then the killing was done forever, and the killers would be mourned by no one because mourning itself would be gone.

Bren's name rose in Rook's throat, unbidden. He said it aloud, into the snow, with all the rest. *Bren.* The runner who'd died in the white because Rook had pushed the pace, because maps were maps and ground was ground and he'd chosen wrong. Saying it didn't bring anything back. But it meant the ledger stayed open. It meant the debt stayed real.

That, Rook thought, was the whole war in a single sentence: they wanted a world with no debts, and everything Rook had ever believed in was a world made entirely of debts, honored daily, paid forward, remembered.

* * *

They camped that night in the tithe-house itself—Vane's suggestion, grim practicality, the engine was dead and its walls were thickest—and Rook sat up late by the banked fire, doing what he always did: writing. His own ledger, the true one, the one that had started as a smuggler's tally of routes and bribes and become, somewhere on the frozen road north, a record of everything the crown wanted unwritten.

He wrote about Brayhollow. He wrote about the man with his wife's ring on a cord. He wrote about the graves with no footprints, and about Tam saying the names, and about the way Isandro had knelt in the snow and given away a piece of their own grief like it was bread and the child was starving.

A shadow shifted across the firelight. Isandro, sleepless too, wrapped in that enormous coat, watching him write.

"You keep everything," Isandro said. It wasn't an accusation. It sounded almost like wonder.

"Somebody has to." Rook tapped the page. "You gave your memories to the tollhouse. The ritual will take the rest. Fine—that's the price, and prices are prices, I've never once argued with a price. But hear me, storm-caller." He looked up, and let the old smuggler's hardness fall away for once, because some things needed saying plain. "When you wake up on the other side of this, blank as Brayhollow—you won't be alone in an empty town. You'll be surrounded by people who were *there*. Who remember you. And I'll have it all down in ink besides, every name, every mile, every stupid brave thing you did. They can take your memory of us. They cannot take our memory of you. That's the difference. That's the whole damn difference, and it's the only weapon we've got that their engines can't melt down."

Isandro was quiet a long moment. Firelight moved on their face, and Rook thought—not for the first time—that he was looking at someone already halfway gone, someone spending themselves like coin, and that the bravest thing about them was how calmly they counted the cost.

"Read it to me someday," Isandro said finally. "If I forget. Promise me you'll start at the beginning and not skip the parts where I was an idiot."

"No promises about the idiot parts," Rook said. "Those are the best entries."

And Isandro smiled—the first real one in days—and for a moment the fire seemed to lean toward them, the way fires do near their kind, and Rook thought: *we might actually win this.*

Then Tam screamed.

She came down the stairs from the loft two at a time, wild-eyed, pointing east through the frost-furred window, toward the dark line of the hills. "Doors," she gasped. "So many doors—opening all at once, miles off, dozens of them, hundreds—" Her breath tore. "Riders. Coming up the valley road. And they're not stopping at the town."

Rook was moving before she finished, snuffing the fire, waking the others, and behind him the tithe-engine's dead copper veins gave one last faint pulse of light—as if somewhere far to the south, something vast had just turned over in its sleep and begun, again, to feed.
