## 17. The Standing Stones Beneath the Ice

The glacier announced itself three days before they saw it.

Tam felt it first—the boy went quiet at the noon fire, head cocked like a dog hearing a whistle no one else could catch. "It's humming," he said. "Under everything. Like a door that's been locked so long it forgot it was a door."

Rook spat into the snow and said nothing. She had stopped arguing about whether to turn back somewhere past the tollhouse, after Wren's price had been paid and Isandro had stood in the cold trying to remember their mother's face and finding, instead of a face, only a shape of warmth where a face should have been. Since then Rook watched Isandro the way sailors watch a storm line on the horizon—measuring, respectful, already grieving.

They climbed through the white hours on the third day, roped together against crevasses that breathed cold up from the dark. Vane led with his hunters' maps, correcting course twice where the ice had shifted since they were drawn. The runner who'd betrayed them was gone now—gone with Odile, gone with the pass, gone with a piece of all of them—and the company moved like people walking behind a coffin: close, silent, unwilling to look directly at the space where someone used to be.

Near dusk, the wind dropped all at once, the way a held breath drops, and there they were.

The Standing Stones rose out of the glacier like bones out of flesh. Nine of them—or what had been nine; two had toppled and lay buried to their shoulders in blue ice, and one was shattered, its fragments scattered across a hundred yards of frozen slope as if something enormous had once struck it in fury. The survivors leaned together at angles no mason would have chosen, black stone veined with lines of pale silver that glowed faintly even in the dying light. Snow did not rest on them. It slid away, deflected, as though the stones still refused to be covered, still refused to be forgotten.

Isandro stopped walking. Their breath came out ragged—not from the climb.

*Home.* The word arrived unbidden and wrong and absolutely right. They had never been here. They were certain they had never been here. And every nerve in their body sang otherwise, the way a tuning fork hums when its twin is struck across a room.

"You feel it too," Tam said. Not a question. He had come to stand beside them without Isandro noticing, small mittened hand hovering near theirs but not touching. "It feels like being remembered."

"Yes," Isandro said, and their voice came out cracked. "Exactly like that."

Vane circled the perimeter with a hunter's discipline while Rook set the camp low among the fallen fragments, sheltered from the wind by the very ruin that grieved her. Sefa's sister Marette—rescued from the Exchange cells, hollow-eyed and grateful—unpacked the covenant-ledger from its oilskin wrapping with hands that shook until she touched the cover, and then, strangely, didn't.

Isandro knelt at the base of the tallest standing stone and put both palms flat against it.

Cold, first. Cold like a blade. Then, beneath the cold, the humming Tam had heard—a deep subsonic pulse, slower than a heartbeat, patient as geology. And threaded through it, faint as a voice through a wall: *words*. Old ones. Words Isandro had never been taught and somehow knew the shape of, the way they knew the shape of storms.

The ledger, open beside them, agreed. The final pages—Odile's founders' hand, generations old—described this place not as a monument but as an *instrument*. A hinge. The point where the veil between realms had been sewn shut with living thread, realm to realm, voice to voice, memory to memory.

And the price of opening it again, written in ink faded to the color of old bruises:

*A keeper must anchor the working. The keeper binds the covenant with what binds them: every memory of belonging. Every bond of home. All of it, freely given, forever. This is the whole of the law, for love given cannot be taken back.*

Isandro read it three times. The words didn't change. The cold coming up through their knees didn't change. Somewhere behind them, Rook was arguing softly with Marette about watch rotations, and the ordinary sound of it made the ledger's sentence land harder, the way grief always lands hardest amid the mundane.

"Read it aloud," Rook said. She had come to stand over them without her usual noise, arms crossed against the cold, jaw tight. "All of us should hear it. No one walks into this blind."

So Isandro read it. The ritual requirements first: voices from both realms, gathered at the heart-stone, speaking the old words in unison while the veil thinned at winter's edge. The elderkin side could be managed—Wren's tollhouse debt had purchased more than passage; the gatekeeper had sworn to send a speaker north, and Isandro had to trust that a creature of bargains would keep this one. On the human side, any gift-bearer's voice would serve.

Then the second requirement. Isandro read it slowly, giving each clause its full weight, because these people deserved nothing less than the whole terrible arithmetic.

When they finished, the silence lasted long enough for the wind to change direction twice.

"No," Rook said finally. Flat. Final. "No. Find another way."

"There is no other way." Isandro closed the ledger. "That's what I keep trying to tell everyone, Rook. The founders looked for another way. That's why they broke the pact instead—they couldn't pay, or wouldn't, and they chose the throne's gold over the price. And look what it bought. A kingdom that eats memories. A hunt that eats people. A winter that won't end."

"So you'll pay it. Just like that." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated herself for it visibly, dragging her composure back up like hauling a net. "You'll give up—" She gestured sharply at the camp, at Marette, at the distant shape of Tam hauling firewood, at Vane returning along the ridge. "This. Us. You just met us, Vasquez. How much can it cost you to lose something you barely have?"

The question hung there, crueler than Rook intended, and true in a way that made it worse.

Isandro thought about how to answer honestly. About the ledger of their own life: ten years of dry ink and borrowed shadows, a name sanded down to a function, a gift buried so deep they'd flinched when it stirred. About the tollhouse, and the hole where their mother's face had been, and how they'd discovered that losing a memory wasn't like losing an object—it was like the object had never existed, like a room in a house bricked over so smoothly you forgot the house had ever had that many rooms.

"I don't know what I'll lose," they said. "That's the truth. Maybe I'll wake up and feel about you all the way I felt about the Exchange—like furniture. Useful. Unloved." They met Rook's eyes. "But here's what else is true. If we fail, everyone loses everything. Every memory, everywhere, harvested to feed a throne while the world freezes. The tithe-towns showed us. You saw the city that forgot itself. There's no version of tomorrow worth having that doesn't run through me paying this."

"And if you refuse?"

"Then I refuse alone, and the rest of you can decide what to do with a leader who asked everything of everyone else and nothing of themselves." Isandro stood, knees cracking in the cold. "I spent ten years learning how to be no one, Rook. Turns out the lesson sticks. But I'm done being no one *on purpose*."

* * *

They worked through the night, because the almanac of the old ledger gave them a window measured in hours, not days: the veil thinnest at the winter solstice's turning, which fell the next dusk. After that, the long winter would seal everything—veil, valley, and every soul in it—under ice that would not melt in any lifetime.

Vane found Isandro at the shattered stone just before dawn.

The captain stood looking at the broken fragments scattered across the slope, gloved hands clasped behind his back, breath steaming. "The Warden's force will come," he said without preamble. "If he knows the Stones exist—and he does, his grandfather broke the pact here—he'll come to end it permanently. He won't wait for spring. He won't wait for anything."

"How long do we have?"

"If he marched from the capital when the Veilhunt faltered? Days. If he used the hunters' roads—" Vane's jaw tightened. "Hours. Possibly less. I left markers pointing him wrong, but the man wrote half those roads himself. He'll read my lie like a letter."

"Then say what you came to say, Captain. We don't have time for circling."

Vane turned. In the pre-dawn gray, his face looked older than his years, carved by the same wind that had shaped the glacier. "I grew up next door to a storm-caller," he said quietly. "Before. Before they took her name out of me and gave me a uniform instead. For twenty years there was a hole in my life shaped like her laughter during thunderstorms, and I called that hole *discipline*, because the alternative was calling it what it was." He looked back at the standing stones. "Whatever happens at dusk—if your ritual works—I want you to understand that some of us were robbed too. The covenant breaking didn't just hoard magic for the crown. It amputated half the world and told the stump it was whole."

Isandro studied him. "You're telling me this why?"

"Because when you give up your memories tonight—" Vane's voice caught, steadied. "Someone should be there who understands what remembering costs. Someone who knows the other side of it. I'm offering to stand witness, Vasquez. It's the only penance I can afford."

Isandro extended a hand. After a moment's hesitation, Vane took it, grip firm as iron, warm inside the glove.

"Witness accepted," Isandro said.

* * *

Dusk came violet and windless.

They gathered at the heart-stone—the tallest survivor, its silver veins now glowing bright enough to cast shadows. Wren's speaker had come, as promised: an elderkin woman named Ishen, tall and gray-barked, with eyes like deep water and a voice that resonated in the chest before it reached the ears. She and Tam stood opposite each other at the stone's base, realm to realm, the child's small voice pitched to meet hers. Marette added her registered gift, thin but true. Rook stood armed at the perimeter with the runners, watching the southern approach, because faith required logistics.

And Isandro stepped into the circle of snow that would not settle around the stone, and understood—with the solemn clarity the mountain had been building in them since they first felt its hum—that this was the last moment they would ever know themselves completely.

Every memory of belonging. Freely given. Forever.

They inventoried it, quickly, greedily, the way a traveler inventories a house before leaving it unlocked: their mother's kitchen, steam on the windows, her voice singing off-key—*that one's already gone, paid at the tollhouse, and the world hadn't ended.* The Exchange's great hall, lamplit, Sefa laughing at some clerical error. Sefa's cot, empty afterward. The flooded undercity, Rook's scarred grin, Tam's hand hovering near theirs on the glacier. The company around last night's fire, passing a pot of stew, arguing about watch rotations. Ten years of hiding, and one week of being found.

Small things. Ordinary things. Everything.

*I paid a decade learning to be no one,* Isandro thought. *Let me spend it buying everyone else the chance to be someone.*

Ishen began to speak. The stone's hum rose beneath their feet. Tam answered, clear and unafraid. The veins blazed silver-white, and the air above the glacier began to shimmer like heat-haze in reverse, the veil wearing thin.

Isandro opened their mouth to join the old words—

And the horizon bloomed orange.

Torches. Dozens of them, then hundreds, pouring over the southern ridge in disciplined columns, and above them, rising on a column of stolen light, a figure in a hood the color of a dead star. The King's Memory-Warden had read Vane's false trail like a letter after all.

The hum faltered. Tam cried out. Rook's shout carried across the ice—*"Company! Shields! NOW!"*—and the night split into motion and screaming wind.

Isandro stayed in the circle, hands on the blazing stone, and did not move.

"Dusk isn't over," they said, to no one, to everyone, to the mountain itself. "We finish this."

The old words rose in their throat like thunder remembering its name.
