## 20. Blank Pages

The ice had gone quiet.

Rook felt the silence in his teeth before he understood it anywhere else—the long winter's grinding pressure, that endless white shriek of cold against stone, simply stopped, like a door closing on a howling dog. He was on his knees at the edge of the ritual circle, Tam's small hand crushed in his own, and for three heartbeats nobody breathed. Then the glacier groaned, deep and vast, and began—impossibly—to melt.

Water ran down the Standing Stones in bright ropes. Steam rose off the black rock. Above them, through a rent in the clouds no wider than a knife cut, a single bar of sunlight fell across the snow and set it blazing like hammered silver.

In the center of it all, Isandro Vasquez lay crumpled among the stones like a coat someone had dropped.

Rook got there first. He always got there first; it was the one thing smuggling had ever taught him that mattered—move toward the falling body, not away from it. He turned Isandro over with hands that shook worse than they had during the battle, during Vane's desperate stand at the ice-line, during any of it.

Isandro's eyes opened.

They were clear. That was the first thing Rook noticed, before the fear arrived. Clear and brown and utterly, perfectly empty—not vacant the way the dead are vacant, but vacant the way a fresh ledger page is vacant. Waiting. Isandro looked up at Rook with polite, mild confusion, the way a stranger looks at a stranger, and said:

"Excuse me. Where am I?"

The words went through Rook like a blade slipped between ribs.

Around them, the company was making noise—Vane shouting orders to hold the perimeter even though the hunters had broken and run, Wren's low elderkin keening from the tollhouse road, Tam crying without any sound at all, just tears freezing on a child's face before the new warmth could catch them. Grief and celebration in the same breath. The winter had broken. The covenant held. The realms were bound again, sealed by a storm-caller who had paid for it with every memory of home and family and belonging they had ever carried.

And now that storm-caller looked at Rook—looked *through* him—and asked where they were.

"Hey," Rook said. His voice came out rough. He cleared his throat and tried again. "Hey. You're safe. You did it. Do you—" He stopped himself. Swallowed the question whole. Of course Isandro didn't remember. That had been the price. They had known the price going in, had stepped forward anyway with that terrible solemn clarity, and Rook had watched them do it and done nothing because there had been nothing else to do.

"Do I what?" Isandro asked. They pushed themselves up onto their elbows, wincing, and looked around at the circle of faces with the careful attention of someone taking inventory. "I'm sorry. Have we met?"

Tam made a sound then—a raw, cracked thing, half laugh and half sob—and bolted. The child ran three steps and stopped, fists clenched, caught between fury and something too big to name, and finally just stood there shaking in the meltwater, staring at Isandro like staring hard enough might shake loose whatever had been taken.

"Tam," Rook said softly.

"They don't know me." Tam's voice splintered. "They called me *little door-listener*. They used to put their coat over my head when the storms got bad so I couldn't hear the thunder screaming. And they don't—" A hiccup. "They don't know me."

Rook gathered the child in without standing, one arm hooked around Tam's shoulders, and kept his eyes on Isandro, who was watching all of this with an expression of dawning, helpless distress.

"I'm sorry," Isandro said again. "I don't understand what I've done. Did I hurt someone? Why is everyone looking at me like that? Like I'm—"

"Like you're dead and alive at the same time," Rook finished for them. He exhaled slowly. Ten years of running cargo past crown patrols and Veilhunter checkpoints had taught him to keep his face still when everything behind it was coming apart, and he spent all of that discipline now. "You're not hurt. Nobody's hurt. Not anymore." He glanced up, found Vane hovering at the edge of the circle, the captain's face gray with exhaustion and something close to awe. "Get blankets. Get the fire going. All of you—give us room."

They gave room. Slowly, reluctantly, the company drew back—Wren last of all, bowing her horned head to Isandro in a gesture old as either realm before shuffling off toward the tollhouse road. Tam resisted, digging in, until Rook murmured, "We'll fix this, little listener. I promise," and the child let themselves be led, looking back every third step.

That left Rook kneeling in the meltwater beside a person who had saved two worlds and didn't know their own name.

"Do you know your name?" Rook asked carefully.

Isandro frowned. Rook watched them reach inward—watched the reaching fail, watched the flicker of alarm when it failed. "It's... there was something. There was—" Their breath quickened. "There was a ledger. I keep ledgers. I know that much. Why do I know that much and not the rest?"

Because you sold the rest, Rook thought. Every memory of belonging, given freely, to anchor a covenant older than crowns. Because you understood better than any of us what losing memory truly means—you'd seen that city where the tithe emptied every mind—and you paid anyway.

Aloud, he said: "Your name is Isandro. Isandro Vasquez."

"Isandro." They tasted it. Their brow furrowed. "That sounds... true. Like something written down properly." A ghost of a smile. "I think I like being written down properly."

Something in Rook's chest cracked open, and he laughed—a wet, broken laugh that embarrassed him immediately. Gods below. Even hollowed out, even blank as fresh vellum, this one was still *them* somehow. The precision. The care. The instinct for order in a world gone mad.

"You would," Rook managed. "Gods, you would."

* * *

They built the fire at the base of the largest Stone, where the meltwater ran shallow and the ground stayed dry. Vane's hunters' blankets went around shoulders; Wren's stores fed the flames with resinwood that burned green-gold and smelled like far-off summers. By full dark the company had arranged itself into a rough ring, and everyone kept finding reasons to look at Isandro and then quickly look away.

Isandro sat wrapped in a blanket like a fledgling bird, watching all of it with hungry, careful eyes. Cataloging. Rook could see the cataloging happening—the same way Isandro had once tracked impossible trades in the Exchange's ledgers, bottle by bottled echo. Now the inventory was people: Vane, sharpening a blade that needed no sharpening. Wren, humming an elderkin tune with no words. Odile Ferran's absence, which sat among them like a cold chair at a warm table—Rook saw Isandro's gaze snag on that gap and linger, puzzled, grieving something they couldn't name.

Finally Isandro spoke into the quiet. "You're all waiting for something."

"We're waiting for you," Rook said.

"For me to become what?" Isandro asked. "I don't have anything to become. I woke up today. As far as I can tell, I have one memory, and it's your face saying my name."

Rook's hand had been resting, for some time, on the satchel at his hip. On the book inside it. Through the frozen pass, through the battle, through the breaking of the winter, he had carried it without quite knowing why—some smuggler's superstition, maybe, or some deeper instinct about what gets saved from a burning house.

He drew it out now.

It was water-stained at the corners and scorched along one edge where a hunter's torch had come too close. The cover was plain black leather, unmarked. But Rook knew what was inside, because Isandro had shown him once, on a night early in the flight north, when the fire was low and trust was newer than it ever got to be: not just the covenant-ledger stolen from the Exchange vaults, but a second book interleaved within it. A private record. Isandro's own hand, chronicling everything—the flight, the crew, the fears, the jokes, the small graces. *A ledger of the journey*, Isandro had called it, half-embarrassed. *In case anyone should ever need to know what happened.*

"In case anyone should ever need to know what happened," Rook said aloud, and opened the book.

Isandro went very still.

"What is that?"

"It's yours." Rook turned to the first page. The handwriting was neat as typeset, slanting slightly rightward, ink brown-black in the firelight. He cleared his throat. His voice wanted to shake. He didn't let it. "It's yours, and I'm going to read it to you. All of it, if it takes all night. Because you wrote it down properly, Isandro. You wrote all of us down. And if your memory's gone—" He looked up and found Tam pressed against Wren's side, listening with wide eyes. Found Vane motionless, knife forgotten. "Then we'll witness it back into existence. That's how ledgers work. An entry isn't real until somebody reads it back."

* * *

He started at the beginning.

*"Day one. Fled the Exchange through the flooded undercity. Broke a window with my mind and I am still not sorry."*

Someone snorted—Vane, of all people, and the sound seemed to surprise him more than anyone.

*"Note: the gift does not feel like a cage when it is answering. It feels like an old friend who has been waiting politely by the door for ten years. This is deeply inconvenient."*

Isandro had drawn their knees up under the blanket, chin resting on them, eyes fixed on Rook's mouth as though the words might be visible there. "Did I write that?" they whispered. "That sounds like someone funny."

"You were someone funny," Tam said, muffled against Wren's fur. "You were funny and you were annoying and you always counted things twice."

*"Day three,"* Rook read. *"Met a smuggler named Rook in the drowned market. He talks too much and lies about his prices but his boat is clean and his motives are legible, which is more than I can say for anyone else I've met this decade. Tentatively keeping him."*

"Tentatively keeping him," Rook repeated, and had to stop and press the heel of his hand against his eyes for a moment. The company waited. Wren hummed a low, steadying note. When Rook trusted his voice again, he went on.

*"Day five. The child hears doors before they open. Today Tam heard mine, which apparently I had closed so thoroughly that even I'd forgotten it was there. Note: children see everything adults bury. Keep this one close. Also: teach them cards, because they cheat already and might as well learn structure."*

Tam laughed—a real one this time, wet and startled out of them—and buried their face in Wren's shoulder. Isandro smiled. It was a small smile, uncertain, the smile of someone hearing about a beloved stranger. But it reached their eyes, and Rook thought: *there. That's the door. That's where we start.*

He read on. Day nine, the deciphering of the broken pact, and Isandro's furious marginal note: *They shattered it deliberately. Two generations of hoarded magic, and they wrote it up in the crown's own hand as providence. Justice outside the law starts here.* Day eleven, the tollhouse, and a line that made Wren close her eyes: *Paid the gatekeeper with my mother's face. I find I regret it less than I expected, which tells me either I am braver than I believed or the memory was heavier than I knew. Possibly both.* Day thirteen, the frozen pass, the storm that learned its name: *I leveled a ridge today. The company looks at me differently now. I don't blame them. I look at myself differently too, and I haven't decided yet whether what I see is a weapon or a weather. I hope, someday, to be weather.*

By the fire, Isandro had begun to cry—quietly, without drama, tears sliding down a face that didn't recognize the grief it carried but carried it anyway. Rook read through it. He read day sixteen and Odile holding the pass, and here he paused, because the entry was short and brutal:

*Odile died so we could run. She sold my name to buy the guild time, and she died buying me time too, and I cannot make the arithmetic of that balance, and I think perhaps it is not supposed to balance. Some debts you carry. You carry them forward. That's what a ledger is for.*

"Some debts you carry," Rook repeated softly, and looked at the empty place by the fire where Odile's cold chair sat. "Forward. That's what it's for."

* * *

The fire burned down to coals twice, and twice Wren fed it, and Rook read. The company drifted in and out of sleep around the ring—Vane took first watch out of habit, then surrendered it to a runner, then sat back down anyway, unable to leave. Tam fell asleep against Wren's ribs somewhere around day fourteen and woke at day seventeen and refused to sleep again, determined to hear every word.

And through all of it, Isandro listened. Listened the way they must have once listened to clients confessing impossible trades—with total, meticulous attention, filing each detail into the growing architecture of a self. Occasionally they asked questions. *What color was my mother's hair? What did the Exchange smell like? Was I kind?* That last one, Rook answered honestly: "You were kind in a way that cost you. You'd have found it easier to be cruel. You chose expensive kindness, over and over. It's all in here."

Near dawn, Rook reached the final entry. It was dated the morning they had reached the Stones, and it was brief.

*If you are reading this to me—if it worked, if the covenant holds and the price was paid—then know this: I wrote you all down because I was afraid of exactly this. Not of dying. Of being unwritten. So write me back. Take turns. Argue about the details. Get them wrong sometimes; wrong memories told with love are still love. And when I ask you who I was, don't tell me a summary. Tell me a story. I was never a summary. None of us were.*

Rook lowered the book.

The sky above the Stones was turning the pale gray-green of true dawn—the first honest dawn in months, light coming up over a world where the winter had broken and the veil was thinning into something passable, something *bridal* rather than burial. Down in the valley, Rook could see meltwater catching the light in a thousand bright threads, all of it running toward the lowlands, toward spring.

Isandro sat with tears on their face and dawn on their hair, and after a long moment they said, quietly:

"Tell me a story, then. Not a summary." A breath. "Start with the window. Start with the part where I wasn't sorry."

Rook grinned—grinned through the ache, grinned because ten years of hiding had ended somewhere on this ice and a new thing was starting here, tonight, page one of a brand-new ledger. He turned the book back to its first leaf.

"Well," he said. "There was this ledger-keeper who broke a window with their mind..."

And around the dying fire, in the first warm morning of a new age, the company leaned in to begin the witnessing—one story, one voice, one remembered stranger at a time.

Far to the south, unseen, the roads down from the mountains were already filling with travelers—gift-bearers who had felt the winter break and were walking, cautious and hopeful, toward the place where the lightning had touched the earth.

Toward the Stones.

Toward them.
