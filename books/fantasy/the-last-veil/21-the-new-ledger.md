## 21. The New Ledger

The first thing Isandro learned about themselves was that they liked the cold.

Not endured it—*liked* it. They discovered this on a morning three weeks after the winter broke, standing at the edge of the glacier-melt with their sleeves pushed up and the wind coming off the ice like a held breath finally released. The others complained about the chill the way people complain about weather, idly, out of habit. But Isandro stood in it and felt something in their chest settle, the way a key settles into a lock they couldn't remember cutting.

"You're smiling," Tam said, appearing at their elbow the way the child always did—soundlessly, though not from stealth. From practice.

"Am I?"

"You do that. When you're near water." Tam squinted up at them with the frank appraisal of someone who had appointed themself chief cataloguer of Isandro's habits, since no one else had the inventory to do it. "You also hum when you sharpen the ledger-knife. Badly."

"I'll have that verified."

"By who? You wrote the verification rules." Tam grinned, gap-toothed, and ran off toward the tent-lines before Isandro could construct a rebuttal—which, admittedly, would have required remembering whether they'd ever been good at arguments. Rook said yes, definitely, insufferably so. Rook was the only witness Isandro had to their own past self, and Rook wielded that authority with the mercilessness of true affection.

Isandro turned back to the valley.

Below the Standing Stones, where the glacier had retreated like a tide going out, the company had built something none of them had a proper name for yet. *Camp* was too small. *Settlement* too bureaucratic. Vane had started calling it the Registry, half as a joke about his old hunters' paperwork, and the name had stuck the way names do when no one offers a better one: a scattering of timber shelters and canvas halls, a long fire-trench, a well cut through thirty feet of ice by a woman named Ossa who could persuade stone to soften like wax. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys in straight, unhurried columns. Somewhere a hammer rang against an anvil. Somewhere else, someone was singing—not well, but loudly, which seemed to be the local standard.

Gift-bearers had begun arriving in the second week. A trickle first: two brothers who could trade warmth between their own bodies and no one else's; an old ferryman whose gift was knowing which way a river wanted to go. Then more, drawn by rumor down the thawing roads—the Veilhunt was dead, its Warden with it, but the fear of registration lived on in people's spines, and so they came to the place where registration meant something different.

Here, there was only the ledger.

It sat now on the trestle table outside the great tent, bound in grey hide, its pages ruled in Isandro's own hand because apparently that was another fact recovered about themselves: their script was beautiful, all sharp ascenders and patient downstrokes. Anyone could write in it. That was the whole design. No crown seal, no guild mark, no mistress's countersignature. Just a name, a gift described in the bearer's own words, and—if they chose—a line for what they were willing to give, and what they needed in return.

Rook had argued for days about that last column.

"It's a weakness," she'd said, jabbing her finger at the page hard enough to dent it. "You're building a list of what every person here can be leveraged with. You might as well hand the next Warden a knife rack."

"And if we don't," Isandro had heard themselves say—voice steady, some recovered instinct supplying the argument—"then we're just teaching people to hide again. Smaller. Quieter. Here instead of there." They'd looked around the fire at the circle of faces, some scarred, all wary. "The covenant didn't break because people had gifts. It broke because they were made to be ashamed of them. I'm not building a fortress. I'm building the opposite of a fortress."

Vane had been the one to break the silence. "I've served three administrations," he said. "Every one of them kept secrets better than they kept promises. Write the column."

So the column stayed. And in three weeks, forty-one names had filled it, and not one person had regretted the honesty—or if they had, they hadn't left.

Isandro crossed the melt-stream on stepping stones Ossa had raised, boots dry, and stopped at the trestle table where the morning's work waited. Two visitors already stood there, huddled together: a young man with frost-burned knuckles and a woman holding his arm, both of them wearing the particular exhaustion of people who had walked a long way on hope and very little else.

"You're the keeper?" the woman asked.

"I keep the book," Isandro said. "It keeps itself, mostly."

The young man swallowed. "They said—I heard, in Lowmark, that the hunt was over. That this place was real. I didn't believe it until I saw the smoke." He flexed his burned hands. "My grandmother could put her hands in a fire and pull out bread. I can't do bread. I can just—" He opened his palm, and a small flame sat there, blue at the heart, trembling not from wind but from fear of being seen.

Isandro watched it without flinching, and watched the man watch them not flinch, and understood that this moment—this small, ordinary non-reaction—was worth more than any ritual. The covenant between realms had been reforged in storm and blood at the top of the glacier. But covenants, Isandro was learning, were not made once. They were made every day, in every unstartled face.

"It's warm," Isandro said simply. "That's a good gift for this valley. We'll want it come autumn."

The flame steadied. The man's shoulders came down an inch from around his ears.

"What's your name?" Isandro asked, opening the ledger. "Your real one. And describe your gift however you like—there's no wrong words. And then the last two lines, if you're willing: what you can offer, and what you need. Nobody checks them. Nobody uses them. It's just so we know how to take care of each other."

The man stared at the page a long time. Then he took the pen in his fire-scarred hand and wrote, in careful letters: *Davin. Fire-hands (small). Can mend and cook. Need: to sleep somewhere my sister isn't afraid.*

His sister squeezed his arm.

Isandro read the entry, nodded once, and said, "Tents are the third row past the smithy. Ask for Ossa—she's fierce-looking but she feeds everyone. Supper's at the big fire when the bell rings."

"That's it?" Davin said. "No oath? No... marking? Nothing?"

"No oaths here." Isandro closed the ledger gently. "Oaths are what people make you swear when they don't trust you yet. We're starting from trust. It's faster."

The sister laughed—a short, startled sound, like she'd forgotten the shape of it—and Isandro felt something turn over quietly in their chest, some part of themselves recognizing a feeling before their mind could name it. Joy, maybe. Or its close cousin, relief. They were still learning to tell their feelings apart, the way you learn birds by song.

* * *

Midday brought Rook up from the lower camp with mud on her boots and a look on her face that meant news, and not the gentle kind.

"Road traffic's picking up," she said, dropping onto the bench across from Isandro and stealing the last of the tea without apology. "Two caravans through the tollhouse in a week. Merchants, mostly—but one of them was asking questions. Not the usual kind. Not *is it safe*, not *how many*. Asking who leads. Asking who decides things." She turned the empty cup in her fingers. "And yesterday a rider came through flying the stag of Callowmere. Didn't stop. Just rode the ridge road slow enough to count chimneys."

"How many does Callowmere count to?"

"With the new tents? Ninety-one." Rook grimaced. "We're getting big enough to be an argument. Every court in the north is going to have an opinion about a settlement where magic registers itself and answers to no throne. Some of those opinions will come with soldiers."

Isandro looked out over the valley—the hammer-song, the chimney-smoke, children racing along the fire-trench with a hoop, a woman teaching two teenagers to shape river-water into clean ribbons above their palms while they shrieked with laughter. Ninety-one chimneys' worth of the opposite of a fortress.

"Then we'd better finish becoming an answer," Isandro said, "before they finish forming the question."

Rook snorted. "Listen to you. Three weeks ago you didn't know your own favorite color."

"I still don't."

"Blue," Rook said. "You wore nothing else for ten years, according to your own handwriting. Page four hundred and six." She tapped the satchel at her hip, where the journey-ledger lived—the record Isandro had kept through exile and flight and war, now the most-read book in the valley. Isandro had asked Rook, early on, to stop reading it aloud in private, because hearing your own life narrated back to you by someone doing voices was a strange kind of torture. Rook had agreed. Mostly. She saved the voices for special occasions.

"Page four hundred and six," Isandro repeated. "What else am I on that page?"

"Stubborn. Terrible at cards. Afraid of being useful, right up until you were the most useful person alive." Rook's voice gentled on the last part, and she stood, brushing off her coat, business again. "Council meeting at dusk. Vane wants to talk watches and walls. Tam wants to talk about the tunnels under the east ridge—he swears he hears a door down there that isn't ours."

"A door?"

"You know how Tam is with doors." Rook shrugged, but her eyes stayed serious. "This one's singing, apparently. Come hear it yourself, if you want."

She went. Isandro sat alone with the ledger open in front of them, forty-two entries waiting, and thought about doors that sang.

* * *

They went at dusk, before the council, with Tam leading and Vane falling into step beside them unasked—his habit now, hovering at Isandro's shoulder with the vigilant awkwardness of a man serving a sentence he'd volunteered for. He'd cut down the Memory-Warden on the ice, and no tribunal had demanded anything of him since, because there was no crown left with jurisdiction here. But Vane sentenced himself daily anyway: to carrying water, to standing the worst watches, to walking three steps behind Isandro as if proximity could balance the books.

"The tunnel's natural limestone," Vane was saying as they descended the east slope with lanterns. "Probably. Limestone doesn't usually hum in fifths, though."

"Doesn't it?"

"You'd know better than me. Apparently." There it was again—that edge under his deference, the hunger of a man who remembered a storm-caller from his childhood and couldn't ask the person beside him if the memory fit, because the person beside him had no memory to check it against. Isandro had stopped resenting it. Now it just made them sad in a specific, complicated way, the way all the recovered facts about their lost decade made them sad: not with grief exactly, but with the sense of a room in their own house they could see through a window but never enter.

The cave mouth opened in the hillside behind a curtain of frozen waterfall, and inside, the air was strangely warm and smelled of wet stone and something older—green, almost, like a forest remembered by rocks. Tam led them down past the lantern-light's reach without slowing, ducking side-passages with the certainty of someone following a voice.

"It's louder tonight," Tam whispered. "Since the thaw. Listen."

Isandro listened.

At first: dripping water, their own footsteps, Vane's breathing. Then beneath it, rising slowly like a chord assembling itself note by note, a hum. Deep and resonant, felt in the teeth more than heard, and threaded through it—Isandro's breath caught—a rhythm. Not random. Patterned. Like speech slowed a thousandfold. Like a word being said over the course of an hour, by something with infinite patience.

They followed it down and down, until the passage opened into a chamber where the lantern-light found walls veined with pale luminous threads, spreading like frost-flowers or lightning frozen mid-branch. And in the center of the chamber floor, half-emerged from the living rock, lay a stone.

Round. Waist-high. Carved over every surface with script that predated every alphabet Isandro knew—and did not know, and yet their fingers, when they reached out without deciding to, traced the grooves like a reader running ahead of their eyes.

The humming came from it.

"This is why the Stones stand where they stand," Isandro heard themselves say, voice gone quiet and certain, some deep stratum of recovered knowledge surfacing whole. "This is an anchor-root. The Standing Stones are its leaves. The covenant doesn't live in the circle—it lives *here*, underground, growing. We reforged the branch. This is the trunk." Their fingertips rested on the carved spiral at the stone's heart, and the hum shifted—warmth blooming up their arm, familiar as their own heartbeat, and for one vertiginous instant they felt the stone *notice* them. Recognize them. The way you recognize a voice you learned before language.

*Storm-keeper,* said the hum, in no language at all. *Welcome home.*

Isandro snatched their hand back.

"It knows you," Tam breathed. Their eyes were enormous in the lantern light, and for once the child who heard every door in the world looked afraid of what they'd heard. "Isandro. It's been saying your name. For weeks. I thought—I thought it was just a sound."

Vane's hand had gone to his sword out of pure reflex; he forced it away, visibly. "Saying it how? Saying it to whom?"

"To anyone who'll carry it up," Isandro said softly. The certainty kept arriving, layer after layer, like sediment settling. "It's not a message. It's a seed. The old keepers grew these chambers all across the ranges—an entire root-system under the mountains, each one holding a piece of the covenant, each one waiting for a keeper willing to tend it." They looked at the luminous veins climbing the walls, branching outward into the dark, and understood with total clarity that the threads ran farther than any map. Toward other chambers. Other sleeping stones. Other places where the broken covenant had once been anchored, and where its regrowth could be encouraged—or corrupted.

Or claimed.

"Callowmere's rider counted our chimneys," Isandro said. "But if the crown's heirs ever learn what's underneath the mountains—every anchor-root, every sleeping stone—" They turned to Vane, and to Tam, and felt the weight of the ledger-table settle onto their shoulders again, multiplied a thousandfold. "We haven't built a haven. We've built the first library of a war nobody's declared yet."

Above them, muffled by fifty feet of rock, the supper bell rang across the valley—small and human and absurdly dear, calling ninety-one households to a shared fire.

Tam reached out and took Isandro's hand, small fingers lacing through theirs with the uncomplicated trust Isandro was still learning to accept. "Come eat," the child said. "The stone's waited two generations. It can wait through supper."

Isandro laughed—surprised by the sound of it, by how easily it came now, how much of themselves they'd rebuilt in mere weeks out of witnessed pages and chosen company. They let Tam pull them toward the daylight, Vane's lantern swinging behind, the anchor-root's hum fading below them like a promise being filed for later.

Tomorrow there would be entries in the ledger, and council arguments about walls, and Rook doing voices. Tonight there was soup.

But as they climbed toward the firelight, Isandro found their thoughts returning again and again to the branching luminous veins in the dark—to all those sleeping stones scattered under the mountains, waiting for keepers who didn't yet know they'd been called.

Somewhere out there, the next one was already humming.
