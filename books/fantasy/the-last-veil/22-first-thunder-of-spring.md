## 22. First Thunder of Spring

The first thunder came in the night, three weeks after the ice began to sing.

Isandro woke in the small stone room they had claimed above the old tollhouse—no longer a tollhouse now, since Wren had declared it a door that opened both ways and asked no more tolls—and lay still beneath the wool blanket, listening. The sound rolled up from the valley floor like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and it was not the sharp, hungry crack of winter storms, not the white lightning that had hunted them across the frozen passes. It was low and warm and patient. The sound of a sky remembering itself.

They were out of bed before their mind caught up with their body, bare feet on cold flagstones, heart hammering with an old reflexive fear—*hide, dampen it down, be nothing*—and then they stopped in the middle of the room and made themselves stand still until the fear passed through them like weather.

That was the practice now. Not suppression. Passage.

On the desk by the window sat the new ledger, its cover stitched from grey leather Rook had bartered off a caravan trader, its pages filled in Isandro's careful clerk's hand: names, dates, small impossible things. *Marta of the Ashline, gift of warming, registered by her own choice. The twins Sef and Dalia, who speak to bees. Captain Aurel Vane, former Veilhunter, gift of finding—which is to say, of never being lost, which he swears is not a gift at all but simply good mapwork.* And at the top of the very first page, in letters Isandro had pressed deep enough to read from the back:

*This book belongs to everyone whose name is in it.*

Isandro touched the cover once, the way one touches a sleeping animal, and went downstairs and out into the dark.

* * *

The valley of the Standing Stones had changed since the winter broke. Where glacier had sheared away, green water ran in braided silver threads; where the crown's hunting force had camped and fought and broken, wildflowers were already pushing up through churned mud, stubborn as debt-collectors. Half the Stones still leaned at angles no mason would forgive, half-buried in ice that refused, even now, to fully release them—but the oldest one, the Anchor Stone, stood clean and black against the stars, humming faintly whenever anyone with a gift walked past, the way a tuning fork hums near its note.

The settlement around it had no name yet. There had been three arguments about that in the common hall, all of them loud, none of them settled. Tam wanted to call it Thunderhold. Rook wanted to call it Nothing Here Worth Stealing. Marta wanted to call it Home and had been shouted down on the grounds that it was too soon and too obvious and would jinx the whole enterprise.

Isandro crossed the trampled grass toward the Stones, and the storm came to meet them.

It built slowly over the western peaks—a bruise-colored wall dragging curtains of rain behind it, flickering from within like a lantern with something alive inside. Ten years ago, Isandro would have felt that approach as a hand closing around their throat. Five years ago, buried in the Exchange's counting-room, they would have felt it only as a pressure behind the eyes, an itch beneath the scars of a life they'd sanded away. Even after the reforging—even knowing what they had paid—they had flinched for weeks at every rumble, some hollowed-out part of them still braced for the cost.

But tonight, standing alone among the Stones with the wind rising, Isandro did the thing that had taken twenty-one attempts and one spectacular accident involving Marta's drying laundry:

They raised their arms, and called the storm by name.

Not a command. A greeting. The old words were gone with everything else—mother, home, the Exchange's candlelit counting room, Sefa's laugh across the ledger desks—but the ledger held fragments, and Rook had read them aloud so many times that Isandro had begun, cautiously, to recognize them the way you recognize your own reflection in a shop window: familiar, slightly strange, undeniably yours. So Isandro used their own words instead. Plain ones. Clerk's words.

"Hello," they said to the storm. "I'm Isandro Vasquez. I kept ledgers for ten years. I erased myself and then I wrote myself back. Come in."

The lightning answered.

It came down in a long silver thread that struck the Anchor Stone and ran through the whole ring, lighting every standing monolith from within, and Isandro laughed out loud—the first full laugh they could remember having, unaccompanied by any memory of whether they had ever laughed before—as the power poured into them not like a cage opening but like a tide coming home. Rain swept across the valley in sheets. They let it soak them. They pulled a second bolt down just to feel it, guiding it wide of the settlement with two fingers' worth of attention, into the empty scree slope where it shattered a boulder and left a glass scar that Tam would absolutely demand to see at dawn.

Above the valley, the clouds broke open and the rain turned briefly golden in the moonlight, and Isandro stood in the center of the ring with their arms spread and their hair plastered to their skull and felt, for the first time since waking blank in the aftermath of the ritual, entirely real.

* * *

"You're going to catch your death," said Rook, "or my death, because if you die I have to write the eulogy and I've seen your handwriting."

Isandro turned. Rook stood at the edge of the ring under an oilcloth cloak, hood back, grey-streaked hair dripping, watching the storm with the expression of a woman auditing a miracle and finding the numbers mostly in order. Behind her, spilling out of the common hall and the repaired gatehouse and the row of new timber houses that still smelled of sawdust, came the others.

They came without fear. That was the part Isandro still could not get used to.

In the city, in the old days, a storm-caller working openly would have emptied a street—doors slamming, shutters banging, mothers pulling children inside, someone running for a hunter or a priest or both. Here, the whole settlement came *out*. Marta arrived with her sleeves rolled up and her hands glowing faintly warm, towing two shivering goat-kids she'd been nursing through the cold snap. The bee-twins came barefoot, laughing, their hives humming a half-step higher than usual. Old Wren came last, moving at elderkin pace, which meant unhurriedly and exactly when she intended, and planted herself beside Rook with her arms folded and her leaf-veined face tilted up to the rain.

"Good storm," Wren said, in the tone of a sommelier.

"It's showing off," Isandro said. "I can tell. That last fork wasn't necessary at all."

"Nothing alive is necessary," Wren said. "That's rather the point of it."

And then Tam came through the crowd like a thrown knife, and hit Isandro at a dead run hard enough to stagger them, arms locked around their waist, face buried in their soaked shirt, laughing so hard the laugh kept breaking apart and reforming.

"You woke me up," Tam said accusingly, into Isandro's ribs. "You woke up the *whole valley*, I heard the doors before you even got outside, I heard the Stone open its mouth—"

"That's what it sounds like?"

"That's what it sounds like!" Tam tipped their head back, rain streaming down their face, and pointed up at the churning clouds with the fierce authority of a child who hears doors before they open and has recently learned that some doors are skies. "It's going to do the big one again. The one that goes all the way around the valley. Do it. Do the big one."

"Tam." Rook's voice, dry as tinder. "Perhaps ask."

"I'm asking! See? Asking!"

Isandro looked down at this child who had blamed them for a runner's death on a frozen road, who had hated them cleanly and completely for a whole terrible month, who had forgiven them somewhere on the march south without ever saying so and now clung to them in front of the entire settlement as though attachment were simply a fact of the world, like gravity, like weather. Something in Isandro's chest that had been a locked room for as long as any memory remained turned over and opened a window.

"All right," they said. "But hold onto Marta. And Tam—if you hear any doors we haven't built yet, you tell me *first*."

"There's one," Tam said instantly, pointing—not at the sky, but east, toward the mountain pass where the road came down from the old kingdom. "Big one. Iron-bound. Lots of boots behind it. Coming tomorrow, maybe the day after."

The laughter didn't die so much as change shape, the way a crowd's laughter changes when a stranger walks in. Rook's eyes narrowed. Wren's chin lifted, nostrils flaring, tasting the far air.

"Hunters?" Marta asked quietly.

"No," said Wren. "Smaller. Heavier. Slower." She glanced sideways at Rook. "Wagons, I'd say. Wagons and a banner."

"Aurel's due back tomorrow," Rook said. "From the border talks." She paused. "He wasn't traveling with wagons."

"He wasn't traveling with a banner either," said Isandro, "last time I checked his route-map. Which I did check, because checking things is apparently the one piece of me that survived the ritual intact."

Tam tugged their sleeve. Above the eastern peaks, where the storm was spending itself against the high crags, the sky had begun to clear—and in the clearing, faint with distance, Isandro heard it too: thunder that did not belong to their storm. Rolling up from the pass. Regular. Patient.

Like wheels on a long road.

* * *

They did not sleep much, and neither did anyone else, but there was no fear in it—that was the difference, the thing Isandro kept cataloging in the ledger of small wonders they were building page by page. In the old world, unknown riders on the eastern road would have meant hiding, scattering, dousing every light. Here it meant the whole settlement staying up around the great hearth in the common hall, arguing cheerfully about defenses while Marta handed out spiced cider and Rook laid Vane's route-maps across three pushed-together tables.

"Could be traders," offered Sef.

"Could be pilgrims," offered Dalia.

"Could be an army," said Rook, "with excellent timing, arriving precisely when our walls are half-built and half our people are asleep. Or—" and here she tapped the easternmost map, where Vane had inked, in his precise hunter's hand, a notation Isandro had puzzled over for weeks— "*it could be the thing he wrote 'ask Isandro' next to, because he refuses to explain it himself, the dramatic bastard.*"

"What does it say?" Marta asked.

Rook slid the map around. In the margin beside the eastern pass, small and deliberate:

*Refugees from the capital. Gift-born families who wouldn't register. Word spreads. They're coming here. —A.V.*

The hall went quiet. Into the quiet, Isandro heard the rain easing on the roof, the Stones humming their low contented note, and Tam, already half-asleep against Marta's shoulder, murmuring door-sounds in their dreams.

"They're coming *here*," Sef repeated slowly. "On purpose. Because of us."

"Because of the Stones," Wren corrected gently. "Because the veil stands open and the winter broke and word travels faster than fear, these days. Because somewhere in the capital a mother with a warming-gift decided that a valley with a storm-caller who greets the rain was a better bet than a register and a wagon with barred doors." The elderkin's ancient eyes found Isandro across the hearth-light. "Because somebody chose to be seen."

Isandro looked down at their own hands—clerk's hands, ink-stained, scarred at the knuckles where the covenant-stone had burned them—and thought about the blank pages they had woken to. Thought about Rook reading their journey aloud, night after night, giving them back to themselves one witnessed sentence at a time. Thought about how the ledger on the desk upstairs had started as one person's attempt to remember, and had become a register no crown commanded and no hunter could confiscate, because its authority was nothing but consent.

"Then we'd better build more houses," Isandro said.

The hall erupted—arguments about timber, about the north field, about whether refugees should pay rent (Rook: yes) or never pay anything ever again (Marta: no)—and Isandro slipped out into the wet night while no one was looking, because there was one more thing they needed to do before dawn.

* * *

The Anchor Stone stood steaming gently in the storm's aftermath, and the eastern sky was going grey at the edges when Isandro reached it. From the ledger's inner pocket they took the folded page they carried everywhere now—Vane's handwriting, the first entry in the second book, the one Rook had titled *Witnesses*:

*For the record: I knew a storm-caller once, when I was small. They were made to disappear, and I was made to forget. Tonight I watched a storm greet a person and a person greet it back, and I remembered everything, and I am not sorry. Whatever comes down that eastern road, I'll be standing on the wall. —A.V.*

Isandro pressed their palm flat against the Anchor Stone. It hummed against their skin, warm as a living thing, carrying—as it always did now—the faint twin pulse of the far realm, the elderkin lands beyond the veil, where Wren's kin were planting their own spring.

"Tomorrow there'll be wagons," Isandro told it. "Families. Scared ones, probably. We'll need bigger fires, and someone will cry at the gate, and Rook will pretend she isn't keeping a list of everyone's names, and she absolutely will be." A breath. The grey light crept down the peaks. Somewhere behind them, the settlement was stirring; ahead, far up the pass, the first iron-rimmed wheel ground against stone. "And I don't know who I was, exactly, before. But I know who I am now. Ledger-keeper. Storm-caller. Found."

The Stone pulsed once beneath their hand—acknowledgment, or welcome, or simply the heartbeat of a pact reforged—and high overhead, unbidden, unafraid, a single bright thread of lightning answered across the clearing sky.

Isandro smiled, turned east, and walked down toward the gate to meet the future—thunder walking beside them the whole way.
