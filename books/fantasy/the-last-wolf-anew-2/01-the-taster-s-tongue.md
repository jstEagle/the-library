## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The stew smelled of rosemary and lamb fat and, beneath both, of something sweeter that had no business being there.

Orsen Whitlock stood at the king's left shoulder with his silver spoon and his carefully blank face, letting the steam curl up into his nostrils while the whole of the great hall pretended not to watch him. They always watched. That was the point of him. A food-taster who went unnoticed was a food-taster the court could forget existed, and a forgotten taster was a dead king waiting to happen. So he wore the grey livery with its embroidered tongue-and-dagger crest, and he stood where everyone could see him, and he let them watch.

Henbane. He was nearly certain. The sweetness sat at the back of the throat like a lie told gently — black henbane steeped long enough to lose its bitterness, dosed not to kill outright but to cloud, to make a king agreeable, suggestible. Someone wanted His Majesty soft-minded for something. A treaty signing, perhaps. Or worse.

He took his spoonful. He held it on his tongue and counted — one, two, three — long enough for the hall to see him swallow, long enough for the poison to begin its slow acquaintance with his blood. Then he set down the spoon, bowed precisely the correct depth, and said, in a voice pitched to carry no further than the high table:

"Your Majesty may wish to dine on the pheasant tonight."

King Aldric the Third, who had survived eleven years on Orsen's tongue and knew exactly what those words meant, did not so much as blink. "The pheasant," he agreed gravely, as though it were a matter of preference rather than survival. "Yes. I find myself in the mood for pheasant."

Around the hall, conversation resumed like water closing over a dropped stone. Somewhere near the kitchens' door, a boy in a scullion's apron went white as flour and slipped out sideways. Orsen marked the exit without turning his head. He would remember the boy's face; he remembered everything now, that was the trouble. Three years of tasting had built up inside him like silt behind a dam — every toxin, every tincture, every subtle venom the palace's enemies had ever sent against the crown, all of it absorbed and catalogued in a body that had learned, somehow, to hold it all. The physicians called it constitution. Orsen suspected it was something stranger, but he had never dared look too closely at the gift that kept him alive, because gifts in Veyra's kingdom were taxed, registered, or burned.

Chancellor Veyra. Even thinking her name made his spine straighten. She was somewhere in the hall tonight — she was always somewhere — seated among the ministers in her dove-grey robes, smiling her patient smile, watching him taste with the particular attention of a woman counting another person's coins. When his eyes passed over her by accident, she raised her wine glass an inch in salute. As though to say: *I see you doing your small work, little taster. I see everything.*

Orsen finished the service with his heart beating slow and even through sheer discipline. It was only later, walking the long corridors toward the servants' gate with the henbane humming faintly in his blood like a struck bell that refused to fully fade, that he allowed himself to feel what he always felt after such moments: the cold, specific fear of a man who has noticed something and must decide what to do about it. Report the scullion boy? To whom? To Veyra's inquisitors, who would have the child on the rack before dawn and might, in their questioning, ask how the taster had identified the poison so quickly, so certainly, from smell alone?

No. He would say nothing. He would do what he always did: survive, observe, keep his mouth shut and his ledger balanced.

And then he would go home and commit treason.

* * *

His shop sat at the crooked end of Tanner's Row, wedged between a chandler's and a house that had been empty so long the ivy had taken it over entirely. By day it sold pickled things and imported spices — legitimate goods, dull goods, the kind of inventory that invited no second glance. But Orsen lived above it, and in the back room above it, behind a false panel that smelled of cedar, he kept his real trade.

Charms.

Not the vulgar kind — not the painted trinkets festival-hawkers sold, all glitter and nothing else. Real ones. Small workings stitched from old knowledge: knots tied in the correct order with the correct words breathed over them, salt-charms for thresholds, fever-knots for children's beds, luck-bringers braided from red thread and rowan wood. All of it outlawed under the Edict of Ash these six years past, when Chancellor Veyra had declared magic a contagion and begun burning it out of the kingdom district by district. The edicts had come first — bans, registrations, confiscations. Then the bonfires. Then the quiet part, the part people whispered about and denied having heard: whole streets forgetting their own names. Districts where the oldest families woke one morning unable to recall their grandmothers' faces. Memory going out of the world like heat from an open door.

Orsen had never learned where his charm-craft came from. His mother, perhaps; his childhood was a haze of her hands tying knots over his cradle, murmuring words he'd half-forgotten. He told himself he sold charms only because he needed coin beyond a servant's wage, only because the work was easy for him and dangerous enough to be worth the risk. He told himself a great many things.

The knock came at the quarter hour past midnight — two knocks, a pause, one more. Malla.

She came in smelling of lye and other people's laundry, a broad, tired woman of fifty with knuckles like walnuts and eyes that had stopped expecting kindness anywhere but here. "Got my wages today," she said, without preamble, setting three coppers on the counter between them. "And my sister's boy is to sit the guild examination next month, and he's clever but he chokes, Orsen, he chokes every time, and if he fails again his father will—" She stopped. Swallowed. "You know what I'm asking."

"I know what you're asking." He took the coppers. Three coppers for a luck-work was robbery in his favor and they both knew it; she'd have paid ten. He went to the cedar panel and brought out a braid of red thread wound around a splinter of rowan, knotted seven times with seven breaths, each knot carrying a word in a language he did not know he knew until it left his mouth. "Tell him to wear it under his shirt. Tell him when his hands start shaking to press his thumb to the middle knot and breathe out slow. And tell him—" Orsen hesitated, then said it anyway, because it was true and true things were rare in his life, "tell him the shaking means he cares. The charm doesn't stop that. It just keeps it from drowning him."

Malla took the charm like it was communion. "You're a good man, doing this. Foolish, but good."

"Foolish covers most of it." He glanced at the shuttered window, at the dark street beyond it where the watch lanterns swung their slow arcs. Six years ago, selling this charm would have earned him a fine. Two years ago, a cell. Now, since the Edict of Ash had grown teeth, it earned fire. Not the clean fire of execution — the other fire, the one that took memories first, that left people standing in the ashes of themselves, smiling blankly while their neighbors wept at them like strangers. "You shouldn't come anymore, Malla. The Row's being watched. There are new faces at the Tanner's Arms, asking questions nobody asks."

"They'll be asking questions wherever I go." She tucked the charm away inside her bodice with the fierce tenderness of a woman hiding a child. "The whole kingdom's being watched, Orsen. Might as well be watched doing some good."

After she left he sat alone in the back room among his outlawed inventory, rolling the three coppers across his knuckles, listening to the henbane finally finish its passage through him — the sweetness souring, fading, filing itself away in whatever deep archive his body had become. He thought about the scullion boy. He thought about Veyra raising her glass. He thought, as he did every night, about how long this could last. Double lives were arithmetic; eventually every sum came due.

He was blowing out the lamp when he heard it.

A sound from the street below. Not footsteps — footsteps he knew, in all their varieties. This was smaller. Softer. The sound of someone very light standing very still directly outside his door.

Then, through the wood, thin as a thread drawn through cloth, a voice speaking words he should not have understood and understood completely:

*"Hlæfdige seocnesse, ic bringe þe giest. Se wulf geman gærs."*

*Lady of sickness, I bring you a guest. The wolf remembers the grass.*

Old Covenant tongue. A language sealed in archives no living scholar could open, a language the Edict of Ash had supposedly erased down to its last syllable — and it was standing on his doorstep at midnight, in the voice of someone very small, saying words that made every charm in his hidden room wake and shiver like leaves before a storm.

Orsen Whitlock stood in the dark of his own shop, hand frozen above the lamp, and felt his careful double life tilt beneath him like a floor giving way.
