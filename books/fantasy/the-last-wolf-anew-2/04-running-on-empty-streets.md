## 4. Running on Empty Streets

Orsen had always known the city by its names.

He could navigate the capital blindfolded, the way any man learns a thing he has walked since boyhood—by the crooked elbow of Tanner's Row, by the way Candlewick Street narrowed to a slit where two leaning houses nearly kissed above his head, by the smell of hot iron drifting from Anvil Court like a rumor of thunder. The city was a body and its streets were veins, and Orsen knew every pulse of it.

Which was why, at half past dawn on the third day of running, he stopped dead in the middle of a street that should not exist and felt the ground tilt beneath him like the deck of a ship.

"Where are we?" Wren asked. The child's voice was flat with exhaustion, but the eyes—those pale, wolf-colored eyes—were sharp as ever, scanning the shuttered windows on either side.

"I don't know." The admission came out of him like a tooth being pulled. "This should be Cooper's Lane. It runs between the dyers' yards and the tannery. I've bought madder root here a hundred times." He turned in a slow circle. The lane was narrow and cobbled, yes, and there was even a faded awning over a doorway that might once have been a dye-shop—but the sign above it read *Marrowgate*, in letters that looked old enough to have been carved by the first founders of the city. And there was no tannery at the end of the lane. There was a wall of blank gray stone, seamless, as if the street had simply decided to stop.

"It's the erasure," Wren said. "The Chancellor's work. When she takes a district's memory, the names go first." The child said it the way other children reported weather—matter-of-fact, faintly bored. "The city forgets what it called itself. Then it forgets what it was for."

Orsen stared at the child. Three days ago, this small figure had appeared on his doorstep speaking a dead language, and he had fled with it because the alternative was worse. He still did not entirely understand what Wren was. But he was learning, hour by hour, that understanding was going to arrive whether he welcomed it or not.

"You can feel it," he said. "The forgetting."

Wren nodded slowly. "It's like standing next to someone who's falling asleep. You can hear their thoughts getting slower. Softer." A pause, and then something almost shy crossed the small face. "The city used to sing. Did you know that? Before. All the covenant-stones in the foundations used to hum when the wolves ran the border. Now most of them are quiet. The ones near here are just—" the child's hand opened and closed, grasping for a word, "—*thinning*."

*Thinning*. Orsen filed the word away with all the others he'd been collecting: *sigil*, *vessel*, *covenant*, words that had lived only in sealed archives three days ago and now lived in his mouth, heavy as stones swallowed whole.

"We keep moving," he said. "South, past the river districts. There's a woman in Saltrow who owes me more than money, and more importantly, hates Veyra more than she fears her."

"Who?"

"A fence named Maren Ostler. She's moved contraband through this city for forty years—charms, relics, banned books. If anyone knows a road out that the Chancellor's hunters haven't thought to watch, it's her." He hesitated. "She also knows everything about everyone. Including, I suspect, me. Which is a risk. But we're out of roads that aren't risks."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate weighing evidence. Then: "You trust her?"

"No," Orsen said honestly. "I trust her hatred. It's older than her fear, and fear bends, but hatred holds its shape."

Something flickered across the child's face—approval, maybe, or recognition. "That's how wolves think," Wren said quietly, and fell into step beside him.

They walked south through a city coming apart at the seams of its own memory.

*

By midday they reached the district called Saltrow—or what had been Saltrow yesterday, or perhaps the day before. Orsen couldn't be certain anymore. The change came overnight, Wren had explained, while the city slept; a whole quarter's worth of names dissolved and reformed like frost on glass, and by morning the people who lived there remembered nothing different, because remembering required something to remember *with*, and that was exactly what Veyra was taking.

He saw it happen in miniature at a well in a square that had no name he recognized. Two women were drawing water, chatting idly, when one of them stopped mid-sentence, her bucket halfway up the rope. Her brow furrowed. Her lips moved silently, chasing a word that had just slipped away from her.

"What was I saying?" she asked her companion, laughing a little uncertainly. "I've lost the thread."

"You were talking about your son's apprenticeship," the second woman said. But even as she said it, her own face clouded. "Except—I can't recall which trade it was. Smithing? No—" Her hand rose to her temple. "There was a smith on this street. Wasn't there? Where did the smithy go?"

Orsen followed her gaze. Across the square stood a building he would have sworn yesterday had been a forge—he could almost smell the coal smoke of it—and today it was a chandler's shop, and had apparently always been a chandler's shop, and the women looked at it without surprise, only with a strange hollow ache neither of them could name, like a tongue probing the gap of a missing tooth.

Beside him, Wren made a small sound, low in the throat, involuntary. Grief, Orsen realized. The child was grieving, and grieving fluently, the way one grieves only for things deeply loved.

"They don't know what they're losing," Wren whispered. "That's the cruelest part. She isn't stealing anything they'd fight to keep. She's stealing the wanting itself." Pale eyes lifted to his face. "Is that why you help people? Because you remember what wanting feels like? Your charms—they're small wants. Luck. Safe births. Rain for crops." A pause. "You sell back to them the things she takes."

Orsen didn't answer right away. He was watching the two women at the well, who had resumed their chatter as if nothing had happened, buckets swinging, voices bright, and the brightness of it was somehow worse than weeping would have been.

"I sell charms because I'm a coward with a family to feed," he said finally. "But—" and here he surprised himself, because the true thing came out before the safe thing could stop it, "—when I make them, when I set the ash and the rowan and speak the little binding-words under my breath, it's the only time I feel like I'm doing what I'm *for*. Does that make sense?"

"Yes," said Wren, simply, and took his hand.

It was such a small thing. Such an ordinary, childish gesture. But Orsen's throat closed around nothing, and he gripped the small fingers hard, and they walked on together through the nameless square like that, hand in hand, fugitive and charge, thief and relic, and he understood with sudden terrible clarity that he would burn down every remaining memory in this city himself before he let Veyra's hands touch this child.

The thought frightened him more than the hunters did.

* * *

Maren Ostler kept her counting-house behind a false wall in the cellar of a funeral parlor—a joke so old and beloved that Maren had told it to Orsen twice already, both times with the same delighted cackle: *"Everyone brings me their dead valuables, dear, and no one ever thinks to look among the actual dead."*

She was smaller than he remembered, or the world had gotten larger around her. Seventy years of salt air and sharper dealing had folded her into a shape like a dried apple, all wrinkles and concentrated sweetness, with eyes the color of pewter that missed absolutely nothing. They missed nothing now. One look at Orsen, one at Wren, and her eyebrows climbed toward her wispy white hairline.

"Well," she said, latching the false wall behind them and lighting a single tallow candle, "the palace rat has grown a spine and sprouted a shadow. Sit down before you fall down, both of you. You look like something a cat dragged in and regretted."

"Maren—"

"I know why you're here. Everyone with ears knows the Chancellor's hounds are sniffing along the river road, and everyone with sense knows you're too clever to take it." She pressed a cup of something hot and bitter into his hands, another into Wren's, and settled onto a stool across from them, studying the child with an expression Orsen couldn't parse—wonder threaded through with something older and sadder. "Look at those eyes. My grandmother told stories about eyes like that. Said the last wolf-speaker died before the Edict, and the world got quieter and stupider the very same week." She snorted. "I thought it was just a story. We tell ourselves stories about the teeth of things that ate us, so we can pretend we won."

"Maren, please. Do you know a way out of the city?"

"I know several." She held up a knotted cord, six knots along its length, each one a route she'd smuggled goods and people through for four decades. Then, slowly, she began to untie them, one by one, letting each fall to the floor like shed skin. "But I'll tell you what I told the last courier who came through here, God rest her foolish courage. The knots are tied to names. And the names are dying." She gestured vaguely upward, toward the city above, toward the thinning. "Saltrow will be gone inside a week—maybe less now that you're sitting in it. Whatever door you leave by, leave by it *soon*."

"There's a hunter patrol on the river road," Orsen said. "Two at the ford, at least. Possibly a tracker."

"Then you'll need someone loud and slow and very visible standing in the wrong place, drawing every eye in the district." She said it lightly, the way she'd say *we're out of cloves, dear*, and Orsen heard it, truly heard it, a full three seconds before he wanted to.

"No," he said.

"Don't argue with an old woman, it's undignified for us both." She was already moving around the cellar, pulling a battered traveling cloak from a chest, pressing coins into his palm, tucking a paper packet of dried meat into Wren's pocket with brisk, rough tenderness. "There's a shrine in the fenlands, northeast, two days' walk—the Wolves' Rest, the old folk call it. Ruined now, mostly. But ruins are where forgotten things go to wait, and if anywhere in this kingdom still remembers how to open a door, it'll be there." She fixed him with her pewter eyes. "You take the child and you walk, and you don't look back, and if you ever get the chance to spit on Veyra's grave, you spit twice, once for me and once for everyone whose names she's chewed up."

"Maren." His voice cracked on the second syllable. "You're seventy years old. What do you imagine you're going to do against hunters?"

"What I've done my whole life, dear." She smiled, and it was the smile of a woman who had buried everyone she'd ever bargained with and found the practice useful. "Sell them something. In this case: a story. A fence fleeing with a stolen child, seen crossing the western bridge at dusk, headed for the coast. By the time they work out it's a lie, you'll be two days gone, and by the time they come back for *me*—" she shrugged, a small dry motion like leaves settling, "—well. I've had a good run. Forty years of selling back the things she takes. That's forty more than she gave anyone else."

Wren stepped forward. The child took Maren's wrinkled hand in both small ones, and spoke—not in the traders' tongue, but in that other language, the old one, the words flowing soft and liquid as water over stones. Maren went very still. Tears stood in her eyes without spilling, and when Wren finished, she laughed, wet and cracked.

"And what was that, then?"

"Blessing," Wren said. "Old kind. For keeping."

"Ha! First one I've ever earned honestly." She pushed them both toward the hidden door, and her voice hardened into the merchant again, brisk and final. "Northeast, through the fen, follow the standing stones where the fog lets you see them. Go *now*, before I lose my nerve and start haggling with death over the price."

At the threshold Orsen turned. "Maren—"

"Out." But her voice broke on it, and for one unguarded moment the old woman's face was naked with love and terror and fierce, furious pride. "Go be what you're for, Orsen Whitlock. Both of you."

Then the false wall closed, and they were alone in the dark of the tunnel, and somewhere above them, muffled by stone and earth, Orsen heard the first bells of the evening watch begin to ring.

He did not look back. Wren's small hand found his again in the darkness, sure as a compass needle.

"Northeast," the child whispered. "To the shrine. To what the wolves remember."

And far behind them, distantly, rising above the ordinary noise of a city forgetting itself, Orsen heard shouting—then the crackle of fire taking hold of a funeral parlor on a street that, by morning, would have no name at all.
