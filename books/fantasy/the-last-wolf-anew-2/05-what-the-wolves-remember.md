## 5. What the Wolves Remember

The shrine had no roof anymore, only ribs of it, black beams reaching up like the skeleton of something enormous that had lain down to die and been too sacred to bury. Wren felt the place before she saw it properly — a humming in her teeth, in the flat of her tongue, the way the sigil at her throat warmed when she crossed the threshold of what remained.

She stopped on the broken step and looked back at Orsen, who was breathing hard from the climb and pretending he wasn't.

"This is it," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she meant it to. She was still learning how big her own voice was allowed to be around him. "This is where they made it."

Orsen leaned against a pillar furred with moss, his coat still carrying the ash-smell of Maren's burning — three days now, and neither of them had washed it out, though Wren suspected for different reasons. His eyes went over the ruin the way he'd gone over the stew at the palace, she thought. Careful. Cataloguing. Looking for the poison hidden in the beauty.

"It's just stones," he said.

"No." Wren stepped inside, and the hum rose up through the soles of her boots into her shins, her hips, her heart, until she could feel it everywhere, the way you feel your own blood if you're very quiet. "It's not. It never was. It's a *mouth*, Orsen. It remembers out loud if you let it."

* * *

They had come north through the hill country for six days, following a road that sometimes wasn't there. That was the thing about the erasing — it didn't take the land itself, not usually. It took the *meaning* of the land. A bridge would stand whole and sound over a river, but nobody could say who built it or why it arched that particular way, and after a while the arching seemed like an accident. Villages kept their walls but forgot their founding songs. Graves stayed tended by habit long after anyone remembered whose bones slept under the wildflowers. The Chancellor called it cleansing, in the edicts. In the districts where it had already finished, people simply called it *before*, and used it as a word the way you'd use a wall: everything bad happened before; everything good began after.

Wren remembered all of it. That was the curse of being what she was. The covenant lived in her like marrow lives in bone — not an addition to her but a structure of her, holding her up, and every memory the covenant held, she held too. She had watched the erasure eat outward from Veyra's court for years of her short life, district by district, and she had learned to walk through forgetting country the way other children walked through tall grass: unbothered, because she carried the field inside her.

But Orsen didn't. Orsen flinched at gaps. When the road lost its name overnight — and it did lose its name, between one dusk and one dawn, the milestone by the crossroads standing blank as an egg — Orsen had stood there a long time with his hand flat on the stone, saying, "It was called Wolfmark Rise. I know it was called Wolfmark Rise," in a voice that got quieter each time, as though he were trying to convince the milestone rather than himself.

So she had brought him here. Because if the covenant was going to ask him for anything — and it was going to, soon, sooner than either of them wanted — he deserved to know what it was asking him to remember.

"Sit down," she said. "In the middle. There's a groove in the floor, see? People knelt there. Hundreds of years of people."

Orsen sat. He was good at sitting where he was told; she supposed that came from tasting other people's dinners for a living, from making yourself small and obedient in rooms full of power. But his hands weren't obedient. They kept moving — touching the hem of his coat, the charm-pouch at his belt, checking and rechecking the small illegal comforts he sold to laundresses and lamplighters, as though the whole world might be survived one luck-charm at a time.

"What happens now?" he asked.

"Now I show you." Wren knelt across from him, took off the wolf-tooth sigil, and set it in the groove between them. It was ugly work, honestly — a yellowed fang bound in tarnished wire — and she watched him try not to look disappointed. Everyone expected holy things to shine. Holy things mostly didn't. Holy things were old and tired and had been waiting a very long time for someone to arrive.

"Whatever you see," she said, "it isn't a dream, and it isn't a story I'm telling you. Try not to fight it. Fighting makes it worse. Maren taught me that." Her throat closed on the name. She pushed past it. "Hold the tooth."

He hesitated. Then — because he was Orsen, because some stubborn honest thing in him couldn't leave even a poisoned cup untasted — he picked it up.

* * *

The shrine opened like a mouth.

Wren had done this once before, alone, at seven years old, kneeling in this same groove with her grandmother's hands over hers, and she had wept until her ribs ached. Doing it for someone else was harder. She had to be the door *and* walk through it, holding both sides open while the centuries poured through, and she felt Orsen's mind beside hers the way you feel another swimmer in dark water — a pressure, a warmth, a startled thrashing that she gentled the only way she knew, by wrapping the oldest memories around him first, the kind ones, the ones worth drowning in.

She showed him the border.

Not the border of maps and customs men — the true one, the living one, a seam of silver light running along the edge of the human lands where the world thinned into forest and deeper forest, and beyond it the wolf-spirits moving like weather. They had not been beasts. She needed him to understand that, and she felt the understanding land in him with almost physical force: they had been *guardians*, vast and patient and hungry in the way winter is hungry, and the covenant had been the leash and the gift both. The wolves would hold the dark things back — the nameless things that pressed against the edges of the world before humans learned to build — and in exchange, humanity would feed them something rarer than meat.

Memory. Story. Attention. Every harvest-song sung at the boundary shrines, every child's tale that named the wolves kindly, every candle lit at dusk facing the treeline — all of it flowed outward along the silver seam like warmth off a hearth, and the wolf-spirits drank it and grew strong, and the border held.

And the tasters — Wren felt Orsen go rigid beside her — the tasters had been the hinge of it. Human mouths were the one gate nothing dark could pass through unseen. So the covenant-keepers trained men and women to taste everything: food, wine, smoke, spellwork, the air itself after certain moons. The First Taster stood at the king's shoulder at every feast, at every treaty-signing, at every rite, and nothing poisoned, cursed, or falsely sworn ever reached the throne. *The tongue is the smallest keyhole,* said the words carved above the shrine's lost altar, and Wren gave them to Orsen in the old tongue and then again in his own, and heard him breathe them back like a man learning a psalm in a language he'd spoken as an infant and forgotten.

*You were made for this,* the memories murmured around him, gentle as snowfall. *Your line was made for this.*

Then she stopped being gentle.

Because the covenant deserved honesty, and so did he, and because her grandmother had shown her the truth at seven years old without flinching and Wren refused to do less.

She showed him the betrayal.

A feast. A coronation feast, two hundred years ago, in a hall lit with ten thousand candles. And at the king's right hand, the Taster — young, sharp-eyed, quick-tongued, with a lean face and a nervous habit of touching his jaw —

Orsen's face.

Not similar. Not a family resemblance. *His.* Wren felt him recognize it before she felt anything else, felt the recognition go through him like cold iron through a fire, and she held the vision steady anyway, because mercy now would be cruelty later.

The Taster had grown tired of being a hinge. Tired of small portions, careful sips, a life measured out in mouthfuls. A sorcerer had come to him with gold and with flattery and with a vial — and the Taster, whose whole sacred office was to stand between the realm and poison, had let the vial pass. Had tasted it himself and pronounced it clean, in a clear voice, before the assembled court. The king died at his own coronation feast. The queen died three days later. And the border — starved of trust, the covenant's heart pierced by the one office designed to protect it — flickered like a guttering lamp, and the dark things noticed, and the first of the eras of forgetting began.

The wolves had not destroyed the traitor. That was the worst of it, the part that had made seven-year-old Wren sob into her grandmother's apron. They had *marked* him. Bound his face and his failure into the covenant's deepest memory, so that every keeper after would know him, so that the office of Taster would carry the weight forever — and so that, if a Taster ever came again willing to pay the debt, the debt could finally be paid.

*The tongue is the smallest keyhole,* the carved words said. *And every keyhole opens twice.*

* * *

The vision released them.

Orsen came back to himself all at once, the way a drowning man comes up, gasping, hands splayed on the shrine floor, the wolf-tooth rattling loose from his grip. Wren knelt across from him with blood on her lip — she always bit it, holding the door open — and watched him kneel there shaking, watching his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. As if they belonged to a ghost wearing his skin.

"That man," he said. His voice was wrecked. "That was —"

"Yes."

"I have his face."

"You *are* his face. His line, anyway. It comes down the blood like eye-color." She paused. "Like a debt."

"The covenant wants me." He wasn't asking. She watched him arrive at it, watch the whole architecture of his quiet double life — the charms, the tasting, the careful small treasons — reorganize itself around this new center of gravity. "It's been waiting. Two hundred years, and it's been waiting for a Whitlock to kneel in this exact groove and pick up this exact tooth."

"It doesn't want you punished." Wren leaned forward. This part mattered. This was the part she'd carried alone through every mile of empty road, and she needed him to hear it clean. "Orsen. Listen to me. The wolves don't punish. They're not men. What they want is *balance restored*. A Taster broke the covenant from the inside, so a Taster reforges it from the inside. That's all. That's the shape of it."

"And what does reforging cost?"

The question dropped between them like a stone into a well, and Wren heard it falling and falling and not landing, and she hated herself for what she did next, which was look away. Because she knew. Grandmother had known. The covenant had whispered its price to Wren in dreams since she was old enough to dream, and she had not said it aloud yet, not to Maren who died for her, not to anyone, because saying it would make it real and because she was selfish, God forgive her, she was nine years old and she was selfish.

"Wren." Orsen's hand came into her view, steadying on her knee. Even hollowed out by ghosts, even two hundred years of inherited guilt sitting on him like wet wool, his first instinct was to steady *her*. "What does it cost?"

She never answered. None of them ever got the chance to answer.

From down the hillside, thin on the wind, came the sound that had emptied half the districts of the kingdom: bells. Not temple bells. Hunter-bells, silver-chased, tuned to ring wrong in the ears of anyone carrying covenant-blood. And beneath them, fainter but closer than any bell had any right to be, hoofbeats on the north road.

Orsen was on his feet before the second peal, pulling her up, pocketing the tooth, and Wren saw the change in him — saw the guilt and vertigo compress, in the space of three heartbeats, into something harder and more useful.

"They found us," she said.

"No." He drew her toward the shrine's rear wall, where the old processional path ran down into the trees, and his voice was calm in the way she'd begun to learn was his most dangerous register. "Veyra found us. And she's about to learn what I taste like when I'm angry."
