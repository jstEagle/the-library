## 6. The Price of Immunity

The healer's cottage sat at the crooked end of a lane that had forgotten its own name. Orsen noticed it the way he noticed everything now—the way a man notices weather in an open wound. Three days since the shrine, two since they'd crossed into the hill-country where the erasure hadn't yet finished its work, and still the world felt provisional to him, like a stew someone might yet salt with henbane.

Maren Ashfoot was old the way riverbeds are old: worn smooth, impossible to dislodge. She'd been a covenant-healer once, before the Edict, and her hands smelled of yarrow and cold iron as she turned them over Wren's small wrists, reading whatever she read there.

"Sit," she told Orsen, without looking up. "You've been carrying that boy through the dark for days and you're holding yourself like a man who's swallowed something and is waiting to find out what."

"I know what I've swallowed," Orsen said. "Three years of it. Every poison the palace kitchens ever feared."

Maren's hands went still above Wren's wrists.

"Say that again," she said quietly. "Slowly."

So he did. He told her about the tasting—how a food-taster's work was not merely to die nobly if the king's supper was spoiled, but to *know*, to catalog, to build a body's memory of every venom and hex that might come disguised as sauce or wine or bread. How the palace physicians dosed him deliberately, small measured horrors, building his tolerance like a mason builds a wall, brick by brick, until nothing common could touch him. How he'd never thought of it as anything but survival. A trade. A tongue trained to betray.

"And the charms," Maren said. It wasn't a question. Word traveled fast among those who still remembered; the hills were full of such people now, hiding like embers in ash.

"Luck-knots. Warding-salt. Small things." He shrugged, and hated how small the shrug sounded. "I sold them because tasting pays poorly and dying pays worse."

Wren, curled on the settle by the fire with a bowl of barley broth balanced on their knees, watched him with those amber eyes that caught lamplight like an animal's. They said nothing. They had said very little since the ford was only a rumor behind them—but their silence had changed quality lately. It watched him. Weighed him.

Maren crossed the room, took Orsen's jaw in her grip with the impersonal firmness of a woman who had set broken bones all her life, and tilted his face toward the firelight.

"Open your mouth."

He did. She peered inside for a long moment, then pressed two fingers beneath his tongue, and something in her expression went strange—not horror, exactly. More like awe arriving late to a funeral.

"Do you know what you are?" she whispered.

"A taster," Orsen said. "A smuggler of luck-charms. A tired man."

"You're a *vessel*." She released him and stepped back, wiping her fingers on her apron as though they'd touched something holy. "Every poison you ever took, every hex you tasted and survived—it didn't pass through you. It *stayed*. Your body learned each one and kept the lesson. Do you understand? You are not immune the way a wall is thick. You are immune the way a book is full." Her voice dropped. "You are an archive, Orsen Whitlock. Three years of deliberate ruin, written down in flesh. There hasn't been a vessel like you since the covenant broke."

The fire popped. Wren had stopped eating. The bowl trembled faintly on their knees, and their eyes were fixed on Orsen with an intensity that made the room feel suddenly too warm.

"What does that mean," Orsen said slowly, "for the child?"

"It means," Maren said, "that the rite can be done."

She moved to the hearth, lifted down an iron kettle black with age, and began measuring herbs into it from jars whose labels were scratched in the Old Tongue. She talked while she worked, the way healers do when the news is bad and they need their hands busy.

"The covenant was not a treaty, whatever the archives call it. It was a marriage. The wolf-spirits gave the realm their vigilance at every border and threshold, and the realm gave them a vessel—a human body that could hold their power without burning, because it had already learned to hold poison, which is power in its cruelest form. The last keeper failed—" Maren's hands paused, just slightly, over the kettle. "The last keeper *chose* otherwise, and the bond frayed, and everything since has been the slow unraveling you've been running through. Districts forgetting themselves. Wolves vanishing from the borderlands. Magic going out of the world like heat out of an open door."

"Veyra," Orsen said.

"Chancellor Veyra." Maren's mouth twisted around the title. "Once, she had another name. But yes. She has been drinking from the broken covenant for longer than your kingdom has had its name, and every drop she takes makes the reforging harder. Because the rite needs a vessel that can hold *everything*—the whole of the old power, undiluted—and survive the pouring."

"And I can."

"You are the only one who can." Maren turned to face him fully, and her eyes were terrible with kindness. "But hear the price before you reach for it, taster. The rite does not take blood. It doesn't take years off your life or strength from your arms. Those would be mercies." She came close again and laid her palm flat against his chest, over the heart. "The rite burns as fuel whatever the vessel values most. That is the old law. Power must be paid for in love, or it curdles into what Veyra has become. The covenant will reach into you and take the thing you cannot bear to lose, and it will not ask permission, and it will not give it back."

The words settled over the room like snowfall. Wren had set the bowl aside entirely. Their small hands were fists in their lap.

Orsen thought: *I value my tongue. My craft. My life.* And knew, even as he thought it, that he was lying the way men lie when the truth is standing right in front of them, small and thin and watching them with wolf-amber eyes.

He thought about the door that should not have opened. About a child speaking a dead language on his threshold, and how some stubborn animal part of him had known—even in dread, even in fear—that he would not turn them away. He thought about teaching Wren which mushrooms lied. About the way the child hummed when they walked, tuneless little songs in the Old Tongue, and how he had begun, without noticing, to walk in time.

"How long do I have to decide?" he asked.

"You misunderstand," Maren said gently. "There is no deciding later. The hunters are already in the valley—I heard their horns at dawn, and they ride with the Chancellor's scent-hounds, which are not dogs at all anymore. If they take you, Veyra will hollow you out for parts and use what's left to keep herself alive another century. So either you run until the world forgets you—and it is learning to forget very well—or you stand at the old stone circle on the ridge tomorrow night, when the moon is dark, and you let the covenant pour itself into you." She held his gaze. "You choose between losing everything by running, or losing the one thing by staying."

* * *

They spent the day preparing, and it was the strangest day of Orsen's life: ordinary tasks performed under the shadow of an ending. Maren ground herbs. Wren slept in snatches, exhausted from the visions the shrine had wrung out of them, waking once to ask in a cracked voice whether the wolves had really been beautiful, and Orsen said they had, and the child smiled and slept again.

In the afternoon, Orsen did the one thing he knew how to do. He made charms.

He had no silver thread and no proper knotwork cord, so he used what the cottage offered—flax twine, a bent nail from the doorframe, dried rowan berries gone soft as old promises. His fingers remembered their work even when his mind wandered. Loop, cross, cinch. He made a warding-charm against hounds. Another against sleep-hexes. A third he didn't name, because naming it would have meant admitting what it was for, and what it was for was *after*—a charm against forgetting, which was a foolish thing to make, because no charm he knew could hold back what the rite would take.

He made it anyway. He strung it on leather and kept it in his pocket, and told himself it was for Wren.

Near dusk, Wren appeared beside him at the table, silent as falling snow, and set something down next to his half-finished knots. A strip of bark, peeled smooth, marked with charcoal letters in the Old Tongue.

"What's this?"

"My name," Wren said. Their voice was still hoarse, still rationed like water in a drought. "My true one. The one my mother gave me before—" They stopped. Started again. "Before the last village. If the rite takes your memories, you'll lose this too. You'll lose me. But if you carry the word itself, somewhere outside your head—" They pushed the bark toward him, chin trembling with the effort of composure. "Maybe a piece stays."

Orsen looked at the charcoal marks. He couldn't read them—he'd never learned, though after the shrine he suspected the knowledge lived somewhere in him now, waiting like a seed under snow. He looked instead at the child who had carried this scrap through burning villages and erased districts, guarding it the way other children guarded toys or talismans, except that it was neither. It was a rope thrown to a drowning man.

"Wren," he said aloud, testing it. "Your mother named you for the smallest bird that sings all winter."

Something broke open in the child's face—not tears, quite, but the place tears come from. "You remember."

"I remember," Orsen said, "everything about you. That's rather the problem."

He tucked the bark into his pocket beside the unnamed charm, and Wren leaned against his side, brief as a struck match, and then went to help Maren with the kettle, humming their tuneless winter song.

* * *

The horns came an hour after full dark.

Not the hunting-horns of mortal riders—these sounded wrong, notes bending in the ear like light through water, and beneath them came a baying that raised every hair on Orsen's arms. The scent-hounds. Whatever Veyra had made of them, they were closing fast through the valley woods, and Maren stood in her doorway with a lantern raised, listening, her face carved from old grief.

"They followed the healer's-road," she said. "Someone talked. It doesn't matter now. Taster—you have until midnight, perhaps less. The stone circle is two hours' climb. Can you make it carrying the child?"

Orsen was already moving. He wrapped the warding-charms around Wren's neck and wrist, stuffed the bark strip deep into his boot where it lay against his skin like a coal. He took down the last of Maren's herb-bread, wrapped it in cloth, thought absurdly of provisions, of practical things, because the alternative was to think about what waited at the top of the ridge—about reaching into his own chest and finding out, once and forever, what the covenant judged most precious in him.

He already knew. He had known since the shrine, since the visions, since a child spoke a dead language on his doorstep and he opened the door.

"Orsen." Maren caught his arm at the threshold. In the lantern light her eyes were wet and fierce. "When the power comes, it will offer you bargains. It will show you doors. Veyra took every door she was offered—that's what unmade her. Promise me you'll refuse them all."

"I promise," he said, and meant it, and wondered how many promises he'd be able to keep.

Then the first hound broke the treeline at the bottom of the garden, and it was not a dog—it was a shape of smoke and hunger wearing a hound's memory—and Orsen swept Wren onto his back and ran for the ridge with the dark moon rising ahead of him and the rite waiting like an open mouth.

Behind them, Maren's lantern flared green with old covenant-light, buying seconds with the last magic she owned.

Above them, on the summit, the standing stones began, very faintly, to sing.
