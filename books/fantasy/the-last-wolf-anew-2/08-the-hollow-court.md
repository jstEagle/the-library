## 8. The Hollow Court

The rite-chamber had been a chapel once. Veyra remembered when it was built—remembered the masons singing as they set the wolf-tooth keystone above the door, remembered the smell of cut granite and beeswax and the particular hush that fell over a room where two peoples had sworn to guard one another until the mountains wore down to gravel.

Now the keystone was pried out and locked in her vault, and the chamber smelled of nothing at all. That was the first sign of what she'd done, all those years ago: a room stripped of its memory held no scent. She had made herself the keeper of a house with no smell in it, and she had been alone inside it for longer than any of them could imagine.

Longer than they *could* imagine. That was the cruelty no one warned her about. Mortals thought eternity was a gift. Eternity was a corridor with no doors, walked at a pace so slow you forgot you were moving, until one day you looked back and the person who had entered the corridor was a stranger whose face you recognized only because you still wore it.

Veyra stood at the center of the chamber, barefoot on cold stone, and unbound her hair for the first time in sixty years. It fell silver-white, though she had been dark-haired when she took the office. The covenant's power did that—burned through a body color by color, warmth by warmth, until what remained was a wick dressed in fine cloth.

She arranged the sigil on the obsidian table. The wolf-tooth charm the taster had carried glowed there like an ember refusing to die, and around it she laid her instruments: seven silver needles, a bowl of water drawn from the old shrine-well before she'd burned the shrine, and the ledger.

Ah, the ledger. Her most honest possession.

She opened it to the first page, written in her own hand four hundred years ago, when her ink had been brown and her letters round with youth:

*I, Veyra of the Ashen Court, last keeper of the Covenant of Teeth and Bread, do swear to hold the bond between wolf and man until a new vessel is found. I will not use it. I will not spend it. I will keep it whole.*

Below that, entries. The first fifty years were careful, penitential, almost devotional: *Withheld. Withheld. Withheld.* Then a famine came—the Year of Gray Wheat—and she had drawn a single thread of the covenant's strength to keep three villages from starving. A small spending. A merciful one.

*Spent: one thread. Reason: children starving in Harrowfen.*

The entry after that came sooner. *Spent: one thread. Reason: plague at the river towns.* Then: *Spent. Reason: war.* Then: *Spent,* with no reason recorded, because by then the reasons had stopped being reasons and started being habits.

She turned pages with a scholar's tenderness. Here was the century she had spent the covenant's power to buy her first extension of life—not to save anyone, just to stay. To keep watching. Because someone had to watch, she told herself then. Because if she died, the bond would die unguarded, and the wolves would fade, and the borders would rot open, and everything her sisters had sealed with their blood would come apart.

Someone had to hold it. And holding it meant using it, because unused power pooled and soured, because a kept thing left in the dark grew teeth, because—

Because she had been afraid, and fear was patient, and patience was the one thing eternity gave you in abundance.

The middle pages blurred. Entries in three different hands—hers, hers, hers—recording the slow conversion of guardianship into ownership. She had not fallen. Falling was quick; falling had dignity. She had *settled*, the way silt settles in a clear stream, grain by grain, until one day the water ran thick and no one could say when it had happened.

The last page held only one line, recent, pressed hard enough to tear:

*Found: a vessel. A child. Spend everything. Finish it.*

"Finish it," she said aloud, and the chamber drank her voice without echo.

That was the truth she had never spoken to any living soul: this was never theft. Theft implied the thing stolen belonged to another. But the covenant had belonged to *her*, first and longest, and she had merely decided—if decision it could be called, after so long alone deciding everything, answerable to no one, her judgment the only altar left standing—that the bond should end the way it began: in a human body, willingly offered.

Except the offering had to be willing. That was the flaw in her design, the splinter in four hundred years of careful work. Power freely given could be reforged; power taken rotted in the taking. So she needed the child alive and consenting. She needed the vessel—a living archive of every poison and ward and binding he had ever swallowed—to hold the shape of the rite. And she needed them both brought gently to the table, which was why she had offered Whitlock kindness instead of chains.

Kindness. She almost laughed. She had been kind once, genuinely, and it had cost her everything except time.

* * *

They brought the taster up from the cells at dusk, and Veyra watched him through the viewing glass with something uncomfortably close to respect.

Orsen Whitlock looked terrible—three days in a stone room will do that—but his eyes moved the way they had moved across her banquet hall the night he detected henbane in the king's stew: cataloguing, weighing, tasting the air itself. He did not know yet what he was. That was the tragedy of archives; they rarely knew their own contents.

His cell was not cruel. She had seen to that. Good straw, a blanket, bread that contained no poison but also no kindness—he would taste the difference; men like him always did. And on the walls, invisible to any eye but a tongue's, she had painted her wards in solutions of bitter aloes and ground moonmetal and one reagent she had distilled herself from a memory she could not otherwise bear to keep.

He sat on the straw for a long time, doing nothing. Then, very quietly, he wet his finger and touched the wall.

Veyra leaned closer to the glass.

He touched the wall again, higher. Touched the floor near the door seam. Ran his tongue along his own wrist, thoughtful, cross-referencing—she could see the habit of it, the taster's discipline, the way a man who has spent years distinguishing arsenic from lead by mouth begins to read the world as a menu of hidden things.

He couldn't see her wards. No one could. But he could *taste* them, layer by layer, the way he'd once tasted a stew and named every herb in it blindfolded.

"Remarkable," she murmured.

The aloes layer went first. She felt it dissolve—not broken, *understood*, the way a lock yields to a key cut from its own shadow. He was reading each ward, learning its composition, and then doing something she had not anticipated: matching it against himself. Against the archive. Three years of royal poisons, of protective draughts slipped into his wine by suspicious ministers, of spell-binders disguised as seasonings—every one of them lodged in his blood like books on a shelf.

Her wards were spells. Spells were, in the end, only very refined poison.

He found the moonmetal next. This one cost him—she watched him sway, watched him press his knuckles to his mouth as though holding something down—but he did not stop. There was a stubbornness in him that reminded her, horribly, of a girl who had once stood in this same chamber swearing an oath with ink-brown letters and a round young hand.

*Do not think of her. Think of the work.*

The seventh ward was the memory-distillate, and she had saved it for last precisely because it was cruel. It tasted of grief. Anyone who licked it would taste her worst century—the winter she had spent the covenant's power to erase a district rather than let it remember what she'd become, the faces she'd unmaken, the street names that changed overnight because she could not bear her own name reflected in them. She had put her shame on the walls of a prison cell as armor, believing no one would drink it.

Whitlock drank it.

He sat very still afterward, longer than he had for any other layer. His shoulders shook once. Whatever he tasted of her, he tasted all of it, and Veyra found herself gripping the viewing frame hard enough to whiten her knuckles, exposed in a way she had not been since the oath.

Then he wiped his mouth, and went back to work.

"He'll have the outer ring down by morning," reported Captain Serris from the doorway, uneasy. "Shall we move him? Re-ward the cell?"

"No." Veyra did not turn from the glass. "Let him work. Every ward he tastes teaches me how deep his archive goes. By the time he reaches the true seal, I'll know whether he can carry the rite—or whether he'll burn."

"And if he burns?"

"Then I have waited four hundred years." She said it lightly, and hated how heavy it sounded. "I can wait one more week."

But that was a lie, and she was too old to lie well even to herself. The covenant's residue in the sigil was guttering. She could feel it through the obsidian table like a heartbeat going thin. If the rite did not begin within days, the last thread of the old bond would fray past saving, and four centuries of solitary keeping would have been for nothing—would have been *worse* than nothing, would have been the slow murder of everything she'd promised to protect.

The clock in her chest, silent for four hundred years, had begun to tick.

* * *

Near midnight she descended to the chamber beneath the rite-room, where the child slept under wards of woven sleep.

Wren lay small and slight on the cot, the wolf-tooth sigil chained to one wrist, breathing in the slow rhythm of enchanted dreams. Even asleep, the boy's eyes moved beneath his lids as if tracking something running through tall grass. Veyra stood over him a long moment.

"I knew your grandmother's grandmother," she told the sleeping child, softly. "Before your people went to the shrines. Before mine went to the court. We were friends, once—all of us, wolves and keepers. You'd have liked the world then. It had a smell."

The child stirred. In sleep, half-dreaming, he answered her—in Old Covenant tongue, the liquid syllables that had not been spoken aloud in a living voice since she herself had been young enough to speak them without shame.

Veyra understood every word.

*You're the one who took the names off the streets,* the dreaming boy said. *Why did you take the names?*

She had no answer that would fit in a language built for honesty. She reached down and, with great care, adjusted the blanket over his shoulder—an absurd, useless tenderness, the gesture of a woman straightening flowers on a grave she had dug herself.

Above her, faint through six floors of stone, she heard a sound like a held breath releasing.

The seventh ward had fallen. Whitlock stood free in his unlocked cell, tasting the open air—and instead of fleeing, instead of shouting, he had gone still, listening, waiting. For the child. Of course. Everything in the man bent toward the child now, the way iron bends toward a lodestone.

Veyra closed her eyes. "So it begins," she said to the empty dark, to the corridor without doors, to the girl with brown ink and a round young hand who had sworn to hold and not to spend.

Somewhere below, soft as snowfall, Orsen Whitlock started walking toward the child—and Veyra rang the bell that woke her hunters, knowing, with the terrible clarity of four hundred years, that whatever came next would be the last page of her ledger, and that she had already written the ending long ago.
