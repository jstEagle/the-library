## 9. The Reforging

Wren had learned the palace the way wolves learn forests: by smell, by patience, by the memory of paths no one else could see. The wards Chancellor Veyra had woven over the dungeons were old covenant-work, twisted to her purpose, and they sang in a frequency only Wren's blood could hear. Three notes. A rising minor third, then a fall—the same phrase the shrine-keepers had used to call the border-wolves home at dusk.

She pressed her palm against the cold iron of Orsen's cell door and hummed the phrase backward.

The lock did not open so much as *forget* it had ever been locked. Iron slumped into rust and rust into red dust, and dust drifted through her fingers like something ashamed of itself. Beyond it, in the dark, she heard him breathing—shallow, deliberate, the breathing of a man who had spent three years learning exactly how much of any given substance his body could survive.

"Orsen." Her voice cracked on the second syllable. She was eleven years old and had been a wolf and a girl and something older than both for longer than that, and still her voice broke. "It's me."

He came forward out of the dark with the unhurried grace of a man who had already decided this might be a trick. His eyes found hers—found the wolf-gold in them—and something moved across his face like weather.

"They took my coat," he said. "And my knives. And apparently my dignity." He looked down at himself: prison linen, bare feet. "You shouldn't have come."

"You're welcome," Wren said.

"I mean it, Wren. What she's doing up there—" He stopped. He'd tasted the air of this place; she saw him doing it now, the way he did everything, reading the world through his tongue. "The whole palace is steeped in it. Concentrated covenant. Enough power to unmake a kingdom or remake one. If I walk into that room, I'm not walking out the man you knew."

"You were never the man I knew," Wren said. "You were the man before that too. I *showed* you."

In the shrine, weeks ago—or a lifetime ago—she had poured the visions into him: the original covenant sealed in blood and honey, the wolves who had worn the borders of the realm like a mantle, and the taster whose face was Orsen's face, kneeling before the first Chancellor with a cup in his hands. Betrayal wore different clothes in every century, but it always knelt the same way.

"I remember," Orsen said quietly. "I've been trying very hard not to."

"Good." She held out her hand. Small, human, five fingers, nails bitten. "Then come remember some more. Upstairs. Where it counts."

* * *

They climbed through the palace's forgotten throat—a servants' stair so old its steps bowed in the middle, walls furred with moss that remembered being sacred. Wren led. Orsen followed, cataloguing poisons out of habit: nightshade bloom in the wall-cracks, henbane pollen drifting from the state apartments above, and beneath it all something that was not a poison at all but frightened him more than any of them—the clean, green smell of living magic, hoarded until it had begun to rot in its keeping.

Veyra had been drinking from the covenant for three hundred years. No one could hold that alone. Not even her.

Especially not her.

They came up into light.

The great hall had been stripped of everything human. Tapestries gone, banners gone, the long banquet table chopped into firewood that hadn't even been burned—just abandoned, as though feasting were a superstition she'd outgrown. In the center of the floor stood the basin: black iron, wide as a wagon wheel, filled to the brim with liquid light. It moved like mercury and smelled like rain on stone that had never known a roof. The concentrated covenant. Every drop of stolen power Veyra had drained from district after district, from memory after memory, distilled into one drinkable ocean.

And above it, arms raised, robes hanging off a body grown too light for them, stood Chancellor Veyra.

She turned before they made a sound. Of course she did. You did not keep a kingdom for three centuries without hearing footsteps.

"The taster," she said, and smiled, and it was the smile from the interrogation room, warm as a hearth, terrible as a hearth. "And the little vessel. Come to watch. Good. Witnesses make rites *real*."

"Veyra." Orsen stepped forward, and Wren's heart lurched, because he was walking toward the basin, toward her, unarmed. "I know what you were. Wren showed me. You kept the covenant when everyone else forgot. Centuries alone, holding the border, and no one thanked you, no one even *remembered*—"

"Do not," Veyra said softly, "presume to pity me."

"—and it hollowed you," Orsen went on, relentless, the way he was relentless about arsenic and belladonna, tasting the truth of things because someone had to. "You didn't steal the covenant because you were evil. You stole it because you were *lonely*, and power was the only companion that stayed."

For one moment—one heartbeat—the Chancellor of the Realm looked like a woman standing in an empty house she had built with her own hands.

Then she moved, and the hall moved with her. Light ripped off the surface of the basin and became chains, became spears, became a cage of dawn around Wren before she could take a second step. She felt the covenant's weight press down on her—not attacking her, worse than that, *claiming* her, trying to pour itself back into the vessel it had been leaking out of for three hundred years.

"WREN!" Orsen's shout. Then, closer, low and fast, at her ear through the bars of light: "*Listen to me. The words. Say them with me when I give the sign.*"

"What sign?"

"You'll know." His hand found hers through the cage, brief and burning. "Whatever happens—whatever I do—you say the words. Promise me."

"I promise," Wren said, and meant it the way wolves mean things: entirely, and forever, and without understanding all the consequences.

* * *

Orsen walked to the basin.

Every step cost him. Not fear—he had long ago made an arrangement with fear, the way he had with poison, taking small doses daily until it lost its grip. What cost him was knowing. He was a taster; knowledge was his profession. And he knew exactly what he was about to do.

The healer in the fen-country had told him plainly: *You are an archive, Whitlock. Three years of the king's plate, every toxin, every counter-charm, every spelled vintage—your body has memorized them all. You are the only living thing that can hold the reforged covenant without breaking.* And then, quieter, the way healers say the true things: *But a rite needs fuel. And the covenant will take what you value most.*

He had pretended not to understand. He had lied to himself for weeks, the last lie of his life, and now there were no lies left, only the basin and the woman above it and the child behind him saying the words under her breath, getting them ready, *sila ven atha mori*, bind and keep and return—

"Step away from it, Veyra."

"Make me, little taster." She descended from her platform of light. This close, he could see what three hundred years of solitary power had done: she was translucent in places, like glass held up to a window, as if she had drunk so much of the covenant that she was becoming it, and the covenant was becoming nothing at all. "You'll take a sip and burn from the inside. I've watched stronger vessels than you try."

"I won't sip." Orsen gripped the basin's rim. The metal was warm. Alive. It knew him—it had always known him, the archive and the vessel recognizing each other across three years and thirty centuries. "I was trained never to sip. You test the whole draught, or you serve nothing at all."

And he lifted the covenant, all of it, oceans of it, three hundred years of theft in a single bowl—

—and drank.

* * *

It began, as all his endings did, with taste.

Honey first—the sealing-honey of the original rite, sweet and ancient, and underneath it blood, copper-bright, and underneath *that* every poison he had ever swallowed rising in him like a choir finding its note. Henbane and nightshade, arsenic and ash, the luck-charms and the death-charms, three years of a double life dissolving into raw material.

The covenant took it all. And then it asked for its price.

It started small. The smell of the laundress's kitchen where he'd sold his first charm. Gone. Then the name of the fence who had died buying them time in the changing streets—he reached for her name and found a hole shaped exactly like it. The road out of the city. The shrine. The visions. The gold eyes.

*No,* he thought, and drank deeper, deliberately, tilting the basin toward the covenant like a man finishing a bitter medicine. *Take it. Take all of it. That's what it's for.*

Because he understood now, in the last clear moment he would ever have: the rite did not run on power. Power was just the currency. It ran on *value*. And there was nothing in him worth more than—

A small figure. Wolf-eyed. Standing at a door that should not have opened. Teaching him Old Tongue words by firelight, laughing at his accent. *Sila ven atha mori*, she'd said. Bind and keep and return. Say it wrong again and I'll bite you.

Every memory of her, offered up like coins from a dead man's pocket.

He spoke the words as the memories left—spoke them in her voice because it was the only voice he had left for them, each syllable purchased with something he could feel sliding away and could not hold onto:

"*Sila ven atha mori. Sila ven—" (the shrine, the wolves, her hand in his) "—atha—" (her name, her actual name, gone, a door closing) "—mori.*"

Bind and keep and return.

Behind him, a child's voice joined his, thin and fierce and perfect, and where their voices crossed, the world healed.

* * *

Light did not explode. Light *returned*—pouring out of the basin, out of Orsen, out of the walls themselves, filling the hollow places in the palace the way water fills a dry riverbed. Wren felt it pass through her, through her vessel-blood, out into the world: the borders remembering themselves, the districts' names growing back like grass, every erased street and forgotten song reasserting its right to exist.

Veyra screamed.

Not in pain. In refusal. The restored covenant rose around her like a tide, offering itself, asking only what it asks of all keepers: *share it, carry it together, let it flow through many hands.* And Veyra—who had held it alone for three hundred years, who had fed on it alone, who had become a glass woman full of borrowed light—could not let go.

Wren watched her choose.

Watched her clutch the returning flood to herself the way a drowning woman clutches water, watched the covenant try to honor her, to make room for her, keeper-of-old, first-faithful—and watched her refuse every open hand. Her translucence brightened, whitened, thinned. For a moment she looked young, the way she must have looked at the first sealing: a tired guardian finally being relieved of her watch.

"I kept it," Veyra whispered. "All this time. I *kept it*."

"Yes," said Wren, who was suddenly sure the covenant could hear. "Now let someone else carry it."

Veyra smiled—that hearth-warm, hearth-terrible smile—and dissolved into the bond rather than release her grip on it. She came apart into light the way sugar comes apart into tea, willingly in the end, and the covenant folded her in, and somewhere in the vast restored weaving of the world a knot that had been strangling the thread for three centuries simply... loosened.

The basin stood empty. The light settled into the stones, into the soil, into the air of a kingdom exhaling after three hundred years.

And in the middle of the hall, on his knees beside a dry iron bowl, sat a royal food-taster who had just saved two realms and did not know why he was crying.

* * *

Orsen wiped his face with the back of his hand and frowned at the wetness there, puzzled, the way he'd be puzzled by an unfamiliar taste in the king's stew.

Around him the hall was full of morning. Real morning, coming through windows he didn't remember being so tall. Somewhere a child was speaking to him—in the common tongue now, oddly, though something about her cadence scratched at a place behind his eyes like a word on the tip of the tongue.

"Are you hurt?" she was asking. "Can you stand? Do you—do you know who I am?"

He looked at her. Small. Solemn. Eyes the color of banked gold.

Something in his chest turned over, heavy and aching, like a key turning in a lock whose purpose had been lost.

"No," Orsen said honestly, and held out his hand anyway, because some things survive the forgetting. "Should I?"
