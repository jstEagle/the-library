## 10. Small Charms

The bell above the door of Whitlock's Charms & Remedies had a crack in it, so it rang a half-tone flat, and Orsen had come to love that imperfection the way a man loves a scar that proves he survived something.

The shop occupied the ground floor of a narrow house on Candlemaker's Row, three doors down from where Marda the laundress had once bought luck-charms in whispers. Now she came openly, twice a month, to restock her household wards and complain about her sister-in-law over cups of Orsen's willowbark tea. Now everyone came openly. The Edict of Ash was ash itself, repealed by a regency council that still wasn't entirely sure what had happened at the palace on the night the sky over the capital had turned the color of moonlight on snow.

Orsen knew. He simply couldn't remember why he'd been there.

That was the shape of his life now: knowledge without memory, like reading a letter written in his own hand but posted years ago.

He arranged the morning's wares with the ritual care of long habit. Luck-charms in the left tray—wolf-tooth sigils carved from riverbone now, since real wolf-teeth were nearly impossible to find, the wolves having returned to the borderlands they'd once guarded and shown no interest in leaving again. Warding knots in the center. Tinctures along the back shelf, each labeled in his own careful hand. And in a locked case near the register, the one thing in the shop he never sold: a small wooden figure of a bird, its wings half-spread, that he had found in his pocket the night everything changed and could not bring himself to part with or explain.

He didn't know why it made his chest ache. He only knew that it did, reliably, every time he looked at it, the way an old wound aches before rain.

The morning trade came and went. A bricklayer bought a charm against falling—a good seller, these days, since the masons' guild had resumed work on the cathedral spire. Two children pooled their coins for a charm that supposedly made vegetables taste like honey. It didn't, quite, but Orsen charged them fairly and told them the magic worked better if they believed, which was true of most magic and most things.

His tongue still worked. That was the strange mercy underneath everything else. He tasted a customer's dropped spoon of elderflower cordial and identified the trace of copper from a poorly cleaned kettle; he sampled a new supplier's honey and named the meadow it came from within a district. The poisons he'd catalogued over three years at the king's table still lived in him like old soldiers, standing at attention, reporting for duty. Henbane, arsenic, water hemlock, the Chancellor's own distilled patience—all of it filed away in a body that remembered perfectly what his mind had surrendered.

*The rite consumes what you value most,* the healer had told him, months and a lifetime ago.

He had valued something enormously, then. He could feel the absence of it the way you feel a missing tooth—the tongue returning again and again to the gap, unable to leave it alone.

* * *

In the afternoon, Marda stayed past her purchase, wrapping her hands around her tea and studying him with the frank concern of an old friend.

"You did it again," she said.

"Did what?"

"Went somewhere. Mid-sentence. Your eyes went all—" She waved a floury hand. "Distant. Like a door opening onto a cold room."

Orsen laughed, because the alternative was not laughing. "I'm told I do that."

"Who tells you?"

"The healer. The council scribes who took my statement." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "They say I was instrumental in ending the Chancellor's reign. They say there was a child involved. They say I asked them—begged them—not to tell me the details, whatever the cost."

Marda was quiet for a moment. Outside the window, the street did its afternoon business: carts, gulls, a crier announcing the reopening of the eastern districts, whose street names had finally stopped changing overnight. The city's memories were coming back the way feeling returns to a numbed limb—painfully, imperfectly, but returning.

"Do you regret it?" she asked. "Whatever you gave up?"

He considered the question honestly, because Marda deserved honesty. "I regret that I don't know what I miss. There's a difference. Grief with a name has edges. You can hold it. Mine is just—" He searched for it. "Weather. An indoor weather that never clears."

"And the bird?" Marda nodded at the locked case. "You never said where it came from."

"I don't know," Orsen said. "I've stopped pretending otherwise. It was in my pocket when I woke in the shrine ruins. Carved alderwood. Cheap knife-work, done fast, by someone with more feeling than skill." His throat tightened, absurdly, over a two-inch piece of wood. "Whoever carved it loved someone. That's all I know. That's all it will tell me."

Marda reached across the counter and squeezed his wrist. Then the bell rang—flat, cracked, familiar—and she gathered her parcels and went, pausing at the door to say, as she always did, "Lock up proper tonight, Orse," and he said, as he always did, "I always do," and neither of them knew why this small exchange felt like a liturgy.

* * *

The child came at dusk.

Orsen was trimming the lamp-wicks when the bell rang, and he called out his welcome without turning around—until he did turn, and stopped.

A child stood just inside the doorway, maybe nine or ten years old, thin in the way of children who have walked a long way recently, wearing a coat too big and patched at the elbows. Dark hair, uncombed. And eyes—

Eyes the color of amber held up to firelight. Eyes with a slant of gold in them that no ordinary human child had, eyes that caught the lamplight and seemed to hold it a moment longer than physics allowed.

Wolf-eyes.

Something moved through Orsen like a current under ice. Not recognition—recognition would have been kinder. It was the *shape* of recognition, the negative space of it, a silhouette cut out of him with the person missing. His hands, resting on the counter, had gone white-knuckled without his permission.

"Evening," the child said. Voice low, watchful, the voice of a kid who'd learned to measure rooms before entering them. "I want a luck-charm."

"We have those," Orsen managed. His own voice sounded far away. "Riverbone. Three coppers, five if you want it blessed properly."

"I don't need it blessed." The child crossed to the tray with a hunter's economy of movement and studied the sigils with an expertise that raised the hair on Orsen's arms. Small fingers turned one over, checking the carving, the grain, the knotwork at the base—and paused.

"This one's wrong," the child said quietly. "The binding spiral. It goes sunwise here—" a tap, "—but it should go the other way. For luck. Sunwise is for warding. Widdershins is for calling things *to* you."

Orsen stared. "Where did you learn that?"

"Someone taught me." The child set the charm down with exaggerated care, and for just a moment the golden eyes lifted, and there was something in them—something vast and old and grieving, wearing a child's face like a borrowed coat. "A long time ago. Or it feels like a long time. It's hard to explain."

"I have time," Orsen heard himself say. "If you wanted to explain."

The child looked at him for a long moment. Looking *for* something, Orsen realized. Searching his face the way you search a crowd for someone you've lost, knowing they won't be there, looking anyway.

"No," the child said finally. "No, I—no. Just the charm, please. Widdershins, if you can fix it."

Orsen fixed it. His hands knew how without consulting him; the knife turned the spiral the correct way while his mind stood off to the side, watching, aching, pressing its tongue again and again into that missing-tooth gap. He wrapped the sigil in brown paper. He took the child's coppers—warm from a pocket, counted carefully, one of them older coinage, pre-Edict, worn smooth by decades of hands.

"Thank you," the child said at the door. And then didn't leave.

Stood there instead, one hand on the frame, the cracked bell silent above them, the last light failing in the street outside. The child's shoulders rose and fell once, deliberately, the way people breathe before doing something difficult.

"You ever get the feeling," the child said, not turning around, "that you forgot something important? Not like losing your keys. Like—you gave it away. On purpose. Because it was the only thing big enough."

Orsen's heart was beating very strangely. "Yes," he said. "Every day."

The child nodded, slow, as if this confirmed something. As if it hurt.

"There's a story I heard," the child went on, voice very steady, the steadiness of someone holding something shut with both hands. "About a taster. Who carried every poison in the kingdom inside him, so that when the time came, he could hold something no one else could hold. And about what it cost him." A pause. "And about whether it was worth it."

"What does the story say?" Orsen asked. His voice was barely working.

"It doesn't say." The child pushed the door open. Cold evening air came in, smelling of woodsmoke and the distant river and something wilder underneath, something furred and ancient and patient. "It just ends with him standing in his shop, and someone walking in, and nobody remembering why the bell sounded like a beginning."

The child stepped out into the dusk. Stopped. Looked back over one shoulder, and the gold eyes caught the lamplight and held it, and held it, and held it.

"My name's Wren," the child said. "In case it matters. In case it ever comes back to you. Names are anchors—that's Old Covenant law. You taught me that." A ghost of something crossed the small face—not a smile, not quite, but the place where a smile would live. "Or somebody did."

Then the child was gone, footsteps quick and light down Candlemaker's Row, and the flat-cracked bell swung empty above the door, and Orsen Whitlock stood alone in his shop with a bird carved in alderwood locked behind glass and a name ringing in him like a struck bell that refused to fade.

*Wren.*

He said it aloud, once, testing it. It fit some hollow in him exactly, the way a key fits a lock it was cut for, and the lock was rusted shut, and the key was warm, and somewhere beneath the rust something enormous stirred in its sleep and began, slowly, to remember.

Orsen picked up his knife. He had charms to finish, orders to fill, a life to keep living in the weatherless weather of his own missing heart. But his hands were trembling, and when he looked down at the wooden bird in its case, its half-spread wings suddenly looked less like decoration and more like a message:

*Waiting. Ready. About to fly.*

He unlocked the case. He took the bird out. And in the space of one held breath—on the edge of remembering, at the threshold of everything—he whispered the name again into the darkening shop, like an answer to a question he hadn't yet been asked.

"*Wren.*"

Outside, at the corner of the Row, a small figure waited in the shadows, watching the lamplit window, ears pricked toward a word carried faintly on the air.

And smiled.
