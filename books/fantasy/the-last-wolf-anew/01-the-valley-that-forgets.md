## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley forgot things the way old women forget names—slowly, politely, and without any sense of loss. It forgot rain for weeks at a stretch and then remembered all of it in a single afternoon. It forgot the road out to the lowlands every winter, letting the snow erase it until travelers swore there had never been more than one way in. Most of all, it forgot magic.

Paloma Marchetti had lived nineteen years inside that forgetting, and she had learned to be very good at forgetting too.

She knelt in Maren's herb garden with dirt black under her fingernails and the first true cold of autumn pressing at her shoulders, coaxing a row of feverfew through the last warm week it would get. Not coaxing with words. Never with words. She did it the way everyone else did: with compost and patience and the flat of her hand pressed gently against the soil as if she could lend the plants some of her own warmth. That much was ordinary. Any gardener might do it.

What was not ordinary was the small, bright attention she kept folded up behind her ribs like a moth in a closed fist—the thing that wanted to reach into the feverfew and tell its roots *grow*, the thing she had not once, not ever in her whole careful life, let out on purpose.

"Stop hovering over those," Maren said from the doorway. Her voice was thinner than the smoke rising off the chimney behind her, and it frayed halfway across the yard. "You'll worry them into bolting."

"You're supposed to be resting."

"I'm supposed to be dying." Maren lowered herself onto the bench beside the door with the care of a woman setting down something breakable. "Resting is just dying with better posture. Come here. My eyes are going soft and I want to look at you while you still have edges."

Paloma wiped her hands on her apron and went. Up close, Maren smelled of dried yarrow and camphor and something underneath both that Paloma refused to name. The old woman's hands had been the steadiest in the valley once—hands that set bones, that pulled fevers back from the brink with poultice and will—but they trembled now against the worn wood of the bench, and the tremble looked less like age than like a candle guttering.

"Maren."

"Don't make your face like that." Maren's eyes were still sharp enough to cut. Gray, then paler gray, then almost white at the rim, like frost creeping across a window. "Sit. Let an old woman be sentimental. It's the one vice I never managed."

Paloma sat. From the bench they could see the whole garden laid out in its tidy squares—feverfew and valerian, comfrey gone leggy, the sage Maren insisted on cutting wrong every single year—and beyond it the valley floor unrolling toward the river, gold with late stubble fields, hemmed by hills that stood shoulder to shoulder like patient cattle. Somewhere past those hills was the rest of the world. Paloma had seen it only twice, on supply trips to the market town at the pass, and both times she had felt it looking back at her the way a cat looks at a bird through glass.

"Do you remember," Maren said, "when you were seven, and you found the robin?"

Paloma went still. "I don't think I do."

"Liar." But there was no edge in it. "Winged-broken, left side of the mill path. You brought it to me crying so hard you couldn't speak, and I told you nothing could be done, and you sat with it anyway in the corner of my workroom with your hands cupped around it like a little lantern." Maren's breath caught, held, released. "And in the morning it flew."

"The neck healed on its own. Sometimes they do."

"It did not heal on its own, child. And we both know what you did, and we have spent twelve years pretending you didn't, because that is how you stay alive in this valley." Maren turned her head, slow as a door swinging, and looked at her. "But I'm done pretending. I haven't got time left for it."

The wind moved through the garden. The sage shivered. Paloma's heart was beating somewhere high up in her throat.

"There are no wards here," Maren said. "No spells. No charms nailed over the doors. Do you know why the valley is dead to magic? Really dead—not thin, not sleepy, but dead, the way a hearth is dead when the chimney's been sealed?" She didn't wait for an answer. "Because someone built it that way. On purpose. Stone by stone, law by law, forgetting by forgetting. This isn't a refuge, Paloma. It's a hiding place. There's a difference, and the difference is that a hiding place has something in it worth finding."

Paloma's mouth was dry. "Worth finding like what?"

Maren smiled, and it was the saddest thing Paloma had ever seen on her face. "Like you."

* * *

That night Maren slept badly, and Paloma lay in the narrow room next door listening to the ragged music of her breathing and staring at the ceiling beam where, in summer, swallows used to nest. The house was full of sounds it had always made—the tick of the stove iron cooling, the settle of the joists—and every one of them sounded different now, the way a room sounds different after someone says they're leaving it.

She thought about the robin. She thought about twelve years of cupped hands, of swallowed light, of the small daily murder of her own self she performed so smoothly she'd stopped noticing the blood. *Grow,* she'd wanted to say to the feverfew that morning. Just that. One word. She had bitten it back the way she bit back everything, and the word had gone down into her like a stone into a well, and she had heard, or imagined she'd heard, it land.

Near dawn, Maren called her.

Not loudly. Maren was past loud. Paloma crossed the cold boards and knelt by the bed, and in the gray light the old woman looked already half-erased, her skin the color of the paper in her oldest books, her eyes open and fixed on the ceiling as if reading something written there.

"Maren. I'll fetch water. I'll—"

"Listen." A hand found hers. The grip was still strong; whatever else was failing, the hands remembered their trade. "There's a chest under the floor of my workroom. Under the third flagstone, the cracked one. You've swept over it ten thousand times."

"Maren, please—"

"A grimoire." The word came out rough, like a bone caught in the throat. "It's yours. It was always meant to be yours. I was meant to give it to you when you were grown, when I was dust, when the timing—" She coughed, wet and long, and Paloma held her until it passed, hating her own useless hands. "The timing is wrong. It's all wrong. They're coming early this year."

"Who's coming? Maren, who's coming?"

But the old woman's gaze had slid inward. Her lips moved, shaping words too faint to catch, and then, suddenly clear, suddenly urgent, her fingers tightening on Paloma's wrist hard enough to bruise:

"The door and the thirteenth turn. Remember it. *The door and the thirteenth turn.* Don't let them find the key. Whatever it costs, don't let them—"

Her hand went slack.

The breathing stopped between one rise and the next, so quietly that for several seconds Paloma knelt there not believing it, waiting for the next breath the way you wait for a wagon you've heard but not yet seen. The stove ticked. Outside, a crow said something rude to the morning.

Maren Solane, herbalist, healer, keeper of secrets, dead in her bed at first light on the fourth day of the falling month, with her last sentence unfinished and her eyes still fixed on whatever was written on the ceiling of the world.

Paloma did not cry. Later she would understand that grief had simply decided to wait its turn, the way winter waits behind a warm week, but in the moment there was only a terrible clarity, and in that clarity she did exactly what her mentor had trained her to do, all her life, without either of them naming the training.

She went to the workroom. She pried up the third flagstone, the cracked one. She took the chest from beneath the floor, and from the chest she took a book bound in green leather gone dark with age, its corners worn round as river stones, and a letter sealed with plain wax and addressed in Maren's upright hand: *For Paloma, when she is ready.*

She broke the seal standing there in the cold workroom among the drying bundles and the labeled jars, surrounded by fifty years of quiet, deliberate care.

*Child of mine, if you are reading this I am sorry for two things: that I am gone, and that I taught you so well to hide. Burn this letter before anyone sees it. Burn the book too, if you can bear to—it would be safer. But I know you, and you won't be able to, and perhaps that is right. Inside you will find the truth about this valley, which I should have given you years ago except that the truth is a door, and doors open from both sides.*

*If anyone comes asking—if soldiers come, if criers come, if anyone asks after the gifted—remember: the door and the thirteenth turn. Count the moons. Trust no one who wears the crown's colors, and trust the ones who hide themselves most of all.*

*I loved you as my own. I guarded you as my charge. Forgive me for never telling you which was heavier.*

*M.*

Paloma read it three times. Then she carried it to the stove, opened the iron door, and fed it to the fire, watching Maren's careful handwriting curl and blacken and go up the flue into the gray morning air.

Then she sat down on the hearthstone with the grimoire in her lap, and the grief arrived all at once, the way the valley's rains arrived, all of it in a single afternoon, and she put her forehead against the green leather and wept until the stove went cold.

* * *

She buried Maren three days later on the hill above the garden, in the little plot where the valley's healers had been laid down for longer than any record showed. Half the village came. Old Tomas dug the grave because his back was still good and his sons were scattered; the baker's wife brought honey cakes for afterward because grief needs feeding same as anything; the children ran wild among the headstones until dusk. The priest from the mission chapel spoke the standard words over the mound—dust to dust, the valley keeps its own—and everyone nodded, and everyone agreed that Maren Solane had been a fine woman, a useful woman, a woman who asked nothing of anyone.

No one mentioned magic. No one ever mentioned magic. In the valley, the word itself had been buried so deep and so long ago that digging it up felt like blasphemy.

Paloma stood at the graveside in her black scarf and let them say their kind, hollow words, and all the while the grimoire weighed against her ribs beneath her coat like a second heart. She had hidden it in the flour bin, then taken it out and hidden it under the loose board in her bedroom, then taken it out again, unable to stand being parted from it, unable to stand holding it. She had not yet opened it past the flyleaf. The flyleaf alone had nearly undone her: *Property of M.S., who was born elsewhere,* in faded ink, and beneath it, newer, firmer: *To be given to P.M., who must leave.*

*Who must leave.* As if leaving were a chore on a list. As if the valley were not the whole shape of the world.

After the burial, neighbors drifted back down the hill with their condolences spent. Paloma stayed. The evening came up copper and violet over the western hills, and the first star pricked through above the ridge, and she stood talking silently to the fresh-turned earth, saying all the things she hadn't managed to say at the bedside—thank you, forgive me, what am I supposed to do now, what did you mean, *who's coming*—until the cold drove even grief indoors.

In the house, she lit one lamp, sat at Maren's worktable, and finally, with hands that shook only a little, opened the grimoire past the flyleaf.

The pages were dense with handwriting in three or four different inks, layered across decades. Diagrams she didn't recognize—circles within circles, stars drawn with too many points, a tree whose roots were also its branches. Marginal notes in Maren's younger, sharper script arguing with an older hand whose letters slanted left. And everywhere, threaded through the ink like veins through marble, words in a language she did not know, though when her eyes crossed them they seemed to almost-mean, the way a dream almost-means.

She turned page after page, the lamp burning lower, the night deepening around the house. And then, near the middle of the book, she found a map.

It was a country she had never seen and somehow recognized, the way you recognize a face in an old portrait—a resemblance running backward through time. Mountains along one edge like a jagged crown. A river bisecting everything. Cities marked in careful capitals, and at the center, drawn twice over and boxed in a heavy hand, a single symbol: an archway, and beneath the archway, darkness.

Beneath it, in Maren's firmest script: *Vessany.*

Paloma frowned. She knew the maps in the mission schoolroom, the ones the tax collectors carried, the names stitched into every prayer for the king. There was no Vessany. There had never been a Vessany. Where the map in the grimoire showed mountains and cities and a river, every chart she had ever seen showed blank parchment—or rather, showed nothing at all, not even blankness, a gap in the world that cartographers filled with a small apologetic flourish and the words *unclaimed waste.*

A country erased. Not lost—erased, the way the valley erased magic, deliberately, stone by stone, name by name.

And there at the archway's base, in ink so fresh it might have been written yesterday, one line in Maren's hand:

*It opens on the thirteenth turn. Eleven remain when you read this. Hurry, child. HURRY.*

Paloma sat back. The lamp hissed. Outside, the wind had come around to the north, carrying the first smell of true winter, and somewhere far off—so far she might have imagined it—a sound rolled over the hills that might have been thunder, or might have been hooves, many horses moving fast through a country that had forgotten how to be afraid.

Eleven remain.

She closed the grimoire and held it against her chest, over the place where the folded-up bright thing lived behind her ribs, and for the first time in her carefully ordinary life, Paloma Marchetti began, quietly and irreversibly, to be afraid.
