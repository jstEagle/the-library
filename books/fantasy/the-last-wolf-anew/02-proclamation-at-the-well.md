## 2. Proclamation at the Well

The crier came to Hollowmere on a morning so ordinary it felt like an ambush in hindsight.

Paloma was in Maren's garden when the first bell rang—kneeling in the black loam between the feverfew and the comfrey, her fingers buried to the second knuckle, teasing out a weed by the root. The garden had been hers alone for eleven days now, since the morning she'd found Maren cold in her bed with her hands folded like a woman waiting patiently for something, and Paloma had not yet learned how to be in it without hearing the old woman's voice correcting her grip. *Looser, girl. You're strangling it. Nothing grows under a stranglehold.*

She had said that about more than weeds.

The bell rang again from the well square—a cracked bronze note that Hollowmere used only for fires and weddings—and Paloma sat back on her heels, dirt on her palms, and felt the first wrongness settle over the day like a change in weather. Bells meant gathering. Gathering meant the crown.

They had never come this deep into the valley before.

She told herself it was a tax notice. A road levy. Something tedious and survivable. She wiped her hands on her apron, pressed the weed-hole closed with her thumb—an old habit, Maren's habit, sealing the earth behind you—and walked up through the garden gate toward the square, telling herself all the way that she was only curious, only a neighbor going to look, only a woman with nothing to hide.

That last part was a lie she had been telling herself so long it had worn smooth as river stone.

* * *

The whole village had crowded into the well square, which was not large—a circle of packed earth ringed by whitewashed houses, the well itself crowned with its little shingled roof, the shrine of the Shepherd standing neglected in its alcove where the plaster saint's hands had crumbled away decades ago and no one had replaced them. Hollowmere did not believe much in anything except keeping its head down.

The crier stood on the well's low stone lip, and he was wrong in every particular.

He wore the crown's colors—black and gold—but he wore them like armor, not livery, and there were six soldiers flanking him with the bored, coiled posture of men who had done this in forty villages already and expected nothing but grief. Their horses stood tethered by the shrine, steaming in the cool air, too fine-bred for these roads. One of them was gray with a white blaze, and it watched the crowd the way a shepherd dog watches sheep—not with hostility, exactly. With inventory.

And the crier himself: young, clean-shaven, with a voice trained to carry over crowds and a scroll in his hand sealed with wax the color of dried blood. He had the look of a man reading words he did not fully understand and was glad not to.

"By order of His Majesty Aldous the Second, King of Vessany-in-Exile, Protector of the Marches—" Paloma heard someone near her mutter *protector of what*, and someone else hush them. "—and by counsel of the Inquisitorial Office, let it be known throughout the valleys and vales of the crown's protection:"

He unrolled the scroll. Somewhere in the crowd a baby started crying and was quickly muffled.

"The Edict of the Hunt. Whereas the crown has learned that persons gifted with unlawful arts walk abroad in the king's lands, concealing themselves among honest folk; whereas such arts are an abomination before the Shepherd and a treason against the throne; whereas the safety of the realm depends upon the rooting-out of this corruption—let it be proclaimed that every person possessed of the gift, or suspected thereof, shall present themselves for registration at the nearest garrison post within thirty days of this proclamation."

A pause. The crier licked his lips.

"Those who register shall be examined, catalogued, and—if found harmless—released under bond."

Someone exhaled audibly, a sound almost like relief.

"Those who do not register," the crier continued, and his voice flattened, "who flee, conceal themselves, or aid others in flight, shall be taken. Taken persons will be conveyed to the Inquisitorial Office at Carreth for examination. The crown thanks the faithful for their vigilance. Reports may be made at any garrison. Rewards are provided."

He rolled the scroll shut with a snap.

The silence afterward lasted perhaps three heartbeats. Then the square broke apart into a hundred small sounds—the rustle of people turning to whisper, a nervous laugh from the baker's son, old Widow Cetch saying *well, thank the Shepherd none of that here* in a voice pitched just loud enough to be overheard by everyone, including, Paloma was certain, the soldiers.

None of that here.

Hollowmere's oldest prayer. The valley was magic-dead—that was the whole of its legend, the story every child learned with their milk-teeth: that long ago something had drained the land dry, and no gift could root here, and so the gifted passed it by, and the crown left it alone because there was nothing to hunt. It was why Maren had come here. It was why Paloma had been raised here, in this bowl of quiet hills where even the stories had gone thin and pale.

It was why she had spent nineteen years folding her gift down so small and flat that sometimes, in the dark before sleep, she could almost convince herself it wasn't there at all. Almost. Never quite. There was always the moment—lighting the stove, striking flint to char—when the flame came a half-heartbeat too eagerly, leaning toward her fingers like a cat toward a hand. There was always the way cut flowers lasted nine days in her room and four everywhere else. Small things. Deniable things.

She had never once, not ever, done anything with it on purpose.

She was still telling herself this when the soldiers moved.

* * *

It happened fast, the way terrible things do—no drama leading up to it, no thunderclap, just a shift in the geometry of the square. Two of the six soldiers peeled away from the well and walked, unhurried, to the far edge of the crowd, where Sera Ilvane stood with her youngest boy by the hand.

Tam. Seven years old. Gap-toothed, freckled across the nose, forever trailing his mother through the market with his father's old cap pulled down over his ears. Paloma knew him the way you know every child in a valley of ninety souls: he had stolen plums from her garden two summers running, and last spring she had caught him crouched by the herb beds, solemnly explaining to a bumblebee that it should visit the comfrey next because it was very sick and needed help, and the bee, she would have sworn on anything, had listened.

The soldiers stopped in front of Sera Ilvane. One of them said something Paloma couldn't catch. Sera's face went through three expressions in as many seconds—confusion, denial, terror.

"No," she said, loud enough now for the whole square. "No, you're mistaken, he's seven years old, he's a child—"

"Mistaken," the soldier repeated, without heat. "Ma'am. Last night, in your house. Four candles."

"I burn candles. Everyone burns candles, we've no lamp oil to spare—"

"All four lit at once," the soldier said. "From cold wicks. All four, ma'am, at the same moment. Your neighbor across the lane saw it through the window."

Every head in the square turned—not to the soldier, not to Sera, but to the house across the lane. To Doss Merrick, who stood at the edge of the crowd looking anywhere else, his jaw working, sweat shining at his hairline even in the cool of the morning.

"You saw it wrong," Sera said. Her voice climbed. "You saw it wrong, Doss, you know him, you've eaten at my table—"

"It's procedure, ma'am." The second soldier had taken Tam's shoulder. Gently. That was somehow the worst part, how gently. The boy looked up at the hand on him, then at his mother, then around at the crowd with the bewildered expression of a child who has been told the game has changed rules mid-play. "Registration is not punishment. If he's registered and examined—"

"He's *seven*—"

"—and found harmless, he'll come home under bond. Ma'am. Don't make this harder."

Sera grabbed for her son and a third soldier stepped between them, not rough, just present, a wall in a black-and-gold coat, and the arithmetic of the thing became suddenly, horribly visible: six soldiers, one widow, one small boy, ninety neighbors standing in a silent half-circle doing absolutely nothing.

Paloma's heart was slamming. Not with fear—or not only fear. With a horrible, electric recognition, the kind of recognition that makes the skin prickle from scalp to sole.

*Candles from cold wicks.* She knew that eagerness. Flame leaning toward small fingers. A gift so small it barely deserved the name, a candle-trick, a bee-charm, nothing—nothing the crown should care about, nothing worth six soldiers and a bell rung twice—

But they cared. That was the lesson unfolding in front of her, plain as a butcher's diagram. It didn't matter how small the gift was. It didn't matter if it was candles. The edict hadn't said *dangerous*. It had said *gifted*. The net didn't have holes. That was the entire design of a net.

"Say something," whispered a voice beside her—Bett, the cooper's wife, gripping Paloma's sleeve, her knuckles white. "Someone say something. This is Hollowmere. They can't just—"

But they could. That was the other lesson. And everyone in the square knew it, and everyone stayed silent, and the silence was the loudest sound Paloma had ever heard.

Sera Ilvane went to her knees in the dirt of the well square, still holding her son's other hand, and Tam—clever, bee-talking Tam—finally understood. His face crumpled. He didn't cry. He turned and looked at the crowd, at all of them, one face after another, the way a child looks for a grown-up who will fix it, and his eyes crossed Paloma's for half a heartbeat.

Half a heartbeat was enough. She would carry it for the rest of her life.

They led him to the horses. He went. Of course he went—he was seven, and there were swords. Sera screamed once, a short torn sound, and then pressed both fists against her mouth, and no one moved, and the gray horse with the white blaze turned its head and watched the boy go with that same patient inventory, and then the soldiers mounted, and the crier remounted his own horse and called out the thirty days remaining until registration closed in his trained carrying voice, cheerful as a man announcing a fair, and they rode out past the shrine of the Shepherd, whose broken stone hands could not bless or stop anyone, and were gone.

The bell did not ring them out. Nobody had thought to ring the bell.

For a long moment the square simply stood there. Then it dissolved into knots of murmuring clusters, and Bett's grip fell away from Paloma's sleeve, and Paloma became aware that she was shaking—from cold or fury or fear, indistinguishable—and that her gift, her folded-flat, buried-alive gift, had risen in her chest like a startled bird and was beating against the inside of her ribs, wanting out, wanting to do *something*, and she clamped down on it so hard that sparks bloomed behind her eyes.

Doss Merrick was still standing at the edge of the square. As the crowd thinned, he looked up, and met Paloma's eyes, and she watched him realize—watched the knowledge move across his face like a shadow of a cloud—that she had seen him see it. That she knew what he had traded. That she was now, in whatever small ledger the crown kept, another name he might someday read aloud.

She turned away first. It cost her everything she had.

* * *

Maren's cottage held the evening light like a cup holds water.

Paloma had not gone back to the square. She had spent the afternoon in the garden doing work that didn't need doing, cutting back dead stalks, turning soil that was already turned, moving through the motions of a life while her mind ran in circles around one question, tightening like a snare:

*How did they see the candles?*

Not Doss—Doss was an excuse, a mouth, a reward-seeker. But someone had taught those soldiers to look for simultaneous flame. Someone had decided that a seven-year-old's candle-trick constituted evidence. Which meant the Edict of the Hunt was not a sweep. It was a sieve, fine-meshed, drawn through every water in the country, designed to catch things far smaller than Tam Ilvane.

Which meant it would catch her.

She sat at Maren's table as the light went amber and then gray, a cup of barley tea untouched at her elbow, and looked at the door to Maren's sleeping room, closed since the day they'd carried her out feet-first. The grimoire was in there. Wrapped in oilcloth, tucked in the bottom of the cedar chest beneath the winter blankets, where Paloma had put it eleven days ago with hands that shook—not from fear then, only from grief.

She had meant to burn it. She had stood at the hearth with it in both arms and meant to, because Maren had spent nineteen years teaching her that the safest gift was the unused one, and a book full of spells was a confession bound in leather. But she had opened it instead, just once, to the flyleaf, and found Maren's handwriting—sharp, slanted, impatient even in ink—and the single line she'd written there:

*If you are reading this, little bird, I am sorry for everything I am about to make you do.*

She had not burned it. She had wrapped it back up and put it in the chest and told herself she would decide later, and *later* had become eleven comfortable, cowardly days.

There were no more comfortable days. The sieve was coming through the valley. Thirty days. Fewer than thirty, now—one already bleeding away, and the garrison post named in the edict was at Carreth, which was three days' ride north, and sooner or later some other Doss Merrick was going to notice that Paloma Marchetti's cut flowers lived nine days, or that her bread never burned, or that the feverfew in her garden grew in patterns too neat for chance, and then there would be a knock, and gentle hands on her shoulder, and a gray horse watching her with patient inventory.

Stay and register, and be examined by whoever examined children in cellars at Carreth.

Run, and be *taken*—the word had a specific weight in the edict, she'd noticed, heavier than *arrested*, heavier than *seized*. Taken.

Or—

Her eyes went again to the closed door. To the chest. To the book.

*I am sorry for everything I am about to make you do.*

Night settled. She lit the lamp—flame leaning toward her fingers, eager, always eager—and ate without tasting, and listened to the house tick and settle around her, and at some point she realized she was listening for hoofbeats on the road and had been for hours.

She got up. She opened the door to Maren's room.

The smell of the old woman was still in there—dried herbs and tallow and something mineral, like rain on slate—and Paloma stood in the doorway a long moment, letting it hurt. Then she knelt at the cedar chest, moved the blankets aside, and lifted out the oilcloth bundle, and unwrapped it on the bed with the reverence and dread of someone opening a wound to clean it.

The grimoire was thick as a brick, bound in green leather gone soft and dark with handling, its spine scarred where clasps had been removed. She had never seen inside it beyond the flyleaf. Nineteen years under Maren's roof, and the book had been as forbidden as fire in a hayloft—*some things you don't touch until you must, little bird, and by the Shepherd's mercy may that day never come.*

The day had come.

She opened it to the first page and stopped breathing.

Not spells. Not yet. The first pages were letters—dated, sequential, in Maren's slanting hand, addressed to a girl who had been born the year the first one was written. Addressed to *my little bird*. To Paloma. By name. Page after page after page, twenty years of them, written in advance by a dying woman who had known exactly when her student would finally be forced to open the covers.

At the top of the very first letter, underlined twice, were the words:

*Read the map first. Burn nothing. Trust no crier, no neighbor, and no road that runs straight.*

Paloma's hands trembled. Outside, the moon was rising over the eastern ridge—thin, new, a nail-paring of light—and below it, far off toward the valley mouth, she could see the dark line of the north road, the road to Carreth, the road everything flowed along eventually.

Thirty days. Twenty-nine.

Somewhere out there, the sieve was already moving.

She began, with careful fingers, to turn the page.
