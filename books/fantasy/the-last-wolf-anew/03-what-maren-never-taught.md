## 3. What Maren Never Taught

The road out of the valley climbed through pine shadow for most of the first day, and Paloma walked it the way she had done everything for nineteen years: quietly, carefully, as though the world were a sleeping thing that might wake if she moved too fast.

By the second day the pines thinned, and she allowed herself, at last, to stop looking over her shoulder. The valley was behind her—a green fold in the mountains where magic came to die, where candles lit themselves only by flint and patience and where no child had ever floated a feather with a thought because there was nothing in the air to think *with*. She had believed it safe the way a bird believes its cage is the whole sky.

Then the crier at the well. Then little Anselm dragged away by his heels, still crying candlelight from his fingertips.

Paloma walked, and the grimoire rode against her ribs inside her coat, heavy as a second heart.

She had not opened it since Maren died. She had burned the old woman's last words—the scrap of parchment that said *the door and the thirteenth turn*—in the kitchen stove before dawn on the day she fled, feeding the paper to the flames until it curled into black lace. It had felt like mercy. Maren had spent sixty years teaching her to be small; the least Paloma could do was honor one final lesson.

But the book itself she could not burn. That was the truth of it, ugly and plain: she was a coward about some things and a thief about others, and the grimoire fell into the second category. She had stolen it from beneath Maren's folded hands on the deathbed table, lifted it while the neighbors sang the passing hymns in the front room, and carried it out through the herb garden where the feverfew was just coming up, and she had not once felt sorry.

Now, on the third morning, camped in the hollow of an old shepherd's shelter with frost silvering the stones outside, she took it out.

* * *

The book was unremarkable to look at—goat-leather gone soft and gray at the corners, a brass clasp worn smooth as a river pebble. Maren had kept it in the bottom drawer of the drying cabinet among the bundles of yarrow, wrapped in oilcloth, as if it were a jar of something that spoiled. Paloma had assumed it was what every hedge-witch in every valley kept: recipes for poultices, moon-charts for planting, remedies against colic and curse alike. A working woman's ledger.

She unclasped it.

The first pages were exactly that. Feverfew for headache, steeped three minutes, no longer. Comfrey poultice, changed daily. A drawing of a mole's skeleton annotated in Maren's cramped left-slanting hand: *bones remember the shape of the digging.* Paloma smiled despite herself, and the smile cracked something in her chest, because the handwriting was so alive, so insistently *Maren*, that turning these pages felt like hearing her voice from another room.

*You'll want willow bark for the pain in your hands when you're older,* one margin note read. *Don't argue. You'll want it.*

*I don't have pain in my hands,* Paloma whispered to the empty shelter. *I never did.*

And then, near the middle of the book, the handwriting stopped being marginal.

The page began without preamble, mid-thought, the way Maren began everything:

*If you are reading this, then I am dead, and you have run, which means the criers have come to the valley, which means the world outside has grown teeth again. Good girl. Run well.*

Paloma's hands went still on the paper.

*There are things I never taught you,* the page continued. *Not because you were slow—you were never slow—but because teaching them would have been like striking flint in a room full of lamp-oil. Your gift is small only if you keep it small. I have watched you light the hearth with a thought when you thought I wasn't looking. Don't be ashamed. Be careful. There is a difference, and the difference has kept you alive this long.*

"I never—" Paloma started, and stopped. Because she had. Twice a winter, maybe. Small warmings, cupped in her palms like a captured moth, always with the door shut, always with her heart hammering. She had believed herself invisible.

Maren had seen everything. Maren had always seen everything.

*Turn the leaf,* the next page instructed, and Paloma turned the leaf.

A map filled the whole spread, drawn in faded iron-gall ink and touched with color that had gone strange with age—the rivers a brownish rose, the mountains shaded in a wash that might once have been violet. It showed a country she did not know, its borders traced with a fine confident hand, cities named in a script half familiar: *Vessamora. The Weeping Steps. Caer Alden. VESSANY*, written across the heart of it in letters tall enough to read across a dark room.

Paloma frowned. She knew her geography—or she knew the geography the valley schoolmistress taught, which was maps of the crown's provinces, the trade roads, the coastline. No Vessany anywhere on them. She'd have remembered a name like that. It sounded like a bell being struck.

She checked the map again, hunting for the joke, the trick. There was none. The country sat there between its neighbors, occupying ground she could almost match to places she knew—the shape of the border fit, roughly, the curve of territory the schoolmaster's maps had called simply *the Old March, unproductive, sparsely settled.*

A country erased. Not conquered—erased. The land was still there; the name was not.

In the northeast corner of Vessany, up in mountains rendered in that strange fading violet, someone had inked a tiny symbol. Not a city mark. Not a shrine. It was a rectangle standing open, drawn in perspective, like a doorway seen edge-on—and around it, in Maren's handwriting, small and fierce and underlined three times:

*The door. Where no country exists. It opens for those who can name it. Thirteenth turn. Do not go alone. DO NOT GO AT ALL.*

Paloma sat back on her heels in the cold shelter, breath fogging, and stared at the contradiction until her eyes watered. *Do not go alone. Do not go at all.* Maren had spent a lifetime being precise. She did not underline things twice by accident.

*You will want to know why,* the following page said, as if the old woman had heard her thinking. *Because I am dead and cannot stop you, I will tell you some of it. Not all. Some truths are loads best carried in the order they were packed.*

*Vessany was real. I was born in it, in a town whose name I gave up before you were born, and I will die having given it up, because names are doors too, little one, and some doors are better shut. A hundred years ago the king of Vessany did a thing no king should be able to do: he unmade his own country. Not the fields and walls—the fields and walls are still there, walk through any village in the Old March and you'll stand in Vessany's dust. He unmade the *name*. And a country without its name cannot be entered except by accident, and cannot be left except through one place, and that one place he sealed with blood.*

Paloma looked up from the page, and the forest outside the shelter's mouth seemed suddenly less ordinary—the light through the pines thinner, the silence more attentive.

*Why are you telling me this now?* she asked the book, absurdly, and turned the page as though it might answer.

*Because the door is waking,* Maren wrote. *It wakes once a century, on the thirteenth turn of the moon after the first frost. And when it wakes, everything that hunts the gifted will feel it pulling, like a fish feels the line. The crown knows. The crown has always known—that is why the Edict comes now, that is why the criers are riding. They are not hunting witches, child. They are fishing. Every gifted soul they take, they will squeeze like a lamp until someone lights their way to the door.*

Paloma's mouth had gone dry. She thought of Anselm, eight years old, candlelight leaking between his fingers as they hauled him into the crier's cart. Squeezed like a lamp. She pressed the thought flat and made herself keep reading.

*You are going to try a spell anyway. I know you. You've been carrying this book for three days like it's a hot stone, and you are Maren's girl through and through, which means curiosity will beat caution the way spring beats frost—slowly, but completely.*

Paloma laughed aloud, a startled, broken sound, and wiped her eyes with her sleeve. Even dead, even burned to ash in a kitchen stove, the old woman saw everything.

*So do it properly,* the next page said. *Sit facing north. Ground yourself—bare feet on bare earth, yes, even in the frost, especially in the frost. Say the word written below, once, softly. It is a warming-word. It will light nothing you don't ask it to light.*

*Probably.*

Beneath the instruction, in a box drawn with unusual care, was a single word in a script Paloma had never seen—letters like standing stones, angular and patient. Yet when she looked at them, she found she could pronounce them. The knowledge arrived whole, the way you know how to swallow, or blink. It arrived the way her gift had always arrived: without permission.

*Probably,* she repeated aloud. "That's very reassuring, Maren."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere downslope a branch cracked—too heavy for a squirrel—and Paloma froze, listening, counting to a hundred before she let herself breathe. Nothing followed. But the feeling stayed with her afterward, the one she'd been shaking off since she fled: of being watched. Not hunted. *Watched*. Attended. Like a pupil in a classroom whose teacher has stepped out but left the door ajar.

She told herself it was guilt. Guilt makes ghosts of everyone we've wronged, and she had robbed a grave of its last secret.

She took off her boots anyway.

* * *

The frost had hardened the ground to iron, but she scraped away the rime in a patch the size of a dinner plate and set her bare feet in it, and the cold bit up through her soles like teeth. North. She faced north. The shelter's shadow lay long and blue toward the south, and the morning sun stood low and white behind her shoulder.

Her breath came in clouds. Her heartbeat sounded loud in her own ears—louder than it had any right to be, loud enough that she glanced over her own shoulder a second time at the empty road.

Nothing. Only the pines, and the frost, and the long pale ribbon of the way ahead, bending down out of the hills toward country she didn't know.

"Once, softly," she reminded herself. "Light nothing you don't ask it to light."

She shaped the word. Her mouth knew it before her mind did—the syllables rising like stones from a riverbed as the current of her attention passed over them—and she spoke it quietly, barely louder than breath.

The world answered.

Not slowly. Not gently. Not the way her small gift had always answered, shyly, like a cat coaxed from under a bed. The warmth leapt up through her feet, through her shins, her knees, blooming in her chest like a swallowed coal, and every needle of frost on every blade of grass within arm's reach flashed to steam at once—a soft collective hiss, a ring of vapor blooming around her in the cold air, and the puddle beside the shelter's wall, skin-thin ice and all, evaporated into a ghost of mist that hung in the windless morning like a held note.

Paloma snatched her feet back as if the ground had bitten her.

For a moment she just stood there, barefoot on wet black earth steaming faintly, watching the last wisps rise. Her heart slammed. Her hands shook—not with fear, she realized, or not only. With something worse. Recognition.

That hadn't been a warming-word. Or rather—it had, and she had warmed it the way a bellows warms a forge. The spell had come to her eagerly, hungrily, glad of her, pouring itself out at a whisper when Maren's notes had promised a candle-flame's worth of care. As if the word had been waiting. As if the word had known her name.

*It answers too eagerly,* Maren had written somewhere—no. No, Maren hadn't written that. Paloma was almost sure she hadn't. But the sentence sat in her memory with the weight of print, and when she flipped back through the pages, hunting it, she found instead a line she had somehow skipped:

*One more thing, and then I'll let you walk. Your gift isn't borrowed, child, and it isn't random. Blood carries it the way soil carries water. Ask yourself sometime why a magic-dead valley needed a witch in it at all. Ask yourself who plants a gardener in a garden where nothing grows.*

Paloma closed the book.

She sat on her rolled blanket for a long time, feet bare and chilled on the damp earth, watching the road. The sense of attention had not faded. If anything it had deepened—patient, sorrowful, unmistakably *fond*—and Paloma found she could no longer pretend it was guilt. Guilt doesn't feel like someone sitting beside you. Guilt doesn't lean in when you read.

"Maren," she said quietly, to the frost, to the pines, to the gray air. "What am I?"

The wind moved. The branches did not answer, and did not need to. She already knew where the answer lived—in a map of a country with no name, in a door drawn open at its edge, in the phrase *blood carries it* —and she knew, with the certainty that had been building since the crier's voice cracked over the well-square, that she was walking toward it whether Maren approved or not.

She put her boots back on. She ate a heel of bread, drank melted snow, repacked the satchel with hands that had nearly stopped trembling. When she stood at the mouth of the shelter, the road ran down out of the hills into a landscape she recognized from the schoolroom maps: the Old March. Unproductive. Sparsely settled.

Ground that had once been called Vessany.

Somewhere on that road, she thought, the villages would start having no names. Somewhere the roads would begin to loop. And somewhere ahead—northeast, in mountains the map had colored violet—something old was beginning, slowly, century-slowly, to open.

She stepped onto the road.

Behind her, in the shelter, in the folds of her abandoned bedroll where she'd left the grimoire wrapped in oilcloth, a page turned on its own—one soft whisper of paper, like a sigh, like a teacher settling back to watch—and neither the wind nor the woman walking away ever heard it.
