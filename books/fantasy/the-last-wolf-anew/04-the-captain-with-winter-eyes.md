## 4. The Captain with Winter Eyes

The village was called Hollowmere, though by the time Aldric Vane rode into it at the head of twelve soldiers, the name had been scraped from the signpost at its edge—someone had taken a knife to the wood with the desperate thoroughness of people who believed that names could be dangerous. He supposed they were right. Names had always been the beginning of everything bad in this country.

He sat his horse at the well and let the silence do his work for him.

Hollowmere was one of those places the valley had swallowed whole: a scatter of stone cottages under a permanent gray mist off the fens, fields that grew turnips and little else, chickens that scattered at the sound of hooves as if they too had something to hide. The villagers came out of their doors slowly, the way animals come to water when they suspect a predator is drinking there. A miller with flour in his eyebrows. Two old women holding each other's elbows. A girl of perhaps nine clutching a doll made of knotted rags.

Aldric dismounted. The iron charm at his throat swung forward on its chain and tapped against his breastplate, and he felt it do its work—the cold pinch of it, the familiar deadening, like pressing a thumb against the pulse point of his own soul. Beneath the iron, something stirred anyway. It always stirred. It smelled the fear in the village the way a hound smells blood on a boot, and it pressed up against the inside of his ribs, wanting out.

*Not here,* he told it. *Not ever.*

"Captain Vane." His sergeant, Corren, reined in beside him, breath fogging. "Edict board?"

"Edict board."

Corren hammered the proclamation to the well post while Aldric read it aloud, because the Edict required reading aloud, and because most of Hollowmere could not read. His voice carried flat and clear across the square, the same voice he used for orders on the field, and he watched the words land on the villagers like stones dropped into still water:

*By order of the Crown of Vessany-in-Exile: all persons possessed of unlicensed gifts shall present themselves for registry within ten days. Those who register shall be assessed, catalogued, and, where deemed safe, permitted to remain under bond. Those who conceal their gift, or flee the assessment, shall be hunted without term or mercy.*

The words were not his. He had not written them. He only said them, twice a week, in villages like this one, and every time he said them he heard the small sounds underneath—the held breaths, the creak of a door easing shut, somewhere a mother's hand closing over a child's mouth before the child could say anything true.

Ten days. He gave Hollowmere the full ten days in his mind already, because he knew how these things went.

"Registry is voluntary," Aldric finished. "Concealment is treason." He hated that sentence. He had delivered it four hundred times and hated it more each time, the hatred wearing a groove in him like water wearing a channel through limestone, slow and unstoppable and invisible until suddenly the ground gave way.

An old man stepped forward—a shepherd by his crook, face seamed like old bark. "Captain," he said carefully, "we're plain folk here. Fen-folk. Nothing in Hollowmere but turnips and rheumatism."

"There usually isn't," Aldric said. "I'll need to see your homes."

It was not a request. They all knew it was not a request, and he knew they knew, and the shame of it settled into him with the familiarity of an old wound that no longer hurt so much as ached, constantly, in cold weather.

* * *

They searched Hollowmere for four hours.

Aldric did it himself rather than letting the soldiers loose, which was a kindness of a kind, though he suspected the kindness was mostly for himself—he could not bear to watch what rougher men did in these houses, the drawers pulled out and the mattresses slit, the shrines to the old country tipped over because the Edict said the old country did not exist and anything honoring it was contraband. In the third cottage he found dried yarrow hung above a cradle. Yarrow was a protection herb. It meant nothing legally. It meant everything actually, and he stood looking at it for a long moment before reaching up and taking it down—not confiscating it, just taking it down, folding it into his glove, as if by removing the evidence he could remove the guilt.

His wolf-sense walked the rooms ahead of him. That was what he called it, privately: the wolf-sense, because naming it plainly was the first step toward using it, and using it was the first step toward the pyre. It was not a gift like fire or light or thought-made-candleflame. It was older and dumber and more faithful than any of those. It lived behind his eyes and along his jaw, in the hinge of his teeth. It tasted fear. It tracked grief across a room like scent across snow. And once a month, when the moon fattened toward full and the iron charms burned cold against his skin, it wanted very badly to run.

He passed through a cottage where a woman was mending nets, and the wolf-sense brushed her and found nothing but salt and worry. Through a smokehouse redolent with hung eels. Through the mill, where the miller's flour-dusted hands shook so badly he could barely hold the lamp steady, and the wolf-sense found nothing there either except terror—which was common enough, terror, in villages when the hunt came through, that it told Aldric nothing at all. Everyone feared the crown now. Fear was the national crop, and it grew in every soil.

Then he came to the last house at the fen's edge, and the wolf-sense woke up like a dog hearing its name.

It was a low cottage, half-swallowed by reeds, with a turf roof going green at the seams. A widow lived there, the neighbors would tell him later—or a woman alone, which amounted to the same thing in the ledger of village suspicion. She met him at the door with her chin up and her hands empty and her heartbeat, which the wolf-sense could hear through three feet of fen air and a plank door, hammering like a fist on a gate.

"Captain," she said. "Nothing here but me and my loom."

"No," Aldric agreed mildly. "Nothing here but you and your loom."

He went in. He searched slowly, thoroughly, the way he always did, giving the house time to confess whatever it was hiding. And in the back room, behind a hanging blanket, he found a boy.

The boy was maybe eleven years old. Thin, dark-haired, sitting very straight on a stool as if he'd been ordered to sit still and had decided to obey with his whole soul. There was a candle stub on the floor beside him, burnt down nearly to nothing—and around the candle, on the packed earth, scorched into the dirt itself, were marks. Little black stars. Dozens of them, overlapping, a constellation of tiny burnings, each one the shape of a flame flaring outward from a wick that had never been lit with a match.

Aldric looked at the marks. The boy looked at Aldric. And the wolf-sense rose in him like water rising in a well, tasting the boy the way it tasted everything—fear, yes, fear in floods—but beneath the fear, faint and unmistakable, that other taste. The taste he had learned to know from the inside. The taste of a gift being held down. The taste of iron discipline laid over living power, the way his own charms lay over his own.

The boy had been lighting candles with his mind. For months, by the look of the floor. Alone, in a back room, secretly, joyfully—little black stars, dozens of them, a child practicing wonder in the dark where no one could see.

And then the crown had come to Hollowmere, and now the boy sat very still on a stool and waited to find out if he was going to die.

"Mother," the boy said, and his voice cracked on it.

"He's my sister's son," the woman said quickly, too quickly. "Orphaned. Simple-minded. He burns things by accident, Captain, he doesn't mean—"

"Marta." Aldric said her name quietly, and she stopped, because the fact that he knew her name frightened her worse than anything else could have. Of course he knew her name. He had spent two weeks learning the names in the parish rolls, because knowing names was his trade now, his whole trade: names, and the people attached to them, and the small forbidden fires they hid in back rooms.

He crouched down to the boy's level. This was procedure, he told himself. De-escalation. You got closer to children so they didn't bolt; you got closer so you could take hold of them if you needed to. That was the official reasoning, and he recited it to himself the way he recited the Edict, twice a week, hating it.

"What's your name?" he asked the boy.

The boy's throat worked. "Tam," he said finally. "Tammel, sir."

"Tammel." Aldric glanced at the scorched stars on the floor. "You've been practicing."

The boy's face did something terrible—it flickered with pride first, one bright instant of *yes, I did it, aren't they beautiful*, before the fear crushed it flat again. But Aldric had seen it. He would carry that instant for a long time. He was carrying too many such instants already; he kept them in some inner pocket the way a miser keeps coins he's ashamed of.

"Here is what will happen," Aldric said, standing, turning to the aunt, pitching his voice for the soldier waiting at the front door to hear. "The cottage will be searched. If evidence of an unregistered gift is found, the child is remanded to the assessment station at Greyford." He paused, and let the pause stretch, and looked at Marta with winter eyes—everyone said he had winter eyes, pale gray-blue, cold, unreadable, and he cultivated the reputation the way other men cultivated mustaches, because a captain whose heart could not be read was a captain who could survive. "However. I saw no evidence today. Did I, Sergeant?"

Corren stood in the doorway with the blank face of a career soldier. "No evidence, Captain. Candle burns. Children play with candles."

"Children do," Aldric said. "Play with candles." He turned back to the boy, and for one moment—one moment only, a moment measured in heartbeats that the wolf-sense counted precisely, thirty-two of them, he remembered that number afterward with horrible exactness—he allowed his eyes to change. Not much. Just the least loosening of the iron, the barest lift of the charm's cold lid, so that for a single breath Tammel looked into the captain's face and saw what lived behind it: the amber ring around the gray, the animal patience, the *same thing that is in you, child, sleeping in me too*.

The boy's mouth opened. Closed. He nodded, very slightly, the nod of one conspirator acknowledging another across a crowded room.

Then Aldric let the iron fall back, and the winter closed over the amber again, and he was Captain Vane of the Hunt, and the cottage was only a cottage, and the floor was only a floor with childish scorch-marks on it.

"The house is clean," he announced to the doorway, to the village, to the ledger in his mind where he was already writing *Hollowmere: nothing found.* "We move on before dusk. Sergeant—mount up."

* * *

They rode out at the fen's edge as the light went copper and low, and it was Corren who noticed, twenty minutes later, glancing back at the smudge of Hollowmere under its evening smoke.

"That boy, Captain. Tammel. He'll light another candle within the month. They always do."

"Yes," Aldric said.

"And someone will inform. Someone always does—for the reward, or the grudge, or just because they can't stand a neighbor having something they don't. And the next captain won't be you."

"I'm aware, Sergeant."

Corren was silent for a quarter mile. Then, carefully, in the tone of a man testing ice: "You let him go."

Aldric kept his eyes on the road. The road looped here—that was the wrongness of this whole country, the roads that doubled back on themselves, the villages with their names scraped off, the maps that showed blankness where a nation used to be. He sometimes thought the land itself had been made complicit in the erasure. Even the roads seemed ashamed.

"I misjudged the evidence," he said.

"You didn't misjudge anything." Corren's voice was low. Not insubordinate—Corren had survived eight years under Aldric by knowing exactly how far loyalty could lean before it broke. "There were forty burn-stars on that floor, Captain. I counted. So did you."

"Then write it in your report, Sergeant, if your conscience demands it."

"My conscience doesn't demand anything. My conscience took early retirement." Corren spat over his horse's shoulder. "I'm asking why yours hasn't."

Aldric didn't answer. The iron charm lay cold against his chest, and beneath it, patient as winter, the wolf paced its narrow den. He thought about Tammel's face in that flickering instant—pride first, pride before fear, the pure animal joy of a thing done well. He had felt that joy himself once, running through the high pines under a fat moon with the charms locked in a box a mile away, before he understood what running cost. Before he learned to nail the box shut forever.

"They'll catch him eventually," Corren said. "Someone always informs."

"I know."

"And you'll have written *nothing found*, and it'll be your signature on the file when they drag him to Greyford, and they'll assess him, and assessors are not—" Corren stopped himself. Assessors were not careful. Everyone knew what assessors were not.

"Then he has a month," Aldric said. "A month of candles."

"A month," Corren repeated, tasting the phrase like something bitter. "That's mercy, is it? A month?"

"It's what I have," Aldric said, before he could stop himself.

The words came out rawer than he intended, and he felt Corren look at him sideways with those retired-conscience eyes, filing the sentence away in whatever drawer sergeants keep sentences like that. Aldric stared hard at the road. The moon was coming up over the fen, three nights shy of full, a swollen white coin with one bite already taken out of it, and the wolf beneath the iron lifted its head and looked at the moon and looked at the moon and looked at the moon.

Three nights shy. He could feel it in his teeth. On the full-moon nights the charms sang, a thin whining note below hearing, and he slept in his armor with the window barred and dreamed of forests he had never seen, in a country that was not on any map anymore.

* * *

Greyford garrison received them at midnight with torchlight and hot barley soup and the particular cheerlessness of all the crown's frontier posts. Aldric filed his report in the commander's office by candlelight: *Village of [name illegible—signpost defaced], population sixty-one, no unregistered gifts discovered. One juvenile cautioned regarding open-flame hazards. Search concluded without incident.*

*Without incident.* He wrote the words and felt them go through him like a blade through cloth, clean and false.

He was sealing the report when the dispatch rider arrived—mud to the hips, horse blown, a courier's satchel slung across his chest with the wax seals of the central office. Aldric signed for the dispatches out of habit and stood reading them by the guttering candle while the garrison snored around him.

Most was routine. Quotas. Rotations. A reminder that the assessment stations were at capacity and that captains should exercise discretion in the volume of prisoners forwarded—*discretion*, the word doing its obscene work, meaning *fewer*, meaning *the pyres at the capital are smoking night and day and the wind carries the smell into the palace gardens*.

The last dispatch was different. Sealed in black wax, marked for the hunt commander's eyes alone.

Aldric broke it and read it twice.

*Inquisitor Serane departs the capital within the fortnight to review conduct of the Hunt in the western marches. Her Excellency notes with concern the pattern of reports from certain officers: searches conducted without findings, quarries released on technicalities, a rate of capture one-third the campaign average. Her Excellency reminds all officers that mercy shown to the gifted is treason of the subtlest kind, and that the subtlety of treason does not excuse it. Her Excellency will arrive unannounced.*

Aldric set the paper down on the table. The candleflame leaned in the draft from the door, and for a moment the shadow it threw of his own hand looked, on the whitewashed wall, like something with claws.

Serane. He had seen her work once, years ago, in a border town whose name had also been scraped from its signpost. She did not shout. She did not threaten. She simply asked questions in her soft dry voice until people began answering questions they hadn't been asked, confessing to gifts they didn't have, pointing at neighbors, at sisters, at their own children—to make it stop, to make her leave, to buy one more hour of being unremarkable. When she was finished with a town, the town informed on itself. She called it *harvesting*. He had heard her use the word, gently, over wine.

She was coming west. Unannounced. To read his reports.

Aldric stood for a long time in the cold office, listening to the wind come off the fen and the iron charm whisper its thin sub-audible note against his skin, and he understood with perfect clarity that his margin had just closed. No more *nothing found*. No more technicalities, no more misjudged evidence, no more months of candles. Every report he had falsified was a thread, and Serane pulled threads, and when she reached the end of them she would find not a careless officer but a wolf in a captain's armor, and she would not need torches or trials. She would need only to ask him questions in her soft dry voice until the truth came up out of him like blood from under a fingernail.

Unless he gave her something else to look at.

He thought of the western marches—the old March, the blank place on every map, the territory where the erased country had been, where the smugglers ran and the roads looped and the rumors gathered like fog. Rumors had been drifting east all season, the kind of rumor the crown paid gold for: a door, someone said, a door out of Vessany, still opening after a hundred years, for those who could name it.

If a witch were moving toward that door—if a quarry worth the Inquisitor's personal attention were loose in the blank country—then every eye, even Serane's soft dry eyes, would be on the chase. And a captain leading that chase, faithfully, relentlessly, could hide almost anything in its dust.

It would require a witch.

It would require finding one, and keeping her found, and controlling the finding.

Aldric pinched out the candle. In the dark, the wolf rose and pressed its face against the bars of the iron, breathing the moonlit night beyond the window, and far off across the fen, in a village with no name, a boy named Tammel was surely awake in a back room, lighting one more small star on the floor.

Aldric put his hand over the charm at his throat, feeling it cold as a coin from a riverbed, and made his decision the way he made all of them now: alone, in the dark, with the wolf watching.
