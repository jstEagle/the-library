## 5. Crossing the Old March

The boundary of the valley was not marked on any map, because there were no maps, but Paloma knew it the way you know the edge of sleep: a thinning, a loosening, a sense that the world had been holding its breath and finally let it out.

She stopped at the last fence post and looked back once. The herb garden was invisible from here—everything was; Maren's cottage sat in its hollow like a stone in the bottom of a cup—but she could see the smoke of the village rising straight into windless morning air, and for one terrible moment she wanted to walk back down. She wanted the ordinary life back, the one built on never lighting a candle with her mind, on measuring out feverfew and chamomile and pretending the tingle in her fingers was only cold.

The child at the well had been dragged away two days ago. His mother's screaming had carried across three fields.

Paloma turned north and did not look again.

* * *

For the first day, the land behaved. The road followed a river, the river ran downhill, the hills wore their trees like coats. She passed a shrine with a worn stone saint whose face had been rubbed featureless by centuries of travelers' thumbs, and she pressed her own thumb to the smooth place without thinking, asking nothing, because asking felt dangerous now.

On the second day the road began to loop.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it. A road that forked into impossible branches would announce itself; this road simply curved when it should have gone straight, bent east when the sun said west, and every mile or so she passed the same leaning milestone with letters so old they'd been worn to shallow scars. She counted her paces. At four thousand paces the milestone appeared again, same lean, same scar shaped like a half-swallowed O.

She stood before it for a long time, her pack straps biting into her shoulders.

"All right," she said aloud, to the road, to the milestone, to whatever patient thing was watching. "I see you."

The milestone did not answer, but the air changed—the way a room changes when someone in it decides to pay attention to you. And then, faintly, as if through water, she heard a bell. One note. Somewhere ahead, or above, or underneath.

Paloma walked on, and this time the road let her.

* * *

The villages had no names.

That wasn't quite true. They had names the way a scarred face has features: technically present, impossible to hold. She would ask at a well—she was thirsty, and thirst overrode caution—and an old woman drawing water would say, "Oh, this is—" and stop, mouth open, eyes going distant as a person listening for something just past hearing. Then she'd smile and say, "It's called what it's always been called," and hand Paloma a dipper of water that tasted faintly of iron and roses.

By the fourth village, Paloma understood: they had all forgotten together. Or been made to forget. The houses were well-built, the fields tended, the children fat and loud. Only the names were missing, scooped out like the flesh from a fruit whose skin still holds its shape. In the churchyard of the third village, every gravestone bore the same inscription: *HERE LIES*, and then blank stone, and then a date. Some of the dates were older than a century. None were newer.

A century. Maren's whisper came back to her, the last words of a dying woman: *the door and the thirteenth turn*. And beneath them, older, half-remembered from childhood lessons delivered in Maren's dry voice over drying herbs: *Vessany. There was a country called Vessany once. There isn't now.*

Paloma had thought it a fable, a story told to make geography interesting. Now she walked through its corpse, and the corpse was inhabited, and everyone smiled.

* * *

She learned the rules by nearly breaking them.

Rule one: don't sleep outdoors under the open sky. The first night she made camp in a meadow because no village would take coin from a stranger who couldn't say where she was from, and she woke at moonset with frost on her blanket though it was high summer, and every star overhead had rearranged itself into a pattern she almost recognized—a pattern that was *almost* the pattern she'd fallen asleep beneath—and something in the grass had been breathing in unison, slow and vast, and she packed up in the dark and didn't stop until dawn.

Rule two: don't answer if something calls your name. On the sixth day a voice from a stand of birches said, warm and reasonable, *"Paloma Marchetti,"* in her mother's cadence, and her whole body turned toward it before her mind caught up. Her mother had been dead eleven years. She gripped Maren's grimoire through the canvas of her pack and kept walking, and the voice followed her for a quarter mile, growing less like her mother and more like something wearing her mother the way a thief wears a stolen coat, until even the warmth drained out of it and only hunger remained, and then even the hunger gave up.

Rule three: don't use the gift.

This rule she broke on the ninth day, and it saved her life, and it frightened her more than the voice in the birches.

A bridge had rotted through over a ravine, its planks soft as bread. The far side was forty feet of empty air and falling water. There was no way around; the ravine ran for miles in both directions; she knew because she'd spent half a day proving it. Night was coming, and rule one forbade the open sky.

So Paloma stood at the broken bridge with her heart hammering and did the thing she had never once done deliberately in nineteen years of careful, terrified ordinariness. She held out her hand toward the far abutment and *reached*.

Her gift answered before she finished the thought.

It came up through her arm like water finding a channel it had always known, eager, abundant, almost laughing—*yes, yes, we've been waiting*—and the distance between her and the far stone closed to nothing, to a step, and she stepped, and the world folded her across the ravine the way a page folds under a thumb. She stood on solid ground. The bridge was behind her. It had taken less than a heartbeat, and her legs buckled, and she sat down hard in the bracken shaking—not from exhaustion, though she was exhausted, but because it had been *easy*. All those years of clenching down on it, of treating herself like a lamp flame in a paper house, and it had been easy, it had been *waiting*.

The grimoire, against her back, seemed heavier than before.

And somewhere in the dusk behind her, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a bell. One note. Closer than the last time.

* * *

She found Tamsin because Tamsin found her.

It was the tenth day, in a village where the well was dry and the people had the particular silence of those conserving words, and a woman detached herself from the shadow of the tithe barn as naturally as if shadows regularly produced people. She was perhaps forty, weathered brown, dressed in the kind of clothes that are expensive specifically so they don't look it. She had a knife at her belt she wore the way other women wore wedding rings, and eyes the color of strong tea, and she looked Paloma over once, head to boots, with the frank inventory of a horse trader.

"You crossed the ravine at Miller's Bend," she said.

Paloma's blood went cold. "There's no crossing at Miller's Bend."

"No," Tamsin agreed pleasantly. "There isn't. Yet you crossed it. I've been walking your backtrail since yesterday, girl, and you leave tracks like a woman being followed by a very polite ghost." She tilted her head. "You're headed deeper in. Into the March proper. Alone, unguided, with a book you keep touching like a rosary." Her smile showed teeth. "You'll die inside a week, and worse, you'll die *stupid*, wandering loops until winter eats you. Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless you travel with someone who knows which roads are lying today."

They sat in the tithe barn's lee, sharing hard bread and harder cheese, and Paloma learned what Tamsin was. Not a smuggler of goods—or not only. Goods, Tamsin explained, were the small trade. The real trade, the trade that had made her name in the lawless country where Vessany used to be, was memory. Erased memory, orphaned memory, the names and histories that had been scraped off the land a hundred years ago and had to go somewhere. They pooled, Tamsin said, like rainwater. They collected in certain objects, certain places, certain people. And there were those willing to pay gold for a bottle of someone else's past—for a name their grandmother lost, for the taste of a festival that no longer existed anywhere but in glass.

"You sell memories," Paloma said flatly.

"I *broker* memories. Selling implies I made them." Tamsin chewed her cheese with equanimity. "The crown scraped a nation off the map, girl. Somebody had to gather up what fell off the knife. Call it salvage."

"And what do you want from me? I have no money worth the name."

Tamsin set down her bread and looked at her for a long moment, and when she spoke again her voice had lost its trader's lilt and gained something older and more careful.

"I want passage-price. Standard rate. From anyone crossing into the deep March, I take payment in kind: something of themselves. Hair works. Blood works better. Years of memory work best of all, but you're young, you haven't got much worth taking yet." She reached out and touched, lightly, the end of one of Paloma's dark curls. "Hair is fine. A lock of it, freely given, cut by your own hand. It sounds like nothing. It isn't. In this country everything is watched, and a piece of you given freely is a lantern. Things will find you faster now. But things will also *help* you faster, and believe me, in the deep March you will need helping."

Paloma thought of the bell. Twice now, once after each time the gift had stirred. Something was following her already, or had been since the valley, or since Maren died, or since birth—she couldn't know which, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"What's in the deep March?" she asked. "Really?"

Tamsin's tea-colored eyes went somewhere far away.

"Ruins," she said. "A capital city with no name that ghosts still argue about. Roads that go where they're needed rather than where they're pointed. And a door." She said the word lightly, too lightly. "Everyone comes looking for the door eventually. Kings come looking for the door." Her gaze snapped back, sharp as the knife at her belt. "Which is why I'm asking, plain and honest: why does a valley girl with a dead mentor's book cross into the grave of a nation?"

Paloma weighed the lie and found she didn't have the energy for a good one. "Maren wrote to me," she said. "Pages in her own hand. My name on them. A door marked on a map of a country that doesn't exist. And a warning about the thirteenth turn of the moon." She pulled the grimoire from her pack and held it, not opening it. "That's all I know. That's everything."

Something moved behind Tamsin's face—too quick to name, gone before it settled. Interest, or recognition, or regret.

"Thirteenth turn," she repeated softly. "Eleven moons left, then, counting from tonight." She stood, brushing straw from her coat, businesslike again. "Cut the lock. We leave within the hour, while the roads are still honest. I know ways into the old capital that the crown's surveyors never found, and you're going to need every one of them."

"Why?" Paloma asked, scissors already in her hand. "Why are you really doing this?"

Tamsin smiled, and for just a moment the smile was sad.

"Because a hundred years ago somebody cut a lock of my hair too, girl, and I've been trying to buy back what it cost ever since."

Paloma cut. Three curls, dark against her palm, and she felt each one leave her like a small door closing. She folded them into Tamsin's offered cloth, and the moment they left her fingers a bell rang—one clear note, closer than it had ever been, ringing not from ahead or behind but from everywhere at once, from the ground itself, as if the whole erased country had just turned its attention upon her.

Tamsin went very still. Then she looked at Paloma with an expression the smuggler had probably never once worn in her life.

"Well," she said quietly. "It's never rung for *me*. What in the name of everything lost did your mentor leave you, girl?"

Far away to the south, back along the road she had traveled, Paloma heard—or imagined she heard—horses. Many horses. Moving fast.
