## 6. Hounds of the Edict

The river told on her before the hounds did.

Aldric Vane felt it first as a wrongness in the water—a thread of warmth running through the cold current where no warmth should be, the way a hearth leaves its ghost in a room long after the fire is banked. He stood at the edge of the ford with his gauntlets off and his bare hand an inch above the surface, and the iron charm at his throat bit down hard enough to make his teeth ache.

*Gifted,* the charm said. *Close. Upstream.*

He closed his fist and let the pain pass through him like weather. Twelve years of practice had taught him that much: the charms did not want obedience, only stillness. Fight them and they dug deeper. Endure them and they grew bored.

"Captain." Sergeant Brellan reined up beside him, breath steaming in the gray morning. "Hounds lost the scent at the treeline last night. Corwin thinks she doubled back toward Lowmarsh."

"She didn't double back." Aldric pulled his gauntlets on, one finger at a time, unhurried. "She's crossing here."

Brellan squinted at the ford—brown water over pale stones, willows dragging their fingers along both banks, nothing else. "You can't know that."

"I can."

He could not explain how he knew. That was the trap he lived inside: every gift the crown had given him a name for was a gift he had to pretend he did not have. The wolf-sense came with the blood, not the charms—the charms only smothered it—and so he walked through the world hearing things no honest man could hear, and reported to his superiors only what an honest man might plausibly have found. It was a second hunt, the one he ran against himself, and it never ended. Some mornings he woke tired before he'd opened his eyes.

"The girl's been ahead of us since Lowmarsh," he said. "Two days' lead, no horse, carrying weight. She won't risk the open moor—no cover, and the criers have her description in every market town from here to the coast. She'll take the water. Cold kills scent. Cold kills hounds." He swung up into the saddle. "Post six men downstream, four upstream. No torches. If she comes through the willows on the north bank, I'll have her myself."

"And if she runs?"

Aldric looked at the water moving eastward, patient as everything else in the world that did not care about edicts.

"Then we run too," he said. "That's what hunts are."

* * *

She came through the willows two hours past noon, and she almost made it.

Later—much later—he would take the memory out and turn it over like a coin, because there was something in it he could not spend and could not throw away: the moment when he saw her and understood, all in a single breath, that he had been lying to himself about what he would do.

She was smaller than the description. The criers said *woman, dark-haired, twenty years or near it, traveling alone*; what came slipping between the willow trunks was a girl with mud to her knees and a satchel strapped across her back like something she'd fight for, hair escaping its braid in wet dark threads. She waded into the ford with her skirts gathered high and her eyes fixed on the far bank, and she moved like someone who had counted the cost of every step and paid it anyway.

The hounds were downstream. His men were downstream. He had arranged it that way without letting himself ask why.

Aldric stepped his horse out of the trees onto the shingle bar, and the sound of hooves on stone carried across the water like a dropped plate.

She froze mid-current. Turned. And then he saw it—the thing that changed everything, though it took him the rest of the day to admit it had. Not fear. Fear he expected; everyone feared the hunt, the guilty and innocent alike, because the Edict did not trouble itself with the difference. What he saw was calculation. Her eyes went from him to the far bank to the depth of the water behind her, weighing, measuring, *choosing*—and beneath the calculation, banked and guarded, something that looked at him and did not see a crown officer at all.

It saw another animal wearing a man's shape.

His charm flared white-hot. He kept his face still.

"Paloma Marchetti," he said. "Apprentice herbalist, Lowmarsh valley. Wanted under the Edict of the Hunt for concealment of gifted status."

"You have the wrong woman." Her voice was steady. Her hands, gripping the satchel strap, were not. "My name is Paloma Reed. I'm a widow from—"

"From nowhere." He dismounted at the water's edge. "Widows don't memorize the patrol schedule in three towns. Widows don't sleep in ditches rather than sign the waybill at the tollhouse. You've been careful, Mistress Reed. Careful people are the ones worth catching."

The water pushed at her, insistent, and she swayed but held her ground, chin up, which meant she was deciding whether to run and already knowing she couldn't. He watched her arrive at the arithmetic. It was the same arithmetic he performed every dawn behind his own eyes, and watching someone else do it made his chest hurt in a way he had no name for.

"If you come quietly," he said, "you won't be harmed. I'll take you to the provincial assizes at Grayford. There's a process. There are forms. Registration, assessment—" He heard how it sounded even as he said it. A cage described in paperwork was still a cage.

"They take the children first," she said quietly. "In Lowmarsh they took the Fenwick boy. Nine years old. He lit candles with a thought. They put him in an iron cart." Her eyes came up and met his, and there it was again, that seeing. "Is that the process? Is that the form?"

He had no answer that wasn't a lie, and some part of him—the part that had let the gifted boy slip out of Harrow's Back through a gap in the picket line, the part he fed lies every single day—refused to speak the lie to this girl in particular.

So he did the only other thing available. He crossed the ford and took her arm.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow above the river, and Aldric falsified his first report by firelight while she watched him do it.

He wrote: *Pursued suspect northeast from the Old March road. Suspect crossed the Sere at the mill ford under cover of dark and evaded the downstream picket. Signs indicate travel toward the coast road. Continuing pursuit.* He signed it, sealed it, set it aside for the courier pigeon at dawn, and looked up to find her studying him with an expression he couldn't read.

"That's a lie," she said.

"Yes."

"You wrote that I went east. I'm sitting right here."

"You are." He fed a stick to the fire. "Officially, you're ten miles away and gaining."

Her brow creased—not softening, he noted, not yet, but working. "Why?"

Because your eyes saw me. Because you did arithmetic like mine. Because nine-year-old boys in iron carts. Because if I deliver you I become the thing I've spent twelve years pretending not to be, and if I don't deliver you I need a reason to keep you close, and this is the only reason I can write down in ink.

"Because I intend to interrogate you," he said instead, "properly. Thoroughly. At my own pace, away from the assizes and their procedures. You're carrying something from Maren Ostrewe's household. The grimoire. I want to know who else has read it, where you learned what's in it, and whether others in the valley share your"—he chose the word carefully, tasting the irony of it—"condition. That investigation requires discretion. Crowds and clerks scatter the truth."

It was a good lie. It had structure, it had procedure, it had the ring of a man ambitious enough to want credit for a network rather than a single catch. Any inspector reading his dispatches would nod along.

Paloma Marchetti looked at him across the fire for a long time.

"You're lying too," she said at last. "But it's a different lie than the one you wrote down."

Aldric held her gaze and did not move. The charm at his throat pulsed once, slow, like a heart remembering itself. Somewhere in the dark beyond the firelight, a nightjar called, and his senses—unbound, uncharmed in this one hour when he always slipped the collar—painted the world for him in smells and pressures: her fear, banked but present; the river's cold breath; the distant musk of deer; and under all of it, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, something wild and green and alive that clung to her like smoke to wool.

Magic. Hers. But more than hers—older, carried, tended. The grimoire.

"Careful," he said softly.

"I'm always careful." She hugged her knees. "I've been careful my whole life. That's what the valley teaches. Never light the lamp too bright. Never wish too hard. Never let anyone see you be what you are." A pause. "You'd know all about that, wouldn't you, Captain."

The word hung between them. *Captain.* Not an accusation. An invitation, laid down like a card.

He should deny it. Every survival instinct he possessed stood at attention and screamed the denial. Instead he heard himself say, "What makes you think—"

"Iron charms suppress, they don't erase. Maren taught me that much before she taught me anything else. I could feel yours from thirty feet. This morning, at the ford—it flared when you looked at me. Like recognizes like." She said it simply, without triumph, and somehow that was worse. "You smell like winter, Captain. Underneath the leather and the iron. Like snow on fur."

For a moment the fire cracked and settled and Aldric Vane, captain of the hunt, sat perfectly still in the ruins of twelve years of discipline. Then, because the truth between two liars is the only currency that holds its value, he reached up and touched the charm at his throat.

"Wolf-blood," he said. "Three generations suppressed. My grandfather wore iron until it ate through his skin. My father died of a fever the charms wouldn't let his body fight. And I—" He laughed, short and airless. "I lead the crown's hunt for the gifted. I find them. I register them. I put them in carts. And every morning I buckle this on and thank the saints nobody can smell what I am."

"Why?" she asked. Not cruelly. Genuinely.

"Because the alternative was being found." He stared into the fire. "There's no middle place, Mistress Marchetti. There's the cage and there's the cart. I chose the cage and they gave me keys and called it a career."

"The valley was a cage too," she said. "We just called it safety."

Something passed between them then, across the fire, across the whole architecture of lies they'd each built their lives inside—the recognition of two people who had survived the same war from opposite sides of the same wall. He watched her decide something. He felt himself deciding something back.

* * *

In the morning he gave her a choice, because a prisoner obeys and he needed something else from her, though he would not name it for another eleven moons.

"The report says you fled east," he told her, saddling his horse in the gray light. "So east we go. You ride with me. You answer my questions—honestly, when honesty costs you nothing, and cleverly when it does; I'm not naive. In return, no irons, no cart, and I keep your name out of every dispatch until I choose otherwise. When we reach Grayford, I'll have extracted everything useful and released you into the countryside with a warning. That's the story. Can you live inside it?"

She stood by the dead fire with the satchel across her body, both hands on the strap, and he saw her weigh it the way she'd weighed the ford: the cost of every step, paid anyway.

"And where are we really going?" she asked.

Ah. There it was. Not *are you lying to me*—she'd already answered that herself—but *what is the lie for*. She wanted to know the direction of his true compass, the way a sailor reads another ship's wake.

"I follow the evidence," Aldric said. "And the evidence says a trained herbalist doesn't flee into open country with no destination. Someone told you where to go. Something about a door."

Her face did not change. It was a very good face, controlled and calm, and that was precisely how he knew: the stillness was purchased, and expensive.

"There is no door," she said.

"No," he agreed, swinging up into the saddle. "There isn't. Which is why we're riding east to look for it."

She hesitated a moment longer—long enough that he understood she was giving him one last chance to be simply her captor, one last chance for her to hate him cleanly. Then she took his offered hand and mounted behind him, her satchel pressed between them like a third passenger, warm at his back where no warmth should be.

The charm at his throat pulsed, slow and hungry.

Aldric turned the horse east, toward the erased country and the thirteenth moon, and told himself—as he would tell himself for many nights after, right up until telling himself became unnecessary—that he was doing this for the investigation.

Behind him, quiet as a secret, Paloma Marchetti began to count the days.
