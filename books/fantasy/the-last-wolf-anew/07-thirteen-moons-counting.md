## 7. Thirteen Moons, Counting

The smuggler's camp sat in the hollow of a hill that did not appear on any map Paloma had ever seen—which, she was learning, meant very little out here. Tamsin's wagon leaned against an old stone wall like a tired animal, its paint flaked to ghost-patterns, and a cookfire crackled in front of it with the particular cheerfulness of fire that has been paid for.

"Sit," Tamsin said, and threw a blanket at her rather than handed it. "You've got river mud to your knees and you're shivering like a struck bell. Sit."

Paloma sat. Aldric remained standing, as he always did, positioned—she had begun to notice this with something like amusement—between her and every possible direction of approach. He had taken off his crown colors two days ago, or rather Tamsin had taken them off him, stripping the blue-and-silver sash from his shoulder with a smuggler's knife and saying, *This is worth more than you are, captain, so I'll keep it.* What remained was a man in a gray travel coat who looked, if one didn't look too closely at his eyes, entirely unremarkable.

Tamsin looked closely at everything. That was her trade.

"Now," she said, settling onto her wagon-step with a clay cup of something steaming and foul-smelling, "you want to hear about the door. So let me tell you how the story goes, and then let me tell you how the story actually goes, because those are two different stories, and people die of confusing them."

Aldric shifted his weight. "If this is a song and dance—"

"It's not a dance, captain, it's an education, and those cost extra." But there was no malice in it. Tamsin drank, grimaced at her own brew, and began.

"The legend first. You'll have heard pieces of it even in your dead valley, because dead things leak. A hundred years ago there was a country here called Vessany. Rich country. Old magic, old roads, old kings. And then one morning it wasn't there—not burned, not conquered, just *gone*. The maps went blank. The neighbors' treaties referred to a nation that no longer answered letters. Travelers who walked in from the east walked out the west without ever knowing they'd crossed anything at all." She tapped her cup against her knee. "That's erasure, that is. Not destruction—erasure. Somebody wanted Vessany forgotten so thoroughly that even the land forgot, and somebody had the power to make it stick."

"And the door?" Paloma asked quietly.

"The door is the part that wouldn't forget." Tamsin's voice dropped, not for drama—Paloma was beginning to know her well enough to tell—but because some things deserved quiet. "Vessany kept a door. Not a door in a wall—a door *out*. Out of what, nobody agrees. Out of the country, out of the world, out of the reach of kings and winters and grief. It opened once a century, on the thirteenth turn of the moon, when the moon makes a full circuit and then keeps walking, one turn past where the year says it should stop. The thirteenth turn is the moon's own secret. Everything else runs on twelve—months, hours, zodiacs—but the moon counts to thirteen when it feels like it, and in that extra turning, the door opens."

"For how long?" Aldric asked.

"One night." Tamsin smiled without warmth. "One night in a hundred years. Which is why the legend survives at all, mind—it's useless as a plan and perfect as a story. Who builds their hopes around a single evening per century?"

*Someone with nowhere else to go,* Paloma thought. She said nothing. The grimoire pressed against her ribs inside her coat, warm as a sleeping bird.

"And now," Tamsin said, "the story as it actually goes." She leaned forward. Firelight caught the scar that ran through her left eyebrow, the one she never explained. "I deal in memories. Bought and sold since I was fifteen, before my voice settled. I've held a hundred people's recollections of the old borders, the old songs, the shape of Vessany's capital towers—and here's the thing, girl. Every memory I've ever bought of Vessany ends the same way. Mid-sentence. Mid-meal. A woman remembers setting down a spoon, and the next thing she knows she's standing in a wheat field that shouldn't exist, holding the spoon. Nobody remembers leaving. Because nobody *left*."

The fire popped. Somewhere up the slope, a nightjar called its two-note question into the dark.

"You're saying they're still here," Aldric said slowly. "The Vessani."

"I'm saying the land is wrong in ways wrongness doesn't usually manage." Tamsin shrugged. "Roads loop back on themselves. Villages have no names because names were the first thing taken. I've traded memories to men who grew up in those nameless villages, and do you know what they remember? Nothing before a certain morning. Their whole lives begin at once, like a play with the first act torn out. Whatever happened here a century ago, it didn't end. It's still happening, very slowly, under the skin of the world." She drained her cup. "And the door is the only stitch in the seam. The only way through that the erasure couldn't close—because a door, by nature, has to open from both sides."

Paloma's hands had gone cold despite the fire. "When," she said. "When is the next thirteenth turn."

Tamsin looked at her for a long moment—the look of a woman pricing something—and then, unexpectedly, gave it away free.

"Eleven nights," she said. "The moon turns thirteenth on the new moon, eleven days from now, over the ruins of the capital. If the legend holds. If the door still answers. If whoever stands before it knows what to say." She stood, joints cracking, and jerked her chin toward the wagon. "There's straw in the back and a lamp with oil enough till midnight. Sleep. Tomorrow we talk price for taking you the rest of the way, and you'll want your wits about you, because I'm going to be unreasonable."

She vanished into the wagon. The flap fell shut behind her.

For a while neither Paloma nor Aldric spoke. The fire burned down to a red heart. Above them the sky was doing something the valley sky never did—too many stars, too bright, as though the erased country had been given someone else's allotment of light.

"Eleven nights," Aldric said finally.

"You counted too."

"I count everything. Habit." He crouched by the fire, forearms on his knees, and stared into it with an expression she was learning to read: duty and doubt arm-wrestling behind his face. "Eleven nights is either a fortune or a funeral, Marchetti. We'll know which soon enough."

*Marchetti.* He'd used her surname twice now, always carefully, the way a man handles a borrowed tool. She wondered what he'd heard about the family, and decided she didn't want to ask yet. Some questions changed the air too much.

"Get some sleep," she said, surprising herself with the steadiness of it. "I'll take first watch."

He glanced up. For a heartbeat something moved across his winter eyes—not suspicion; something quieter, almost startled, as if kindness in his direction required translation. Then he nodded once and lay down on the far side of the fire, boots on, sword hand loose, the way soldiers sleep even when they trust the person keeping watch.

Especially, perhaps, when they trust the person keeping watch.

* * *

She could not sleep anyway, so she practiced.

Not immediately. First she made herself useful—fed the fire, checked Tamsin's picket lines, refilled the water skins from the stream that ran behind the hill with water that tasted faintly of iron and, underneath the iron, of something sweeter she chose not to name. The land here was wrong; Maren would have said *don't drink what you can't name*, but Maren had also written pages and pages addressed to her by name in a book about a door in a country that didn't exist, so perhaps Maren's rules deserved reexamination.

By the time the moon cleared the ridge—it was fat, waxing, hurrying toward its forbidden thirteenth lap—the camp was silent and Paloma had run out of chores. She sat cross-legged by the embers and unwrapped the grimoire.

The pages written to her were the ones she returned to. *Palo-mia*, Maren had written, using the diminutive she'd only used when Paloma was small and feverish, *if you are reading this, then the valley has failed, or I have, and either way the fault is mine and the path is yours.* Below that, the map: Vessany rendered in ink gone brown as dried blood, its mountains and rivers drawn with the confidence of someone who had walked them all, and in the northwest corner, where the capital should be, a small square mark and beneath it three words in Maren's cramped hand:

*It remembers names.*

She had read those words forty times. They still raised the hair on her arms.

In the valley, her gift had been a thing she strangled nightly. Small magics came to her unbidden—a wilted cutting greening under her fingers, a kettle boiling before the flame touched it, a candle lighting when she was frightened and thinking hard about light. She had learned to smother each one at birth, to think dull thoughts, to keep her hands busy and her mind flatter than the valley floor. Maren had taught her the suppression as diligently as any herb-craft: *Breathe past it, child. Want nothing. Wondering is a doorway and doorways go both ways.*

But the valley was four days behind her, and wonder had gotten in.

She laid her palm flat above the embers—not touching, hovering—and did the thing she had never once done deliberately in twenty-two years. She reached for the heat.

It came like a dog hearing its name.

The warmth leapt up her arm, joyful, shameless, pooling in her palm until her skin glowed faintly gold, and Paloma gasped—not from pain, there was none, but from the sheer *eagerness* of it. In the valley her gift had been a fugitive. Here it was a creature let off a chain, and it pressed against her hand and her ribs and her heartbeat as if it had been waiting a hundred years, as if it knew whose blood it belonged to and where that blood was from.

"Careful," said Aldric's voice, low, from the darkness.

She hadn't heard him wake. Of course she hadn't; he was wolf-blooded under those iron charms, whatever exactly that meant, and wolves did not sleep like men. He sat up slowly, watching her glowing hand with an expression she couldn't parse—hunger? fear? recognition?

"I'm not hurting anything," she said.

"No." His voice was rough. "That's not what I—" He stopped. Started again. "In the villages I've cleared, gifted folk learn to hide by making themselves smaller. Every day, smaller. Until some of them can't find themselves anymore." He nodded at her hand. "You're not doing that."

"No," she agreed, and heard the wonder in her own voice, and did not crush it. "I'm not."

She turned her palm upward and, deliberately, deliberately, asked the ember-fire to rise.

A thread of flame lifted out of the coals, thin as a ribbon, and stood upright in the cold air between them. It bent toward her the way a plant bends toward sun. When she breathed in, it lengthened; when she breathed out, it swayed. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever made, and it was almost nothing—a party trick, a candle-flame's cousin—and tears pricked her eyes, which was absurd, which she refused to explain to a man who hunted people like her for a living.

Except he didn't, quite, anymore. He falsified reports. He let boys slip through hedges in the dark. He sat across from her gift right now without reaching for iron or rope, and his face held none of the edict in it at all.

"Does it frighten you?" she asked. "Seeing that."

He considered the little flame for a long time. The light moved in his eyes and made them less wintry, or maybe revealed that they had never been as wintry as advertised.

"My grandmother had it," he said at last. "The blood. She could hear weather three days off, feel storms in her teeth. My father beat it out of her vocabulary if not her bones." His jaw worked. "They put iron charms on me at fourteen. Cold metal against the skin, every day since. It doesn't hurt. That's the worst part. It just... mutes everything. The world through iron is like music through a wall." He looked at the flame. "So no. It doesn't frighten me. It sounds like music."

Paloma let the flame sink gently back into the coals. Her hand trembled—not with fear, with fullness, like a cup filled exactly to the brim. She closed the grimoire. Eleven nights. Eleven nights to walk to a ruined capital in a country that had been deleted, and stand before a door that remembered names, and say something she did not yet know how to say.

"Aldric." She used his given name for the first time; she saw him register it. "When we get there—if the door opens—what will you do?"

He was quiet long enough that the nightjar called again, closer now.

"Honest answer?" he said. "I don't know. Ask me in ten days."

It wasn't reassurance. But it was true, and after a life spent among the valley's careful fictions, she found she preferred truth even when it had teeth.

She wrapped the grimoire, banked the fire, and took her watch. Behind her, Aldric slept—or lay still pretending to, which amounted to the same courtesy. Above the eastern ridge the moon climbed, swollen and silver and counting.

Ten days, she corrected herself, as the sky paled toward a dawn the erased land seemed to produce reluctantly, grudgingly, like a debt paid in worn coin. Ten days, and somewhere ahead a city with no name waited to tell her why her mentor had died whispering about doors—and why, in the last hour of her long guarded life, Maren had looked at Paloma with such terrible tenderness, and said, as her final word, not goodbye.

*Run.*
