## 8. The City with No Name

The road forgot where it was going three miles from the city.

Paloma felt it first in her feet—the way the packed earth seemed to hesitate beneath her boots, as if the land itself could not remember that a capital had ever stood at the end of this track. Aldric felt it too. She watched him slow his horse, his head tilting the way it did when his suppressed senses strained against their iron leash.

"The map says there should be walls," he said. "A gate called the Lion's Mouth. A bridge over the Serle."

The Serle was there—a wide, slow river the color of old pewter—but no bridge crossed it. Instead, stepping-stones of pale stone rose from the water in an impossible arc, each one carved with letters worn nearly smooth. Tamsin had told them: *You don't cross into Vessany. You remember your way across.*

Paloma dismounted and knelt at the river's edge. The nearest stone bore a single glyph, half-erased by a century of water. She knew it. She had seen it a hundred times in Maren's grimoire, in the margins, in the headers of pages written to her by name.

"Serene," she said quietly, sounding it out. "It's a word. They're all words."

"What kind of words?"

She touched the second stone without stepping onto it. *Threshold.* The third: *Welcome.* The fourth made her breath catch, because she knew it not from the book but from memory—from Maren's voice, murmuring over seedlings on summer evenings when she'd thought Paloma wasn't listening.

*Home.*

"They're a sentence," Paloma said. "Or part of one. The crossing is a sentence, and you have to mean it."

Aldric swung down beside her, close enough that she could smell the iron of his charms and, beneath it, something wilder—pine and cold air and wolf. He had been quieter since the ford, since the lie they had agreed to see through together. Quieter, but not colder. He looked at the stones the way he looked at everything now: as if reassessing what he'd been ordered to hunt.

"A door out of an erased country," he said, "and even the way in is a riddle."

"Not a riddle. A courtesy." Paloma stepped onto the first stone. The glyph flared faintly warm under her boot—not light, exactly, but the memory of light, like a hearth long gone cold that still held shape in its ashes. "Vessany didn't want just anyone walking out. It wanted people who belonged."

She crossed the whole sentence before Aldric moved. When she reached the far bank and turned, he was still standing at the water's edge, and for a moment his face held something she had never seen on it—something almost like fear, or like longing, though she couldn't have said which, and neither, she suspected, could he.

Then he crossed, stone by stone, and did not look back either.

*

The city had no name, so the city had every name.

That was how it announced itself, anyway. One moment the road ran through scrubland and broken terraces; the next, walls rose around them—or rather, the suggestion of walls, stone gone translucent in places, like breath on glass. Gates stood open where gates should have been barred. Streets unspooled ahead in perfect order, cobbled, lamp-lit, lined with houses whose windows glowed with evening firelight—and all of it empty, all of it faintly wrong, like a stage set struck between performances.

Aldric rode with his hand nowhere near his sword, which was its own kind of alarm. He had explained it once, tersely, on the road: steel drawn inside Vessany's borders would be answered. By what, he hadn't said. Paloma hadn't asked.

"It's replaying," she said. She reined in at a corner where a baker's shop stood whole and warm-smelling, bread ghosting through its open shutters, though no baker had lived here in a hundred years. "The night it ended. It's been replaying ever since."

"You sound certain."

"I'm not certain of anything anymore." But she was, a little. The grimoire had grown heavier in her saddlebag as they'd ridden east—heavier every day, as if the book were drinking the country back into itself, page by page. "Maren wrote that erased things don't die. They wait. A country is a very large thing to wait."

They left the horses tethered at a fountain that flowed upward, silver threads rising into a stone basin and vanishing there, drunk by nothing. On foot, they followed the great avenue toward the heart of the city, where a citadel climbed against the darkening sky—half real, half remembered, its towers flickering like a candle guttering.

And everywhere, the people.

Not people. Not quite. They moved through doorways and market stalls and children's games, translucent as smoke given purpose, and none of them saw Paloma or Aldric. A woman shook out laundry that cast no shadow. Two old men argued cheerfully over a dice game whose pieces lay scattered and forgotten in real dust. A boy ran past them laughing, chasing nothing, and Paloma turned to follow him with her eyes and found her throat tight and her eyes burning.

"They don't know," she whispered. "They're living their last night over and over, and they don't know it's their last night."

"No." Aldric's voice was rough. He had stopped walking. He was staring at a doorway across the street, where a young man in a soldier's coat embraced a girl and pressed something small into her hand—a ring, maybe, or a coin—and the girl was laughing and crying at once, and neither of them was looking at the sky, where above the citadel the stars were beginning to go out one by one, swallowed by a darkness that was not cloud. "No, I don't suppose they do."

Paloma had never heard him sound like that. She had heard him hard and dry and wry; she had heard him lie fluently to his own soldiers at the ford. This was none of those. This was grief arriving a century late, with no one left alive to claim it.

"We should keep moving," she said gently. "Before full dark."

He nodded once and walked on, and if his hand brushed hers as they passed under the lamplight, neither of them remarked on it.

*

The citadel's great hall still had its doors open.

Inside, the ghosts were thicker, the replay sharper, as though the building itself were the drumskin and the memory the drumbeat. Torches burned along walls hung with banners bearing a crest Paloma recognized from the grimoire's frontispiece: a white stag crowned in thorns. Courtiers clustered and murmured. Guards stood at rigid attention. And at the far end of the hall, on a dais beneath a canopy of state, a king sat listening to a man who gestured wildly at a table spread with maps.

Paloma moved closer, and the hall obliged her—the scene sharpened, sounds resolving out of the general murmur like figures surfacing from deep water.

"—cannot hold, Majesty. The borders are already failing. The March lords are dead or fled, and the enemy knows what lies beneath the city. If they take the door—"

"Then no one takes it." The king's voice was quiet, and that quietness was worse than shouting would have been. He was younger than Paloma had imagined—dark-haired, hollow-eyed, wearing his crown like a debt. "I built this country, Corven. Stone by stone. I will not hand its bones to butchers."

"There must be another way."

"There is another way." The king rose. Around the hall, the court went silent in a ripple, like wind through wheat. "There has always been another way. Bring me the Warden's daughter."

Paloma's blood went cold.

The word *Warden* landed somewhere behind her sternum and stayed there. Maren had never used it. Maren had been an herb-witch in a valley that forgot itself, who taught children the names of plants and taught Paloma, alone and secretly, to keep her small gift folded up small as a moth. But the grimoire's final chapters had been growing clearer as they traveled, ink darkening on the page, and Paloma had read fragments—*the Warden's line*, *the valley that forgets so the key may sleep*—that she had not yet dared to think all the way through.

Beside her, Aldric had gone very still. "Paloma," he said low. "Your hands."

She looked down. Light was leaking through her closed fingers—not from any spell she'd cast, but from the grimoire itself, which she realized she was clutching through the leather of her satchel, the book pulling toward the dais like iron to lodestone.

On the dais, the king descended the steps, and the crowd of ghosts parted for him, and the replay carried them all forward through the last hour of a nation—through corridors, down stairways cut into living rock, past wards that flared and died, until they came to it.

The door.

It was smaller than Paloma had expected. Smaller than anything that important had any right to be: a plain arch of grey stone set into the cavern wall, uncarved, unlabeled, utterly ordinary except that looking at it hurt—not the eyes, but something deeper, the way looking at a loved one's coffin hurts. The air around it bent. It did not reflect torchlight. It did not seem entirely *there*.

And before it stood a girl.

Sixteen, perhaps seventeen. Dark-haired, olive-skinned, with a stubborn set to her chin that Paloma knew intimately, because she shaved around that same chin every morning in whatever water the road provided. The girl wore a valley smock. She was crying, but she was not struggling, and the guards around her were not touching her, and Paloma understood—with a lurch that turned her stomach—that the girl had come willingly.

"Say it freely," the king said softly, kneeling before her, and his crown caught the torchlight and threw it back wrong, "or not at all. That is the only law the door keeps. It cannot be forced. It can only be *given*."

"My family," the girl said. Her voice cracked. "You swear my family goes free. Out. Through. Before you seal it."

"On my life and crown."

The girl wiped her face with her sleeve, in a gesture so familiar, so *Paloma's own*, that Aldric's hand found her shoulder without either of them deciding it should. Then the girl straightened, faced the grey arch, and spoke a name.

Paloma did not hear it.

Not because the ghosts' voices failed, but because the moment the name left the girl's lips, the world *withheld it*—the syllables folding into the stone like water into sand, taken, kept, owned forever. The arch drank the name and shivered, and light poured out of it, terrible and gold, and the girl smiled through her tears as though she had been let out of a cage after all—

—and then the replay broke apart, ghosts unraveling into motes, torchlight collapsing inward, and the cavern was dark and real and a hundred years dead, and the door was only a door-shaped absence in the rock, waiting.

Paloma realized she was on her knees. She didn't remember falling.

*

They made camp in a guardroom off the ruined corridor, where a hearth still drew properly, as if the city's bones remembered warmth. Aldric got a fire going while Paloma sat with the grimoire open across her knees, reading by flame-light the pages that had spent a week refusing to make sense.

The ink had finished darkening. The last chapter was legible now, and it was written in Maren's hand, and it was dated the day Paloma had been born.

*Maren of the line of Wardens,* it began, *born in the city that has no name, in the year the door was sealed. My mother gave her sister's daughter to the door, and the door took a name instead of a life, and the country died anyway, because a seal is not a salvation—it is only a postponement.*

Paloma read it twice. Three times. Her lips moved without sound.

*A seal holds while the giver's blood endures within the land that received her. My family fled to the valley the king's wardens raised—magic-dead, mapless, forgotten on purpose—so that the bloodline might hide and the seal might hold. I am the last Warden born inside Vessany. The duty passed to me when my mother died, and I carried it out of a burning country and buried it in a garden of herbs, and I swore I would never let a child of ours learn enough magic to matter.*

*I failed. Paloma, forgive me. I failed because you shine.*

The page blurred. Paloma blinked and found her cheeks wet.

"She was born here," she said aloud. Her voice came out strange, flattened, like a message passed through too many hands. "Maren. In this city. Under this—" She gestured helplessly at the dark corridor beyond the firelight, where the door waited in its cavern. "Her family was the seal. The valley isn't a refuge, Aldric. It never was. It's a—"

"A cage," Aldric said quietly. "Built around a bloodline. Built around you."

"Not me. Before me. Generations of us, born and buried in that valley, and none of us knowing why we couldn't leave, why the roads always looped home, why the magic wouldn't wake there—" She laughed, and it was an awful sound. "I thought I was hiding my gift from the valley. The valley was hiding *me* from everything else."

Aldric crouched across the fire from her. In the shifting light his winter-pale eyes looked almost warm, and there was ash in his hair and exhaustion carved into his face, and he said nothing for a long time. He was learning, she thought, when silence was worth more than comfort.

Finally: "The girl in the vision. The one who gave her name."

"My grandmother's sister. Or great-aunt. Some aunt." Paloma pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "And the door doesn't want blood. It doesn't want sacrifice, not really. It wants a *name*. Freely given. Permanently." She looked up. "Do you understand what that means? Whoever opens it—"

"—loses theirs," Aldric finished. His jaw tightened. "So the door opens for you, and you stand on the threshold, and everyone else walks through to freedom while you stay. Forever. As what?"

"As the keeper. As the seal." Paloma closed the grimoire. "As the new Maren. As the next aunt in a line of aunts."

The fire popped. Somewhere far off in the dead city, a bell began to ring—no rope pulled it, no bell existed anymore, but the sound came anyway, tolling slow and bronze through the ruins, marking some hour that no clock in the world still counted. Paloma listened to it and thought: *eleven nights until the thirteenth turn. Eleven nights, and the door already knows my name, and I am starting to suspect I've known its price since before I was born.*

Aldric fed another branch to the fire. When he spoke again, his voice was careful, stripped of command, just a tired man asking an honest question.

"When the moon turns," he said, "what are you going to do?"

Paloma looked at the dark corridor, where a door-shaped absence waited in the rock for someone with her blood and her courage and her name.

"I'm going to stop pretending I don't already know," she said.
