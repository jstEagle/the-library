## 9. Iron Charms

The charms had been singing to him for three days.

Not aloud—nothing so merciful as sound. It was more like a toothache of the soul, a low grinding pressure behind his eyes that came and went with the wind. Aldric Vane had worn the iron since he was nine years old, when a hedge-witch in his father's village had pressed the cold band against his brow and told him, *This will keep the wolf quiet, boy. Feed it nothing and it will stay small.* For twenty-two years he had fed it nothing. He had starved it so thoroughly that some nights he could almost believe the witch's promise: that the gift would die of neglect, that he would grow old as an ordinary man with ordinary senses and no howling thing pacing the walls of his chest.

But iron was not patient, and neither was the wolf.

He stood at the edge of the checkpoint at Stonebridge Ford and watched the crown's soldiers turn over a farmer's cart, and the charms sang, and beneath the singing he could hear everything—the river's cold argument with its stones, the heartbeat of the horse three fields off, the particular rust-and-rot smell of fear on the men waiting in the line. The world had become too loud. That was how it always began.

"Captain." Sergeant Hollis, broad and incurious, saluted with a half-eaten apple still in his fist. "Inquisitor's writ says every traveler crossing south gets the full inspection. Names, business, and—" he patted the lead-lined box at his hip, the one holding the testing stones— "the touch."

"I know what the writ says."

"You look tired, Captain, if you don't mind my saying."

"I mind," Aldric said, and walked the line.

The line was short this morning: two wool merchants, an old woman with a goat that hated everyone equally, a family of tanners with their wagon reeking of lye and lime, and—

And her.

Paloma Marchetti stood four places back with her hood up and her hands folded around the strap of her satchel, wearing the expression he had come to think of as her armor: mild, forgettable, a face like a closed book left spine-out on a shelf. Three weeks ago he had dragged her out of a river with her wrists bound, intending to deliver her to the Edict and be done with the whole rotten hunt. Instead he had written a false report, invented a fictional interrogation, and appointed himself her escort under the thinnest pretext ever sold to a commanding officer. *Prisoner transfer,* his reports said. *Witch under close watch, en route for formal assessment.* Every word of it a lie, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said it, because saying it aloud would make the lie into something they had chosen together rather than something that had simply happened to them.

She glanced at him now—just once, just long enough to ask a question without asking it—and he gave her the smallest shake of his head. *Don't run. Running is death.*

Her chin dipped a fraction. She stayed in line.

*You fool,* he told himself, walking back toward the inspection table. *You absolute fool. You are standing between a hunted woman and eleven soldiers with lead boxes full of truth-stones, and your own charms are failing, and if you so much as flinch wrong today they will put you in irons beside her.* The wolf turned over lazily inside him, amused. It liked the danger. It had always liked the danger; that was the horror of it.

"Hollis," he said. "Who's working the stones?"

"Corporal Vey and Corporal Dann. Why?"

"Because Vey holds them too long and Dann holds them too hard, and either one of them will have half this line crying by noon." He unclasped the flap of his command case and drew out the writ, letting the wax seal catch the light. "New orders from the Inquisitor's office. Testing stones are to be used only on suspicion, not wholesale. We're frightening the trade routes half to death, and the quartermaster's already complaining about grain not moving."

Hollis squinted at the document. Aldric held it steady, though his pulse was loud enough in his own ears that he feared the man might hear it too. The writ was real. The orders on it were not—they were his own careful forgery, inked two nights ago by candlelight while Paloma slept ten feet away and he told himself, again, that he was only buying time before turning her in. Time for what? Time had stopped being a coherent concept somewhere around the third week.

"Suspicion only," Hollis read slowly. "That'll make the Inquisitor's people twitch."

"The Inquisitor's people can twitch in their offices. Out here we follow the captain's writ." He let a blade of frost enter his voice. "Unless you'd like to write Serane yourself and explain why the ford backed up six miles."

"No, sir." Hollis bit his apple. "Suspicion only. Understood."

Aldric nodded and turned to the table—and the world lurched.

It came without warning, the way it always did when the iron cracked further: a flood. Every scent in the valley arrived at once, sorted and named by something ancient in his blood—the tanners' lye, the old woman's goat-milk and misery, river silt, wet iron, woodsmoke, and underneath all of it, threading through the morning like a signature, *her*: rosemary and paper and something faintly green, the smell of a garden he had never visited but knew anyway. His hearing opened like a door kicked down. He heard the merchants' whispered haggling forty feet away. He heard Hollis chewing. He heard the heart of the horse, the hearts of the soldiers, the quick anxious drum of Paloma Marchetti's pulse—and worse, he heard his own heart answer it, falling into rhythm, two drums finding the same beat.

*No.* He gripped the edge of the table. *Not here. Not now.*

The charm at his throat—a flat disc of forged iron on a leather cord, the oldest of the three—gave a single sharp *crack*, audible only to him, or so he prayed. Pain lanced through his collarbone like a splinter of winter. The flood receded a hand's breadth, but it did not recede fully. The wolf had found the gap and put its shoulder against it.

"Captain?" Hollis was looking at him oddly. "You've gone gray as the stones."

"Bad night's sleep." He made himself straighten. "Next traveler."

The wool merchants passed without incident—no suspicion, no stones, just names taken and waved through. The old woman and her goat followed. Then the tanners, their wagon creaking, their children staring at the soldiers with the wide blank eyes of children who had learned early what soldiers meant. And then there was only her.

Paloma stepped up to the table and lowered her hood.

Up close, in daylight, without a river between them, she was not what the wanted-notices promised. The notices called her dangerous, corrupted, marked by the old blood. What stood before him was a tired woman with ink stains on her fingers and a healing scrape along her jaw from the ford, whose eyes did the thing he hated most: they looked at him and saw him. Not the captain. Not the uniform. Him.

"Name and business," said Corporal Dann, who had not gotten the memo about restraint and was already reaching for the stone box.

"Aldric handles this one," Aldric said quickly. "She's mine. Prisoner transfer."

Dann blinked. "Transfer? To where, Captain?"

"To the Inquisitor's field office at Marrow Cross." The lie slid out smooth as river water; he had rehearsed it for exactly this moment. "Assessment and registration. She's registered, documented, and collared under my authority." He touched his own throat where a collar would sit, a gesture the soldiers understood, a gesture that was also, horribly, the exact place his charm had cracked. "You want to countermand a captain's custody order, Corporal, take it up with Serane."

Dann looked at Paloma. Paloma looked at the ground with magnificent docility. And then, because the world had apparently decided today was the day everything would go wrong, Dann shrugged and said, "Writ says suspicion only now, but procedure's procedure—prisoner transfers get verified. One touch on the stone, miss, and you're on your way."

The box opened. Inside lay a chip of grey testing-stone, dull as grave-dirt. Aldric knew how they worked; he'd used them often enough on other people's lives. They drank magic the way sand drank rain. A gifted hand within arm's reach and the stone warmed, darkened, woke. It did not care about iron charms. Iron muzzled a gift; it did not erase it.

Paloma's hands were folded in her lap. Her breathing hadn't changed. But Aldric could hear her heart now, could hear the way it stumbled once and steadied, and he could see the calculation move behind her eyes like a fish under ice.

"There's been a mistake," she said.

Everyone looked at her.

"I'm not the prisoner." She lifted her head, and her voice changed—not louder, but somehow *wider*, the way a room feels bigger when a lamp is lit. "I'm the courier. I've been traveling with Captain Vane since Coldharbor because the Inquisitor's office doesn't trust its own dispatches to the regular post. Ask him." She turned those clear grey eyes on Aldric, and there was steel in them and something else, something almost like an apology. "Show them the seal, Captain. The dispatch case. You've been carrying it since Coldharbor."

There was no dispatch case. There was no courier arrangement. She was improvising a story out of thin air with eleven armed men watching, and doing it with such calm certainty that even Aldric felt, for one vertiginous instant, that maybe it was true.

"My dispatch case," Aldric repeated.

"Under the writs," she said gently, as though reminding a man of his own household. "The sealed ones. The ones you're not supposed to open."

Slowly—there was nothing else to do; hesitation was the only thing that killed a lie—Aldric opened his command case again. His fingers found the false-bottomed compartment he kept for documents he should not possess, and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, lay... nothing. He felt the emptiness of it, felt the story dying in the air—

—and then his fingers closed on paper. Actual paper, folded, sealed with a blob of grey wax stamped with a mark he didn't recognize: a door, drawn in a single unbroken line.

He had never put anything there. He was certain of it. He was also certain, with the strange doubled certainty of a man whose senses were cracking open, that the seal smelled faintly of rosemary and green things.

He handed it to Dann without a word. Dann broke the seal, scanned the page, and his shoulders dropped into deference. "Courier confirmed. Apologies, Captain. Miss." He snapped the box shut over the untouched testing-stone. "Move along."

Paloma gathered her satchel. As she passed Aldric, close enough that only he could hear, she murmured, "Your charm is bleeding. I could smell the iron burning from the line. Walk. Now."

* *

They walked.

Out of the checkpoint, across the ford, up the southern road until the soldiers were specks and then memories. Aldric said nothing for the first mile because speaking required breath and breath required a calm he did not have. The pain in his collarbone had settled into a deep cold ache, and beneath it the wolf pressed against the broken place, sniffing at freedom, and every bird that crossed the road he heard in perfect detail, and twice he caught himself cataloguing Paloma's footsteps by weight and mood alone.

At the mile-marker, a leaning stone carved with a name no map remembered anymore, she finally stopped and faced him.

"Open your collar."

"That's not—"

"Open it, or I open it for you, and I promise my way is less gentle."

He worked the clasp with fingers that had gone clumsy. The cord fell away. The charm swung free—and in the daylight he saw what he had been refusing to feel: a hairline fracture straight through the iron disc, edges blackened, as though the metal had been heated from within and split. The second charm at his wrist showed a matching crack. The third, the ring his father had given him, was intact, but when he flexed his hand he could feel it humming, a wasp in amber.

"How long?" Paloma asked quietly.

"Since the ford. Properly since three days ago. Truly—" he laughed, a dry joyless sound— "truly, twenty-two years."

She waited. She had a talent for waiting, this woman; she made silence a room you could stand in.

"You know what I am," he said. "You've known since the river. You watch me the way a person watches a snare they haven't stepped in yet."

"I know you wear iron and flinch around dogs. I know your eyes change color when you're angry, which you seem to think nobody notices." Her voice was not accusing. That was the worst part. "I know you let a gifted boy slip through a net in Harrow Fen and then reported his capture anyway, and I know you've hated yourself for both halves of that sentence ever since."

"He was twelve."

"Yes."

"They'd have burned the gift out of him. Or burned him with it. The Edict doesn't distinguish." Aldric looked down at the cracked charm lying in his palm, this little disc of cold metal that had been his cage and his shield for two decades, now ruined, useless, a broken tooth. "My mother was valley-born. Before the dead zone, before the Edict—you wouldn't know the village, it isn't on any map anymore. She married a soldier, moved north, and when I started hearing things I shouldn't hear—doors before they knocked, weather before it came—she took me to a hedge-witch and begged for iron. Said she'd seen what happens to gifted children in the king's country. Said the wolf in me would get us both killed."

"And the witch said?"

"*Feed it nothing and it will stay small.*" He closed his fist over the broken charm. "So I fed it nothing. Twenty-two years. No moonlit runs, no opening the senses, nothing. I became the best hunter the Edict has, do you understand why? Because I know exactly how hiders think. I know every trick because every trick is one I've used. I can smell fear, track guilt, hear a lie in a man's pulse—and I built all of it into a career of dragging people like me out of their lives." His voice roughened. "I told myself I was different. I told myself iron made me clean. But the iron doesn't make you clean, Marchetti. It just makes you quiet."

The wind moved through the bare trees. Somewhere far off, a crow complained about the morning.

"Why tell me now?" she asked.

"Because the charms are dying." He held up the fractured disc, then the humming ring. "Iron fails. Slowly, then all at once—I can feel it going, like a wall coming down brick by brick. Days, maybe. Less, if—" he stopped.

"If what?"

"If I stop feeding it nothing." He made himself say it plainly, because she deserved plainness and because saying it aloud was the last act of a man setting fire to his own exit. "The gift grows when it's used. That's the truth the witch never told me—or the truth I refused to hear. Starving it doesn't kill it. Starving it makes it hungry. And lately it's been very, very hungry, and when the last charm goes, whatever's in me comes out all at once, untrained, in front of whoever's nearest." He met her eyes. "Which, given current arrangements, will most likely be you. Or Serane. Possibly both."

Paloma was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached out—not hesitantly, not dramatically—and took his wrist, the one with the humming ring, and turned his hand over as if reading a page.

"It's not a monster, you know," she said. "Whatever they told you it was. Mine answered me for the first time three weeks ago and I nearly sicked it back up out of terror, and you know what it was? It was eager. Like a dog that's been locked in a cellar its whole life, falling over itself because someone finally opened the door." She looked up at him. "It doesn't want to eat you, Aldric. It wants to be *used*. There's a difference, and it matters."

"You sound like the grimoire."

"I sound like Maren, which is worse." Something flickered across her face—grief, old and well-practiced—and then she let it go. "When it comes, don't fight it alone. That's all I'm asking. I've spent my whole life hiding a gift in a valley designed to smother gifts, and the one thing I learned too late is that hiding and being free look identical from the outside and feel nothing alike from the inside."

He stared at her. Here was the woman he had hunted, captured, lied for, escorted, argued with across a hundred miles of bad road—offering him the one thing no one had ever offered: not absolution, not pity, but *company*. Company in the specific shape of the thing he most feared.

"The wolf," he said at last, testing the word in the open air for the first time in his adult life, and finding that the sky did not fall. "They call it the wolf-blood. Old northern line, my mother claimed. When it comes, I won't be—I won't be entirely a man. That's what the iron was really for. Not the hearing. Not the tracking. The rest of it."

"I know," Paloma said simply. "Tamsin told me. She said the last true wolf-blood in these marches was a border-warden who died protecting a village that never learned his name." A pause. "She said it like it was a tragedy. I'm beginning to see why."

The crow passed overhead again, closer, and Aldric tracked it without meaning to—wingbeats, wind-shift, the small wet gleam of its eye—and realized the senses weren't receding anymore. The crack in the iron wasn't closing. Nothing was going back to the way it had been. He slipped the broken charm into his pocket anyway, out of some stubborn instinct older than sense, and felt the ring hum against his finger like a held note.

"Serane will notice," he said. "The forged writ, the empty reports, the prisoner who walks beside me instead of behind her. She notices everything. It's her gift, if she has one—which, knowing Serane, she does, and it's registered, and it's legal, and it's crueler than anything the Edict hunts." He looked south, toward the ruins where the thirteenth turn was gathering like a storm on the horizon. "Two more moons, you said."

"Eleven nights," she corrected softly. "Then the twelfth. Then the thirteenth."

"Then we'd best not be here when the wall finishes coming down." He picked up his pack, and found that his hand had stopped shaking. "Come on, Marchetti. We're losing daylight, and I have a feeling we're about to run out of road that pretends to be safe."

She shouldered her satchel and fell into step beside him—not behind, beside—and as they walked, he realized the wolf had gone quiet. Not muzzled. Not starving.

Waiting. Patiently, like a creature that had just been promised, at last, that the door would open.
