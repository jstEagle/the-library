## 10. The Smuggler's Price

The safehouse Tamsin chose was a tanner's loft above a dead river, where the smell of old hides still clung to the beams like a ghost that refused to be evicted. Paloma had been there two days when the moon came up fat and wrong-colored over the ruins of the nameless city, and Tamsin finally stopped pretending she'd come upstairs just to check the shutters.

She brought wine. That was the first warning sign, though Paloma didn't read it correctly. In three weeks of traveling together—through looping roads and villages whose names slid off the tongue like wet fish—Tamsin had never once shared her drink. She watered it, she hoarded it, she counted every bottle in every cache like a mother counting children at a fair. Now she set down two clay cups and filled them both without asking, which in the economy of Tamsin Voss was roughly equivalent to weeping.

"Drink," she said. "You'll want it."

Paloma looked at the cup. Outside, the thirteenth moon of the count hung low over the broken skyline, eleven turns gone, two remaining. She could feel them passing now, the way you feel a fever burning down toward its crisis. Every night since Moonturn Nine—since the skirmish in the ruins, since Serane's soldiers saw her light rise out of her own hands like a small defiant sun—every night the countdown had lived behind her ribs like a second heart.

"If this is about Aldric—" she began.

"This isn't about the wolf-boy." Tamsin sat across from her on an upturned crate, elbows on knees, and for once didn't fidget with anything. No knife to sharpen, no coin to flip, no lock of someone's hair to twist between her fingers. Her hands were just hands, lying open on her thighs, and that frightened Paloma more than any blade. "This is about what happens when the hunt finds us here. Because they will find us here. Serane doesn't lose people in cities; she loses them in the country between cities, and we've run out of country."

"We have the antechamber route. You drew it yourself."

"I drew it from memory." Tamsin smiled, and it was her worst smile, the one that showed too many teeth and no joy at all. "And my memory, little witch, is for sale. Has been for years. That's rather the point."

The wind moved through the dead river below, carrying the smell of standing water and old ash. Somewhere in the ruined capital, ghosts were replaying their one night—the king on his throne of stones, the sacrifice, the door swallowing itself—and Paloma had learned to sleep through their murmuring the way farmers sleep through weather.

"I don't understand," she said.

"No." Tamsin drank. "You don't. But you're cleverer than you look under all that Maren-trained caution, so let's not insult each other. Ask me how I know the way into the antechamber. Ask me how a smuggler comes to hold maps of a door that a king spent a century burying."

Paloma's mouth went dry. The wine sat untouched in her hands, warming toward body temperature, suddenly unbearable.

"Tamsin."

"Sold it," Tamsin said simply. "Eight years ago. To a crown surveyor with soft hands and a ledger full of other people's secrets, who paid me in something better than gold." She tapped her temple. "I got to keep everything. Do you understand what that means, in my trade? Everything. My mother's face. The name of the boy I loved at fourteen. The sound of the sea at Carrow Point, if Carrow Point ever existed, which I'm no longer certain of. Most smugglers who work the erased territories trade memories because they have to—we cross ground that eats names, and you pay the toll or the ground keeps you. But me? I sold the door's location *first*, and bought back my whole self with the proceeds. I'm the only person alive who remembers Vessany entire, and I only remember it because I sold the one thing worth remembering."

Paloma set the cup down very carefully, as though the floor might be listening. Perhaps it was. This city listened to everything.

"You led us here anyway."

"I led you here because you were going to come with or without me, and dead witches can't pay tolls." Tamsin's voice stayed level, almost gentle, which made it worse. "But yes. And now Serane has the same maps I sold the surveyor, because the crown doesn't forget purchases, and I've spent eight years watching the roads I sold get garrisoned one by one. There is exactly one way left into the antechamber, and it closes the moment Serane decides to close it. Which will be before the thirteenth turn. She's not stupid. Cruel, thorough, and dressed like a funeral, but not stupid."

"So we go around."

"There is no around. There is through, or there is nothing." Tamsin leaned forward. "Unless."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and Paloma heard it falling for a long time before it hit bottom.

"Unless what?"

Tamsin reached into her coat—the long grey coat with forty pockets, each one a small country of its own—and drew out not a knife, not a vial, but a strip of pale leather, worn soft with handling. On it, inked in a script Paloma recognized from the grimoire's margins: a contract. Old. Notarized in wax that had gone the color of dried blood.

"A memory-broker's lien," Tamsin said. "The surveyor kept records. The crown keeps everything. When I sold the door's location, I signed a clause—standard practice—that gives the seller right of first repurchase. If I ever bring the broker an asset of equal or greater value, the sale reverses. The crown's copy burns. Their maps burn. Serane marches her soldiers into a maze that no longer exists." She turned the leather over in her fingers. "It's the only honest contract I've ever signed, and I signed it drunk, which is why it's the only honest one."

"And the asset." Paloma's voice sounded far away to herself. "Equal or greater value."

"The location of a door out of an erased nation." Tamsin met her eyes. "Versus the memories of a valley that shouldn't exist, held by the last living keeper of its bloodline. Yes. That would balance the scale."

The silence that followed had texture. Paloma could feel it against her skin, fine-grained and terrible, like ash.

"My memories of the valley."

"Maren's garden. The well. Your neighbors' faces. Whatever the valley was before it became a cage—you'd trade all of it. You'd walk out of here free and never know why you flinched at the sight of rosemary. Never know you were loved. Never know what you're choosing when you choose the door." Tamsin spread her hands. "That's the price. I'm sorry. I've spent three weeks deciding whether to say it, and I've said it badly, and I'm saying it anyway because in two days Serane seals the pass and after that everyone dies or worse."

*Everyone dies or worse.* Paloma thought of the child at the well, dragged away by crown soldiers for lighting candles with a thought. Thought of Maren dying with the door's name caught between her teeth. Thought of Aldric, downstairs somewhere, keeping watch with iron charms biting into his skin like a penance he'd forgotten how to remove.

"You knew," Paloma said quietly. "When you took the lock of my hair at the Old March. You were already pricing me."

"I was already *weighing* you." For the first time, something cracked in Tamsin's voice—not guilt exactly; Paloma suspected Tamsin had surgically removed that organ years ago—but something adjacent. Regret's poorer cousin. "There's a difference. A scale weighs. It doesn't want. And I didn't want this, little witch, whatever you decide to believe about me tonight."

Paloma stood. Her legs felt borrowed, but they held.

"When I was nine," she said, "Maren caught me making the feverfew bloom out of season. Just one flower, in November, because I missed summer. She didn't yell. She sat me down and she told me: *everything costs, and the trick of a good life is choosing your prices before they're chosen for you.*" She looked down at Tamsin, at the contract in her hands, at the woman she had crossed a haunted country beside. "You're choosing mine for me. Right now. Tonight. With wine and a sad story."

"It's not a sad story. It's the truest thing I own."

"That's what makes it sad."

* * *

They argued until the candles burned to stubs. Paloma would give that much to the night—it had been honest, in the way of knives. Tamsin laid out the arithmetic again and again: the memories versus the door, the door versus Serane, Serane versus the two of them dead in a cellar while the crown walked through a hole in the world. And every time, Paloma came back to the same place, though it took her hours to find the words for it.

"You're asking me to buy freedom with amnesia," she said finally. "To walk through that door and arrive nowhere knowing nothing. What kind of escape is that? You'd be sending a stranger through. Some blank girl with my face and my gift and no reason at all to use it. Maren would have died for nothing. The valley would have hidden me for nothing. *I* would have survived for nothing."

"The stranger lives."

"The stranger isn't me." Paloma picked up the contract, weighed it, and set it on the crate between them like a verdict. "That's the answer. No."

Tamsin studied her for a long moment. Then she laughed—a short, ugly sound with no humor in it, aimed mostly at herself.

"I told them you'd refuse," she said. "Back when I took the job. I told the surveyor: the Marchetti girl won't sell. He said everyone sells. I said, not her, she's the kind that buys." She shook her head. "He laughed at me. Eight years I've waited to find out which of us was right, and I hate that it's him."

"You took the job." Paloma seized on it. "What job?"

For the first time all night, Tamsin looked away.

"Watch you," she said. "Report the bloodline's movements. It was supposed to end years ago—I assumed the valley's wards would kill me if I went near, and they nearly did, ask my left knee—but then the Edict came down and the hunt started moving, and my handler woke up like a spider hearing a fly." She met Paloma's eyes again, and there it was at last, the confession underneath the confession, the one she'd carried up the stairs with the wine. "Every mile we've traveled together, I've written it down. The ford. The grimoire. The wolf-boy. All of it goes in my reports, and my reports go to the crown, and the crown goes to Serane."

The room tilted. Paloma gripped the edge of the crate.

"Aldric," she said. "You reported Aldric's charms. You reported what he is."

"I report what keeps my reports believed. Yes."

"And the antechamber route you drew us. Is *that* real, or is it a corridor lined with soldiers waiting for your signal?"

"It's real." Tamsin stood, slow, joints cracking like the old tannery beams. "That part I swear on whatever I have left that's worth swearing on. I need you to reach that door more than Serane does—because if the crown takes it first, my contract's worthless, and if you seal it—" she paused, and Paloma watched her choose honesty like a woman stepping off a ledge— "and if you seal it, the erased country stays erased, and everything I bought back with its location becomes the only version of Vessany left alive. Inside my head. I'd be the last citizen of a nation that never existed. I've decided, these past three weeks, that I can live with being that. Barely."

"So which are you?" Paloma's voice came out low and shaking. "My friend or their spy?"

"Yes," said Tamsin.

* * *

She left before dawn, because spies and friends share that habit, and Paloma did not stop her. There was a moment at the door—Tamsin with her hand on the latch, the grey sky coming up behind her like dirty linen—when either of them might have said something that would have mattered forever. Neither did. Tamsin tossed something small onto the crate instead: a folded square of paper, oilcloth-wrapped, sealed with plain tallow.

"The route," she said. "Unreported. The one map I never wrote down for anyone, drawn fresh tonight, from memory, which as established is considerable. If you leave within the day, you beat Serane's riders to the pass by half a night." She hesitated. "The hair. From the Old March. I never sold it. I couldn't figure out what it was worth, and I've decided that's because it wasn't for sale. It's in the left pocket of my grey coat, if anyone ever asks you."

"Tamsin—"

"Don't." The smuggler's face closed like a shop at dusk. "Friends forgive. Spies exploit. I'm neither, tonight, so do us both the mercy of hating me cleanly."

Then she was gone, boots silent on the tanner's stair, and Paloma stood alone in the hide-smelling dark with a map she hadn't dared hope for and a friendship she hadn't known she'd been counting on until it walked out the door.

She unwrapped the paper with hands that wouldn't quite steady. The route was there, exact and lovingly detailed—drainage galleries, a collapsed chapel, the long descent beneath the roots of the city—and in the corner, in Tamsin's cramped hand, a single line:

*Two turns left. Spend them like a miser.*

Below that, smaller, as if written after long argument with herself:

*I hope it's worth what it cost. Both of us.*

Paloma folded the map into the grimoire, next to the pages Maren had written to her by name, and went downstairs to wake Aldric. They had half a night's lead and a door to reach, and somewhere behind them, riding hard along roads a traitor-friend had once sold to the crown, Inquisitor Serane was already awake.
