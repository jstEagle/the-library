## 11. Moonturn Nine

The Inquisitor arrived at dusk, and the ruins went quiet the way a forest goes quiet before wolves.

Aldric felt it first through his charms—a pressure against the iron at his throat, like the whole night leaning in. Then came the sound: hooves on broken flagstone, unhurried, deliberate. Serane did not ride as though she expected anything in this dead city to threaten her. She rode as though the dead city should be afraid.

He stood from the fire he'd allowed himself—small, screened by fallen masonry—and straightened his captain's coat. Beside him, Paloma looked up from the grimoire, her face doing the thing he'd come to admire and dread: going smooth and empty as river ice.

"That's her," Aldric said quietly. "Inquisitor Serane."

"You've never mentioned her."

"There was nothing good to mention." He watched the riders emerge from the colonnade of the old processional way—six of them in crown black, and one in gray so pale it seemed cut from fog. "She doesn't send soldiers ahead of her. She sends questions. By the time she arrives, she already knows which answers are lies."

"Then we're finished," Paloma said, not with fear but with a kind of arithmetic calm that frightened him more.

"Not yet." He caught her wrist—her pulse quick under his fingers, steady despite everything. "You go to the cistern tunnels. I bought us an hour of cover story when I reported the witch sighted moving south along the ford road. She'll want to interrogate me about my failures before she does anything else. Inquisitors always start with failure."

Paloma held his eyes. Moonturn nine hung above the ruined skyline, three nights past full, thinning toward its last quarter. Eleven turns since the well in the valley where they'd read the Edict aloud. Two remained after this one.

"If she breaks you," Paloma said, "she'll break me by morning."

"Then don't let me break." He released her wrist. "Go."

She went, silent as smoke between the fallen pillars, and Aldric turned to meet his commander with the mask fitted tight over his face—the same mask he had worn for eleven years, iron charms cold against his skin, wolf-blood leashed behind them like a second heartbeat he refused to acknowledge.

Serane dismounted herself. That was the first wrongness. Inquisitors of the Edict did not dismount for captains; they waited to be bowed to. But Serane stood in the ruin-light, small and neat in her gray, gray eyes and gray hair and a voice like ash sifting, and looked at Aldric Vane as if he were a document she was reading for the third time.

"Captain."

"Inquisitor."

"Eleven reports." She drew a leather folio from beneath her cloak—not a threat, exactly, but near enough. "Eleven months of reports, and in every one, the Marchetti woman is two days behind you. Ahead of you. Beside you and gone. You have tracked her across four hundred miles of country that has forgotten its own name, and you have never once been where she was when you arrived." Her head tilted, birdlike. "Either she is the most gifted fugitive the crown has ever hunted, Captain, or your reports are a work of fiction. I have decided they are both."

"The woman is clever—"

"She is nineteen years old," Serane said, "raised in a valley without a drop of magic in it, carrying a dead hedge-witch's book. And you are the crown's best hound." She stepped closer. Ash sifted. Somewhere in the dark, one of her soldiers shifted, and the sound was very loud. "Do you know what I do with hounds that stop hunting, Aldric Vane?"

"I can imagine."

"No." Something moved behind the gray of her eyes—something almost gentle, which was the most frightening thing he had seen all year. "You imagine escape. I am telling you there isn't one. Not for her. Not for you." She let the folio fall closed. "I know about the charms, Captain."

The world narrowed to the iron at his throat. Aldric kept his breathing level, his face still, and inside the cage of his ribs the wolf pressed its ears forward and listened.

"I don't know what you mean."

"A boy in Harrow's Ford, eight years ago. A village child who saw a soldier turn into something else in the burning house, and whose family was paid well and relocated, and whose testimony was filed under *confusion due to smoke*." She said it flatly, without triumph. "I have spent eleven months reading your file, Captain. The crown pays me to find the cracks in people. You are nearly all crack." Her voice dropped. "Deliver the witch, and the file burns. Fail again, and you will stand trial beside her—one for the door, and one for being exactly what the Edict says should be culled."

"And if I deliver her?"

"Then the crown gets what it actually wants, and you get to keep pretending to be a man." Serane turned back toward her horse, then paused. "We move on the ruins at moonset. My scouts found fresh footprints in the eastern quarter. Villagers, Captain. Squatters living in the bones of Vessany, hiding gifted children the way rats hide rats. If your witch has been feeding among them—" she glanced back, and the gentleness was gone entirely, "—then tonight you will finally have your report worth writing."

She mounted. The hoofbeats faded down the processional way, and Aldric stood alone in the dark with the iron burning cold at his throat and understood that the hourglass had been turned over, all its sand at once.

Paloma heard the soldiers coming long before she saw them—but then, she had learned to hear things lately that Maren had never taught her to hear. The grimoire had opened to her like a wound opening, and everything it touched grew sharper: the scrape of a boot on old tile forty yards off, the small animal terror of children being woken in a collapsed granary, the taste of fear on the wind like copper coins.

The squatters of the eastern quarter—nine families, maybe thirty souls, living in the shell of a Vessany that maps refused to remember—had taken her in for six days. Fed her barley soup. Let her sleep under a roof that didn't ask her name. There was a girl here, Wren, ten years old, who could make candles lean toward her like flowers toward sun, and who had shown Paloma the trick shyly, secretly, the way Paloma had once shown no one at all.

Now torchlight bloomed in the eastern quarter, and boots were everywhere.

"They'll take the children first," Tamsin had told her once, over cards in a smuggler's camp, "because children are cheap to hold and dear to trade." Tamsin was gone now, sold to her own arithmetic somewhere south, and Paloma had no one left to tell her what to do.

So she ran toward the torches instead of away from them.

The granary had been a temple once; you could still see the ghost of painted stars on its surviving vault. Crown soldiers were dragging families out into the square—moors and grandfathers, and two children, and Wren, kicking, a candle-end clenched in her fist that flared and guttered with each beat of her heart because she couldn't help it, because fear made her gift leak out of her like light through a cracked shutter.

"Gifted child," a sergeant was saying, with the satisfaction of a man finding coin in the street. "That's a bounty times three. Bind her hands—"

Paloma stepped out of the dark.

Later she would not remember deciding. She would remember only the moment before—the girl's face, streaked with ash and furious tears, so exactly the face Paloma had worn at ten years old in a valley that taught her to be nothing—and the moment after, when the world answered her.

"Let her go."

The sergeant laughed. It was a short laugh.

Every torch in the square went out.

Not blown—*called*. Paloma reached into the flames the way Maren had taught her to reach into soil, feeling for the living part of a thing, and pulled, and thirty points of fire crossed the darkness to her hand like startled birds and settled there, circling her raised palm in a slow ring of light. The soldiers' shadows wheeled around them, enormous, directionless. Someone screamed. Someone else dropped a sword.

"Gods," whispered a woman in the crowd of captives. "*Witch.*"

The word landed on Paloma like a mantle being laid across her shoulders. All her life she had ducked beneath it. Here, in the bones of a murdered country, wearing a ring of stolen fire, she found she could stand under its weight.

"Run," she told the families, and they ran, streaming past her into the warrens of the ruined quarter, and she held the light high so they could see their way, which meant every crossbow in the square could see her too.

The first bolt she stopped midair. It simply ceased, three feet from her chest, its motion handed off into nothing, clattering to the stones. The second she deflected into a wall. The third never left the bow, because the archer was looking at the ring of fire in her palm and his hands had forgotten their work.

Then the iron rang.

Not a bell—an *absence* of bell, a cold note struck somewhere behind the world, and Paloma knew it instantly because she had slept beside that sound for weeks. Wolf-charms. Many of them, all cracking at once.

Aldric came out of the dark on the soldiers' side of the square, and he was not entirely a man.

She had seen the edge of it once, in the chapel after the ambush at Moonturn Seven—the gold flicker in his winter eyes, the too-long stride. This was past the edge. His coat split at the shoulders seams; shadow poured off him like heat-haze; he hit the sergeant's shield line and it broke like a fence in a flood, men flying, and through it all she could hear the iron charms at his throat shrieking as they failed one by one, links fusing, metal weeping.

"*Captain!*" someone shouted. "*It's the captain—gods, it's Vane—*"

"Say that," Aldric growled—it barely qualified as speech, gravel and thunder—"and die."

They chose silence. They fled. Within a minute the square belonged to the dead city again, to the painted stars overhead and the scattered torch-irons and the two of them standing in the wreckage of eleven months of careful lies.

Aldric turned. What was left of his charms smoked on their chain. One eye was still his own winter-gray; the other burned amber, pupil gone to a wolf's bright slit. His hands were wrong. His breathing was enormous.

"Well," Paloma said, and her voice shook only a little. "There goes the pretext."

He laughed—a raw, startled sound, half-wolf—and then swayed, and she caught him as the change receded and left him human and hollowed out, ninety pounds of borrowed strength called due all at once. She got him down onto a fallen pillar, kneeling over him, her ring of fire still circling above them both like a small loyal moon.

"You shouldn't have done that," he rasped.

"You shouldn't have either."

"Yes, but I've had practice ruining my life." He looked up at her, and the amber was fading from his eye, and underneath it he was just Aldric—tired, terrified, and free in a way she recognized because she was wearing the same freedom herself. "It's done now. Both of us. There's no report that covers this."

"No," Paloma agreed. Above them, moonturn nine slid westward, thin and patient. "Two more turns, Aldric."

"Two more turns." He closed his eyes. "And Serane knows exactly where we are."

As if the name had summoned her, a horn sounded far off in the western quarter—one long note, then another, unhurried. Not a hunt-call. A proclamation-call. Somewhere out in the dark, a crier unrolled a scroll by torchlight, and his voice carried across the ruins with terrible clarity:

*"By edict of the crown: the fugitive Paloma Marchetti, witch of the dead valley, is declared quarry of the realm. Taken alive, for the door must be opened. Any who shelter her share her sentence. So it is written—"*

Paloma listened to her own name travel across the city that had erased itself, and found that she was smiling.

"What?" Aldric asked.

"Nothing." She let her ring of fire go, one flame at a time, back into the scattered torches until the square glowed gently, ordinarily, hers and no longer stolen. "I've spent my whole life making sure no one knew my name. Now the whole kingdom's shouting it." She helped him to his feet. "Come on. We have two turns of the moon, a door that needs a name, and an Inquisitor who reads people like documents. I think it's time we stopped running and started reading her back."
