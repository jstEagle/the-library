## 12. What the Valley Was For

The chapel had no roof anymore, and so Paloma read the grimoire by moonlight, which felt fitting in a way she could not yet name.

Aldric slept against the far wall with his bandaged shoulder propped on folded wool, his breathing slow and even, the iron charms at his wrist dull as old coins. He had refused to sleep until the wound stopped weeping, and then he had refused anyway, until exhaustion took the argument out of him. Now he dreamed—she could see it moving behind his eyelids, the quick flicker of a wolf's eyes under a man's—and she was glad of it. One of them should be allowed to rest.

The fire had burned down to a red memory. She fed it two splints of pew from the ruined altar rail, sacrilege that would have made Maren laugh, or scold, or both at once.

She had been avoiding the last pages for three nights now. She knew it the way you know an unwashed dish in a sink, an unsaid apology, a letter unopened on a table. The grimoire's earlier sections were Maren the teacher: careful diagrams of rootwork and weather-sense, marginal notes in her cramped leftward hand, little jokes tucked into recipes like raisins in bread. *Do not attempt this during a thunderstorm, child, unless you wish to meet your ancestors early.* Paloma had laughed over those pages on the road, alone, and once with Aldric watching her with something soft in his winter eyes.

But the last gathering of leaves was different. The paper was older. The ink had gone brown, and the handwriting—still Maren's, unmistakably, but younger, harder, written by a woman with steadier hands and less mercy in them—slanted downhill as though the writer were hurrying toward something she dreaded.

Tonight there was nothing left between Paloma and those pages but the reading of them.

She turned the leaf.

*If you have come this far,* it began, *then I am dead, and the valley has let you go, and I am sorry for all three.*

Paloma's breath went shallow. Around her the chapel held its silence—the broken columns, the moon through the absent roof, the small animal warmth of the sleeping captain—and she read on.

*You will want, first, to be angry with me. Take your time with it. Anger is a door too, and some doors must be walked through before they can be closed.*

*I did not find you, Paloma Marchetti. I was sent for you.*

The words seemed to darken as she read them, the way water darkens when something rises beneath it.

*You were born in the ninth month of a bad year, in the village of Hallowmere, at the mouth of the valley. Your mother's name was Sera. Her name was Sera, and hers was Ilva, and hers was Vessa, and if you follow the line back far enough you will reach the woman who gave this country its name, because your blood is not from Vessany. Your blood **is** Vessany. It runs in one family only, thinner each generation but never gone, like a river under ice. The crown hunted that blood once. The kings of the old line called it the Key, and they built the valley to keep it.*

Paloma looked up. The moon sat in the broken roof like an eye. She looked back down, and made herself keep reading.

*You know the valley as I taught you to know it: poor, quiet, magic-dead, safe. Safe because nothing grows there—not herbs beyond my garden, not gifts beyond smallness, not danger beyond hunger. I told you the land itself had been burned out long ago by some old war. That was true the way a cage is true when you call it a room.*

*The valley is not dead, child. The valley is holding its breath.*

*It was made that way. Deliberately. By workers whose names were struck from every record, in a single generation, at ruinous cost—a working so large that they had to kill the land to cast it, smothering every current of gift across forty miles the way you smother a fire with ash. They built a bowl of silence around one house in Hallowmere, around one cradle, around one bloodline, so that the Key could never wake and never be found and never, ever be carried to the door.*

*You grew up inside that silence. You thought your gift was small. Your gift is not small. Your gift has been drowning since the day you were born, held under by a country's worth of dead air, and the wonder is that it lived at all. Out here, on the road, in the old territory where the air still moves—you have felt it. What answers you now answered you then. You simply could not hear it over the weight of everything pressing down.*

Paloma set the book down.

Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to someone standing nearby, someone she had never properly met.

The garden. She saw it whole, suddenly, the way you see a face clearly only after the person dies: the low stone wall Maren had built herself, hauling stones up from the riverbed year after year; the rows of herbs arranged not by use or season but in a spiral, a pattern Paloma had always assumed was old women's whimsy; the way Maren had stood in that garden on stormy nights, arms crossed, watching the sky like a soldier watching a border.

Not a garden. A wardstone. A watchtower. A cell with flowers planted in it.

And Paloma had spent nineteen years pulling weeds in it, believing she was ordinary, believing the heaviness in her chest whenever she reached for anything more was her own failure, her own smallness. She had apologized to her own gift. She had starved it and thanked it for being patient.

*Maren didn't hide me,* she thought. *She guarded me. Like a key in a box. Like something dangerous.*

The distinction should not have hurt more than the lie. It hurt infinitely more. A lie could be forgiven; it might even have been kind. But guarding—that implied a threat. That implied Maren had looked at the child Sera brought down from Hallowmere and seen, beneath the smallness and the dirt-caked fingernails, something that entire nations had bled to contain.

*You will ask why I am telling you now, at the end, instead of at the beginning,* the brown ink went on. *Because a secret kept is a chain, and a secret given freely is a choice, and I wanted your life to contain at least one real choice before you learned how few of them you were actually offered. You chose to tend my garden. You chose to sit with me while I died. No one made you do those things. Hold onto them. In what follows, hold onto them.*

*Here is what I was, and what you are.*

*Three hundred years ago—no. A century ago, and a little more, when the maps still had Vessany on them, the last king of the old line looked at the door beneath his capital and understood that his dynasty was finished. Wars, debts, plague, the slow rot of a court eating itself. He had one possession of value left: the door. A way out. Not merely out of the country—out of the world's agreement, out of history, into whatever lies on the other side of names. And the door had a lock, and the lock had a key, and the key was not a thing at all.*

*It was a bloodline. Ours.*

*The door does not open for crowns or armies or gold. It opens for the family it was bound to at its making—bound, I believe, willingly, by the first Vessa, who gave her name to it as its maker's price. Every generation since, our blood has been the hinge. Near us, the door stirs. Through us, it can be opened. And so the king faced a choice: destroy the door, or destroy us.*

*He tried the second. We scattered. Some fled into other countries and were swallowed. One branch fled into the mountains north of the old marches, into a poor green valley where nobody important had ever bothered to look—and there, in desperation, they did the unthinkable. They hired the silence-workers. They paid with everything they had, land and gold and years of their lives, to build a cage around themselves. To make their own blood dormant. To become, in effect, a family of locked doors.*

*Your grandmother's grandmother signed that bargain. Every generation since has renewed it without knowing, simply by staying, simply by being born inside the bowl of silence, letting the dead air press the gift flat. That is why the valley forgets things, Paloma. That is why roads loop and travelers lose days and no messenger ever remembers to come back. Forgetting is the mortar of the cage. The whole valley was built to be unable to remember what it is holding.*

*Including me. I did not remember either, at first. I was born there. I was raised there. I found the truth the way you are finding it—in books, in fragments, in the wrongness of a land that will not hold a straight thought about itself. And when I understood what the valley was, I went looking for the Key, expecting an old woman, or a relic, or a child already taken.*

*I found your mother, pregnant, frightened, running.*

*Sera knew enough to run and not enough to hide. She had felt the cage from the inside her whole life—felt it the way you feel a collar, without knowing the word for it—and when her gift began to rise despite the dead air, stronger than any gift had risen in the valley for a century, she panicked. She ran for the marches. She got as far as Hallowmere.*

*I hid her. I lied to the silence-workers' remnants who still kept the old watch—I told them the bloodline had ended, that Sera's child was fathered outside it, that the Key was broken at last. Whether they believed me or chose not to look, I cannot say. The valley is good at choosing not to look. Then Sera died bringing you into the world, and I was left with a squalling infant and a decision I have never stopped paying for.*

*Keep you in the cage, and let you grow up small and silent and alive. Or carry you out, past the marches, and let your gift breathe—and let every crown, every inquisitor, every hungry king's man in the wide world smell what you are.*

*I kept you in the cage. I told myself it was love. Some mornings I even believe it.*

Paloma did not realize she was crying until a tear hit the page and bloomed in the old brown ink, blurring the word *cage*. She wiped it carefully with her sleeve, the way she'd have wiped dirt from a seedling, and read on through salt-blurred eyes.

*Now you know, and knowing will change nothing about what pursues you, and everything about what you carry. So hear the rest, the part that matters, the part I have rewritten forty times because every version sounded like a command and I swore I would not command you from beyond the grave:*

*The door opens on the thirteenth turn of the moon, once a century, for a voice of our blood speaking its true name. That much the legends get right. What the legends leave out—what I have only lately pieced together, here at the end—is that the door takes as well as gives. It is a hinge, and hinges swing both ways. Whatever passes through is passed out of the world entirely, unremembered, unnamed, gone. And whatever opens it is bound to the opening. The door does not distinguish between the one who escapes and the one who unlocks.*

*I may be wrong. I pray I am wrong. But if I am right, then understand what the crown wants, what Serane wants, what the fat desperate king in his crumbling capital wants: not you, child. Not your death. Your **voice**. They want to bring the Key to the lock and make it turn, and they do not care—or perhaps they do care, precisely—that turning it may cost the keyholder everything she is.*

*Aldric Vane will have told you he is your enemy. Judge him slowly. Iron charms crack, and men under iron are not always what the iron says.*

*And hear this last, because I will say it only once and then I will stop writing, because my hand hurts and the candle is dying: whatever you choose—through the door, away from the door, against the door—choose it as yourself. Not as the valley's prisoner. Not as my penance. Not as the Key. Nineteen years I watched you grow in dead ground, and I tell you truly: you are the strongest thing that valley ever grew, and it never once managed to make you small. Don't let me do it now, from six feet under.*

*The garden wall needs mending on the north side. If you ever go home, mend it.*

*—Maren, who loved you badly, and completely.*

The last word trailed off into a scratch, as if the pen had been set down mid-thought, or lifted.

Paloma sat for a long time without moving.

The moon crossed a gap in the broken roof and moved on. The fire ticked and settled. Somewhere out in the ruins, wind moved through stone corridors with a sound like slow breathing, and she thought: *the city is doing what the valley did. Forgetting. Holding its breath. Everything in this country breathes around a held breath.*

Her whole life rearranged itself around the new center of gravity, the way a field rearranges after a flood, leaving everything recognizable but nothing in its place.

The crier's bell at the well, and the neighbors who had never quite met her eyes. She had thought it was because she was Maren's odd apprentice, the girl who talked to plants. It was because the valley, without knowing why, knew what she was. The cage knew its key.

The day she was nine and had lit the kitchen candles with a thought, just once, just to see, and Maren had slapped her hand—not in anger, Paloma remembered now, remembering with terrible clarity—but with a fear that had no anger anywhere in it. Maren's hand had shaken for an hour afterward. *Never again, child. Never again where anyone can see.* She had thought it was about the Edict. The Edict wasn't even written yet.

The spiral garden. The looping roads. The way no traveler ever stayed longer than a night, and none could say afterward exactly where the valley lay. The way her own dreams had always felt pressed flat, like flowers in a heavy book—all color, no wind.

And underneath it all, the smallness. The lifelong smallness she had mourned and accepted and finally, out here in the living air, begun to shed like a coat two sizes too tight. She had thought she was growing. She had only been taking off the cage.

"I was never hidden," she said aloud, very quietly, to the empty roof and the moon. "I was buried."

Aldric stirred against the wall. His eyes opened partway—those winter-grey eyes, sharp even half-dreaming—and found her hunched over the glowing pages with tears drying on her face.

"Bad news?" he asked, voice rough with sleep.

She almost said *no*. It was reflexive, the oldest reflex she owned: *it's nothing, I'm fine, don't trouble yourself.* Nineteen years of being small and fine and no trouble at all, in a valley built to keep her that way.

"No," she started, and stopped, and heard the lie forming and refused it. "Yes. Both." She closed the grimoire gently, resting her palm on its cover as if it were a sleeping animal. "Maren wrote to me. At the end. All of it."

"All of what?"

"Why the hunt matters. Why the door matters." She looked at him—at the wolf-blood hiding behind his cracked iron, at the man who had falsified reports and taken a blade meant for her and never once asked her to explain herself. "Why *I* matter. Aldric—I'm not just running from the hunt. I'm what the hunt is for. My blood is the key to the door. The valley I grew up in—" her voice caught, and she pushed through it, "—the valley was built as a cage. Around people like me. Around *me*. Everything I ever loved was the bars."

He was fully awake now. He came and sat beside her without asking, close enough that she could feel his warmth against the chapel cold, and for a while he said nothing, which was the right thing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a wound.

"The garden," he said finally. "The one you talk about. The herbs."

"A prison wall," Paloma said. "With rosemary on it."

"And she kept you there anyway."

"She kept me there to keep me *alive*." Paloma heard the defense leave her mouth before she could examine it, and realized, with a strange floating grief, that she meant it. That she would defend Maren forever, and resent her forever, and both things were true and neither canceled the other. That was what love was, maybe. Two truths that refused to cancel.

"Then she succeeded," Aldric said simply. "Whatever else. She succeeded."

Paloma laughed—a short, wet, broken sound—and leaned her head briefly against his uninjured shoulder, and let herself have that one moment before she pulled back.

Because there was no time. There had never been time; she'd simply never been told. Eleven turns had fallen since Tamsin counted them under a smuggler's lantern. The thirteenth moon was rising somewhere past the horizon, patient as a debt.

And now she understood what waited at the bottom of those stairs beneath the ruins. Not freedom. A lock. And the crown coming with armies to force the key into it—her voice, her name, her self—whether it turned willingly or not.

"Maren says the door takes as well as gives," Paloma said. "Whoever opens it—" She couldn't finish it. She made herself finish it. "Whoever opens it may be bound to the opening. May not walk through at all."

Aldric went very still beside her. When he spoke, his voice was low and even in the way she had learned meant danger—not danger to her. Danger to whatever threatened her.

"Then the king doesn't want a door," he said. "He wants a sacrifice."

"Yes."

"Then we don't open it."

"We have to know," Paloma said, and her hand found the grimoire's worn spine. "There's more in these pages. There has to be. Maren spent her whole life learning the shape of this cage—if anyone found a way to break the lock without paying the price, it was her." She stood, knees stiff, and looked up through the missing roof at the wheeling stars, at the moon already fattening toward its thirteenth turn. "Come on. We read until dawn. Because if the only choices are the crown's door or no door at all—"

She stopped. Far off across the ruins, thin as a needle, a horn sounded. Once. Twice. Then silence—the particular, listening silence of men in the dark who had heard an answer.

Aldric was on his feet, hand at his sword, all softness gone. "They've picked up our trail."

"Then they can pick up our trail while we read," Paloma said, and was surprised to hear how steady her voice came out—steady, and not small at all.

She knelt, reopened the grimoire to the first of Maren's final pages, and began again from the beginning, this time hunting not for the truth of what she was, but for the one thing the truth had not yet given her:

*a way out of the sentence.*
