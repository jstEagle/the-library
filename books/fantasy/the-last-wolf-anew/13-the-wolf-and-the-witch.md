## 13. The Wolf and the Witch

The arrow took him between the shoulder blades, and the world went white.

Not pain—not at first. Just white, the way snow is white, the way a page is white before anyone writes on it. Aldric heard himself make a sound that was not a word, felt his knees find the mud of the forest road, and through the ringing in his skull he registered two things with the cold precision that had kept him alive for eleven years of service: first, that Paloma was screaming his name; second, that the archer was already nocking a second shaft.

He did not think. That was the thing he would remember afterward, lying in the dark of the chapel with his blood soaking into hundred-year-old stone—that for once in his carefully governed life, he did not think.

The iron charms broke.

He felt them go, one after another, like small bones snapping under a boot heel: the collar-piece first, then the wrist-bands Serane's own smiths had fitted to him eight years ago, then the little ring of cold metal against his sternum that he had touched every night before sleep the way other men touched a saint's medal. The suppression spells shattered, and what they had been holding down came up out of him like water from a broken well.

The wolf came loose.

He was aware of it in fragments—the ground suddenly loud, every root and worm and dying leaf beneath the frost audible; the archer's heartbeat stuttering somewhere upslope; the copper reek of his own blood painting the air in strokes too broad to ignore. His body changed around the arrow. He felt his spine lengthen, his shoulders reshape, fur rising along his arms in a tide, and the second arrow sang past where his throat had been and buried itself in a pine trunk with a sound like a struck bell.

Then he was moving, low and fast, and the forest tilted into the simple geometry of hunt and prey.

He remembered killing the archers. He would not, later, be able to say how many—three, he thought, maybe four; men in crown gray who had been sent to do exactly to him what he had spent eleven years pretending he might someday do to others. He remembered the wet heat of it, the astonishing ease. He remembered, most of all, standing over the last of them with the man's sword arm ruined and his own jaws aching with the shape of words he could no longer form, and hearing Paloma come crashing down the slope with Maren's grimoire clutched to her chest and her gift flaring off her skin like heat-shimmer—

—and stopping.

She stopped too. Ten paces away, breath smoking in the cold, eyes enormous. Looking not at the dead men but at him. At all of him.

"Aldric," she said, very quietly, the way you speak to something wild. "Aldric, it's me."

And the wolf, which should have known nothing but the red arithmetic of the moment, sat back on its haunches and looked at her, and knew her, and was ashamed.

* * *

They walked north through the falling dusk until the trees thinned and the land opened into one of Vessany's old high valleys—a bowl of frozen grassland ringed by hills, and in its center, impossibly, a chapel.

It should not have been there. Nothing should have been there; this whole country had been scraped off the maps a century ago, its villages burned or renamed, its roads left to loop back on themselves like sentences that couldn't end. But the chapel stood whole, small and gray-walled, its slate roof furred with moss, its door unlocked as if someone had stepped out moments ago to fetch water and meant to come right back.

Inside: dust, a fallen beam, six rows of pews rotted soft, and an altar stone worn smooth in the middle as if by centuries of hands. No windows remained whole except one, high in the eastern wall, a roundel of colored glass depicting something the iconoclasts had missed—a door, rendered in chips of blue and gold, with a sliver of moon above it.

Paloma looked at that window for a long time and said nothing.

Aldric made it as far as the second pew before his legs quit. She caught him under the arm, swore at his weight, and lowered him down onto the wood with a care that was almost angry. Then she cut the ruined shirt off his back and saw the arrowhead still seated below his left shoulder blade, and her face did something complicated that she tried very hard to make into nothing at all.

"This is going to hurt," she said.

"You're a healer now?"

"I'm a hedge-witch's apprentice with a grimoire I've read half of." Her hands were already moving, tearing linen, heating water over a fire she lit with a snap of her fingers—casually, effortlessly, the way she'd never dared light so much as a candle in the valley. "Maren knew bonesetting and poultices. Some of it stuck. Hold still."

"Hold still," he repeated. "That's my line."

"Not anymore." She set her palm flat between his shoulders, over the wound, and closed her eyes. "I'm going to draw the head. It's iron, so it won't have poisoned you, but it's barbed, and if I pull wrong you'll bleed out in a chapel nobody's prayed in for a hundred years. So hold *still*, Captain."

He held still.

It hurt the way he'd always privately imagined dying would hurt—total, clarifying, without negotiation. He bit down on his own forearm and made no sound, because making sound was a habit he had never permitted himself, and because the wolf under his skin, newly unchained and raw, wanted to howl badly enough that he distrusted everything it wanted.

But when the arrowhead came free and Paloma pressed her gift into the wound—not healing it, he understood dimly; she didn't have that in her, few did—but *quieting* it, slowing the bleeding, banking the fire of it down to coals, some sound escaped him anyway. Low, involuntary. Almost a growl. Almost a moan.

Her hand stilled on his back.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"For what?"

"For this." Her voice cracked on the word. "For the road. For Tamsin's ford, for Moonturn Nine, for every mile of this. You've been shot because of me. You've—" She laughed, wet and disbelieving. "You turned into a *wolf* because of me. You broke charms you've worn for years, charms that were probably all that stood between you and whatever the Inquisition does to people like us, and you did it in four seconds without hesitating, and I keep thinking: why? Why would you—"

"Because they were going to kill you," he said.

The words came out simpler than anything he'd said in years. He watched them land on her like weather.

* * *

She bandaged him by candlelight. There was a box of tallow candles behind the altar, ancient but sound, and she lit three of them with her fingertips while he watched, and the small ritual of it—the warming of wax, the steadying of flame—seemed to settle her breathing.

"The charms," she said at last, tying off the linen. "Tell me about them."

"What do you want to know?"

"When. Where. Who put them on you." She came around the pew and sat facing him, cross-legged on the rotting wood, candlelight moving on her face. "I've watched you touch that collar-piece every night since the ford. Like a man checking a lock. I want to know what's behind the lock."

He looked at the fire instead of at her. It was easier.

"My mother's family were wolf-blooded. Northern stock, from before the Edicts—shepherds, mostly, the kind of gift that keeps your flock safe and your children fed. My father was a soldier. When I was nine, the winter I shifted for the first time, he didn't send for a priest or a smuggler. He sent for the Inquisition." A breath. "There was a program. Gifted children taken young, bound with iron, raised inside the hunt. They called us hounds. It wasn't a metaphor."

Paloma had gone very still.

"They put the charms on me at ten. They taught me to track people like my mother. They told me the gift was a disease and the charm was medicine, and I believed them, because believing was easier than being what I was." His jaw worked. "Eight years, Paloma. Eight years I hunted gifted folk across three provinces and wrote reports calling it mercy, because at least the ones I caught got registered instead of burned. And every night I touched the iron and thanked it. Do you understand? I *thanked* it."

"Yes," she said softly. "I understand."

"Do you?" He finally looked at her. "You hid in a valley where magic can't work. I hid inside the thing hunting me. We're not the same."

"No," she agreed. "We're worse. We hid better."

Something that was almost a laugh moved through him and cost him a wince. Above them, the candle flames leaned in a draft from the broken window, and the glass door in the eastern roundel caught the light and threw a small gold coin of it onto the altar stone.

"Maren knew," Paloma said, after a while. "About hiding. About what it costs." She drew her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. "I found pages in the grimoire last week—pages written to me, by name, in her hand, dated the year I was born. She'd been planning my whole childhood before I existed. Every lesson she didn't teach me was a decision. Every spell she withheld was a wall." Her voice dropped. "I used to think she was afraid for me. Now I think she was building me, the way you build a cellar. Something to survive in."

"And do you hate her for it?"

"I don't know." Honest, at least; he'd give her that. "I loved her. I buried her. Both things are true and they don't cancel." She looked up. "Do you hate your father?"

"I used to. Then I stood in a village square last spring and told a woman her son would be safer registered, and I heard his voice coming out of my mouth." He shook his head slowly. "Fear doesn't excuse him. But it explains the shape of him. And I know the shape from the inside now."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the frozen grass with a sound like a long exhale, and for a moment the whole valley seemed to breathe around the chapel.

"Serane will have felt the charms break," he said. "Iron that old screams when it dies. By morning she'll know exactly what ran through this forest, and she'll stop writing polite letters about my empty reports."

"Then we don't sleep here."

"We sleep here." He said it flatly, and she raised an eyebrow, and he almost smiled. "I can't walk, witch. And you can't carry me. Whatever comes tomorrow, we'll meet it standing. Tonight we're allowed to bleed."

* * *

Later—he never knew how much later, only that the candles had burned down to thumbs and the moon through the roundel had climbed—she asked him the question.

"What would freedom even mean? For you."

He was drifting at the edge of fever-sleep, the wound hot and distant, the wolf curled somewhere just beneath his thoughts like a banked fire. The question pulled him back.

"What?"

"Everyone keeps talking about the door." She nodded at the roundel, at the blue-and-gold sliver of moon above it. "Tamsin. The ghosts in the capital. Even the grimoire. A way out of Vessany, out of the Edict, out of all of it. And I keep lying awake trying to imagine walking through it and I realize—I don't know what's on the other side. I don't know what I'd *do*. I've been hiding so long that hiding is the only plan I have." Her chin rested on her knees. "So. If you walked through. What would you do? Who would you be, with the charms off and the Edict gone and no one hunting either of us?"

He was quiet for a long time.

"I don't know," he said finally. "I joined the hunt at ten. I've never been a person who wasn't property. Charmed child, crown hound, captain—it's all the same chain, different links." He turned his head against the pew back to look at her. In the candlelight her eyes were steady and very dark and waiting, and something in his chest that was neither the wolf nor the soldier leaned toward her like a plant toward a window. "I used to think freedom would be a place. Somewhere north, past the border ranges, where the Edicts thin out. A cabin. Snow. No reports to write."

"And now?"

"Now I think it might be a person."

The words hung in the chapel air, and he did not take them back, though every trained instinct in him screamed to. The candles burned. Her breath caught—small, audible, unmistakable in the silence.

"Aldric."

"I know." His voice was rough. "I know what I am. I'm the man who dragged a child out of a well-square in front of you. I'm the hunt, wearing a man's face. You'd be justified to—"

"Stop." She moved. Crossed the space between the pews and knelt in front of him, close enough that he could see the candlelight trembling in her eyes, and took his face in both hands—careful of the bandage, careful of the bruise, careful of him, and no one had been careful of him since he was nine years old. "Listen to me. On the ford, you had orders and a writ and every reason in the world, and you chose to lie for a stranger. In the capital you stood between me and the ghosts. Today you broke your own cage with your bare hands because they aimed at me." Her thumb traced his cheekbone, feather-light. "You are not the hunt. You are the man the hunt couldn't make."

"Paloma—"

"Two turns of the moon," she whispered. "Two turns, and the door opens, and I have to decide whether I go through it. And I have been so afraid, Aldric, because going through means leaving everything, and staying means— I couldn't see any version of the choice that didn't end with me alone." Her forehead came to rest against his, and her breath mingled with his, and the wolf under his skin lay utterly still, listening, as if it had waited its whole caged life for exactly this. "But tonight, in this chapel, I realized there's one version I hadn't counted. The one where whatever I choose, I'm not alone in it. That's all. That's everything. That's the whole of what I have to give you."

"It's enough," he said. "It's more than enough."

She kissed him then, gently, the way you kiss something you are afraid of breaking—or something that has already been broken and has healed crooked and beautiful. The candle flames bowed. High above, in the roundel, the painted moon seemed almost full.

When she drew back, her eyes were bright and fierce. "When this is done," she said, "when the door is answered one way or the other—there's a valley south of here that needs a garden tended, and a woman buried in it who'd have liked you, and a child I met in Moonturn Nine who deserves someone to teach her that her gift isn't a disease." Her hand slid down to rest over his heart, over the cold absence where the iron ring used to hang. "Come with me. Not through the door. *Here.* Build something with me on this side of it."

"Yes," he said, and the word cost him nothing at all. "Yes."

She smiled, and for a moment the chapel was warm.

Then the candles guttered—all three at once, though there was no draft—and Paloma's head snapped toward the eastern window, and Aldric felt the hackles rise along his new-loose skin as every sense he owned screamed the same message at once.

Horses. Many horses. Coming down the valley road at a trot, with lanterns shuttered, and at their head something that tasted in the air like burnt iron and old paper.

"Inquisitor," he breathed.

Paloma was already snuffing the candles with her mind. "How long do we have?"

Outside, far off down the frozen dark, the first lantern came unshuttered—a single point of light, patient as a drawn blade.

"Till dawn," Aldric said, and reached for his sword.
