## 14. Serane's Bargain

The inquisitor's tent smelled of ink and cold iron.

Aldric Vane had stood before commanders, magistrates, and once—memorably—a bishop who had tried to have him hanged on a rumor. None of them had made his skin prickle the way the inside of this tent did. It was not the censer burning some resin that stung the eyes. It was not the six silent guards in gray, or the map table weighted with daggers at its corners, or even Inquisitor Serane herself, small and neat as a locked box, her hands folded atop a ledger as though she had been waiting for him since dawn.

It was the charms.

He wore nine of them: iron nails bent into rings, a saint's medal with the face worn blank, a wolf tooth he'd told himself was only a keepsake. They hung against his chest beneath his gorget, and every one of them had begun to hum. Not audibly—he felt it in his teeth, behind his eyes, a low vibration like a plucked string refusing to die. The charms did that when another gift came near. He had spent eleven years teaching himself to ignore it.

Serane's gift, whatever it was, sang like a struck bell.

"Captain Vane." Her voice was pleasant, unhurried, the voice of a woman reading aloud to children by a fire. "Sit."

He sat. The campstool creaked under his armor. Outside, the encampment of the Edict Hunt sprawled across the floodplain below the ruined capital—a city of canvas and torchlight, four hundred soldiers and two hundred hired hounds-men, all of them arrayed against one woman and the stones she hid among. Aldric had led this hunt for three turns of the moon. He had written reports for three turns of the moon. Empty ones. Careful ones.

He wondered, now, which word she would use first: *insubordination* or *treason*.

"Do you know what I did before the crown gave me the Hunt?" Serane asked. She opened the ledger, wet her thumb, turned pages with the delicacy of someone handling relics. "I audited granaries."

"...Granaries."

"Twelve years of granaries." She looked up, and her eyes were pale gray, almost colorless, the eyes of a fish seen through ice. "Every barrel counted, every shipment weighed, every lord's tally compared against his tithe. People think an inquisitor hunts witches because witches are frightening. Witches are *small*, Captain. A witch lights candles. A witch heals a cow. What frightens the crown is arithmetic. Do you know what I found, auditing?"

"I imagine you found theft."

"I found a kingdom eating itself." She said it without drama, the way she might report a shortfall of barley. "The royal mint has been striking coins with less silver in them each year—the same faces, thinner metal. The army is owed nine months' pay. The western provinces haven't sent taxes in four years because their lords have stopped pretending to govern. And the king—" she closed the ledger softly, "—the king has taken to meeting with shipwrights. Shipwrights, Captain. In a landlocked country. Does that interest you?"

Aldric kept his face still. Nine months' pay explained the desertions he'd been ordered not to report. But shipwrights—

"The king means to leave," Serane said.

* * *

She let the words sit in the tent's smoky air while she poured wine from a clay jug into two cups, and pushed one across the map table to him. He didn't drink. She didn't seem to expect him to.

"There is a door," she went on. "Out of Vessany. You've heard the smugglers' stories—the erased nation, the country the old king burned off the maps a century ago. Every smuggler from here to the coast trades in fragments of it. Names of villages that no longer exist. Memories sold by the ounce. And underneath all that folklore, Captain, there is a stone door, and it opens, and where it opens onto is not our business and never will be. What matters is that it opens *out*. Away. Permanently, if the stories hold."

"And His Majesty believes a fireside tale."

"His Majesty believes my audit." For the first time something moved behind her face—not warmth, nothing so generous, but a kind of bleak satisfaction. "I traced the treasury's missing gold for three years. It wasn't stolen. It was *moved*—in stages, over a decade, into accounts that lead nowhere in Vessany. Nowhere in any country we have treaties with. Gold does not vanish, Captain. It goes somewhere. And the only somewhere our records cannot follow is a country that was erased."

Aldric's mouth was dry. He thought of Paloma—of the witch, he corrected himself, the way he corrected himself a hundred times a day and never successfully—walking through those nameless villages, asking her careful questions. He thought of her face when Tamsin the smuggler talked about the thirteenth turn. He had assumed she wanted escape the way any hunted thing wants escape: desperately, simply.

He had not understood that she was racing a king.

"The Edict of the Hunt," Serane said, "was drafted in the same month the last of the treasury transfers cleared. You may draw your own conclusions. A hunt for the gifted flushes out exactly the people who can find and open such a door. We do the crown's searching for it. Every gifted soul we register, we interrogate; every interrogation asks, sooner or later, the same question: *have you heard of the door?*" She smiled without any pleasure in it. "You have been asking that question too, Captain. I've read your reports."

Here it was, then. He folded his hands and waited.

"They are very well written," Serane said. "That is what condemned you. A man hunting honestly writes messy reports—false leads, dead informants, wasted weeks. Yours are clean. Too clean. Three turns of moon, four hundred men, and not one solid trail? Either you are incompetent—and your record says otherwise—or you are *editing*." She turned the ledger around to face him. "Page forty-one. The ford at the Marren. You reported capturing a fugitive and releasing her for interrogation under escort. Your escort log lists two troopers who, I find, were reassigned to the southern road that morning and could not possibly have been present. There was no escort, Captain. There was no interrogation. There was a woman at a river with magic in her hands, and a captain with winter eyes who looked at her and let her walk."

The charms against his chest were screaming now, silently, a chorus of bent nails and dead saints. He realized his hands had gone white-knuckled and forced them open.

"What is she to you?" Serane asked, and there it was—not the magistrate's question but the auditor's, precise, itemized, already priced.

"Nothing."

"You lie the way you write reports. Cleanly." She rose, walked the length of the table, and stopped close enough that he could smell the resin smoke clinging to her gray robes. "Captain Vane. Aldric. I am going to make you an offer, and I want you to understand that it is not mercy. I don't deal in mercy; it doesn't balance the books. I deal in purpose."

* * *

"Pardon," she said.

The word dropped into the tent like a coin into a dry well.

"Full pardon for your gift. Yes, your gift—I have known since the second week, when your hounds went strange around you at the checkpoint at Hollowmere and you went white as whey. Wolf-blood. Old blood, northern strain, suppressed so long I'm surprised it hasn't rotted through your iron yet. The Church calls it bestial. The Edict calls it registrable." She tilted her head. "I call it useful. Under my warrant, a registered gift in service of the crown is not a crime. It's an *asset*. You would be the first. The precedent matters, politically. The king likes precedents that flatter him."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange, you deliver the Marchetti girl to me before the thirteenth turn completes. Alive. Unspoiled." Serane's colorless eyes held his. "Because here is what your witch knows and you perhaps do not: the door demands a price. Everything true in these mountains demands a price. And a woman of valley blood—a keeper's bloodline, bred in that little magic-dead pocket of nowhere for one purpose and one purpose only—is worth more to that door than any king's gold. The old king of Vessany paid it once, a century ago, and look what it bought him: erasure. Safety. A country wiped so clean that even its ghosts repeat themselves. Our king means to pay it again—with whoever stands nearest when the moon turns. Preferably someone else. Ideally a frightened girl with a grimoire."

Aldric heard his own heartbeat. Slow. Wolf-slow. The charms were hot as coals against his sternum.

"You want her as a sacrifice."

"I want her *found*," Serane said, with the mildness of a woman correcting a column sum. "What the crown does with her afterward is above both our stations. Deliver her, and you walk away pardoned, registered, commissioned—purpose, Captain, real purpose, a hunt that hunts actual monsters instead of candle-lighting children. Or—" she returned to her chair, sat, smoothed her robes, "—you refuse, and I take the escort log to the tribunal at dawn. Desertion of duty. Concealment of a registrable gift. Aiding a fugitive of the Edict. You'll hang beside the boy you let slip through at Thornwick, and she will still be found—by men less gentle than you, on a schedule less forgiving. The moon does not wait for our convenience."

Outside, wind moved through the floodplain camp, snapping canvas, carrying the smell of ash from the ruined capital. Somewhere out there in the dark, among stones that remembered a massacre, Paloma was reading a dead woman's handwriting by candlelight, counting nights.

Eleven, he thought. Ten, now. Maybe ten.

"You said the king meets with shipwrights," Aldric said quietly. "For a door."

"For a *doorway*," Serane corrected. "Ships, Captain, are merely the shape hope takes when it has money. His Majesty intends to step through with his court, his guard, and what remains of the treasury, and let this bankrupt, haunted, ungrateful country close behind him like a mouth." She folded her hands again, neatly, finally. "Unless, of course, someone seals it first. Which brings me to the other half of my offer, and I confess this part I offer to no one else."

Aldric looked up.

"If the girl cannot be delivered—if she reaches that stone and chooses to spend herself on it—there is a version of tonight where the crown loses its escape and gains a martyr, and I lose my warrant and my head along with it." Serane's voice stayed level, pleasant, terrible. "There is another version. One where an inquisitor, arriving at the critical moment, finds the door sealed by a keeper's sacrifice and reports to His Majesty that the passage proved impassable after all. One where a certain wolf-blooded captain, having served the realm faithfully in its final hour, finds his pardon granted regardless—and a certain witch's ashes given honorable mention in no report at all." She leaned forward. "I am not asking you to betray her, Captain. I am asking which ending you intend to write. Because I have audited enough men to know that you have already chosen. I merely need to know whether to put your name in the column marked *asset*—or the one marked *expense*."

The tent flap stirred. The censer smoked. On the map table, between the dagger-weighted corners, the ruins of the capital were drawn in fine black ink, and beneath them, unmarked, uninkable, the place where a door waited for the thirteenth turn of the moon.

Aldric stood. His chair scraped back loud as a musket shot.

"I'll give you your answer," he said, "on the night."

Serane smiled her fish-through-ice smile and opened her ledger to a fresh page.

"Then I'll pencil you in," she said, "under *undetermined*—and Captain? Tell her the moon is ahead of schedule. My astronomers say the thirteenth turn begins tomorrow at dusk."

She dipped her pen. Aldric walked out into the dark, past the guards who did not look at him, past the tents and torches and the sleeping machinery of the hunt, and stood at the edge of the floodplain looking up at where the ruined city climbed the hill, one light burning high among the stones like a fallen star.

Ten hours, then. Not ten days.

He broke into a run.
