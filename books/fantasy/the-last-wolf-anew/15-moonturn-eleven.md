## 15. Moonturn Eleven

Two turns left. I counted them the way Maren taught me to count seeds—pressed into a fold of cloth, one by one, each with its own weight and its own promise of something that might never sprout.

The moon hung over the ruins of Vessany like a coin held up to the light, testing whether it was true silver or only tin. Aldric crouched at the lip of the ridge beside me, his breath fogging in the cold, and for a long moment neither of us spoke. Below us the dead capital spread out in the dark: walls without roofs, streets without names, windows like empty sockets in a face that had forgotten it was ever handsome.

"The antechamber is under the palace," he said at last. "Every account agrees. The old processional stair, descending from what used to be the throne room."

"And every crown soldier in three provinces agrees with them," I said. "I counted forty fires."

"Forty-one." He said it without apology. Wolf eyes saw better than mine; I was learning not to bristle at being corrected. "They've dug in. That's not a garrison, Paloma. That's an occupation. Someone told them exactly where to stand."

*Serane,* we both thought, and did not say it. Saying her name felt like inviting her.

We had come down from the hills on the ninth turn's last night, moving through country that still refused to behave. Roads looped when they should have run straight. A village we passed twice had two different wells, though both were dry and both bore the same carved wolf, worn smooth by hands that no longer existed to touch it. The land remembered Vessany the way a body remembers an amputated limb—an ache with nothing attached to it.

And beneath us, under all that forgetting, waited the door.

I felt it before I saw anything, the way you feel weather coming in your teeth. The grimoire rode against my ribs, warm as a sleeping animal. Two nights ago its pages had shown me the name—not all of it, not yet, but enough. Enough to know that the door wanted more than words. It wanted a name *given*, freely, permanently. It wanted someone with valley blood.

It wanted, though I hadn't yet let myself say it aloud, me.

"We go down tonight," Aldric said. "Not through the front. There's a drainage channel on the river side—the city's plumbing, if Vessany had plumbing, and everything I've seen says it did. It lets out below the guardposts."

"You've been reading Tamsin's maps."

"I've been memorizing Tamsin's maps." His mouth twitched, almost a smile. "She'd charge us for that too, if she knew."

The mention of her landed between us and sat there. We hadn't spoken of Tamsin since the night she offered to buy my memories of the valley and I refused, and she looked at me across that smuggler's table like a woman watching a door close. I had called her a traitor in my head a hundred times since. I had also, less often but more honestly, wondered what I would have done with a price like hers hanging over me.

"Come on," Aldric said, and we went down into the dark together.

* * *

The drainage channel smelled of a century of rain and nothing else, which was wrong—old stone should smell of rot, of moss, of time. But this was Vessany, and even its gutters had been scrubbed of memory. We moved by feel, our shoulders brushing the walls, Aldric's hand steady on my elbow when the floor dropped away into unseen steps.

He had changed since the chapel. Not visibly—he still wore the captain's grey like a second skin, still moved like a man built for doors kicking in—but something in him had been unstrung and restrung at a different tension. He slept now, actually slept, instead of lying awake listening to his own blood. On the tenth night he had shifted in the open, deliberately, under the moon, just to prove to himself that the sky didn't fall. When he came back to himself, kneeling in the frost with steam rising off his bare shoulders, he'd looked at me like a man surfacing from deep water.

"Well?" he'd asked.

"You're bleeding," I'd said. "But you're smiling."

Both things had been true. Both things, I suspected, were going to be rare commodities in the nights ahead.

The channel turned, narrowed, and opened its throat into darkness with a draft moving through it—air from below, air that had touched the door. My gift woke against my will, prickling along my arms like static before lightning. The grimoire grew so warm I had to shift it in its sling.

Aldric froze, one fist raised.

Light ahead. Torchlight, guttering amber around a corner of ancient masonry, and beneath it voices—two men, bored, the particular boredom of soldiers guarding something they don't believe in.

"—telling you, there's nothing down here but rocks and rats. Why the Inquisitor wants the rat-cellar guarded—"

"Not your question to ask."

"It's always my question to ask. You ask it, then. Go on. Knock on Serane's door and say *pardon, ma'am, what exactly am I dying for?*"

Silence. Then, quieter, the first soldier again: "My cousin was at the ford. Said Captain Vane let the witch walk right past him. Said he *escorted* her." A pause. "Funny thing to escort."

Aldric's jaw worked. I put my hand flat against his back and felt the tremor in him—not fear, fury—and felt it settle because my hand was there. Whatever we were to each other now, it lived in gestures like that. Words had become expensive. We spent them carefully.

We backed away from the torchlight and found another way—a crack in the wall wide enough for me and, after he exhaled and folded himself smaller than a man that size should fold, wide enough for him. It let us out onto a gallery above a vast sunken chamber, and there we stopped, both of us, because there it was.

The antechamber.

Even ruined, even robbed, it took the breath out of me. Pillars like the trunks of trees that had never grown on this earth. A floor of black stone inlaid with silver lines that formed—when I let my eyes unfocus—words. The same word, repeated, spiraling inward toward a stairway at the far end, descending into a glow that wasn't firelight and wasn't moonlight and wasn't any light I had a name for.

The door's true name, written on the floor of its own house, worn nearly smooth by ten decades of nobody left alive to read it.

And between us and it: soldiers.

Twenty of them, at least, posted in pairs along the gallery and the floor below, torches burning in iron brackets, pikes stacked within reach of every hand. They had camp beds down there. Cookfires. They were not visiting. They had been sent to live in the doorway of a nation's last secret until their king could walk through it and abandon everyone who'd paid for his crown.

"There," Aldric breathed, pointing with his chin. At the foot of the descending stair, two figures stood apart from the rest—one tall, robed, still as a drawn blade. Even at this distance I knew the set of those shoulders.

Serane. She had come herself.

And beside her, hooded, wrists bound in iron wire that I could smell from the gallery—that I could *feel*, a dead cold spot in the warm dark—someone small and sharp-featured, arguing even in captivity, mouth going a mile a minute while a soldier held her by the arm.

Tamsin.

* * *

We lay flat on the gallery in the shadow of a fallen pillar, close enough that I could count the grey hairs in Aldric's temple, and watched.

Serane circled Tamsin slowly, the way I imagined she circled everything—looking for the seam. Her voice carried up to us, stripped of warmth, patient as erosion.

"You sold the crown this location eleven years ago, mistress Ferrow. Sold it once already. And yet here you are again, caught in the ruins with maps in your possession that predate your sale. Which suggests either that you cheated your buyers—" she stopped, tilted her head, "—or that you've been trying to buy it back. From whom, I wonder?"

"I'm a trader," Tamsin said. Her voice was thinner than I remembered, scraped thin. "You want to buy something, I'll sell it. You want to hang somebody, I'll be convenient. But don't dress it up as philosophy, Inquisitor. Neither of us believes in anything but leverage."

Something flickered across Serane's face—not anger. Recognition. For one strange moment they looked like two women who might, in another country, have been friends.

"Leverage," Serane said. "Yes. Here is yours. The man who bought your information eleven years ago kept records. Those records name you, describe you, and list the payment: certain memories, purchased from a memory-broker in the border towns. Memories of a girl who ran messages for the last free villages of Vessany. Who carried water to rebels in these very ruins. Whose father died on this floor." She let that sit. "You didn't just sell a door, mistress Ferrow. You sold the only person who still remembered what it cost. Yourself."

Down on the black floor, Tamsin went very still.

From our hiding place, I watched it happen—I watched a woman hear the full accounting of her own life read aloud by her enemy. And I understood suddenly, with a lurch, why she had come back. Not for profit. Not even for redemption, maybe. She had spent eleven years trading in erased memories, buying and selling other people's yesterdays, because she had sold her own and couldn't stand the silence where it used to be.

She'd come home to the crime scene.

"I can give you the witch," Serane continued, softly now, which was worse. "Pardon. Land. Your memories returned—yes, we found the broker's vault; the crown confiscates such things. Everything you sold, given back. All I require is that you tell me how the door opens. You've seen the grimoire. You've traveled with the Marchetti girl. Speak, and walk out of here whole."

The torches cracked. Somewhere above us, wind moved through a roofless hall, moaning through empty window sockets.

And Tamsin started to laugh.

It was terrible laughter, wet and ragged, and it echoed off the pillars, and the soldiers shifted uneasily as though the dead capital itself disapproved. She laughed until she bent double against her bonds, and then straightened, and spat at Serane's feet.

"You want to know how it opens?" she said. "Ask the girl with the book. Because I'll tell you what I know for free, Inquisitor, as a professional courtesy: whatever she pays, it'll be more than you're offering, and it'll be worth more too." Her chin came up. "Eleven years I made money off people's missing pieces. You think I don't recognize the shape of this deal? You're not offering to make me whole. You're offering to make me *yours*. Whole, and yours, and holding the door open for a king who'd sell this entire country twice to save his own skin." She smiled, and it was the old Tamsin smile, sharp as a smuggler's knife. "No sale."

Serane regarded her for a long moment. Then she nodded to the soldiers. "Take her below. Separate cell. If the witch comes for her—and she will—we'll learn a great deal from the rescue attempt."

They dragged Tamsin toward a side passage. At the threshold she stopped, twisted against her guards' grip, and looked up—straight up, directly at the gallery where we hid, at the exact patch of shadow where we lay.

She couldn't have seen us. It wasn't possible.

She winked.

Then they pulled her through the door and she was gone, and I was pressing my hand over my own mouth to keep from crying out, and Aldric's arm was iron across my back, holding me down, holding me together.

"She knew we were there," I whispered.

"Tamsin always knows where the exits are." But his voice had gone rough. "Paloma—she led them to catching her. Don't you see it? She got caught on purpose. With maps, with evidence, with that mouth of hers. She walked into Serane's hands the day we entered the city." He shook his head slowly. "She's not bait they set. She's bait she *made*. She's putting herself in the middle of the board so that when we move, half those soldiers move with her."

"To buy us a distraction," I said. "With her own past. The one thing she spent eleven years trying to buy back—and she's spending it instead."

Below us, Serane stood alone at the foot of the stair, facing the glow that rose from the door's threshold, and even from this distance I saw her reach out one gloved hand toward the light—and stop, short of touching, the way a person stops short of a flame. Then she turned and climbed the stairs and was gone, and the chamber settled into torchlit routine: twenty soldiers guarding a name they couldn't read, written in silver on the floor beneath their boots.

Aldric rolled onto his back and stared at the vaulted dark above us. I lay beside him. Our shoulders touched. Above the ruin, invisible through a hundred feet of stone, the eleventh moon climbed.

"Two nights from tomorrow," he said quietly. "When the thirteenth turn rises, she'll stage her escape—or her execution, depending on how it goes—and every eye in that room follows her. That's our stairway. That's our opening." He turned his head, and his winter eyes found mine in the dark, and for a moment the captain fell away and it was only Aldric, afraid, asking. "Whatever the door asks of you—whatever the name costs—you tell me first. Promise me that. No more secrets spent alone."

I thought of the grimoire against my ribs. Of the final pages I had read and not told him about, the symmetry waiting there like a blade wrapped in silk. *Whoever gives the name cannot pass.*

"No more secrets," I said.

It wasn't a lie. It was only a debt I meant to pay later, and I hated myself for the difference, and I kissed him anyway, there in the dark above the door, tasting dust and smoke and the two remaining turns of the moon.

Far below us, faint as a heartbeat through the black stone, something turned over in its sleep—and began, very slowly, to wake.
