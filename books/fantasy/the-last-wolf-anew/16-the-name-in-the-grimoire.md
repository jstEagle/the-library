## 16. The Name in the Grimoire

The cipher broke on the third night, and it broke because of a mistake Maren had made forty years ago.

Paloma found it the way she found most things now—in the margins, in the small human errors of a dead woman's hand. The grimoire's final chapters were written in sliding script, letters that shifted meaning depending on which moon-word anchored them. Aldric had fallen asleep sitting up against the chapel wall, his wounded shoulder bound in linen she'd boiled clean, his face slack with the particular exhaustion of a man who had finally stopped pretending he wasn't tired. The candle between them guttered low. She should have slept too. Instead she sat with the book open across her knees, tracing the same passage for the hundredth time, when she noticed that one word—the word that appeared at the head of every page of the last chapter—had been written *twice*.

*Vessa. Vessa.*

Not a repetition. A correction. Maren had written the name once, struck it through, and written it again with a single stroke different—a stroke Paloma had taken for ink-blot or haste. But Maren was never hasty. And she never blotted her ink.

Paloma's hands began to shake before her mind caught up to why.

She worked by candlelight, matching the corrected spelling against the moon-tables in the grimoire's middle pages, against the star-charts Tamsin had sold them for the price of two favors and an apology, against the fragments of ghost-song they'd heard replaying in the ruined capital—that endless night the erased city lived over and over, the king's voice crying out the words of sealing while something behind the door screamed in a language that had died with its country. Each source was a thread. She pulled them all toward the center, and the center, when it came into focus, was a name.

Not *Vessa*. That was the country's name, the map-name, the name printed on charts a century old and burned from every archive since. The door did not answer to the country. The door answered to what the king had called it in the last hour of the last night—what he'd named it as he sealed it, weeping, according to the ghosts who could not stop reenacting him: *the way out of me.*

That was not poetry. Paloma understood that now, her candle trembling, Aldric breathing slow and even three feet away. It was grammar. In the old tongue of Vessany—the tongue Maren had taught her in pieces disguised as lullabies and herb-lore, so that Paloma would carry it without knowing she carried it—the phrase *the way out of me* was a construction, a formula, and the door's true name was built inside it like a seed inside fruit.

*Il-vessan atha.* The road out of the body of the land.

But there was more. There was always more; the book was a well that deepened the longer you looked down it. Beneath the name, in plain script at last, unencoded because Maren had wanted no mistake possible, were four lines. Paloma read them aloud without meaning to, her voice a whisper in the dark chapel, and the words seemed to settle into the stone around her like silt into water.

*A door is a promise made of hunger.*
*It opens for a name freely given.*
*It takes what it is given.*
*And the giver remains.*

She read them again. Then again, slower, the way you press on a bruise to learn how deep it goes. *The giver remains.* She thought at first it meant the giver would be bound somehow, tethered, obligated—a debt owed to stone. That would have been bearable. That would have been a price like any other price, and she had been learning all year what prices looked like.

But the next paragraph, in Maren's tightest hand, spelled it out with a cruelty that could only have been mercy, once:

*Understand this, girl, whoever you are, whoever I failed to make you: the door does not accept a name the way a merchant accepts coin. A name given to the door becomes the door. You will not lose your name as a man loses a purse. You will become the hinge itself. The one who speaks the name opens the way—and stands forever on the threshold, neither in nor out, holding it open for others. No one who opens the door has ever walked through it. That is not a rule imposed upon the door. That is what the door is.*

Paloma closed the book.

Her hands were very still on the cover, which surprised her. She had expected shaking, tears, some dramatic collapse of the kind she'd read about in the few novels smuggled into the valley. Instead there was only a terrible quiet, a clarity like the air after the first frost, when every distant thing suddenly looks close enough to touch.

*Whoever opens the door cannot pass through it.*

She thought about the thirteenth turn rising in nine days. She thought about the valley—Maren's garden going to seed untended, the neighbors who had never known her, the whole soft green cage of it that she had fled and learned, too late, was built around her like a lock around a key. She thought about the child in the well square, dragged away by crown soldiers for lighting candles with a thought, and whether she was still alive, and whether the Edict would ever end if the door stood open for a bankrupt king to slip through with his court and his coffers, leaving a hunted country behind him.

And she thought about Aldric, asleep against the wall with his wounds stitched shut by her own hands, who had broken his iron charms in a chapel that smelled of ash and old incense and told her what he was—wolf-blooded, gift-suppressed, a hunter who had spent his whole life pretending to be exactly what he hunted. He had said it plainly, the way he said everything, kneeling in the candlelight like a man laying down arms: *I let a boy go once because I knew what it was to run. I've hated myself every day since, for the letting go and for the hating.*

She loved him. She had known it since Moonturn Nine, since the skirmish in the ruins when her gift had flared out of her like a struck bell and she had turned, certain she would find horror on his face, and found instead something worse and better: recognition. Two hidden things seeing each other by their own light.

If she opened the door, she would stand on its threshold until the world ended or the door did. She would hold it open for him—for everyone—for a king who wanted to abandon the ruin he'd made. She would watch Aldric walk through into whatever waited beyond the maps, and she would remain. Not dead. Not free. *Remained*, in the oldest sense of the word: kept, kept, kept, like a flame in a lamp, like a key in a lock.

Unless she refused. Unless she sealed it, spending her gift entire, binding the door forever—and the plan she'd carried since Moonturn Seven crystallized into something she could finally see whole, and its shape was a coffin, and its shape was a garden, and she could not tell which.

* * *

"You've been awake all night."

Aldric's voice came rough with sleep. He had shifted sometime in the dark hours; he sat forward now, wincing at his shoulder, his winter-grey eyes finding her face in the candle's last light with that unsettling precision of his—not wolf-sense exactly, but adjacent to it, the habit of a man who had spent decades reading rooms for danger reading her instead.

"The name," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her, flattened, like paper. "I found it."

He came to sit beside her slowly, favoring the wound, and did not reach for the book. He watched her instead. He was very good at waiting; the hunt had taught him that.

"It's *il-vessan atha*," she said. "The road out of the body of the land. But that's not—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a cost, Aldric. There's always a cost, but this one is—"

"Tell me."

So she told him. She read him the four lines, and then Maren's paragraph, and she watched his face do something she had almost never seen it do: nothing. Absolute stillness, the stillness of a man standing very carefully on ice and listening for the crack. When she finished, the silence lasted long enough for the candle to drown itself in its own wax, and she lit another with a thought, without thinking, the flame leaping obediently to her will—and even that small ease of power felt, in that moment, like counting coins she might soon have to spend down to the last copper.

"Then we don't open it," Aldric said at last.

"We have to."

"No." He turned to face her fully, and the new candlelight caught the scar along his jaw, the iron-charm burns on his throat that would never quite fade. "Listen to me. We came here believing the door was a way out. For you. For everyone the Edict hunts. If the price is your whole self—if it's *forever*—then it isn't a door, it's a trap wearing a door's shape, and I will not—"

"Aldric." She took his hand. His knuckles were scarred; his grip, when he finally closed it around hers, was careful in a way that undid her. "Think about what happens if we seal it instead. The valley survives. The cage dissolves—the magic-deadness lifts, everyone in it becomes what they actually are, free. The door never opens for Serane's king, never opens for anyone. The hunt ends, because the thing the hunt is really for stops existing."

"And you?"

"I stay," she said. "I stay anyway. That's the symmetry no one tells you: sealing it costs the same. My gift, my name, my—" She laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "Myself, I suppose. Just in the other direction. Open, I'm the keeper of a doorway. Sealed, I'm the grave-marker of one. Either way I'm the woman who gave her name to a piece of stone."

"Or," he said, "you give up both plans, and we find a third road. Tamsin knows things. The ghosts in the capital know things. Somewhere in a century of erased history there's a precedent, a loophole, a—"

"There isn't." She said it gently, and saw him hear it, and saw the fight go out of him the way water goes out of a cracked bowl. "I've read every page, Aldric. Maren spent forty years looking for the loophole. The last chapter isn't instructions for escaping the price. It's an apology for not finding one."

He bowed his head. For a moment he was only a tired, wounded man in a ruined chapel, and she understood that he was grieving, and that he was doing it silently and immediately, without bargaining, because that was how he did everything—he had decided years ago that he would rather break than bend dishonestly, and here he was, breaking.

"When?" he asked finally.

"Nine nights. The turn rises."

He nodded slowly. Then he lifted his head, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—something that frightened her more than Serane's soldiers, more than the whole machinery of the hunt bearing down on them.

"Then I have nine days," he said quietly, "to convince you that you're worth more than a door."

"Aldric—"

"No. Hear me." His grip tightened on her hand, gentle and immovable. "You think this is arithmetic. One life against a valley, against a country, against every gifted soul under the Edict. You've already done the sum and decided the remainder doesn't matter because the remainder is you. But I spent my whole career hunting people who made that sum. Do you know what they all had in common? Every gifted soul I ever took in—they'd all decided, somewhere deep, that they were the part of the equation that could be sacrificed. That's what the Edict actually kills. Not people. The belief that they're allowed to want to live."

"That's not what this is."

"Isn't it? Then tell me what you *want*, Paloma. Not what the valley needs, not what the door demands, not what Maren wrote in her last chapter. What do you want?"

The question went through her like cold water through cloth. She opened her mouth to say *freedom*—the word she'd carried out of the valley like a stolen heirloom—and found it wouldn't come. Because freedom had been the door, and the door was a lie shaped like hope, and underneath the lie, when she finally looked honestly, was something smaller and more terrible:

She wanted mornings. She wanted a garden with someone's help hauling the water. She wanted to teach some frightened child that her gift was not a disease. She wanted Aldric's grey eyes in the first light, unguarded, looking at her the way they looked at her now. She wanted a life, not an exit from one.

"I want to choose," she said at last, barely above a whisper. "That's all I've ever wanted. Everyone else chose for me—Maren, the valley, the Edict, the door. I just want one thing that's mine."

"Then choose this," he said. "Choose to live past the thirteenth turn. Choose the ugly option, the selfish one, the one where you walk away from a stone that's asking for your name and don't look back. Let the door stay shut and locked and hungry. Let the valley stay caged a little longer, and let someone else, someday, find a better answer—one that doesn't require you."

"The valley won't survive the Edict that long. Serane's already—"

"Then we'll fight Serane," he said simply. "We'll fight her for nine days, ninety days, nine years. I've been fighting for the wrong side my whole life. Let me finally do it right."

Outside the chapel, far off in the dark, a horn sounded—long, low, and answered by another, closer. Then a third. Aldric was on his feet before the echo died, wolf-quick despite the wound, dousing the candles with a sweep of his arm.

"They've found us," he said. "That's a search-line closing, three horns—Serane doesn't spread a line unless she's sure."

Paloma thrust the grimoire into her coat, pressed her palm flat against its cover through the wool, and felt the name sleeping inside it like a coal. Nine days. She had nine days to decide whether to become a door, a grave, or a runaway—and the world, with its usual indifference to human arithmetic, had decided to shorten the count.

Aldric pulled her toward the chapel's back wall, where the old smuggler's gap Tamsin had shown them gaped into the night.

"How fast can you run?" he asked.

"Faster than I could a year ago," she said. "Ask me again in nine days."

Something flickered across his face—fear and love and grim laughter all tangled together—and then they were moving, into the dark, with the horns calling behind them and the name burning against her heart like a second, smaller moon.
