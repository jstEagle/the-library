## 17. Thirteenth Turn Rising

**Aldric**

The moon did not rise so much as arrive.

It came up over the broken crown of the ruined capital like a thing summoned—vast, copper-colored at first, then paling as it climbed, and with every finger's width it gained, Aldric felt the iron charms against his chest grow warmer. Not painfully warm. Not yet. But warm the way a lock grows warm before it fails, and he had worn these charms long enough to know every stage of their dying.

He stood at the top of the stairway in the old palace's root, where the marble steps descended into darkness in a spiral narrow enough for one man to hold. Behind and below him, Paloma was already three landings down, the grimoire open across her forearms, her lips moving through syllables that made the torch flames lean toward her like listeners.

"Say it again," she had told him an hour ago, when there was still time for words. "If I don't come back up—if the door takes what I think it takes—you say my name until you can't say anything else. Promise me."

He hadn't promised. He'd said, "Come back up," which was not the same thing, and she'd known it, and neither of them had pressed further because there are truths a person can carry only if they don't look at them directly, and they were both carrying several.

Now the moon cleared the last ridge of ruin, and the thirteenth turn began.

He felt it before he saw any sign of it—a pressure behind his eyes, a loosening in his joints, the wolf rising in him like water finding its level. The charms flared hot enough to sting. For thirteen months he had kept the gift leashed; tonight, with the whole erased country humming beneath them like a struck bell, the leash felt less like discipline than like holding a door shut against a flood.

And far off, across the shattered avenues, he heard the hunt coming.

Not horses. Serane didn't ride horses into battle; she rode fear. What he heard was boots—hundreds of them, moving in the disciplined cadence of crown soldiers who had been promised something, and dogs, real ones this time, and beneath all of it a sound he knew from his childhood nightmares: the low harmonic whine of the Inquisitor's chains, silver-wrapped, singing to themselves as they swung.

"Aldric." Paloma's voice floated up the stairwell, thin with distance. "They're early."

"They're not early." He drew his sword and set the scabbard aside, laying the bare blade flat on the stone where he could reach it without looking. "The moon is late. It always is."

* * *

**Paloma**

The stairway smelled of dust and cold metal and, underneath everything, faintly of juniper—the same scent that clung to Maren's grimoire, the same scent, Paloma realized only now, that had hung about their herb garden all those years, so constant she had never once thought to wonder why a valley that grew nothing else grew juniper along its eastern wall.

She stopped on the landing and put her hand against the wall. The stone was warm here. Everything below the third landing was warm, as though the door exhaled through the rock, slow and patient, a breath it had been holding for a hundred years.

Eleven pages of the grimoire were left unburned. She had read them eleven times since Moonturn Nine, and each reading had peeled away another layer of Maren's careful, terrible honesty. The final cipher had come apart two nights ago, by candlelight, with Aldric asleep sitting up against a fallen column with his sword across his knees: the name of the door, written in the old Vessany script, wrapped in a grammar that only worked aloud, only worked at night, only worked now.

She had spoken it once, silently, just shaping it behind her teeth—and the stone under her palms had shivered like a horse feeling a fly.

Above her, faint through six floors of ruin, she heard the first horn.

Her hands were shaking. She looked at them with a kind of clinical disappointment—these hands that had learned to light candles with a thought, to coax fever out of a child's brow, to do all the small quiet magics Maren had taught her as though they were gardening—and willed them still. They obeyed. Her magic answered too eagerly; her body, at least, could still be trained.

"One more flight," she said aloud, to herself, to the ghost she sometimes felt walking beside her in Maren's silence. "Then we see what you really built."

The horn sounded again, closer, joined by a second. And then, cutting through both, a sound that turned her blood to ice water:

A howl. Long, deliberate, unmistakably human-throated even in its wolf-shape. Aldric, calling down to her the signal they had agreed on and never believed they'd use.

*Go.*

* * *

**Aldric**

They came up the grand ramp on the north side, and he counted them the way he'd taught himself to count as a boy hiding in a root cellar while grown men searched the house above: by sound, by weight of footfall, by the particular music of armor. Forty at the front, maybe sixty total, spread in a crescent because Serane always sent a crescent—she thought in encirclements, in nets, in closing things.

The Inquisitor herself came last, as she always did, letting her soldiers be the wave that broke first.

Aldric stood at the head of the stairway with his sword bare and his charms glowing through his shirt like coals, and he let go.

There was no dramatic tearing, no cinematic moment of transformation. The wolf simply arrived, the way dawn arrives—suddenly everywhere, undeniable, having been true the entire time. His senses opened like gates. He could smell the soldiers' terror-sweat, the tallow of their torches, the dog-musk, and under it all, threading toward him like a hook through water, the cold incense-and-iron scent of Serane. His bones lengthened. His spine curved. His hands became paws and his paws became weapons, and the last human thing he saw clearly was the moon, full and white and merciless, hanging directly over the stairway like a witness.

The first soldiers around the corner died quickly, because they expected a man and met a wolf the size of a yearling calf, and because he had spent thirteen months learning exactly how much violence he was capable of and hating himself for knowing.

He did not kill the boy at the rear—the one who dropped his spear and ran. Some habits survived even this.

Behind him, below him, he heard Paloma's footsteps descending, steady as a heartbeat, growing fainter, going down toward the door, and he planted himself on the landing above the final turn of stairs and understood, with the simple clarity of the wolf, that he would hold this ground or die on it.

Serane's voice rose over the chaos—not shouting; Serane never shouted. It carried anyway, the way certain voices do, as though the air itself were conscripted.

*"Captain Vane."*

The soldiers parted. She walked through them with her chains gathered in one fist, silver links whispering against each other, her pale face lit orange by the burning brazier a soldier had overturned. She looked at the wolf on the stairs—at the torn uniform shirt, the iron charms cracked open on the flagstones like eggshells—and something moved through her expression too fast to name.

"I wondered," she said. "Thirteen months of empty reports. Thirteen months of a hunt that never quite closed. I told myself: Vane is thorough. Vane is careful." She tilted her head. "I should have trusted what I felt at the checkpoint at Drenn's Ford. You looked at me that day like a man looking in a mirror."

The wolf said nothing. Wolves cannot. That was the mercy of it.

"But you're standing between me and the door," Serane went on, softly, drawing a single chain from the coil, letting it swing, "so whatever you are, Captain, tonight you're an obstacle. And I have a king waiting, and a moon that will not wait with him."

She flicked her wrist. The chain sang.

The wolf met it midair.

* * *

**Paloma**

She heard the fight as weather—a rolling thunder through the stone, the shriek of chain on fang, shouts cut short. Each sound landed in her chest like a fist, and each time she made herself take the next step down instead of turning, made herself trust him the way he had asked her to trust him at the ford, at the chapel, in all the small surrenders that had built, without either of them naming it, into the thing she would not call love because naming it tonight felt like spending it.

The last landing was round, a chamber no wider than a mill wheel, and the door filled it.

She had imagined stone. It was not stone—or it was, but stone the way a seed is wood. It stood ten feet high and shaped like nothing architectural: a slab of grey-green material veined with lines that moved when she wasn't looking directly at them, like water remembering it used to be a river. No hinges. No handle. Only, at the height of her heart, a recess the size of two cupped hands, and inside it, carved deep and worn shallow by a century of absence, the space where a name goes.

Warmth poured off it. Not heat—warmth like a hearth in a house where someone is expecting you.

"You've been waiting," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she intended. "A hundred years, and you've been waiting."

The door said nothing, but the veins in its surface brightened, very slightly, the way a sleeper breathes deeper without waking.

She set the grimoire on the floor, opened to the last page, and knelt. The final cipher, Maren's hand gone ragged with age and urgency, laid out the grammar of the opening like a recipe for bread: *the blood of the valley speaks the name; the name given freely opens; what opens cannot close behind the opener.* She had read it fifty times. On the forty-ninth reading, she had finally understood what *cannot close behind the opener* meant, and she had closed the book and walked out into the rain and stood there until Aldric came looking for her, and he had stood in the rain too, saying nothing, because he had understood it on the first reading and had been waiting, all this time, for her to catch up.

That was later. That was after. Right now the door breathed warmth and the moon was at its zenith and somewhere above her the man she loved was holding a staircase alone against sixty soldiers, and she had perhaps a quarter hour, perhaps ten minutes, perhaps less.

She pressed her palm flat against the door.

It knew her instantly. The warmth surged up her arm, intimate and enormous, and images that were not images flooded her—not pictures but certainties: *this one, this blood, this is the key the valley was grown around.* The door recognized her the way a lock recognizes its own key, and the recognition was so complete, so welcoming, that she nearly wept.

"Yes," she whispered. "I'm here. I'm the last of what you were made for."

Above her, muffled by stone, the fighting changed character—a scream that was half human, the clang of a sword dropped, Serane's voice rising in genuine anger for the first time in all the months Paloma had known of her.

Less than ten minutes, then.

She took a breath, found the first syllable of the name behind her teeth—

—and the temperature of the chamber dropped.

Frost feathered across the grimoire's open page. The door's warmth guttered like a candle in wind. Paloma spun, magic already leaping to her hands faster than fear could follow it, and found the stairway mouth behind her filling with silver light: chains, dozens of them, descending the spiral on their own like serpents swimming, and beyond them, unhurried, the pale face of Inquisitor Serane, bloodied at the temple, her coat torn, her eyes bright with the particular joy of a woman whose worst suspicion has just been gloriously confirmed.

"There she is," Serane said. "The witch of the nameless country." She stepped onto the landing, and the chains coiled up around her shoulders like a living mantle. "I confess I hoped it would be you. A girl who hides in a dead valley, carrying a dead woman's book—it's almost poetic. The king wanted the door." Her gaze slid past Paloma to the great grey-green slab, and something naked and hungry crossed her face. "But the king doesn't know what I know. The door doesn't want a king. It wants *you.*"

Paloma backed toward the door, one hand still raised, fire gathering in her palm with an eagerness that frightened her more than the chains did. "Stay back."

"No," Serane said gently, walking forward, one step, another, frost crackling under her boots. "I don't think I will. You see, whatever happens in the next few minutes—whether you open that door or I drag you back to the capital in irons—someone is walking through it tonight. And I have spent my whole life serving a country that is drowning, witch, and I am *tired.*"

From far above, faint through the stone, came a sound that stopped Paloma's heart: a howl, thinner than before, ragged at the edges, and beneath it the ring of a sword being drawn by someone else's hand.

Serane smiled. "Your captain is losing."

Paloma looked at the door. At the recess shaped like cupped hands. At the moonlight bleeding down the stairwell, silver and final, the thirteenth turn at its height.

She opened her mouth—and spoke.
