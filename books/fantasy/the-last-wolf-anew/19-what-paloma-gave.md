## 19. What Paloma Gave

The door did not look like a door.

It looked like the absence of one—a seam of darkness in the living rock, taller than two men and narrow as a coffin lid, its edges worn soft by a century of nothing touching it. No hinges. No handle. Only the name, which was not carved anywhere but which Paloma could feel pressed against the inside of her teeth like a word she had been holding in her mouth since before she could speak.

*Vessany-who-was-Vessany-when-the-moon-counts-thirteen.*

She stood at the foot of it with her palms open at her sides, and the antechamber breathed around her—cold stone air that moved in slow tides, as though the whole buried hall were the chest of some sleeping animal. The grimoire hung heavy in the sling across her back. Above her, through forty feet of rock and rubble, she could hear the thirteenth turn beginning: boots on stone, Serane's voice cracking like a whip, and beneath it all, lower and more terrible, the sound of Aldric fighting.

Not fighting like a soldier. Fighting like a wolf.

She had heard him once before, on the night of moonturn nine, when he had let the gift slip for three seconds to break a man's sword arm—and even those three seconds had sounded like something out of an old wife's tale, too large and too wild for the quiet captain who drank his tea without sugar and folded his cloak into a perfect square. This was not three seconds. This was a country of howling, echoing down the stairway, and every cry of it went through her like a hook.

*Hold the stair,* he had said. *I'll hold it until there's no stair left.*

And she had run, because that was the bargain they had made with each other without ever saying it aloud: *you go down; I go nowhere but after you.*

The door waited.

Paloma pressed her hand flat against the dark seam. The stone was neither warm nor cold. It was *expectant*, the way Maren's garden had been expectant on the morning before a frost—the whole valley holding still, waiting to see what would be asked of it.

"I know what you want," she said quietly. Her voice fell into the dark and did not echo. "A name. Freely given. Valley blood, spoken into you, and never taken back." She laughed once, a small broken sound. "Maren wrote eleven pages about how to find you. She spent one sentence on what you cost. Cowardly old woman. Loving old woman."

The seam did not answer. Doors like this one never answered; they only *listened*, the way the valley had listened to her for twenty-three years, cataloguing every small forbidden flicker of her gift, keeping her safe the way a lock keeps a key safe.

Behind her, up the long stair, the howling stopped.

Her heart stopped with it.

Then—boots. Many boots, descending fast, disciplined, unhurried in the way of soldiers who have finally won. And over them, cutting through the dark like a blade drawn slowly from its sheath, came a voice Paloma knew only from proclamations and rumor: smooth, dry, patient as rot.

"Captain Vane is remarkably durable," said Inquisitor Serane. "But even wolves tire."

Light bloomed at the top of the stair—lanterns, a dozen of them, and the shadows of men spilling down ahead of them like oil. Paloma did not turn around. She kept her hand on the door, and she felt, with a strange and terrible clarity, that the door felt her heartbeat quicken and was pleased by it.

*Freely given.* Fear was allowed. Fear was human. The door wanted only that she choose.

Footsteps rang closer. A lantern's glow slid along the walls, gilding the ghost-murals she and Aldric had found on moonturn eight—the erased capital painted in its last living night, the king with his hands full of fire, the door drinking a sacrifice. The ghosts still replayed that night somewhere deeper in these ruins. Paloma had watched them twice. She had promised herself she would never watch a third time.

"You are a difficult woman to corner," Serane said behind her. "Do you know I've read your file? There isn't one. Twenty-three years in that valley and no record, no registration, no mistake. You were invisible." A pause, silk over steel. "Someone made you that way. Someone very good."

"Maren," Paloma said. "Yes."

"And yet here you stand, at the only door in the world that matters to my king, holding the only book that can open it." Serane came around into Paloma's sightline then, unhurried, lantern held high. Up close the Inquisitor was smaller than her reputation—grey-eyed, grey-haired, neat as a ledger—with a face that did arithmetic rather than feeling. "So let us not waste the thirteenth turn pretending. My sovereign wants passage. He wants it tonight. And you, witch, want your wolf to live. These facts arrange themselves beautifully, don't you think?"

Paloma's throat closed.

"He's alive, then."

"For the moment. Bleeding. Impressive. He took six men down the stair before we persuaded him to stop." Serane set the lantern on a broken plinth, and its light steadied. "Here is the shape of the evening, Marchetti. You will open this door. You will step aside. My king and his chosen household will pass through, and the crown will be free of a bankrupt country it no longer wishes to govern. In exchange, Captain Vane lives, pardoned, decorated even. The hunt ends. Everyone gets what they deserve." Her mouth curved, thin as a knife's edge. "You get what you've been walking toward for thirteen turns of the moon. Freedom. A country that isn't this one."

*Freedom.* The word landed in Paloma like a coin dropped down a well—she heard it fall and fall and never heard it strike bottom.

Because she had read the final pages. She had sat in a ruined chapel with Aldric's blood on her hands and read what Maren had hidden in the grimoire's last quire, in ink so old it had gone brown as dried flowers:

*A door opens one way. It takes a name to open and it keeps the name that opens it. Whoever speaks the true name into the stone belongs to the stone forever. They cannot follow. They cannot pass. The door out of Vessany is also the door that stays shut behind you.*

Serane did not know that. The king did not know that. They thought the door was a road. It was not a road. It was a mouth.

"Your majesty may come down, then," Paloma said.

Serane's grey eyes flickered—the first genuine thing in them all night. "You agree?"

"I agree to open it."

Boots retreated up the stairs. Voices. Then slower footsteps, many, and a smell that reached the antechamber before the man did—oils and incense and the sour sweetness of a body kept alive past its natural term by physicians' arts. King Aurel of Vessany-that-was-not descended his ancestors' stair like a man returning to a house he'd burned for the insurance money.

He was not what Paloma expected. She had imagined a tyrant out of ballads, all black armor and cruelty. Instead she saw a tired, elegant ruin of a man in travelling clothes, silver at his temples, a scholar's stoop, kind eyes gone loose and hungry. He looked at the door the way starving men look at bread.

"There it is," he whispered. "There it is. A century my family has paid cartographers to erase you, paid priests to unname you, paid *inquisitors*—" a glance at Serane, cold as payment itself "—and here you were the whole time, under our feet, patient as debt." He turned to Paloma and, horribly, smiled at her with real warmth. "You must be Maren's girl. The valley key. Do you know, I have dreamed of this door since I was a boy? Every heir does. We're told a story—that a wicked ancestor sealed away treasure. We grow up and learn the truth. He sealed away an *exit*."

"The truth is slightly worse than either story," Paloma said.

"Truth usually is." He waved a ringed hand, generous as spring. "Speak the name. Take whatever reward you like. Gold, land, my gratitude—which is worth more than gold, despite appearances. Open the door, child. My kingdom is dying of me. Let me at least die somewhere else."

And Paloma looked at him—this tired, hollow, fleeing king—and understood, in one clean stroke, everything Maren had tried to teach her in a dead language of herbs and silence.

*Power is not the ability to take what you want. Power is knowing what you are willing to lose, and losing it on purpose.*

She thought of the valley. Not the magic-dead cage of it, not the sentence she had discovered written into her own bloodline—but the *garden*. Maren's rows of feverfew and valerian. Tamsin's crooked grin. The neighbor children she'd taught to whistle. Aldric's folded cloak. All of it built on ground that had been poisoned into peace so that she could be kept like a key in a drawer—and if the door opened tonight and the crown poured through it and vanished, the valley would remain what it had always been: a prison that happened to have flowers in it.

But if the door *closed*—truly closed, sealed by a keeper's name given freely—

The grimoire had been specific. She had read it four times in the dark of the chapel while Aldric slept against her shoulder. *Sealed by a freely-given keeper's name, the door does not merely shut. It unmakes what was built to guard it. The warding dies. The valley becomes ordinary land—giftless, yes, but free. Nothing caged, because nothing remains to cage.*

The magic-dead valley would dissolve. The spell that had flattened every gift within its borders—including hers, including the gifts of every child born there for a hundred years—would end, and end *clean*, leaving only soil and sky and people who would never know they had been prisoners, and would now simply be neighbors.

Everyone she loved would wake tomorrow in a plain, ordinary place where magic didn't reach—and wouldn't need to, because the door would be shut by a keeper who chose it, and a keeper who chose it could not be used as a key again.

The price was herself. Her name. Her escape. Thirteen turns of walking toward freedom, and freedom standing right here, wide as the world beyond the world—and she would give it away, deliberately, the way you give away the last of the bread to someone hungrier.

*Choosing your bonds is the only freedom there is,* Maren had written on the last page, in letters that shook. *I am sorry I never taught you that in words. I tried to teach you in a garden.*

"Aurel," Paloma said.

The king blinked. "Child?"

"Your great-grandfather gave this door a sacrifice. Fire and blood and a nation's name, torn out of every map." She drew a breath that went all the way down to the bedrock of her. "I'm going to give it something better. I'm going to give it a *choice*."

Serane moved first—of course she did, she had always been faster than everyone's grief. "Stop her! Stop—"

Paloma spoke the name.

Not aloud—not entirely. Some of it was sound, the syllables Maren had spelled out phonetically in the margins, the true name of Vessany-who-was-Vessany, the door's own deep word. But the rest of it was not sound at all. The rest of it was *her*: twenty-three years of feverless summers, of hiding candleflames behind her eyes, of Maren's hands in the herb rows, of a wolf-captain saying *your gift is not a sickness* in a chapel full of smoke. She gave the door the valley and the garden and the grimoire and the fear and the thirteen counted moons, and she gave it the one thing she had never given anyone:

*I am Paloma Marchetti,* she said into the stone, *and I am yours, and I stay.*

The door opened.

Light came out of it—not gold, not white, but the color of the moment just before dawn when the world is all promise and no detail—and it filled the antechamber like water filling a cup. The king cried out and lunged toward it, arms wide, robes streaming, a boy again for exactly one heartbeat.

The light touched him. And stopped.

Because a door that has been given a keeper is no longer a door. It is a threshold with an owner, and the owner decides.

"No," Paloma said, and the word went through the light and through the stone and through the king's oldest dream. "This way is shut. Sealed by name. Seal witnessed by blood and by choice. Go home, Aurel. Rule the country you have. That's the work. That was always the work."

He screamed. She would remember that—the screaming, and the way it wasn't rage but grief, pure and childish, a boy's tantrum at a locked garden gate. Serane dragged him back from the light with both hands, shouting orders that dissolved into chaos, soldiers surging and falling back from radiance that did not burn but *refused*.

Above, faint through the rock, someone began cheering. She would learn later it was Tamsin, arriving at the head of the stair with half the smugglers of the old march and a stolen crown wagon, having decided at last that some prices were worth paying forward.

The light began to close.

And Paloma understood, in the last moment, that the sealing was taking its payment *now*—that the name was leaving her the way color leaves a sunset, gently and completely. She felt Marchetti go first, dissolving off her like a coat handed back to a dead woman. Then *apprentice*. Then *key*. Then *witch*, then *quarry*, then *girl*. The door drank them all down with something like tenderness, and what remained standing in the radiance was no one at all—a person-shaped space where a life used to be, ready to be filled with whatever came next.

It hurt less than she expected. It felt, in fact, like setting down something she had carried so long she'd forgotten it was heavy.

The last thing the door showed her, in its closing light, was not Vessany. It was a garden—her garden, Maren's garden, growing wild and ordinary under a plain grey sky, no wards, no walls, no spell in any inch of it, just green things choosing to grow.

Then the seam in the rock became simply rock, seamless, ancient, dumb as any other wall in the world. The antechamber went dark except for Serane's fallen lantern, guttering.

And the valley, forty feet above and a hundred miles of habit away, quietly forgot it had ever been a cage.

Paloma sank to her knees on the cold stone, empty-handed, empty-named, lighter than she had ever been in her life—and terrified, and laughing, or perhaps weeping, or perhaps all three at once.

The dark at the top of the stair filled with limping footsteps.

Heavy ones. Dragging ones. Wolf-slow, wolf-stubborn, unmistakably *his*.

"*Paloma.*"

The voice cracked halfway through her name—the name that was already ash, already given, already no longer hers—and she turned toward the sound of it with her hands out in the lantern-light, reaching for whatever she was now.

"*Paloma!*"
