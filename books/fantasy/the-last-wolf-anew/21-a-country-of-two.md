## 21. A Country of Two

**Paloma**

The valley did not know us.

That was the first thing I understood as we came down the old switchback road in the last gold of an autumn afternoon, Aldric's hand warm around mine, the mountains rising on either side like sleeping animals. The valley did not know us, and it did not need to. Whatever had watched from its stones for a hundred years—the patient, buried attention of a cage built around a key—was gone. I had felt it go. I had *made* it go, spending my name like a coin I could never mint again, and what remained was only land: honest dirt, dumb rock, air that carried nothing but the smell of woodsmoke and late apples.

I had expected to feel hollowed. Instead I felt—light. Terribly, dangerously light, the way a bird must feel the first morning after the net is cut away.

"Stop," Aldric said.

We stopped. The road bent and there it was: Maren's cottage, smaller than memory had kept it, its thatch patched with moss, its garden a wilderness of gone-to-seed herbs and the pale skeletons of last summer's beans. The door hung crooked on one hinge. A shutter had come loose and knocked against the wall in the wind, patiently, all the months we'd been gone, saying *no one home, no one home.*

But the well was still there. And the rosemary had gone mad without anyone to prune it, spilling over its bed like green water, filling the whole hollow with its scent.

Aldric squeezed my hand once and let go, because he understood—I saw it in his winter eyes—that this was something I had to walk into alone.

The cottage smelled of dust and time. My cot still stood by the window. Maren's chair sat by the cold hearth, exactly where she had died in it, and on the table lay a clay cup with a ring of dried tea at the bottom, the last cup she had ever drunk from, and no one had washed it, because no one had known to.

I picked it up and held it against my chest and finally, finally cried—not the ragged grief of the road, which had been grief and fear tangled together, but something quieter and worse and better. Grief with nowhere left to hide. She had guarded me my whole life and I had never once thanked her, and now the only way to thank her was to live in her house and pull her weeds and become, slowly, someone she might have been proud of.

When I came out, wiping my face with my sleeve like a child, Aldric had already righted the gate.

"Well," he said. "Someone has to teach you to mend a hinge properly."

"You're staying, then."

He looked at me for a long moment. He was not a handsome man in the way ballads mean it—he was weathered and scarred and there was a new one along his jaw from Serane's collapse, still pink—but when he looked at me he looked with his whole self, nothing held back, nothing behind iron. That was the thing about Aldric Vane: everyone else got the closed face. I got the open one.

"I resigned my commission in the ashes of the capital," he said. "I told them I was done hunting. They asked what I would do instead." His mouth twitched. "I said I was going to grow roses."

"Rosemary."

"I said what I said."

I laughed until my ribs hurt, and then I kissed him in Maren's ruined garden, among the runaway herbs, under a sky that owed us nothing and gave us the evening anyway.

* * *

**Aldric**

The valley made me uneasy for the first month, though I never told her so.

I had spent eleven years being paid to find people, and every instinct I owned insisted that a place which hid a bloodline for a century did not simply stop hiding it. I woke at night listening. I walked the perimeter the way I'd walked checkpoints, counting exits, marking sightlines. On the third week I caught myself setting a watch rotation for a village of forty people who had never asked for one, and I stood in the middle of the empty lane at dawn feeling like a man arguing with a ghost that had already been laid to rest.

There were no ghosts. That was the whole point. Paloma had unmade the cage, and the cage had been the only magic here, and now this was just a valley where people grew barley and lied about the price of wool.

It took the wolf in me longer to believe it. He paced. He wanted a border to run, a threat to answer. So I gave him work instead: I let him run the high ridges at moonrise, free of the iron for good—free of everything—and he ran until his lungs burned, and came back each night a little more settled, a little more mine, until running stopped being escape and became simply joy.

And I rebuilt things. It turned out I was good with my hands in ways I'd never been allowed to be. I mended the hinge. I rehung the shutter. I cut the rosemary back and learned, from Paloma's scolding, that you're supposed to leave some woody growth. By midwinter I had repaired three roofs in the village and nobody called me Captain anymore; they called me Aldric, or Vane, or—when I fixed the miller's gate after two years of it sagging—"that wolf-blooded fellow," said fondly, because word travels strangely in small places, and somehow the truth of what I was had arrived before any lie could, and no one cared. One old woman pressed a heel of bread into my hands and said her mother had been a hedge-witch too, back before it mattered.

*Back before it mattered.* I carried that phrase around for days like a warm stone in my pocket.

Paloma noticed everything, of course. She was in the garden one thawing afternoon, tying up pea shoots, when she said, without looking up:

"You've stopped counting the doors."

I thought about it. "Have I?"

"When we met, you counted every exit in every room. You do it less." She brushed dirt from her knee and smiled at me sideways, and the sun caught the grey coming in at her temple, which I loved, because it meant she was aging somewhere safe. "You're building something, Aldric. Not guarding. Building."

"It feels the same," I admitted. "In the hands."

"It isn't." She stood, took my calloused palm, and pressed it flat against the soil. "Feel. Guarding holds things in or keeps them out. Building holds things *up.*"

Under my hand, through the cold crust of March earth, something small and green was deciding to rise anyway. I thought: *this is what she did to me. This is what she does.*

* * *

**Paloma**

The child came to us in April, the way trouble always comes to people who have made a habit of mercy.

Her name was Wren—her mother's choice, her father long gone to the lowland mills—and she was eight years old and furious about it. She arrived at our gate at dusk with a dead chicken under one arm and her chin set at the angle of someone awaiting execution, and she announced, in the ringing voice children use when they have decided honesty is their last weapon:

"I killed it with thinking. I didn't mean to. Are you going to tell?"

I looked at the chicken. I looked at the child, whose eyes were too bright and whose fists were white-knuckled, and I saw myself at eight years old in Maren's kitchen, having just made the teapot boil without touching it, waiting to be beaten or blessed.

"No," I said. "I'm going to teach you."

Wren blinked. "Teach me what?"

"How to hold it gently. Come inside. We'll bury the hen properly first—it deserves that much."

Aldric cooked while I dug the little grave by the fence, and Wren stood over it sniffling and pretending not to, and afterward we ate stew at Maren's table, and the child talked with her mouth full about how it had started with sparks off her fingers when she was angry, and then the candle thing, and then the hen. Her gift was raw and hot and entirely undirected—a bonfire in a paper house.

"She needs grounding," I told Aldric that night, in bed, in the dark. "Not suppression. Suppression is what they tried with you, and look how that ended."

"Iron charms and self-loathing," he said. "A classic education."

"Maren taught me differently. She never once told me *don't.* She told me *slow down.* There's a difference." I traced the scar on his shoulder, the old one, from a hunt years before me. "Will you help? You know what it costs to hide. You know the shape of the damage."

He was quiet a long time. When he spoke, his voice had gravel in it. "I know the shape of the damage," he agreed. "That's why I'll help. Nobody should learn to be afraid of themselves before they learn anything else."

So we began. Three evenings a week, Wren came after chores, and we sat at the hearth, and I put a single candle between us.

"Not lighting it," I said, the first evening. "Anyone can light it. That's easy and useless. We're going to light it *slowly.* Over ten breaths. And then we're going to put it out slowly. And then we're going to do nothing to it at all, and you're going to sit there and let it burn, and not touch it. That last one is the hardest lesson in the world."

"It sounds boring," Wren said.

"It is boring. Magic is mostly boring, if you're doing it right. All the exciting magic belongs to people who die young or ruin villages."

She stuck out her tongue at me, and I liked her enormously.

Aldric's part was stranger. He would sit across the room with the firelight on his face, and when Wren's gift surged—and it surged often, at frustration, at laughter, at the unfairness of bedtime—he would say, calmly, "What does it feel like? Tell me true." And she would groan and squirm and eventually say things like *like bees* or *like when the river takes the ice out* or, once, devastatingly, *like when Papa left and I couldn't stop it,* and the room would go very quiet, and Aldric would nod and say, "Yes. Mine used to feel like that too. Like something happening to me instead of something I was doing." And then he would show her, deliberately, unhurried, the amber flash of his eyes, the way his hearing sharpened till he could name the moth at the window—and she would watch him hold it, easily, without fear, the way you'd hold a bird you'd tamed rather than caged.

Children believe what they see demonstrated far faster than what they're told. Within a month she could bank her own fires. Within two, she lit the candle over ten breaths, blew it out over ten more, and then—triumphant, wriggling—did absolutely nothing to it at all.

"That," I said, "is the hardest lesson in the world."

"I did it," she whispered, staring at the steady flame like it was a miracle, which, in a way, it was.

"You did." I caught Aldric's eye across the hearth. He was trying very hard not to look moved and failing completely. "Now do it again tomorrow. Magic is mostly repetition. The rest is love, and we'll get to that later."

* * *

**Aldric**

Summer came, and with it the question I had been circling since the snowmelt, the way a wolf circles a den before daring to enter.

I am not a man of many words. I have written reports that ran forty pages and letters to Paloma that took a week and three burned drafts. But standing in the rebuilt garden on Midsummer's Eve, with the rosemary in riot again and the beans climbing the frames I'd built and the whole valley smelling of sun-warm sage, I found that the words were simple after all. They had been simple the entire time. Everything hard in my life had been the hiding; the truth came out plain as water.

She was on her knees in the herb beds, dirt to the elbows, arguing with a row of thyme that refused to respect the line she'd drawn for it. I knelt beside her. Took her hand—soil and all.

"Marry me," I said. "Not because of the door, or the hunt, or anything we survived. Because of this. Because of Tuesdays. Because I want every ordinary day you'll give me."

The thyme went on misbehaving. Somewhere down in the village a dog barked twice and gave up. Paloma looked at our joined hands, filthy and interlocked, and then at me, and her eyes were shining and exasperated in equal measure.

"You absolute romantic," she said. "You proposed in a weed patch."

"The weed patch seemed important to you."

"And you want to spend your life with a woman who talks to plants and buries chickens and teaches children to do nothing beautifully."

"Yes," I said. "That is precisely and entirely what I want."

She kissed me then, tasting of earth and salt, and when she pulled back she was laughing and crying at once, which I have since learned is simply how she experiences joy.

"Yes," she said. "Obviously yes. But I'm keeping my name in the ceremony, whatever name turns out to be mine. I spent a year earning it."

"It's the only reason I asked," I told her. "I don't want a woman given to me. I want one who chose."

* * *

**Paloma**

We married at harvest, in Maren's garden, under an arch Aldric built of apple branches because he claimed he couldn't draw a straight line but could certainly bend one. The whole village came. Tamsin sent a parcel from who-knows-where containing two silver cups of obviously dubious provenance and a note that read, in her slanted smuggler's hand: *Paid my debts. Owed you both. Don't ask where these were.* Wren carried the flowers and glowed so brightly—with pride, not magic, though it was hard to tell the difference sometimes—that half the guests remarked on it.

I wore my hair braided with rosemary. I said vows I wrote myself and rewrote four times and finally abandoned in favor of speaking plainly, which is the whole lesson of my life, apparently, learned over and over at greater cost each time: *say the true thing.*

"I was named for other people's purposes," I told him, in front of everyone, holding both his hands. "Daughter, apprentice, quarry, key. Today I name myself, and what I choose is this: you, and this ground, and every unremarkable morning of it."

"Aldric," he said, when his turn came, and his voice cracked on the single syllable, and the whole village pretended not to notice, and I loved him past all sense or measure.

That night we lay in the loft with the window open to the smell of cut hay, and I listened to his heartbeat slow toward sleep, and I thought about names. I had given mine to a door and built another from scratch, and neither of them was the one I'd been born with, and all of them were true. Identity, it turned out, was not a inheritance you defended or a debt you paid. It was a garden. You decided what to plant. You weeded it yourself. It grew toward whatever light you gave it.

Below us, in the dark, Wren slept over in the spare room after helping with the feast, dreaming whatever eight-year-olds dream, her small fierce gift banked safely in her chest like a coal that would wait a lifetime if it had to, or blaze tomorrow if she chose.

The valley slept too—ordinary, unmagical, free. No cage. No key. Nothing to guard.

Only a country of two, and room enough in it, we had discovered, for whoever needed shelter next.
