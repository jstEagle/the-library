## 22. The Last Wolf Anew

The garden had grown wild in the way that gardens do when they are loved but not commanded. Paloma walked its rows at dusk, barefoot, the way Maren used to, letting the chamomile brush her ankles and the rosemary release its sharp green breath into the cooling air. Nine years since she had come down from Vessany with a wolf at her side and no name worth speaking. Nine years of dirt under her nails and children's laughter drifting up from the village below.

The valley did not remember her. That had been the point of everything—the sealing, the sacrifice, the long slow unmaking of the cage. The valley was free now, ordinary land like any other land, and ordinary land keeps no ledger of what it cost. The villagers who had bought her honey and mended her roof knew her only as the woman who kept the herb garden on the hill, the one who taught letters to children who could afford no tutor, the one whose friend came every month from the capital with salt, news, and an appetite that emptied any larder he was left alone with.

Aldric would arrive tomorrow. He always arrived before the anniversary night, though neither of them ever said why. They did not need to say it. Some things lived better unspoken, like seeds in winter soil.

She knelt by the sage bed and began to pinch back the flowering tops, working by feel as the light failed. Her gift—what remained of it—was small now, domestic, a candle flame banked low. She could still coax a stubborn seedling toward the sun. Still find, sometimes, the lost thing a neighbor wept over—a ring, a goat, a child's wooden bird—by closing her eyes and listening with something deeper than ears. But the vast tide of power that had once roared through her, enough to seal a door older than kingdoms, had gone out of her the night she gave her name to the stone. What she had left was what Maren had always told her she'd had all along: not a weapon, not a wonder. A way of paying attention.

*That is all magic ever is,* Maren had written, in the last pages of the grimoire. *The world is already singing. Magic is only the courage to sing back.*

Paloma sang back quietly these days. It was enough. It was, she had learned, more than enough.

She was reaching for the last sprig of sage when the light changed.

Not dimmed—*changed*, the way air changes before lightning, the way water goes glass-still before a storm. Every hair on her arms lifted. Down in the village, a dog began to howl, then another, and then, absurdly, the baker's cat shrieked somewhere near the well. The birds went silent all at once, the whole valley holding its breath.

Paloma stood slowly, sage forgotten, and looked north.

Far away, beyond the hills where Vessany's ruins still slept under their blanket of moss and forgetting, something had opened one eye.

*The thirteenth turn.* She counted without meaning to. Nine years, and the moon overhead was fat and rising, and she had lost track—she had *let* herself lose track, that was the truth of it—but the moon had not lost track of anything. Once a century, Tamsin had said, laughing around her stolen pipe. Once a century the door remembers itself. And tonight, apparently, it remembered *her*.

She felt it the way you feel someone say your name across a crowded market—not heard so much as turned toward. Something ancient and patient had spoken into the dark between the stars, and the sound it made was shaped exactly like the shape of her, like a key cut to fit a lock that walked and breathed and grew sage.

*Come,* said the door, without words, without sound. *The passage stands open. One last time.*

Paloma stood in her garden for a long moment, heart hammering like a girl's again, and then she did the sensible thing. She went inside, put on her boots, filled a flask with well water, took down her cloak from its peg—and stopped, hand on the wool, when she saw the second peg beside it. The longer cloak. The one sized for shoulders broader than hers.

He wasn't here yet. He'd never forgive her if she went without him. He'd never forgive himself.

But the door had said *one last time*, and doors like this did not wait on wolves walking mountain roads.

She took both cloaks anyway.

* * *

She rode through the night and half the next day, taking the old smugglers' paths that Tamsin had mapped for her a lifetime ago—Tamsin, who had traded her own memories for redemption and lived out her days in the capital telling stories about a past she couldn't remember, happy in a way none of them had expected. Tamsin was three years dead now. Paloma still caught herself wanting to tell her things.

The land grew wronger as she climbed, the way it always did at the borders of the erased country. Roads that looped politely back to themselves. Trees that leaned to watch her pass. A stream that flowed uphill for the length of a song and then thought better of it. The uncanny had faded from Vessany in the nine years since the door was sealed—the ghosts had gone quiet, the replayed night of the king's great sin finally allowed to end—but the bones of the strangeness remained, the way a broken arm remains crooked even after it heals.

By late afternoon she stood on the ridge above the ruined capital, and there, at the edge of her sight, she saw him.

A wolf, gray and enormous, loping along the far ridgeline with the easy devouring stride of a creature built for distance. Even at this range she knew him. You spent thirteen turns of the moon falling in love with someone, you learned the way they moved the way you learned your own heartbeat.

*Aldric.*

He had seen her too; the wolf paused, head lifting, and then he was gone behind the rocks, and a minute later a man came down the trail at a jog, pulling his shirt on as he came, gray-eyed and gray-templed and scowling the scowl she loved best.

"You went without me." No greeting. There never needed to be one.

"The door doesn't wait on wolves."

"The door," Aldric said, stopping in front of her, breathing hard, "doesn't get to have you." His voice was rough. His eyes were doing the thing they did when he was frightened and refusing to call it fear. "I felt it wake at moonrise yesterday. I broke every record I've ever set getting here, and you—" He gestured at her, at the dust on her cloak, at the whole insolent fact of her. "You were already *here*. Of course you were."

"Someone had to make sure it wasn't a trick."

"And is it?"

"I don't know," Paloma admitted. "It's asking. That's new. Last time it demanded. This time—" She searched for the word. "*This time it sounds lonely.*"

Something moved behind his winter eyes. He looked north, toward the ruin where the antechamber waited beneath the fallen city, where they had once held a stairway against an army and lost everything except each other.

"Then let's go answer it," he said. "Together. And this time, nobody gives anybody anything. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Paloma said, and found that she meant it.

They walked down into the erased country side by side, the wolf and the witch, one last time.

* * *

The antechamber was smaller than memory had made it.

That was the first thing she noticed. Nine years ago, descending the stairway with Serane's soldiers dying on the steps above her, the space beneath the ruins had seemed vast as a cathedral, vast as the sky. Now it was just a room: round, low-ceilinged, its walls of seamless black stone, its floor worn smooth by feet that had been dust for a century before the kingdom fell. The door itself dominated the far wall—a slab of darker darkness, taller than two men, carved with characters no living scholar could read.

Except that now, faintly, the characters were lit.

They glowed like embers under ash, pulsing slow, and with each pulse Paloma felt that turning-toward again, that calling of her name. The air tasted of ozone and old grief.

"It's weaker," Aldric murmured. He had shifted back to man-shape at the stairway's foot and now stood close at her shoulder, close enough that she could hear the wolf pacing inside his chest. "Or tired."

"Doors get tired," Paloma said softly. "So do keepers."

She crossed the smooth floor alone. Some distances had to be walked by one person.

Up close, the door was warm. Not sun-warm—*body*-warm, blood-warm, the warmth of something alive that had been waiting a very long time for someone to touch it. The glowing characters swam before her eyes, and for a moment—one strange, suspended moment—they resolved into legibility, the way a face resolves out of a crowd.

And she understood what it was offering.

Not escape, not anymore. The king was long dead, his crown bankrupt and passed to heirs who had never known what their hunt was truly for. The country beyond the door—the country Vessany had been severed from a century ago—was just a country now, with roads and taxes and weather, nothing anyone needed to flee to or from. The door had no purpose left. It was a wound in the world that had healed over wrong, and once a century it woke and remembered the pain.

What it offered her was simple, and terrible, and gentle: *passage*. Walk through, and be free of this land forever. Be free of the grave you dug with your own name. Somewhere on the other side, there was a life that owed nothing to the valley or the door or the dead. All she had to do was speak the name she'd given away—speak it, take it back, unmake her choice of nine years ago.

The door could give it back. That was the loneliness underneath the offering: *take back what you paid. Undo it. Let me be a door instead of a debt.*

Paloma laid her palm flat against the stone.

Warmth flooded up her arm, and with it, images—not memories, offers. Green country beyond imagining. Rivers with names she'd never learned. A version of herself who had walked through at twenty-two with a grimoire under her arm and a wolf howling on the stairs above, a Paloma who had never sealed anything, never sacrificed anything, never learned what it cost to choose your bonds over your freedom. That Paloma beckoned from the other side of the dark, smiling, unburdened, a stranger wearing her face.

*Come,* said the door. *Be her. Or simply go. Either way, you need not stay.*

Behind her, Aldric had gone very still. She could feel him not moving the way you can feel a held breath across a room. He had promised: nobody gives anybody anything. He would not stop her. He would not follow. He would stand on this side of the stone for the rest of his life if she asked it, and that was precisely why she could ask it, and precisely why she wouldn't.

"Thank you," Paloma said aloud, to the door, and her voice didn't shake. "But I'm not her."

The glow flickered, questioning.

"She sounds lovely," Paloma went on, and found she was almost laughing. "That woman on the other side. Free and light and owing nothing. But I know something she doesn't." She pressed her palm harder against the warm stone, the way you'd press your hand to the forehead of a fevered child. "I know what it's for. Freedom isn't a door out. I looked for it in a door for thirteen turns of the moon, and what I found on this side of the choosing was a garden. A man who breaks records to reach me. Children learning their letters. Sage that needs pinching back. You don't get that by leaving. You get it by staying, and staying *on purpose*, which is the only kind of staying that counts."

The door pulsed, slower now. Listening.

"So I'm going to do what a keeper does," she said. "Not because I'm bound. Because I *choose* it. Again. Every year, every morning, every ordinary day—that's the vow, and it's the only one worth making."

She drew a breath, and spoke her name.

Not Marchetti. Not the name Maren had guarded, nor the name the grimoire had carried, nor the name the door had eaten nine years ago. The name she had built, syllable by syllable, out of garden soil and candlelight and a wolf's gray devotion. Her true name, self-made, the one no edict had ever hunted and no crown could ever spend:

"*Paloma of the Valley. Keeper by choice.*"

The characters flared—white, then gold, then a soft and settling ember-glow—and the door exhaled. She felt it pass through her like wind through wheat, all that century of waiting, all that loneliness, acknowledged at last and released. The warmth under her palm cooled to the temperature of honest stone. The glow dimmed, steadied, and became something she had never seen it be before:

Quiet. Not dead. Just—at rest. A door that no longer needed to open, because it had finally been answered.

"There," Paloma whispered. "You're not a debt anymore either."

She stepped back. Aldric crossed the chamber in four strides and pulled her against his chest, and she felt the tremor running through him that he would deny until his dying day.

"You're staying," he said into her hair. It wasn't a question. It was a man confirming a miracle.

"I'm staying," she said. "Walk me home?"

* * *

They came down off the mountain two days later, dusty and road-hungry, and the valley opened before them gold and green and utterly indifferent to everything that had ever been sacrificed upon it—which was, Paloma thought, exactly as it should be. Land that needed no guarding. A country of two, plus a village, plus a garden going gloriously to seed in her absence.

At the bottom of the hill path, a girl was waiting.

She couldn't have been more than ten, one of Paloma's letter-children, the miller's daughter with the quick hands and quicker mind—and she was standing very straight in the middle of the path, eyes wide, holding out her cupped palms like an offering.

In her hands, a candle flame burned steady in the open wind.

"I didn't mean to," the girl said, chin trembling but voice fierce. "I was only thinking at it. And it *listened*, and I didn't know who else—I thought maybe you—"

Paloma knelt in the dust, road-weary, nine years a keeper, and smiled.

"Yes," she said. "I think maybe me. Come and sit with me, little spark. Let me tell you a story about a valley, and a door, and a woman who learned that the bravest magic of all—" She glanced up at Aldric, standing sentinel behind her, gray-eyed and home. "—is knowing which side of the door you belong on."

Above them, the evening star rose over the hills, and the valley, remembering nothing, held them all.
