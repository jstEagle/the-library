## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

There are three things you must never do in Vael Hollow, my grandmother used to say, and she said it the way other people say *remember to buy bread*: name the sword-forms aloud, cross the border stones after dark, and look up.

I had already broken two of them by moonrise. The third was about to break itself.

The Quiet Festival begins tomorrow, which in Vael Hollow means everyone sweeps their doorsteps twice, argues amiably about whose turn it is to bake the silence-bread — a loaf so dense and flavorless that eating it is meant to be a form of prayer — and then sits very still for one entire evening, listening to nothing, on purpose. It is, I am told, an honor. It is also the single most boring night of the year, which is why every child in the valley dreads it and every adult pretends they don't.

For me it has always been something else. My grandmother died on the eve of the Quiet Festival eleven years ago, and since then the night before the festival has been mine alone: the one night of the year when I take her practice blade down from above the hearth and work through the forms in the dark of our yard, where no one can see and no one can ask me what, exactly, I think I'm doing.

Because here is the thing about the sword-forms: I was never taught them. Not formally, not with words. Grandmother never once stood behind me and corrected my grip. But when I was seven years old I watched her go through them exactly once, at night, thinking I was asleep, and my body apparently decided on the spot that it would rather remember this than how to be a normal person. They live in my hands the way other people's names live in their mouths. I have never had to learn them. I only ever had to stop hiding them.

And I am forbidden to name them aloud. Which has never struck me as strange until recently, because who names a thing like *the fourth form*, the one where the blade falls like water finding a crack? You'd sound ridiculous. Except that sometimes, mid-swing, I feel a word sitting right behind my teeth — not a word in the valley tongue, something older, rounder, with weight to it, the kind of word that would land somewhere if I let it go — and I bite down on it, because Grandmother's voice is still very clear in my head after eleven years, and it says: *never aloud, Mara. Never.*

So tonight I did what I always do. I waited for the last lamp in the village to go out, took the blade from its pegs — it hums faintly against my palm, a hum I've spent eleven years pretending not to notice — and stepped into the yard.

The first thing wrong with the night was the quiet.

I know. Quiet Festival. But there are kinds of quiet the way there are kinds of bread, and the valley's ordinary quiet is a warm loaf, full of small sounds: the millstream worrying at its stones, old Hedda's dog complaining at the moon, the creak of the willow by our gate. Tonight all of that was gone. Not muffled — *gone*, as though someone had reached down and lifted the sound out of the world like a hand lifting a coin off a table. The stream still ran; I could see it silvering past in the starlight. It just didn't make any noise.

The world was holding its breath. I want to be precise about that, because it wasn't stillness, and it wasn't calm. It was the particular quality of air in the half-second before a blade comes down — that stretched, attentive nothing. Every animal in the valley knows that half-second. Tonight the whole valley was living inside it.

I told myself it was festival nerves. I told myself that the way you tell yourself things you don't believe while your hands are doing something wiser than your mouth.

I began the forms.

First form: the greeting, blade rising slow as smoke. Second: the turning-away. Third, the listening cut. By the fourth — the water-cut, the one with the word hiding under it — sweat was running down my spine despite the cold, and I noticed the second thing wrong with the night, which was the stars.

Or rather, the absence of some of them.

I'll admit it took me a while, because I don't look up much — see above, re: grandmother's three rules — but when I paused to breathe between forms, something nagged at the edge of my vision, and finally I gave in and raised my eyes.

The Shepherd was gone.

Not hidden behind cloud. There wasn't a cloud in the sky; the rest of the heavens were strewn with stars like salt on black wool. But the crook-shaped constellation that has hung over the valley every night of my life, the one Grandmother pointed out to me when I was small — *that's the Shepherd, he watches the flocks, he watches us* — simply was not there. A patch of sky, starless, smooth, like a hole cut in cloth with the edges still crisp.

I stood very still. The blade hummed against my palm, louder than usual.

*Wind,* I told myself. *High haze. Some trick of the eye.* And then, while I was busy telling myself that, the Weaver went out too — six stars, one breath, gone, leaving a second clean-edged wound in the sky.

I have practiced sword-forms in thunderstorms. I have held this blade while a bull elk crashed through our fence close enough to touch. I have never once dropped it. I dropped it now. It fell into the grass without a sound, which was somehow worse than a clatter — even the ground declined to comment — and I stood there watching the sky erase itself, constellation by constellation, north to south, like a hand wiping a slate clean. The Crone. The Twins. Each one vanishing not like lights going out but like lights being *unwritten*, the darkness where they'd been too complete, too tidy, to be anything natural.

By the time the last of them guttered — and I mean guttered, the way a candle does, the whole sky flickering once like a flame deciding whether to bother — I was counting under my breath, an old habit, the way you count when you're frightened and won't admit it. And on the count of nine, the candle in our window went blue.

Grandmother's death-day candle. Every year on this night I set it in the window and light it and let it burn down to nothing, and every year it burns yellow and steady and stupidly ordinary, and every year I pretend that's a comfort instead of an anticlimax. Tonight it burned blue. Not the blue of a salt-flame or a spirit-lamp. Blue like deep ice. Blue like the color the blade-hum would be if sounds had colors. It threw my shadow up the wall of the house — long, wrong-angled, holding a sword I was no longer holding.

I picked up the practice blade. My hands were shaking. I made them stop, mostly, because whatever else was happening, I was not going to meet it empty-handed.

"Mara?"

Tamsin. Of course it was Tamsin, standing at my gate in her festival shawl, because Tamsin has never in her life observed a boundary more complicated than a fence, and because she is the kind of friend who notices when you've stopped coming to choir practice and shows up at your door with bread and opinions. She was looking up, too. Her face in the blue candlelight was the color of milkweed.

"You're seeing it too," I said. Stupid thing to say. Her face said it for me.

"The stars," she whispered. "Mara, the stars are — where are they *going?*"

"I don't know."

"Should we wake Elder Brannoc?"

"And tell him what? That the sky is misbehaving?" I heard my own voice come out flat and hard and didn't much care for it. "He'll say it's weather. He'll say it's always been like this, if we'd only paid attention. You know how he gets."

Tamsin looked at me strangely then, and I realized I'd said something odd without meaning to — *it's always been like this* — as if I knew what the sky was supposed to do, as if I were defending it, apologizing for it. As if some part of me already suspected that the valley and the sky had an arrangement, and that the arrangement had just come apart.

"Come inside," she said. "You shouldn't be out here. It's — Mara, it feels like the whole world is waiting for something."

It does, doesn't it, I thought. That's exactly what it feels like. Like the half-second before the cut. Like the whole valley is a held breath, and somewhere out past the border stones, something is about to exhale.

"I'll be fine," I said, which is what you say to the people you love when the truth would frighten them more than the lie. She hugged me — she could feel the tension in me, I know she could, and she didn't mention it, which is its own kind of love — and went home through the starless streets, and I watched her until the dark took her, and then I went inside and sat by the blue candle with the blade across my knees.

Here is what I did not tell Tamsin. Here is what I am writing down now, in the hour before dawn, because if the world ends tomorrow I would like at least one honest document to survive it:

When the last constellation went out, the blade in my hands spoke.

Not hummed. Not buzzed. *Spoke.* One syllable, low, felt in the bones of my wrist more than heard — the same shape as the word that lives behind my teeth during the fourth form, or near enough that my skin crawled with the kinship of it. And for one moment — one — the yard around me was not my yard. The fences were taller. The hills wore walls I have never seen. There were banners on the ridge, colors I don't have names for, and the air smelled of woodsmoke and snow and something vast and patient breathing far away, something that felt, absurdly, like being *watched over*. Then it was gone, and it was just my yard, and the blue candle, and the empty sky.

Grandmother's three rules. Name nothing aloud. Cross nothing after dark. Look up at nothing.

I always thought they were rules for keeping a girl safe.

Standing in my kitchen with a humming blade and a blue flame and a sky wiped clean, it occurs to me, for the first time in my life, that they might have been rules for keeping something else safe. Something bigger than me. Something with borders.

Tomorrow is the Quiet Festival. Tomorrow the whole valley will sit still and listen to nothing, and Elder Brannoc will say the words about peace and continuity and the blessed sameness of our days, and no one will look up, because there is nothing up there anymore to look at.

At first light, I'm walking to the border stones.

I need to know if they're still warm.
