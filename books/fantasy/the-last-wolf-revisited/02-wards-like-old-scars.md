## 2. Wards Like Old Scars

I did not sleep. I want that on the record, in case anyone later asks whether the last swordspeaker of Vael Hollow panicked on the night the stars went out. I did not panic. I lay awake in my grandmother's bed with the blue-guttered candle burned down to a coin of wax and my practice sword propped against the wall like a guest who'd overstayed, and I stared at the ceiling beam and composed a list of perfectly rational explanations.

Cloud cover. Valley fog — we were famous for it; half the village made their living off the mist apples that only ripened in it. A trick of the eyes after too many hours of sword-forms in the dark. My grandmother's candle had always guttered strange colors near the end; Tamsin once swore she saw hers burn green the night her mother's cow died, and nobody built a philosophy out of that.

The list was very thorough. It was also, I noticed around the fourth hour, arranged in order of how badly I wanted each explanation to be true, which is not a method any scholar would endorse.

By the time the light came up gray and thin over the eastern ridge, I had stopped composing lists and started dressing. If the world was ending, I intended to meet it with my boots laced properly. Grandmother's rule. She had about nine hundred rules and most of them boiled down to *be ready for something you're not allowed to name*, which, now that I say it aloud in my head, should probably have told me more about my childhood than it did at the time.

* * *

The border stones start at the old mill race and run west along the ridge in a line so straight that Corvin — no. Not Corvin yet. Let me begin again, since beginnings matter and I'm told I'll be writing this all down eventually.

The border stones start at the mill race and run west, forty-one of them, waist-high, gray as old teeth. Every child in the Hollow learns to count them before they learn their letters. We touch them on festival days. We are taught, gently, endlessly, without ever being told what exactly we are being protected from, that the stones are warm.

That was the whole of our theology: *the stones are warm.* Touch one and you felt it, a low animal heat like a sleeping dog's flank, steady in summer, steady in snow, steady the day my grandmother died when I pressed my palm against the third stone and sobbed into its warmth because everything else in the world had gone cold and this hadn't. The wards lived in the stones the way a heartbeat lives in a wrist. You didn't think about them. You counted them, you touched them, you went home.

So you will understand why, when I laid my hand on the first stone that gray dawn and felt nothing at all, I stood there for a long moment doing absolutely nothing, like a woman who has reached for a stair in the dark and found morning instead.

Cold. The stone was cold. Cold as any rock on any ridge in any forgotten country, cold as the dead hearth in my kitchen, cold in the way that ordinary things are cold, which is to say: in the way that magic things are never supposed to be.

I touched it again, harder, as if the first touch had been shy. I put both hands on it. I leaned my cheek against it, which I had not done since I was six years old, and the granite bit my skin with the honest indifference of plain stone.

"Come on," I said to it. "Don't do this."

Stones, famously, do not answer. But ours had always hummed. Under your palm, deep as a buried river, there was a vibration you felt more than heard — the wards breathing, the valley dreaming itself safe. My whole life, that hum had been the floor under every step I took. I had built my entire personality on top of it the way you build a house on a foundation you never inspect.

The floor was gone.

I walked the line. Forty-one stones. I touched every single one, and by the eleventh my fingers were numb from the morning chill, and by the nineteenth I had stopped pretending I was checking and started admitting I was begging, and by the thirty-fourth I sat down in the wet grass beside a stone and laughed — actually laughed, one ugly bark of it — because the absurdity of it finally caught up with me. A hundred years, maybe five hundred, maybe a thousand, nobody knew, the wards had held. Through plagues and famines, through the year the river froze solid and the year the mist apples bloomed twice, through my grandmother's whole long careful guarded life and mine on top of it. And they hadn't broken. That was the terrible part. They hadn't shattered or cracked or flared some dramatic last warning. They had simply stopped, sometime in the dark hours while I lay awake making lists, as quietly as an old man dying in his sleep. As quietly as stars going out one constellation at a time.

Because those two things were not separate events. I want to be clear that I understood that immediately, standing there in the wet grass with numb hands. Whatever had eaten the sky last night had eaten this too. One mouth. One meal.

* * *

Old Sef ran the mill and the village council and, unofficially, everything else, in the way that certain men run certain valleys — not by decision, exactly, but by gravity. He was seventy-some years of beard and stubbornness, and he was already at his door when I came down off the ridge at a pace I told myself was brisk and everyone else correctly identified as running.

"Mara." He looked me over the way he looked over floodwater reports. "You've been at the stones."

"You've heard."

"I've heard a girl went up the ridge before breakfast touching rocks." He said it comfortably, the way you'd describe someone counting fence posts. "It's the Quiet Festival tomorrow. People get strange before the Festival. Your grandmother used to walk the line three times the week before, remember? Said she liked to check."

"She checked because—" I stopped. Swallowed it. "Sef. The stones are cold."

He considered me with the patience of a man who had watched weather come and go for seven decades and had never once been impressed by it. "Stones are always cold at dawn. Sun hasn't hit the ridge."

"The sun is hitting the ridge right now." I grabbed his hand — he let me, surprised into stillness — and pressed his palm flat against the gatepost of his own mill yard, the ward-anchor post, the one every child in the Hollow got lifted up to touch on their naming day. "Feel. Feel for the hum. Tell me what you feel."

He frowned. Concentrated. I watched his face do something complicated and then, with the smoothness of long practice, resolve itself into nothing at all.

"Wood," he said. "It's a post, girl. It's oak. It feels like oak."

"Sef—"

"It's the Festival," he said, taking his hand back and patting my shoulder with it, which was either comfort or dismissal depending on how generous you were feeling, and I have never in my life been feeling generous. "People get strange before the Festival. You've been up all night — I can see it on you. Go home, sleep, and tomorrow we'll light the candles and eat too much honey bread, and you'll feel foolish, and that'll be that." He smiled at me then, kindly, and it was the kindness that frightened me more than anything else that morning, because it was the kindness you give someone who is wrong, and I could see — I could *see* — that behind his eyes some part of him had felt the empty post and decided, instantly, permanently, not to have felt it.

"Sef," I said slowly, "when did you last actually touch the post? Not yesterday. Actually press your hand to it and listen."

"Now that's enough." The kindness dropped away and the councilman came up from underneath it, the one who kept the peace by keeping the questions small. "Nothing has changed in this valley in a hundred years. Nothing changed under your grandmother, nothing changed under the floods or the hard winters, and nothing changed last night. The stars were fog. The stones are stones. Go home, Mara."

And here is the thing I have thought about many times since: he wasn't lying. Or rather — he was, but not in the way liars lie. He believed it even as he said it, believed it the way you believe the sun will rise, and I watched him believe it in real time, watched the belief close over the hollow place in him like water closing over a dropped stone. Whatever had gone out of the wards had gone out of the villagers too, or had perhaps always been kept out by them, some great soft blanket of not-noticing woven over the whole valley. My grandmother's voice, uncharacteristically: *the wards hold more than the border, little wolf.*

Little wolf. I'd thought it was just what grandmothers called granddaughters.

"Nothing has changed," Sef repeated, and patted my shoulder again, and turned back into his house, and shut the door on me with the finality of a man closing a ledger on a balanced account.

* * *

I walked the line again anyway. All forty-one. In full daylight, in front of witnesses, so that there could be no argument later that I'd imagined it in the dark.

Tamsin found me at the twentieth stone. Of course she did. Tamsin could find me anywhere; it was less a skill than a law of nature, like water finding low ground. She came up the hill with her apron still floury from festival baking and her face set in the particular expression she reserved for my worst ideas, an expression with a long and distinguished history.

"Tamsin," I said, before she could open fire. "Touch this stone and tell me what you feel."

"No."

"Tamsin."

"Mara Almeida, it is two days before the Festival, you look like something the river refused, and Sef says you were up on the ridge at dawn scaring yourself over *rocks*." She planted herself in front of me, arms crossed, flour dusting one sleeve. "What happened last night?"

I looked at her. She looked at me. And I opened my mouth to tell her — about the stars going out constellation by constellation, the Hunter first and then the Sisters and then the slow swallowing of everything down to the last dim star at the sky's hem; about the candle burning blue; about the stone under my hand with no more life in it than a grave marker — and then I watched her face while I drew breath to say it, and I saw it happen. The little flicker. The almost-hearing. Her eyes going slightly past me, the way eyes slide off a word you don't want to read. Some door inside her swinging politely but firmly toward *later, not now, nothing is wrong, the stones are warm.*

She was my oldest friend. She had held my hair back at my grandmother's wake and split her bread with me through two bad harvests. She loved me the way family loves, which is to say blindly, and the blindness, I realized on that hillside, was not entirely her own doing.

"Cold," I said finally. "The stones are cold, Tam. All of them."

"They're stones," she said gently. "They're supposed to be cold. That's why the wards keep us warm." And she said it with such simple, settled certainty that for one vertiginous moment I doubted everything — the dawn, my hands, the twenty empty stones behind me, the whole gray morning. Maybe I had it backwards. Maybe the warmth had never been in the stones at all; maybe it was poured into them nightly from somewhere else, and the somewhere else was fine, was tired, was resting—

I pressed my palm flat against the twentieth stone one more time. Granite. Wet grass smell. A bird singing somewhere, idiotically cheerful.

Ordinary. The most frightening word in the language, that morning.

"Come home with me," Tamsin said. "Sleep. Eat. Tomorrow we'll do the Festival, and you'll laugh at yourself, and if you still want to worry about rocks next week I'll worry about them with you, I promise." She hooked her arm through mine, warm and solid and utterly sincere, and began steering me downhill, and I let her, because there is a loneliness so complete that even being wrong together looks like shelter.

But my hands shook the whole way down. Not from the cold. From the other thing — the thing I couldn't say in front of her, the thing that sat in my chest like swallowed river ice: *she didn't feel it either.* Not Sef, not Tamsin, not the children chasing each other through the mill yard who should have felt the absence in their bones the way you feel a missing tooth. Everyone warm, everyone certain, everyone asleep in a house whose walls had quietly stopped existing, and I alone standing in the doorway seeing the night through where the roof should be.

Seeing what others refuse to see, my grandmother used to say, is the loneliest work there is. I'd thought she meant something noble. She'd probably meant exactly this: a hillside, a shaking pair of hands, and forty-one stones that had gone silent overnight while an entire valley smiled and baked its festival bread.

* * *

I left Tamsin at her gate with promises about the Festival I mostly meant, and I did not go home.

I went to my grandmother's cottage instead, which had stood locked since her death two springs past because I could not bear it and because — though I never said this to anyone — I kept expecting her to come back. Dead women do not come back. I know this. But my grandmother had spent my entire childhood implying that rules were things other people obeyed, and grief makes mystics of us all.

The key was where it had always been, under the loose sill, wrapped in oilcloth, and my hand shook so badly fitting it to the lock that I scraped half the skin off my knuckle. Inside, the cottage smelled of dust and dried herbs and her — juniper and iron and something faintly animal, something I had never named and suddenly, standing in that doorway, did not want to name.

I told myself I'd come for her shawl, because the cottage was cold, because I was cold. I told myself a great many things. What I actually did was stand in the middle of the room turning slowly in a circle like a compass needle until I faced the corner where her chest sat under a dust sheet, the locked chest, the one she'd once caught me prying at when I was nine and struck my hand — my grandmother, who never raised her voice, much less her hand — and said, in a tone I had never heard from her before or since:

"Not until the country needs it."

I had laughed. Nine-year-olds laugh at everything. *What country? This one?* And she had gone very quiet and very still, the way the stones had gone quiet, and said only, "Eat your supper, little wolf."

The chest sat under its sheet like a patient thing. It had waited two years. Last night the stars had waited a thousand, maybe, before giving up.

I pulled the sheet off.

The lock was old iron, older style than anything else she'd owned, and it took me a quarter hour and a poker from her own hearth, and when it gave at last with a crack like a knuckle popping, the sound went through the silent cottage and out through the silent valley and I flinched as if the wards themselves had heard — as if somewhere a sleeping dog had stirred and not woken.

Inside, on top, folded in cloth gone stiff with age: a blade. Short, ceremonial, wickedly plain, with a wolf's tooth set into the pommel and letters worn nearly smooth along the fuller in a script I didn't know and that, some deep animal part of me insisted, I had known once, before I was born, in whatever place souls wait.

Under the blade, paper. A letter, sealed with wax pressed by the same tooth. My grandmother's handwriting on the front, cramped and urgent, the handwriting of a woman writing fast:

*If you are reading this, the country has begun to die.*

I sat down on her dusty floor with the letter in my shaking hands, and outside, far off, a bell rang noon in a valley that officially did not exist, in a country that had begun to die, and I broke the seal.
