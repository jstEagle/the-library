## 3. A Stranger With No Shadow

The stranger came to Vael Hollow the way trouble always comes: politely, and on foot, and asking nothing more than a bowl of soup.

I saw him first from the forge roof, where I'd gone to fix a slate that the wind had been worrying all morning. The road into the valley runs along the river for two miles of gray fields, flat as spilled milk, so you can watch someone approach for the better part of an hour if you're inclined to watch. Most people aren't. Most people in Vael Hollow have perfected the art of not watching, the way monks perfect silence. It's practically a sacrament here — *don't look, don't ask, don't remember* — and my neighbors practice it with a devotion that would shame the devout.

But I was on a roof with nothing but a slate and my own bad habits, so I watched.

He walked like a man who had measured every road he'd ever taken, which is to say he walked oddly — pausing at the bend where the river cuts south, standing a long moment at the milestone that doesn't mark any distance anyone admits to. At the milestone he crouched, took something from his coat, and did something I couldn't make out from the roof. Writing, maybe. Or drawing.

And then there was the shadow.

I want to be careful about how I say this, because I've had three days to doubt myself and I still can't make it not true. The sun was high and hard, the kind of light that throws your shadow out crisp as a cut silhouette. Mine lay beside me on the roof slates, sharp-eared and sharp-nosed, faithful as ever. His lay behind him. Not stretched behind him the way shadows do when the light's wrong — *lagging* behind him. Half a step back, always half a step back, no matter how fast or slow he walked, as though his shadow were following him rather than attached, as though it kept having to hurry to catch up.

When he stopped at the milestone, his shadow stopped a half-step late.

I sat down on the roof ridge and told myself several reasonable things. That distance plays tricks. That heat shimmer off the fields bends light. That a woman who'd spent the night before watching stars go out one constellation at a time might be primed to see strangeness everywhere she looked, the way a hungry man smells bread in every wind.

Then I watched him cross the bridge, and his shadow crossed it after him, and I stopped telling myself reasonable things and started climbing down very carefully so I wouldn't fall and give myself an excuse to dismiss everything.

* * *

By the time he reached the village square, half of Vael Hollow had found reasons to be outdoors. Nobody looked at him directly, of course. They looked at their fences, their laundry, their boots. A whole village performing indifference with the intensity of actors who've forgotten their lines. Old Maret from the bakery dropped a tray of rolls and didn't notice, which tells you something, because Maret has never once in her life failed to notice baked goods in peril.

He was younger than I'd guessed. Thirty, maybe, with the kind of face you forget while looking at it — brown hair going gray early at the temples, ink stains on his fingers like a scribe's, a traveler's coat worn soft at the elbows and patched with cloth from at least four different countries, none of them ours. Over one shoulder he carried a leather tube sealed with wax, the way soldiers carry dispatches and priests carry censures. He held it the way those men hold such things, too. Like it mattered more than his life, or possibly *was* his life.

"Good day," he said, to the square in general.

The square pretended to be a different square, elsewhere.

"I'm looking for lodging," he tried. "And directions. And—" he paused, and I watched him do a thing I would come to know well, this flicking glance at the ground, checking on his shadow like a man checking a restive horse, "—and perhaps someone who knows the local landmarks."

Silence. Somewhere a shutter closed. Maret began sweeping up her ruined rolls with tremendous focus.

I should have done what everyone else was doing. My grandmother raised me better than that, or worse than that, depending on how you read her lessons. *Be quiet, be small, be stone*, she said, over and over, all the years she lived. *Vael Hollow survives by being nobody.*

But my grandmother was dead, and the stars had gone dark the night before, and I had spent the whole morning walking past the border stones feeling cold coming off them like breath from a cellar, and I was tired — tired in the bones, tired of being stone. So instead of doing nothing, I heard myself say:

"There's a room above the smithy. Bram won't rent it to you because Bram doesn't like strangers, but he'll rent it to me because I fixed his chimney, and I'll sublet it to you because I don't like strangers either, and that way we'll both know where we stand."

Every head in the square turned about forty degrees toward me without the owners of those heads moving anything else, which was impressive, in its way.

The stranger looked at me. Up close his eyes were a cartographer's eyes, the pale gray of a map with most of its names rubbed off, and they went over my face the way his kind go over coastlines — noting, measuring, filing away. Then he smiled, and it transformed him entirely, and I understood that this was a man whose smile was a tool he kept sharpened.

"That's the kindest insult I've been offered in years," he said. "I accept."

* * *

His name was Corvin. He paid for the room with coins from four different realms and tipped generously, which bought him more goodwill in Vael Hollow than honesty ever would. He ate at the common table and talked just enough — weather, roads, the price of wool downriver — giving away nothing and seeming to, which is a rare gift and I've since learned it's the first tool in a cartographer's kit. Maps are mostly things left out.

It was the shadow that made me certain, though. We ate by lamplight in Bram's kitchen, and the lamp threw both our shadows against the wall, mine steady as paint, his arriving a beat behind every gesture. When he lifted his cup, the cup-shadow lifted alone; the man-shadow followed after, apologetic. When he laughed, the shadow laughed a moment too late, like an echo in a canyon too narrow for echoes.

"You're staring," he said pleasantly.

"Your shadow's slow," I said.

Bram, who had been pretending to clean a pot, became very interested in the pot.

Corvin set down his cup. For the first time since the square, the pleasantness fell off his face like a mask slipping its cord, and underneath was something tired and wary and young. "Most people take days to mention it," he said. "Weeks, if they're polite. You took a meal."

"I'm not polite."

"No," he agreed, and there was something almost warm in it. "I could see that across a bridge." He turned his hand over in the lamplight and studied it as if consulting notes. "It happened in the Ashfall Reaches, six years ago. There was a place there that had been erased — struck from every record, unmade by treaty. I mapped it anyway. The guild called it a crime. The penalty for mapping erased places is that they map *you* out in return. They took my name from the registers and my shadow from... wherever shadows are kept. What follows me now is a copy. A sketch. It does its best." He shrugged, and the shrug-shadow arrived late, like a guest who'd lost his nerve at the door. "You get used to it. You never quite stop noticing."

"And the erased place," I said. My voice came out strange. "You mapped it. Did it work? Mapping something that isn't supposed to exist?"

He looked at me for a long time. The lamp guttered, and I thought — absurdly, superstitiously, in a way I hadn't thought since childhood — of my grandmother's candle burning blue.

"Why do you ask?" he said.

Because the stars went out last night, I wanted to say. Because the wards are cold. Because my grandmother lied to me for twenty years with love in her mouth and I can't get the taste of it out. Because last night I practiced sword-forms I was forbidden to name aloud and my blade hummed like something waking up, and I am afraid of what's under my own skin.

What I said was: "Professional curiosity."

He smiled again, but this time it wasn't the sharpened tool. This one was smaller and realer, the smile of a man recognizing a fellow liar and deciding to let it pass.

"Then tomorrow," he said, "over breakfast, I'll show you professional curiosity. Fair warning — it's heavier than it looks."

* * *

He spread the map on Bram's worktable at dawn, weighting the corners with horseshoes, and I will tell you honestly that my hands shook before I even touched it. I knew. Some part of me knew before the first corner flattened, the way you know a storm is coming from the smell of the air.

It was drawn on good rag paper in iron-gall ink gone brown with age, and it was a map of Vael Hollow.

Our valley. Our river, our bridge, our crooked square. Every farmhouse rendered in the careful miniature script of a master's hand. But it was a Vael Hollow from long ago — the orchards bigger, the mill where the tannery now stands, the great house on the hill drawn intact with its roofs on instead of the ruin we call the old tower. And the border stones were there, all twelve of them, each marked with a symbol I'd seen only once before in my life: carved small and secret on the underside of my grandmother's oath-blade's hilt.

"They gave me this to destroy," Corvin said quietly. "Twenty years ago, when I was young and ambitious and believed the guild's catechism — that some maps are dangerous, that forgetting is a mercy, that a well-ordered world has fewer places in it than an honest one. I rode out here to burn it." His finger hovered over the paper without touching. "I sat on that hill—" he pointed at the rise above the square, and my skin crawled, because I'd picnicked on that hill my whole childhood, "—for three days, trying to make myself do it. In the end I couldn't. I told the guild the map was destroyed and kept this one. It cost me my name and my shadow and every road home I had. I've spent twenty years telling myself it was worth it."

"Why?" My voice cracked on it. "Why keep it? What's on it that's worth that?"

He drew a breath. Outside, the village was waking — carts, chickens, Maret's bell — all the ordinary sounds of a valley that had convinced itself it was ordinary.

"Doors," he said.

"What?"

"Look at the walls." He traced the great house, the tannery, the chapel, the long wall behind the smithy where I'd played as a child and stubbed my toe on a thousand occasions. Where the walls stood on the map, there were marks — tall, thin, precise rectangles, drawn with a care that bordered on reverence, each one labeled in that old miniature script. Doors. Doors set into walls where I knew — I *knew*, the way you know your own hands — there was nothing but stone.

"Eleven doors," Corvin said. "In a valley of maybe four hundred souls. No village builds eleven ceremonial doors and then bricks every one of them shut. Unless the doors weren't built to be opened. Unless they were built to be *forgotten*." He finally looked up at me, and his pale gray eyes weren't a cartographer's anymore; they were a man's, frightened and awake. "This isn't a map of a village, Mara Almeida. I've known that for twenty years and I've run from knowing it. This is a map of a wound dressed up as a valley. Somebody erased this place from the world and bricked up every way back in — or every way back *out* — and then taught the survivors not to see."

The fire popped. My shadow lay steady against the wall. His lay a half-step behind him, patient, waiting for him to catch up to himself.

"My grandmother's chest," I heard myself say. "There's a locked chest under her bed I've never been able to open. She said—" My throat closed. Twenty years of her voice: *be quiet, be small, be stone.* "She said it was nothing. She said a lot of things were nothing."

Corvin began rolling up the map with the tenderness other men reserve for sleeping children.

"Well," he said. "Shall we find out what she buried?"

* * *

We were halfway across the square when Tamsin caught us — Tamsin, my oldest friend, running with her apron still on and flour to her elbows, and her face wearing the particular expression I would learn, in the terrible weeks that followed, to dread: kindness arranged over fear like a cloth over furniture.

"Mara." She grabbed my sleeve. Her eyes flicked to Corvin, to the map tube on his shoulder, back to me. "The elder council's asking after you. They're asking after *everyone* who spoke to the traveler. Mara, what did you show him? What did he *show you*?"

Behind us, the morning sun threw our shadows long across the cobbles — mine sharp and faithful, his lagging half a step behind, hurrying to catch up.

"Tamsin," I said slowly, "why does the council care about a map?"

She didn't answer. She looked at me the way you look at someone standing on ice you know is thin, and over her shoulder, at the edge of the square, Elder Sarel was watching us with an expression I had never seen on a face in Vael Hollow before.

It was the expression of a man seeing a door.
