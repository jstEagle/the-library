## 5. The Ember That Would Not Die

I have been thrown out of better rooms than this one.

That's not boasting; it's inventory. The Cartographers' Guild in Lumenport ejected me from a chamber with a ceiling frescoed by a man who went blind doing it, which is the kind of dedication I respect and also the kind that gets you exiled, apparently. Before that, a tavern in Grayharrow threw me out for drawing the coastline wrong—except I hadn't drawn it wrong, I'd drawn it *honestly*, which is worse. And before that there was a house I was born in, though nobody threw me out of that one. It simply stopped being on any map, along with everyone who lived in it, including me.

So when the innkeeper of Vael Hollow looked at me across his bar with the flat expression of a man deciding whether to reach for the broom or the pitchfork, I felt almost nostalgic.

"You'll be moving on," he said.

"Almost certainly," I agreed. "But not tonight, and not without supper. You can choose which of those offends you more."

His jaw worked. Behind him, the taproom had gone quiet in that particular way villages go quiet—not silence but a held breath, forty people pretending to drink while listening with their whole spines. I've been listened at before. It's flattering, in a way that makes your skin crawl.

The girl was there too. She'd come in half an hour after me, smelling of dawn air and cold iron, and she'd taken the corner table the way soldiers take corners: back to the wall, sightlines clear. Mara Almeida. I didn't know her name yet then, but I knew her type. I'd spent three years cataloguing her type—the ones who see the wound in the world and can't convince anyone else it's bleeding.

She was watching me watch her, and she was watching something else besides: the little brass lamp on my table, where my ember sat in its dish of ash like a coal that had forgotten to die.

That was the problem, of course. Not me. Never me. The ember.

* * *

Let me tell you about the ember, because everyone asks eventually, usually right before they try to take it.

It is the size of a walnut and the color of a banked forge, and it has burned—without fuel, without air, without so much as a flicker of complaint—for as long as I have carried it, which is six years, and for some time before that, according to the man I took it from, who was dying and grateful to hand off the weight. It does not scorch what it rests on. It does not go out in rain. Once, crossing the Saltmarsh Reach, I dropped it in a foot of brackish water and fished it out glowing, mildly offended, as if I were the one who'd made an error.

The Guild called it a curiosity. Then they called it a forgery. Then they called it evidence, and evidence is the word that ends careers.

Because here is what a cartographer knows that other people don't, or rather here is what a cartographer *notices*, since everyone knows it and most people manage to forget: maps are not pictures of the world. Maps are arguments about the world. Every border is a sentence somebody won. Every blank space is a thing someone paid to keep blank. And when you spend your life comparing old maps to new ones, you start to see the edits—and once you've seen the edits, you can't stop seeing them.

There was a country called Sarnholt.

I want to be careful with that sentence, because "was" is doing a lot of work in it, and because the first time I said it aloud in a Guild review chamber, three senior cartographers looked at me the way you look at a man who claims he's seen a mermaid: pitying, and slightly afraid of catching whatever he has.

There *was* a country called Sarnholt. It appears on maps from two hundred years ago. It appears in trade ledgers, marriage records, a treaty annex, the marginalia of at least four saints' biographies. And then, roughly a century ago, it appears nowhere at all. Not conquered. Not dissolved—dissolution leaves paperwork. Sarnholt simply *stops*, the way a word stops if you say it enough times, until it's just a shape your mouth makes around nothing.

The official explanation—which exists only in a single sealed annex that I copied illegally and then burned, because you burn things like that or they burn you—is a treaty clause. A *redaction clause*. The signatories did not defeat Sarnholt. They unwrote it. Borders redrawn, records purged, names struck, roads rerouted so that travelers would pass through its territory without ever registering they'd passed through anything at all. An entire nation edited out by committee. Somewhere in a government archive there is a pen that did more damage than any army of the last thousand years.

And Vael Hollow sits at the center of the hole where Sarnholt used to be.

I figured that out two years ago, standing in the Guild's map room at three in the morning with a candle and a stolen key, overlaying a survey of the Hollow onto a pre-Redaction chart of Sarnholt and watching the valleys line up like interlocked fingers. The heart of the hidden country, still beating under a century of forgetting. I remember laughing. Alone in a dark room, laughing at a map. That should have told me what the next years would cost.

So no—I didn't come to Vael Hollow for the ale. I came because the ember has been pulling north-northwest for six years, patient as tide, and every mile it pulled, the maps got emptier, until empty ran out and there was the Hollow, full of people pretending their valley wasn't the exact center of everything missing.

The ember doesn't pull like a compass needle. It pulls like hunger. Some nights it warms against my ribs through the leather of its case, insistent, and I lie awake in whatever ditch or barn has accepted me, and I think: *you are a piece of something that was erased, and you remember.* Dragons don't forget. That's not sentiment; that's natural history. A dragon's fire is memory given heat. Which means somewhere in the world—or under it, or inside the seam of it—there is a dragon burning down to its last coal, holding the memory of Sarnholt the way a dying hearth holds the shape of the house it warmed.

And it wants to go home. Or it wants me to carry it home. With embers, as with oaths, I'm learning, the distinction matters less than you'd hope.

* * *

The innkeeper chose supper over offense, which showed more sense than the Guild ever managed, and I ate my stew under forty sets of eyes while the girl in the corner watched my lamp like it might stand up and walk.

When the room thinned out—when the held breath finally released into yawns and boots scraping toward beds—she crossed the floor and sat down across from me without asking. I approved of that. People who ask permission to sit give you time to lie to them.

"That light," she said. "Where did you get it?"

"A dead man."

Her eyes came up sharp. Good. Most people flinch at the wrong part of that answer.

"He was a shepherd," I said, "out past the Gray Fields, in country that isn't supposed to exist anymore either. He'd carried it thirty years. His grandfather found it in the ashes of a shrine." I turned the dish a quarter-turn on the table, and the ember's glow caught her face, and I watched something move behind her eyes that I recognized because I'd seen it in my own mirror for years: recognition. The look of someone seeing proof of a crime everyone told them was imaginary. "He said it sang to him sometimes. Did yours sing to you?"

"I haven't got one."

"No," I said. "You've got wards. Cold ones, as of yesterday. And stars going out one constellation at a time. And a grandmother's candle burning blue on a night it shouldn't." Her whole body went still, the stillness of a drawn bow, and I raised both hands, palms out, the universal gesture of a man who would very much like to keep all his fingers. "Villages talk. Even quiet ones. Especially quiet ones."

"You've been here one day."

"One day is generous. I've been here since sundown, and I already know three things your elders will swear aren't true. That's my profession, as it happens. Knowing things that officially aren't true." I leaned back. The bench creaked its disapproval. "Corvin Hale. Formerly of the Royal Cartographers' Guild, Lumenport chapter, currently of nowhere in particular. Exiled for mapping places that no longer are. You may laugh. Everyone does, right up until they understand it."

"And what places are those?"

"Sarnholt," I said.

I've said that word perhaps fifty times in six years, and I can count the reactions. Most people blink politely. Scholars get interested and then get careful. One man in Grayharrow spat and left. But the woman across from me—Mara, though I didn't learn the name until later that night—she did none of that. She went white, then went fierce, the way ice goes before it cracks.

"Say that again," she said quietly.

"Sarnholt. The hidden country. The one that never fell, because falling requires having existed in the record, and theirs was—" I stopped. Because she'd put her hand flat on the table between us, and on the back of it, in old ink gone brown, there was a mark I knew from exactly one place: the seal margin of the treaty annex. A wolf-tooth sigil. The mark they put on documents belonging to the country that was never allowed to belong anywhere.

"Well," I said, after a moment. "That saves some introductions."

* * *

We talked until the candles died, which I note with professional interest, because mine burned normally and hers guttered blue twice, and neither of us remarked on it aloud, which is how alliances start: two people agreeing not to say the frightening thing yet.

Here is what I learned, in the manner of a man taking bearings.

She was the granddaughter of the valley's oath-keeper, raised on prohibitions instead of stories, taught sword-forms she wasn't allowed to name. She'd walked the border stones at dawn and found them cold. She'd watched the sky lose its constellations like a man losing teeth, one by one, in order. And she had spent her whole life feeling like the only awake person in a house of sleepers, which—forgive the vanity—is precisely how I spent mine, except my house was an empire and my sleepers wore robes and held pens.

We were not alike. Let me be clear about that, because she'd say it herself, later, angrily, and she'd be right. Mara Almeida was born into her exile; it fit her like a bone fits a body. I'd earned mine, clumsily, one honest line at a time. She had grief. I had guilt. Grief makes you steady. Guilt makes you fast. We were going to need both, though I didn't know that yet. That night I just knew she was the first person in six years who looked at my impossible lamp and asked the correct question, which was not *what is it* but *what does it want.*

"It wants north," I told her. "North and west, deeper into the Hollow, toward the high ground where the old maps show a shrine. There's a dragon at the end of this, or what's left of one. Ember like this doesn't wander. It's a piece cut from something living, and pieces go home."

"And if we follow it?"

"Then we find out whether your valley is a village with odd weather or the last living heart of a murdered country." I turned the ember again. Its glow lay steady between us, warm as a shared secret. "Fair warning: I've followed it six years, and every answer it's given me has been worse than the question."

She looked at the window, where the sky over Vael Hollow hung starless and black as spilled ink, and I watched her decide. It's a specific thing, watching someone decide. Their face stops performing. Hers went young and old at once.

"My grandmother left me a locked chest," she said. "I couldn't open it. Tonight I think I could."

"There's always a chest," I said. "In my experience the chest is the country's favorite hiding place. Countries hide in chests the way snakes hide in grass."

"Are you offering to help me open it?"

"I'm offering breakfast," I said, "and the company of the only other person in this valley who knows the stars are missing. Beyond that, we'll negotiate. I've been exiled long enough to know that trust is a currency best spent in small amounts."

Something that was almost a smile crossed her face, brief as a fish rising. "You talk too much for a cartographer."

"Cartographers talk exactly as much as the territory requires. The territory lately requires a great deal." I stood, joints announcing themselves, six years of ditches and bad benches filing their complaints. At the door I paused, because fairness demanded it, and fairness is a habit I cling to the way drowning men cling to driftwood. "One thing you should know before you decide anything. The Guild didn't exile me for making false maps. They exiled me for making true ones. And the last order they gave me—the order I'm technically still running from—was to come here and destroy the final surviving map of Sarnholt." I met her eyes. "I kept it instead. It's in my pack, and it shows doors in your valley where there are only walls. If that frightens you, it should. If it makes you want to throw me out—"

"The innkeeper tried that already," Mara said. "It didn't take."

* * *

Upstairs, in the narrow bed the innkeeper rented me with visible reluctance, I took out the ember and set it on the sill, and for the first time since Grayharrow it moved.

Not much. A lean, the way a flame leans toward a draft. North-northwest, same bearing it has held for six years—but stronger tonight, warmer against my palm when I cupped it, almost eager. As if it had smelled something. As if, after six years of dragging a tired man through erased country, it had finally arrived within range of whatever it had been reaching for.

Or whoever.

I thought about the wolf-tooth mark on her hand. I thought about a chest that wouldn't open for anyone but the right blood. I thought about the annex, and the clause, and the phrase I'd copied out by candlelight with my hands shaking: *the binding shall endure upon the heirs of the first wolves, unto the last of their line, and the door shall open to no other word.*

Embers don't wander. Oaths don't either. Both of them wait, patient as stone, for the person they were made for.

Downstairs, faintly, I heard the front door of the inn open and close—her, going home to a locked chest and a dead woman's secrets—and the ember leaned harder north, glowing like a small stubborn heart that refused, absolutely refused, to die.

Tomorrow, I thought, we open the chest.

Tomorrow, the country starts telling the truth.
