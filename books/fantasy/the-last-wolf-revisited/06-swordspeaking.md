## 6. Swordspeaking

I told myself I was going out to check the fence line.

It was a good lie. It had the shape of a chore, the dull, respectable weight of a chore, and chores were the currency of normal in Vael Hollow. If Tamsin saw me leave at dawn with my grandmother's sword wrapped in sacking over my shoulder, she'd see a woman doing fence work, not a woman running from her own kitchen table and the letter that sat on it like a wound.

*If you are reading this, the country has begun to die.*

I hadn't slept. I'd sat with the letter until the candle drowned itself in its own wax, reading the same lines until they stopped being words and became weather—something pressing on me from all sides. *You are not a girl with a sword, Mara. You are a wall that learned to walk.* Grandmother had always been dramatic in her letters. I'd thought that was just how old people wrote when they missed being young.

The wards had been cold for two days now. The border stones that should have hummed against my palm like sleeping bees gave me nothing but rock and morning frost. Elder Sable had patted my hand and told me stones cool off in autumn, as if the wards were a kettle someone had forgotten to relight. Everyone wanted so badly for nothing to be wrong that they'd made a religion of it. I was starting to understand why. Seeing clearly was exhausting. Seeing clearly was lonely.

So: the fence line. North pasture, where the boundary wall ran along the ridge and the valley's edge thinned into gray moorland. Outside the wards' strongest reach. I told myself I was going to fix a fence because fences were simple things, made of posts and wire, things that stayed what they were.

I brought the sword anyway. That was the part of the lie I didn't look at too closely.

* * *

The morning had that held-breath quality again—the same stillness as festival eve, as if the whole valley were an animal listening for something in the brush. No wind on the ridge, though the grass below bent and swayed. Clouds moving without sound, too slow, like they were being carried rather than traveling. Even my footsteps sounded borrowed.

The north pasture fence ran crooked along the hillside, half-swallowed by gorse. I set down the sacking, unwrapped the sword, and stood there holding it like an apology.

It wasn't a beautiful blade. Grandmother's swords were working tools, plain steel with a grip wrapped in leather gone dark from a century of hands. But it had always felt heavier than its weight, the way certain books do, or certain doors. And since the stars went out, it had been worse—it had felt *aware*. Like carrying a cat that was pretending to sleep.

"Right," I said to it. "Fence."

There was a post leaning near the wall, one of the boundary markers with the old sigils worn nearly smooth. I knelt by it, dug around the base, and got both hands on it to lever it upright—and that was when I noticed the silence had changed shape.

Not quiet. *Attentive.*

I looked up. The gray fields beyond the wall stretched to the horizon, colorless as unwritten paper, and somewhere out there was whatever had eaten the stars. And between me and it, the last thin veil of the valley's warding lay like frost on a window, barely thick enough to blur what was behind it.

My grandmother's voice came back to me uninvited, from years ago, from the one lesson she ever gave me about the family gift: *Never outside the walls, Mara. Inside, the wards hold your tongue shut kindly. Outside, the world can hear you.*

I'd assumed she meant shouting. Carrying news, gossip, the ordinary danger of a loud mouth in a hidden place. Standing there with my hand on a leaning post, I understood—with the slow, sick drop of a floor giving way—that she had never once in her life used a word carelessly.

The sword hummed.

Not rang—hummed. A low note under the threshold of hearing, felt in my palm bones and my teeth, and threaded through it, faint as a name called across water, were *words*. Not in any language I knew, and yet I knew them, the way you know a tune before you know its lyrics. They rose in the steel like bubbles through honey, and each one pressed against the inside of my skull asking to be said.

"No," I told the blade, reasonably. "No, we are not doing that."

The words did not care about reason. They were patient, the way inheritance is patient. They had waited a hundred years under wards and silence and a dead woman's locked chest, and now the door was cracked and they were coming through whether I braced against it or not.

I let go of the fence post. That was my first mistake—or my first honest act; history would have to decide. The words needed my voice, and my voice lived closer to my hands than anywhere else. Sword-forms, grandmother had called them, though she forbade me the true names. *Forms*, she said, as if they were shapes I poured myself into, empty vessels. She never told me the shapes were sentences.

I drew breath to steady myself, and the breath came in already shaped—a vowel, an opening syllable, something old and consonant-heavy gathering behind my teeth like a sneeze made of language.

I clamped my jaw shut.

The pressure kept building anyway. Behind my eyes, behind my breastbone, in the scar tissue of my left palm where I'd gripped a hot pan at fourteen and never quite healed right. The word wanted *out*. And beneath the terror—underneath it, shameful and bright as a coin at the bottom of a well—was something else. Curiosity. Want. The exhilaration of a held note finally resolving.

Every swordspeaker in a thousand years had felt exactly this, I realized. Every one of them had chosen. That was the horror of it. Not compulsion—*invitation*. The gift never forced. It just opened the door and waited to see who you were.

I am not proud of what happened next. I'd love to tell you I wrestled the word down through discipline and virtue, that I bit it back and walked home wiser. What actually happened is that a rabbit burst from the gorse beside me, all panic and white tail, and I flinched—and in flinching, gasped—

—and the gasp had the word in it.

*Vael-sarn-eth.*

Three syllables. Less than a heartbeat. I heard them leave me and felt them leave me, felt something pass out through my lips and into the morning like heat off a struck anvil, and the world *answered*.

The fence did not explode. That would have been simpler, more honest, more like the stories. Instead the fence *listened*. Every rail, every nail, every length of wire along forty yards of pasture boundary turned toward me—not physically, but in some deeper way, the way a crowd turns toward a voice—and then, having listened, obeyed. The word I'd spoken was an old command-form, though I wouldn't learn its meaning until later, and its meaning was roughly *be unmade, gently*.

The nails slid out of the rails and hung in the air, quivering, a constellation of iron. The rails drifted apart from the posts with a soft creaking, like a boat unlashing. Then the whole structure sighed—one long exhalation of timber and wire—and came apart into its components, which rained down into the grass in tidy, terrible order: nails sorted by size, wire coiled, rails stacked in a neat cordwood pile that no human hand had built.

Forty yards of fence. Spoken into splinters and sortment in less time than it takes to say a sentence. Because I was startled by a rabbit.

I sat down hard in the grass.

My hands were shaking. Not fear-shaking, or not only—I knew fear-shaking, had known it my whole life in small doses, and this was different, this was shaking the way a bell shakes after it's been struck, still ringing with what had passed through it. The word was gone from my mouth but its echo lived in my bones, and gods help me, the echo felt *good*. That was the worst discovery of the morning, worse than the fence, worse than the letter. Somewhere underneath my terror, some inherited thing in me had enjoyed that enormously, and it was already hungry for the next word.

"Oh," I said aloud, very quietly, to the ruined fence and the attentive morning and the gray fields beyond the wall. "Oh, grandmother. *This* is why."

This is why we never named the forms. This is why the wards, why the silence, why a lifetime of *don't say, don't show, don't be*. Not because the gift was evil. Because the gift was *load-bearing*. Because every word I spoke outside these walls might prop something up or pull something down, and I had spent twenty-six years learning to speak without knowing my voice was a blade.

And underneath that, colder: the letter's promise. *A wall that learned to walk.* If the oath-blade answered to my voice, then the oath itself might. The country's binding lived in words—words I could apparently *speak into being* without knowing their meanings. What else was I carrying? What other sentences slept in my blood, waiting for a startle, a stumble, a bad dream?

I picked up the sword. My hand knew the grip better than my mind approved of.

"You and I," I told it, "are going to have a conversation about boundaries."

The humming softened. Almost smug, if steel can be smug. Steel absolutely can be smug; I've since confirmed it.

I tried, then, deliberately, to do the opposite of what I'd done. To take one of the drifting feelings in the air—that pressure of gathered words behind my teeth—and press it *down*, fold it small, swallow it back where it came from. I stood in the wreck of my own accident and practiced silence like a form, like a sword-form made of nothing, arms at my sides, breath even, mouth shut. The words in the blade dimmed, grudgingly, banked coals.

And in that silence I finally heard what the morning had been holding its breath for.

Hoofbeats. Far off, on the north road, coming down out of the gray fields toward the valley. More than one horse. The rhythm of them was wrong for travelers—too spaced, too deliberate, riders keeping formation. And beneath the hoofbeats, faint but unmistakable, a sound I had no business recognizing and recognized instantly anyway, the way you recognize a face from a story:

Singing. Low, male voices, in unison, in a language that made the sword in my hand flare bright with recognition and grief.

The treaty-hunters had found the crack in the wall.

I snatched up the sacking, wrapped the blade with hands that almost obeyed me, and turned toward the village—and stopped, because the second thing arrived, quieter than the hoofbeats but closer: a shimmer at the tree line, half a step behind a man walking out of the shadows, a traveler with his shadow lagging after him like a dog that had stopped to sniff something.

Corvin. The map-carrier. The stranger with doors drawn where our walls were.

He raised one hand, not quite a wave, his eyes flicking from me to the impossibly neat pile of disassembled fence to the sword-shaped bulge in the sacking.

"Fence trouble?" he asked.

I looked at the wreckage of everything I'd ever been told about myself, scattered across forty yards of pasture in suspiciously orderly piles.

"You have no idea," I said. "And you need to hear something. There's riders on the north road, and they're singing."

His face went the color of his own missing shadow. "That," he said, "is not possible. They're a hundred miles—" He stopped. Looked east, at the gray fields, at the country eating its own edges, and recalculated everything. "How fast can you run?"

"I grew up hauling water uphill," I said. "Why do cartographers ask stupid questions?"

"Because," Corvin said, already turning back toward the village at a pace just short of a run, "the song they're singing is the one my guild uses to erase maps. And if they've reached your valley's borders, then whoever sent them knows exactly where the Door is—and they think *you're* the key."
