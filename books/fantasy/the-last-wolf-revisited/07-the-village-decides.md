## 7. The Village Decides

*Tamsin*

I have known Mara Almeida since we were six years old and she pushed me into Miller's Pond for calling her sword a stick. It *was* a stick, but that was never the point with Mara. The point was always what a thing was going to be.

So when she came to me at dusk, smelling of border stones and panic, asking me to keep quiet about the stranger with the lagging shadow — I should have known I would fail her. Not because I'm disloyal. Because I love her, which in Vael Hollow amounts to nearly the same thing.

"You understand," I said, "that if I don't tell the council, and they find out anyway, it's worse for you."

"Then don't find out," she said, with that grin she uses when she's asking for something enormous and pretending it's small.

"Mara."

"Tamsin." She took my hands. Her fingers were cold. Cold hands, warm heart, my mother used to say, but lately Mara's hands had been cold all the way through, like she was holding something on the other side of them. "The wards are failing. You felt it at the festival. The candles burned blue. The *stars*, Tamsin."

"The stars came back."

"Some of them. In the wrong places." She said it quietly, the way you tell someone their house is on fire while they're holding a sleeping child. "If the council knows I've been asking questions — if they know about Corvin, about the map — they'll move faster than they otherwise would. Maybe faster than they can think. I need time."

"And lying to Elder Sorrel buys time?"

"Lying to Elder Sorrel buys *everything*," she said. "It's practically a civic duty."

That's Mara. Even frightened, even with the sky coming apart over her head one constellation at a time, she can make me laugh, and she knows it, and she uses it, and I let her, because I have been letting her since we were six.

I told her I'd say nothing.

I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, in ink: *Tamsin Reeve said she would say nothing.*

I lasted eleven hours.

* * *

*Sorrel*

Forty-one years I have sat on the council of Vael Hollow, and in forty-one years the agenda has not once changed. Well water levels. Fence disputes. Whether young Pell is permitted to marry outside the valley (he is not; he sulked; the matter resolved itself). This is what governance looks like when your entire purpose is to remain perfectly, invisibly still. We do not rule so much as we hold our breath, generation after generation, and call the holding peace.

So when Tamsin Reeve asked to speak before the vote, I knew before she opened her mouth that the breath was about to come out of us all.

She stood where petitioners stand, in the circle of worn stones beneath the meeting oak, and she looked older than her twenty-three years. Grief does that. So does guilt. I have learned to tell them apart by the hands — grief wrings them, guilt holds them very still.

"Elders," she said. "Mara Almeida has a stranger staying at her grandmother's house. A man who carries maps of places that don't exist. And Mara has been walking the border stones at dawn, and her grandmother's chest is open, and last night I saw her blade singing."

The silence afterward had texture. I could feel every elder doing the same arithmetic I was: *the Almeida girl, the oath-warden's blood, the one we let pretend she was only a blacksmith because pretending has kept this valley alive for a hundred years.*

"She told you this?" I asked. "Or did you pry it out?"

Tamsin's chin came up. "She asked me to keep it secret. I'm telling you because I love her."

There it is. I have heard every kind of betrayal dressed as devotion in my years under this oak — husbands reporting wives, children reporting parents, once a woman reporting her own reflection, though that was the fever-year and best forgotten. They all wear the same kind face. Kindness is the most dangerous thing we farm here, along with turnips.

"You did right," I told her, and watched her flinch, because being told you did right when you know you did something else is its own small cruelty.

"The vote," said Elder Bran, who is ninety and mostly hat. "The old question. Full sealing."

Full sealing. Three words we keep in the back of the charter like a knife in a drawer. Not merely maintaining the wards — deepening them. Closing the valley's memory against the outside entirely. No more festival candles lit for the vanished country. No more songs with the strange place-names left unexplained. No more *noticing*. The founders wrote it as a last resort: *if the hiding fails, hide deeper, until there is no one home to find.* What they were too polite to write was the rest of it: the sealing takes memory. That is its fuel. Every sealing asks the valley to forget a little more of itself, and the valley, being made of people, obliges.

We voted once before, in my grandmother's day, when a surveyor from the lowlands wandered in with instruments. Partial sealing then — we burned his papers and his name, and for a season nobody could say the word *north*. It held.

But Mara Almeida is not a surveyor. Mara Almeida is the hinge.

"All in favor of full sealing," I said, "and the wardenship invoked upon the Almeida line."

Nine hands rose. Ten. Eleven.

Mine stayed down until the last moment. Then it rose too, because I am a coward with good posture, and because I genuinely believe — God forgive me, I *believe* — that forgetting is kinder than dying.

* * *

*Mara*

I knew something was wrong when Tamsin wouldn't look at the anvil.

We'd been friends long enough that she had a whole vocabulary of avoidance, and refusing to look at things was her most fluent phrase. She stood in my forge doorway that morning asking after my health in a voice pitched for a funeral, complimenting hinges I hadn't made, and every sentence landed three feet to the left of anything real.

"You've been to the council," I said.

The pause was answer enough, but she gave me words anyway, because Tamsin has never once taken the coward's exit without leaving a note explaining herself. "The vote was last night. Full sealing. They invoke the wardenship today — Mara, they're coming to bind you to the valley stone. It's the old rite. Your grandmother's rite. Once it's done, you won't be able to leave even if the wards fall. You won't be able to *want* to leave. That's what it does."

I set down my hammer. My hand was steady, which surprised me. Somewhere inside, apparently, I had already known; some part of me had been counting Tamsin's eleven hours alongside her.

"You told them about Corvin."

"I told them everything." She crossed the forge and grabbed my shoulders, and her eyes were wet and furious and certain. "Because if I didn't, and they found him here, they wouldn't just seal the valley, Mara, they'd seal *you into it*, mid-sentence, with half a truth in your mouth. At least this way — at least this way you get a choice about how you're caught."

"That's not a choice. That's a flavor of trap."

"Yes," she said simply. "It is. I picked the one with the softest walls."

And there it was — the whole history of our friendship turned inside out in one sentence. She has spent our lives building soft walls around me and calling it care, and I have spent our lives pressing my face against them and calling it freedom, and neither of us ever named it until now, standing in a forge that smelled of iron and cooling ash, with the council's riders already saddling their horses somewhere behind her.

"I have to go," I said.

"I know." Her grip tightened. "That's why I'm telling you where they aren't. The north ford road — Bran argued to post watchers there and lost, because Bran loses arguments now, he argues with his hat more than the room. You have until sundown. Less, if Sorrel finds her spine."

"Why are you helping me? After—" I couldn't finish it. *After selling me* sat between us like a third person.

Tamsin wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, hard, angry at the tears for existing. "Because loving you means telling the truth even when the truth is a knife, and I told the council the truth. This is also the truth, Mara. You can't stay. Whatever's happening to the wards, whatever's waking up in your blade — staying won't save this valley. It'll just mean the valley dies with you in it, comfortable." She stepped back. "Go be caught somewhere worth catching you."

I hugged her. Briefly, hard, the way you close a book you may never finish.

Then I went to find Corvin, and the map, and the ember that burns without fuel, and whatever my grandmother started that I am apparently doomed to finish.

* * *

*Corvin*

I have been thrown out of better towns than this one, which is not a boast so much as a résumé. But Vael Hollow wins a certain distinction: it is the first place that decided to erase itself *at* me rather than merely expelling me.

We heard the council's decision before any rider reached us — or rather, we heard the absence of it. Mara noticed first, because she notices everything now, the way a tooth notices sweetness. "The mill wheel," she said, stopping dead on the path. "Listen."

I listened. Nothing. An hour ago the mill had been groaning through its endless argument with the stream. Now the stream ran silent past a wheel that turned without sound, like the world had been drawn by someone running low on ink.

"They're not waiting for sundown," she said. "They've begun the sealing early. Listen to the birds, Corvin."

The birds were there. I could see them — thrushes, a pair of crows arguing over the fence line. But the space where their sound should have been had gone smooth and blank, like a page scraped clean for rewriting.

"They're spending memory to deepen the wards," I said. "Small ones first. Sounds. Names of distant things. It spreads inward toward people. Toward *us*." I had seen the aftermath of a sealing once, in the guild archives — a village called Harrowmere, reduced to a paragraph and a smudge. The guild taught us that sealings were merciful. The guild taught us many useful lies.

"Then we run now," Mara said, "and we run north, and we don't stop until the forgetting runs out of valley."

"There's a problem with north."

"There's always a problem with north. That's what makes it north."

"The gray fields," I said, and unrolled my map across a fence rail, weighting the corners with stones. There — the wide pale band beyond the valley's edge, the territory no guild map acknowledged, which is how I learned it existed. Places erased don't vanish. They fade to gray and start eating their neighbors. "The country's edges are already dissolving. If the valley seals fully while we're crossing—"

"We'll forget which direction we were running."

"We'll forget we were running."

Mara looked at the map a long moment. Then she looked up, and the fear in her face settled into something harder and cleaner, and I thought: *there she is. The last swordspeaker, deciding to be the first something else.*

"Then we run faster than forgetting," she said.

Behind us, faint and getting fainter, the mill wheel turned its soundless turning, and the crows went on arguing silently over a fence that would soon have no name on either side of it. We ran.
