## 8. Flight Across the Gray Fields

### Mara

We left Vael Hollow before the council could finish deciding what to do about me, which I consider excellent time management on my part.

Corvin had a head start on panic. He'd been packing since midnight, folding his maps with the tenderness most people reserve for infants or bread. By the time I came down the hill with my grandmother's oath-blade wrapped in a shawl and her wolf-tooth sigil biting cold against my palm, he was standing at the north gate looking like a man about to apologize to an entire landscape.

"Any route in particular?" I asked.

"North," he said. "Always north. The country is dying from its edges inward—Sarnholt's borders were drawn like a knot, and if it unravels, it unravels toward the heart. We want to stay ahead of the unraveling."

"You make it sound very tidy."

"I'm a cartographer. Tidiness is my religion." He glanced at the blade under my arm, then away. "I'm sorry about your village."

"They haven't burned me at the stake yet. There's still time for sentiment."

He almost smiled. It was the first thing about him that looked real—the shadow at his heels lagged a half-step behind even his smiles, which meant every expression he wore arrived slightly after he did. I was going to have to get used to that, or go mad, and I'd been told by several people that I wasn't far enough along to justify the trip.

The gray fields began two miles past the last boundary stone.

I had heard of them the way you hear of weather from another country—abstractly, dismissively. Grandmother called them *the forgetting* and said it happened only to places nobody loved enough. She was wrong about the mechanism, I think now. But she wasn't wrong about what we found.

The wheat went first.

Not died—died has a color, a slump, a smell. This was different. The wheat simply stopped being *insisted upon*. One row stood gold and heavy-headed; the next row was the idea of wheat, a pale suggestion of stalks; the third row was nothing at all, not even soil, just a smooth gray absence that my eyes kept sliding off like water off oiled cloth.

"Don't look directly at it," Corvin said quietly. "It notices being noticed. That speeds it up."

"That is the least comforting sentence anyone has ever said to me."

"Good. Comfort makes people slow."

So I walked with my eyes fixed on the horizon instead—a horizon that, I realized within the hour, was also negotiable. Hills I could see clearly at midmorning were fainter by noon, their outlines gone soft as breath on glass. Behind us, the road we had walked was no longer there. Not washed out, not overgrown. *Unwritten.*

I checked three times. Corvin checked twice more, professionally, marking our backtrail on a scrap of paper with a pencil stub. His hand shook on the second check.

"It's faster than yesterday," he said. "When I crossed the Marches a month ago, the fading took days. Now it takes hours." He looked up at me, and there was something underneath the wry cartographer manner that I recognized because I'd worn it my whole life: fear wearing competence as a coat. "Mara. How fast can you walk?"

"How fast do you need?"

"Faster than that."

### Corvin

Here is what they don't tell you about exile: you spend years imagining the moment you stop running, and when it finally comes, it doesn't feel like arrival. It feels like being caught.

I'd mapped erased places for eleven years. The Guild called us Reclamation Surveyors, which was a lie so elegant it deserved framing. What we did was walk into country that treaties had unwritten and record what remained, so the Guild could decide how much forgetting was structurally sound. Sarnholt was the big one. A whole nation edited out a century ago—borders dissolved, names struck from registers, songs reworded, gravestones turned face-down. The treaty-masons had done thorough work. They always did.

Except the work hadn't held. Nothing made of lies holds forever; it just sags at different rates. And lately, all across the old Sarnholt interior, my instruments had begun screaming.

Which is why I was in Vael Hollow at all, following an ember that burned without fuel, and why I was now walking north beside the last swordspeaker while her homeland ate itself behind us.

She walked well, I'll give her that. Villagers walk badly—short steps, eyes down, trained by a lifetime of staying small inside wards. Mara Almeida walked like someone who had spent twenty-odd years practicing sword-forms she wasn't allowed to name, which is exactly what she'd spent them doing. Long stride, weight forward, always angled slightly toward whatever might come at her from the dark.

"The ember," she said, around midafternoon. "Show me again."

I opened the case. Inside, on a bed of ash, sat a coal-sized flame that had been burning continuously for—I had no idea how long. It consumed nothing. It gave heat but no smoke. When I'd first stolen—*acquired*, let's say *acquired*—it from a Guild vault, my supervisor told me it was a curiosity. Then it started pulling. Leaning, actually. Like a compass needle made of fire, always straining in the same direction.

North-northeast. Toward the heart.

Mara crouched over it, and I watched her face do something complicated. "It's warm," she said. "Why is it warm? Fire needs something to burn."

"So does a country," I said, before I could stop myself.

She looked at me.

"A country runs on belief," I said. "Names, mostly. You name a field, fence it, tax it, put it on a map—and it stays where you put it. Sarnholt was un-named. Every register, every deed, every song. The treaty didn't destroy anything. It just stopped telling the world the country existed, and the world, being agreeable, believed it." I closed the case. "But belief leaks. Somebody always remembers. And something in Sarnholt's heart apparently decided to keep insisting. The ember is a piece of that insistence. It's the last coal of a fire everyone agreed had gone out."

"And the gray fields?"

"Are the agreement winning."

She absorbed that in silence, the way she seemed to absorb everything—completely, and then filed it somewhere behind those gray eyes where I suspected very little ever got thrown away.

"My grandmother knew," she said finally. "Didn't she."

"I'd bet the map on it."

"She left me candle-trimming and ward-tending and a valley full of people who've never once wondered what's over the hills." Her jaw worked. "A hundred years of hiding, and she never told me we were *hiding something*."

"Would you have kept the secret, if she had?"

"Yes." No hesitation. Then, quieter: "That's what makes it unforgivable. I would have been good at it."

I didn't say anything. Some griefs you just walk alongside.

By dusk we reached the first farmhouse, or what had been one at lunchtime. The building was still there—stone doesn't forget as fast as wheat—but the fields around it were gray to the fence line, and the fences themselves were becoming theoretical. A dog watched us from the doorway. I want to be precise about this: it wasn't a ghost, and it wasn't starving. It was a dog whose *dogness* had been reduced to essentials. Brownish. Four legs, approximately. Watching us with eyes that had forgotten how to expect anything.

Mara fed it half our bread. The dog ate it, wagged something that used to be a tail, and followed us for a mile before the gray took it too—one moment trotting at her heel, the next moment a place where a dog had recently been considering being.

She didn't cry. She walked faster. I noted, in the professional corner of my mind that never shuts up, that this was the second-fastest walker I had ever surveyed with, and resolved to tell her so at some point when it wouldn't sound like an insult.

* * *

### Mara

We slept in shifts in a barn that remembered it was a barn only in patches—the roof solid over the north stalls, open sky over the south, as though the forgetting were eating its way through the structure course by course. I took first watch because I didn't trust myself to sleep, and because the stars were doing it again.

Going out. One constellation at a time, the way they had on festival eve, except now I could watch it happen in country that had no wards to blame. A bright star over the northeastern ridge dimmed, guttered, and became a hole in the sky. Then its neighbor.

"It's the same pattern," Corvin said from the dark. I hadn't heard him wake; his shadow probably covered for him. "The Guild has star-charts of Sarnholt from before the treaty. The constellations are going out oldest-first. As if the sky is unwriting itself top to bottom, matching the land."

"Stars don't need belief."

"Don't they?" He sat up. In the ember-light his face was all planes and tiredness. "Every star up there has a name somebody gave it. Sailors steered by them. Farmers planted by them. Priests swore by them. Maybe naming things is how you hold them up." He paused. "Maybe the whole world is heavier than we think, and everything we've ever called anything is holding up its little corner of it."

"That's either beautiful or horrifying."

"I find it's usually both. That's how you know it's true."

I should have slept. Instead I drew the oath-blade halfway from its wrappings and let the moonlight—or what moonlight remained—find the lettering on the flat. Words in no alphabet I knew, though they itched at the edge of recognition like a language I'd spoken before I had teeth.

*If you are reading this,* my grandmother's letter had begun, *the country has begun to die.* And further down, in handwriting gone cramped and hurried: *The words are not written anywhere, little wolf. They were never allowed to be. You will know them the way you know your own heartbeat—when the time is right, and not one moment before, and you will hate me for it, and you will be right to.*

I hated her a little right then, in the half-roofed barn, watching stars go out over a country that officially did not exist. I expected the hatred to be simple. It wasn't. Underneath it ran something worse: understanding. She had hidden me the way you cup a match against wind. She'd just never asked whether the match wanted to be saved or wanted to burn.

"Your turn," I said to Corvin, wrapping the blade away.

He grunted acknowledgment. As I lay down, I heard him murmuring to the ember—coordinates, maybe, or apologies. The light through my eyelids pulsed steady as a pulse.

### Corvin

Day three, the gray caught up with our shadows.

I want to be careful describing this, because Mara will read this account someday—she reads everything eventually, it's one of the more alarming things about her—and she'll accuse me of exaggerating. So: precisely. On the morning of the third day, my shadow began arriving late. Not the usual half-step. Minutes. I would raise my arm and the arm-shaped darkness would follow a slow count later, like a servant who'd stopped respecting me.

"It's the country," Mara said, watching it with her head tilted. "You're mapped to places that no longer are. Half of you is filed under addresses the gray has eaten."

"That's not how mapping works."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "But it's how *you* work, apparently."

I hated that she was probably right. The Guild had warned us—obliquely, in the way bureaucracies warn you about the thing they sent you into—that surveyors of erased territory sometimes came back *thin*. Not thin-bodied. Thin-edged. Men whose names slid off tavern ledgers, whose letters arrived already yellowed, who had to introduce themselves twice to their own families. The trade's occupational hazard wasn't death. It was being edited.

And here was the thought I hadn't said aloud to anyone, the thought that had gotten me exiled: if the gray was coming for me anyway, I would rather it came while I was carrying the one copy of the true map of Sarnholt than after I'd surrendered it. My guild brothers thought I'd burned it, per orders. I had kept it. Folded against my heart, next to the ember, two pieces of contraband: the country's shape and the country's fire.

Noon brought us to a river that was having second thoughts.

It still ran—that much water remembers—but its banks had gone vague, and where it met the gray fields the current simply *stopped mattering*, spreading into a sheet of dull silver that neither soaked nor flowed. Fish hung in the shallows like ornaments. On the far bank stood a milestone, and I swear on every map I've ever loved that the inscription was erasing itself while we watched—not weathering, not defacing. The letters were closing up, stone flowing smooth as wax, and beneath the dying words of some road-name I could just make out the older carving it had replaced:

**SARNHOLT — 12 LEAGUES TO THE HEART**

Mara read it upside down from where she crouched at the waterline. "Twelve leagues," she said. "Is that real?"

"It was real a hundred years ago." My voice did something embarrassing. I cleared my throat. "This stone was supposed to have been destroyed in the Reclamation. Every marker was. Someone buried this one face-down instead of breaking it." I looked up at her. "Somebody *loved* this country enough to cheat, Mara. All this time. There have always been cheaters."

She touched the stone the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. And I will record what happened next exactly, because it matters: the letters stopped closing. For ten seconds—counted, I counted—the carving held, sharp and black as the day it was cut. Then the gray resumed, gentle as snowfall, and the stone went on forgetting.

But it had waited, I think, a hundred years, just to be read once more.

"We cross here," Mara said, standing. "Before the ford goes."

"The ford isn't going. Fords don't—"

The ford went. Between one blink and the next, the shallow crossing became deep gray nothing, featureless as spilled milk, and the fish were ornaments in no water at all, and the river's *river-ness* drained away downstream ahead of the fading like a crowd exiting a theater.

Mara looked at me. I looked at Mara.

"Upstream," we said together, and ran.

* * *

### Mara

We ran until the land remembered itself again—two miles north, where the fading hadn't reached, a living river laughed at us from between honest banks, and we drank like animals and laughed like idiots and I felt, for one disgraceful minute, *happy*, which should have warned me.

Because that was the thing about the gray fields that no one had told me, the thing I understood only on the fourth day, sitting on a hilltop while Corvin marked our progress on paper that was itself beginning to fade at the edges: the countdown wasn't behind us. It wasn't chasing us. It was *us*. Every field that vanished was a field someone had once named, fenced, sung to, argued over, been born in. The country wasn't being eaten. It was being *unloved*, systematically, by a treaty that had taught the world to stop saying its name—and every league of gray was a thousand people who had woken up one morning a century ago unable to remember why their grandmother's songs made them cry.

My grandmother had been one of them. Had *chosen* to be one of them, or been chosen, and had carried the whole unremembered nation inside a valley small enough to hide behind wards, waiting a lifetime for a granddaughter with the right blood and the right words.

*I will hate me for it, and you will be right to,* she'd written. No, that wasn't it. The other way around. Her handwriting had been failing by the end. I'd read it forty times and each time the pronouns landed differently, and I was starting to suspect that was deliberate, that even her apology was a door with two sides.

"Mara." Corvin was standing, staring northeast, and his face had gone the color of the fields. "Come look at this. Quickly."

I came up beside him. Below, in the valley's throat where the ember pulled hardest, the gray fields ended—not gradually, as they had for four days, but in a clean line, like a tide mark. Beyond the line, the land blazed green and gold and stubbornly, defiantly *named*: hedgerows, lanes, a village with smoke rising from chimneys, orchards heavy with fruit.

And rising above the village, a hill that should not have been there. I knew every hill in these marches from Corvin's maps, and this one appeared on none of them. Its flank was split by a seam of light the color of starlight, narrow as a knife-cut, patient as a scar.

The ember in its case leaned so hard toward it that the flame bent horizontal, straining like a hound at the end of its rope.

"That's not on any map," Corvin whispered. "That hill is not on *any* map."

"No," I said. My hand had found the oath-blade's hilt without asking me. "I think it's the one place the maps were trying to protect."

Behind us, far to the south, the last star of the morning went out with a sound I felt in my teeth rather than heard—a soft, vast *closing*, like a book finishing itself.

The seam of light on the hillside flickered. Once. Twice.

Then it began, slowly, to widen.
