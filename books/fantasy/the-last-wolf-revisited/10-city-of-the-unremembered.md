## 10. City of the Unremembered

The city announced itself by smell before anything else—woodsmoke and old paper, the particular sweetness of beeswax candles burned in large quantities, which is a smell I had never encountered in Vael Hollow because my grandmother considered scented things a form of vanity and vanity a form of invitation.

"Smoke," Corvin said, stopping on the ridge beside me. He said it the way other men announce a landslide. "That means people. People mean questions."

"People mean soup," I said. My stomach had been making its opinions known for two days, and I was no longer in a position to overrule it.

He looked at me with that expression he wore when I failed to appreciate the gravity of something—the cartographer's squint, as if I were a coastline he suspected of being drawn wrong. "Mara. A city that isn't on any map. In a country that officially does not exist. Doesn't that trouble you?"

"Everything troubles me," I said. "I've simply decided to be troubled while walking toward it instead of away from it. It's more efficient."

But privately—and this is the trouble with first-person accounts; you learn all the cowardly things eventually—I will admit the city did trouble me. We crested the ridge at late afternoon, and there it sat in a bowl of hills like a coin at the bottom of a well: walls of gray stone, slate roofs gone green with moss, chimneys standing up against the darkening sky like fingers crossed behind someone's back. It was beautiful in the way forgotten things are beautiful, which is to say it was beautiful and knew nobody was looking.

And it wasn't on any map. I know because Corvin checked. He checked three times, unfolding his forbidden copy of Sarnholt over his knee until the wind threatened it and I threatened him, and each time the bowl of hills where the city sat showed nothing but the careful blankness of a surveyor who found nothing worth finding.

"There should be forty thousand people down there," he said quietly. "There are none. Officially."

"Officially," I agreed, "there's no me either. We're practically neighbors."

We walked down into the last light, and the gates were open, and nobody stopped us, which was somehow worse than if they had.

* * *

The streets smelled of tallow and rain and, underneath it, ink. That was the strangest part—not that the city existed, but what it existed *of*. Every doorway bore a lintel carved with names: not the names of the families inside, but names worn nearly smooth, older names, sanded down by generations of hands touching them as they passed through. I saw a woman pause at her door and press her thumb to the carving the way my grandmother used to cross herself before bed, out of habit so deep it had become anatomy.

"They're not decoration," Corvin murmured. He'd gone pale in the way he did around documents. "They're records. When a family loses its name—when it's struck from whatever registry exists here—they carve a stranger's name over their door instead. So the house remembers having been somebody."

"That's the saddest thing I've ever heard."

"You grew up in a valley that erased an entire country. You have no room to talk about sad."

"I have every room," I said. "I'm just choosing to stand in this room right now."

A boy of maybe ten fell into step beside us, uninvited, the way children do everywhere, which was the first genuinely comforting thing I'd seen since the stars went out. He studied Corvin's shadow, then mine, then frowned with professional disappointment.

"You both cast proper ones," he said. "Most visitors don't."

"We're not most visitors," I said.

"No," he agreed solemnly. "Visitors come from somewhere." And he peeled off down an alley, leaving that sentence sitting in the air between us like a dropped knife.

* * *

They took us to the palace—or what had been a palace once, and was now something between a library and a courthouse and a very large, very quiet argument. The hall we were led through was lined floor to ceiling with shelves, and the shelves held books, but not only books: bundles of letters, jars of wedding rings, boots, a child's wooden horse, a lock of hair sealed under glass. An archive of objects whose owners could not be found anywhere else.

"She collects them," our escort said. She was a broad woman with ink-stained fingers and the patient voice of someone who explains the same thing daily to strangers. "Things that belonged to the unremembered. When a name goes, the objects go strange. They start to fade. She keeps them here so they stay solid."

"And she keeps the names?" I asked.

Our escort stopped walking and turned to look at me properly for the first time. Her gaze went to my pack, where the oath-blade rode wrapped in oilcloth, and something moved across her face that I couldn't read—which frightened me more than anything readable would have.

"The queen keeps everything," she said. "Come."

* * *

The librarian-queen received us in a round chamber at the top of the tower, lit by a hundred candles and one window, through which I could see the sky going the color of dirty pewter. No stars yet. There hadn't been stars for eleven days, and I had learned to stop flinching at the absence, which I understood was its own kind of dying.

She was smaller than I expected. Old, but not frail—old the way a fortress is old, all weight settled low and load-bearing. She sat at a desk covered in ledger books, writing, and she did not stop writing when we entered. She finished her line, blotted it, set down her pen, and only then looked up.

Her eyes were the exact gray of the fields we'd crossed to get here.

"A swordspeaker," she said. Not a question.

"I don't—" I began.

"Don't insult us both, child. I watched you walk up my hill carrying a blade wrapped like a corpse and wearing a wolf-tooth sigil under your shirt where you think it's hidden. I have spent sixty years learning to read what people carry." She folded her hands. "Corvin Asch. Exiled from the Cartographers' Guild for the crime of accurate work. And Mara Almeida, of Vael Hollow. Except those aren't your names, are they? Those are the names the treaty left you. Like clothes someone else chose."

Beside me, Corvin made a small sound, the sound of a man watching his life get catalogued by a stranger in under a minute.

"How do you know all that?" I asked.

"Because knowing is the whole of my office." She rose, and I saw that she was taller seated than standing seemed to promise, or perhaps it was the room bending around her the way rooms bend around certain people. "This city has no name. Do you understand? Not secret—a secret implies someone is keeping it. It has *no name*. Every person in it was struck from the record of some nation that decided they had never existed. Deserters. Debtors. Heretics. Whole villages that stood in the way of a border. They came here because here, at least, the forgetting stops at the gate. Inside these walls, we keep what the world threw away." She gestured at her shelves, at the rings and the letters and the little wooden horse. "I am the keeper of the unwritten. And in sixty years, I have seen exactly one wolf-tooth sigil, and it was on a letter I have waited my whole life to deliver."

My heart did something complicated. "A letter?"

"In time. First, the matter of the toll." She came around the desk, and up close her gray eyes had flecks of black in them, like ink stirred into ash. "Nothing enters my collection without payment. That is not cruelty; it is arithmetic. Everything kept must be paid for in kind. You want shelter tonight. Food. And you want the thing you actually climbed my hill for, though you haven't admitted it even to yourselves—you want to know where the Door of Return is. Am I wrong?"

Neither of us answered. Being right can be worse than being wrong; it closes doors.

"Then the toll," she said, "is the blade."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened inside me in that moment, because honesty is the only thing I have left that nobody else has edited.

The oath-blade was the first object I ever loved. Before I knew what it was—an instrument of binding, a leash forged in the shape of a gift—I knew it as the thing Grandmother kept above the hearth, the thing she cleaned on the eve of every Quiet Festival while telling stories about women who fought wolves and won. When she died, it passed to me along with the candle stub and the locked chest, and I carried it across half a dead country believing it was proof of what I was.

Last heir. Last swordspeaker. Last of a nation that had been erased so thoroughly that its own descendants were raised as gardeners and farmers, told the wards were for weather.

And now an old woman in an unnamed city wanted to put it on a shelf next to a child's wooden horse.

"No," I said.

"Mara," Corvin said carefully, in the tone of a man doing arithmetic about doors and exits.

"No," I said again. "It's not mine to give. That's the whole point of it—it never belonged to any single one of us. It belongs to the oath. And the oath belongs to—" I stopped, because the sentence finished itself in my head in a way I didn't like: *the oath belongs to everyone it was sworn for, and everyone it was sworn for is dead, or scattered, or forgot they were ever anyone at all.*

The queen watched me struggle with it, and I'll say this for her: she didn't gloat.

"Child," she said gently, which from her sounded like a formal title, "do you know what I would do with your blade? Nothing. I would shelve it. I would keep it safe the way I keep everything—perfectly, uselessly, forever. It would never rust, never be stolen, never be swung in anger or grief or hope. Is that what you want for it?"

And there it was. The temptation. Not the food, not the walls, not even the answer she was holding like a candle in a dark room. The temptation was the shelf itself—the idea of a place where nothing you loved could ever be lost, because nothing you loved could ever be *used*. I thought of Vael Hollow, warded and warm and starless-safe, where my grandmother had hidden me my whole life the way you hide a letter you're afraid to send. Belonging without freedom. Safety without sky. I had already lived in that city; it was called home.

"No," I said, for the third time, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "But I'll make you a different offer."

The queen's eyebrows rose, an event that I gathered was roughly annual.

"When this is done—whatever 'this' turns out to be—I'll bring the blade back. Or what remains of it. Freely given, freely shelved. But it walks with me to the Door, because it's the key and I'm the lock, and separating us now would be like cutting a word out of a sentence and expecting the sentence to still mean something."

Silence. The hundred candles leaned in their flames, all in the same direction, though there was no draft.

Then the librarian-queen laughed—a short, dry sound, like a book closing.

"Sixty years," she said, "and no one has ever counter-offered with the truth. Very well, wolf-heir. Keep your blade. But you'll pay the toll another way, and you'll pay it tonight." She returned to her desk and drew a fresh sheet of paper toward her, and dipped her pen, and looked at me with those ash-and-ink eyes.

"Your name. Your true one—not the treaty-name, not the name your grandmother gave you to hide behind. The name underneath, the one the country would have called you if it had ever been allowed to call you anything at all. You'll speak it aloud, in this room, and I will write it down, and it will go on my shelf until the day it can be given back to you."

"And if I don't know it?" I asked. My mouth had gone dry. "If nobody ever taught it to me?"

The old woman smiled then, and it was not entirely a kind smile, but it was an honest one, which is rarer.

"Then, child," she said, "you and I are going to sit at this desk until dawn, and we are going to find it together. Because I hold the letter your grandmother wrote, and I have been waiting sixty years for someone with your face to walk up my hill and ask me the only question that matters."

She turned the paper toward me. On it, in a hand I recognized with a violence that went through my chest like cold water—the tight, slanted, stubborn hand that had labeled every jar and every seed packet in my grandmother's kitchen—was written a single line:

*To the keeper of names. If a girl with the wolf-tooth comes asking, tell her the truth:*

And beneath it, the ink had faded, or been deliberately blotted, and the rest was unreadable.

"What does it say?" I whispered. "What's the rest?"

"That," said the librarian-queen, dipping her pen, "is what the toll buys. Sit down, Mara Almeida. Tell me who you are. And we shall see whether your grandmother trusted you enough to finish her sentence."

Outside the window, the starless dark pressed against the glass like a face. And somewhere below us, in my pack by the door, the ember pulsed—once, twice—like a heart counting down to something.
