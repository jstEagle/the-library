## 12. Tamsin at the Gate

The council gave me three days and a horse named Bristle, which tells you everything you need to know about how seriously they expected me to take this mission. Three days to ride north, find Mara Almeida, and drag her home by the ear if necessary. Elder Hobb said it like he was asking me to fetch a stray goat, and I nodded like a woman fetching a goat, because that is what we do in Vael Hollow when the world cracks open: we nod, and we fetch things, and we do not look up.

I'd known Mara since we were six years old, when she pushed me into the millpond for calling her sword-forms "dancing." I had loved her for most of those years in the quiet way you love someone who is always looking at a horizon you can't see—steadily, uselessly, without much hope of being looked back at. Which is why it stung so precisely when I was the one who told the council where she'd gone.

Let's be clear about what happened, since I've had two days of riding to lie to myself about it and I'm tired of the exercise. I didn't betray her out of spite. I did it out of love, the stupid kind, the kind that thinks love means keeping someone small enough to survive. She stood in my kitchen with frost still on her cloak and told me the stars had gone dark over the valley and the border stones were cold as riverbed gravel, and she said it the way she says everything—plainly, like a person handing you something heavy and expecting you to hold it—and every word of it was true, and I reported her anyway.

Because if she was right, then everything was ending. And if she was wrong, she was a frightened woman running into the gray fields alone, and either way the only tool I had was the council. You go to war with the army you have. Mine was seven elders and a horse called Bristle.

So here I was, third morning out, riding north through farmland that was supposed to be farmland.

* * *

The first wrong thing was the fence posts. I noticed them around midmorning, past the Hollis farm, where the barley should have been shoulder-high by now. The posts were there. The wire was there. The barley was there, sort of—a suggestion of barley, the idea of it, green-gray stalks that didn't move when the wind moved. It was like walking past a house where everyone is pretending to be furniture. The wind went through that field and the field declined to comment.

I stopped Bristle and got down and touched one of the stalks. It felt like touching the memory of a plant. Not soft, not dry. Just absent in a way my fingers couldn't argue with.

"Right," I said aloud, because talking to myself has been a habit since childhood and Mara was never around to talk to instead. "That's fine. Everything's fine."

Everything was not fine. By noon the grayness had a name I refused to give it, and by late afternoon it had edges I could see moving.

The Hollow's outskirts fade gradually—that's how it's always been described, in the way nobody actually describes anything in our valley, because description requires admitting there's an outside—but from the ridge above the old shepherd tracks I could see it happening in real time. The fields nearest the valley were holding. Past them, color drained away in bands, green going sage, sage going ash, ash going nothing at all, and at the very edge of seeing, the land simply stopped being land. Not cliff. Not mist. Just a place where my eyes reported grass and my mind reported nothing and the disagreement made my teeth ache.

And it was eating inward. Slowly—I want to be honest, slowly, at the pace of a tide rather than a flood—but while I watched, a stand of elms that had been elms when I was a girl went from gray to less than gray. There was no sound. That was the worst part. A tree should get some kind of send-off. This was just subtraction, patient and total, like the country was a ledger and somebody upstairs had finally gotten around to balancing it.

Bristle refused to go past the second band of gray. Horses are smarter than councils; this has been established. I left him hobbled at the last honest fence post and walked on foot into the fading, because I am apparently also dumber than a horse when it matters.

The gray wasn't cold. I'd expected cold. It was worse—it was neutral. Temperature without opinion. My own footsteps sounded correct but arrived a half-beat late, and once I caught myself listening for an echo and getting none, and I understood with a lurch why Corvin the mapmaker traveled with his shadow lagging behind him. Out here, even your shadow starts negotiating its terms.

I walked until the sun sat low and orange behind me, throwing my shadow long ahead of me into the gray, and I'll tell you what I saw, because it's the reason for everything that came after.

I saw the edge of Vael Hollow, the actual edge, the hem of the place I had lived every one of my thirty-one years—and it was fraying. Threads of it lifting off into nothing. The orchard on the Kettleman holding, the pasture beyond it graying, the shepherd track beyond that already half-gone, the little stone gatepost where children scratched their initials—mine among them, a crooked **T** carved with Mara's knife when we were eleven—standing in gray that used to be the world.

My initials were going to be eaten. That was the thought that broke me open, standing in a field with no smell in it. Not the stars, not the wards, not the grand fate of hidden countries. A gatepost with a child's bad knife-work, and the certainty that it would not exist by winter.

Mara had seen all this days ago and tried to say it, and I had sent elders after her.

* * *

I caught up with them at dusk, and I use "caught up" loosely, because they weren't hard to find. They travel loud, the council's men, four of them with torches and rope and the particular confidence of people who have never been outside a ward in their lives. Dannel Ferro led them, Hobb's nephew, broad and decent and dull as a fence rail, and when he saw me he grinned like I'd brought sandwiches.

"Tamsin! Thank the Quiet. Council sent word—you're to join us. Four's not enough to bring her in if she fights."

"Mara won't fight you."

"She's gone strange, they say. Speaking words that aren't words. Split a fence to kindling with her bare blade, out past the stones." He said it comfortably, the way you report weather. Behind him, the other three laughed, and the laughter sounded thin out here, under a sky with no stars in it, and none of them looked up long enough to notice that.

They'd made camp in the last green, right at the border of the fading, and they'd pitched their backs to it. All four bedrolls faced the valley. Nobody wanted to sleep looking at the nothing. I took bread and sat near the fire and watched the gray line, maybe forty paces off now, and I timed it against the burn-down of my candle stub, because that's the kind of woman I am—I count things when I'm frightened, it's the only prayer I know.

Forty paces in the time it takes a thumb-width of candle to drown itself.

Dannel sat down beside me. "Strange country," he said, and shivered, and I realized he *had* looked, just once, and hadn't let himself look again. "You think it's true, what she said? About the wards?"

"I think," I said carefully, "that we should ask her before we put rope on her."

"That's the spirit." He clapped my shoulder. "We take her gentle. Hobb wants her questioned, not hurt. She's still Almeida's granddaughter, whatever's got into her."

*Whatever's got into her.* As if the truth were a fever. As if the whole country dying at its edges were something Mara had caught, like rain, like a cold, and if we wrapped her in wool and brought her home she'd sweat it out and we could all go back to the festival preparations and the candle-lighting and the beautiful, suffocating quiet.

I lay awake a long time that night watching the gray line come on, forty paces, then thirty-eight, then thirty-six, and somewhere in the small hours I stopped being angry at myself for reporting her and started being afraid of what I'd do next, because I could feel the two loyalties pulling in opposite directions like tectonic plates, and one of them was going to lose, and I already knew which.

Love isn't keeping someone safe. That's the lesson the gray fields taught me between midnight and dawn, in their patient voiceless way. Love is telling the truth while there's still time for it to matter. Everything else—the protecting, the fetching-home, the reporting—is just fear wearing love's coat.

* * *

We found her sign the next afternoon, or rather Corvin found us, though he didn't know it yet.

There were two sets of prints heading north through the last of the grass—one boot-heavy and human, one strange, long-strided, the print of a man whose weight lands oddly, like he's always half a step from where he seems to be. Beside them, every few hundred paces, the ground held marks I couldn't read: scorched crescents, as if something small and hot had rested there. Ember-marks. I remembered Mara, years ago, drunk on festival honey-wine, saying her grandmother kept a coal that never died, and how we'd laughed, and how she hadn't.

Dannel wanted to push hard and take them by nightfall. I argued for caution—we were deep in the fading now, the gray on three sides, the sky overhead gone the color of dirty pewter where the stars should have been wheeling—and Dannel, being decent, gave me the rear position, which meant I rode last, which meant that when I turned Bristle sideways and put him at a trot toward the western gray, nobody saw for almost a minute.

A minute is a long time when you're committing treason.

I heard him shouting behind me—Tamsin! TAM SIN, you mad woman—and then I heard something else underneath it, and that something else saved my life and damned my plan in the same instant: a low grinding hush, like a millstone turning underwater. The gray was moving faster here. Faster than my candle-timing, faster than anything. Ahead of me the fading came on like weather, grass lying down into grayness in long strokes, and Bristle screamed and planted all four feet, and I came off him badly and rolled, and when I got up he was bolting back south toward the torches and the voices and the valley, and I was alone on foot between the council's men and whatever was coming.

I ran west. Don't ask me why west; the prints had gone northwest and the ember-marks pointed like a compass rose, and when your country is being erased around you, you follow the hottest thing you can find.

The gray nearly had me twice. The second time I felt it—not cold, not pain, just a terrible polite *attention*, like something reading me to decide whether I was worth the ink—and then the attention slid off, and I stumbled out of the fading into ordinary twilight grass with my hands shaking so hard I had to sit on them.

And there was a fire. Small, wrong, fuelless, burning in a ring of scorched crescents exactly like the trail-marks, and beside it, wrapped in a cloak, a man with a lagging shadow and a face full of地图—no, I don't know why I said that, I mean a face full of maps, ink-stained fingers, eyes that catalogued me before I'd finished standing up.

"You're the friend," he said. "Tamsin. She talks about you when she sleeps."

"I'm the traitor," I said, because if we were doing truths tonight, we'd do all of them. "The council sent me to drag her home. There are four men behind me with rope, following our tracks, maybe an hour back, and the gray fields are moving faster than anyone knows, and the valley's outskirts will be inside the fading within days. Weeks if we're lucky. I came to warn her, and I nearly got eaten proving the warning was real, and I would like to sit down now."

Corvin studied me the way he probably studied coastlines—with patience and a certain professional grief. Then he moved aside and nodded at the fire.

"The ember doesn't lie to people who've been in the gray," he said. "It burns brighter for them. Sit. She's checking the snares. She'll want to hear this herself."

I sat. The fire warmed me in a way that had opinions, and my shadow, I noticed, crept back around to sit properly beneath me, and I could have wept at the simple decency of a shadow arriving on time.

Mara came out of the dark ten minutes later with two rabbits and a sword that hummed faintly, a note below hearing, like a struck glass remembering it was struck. She saw me and stopped dead, and everything we were to each other passed across her face in order—joy, suspicion, hurt, hope—before settling on the expression she wore best: the flat waiting look of a woman letting you hang yourself with your own rope.

"Tamsin."

"They sent me after you."

"I know. I saw your horse's prints yesterday, circling. You lost them on purpose?" 

"Today. On purpose today. Yesterday I was still lying to myself." I stood up, because some things you say standing. "Mara, I told the council where you went. Before you left, I told them. I want you to hear it from me and hate me for the right reason. And then I need you to hear the rest of it, because I walked the edge of the Hollow yesterday and I counted the gray against a candle stub and it is coming, it is coming for the gatepost with our names on it, and the elders are voting to seal the valley and forget the outside entirely, seal it and dim it and call it peace, and they sent four men with rope to bring you back to a funeral and call it a homecoming—"

"Tamsin." Her voice cracked across mine like a whip made of silk. "Breathe."

I breathed. The rabbits got spitted. Corvin fed the impossible fire a stick it did not need, ceremonially, the way you'd pour out a cup for the dead. And Mara sat down across from me, and her sword hummed between us, and she said, quietly, "Say the rest."

So I said the rest. About the fence posts that wouldn't answer the wind. About the elms going out like candles. About Dannel and his torches, an hour behind me at most, following tracks that led straight to this fire. About the vote—because I'd heard the shape of it before I rode out, Hobb's voice saying *the valley must be made whole by being made small*, and the terrible relieved nodding of seven old people choosing blindness together.

"They'll seal it," I finished. "Seal it and forget it. Our whole lives, Mara, filed away. And they'll do it kindly, they'll do it *lovingly*, and it will work, because kindness works, that's what frightens me—it's always worked on me."

Mara was quiet a long moment. The fire popped its unnecessary pop.

"You came to warn me," she said at last, "instead of capturing me. Knowing what it costs."

"It cost me a horse. He'll forgive me. He likes you better anyway."

Something moved through her face then that I hadn't seen since we were girls on the millpond bank—not forgiveness exactly, something rawer and better, the look of a person recognizing that the ground under her had one true stone in it after all. She reached across the fire and gripped my wrist, hard, the way you grip someone pulling you out of water.

"The Door of Return," she said. "Ashvane. The oath. We leave at first light for the shrine, and then—" She glanced at Corvin, at the map cases, at the north dark. "And then everything changes, one way or the other. You can still go back. If you catch them before—"

"Dannel can't catch his own hat in this light, and you know it." I pulled the council's sealed orders from my coat and fed them to the ember, which accepted them with what I swear was satisfaction. "Besides. Someone has to keep the record. Corvin maps the country, you speak it into existing—someone has to write down the truth about the people in it. Starting with the truth about me."

Corvin was already unrolling fresh paper, because of course he was. "First entry," he said, dipping his pen. "*Tamsin of the Hollow, who chose the hard truth over the kind lie, in the year the stars went out.*"

Behind us, far off in the pewter dark, a torch-flame winked along the ridge line. An hour behind, like I'd said. Maybe less. The gray fields ground softly onward in the west, patient as a tide, and the little fire that needed no fuel burned up straight and bright, pointing north.

Mara stood, and her sword sang its subterranean note, and she looked at the torchlight, and then at me, and smiled the millpond smile.

"Well," she said. "Let's give them something to write home about—or they would, if home were still going to exist. First light, friends. We have a door to open, and I intend to be rude about knocking."
