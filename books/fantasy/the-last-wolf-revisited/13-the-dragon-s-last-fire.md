## 13. The Dragon's Last Fire

The ember led us north for three days, and the whole time it behaved less like a flame and more like a hound with a scent.

It sat in the iron lantern Corvin carried—a coal no bigger than my thumbnail, glowing a deep, patient red, the color of a hearth at the moment just before you decide to go to bed. It never flickered. It never dimmed. It did not want food or air or anything a fire is supposed to want. It only pulled. When we walked wrong, it grew sullen, its light going brownish, like a sulking child. When we turned true north, toward the hills that weren't on any map because they hadn't existed long enough to be mapped or unmapped, it brightened until the lantern threw our shadows twenty feet ahead of us, as if it were eager.

"Dragons," Corvin said, on the second morning, "are famously stubborn. I'm told this one was worse than most."

"You're *told*." I shifted my pack strap where it had worn a groove into my shoulder. "You've said that twice now. Told by whom?"

"People who are now ash." He said it lightly, the way he said most terrible things, and kept walking.

That was the other thing about the gray fields: conversation filled them up. The land had gone the color of wet paper, featureless except for what it remembered—here a fence post that faded in and out like a bad tooth, there an orchard of trees that bore apples only when you looked directly at them. Behind us, I knew, Vael Hollow's outskirts were dissolving into that same gray, eating itself edge-first, and every hour I spent walking north was an hour the valley spent becoming nowhere. I tried not to do that arithmetic. I was bad at not doing it.

Tamsin had been gone two days, back down the south road with her warning delivered and her choice made, and I missed her with a specific, grinding ache—the ache of someone who has finally stopped being alone and then immediately left their only ally behind. Corvin was good company in the way a knife is good company: sharp, useful, occasionally drawing blood by accident.

So I talked to the ember instead.

"Don't judge me," I told it, when Corvin went ahead to test whether a river that might not exist would hold his weight. It glowed. Everything about it glowed, including, I was beginning to suspect, opinions. "You don't know what it's like, being the last of something. Everyone looking at you like you're a door they have to walk through."

The ember pulsed once, gently, and I chose to take it as sympathy rather than condescension.

On the third day the gray began to change.

It came on slowly, the way dusk does—you didn't see it happen, you only noticed that the world had gone from the color of nothing to the color of almost-something. Faint veins of black ran through the fields like ink dropped in water. Then stones. Then a road—not a built road but a *remembered* one, rising out of the ground in patches, cobbles surfacing like drowned things coming up for air. And the smell changed too. Ash and cold stone and, underneath it, something older: resin, smoke, the mineral smell of a cave that has held fire for a very long time.

Corvin stopped at the base of a hill that shouldn't exist. It shouldn't exist because I had grown up forty miles south of here, and there was no hill. There was a shallow dip in the moorland where sheep grazed and children were warned away from bogs. But the hill rose steep and dark before us, wearing pines like a mantle, and set into its face was a doorway.

Not a grand doorway. That was the thing that undid me, a little. After everything—after the wards failing, after the letter in Grandmother's hand beginning *If you are reading this, the country has begun to die*, after speaking a fence into splinters with words I'd never learned—I expected the keeper of Sarnholt's last door to be dramatic. Carved lintels. Ancient script. A threshold you had to approach on your knees.

Instead: a low stone arch, half-swallowed by the hillside, framed by two leaning pillars that some mason had cut a thousand years ago and some other mason had repaired a hundred years ago with visibly inferior mortar. Someone had hung a wind chime above it, once. Only one copper leaf remained.

"It looks like a garden shed," I said.

"It looks like a place people used," Corvin corrected quietly. "There's a difference."

The ember in the lantern flared so bright we both flinched. Then it began to move—actually move, straining against the iron mesh like a moth against glass, throwing itself toward the arch in little desperate surges of light.

"All right," I told it. "All right. We're going."

* * *

Inside, the shrine was warm.

That was the first surprise. Outside the air had the raw bite of northern autumn, but within the hill the warmth wrapped around us like a blanket someone had just gotten up from—as though the place had been occupied moments ago and had not yet noticed its guests were centuries late.

The passage sloped down and opened into a chamber the size of my grandmother's kitchen, which felt deliberately small, somehow. Intimate. The walls were black stone veined with gold, and every surface was dusted—no, *coated*—in ash. Ash drifted in the air itself, fine as flour, catching the light in slow spirals. And in the center of the floor, in a shallow bowl of volcanic glass, burned the source.

I had imagined, following that stubborn little coal across three days of dying country, that we were walking toward a dragon. Something with wings and teeth and a voice like rockslides. What waited in the bowl was smaller than the lantern-ember we'd carried. Smaller than my fist. A single flame the size of a walnut, burning a deep, exhausted orange, swaying slightly, the way a candle sways when the person carrying it is tired.

"That's it?" I heard myself say, and hated how it sounded. "That's the dragon?"

The flame flickered—and laughed.

*I am reduced,* said a voice that came from everywhere and nowhere and mostly from inside my own chest, a voice like a banked fire settling, like coals shifting in a grate three rooms away. *Do not measure me by my remainder, swordspeaker. Measure me by what I have outlasted.*

Corvin sank to one knee so fast he nearly overbalanced. "Ashvane. Ember-of-the-East-Fire. Last warden of the Door of Return." He recited it like a man unloading a weight he'd carried across a country. "My guild's oldest records name you. We thought you were legend."

*Your guild thought many convenient things.* The flame swayed. *Kneel if it comforts you. I stopped requiring kneeling four hundred years ago; it made my neck hurt, in the days when I had one.*

I stayed standing. Partly out of defiance. Mostly because I wasn't sure my knees would cooperate. "You're the keeper of the Door."

*I am what remains of him.* The light dimmed, then steadied, the way a person gathers themselves before hard news. *Once I was mountain-long. Once my breath drew the borders of Sarnholt and my sleeping body WAS its western wall. When the treaty came—the unwriting, the great editing—your first wolves swore themselves as the country's walls so that it could hide. Someone had to remain to keep the way back open. I volunteered.* A pause, dry as old paper. *Volunteering, I have learned, is how dragons end up the size of walnuts.*

"The Door," Corvin said. "Is it—"

*Behind me. Below. Where it has always been.* The flame turned—not literally, flames don't turn, but I felt its attention shift onto me like a physical weight, warm and enormous despite its size. *And it is why you have come. Do not insult us both by pretending otherwise, Mara Almeida. Your grandmother lit this path for you before you could walk. The oath-blade at your hip has been humming since you crossed the old border-stones. You carry the sigil of the wolf. You are the last heir of Sarnholt, and the Door opens to your voice or not at all.*

My hand had gone, without my permission, to the oath-blade. Through the leather of its wrapping I could feel it vibrating faintly, like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing. "Then tell me what to say," I said. "Tell me the words, and let's be done with it. The valley is dissolving. The fields behind us are gray all the way to the river. Whatever this costs, it costs less than losing everything."

The chamber went quiet. Even the drifting ash seemed to hang motionless.

*Ah,* said Ashvane at last, and there was something in the ancient voice I hadn't heard before—something old and tired and sorry. *There it is. The hunger to be finished. Your grandmother had it too, near the end.*

"What cost?" Corvin asked quietly. He had risen. His eyes moved between me and the flame like a man reading two documents at once. "What aren't you telling her?"

*I am telling her exactly,* said the dragon, *what she asked to know. Listen, then, both of you, and understand that I take no pleasure in it.*

The flame rose from the bowl. Just slightly—an inch of height it hadn't had before, fed, I think, by the act of speaking plainly.

*The Door of Return restores what was unwritten. Speak the truth-words willingly, and Sarnholt returns to the world entire: its borders redrawn, its cities raised from memory, its fields un-grayed, its people's descendants gathered home under its protection. The valley of Vael Hollow stands visible again. The stars come back. Every fading farmstead between here and the sea blooms green in a single night.*

Warmth flooded through me at the picture of it. Home, whole. The orchard bearing apples even when nobody watched.

*And,* Ashvane continued, without pausing, without mercy, *every descendant bound by the old oath is bound again. Completely. Permanently. The first wolves traded their freedom to become the walls of the hidden country. Their gift—the swordspeaking, the word-magic in your blood—is not a gift, Mara Almeida. It is a leash, and the collar end of it is fastened to the nation's survival. Renew the oath and you renew the binding: you, and your friend's children, and the farmer's daughter in the next valley who doesn't know she carries wolf-blood, and every soul descended from those who swore. They will be the walls again. They will serve again. They will die defending borders they never chose, generation upon generation, until someone else sits where you sit and faces the same choice with worse odds.*

The warmth in my chest curdled.

"My grandmother—" My voice came out strange. Flat. "She knew. She sent me here knowing that."

*Your grandmother loved you,* said Ashvane, *the way walls love: completely, and without ever asking the stone its opinion. She believed protection was worth any chain. Many did. It was the founding argument of our nation, in fact—it is why the first wolves swore. They were afraid. Fear builds excellent fortresses and terrible homes.*

I looked at my hands. The hands that had spoken a fence into splinters, that had shaken at the border stones, that had held the letter that began with the death of a country. All my life I had thought the silence in Vael Hollow was peace. All my life I had thought the wards were a wall keeping danger out, when all along they were a wall keeping *us* in—keeping the leash short, keeping the wolves tame, keeping the heir small and safe and ignorant until she was needed.

Needed as what? A key. A voice. A door-hinge for a nation that had traded a thousand souls' freedom for its own hiding.

"Mara." Corvin's voice, careful. He'd seen my face do whatever my face was doing. "Breathe."

"I am breathing." I was. Barely. "I just—my whole life. My whole life was the inside of a lock, and I thought it was a house."

*Now you understand the cost,* Ashvane said softly. *Open the Door, and Sarnholt lives—and everyone like you is collared forever. Refuse, and the country dies clean, and the last free wolf spends her life watching the gray eat the world she loves, knowing she could have saved it at a price she refused to pay. There is no door here that opens only one way, child. There never was. That is what oaths ARE.*

"And you've guarded that choice," Corvin said slowly, "for a thousand years."

*I have guarded the question,* the dragon said. *For a thousand years I have kept the question alive so that someone could finally answer it honestly. Your grandmother answered it in advance, on behalf of generations unborn. I did not stop her. I have regretted that since the day she walked out of this hill with victory in her eyes and chains in her hands.* The flame guttered, recovered. *I am very small now. I can hold the Door open perhaps once more—but only once, and not for long. So consider carefully, last heir. Consider what you are, and what you are not, and which of those you intend to save.*

Silence filled the shrine, thick as the ash.

I thought of Tamsin, choosing the hard truth over the easy loyalty. I thought of the librarian-queen, who collected names because names were doors. I thought of the fence I had splintered with words I never learned—words that had felt, in that terrifying golden moment, less like obedience than like *speech*. Like me.

Somewhere below us, beneath the floor of this warm ash-choked room, a seam of starlight waited in a hillside that shouldn't exist, holding its breath for a thousand years, waiting to find out what kind of answer it would get.

"Ashvane," I said. "Show me the Door."

The flame flared—and in the flare, for just a heartbeat, I saw reflected in the black glass bowl something that made my blood run cold: shapes at the mouth of the tunnel behind us. Tall figures. Moving without sound.

*They found you faster than I hoped,* the dragon said grimly. *Hunters of the Treaty. Come, then, last wolf. Choose quickly—or choose fighting.*
