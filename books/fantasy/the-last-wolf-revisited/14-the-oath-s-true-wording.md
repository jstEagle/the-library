## 14. The Oath's True Wording

I have been a mountain and I have been a match, and I will tell you honestly: the match is worse.

Dragons do not dream the way you dream, in fragments and borrowed faces. We remember in long unbroken reels, and when the body fails, the memory is all that's left burning. So while the girl Mara slept in my shrine of ash, and the cartographer Corvin bled quietly into his bandages beside her, I did what old dragons do. I went back to the beginning.

Come. Walk with me through it. Mind the embers; they bite.

* * *

It was autumn when the wolves came up out of the lowlands, nine hundred of them in their human skins, and the country they found was already dying.

You must understand what Sarnholt was before the treaty. It was not large — no empire, none of that tedious ambition — but it was *writable*. That sounds like nothing until you have watched a place lose the property. Rivers whose names could be spoken by strangers. Roads that appeared on maps drawn three kingdoms away. Children born in Sarnholt who could grow up and leave, and be remembered when they did. We take these things for granted the way fish take water for granted, which is to say, completely, until the day the water goes strange.

The treaty came because we were useful and therefore dangerous. The great powers of that age had learned what I had known for centuries from my perch on the high crags: that a small country with strong magic is either a shield or a wound, depending on where you stand. They could not conquer Sarnholt — its wards were woven too deep, its people too stubbornly themselves — so they did something more elegant. They negotiated it into silence. Unwritten. Unmapped. Struck from every ledger, every chronicle, every tongue that mattered. Its borders would still hold. Its people would still live. But the world outside would forget it had ever been, and the world inside would forget it had ever been anything else.

The terms required a binding agent. Something alive, willing, and permanent.

That is where the wolves come in, and where I come in, though I did not want the role. I was young then — young for a dragon, which is to say merely enormous — and I had made the mistake of loving Sarnholt openly. The treaty's architects needed a witness who could not die of old age and could not be bribed, because I owned everything worth bribing with. So they appointed me keeper of the Door of Return, and set me the task of teaching the first wolves the words.

Nine hundred came. Only thirteen swore.

I want that number remembered. Thirteen, out of nine hundred. History — what little history survived — has a way of inflating heroes, of making it sound as if the whole nation knelt together in noble consensus. It did not. Most of them looked at the price and walked away, and they were right to. The ones who stayed did not stay because they were braver. They stayed because of who they loved, and where those loved ones stood, and how thin the walls of a dying country can feel on an October night.

The eldest among them was a woman named Vaela. Yes. That Vaela. Your valley carries her name like a hand-me-down coat that no one remembers was ever tailored.

* * *

On the night of the swearing, I gave them the truth first. It was the only courtesy I had to offer, and I gave it fully, because a lie at the foundation of a thousand-year wall is a crack with patience.

"Understand what you are about to become," I told them, there on the hillside above the Door, under stars that still shone. "You will swear yourselves as walls. Not defenders of walls. Not keepers of walls. Walls. Your freedom will be the mortar. Every year you live bound, the country lives hidden. Every child you have will inherit the binding in their blood, whether they consent or not. There is no unswearing. There is no holiday from it. When you die, your death feeds the ward, and your children's deaths after you, unto the last generation."

Vaela asked the question I have been asked only once since, by a girl with her jaw and her stubbornness, a thousand years later, standing in my shrine smelling of woodsmoke and fury:

"And our gift? The swordspeaking? Is that ours?"

"No," I said. "It is the leash."

I watched that land on her like weather. She was a swordswoman of considerable reputation, and I could see her doing the arithmetic every proud fighter does — *my strength, my skill, mine* — and finding the sum suddenly held by someone else.

"The words your blood will carry," I said, "are the oath itself, wearing the costume of a talent. You will speak and things will obey, yes. Doors, blades, fences, weather, if you push far enough. And every act of speaking will tighten the binding one thread further. Your gift is not power over the world, Vaela. It is the world's power over you, dressed up so you would use it gladly."

Silence on the hillside. One of the thirteen, a young man called Bren of the Fenmarch — his line died out four hundred years ago, incidentally, and the ward did not care — said, "Then why does it feel like singing?"

"Because," I said, and here even a dragon's honesty has limits, "the most effective cages are built of things you love."

They swore anyway. All thirteen. I spoke the words and they spoke them back, each voice laying down a stone, and I felt the country close around itself like a fist, gentle and total. The stars did not go out that night — that horror was reserved for the treaty's final mechanism, the slow dimming that would signal the end — but they grew *distant*, somehow, as if seen through glass. The wolves walked down the hill as ordinary farmers and smiths and mothers, and Sarnholt vanished from the world's memory between one heartbeat and the next.

And I sat down on my crag to keep watch over a door that would open, by design, only twice: never, and at the end.

* * *

Here is what the histories — mine, the only history that lasted — do not tell you about the centuries that followed.

They tell you the wolves were honored. In the early generations, they were. The Oathbound wore the wolf-tooth sigil openly, and children were taught their names, and once a year the whole hidden country celebrated the Quiet Festival in their honor — a festival of silence, mind you, which should have been the first clue that gratitude had curdled into something else. Gratitude that requires silence is not gratitude. It is management.

By the third century, the sigil was worn privately. By the fifth, the sword-forms were practiced without being named aloud. By the eighth, the Oathbound were not honored ancestors but an embarrassing necessity, like a privy — essential, invisible, discussed by no one in polite company. The country forgot that anyone had ever chosen this. It remembered only that some families were *like that*: touched, strange, best kept at arm's length and at work.

And always, always, the gift. Passed down like a title nobody wanted, arriving in each generation in some child who could not help but speak to blades and hear them answer. Do you know what it does to a family, to watch for the signs in every grandchild? To hope — genuinely hope — that the child will be *ordinary*, because ordinariness means escape? The Oathbound loved their children the way besieged garrison soldiers love reinforcements: desperately, and with a private arithmetic of dread.

Vaela herself lived two hundred years, sustained by the oath she'd sworn, which she came to regard less as an honor than as a sentence with excellent benefits. Toward the end she climbed my crag one last time, old and straight-backed and furious, and said: "You could have refused to teach us."

"I could have," I agreed. "And the treaty's enforcers would have burned Sarnholt unwritten instead of written-hidden, and everyone you loved would have been scattered into forgetting with no door to come home through. I chose the cage with a lock. It seemed the better cruelty."

"It was," she said. "That's what makes me angry." And she laughed — Vaela laughed at everything, it was her worst quality and her salvation — and she said, "Keep the words true, dragon. Keep the Door honest. Someone in my line will come to you someday wanting to know exactly what we traded, and I want them told plainly. No glory. No gloss. Just the price, itemized."

So I have kept it. A thousand years, itemized. And now the bill has come due, and the customer is asleep in my ashes with soot on her cheek.

* * *

She woke near dawn, the way the exhausted do, all at once and unhappily.

Mara Almeida. Last swordspeaker, which is a title like *last survivor of a shipwreck* — technically accurate, emotionally obscene. She sat up in the gray light filtering through my shrine's broken roof, looked at the sleeping cartographer, looked at the coal of me flickering on my pedestal, and said, "You were talking in your sleep. Or smoking. It sounded like a very old man reciting a contract."

"I was remembering aloud," I said. "A privilege of the elderly. Sit. There are things you must hear before the Door, and I promised your ancestress I would deliver them plainly."

"Ancestress," she repeated. Of course she'd caught it. She catches everything; it is her least restful quality.

"Vaela," I said. "First of the thirteen. First of your line. The woman your valley is named for and your oath is patterned on and your gift descends from." I let the coal brighten, just enough to make the shadows lean. "Ask me what the gift is. You have been wondering since the fence."

Since the fence. Three days ago, outside the weakened wards, she had raised her grandmother's blade and spoken a word she'd never been taught, and a fencepost had come apart into splinters with a sound like applause. I had watched her face in that moment. Terror and exhilaration, braided. The classic expression of someone who has just discovered the family inheritance is haunted.

"What is it?" she said. Flat. Braced.

"It is the oath," I said. "Spoken small. Every word your blade hums is a clause of the original binding, echoing down the bloodline. You do not command the world, Mara. You *renew* it. Each time you speak, you re-lay a stone in the wall your ancestors built — willingly or not, knowingly or not. Your whole life, every time the sword-speech rose in you and felt like coming home, what you were feeling was the leash settling comfortably around your throat. It was designed to feel like that. Vaela was told as much before she swore, and she swore anyway, because the alternative was watching everyone she loved be forgotten."

She didn't move for a long time. Dawn came up properly, gilding the ash, touching Corvin's bandages. Somewhere outside, Tamsin — arrived the night before with frostbite and a changed heart, another story, hers to tell — was feeding the horses with the exaggerated calm of someone who has recently betrayed and been forgiven and doesn't trust either yet.

"My whole life," Mara said finally, "I thought the valley was keeping me safe from the world."

"The valley was keeping the world safe from forgetting you," I said. "And keeping you safe from choosing. Those are different safeties. Only one of them is yours."

"And the sword-forms? The ones Grandmother taught me in secret, that we couldn't name? Were those the leash too?"

"The forms are older than the oath," I said, and heard my own voice go soft, which for a dragon the size of a teacup flame is a considerable feat. "Vaela brought them up out of the lowlands. They were hers before she was the Wall's. Whatever you make of them — duty, dance, inheritance, art — that part at least was freely chosen once, by a woman with mud on her boots and a laugh like a gate hinge. The oath took everything else. It never managed to take that."

Something in her shoulders changed. Not relaxation — recalibration. I have watched a thousand years of Oathbound receive this truth, and they break along one of two lines: those who shatter, and those who sharpen. Her grandmother had shattered, quietly, over decades, into secrecy and locked chests. This one was sharpening. I could practically hear it.

"So the words I need for the Door," she said. "The truth-words. If my gift is the oath wearing a costume—"

"Then the words you were never taught are the ones underneath the costume," I finished. "Yes. The Door opens to the oath spoken *knowingly and willingly*. Not inherited. Not stumbled into. Chosen, with full knowledge of the price — which I am required to give you, itemized, per instruction of the first Wall herself."

"Give it to me now."

"You will restore Sarnholt entire. Every erased village, every scattered descendant, every unmapped road returned to the world's memory. And every living carrier of the blood — yourself, your friend Tamsin, infants in cradles who will never know a time before — will find the old binding *reaffirmed*. Walls again. Leashes again. Their gift returned to them as law rather than accident. You will save a country and collar its people in the same breath, and you will not be able to undo the second half without undoing the first."

The wind moved through the broken roof. Corvin stirred, groaned, did not wake — the guild's poison was thorough, and I had given him perhaps two days before the fever decided for him, which meant the Door could wait for no one's convenience.

Mara stood. She picked up her grandmother's blade, turned it once in the light — and I saw her look at it differently now, the way you look at a beloved dog after learning it was trained to hunt you.

"Ashvane," she said. "When Vaela swore — did she know her descendants would hate it?"

"She hoped they wouldn't need to know it at all," I said. "Hope was the mortar's other ingredient. It failed, as mortars do. But she also left an instruction, and I have kept it a thousand years, waiting for the last of her line to stand where you're standing."

"Which is?"

"*Tell her plainly,*" I quoted, "*and then get out of her way.*"

For the first time since she'd walked into my shrine smelling of woodsmoke and fury, Mara Almeida almost smiled. Then her eyes went past me, to the eastern wall of the shrine, where the ash on the stones had begun — very faintly, very wrongly — to glow.

Not my glow. I knew my own light the way I knew my own grief.

Someone was approaching the Door from the other side. And they were carrying fire that did not belong to any dragon I had ever mourned.
