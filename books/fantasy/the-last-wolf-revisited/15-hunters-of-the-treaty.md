## 15. Hunters of the Treaty

I want to tell you I heard them coming. That some wolf-blood instinct pricked up my ears, that Ashvane's ember flickered in warning, that Corvin's maps rustled with premonition. None of that happened. What actually happened was that Tamsin burned the porridge, and I was laughing—actually laughing—for the first time since the stars went out when the first arrow took the shrine door.

It buried itself in the wood with a sound like a knuckle rapping politely. Then a second one arrived less politely, and the door split down the middle.

"Everyone," said Ashvane, from his coal-sized perch on the altar of ash, "is about to have a very bad morning."

* * *

Let me back up. Three days we'd been in the shrine, and it had become, against every expectation I owned, almost domestic. The shrine sat in a fold of hills that Corvin swore did not exist on any surviving map—a hollow within the hollow of the world—and inside it was warm the way a banked hearth is warm, because the entire building was essentially a fireplace and its fuel was a dying dragon.

Ashvane was the size of a fist now. He had been a mountain once; you could see it in the way he held himself, the coiled memory of wings that would have blotted out valleys. Now he sat on his altar and complained about our cooking and told stories in a voice like gravel poured slowly, and I loved him the way you love a grandfather who is also, technically, an incendiary weapon.

Tamsin had caught up to us two days after the gray fields nearly ate her, and she'd spent the whole time apologizing with labor. She chopped wood for a dragon who didn't eat wood. She mended Corvin's coat, which needed mending the way the sea needs water. She cooked, because she'd always cooked, because in Vael Hollow love was expressed through food and worry and never, ever said aloud.

So there we were. Porridge. Laughter. My guard so far down it had achieved archaeology.

The third arrow came through the ruined door and pinned Corvin's sleeve to the table, pinning the map beneath it.

I stopped laughing.

"Treaty enforcers," Ashvane said, and for the first time his ember guttered—not from weakness but from something I would learn, much later, to name as grief. "They've come for all three of us. Well. Two of us and the map."

"The map?" Corvin was already pulling the arrow free, already rolling the map with hands that shook only slightly, which for Corvin constituted heroism.

"They cannot allow Sarnholt to be drawn again. A country exists twice: once in its land, once in its record. Kill either and you can argue it never was." His ember pulsed. "And they cannot allow the last heir to reach the Door speaking willingly. An oath renewed by force is still an oath. An heir killed before the Door—" he paused— "the country dies quiet. No witnesses. No blame."

"And you?"

"I am the last fire of the old world. They will put me out like a lamp."

* * *

There were six of them. I know because I counted, and then I counted again while running, because counting kept me from screaming.

They wore no colors. That was the first thing that frightened me properly—the absence of livery, of sigil, of anything that could be traced back to a crown or guild or god. Their armor was the gray of the fields themselves, matte and light-eating, and their faces were wrapped to the eyes. They moved like men who had done this many times and expected to do it forever. Not hatred. Maintenance. We were not enemies to them. We were weeds.

The first one came through the doorway and I did the thing my grandmother had forbidden me to do in the valley, which was everything.

My blade came off my back and it was already singing—that hum I'd first heard outside the wards, the words-under-words I'd never been taught but somehow knew, the way you know your own heartbeat. The swordspeaker's gift wasn't magic laid over steel. It was older than that. The blade remembered what it was for, and when I let it, it told me.

I spoke the first form. The one I'd practiced ten thousand times as dance, as exercise, as nothing. In Vael Hollow it was called *Still Water*. Outside the wards, under a sky with holes in it, with a treaty hunter bearing down on me, it turned out Still Water meant *be unmoved and move all things*, and the man's charge broke around me like a river around a stone, and my sword touched his shoulder and said a word, and he slept. Just slept, crumpled mid-stride, breathing.

I stared at him. Some traitorous part of my brain noted: *good, nonlethal, grandmother would approve*, and then another hunter shot at my face.

Ducking is also a form. Nobody writes that down.

* * *

"Back way!" Corvin shouted, shoving the map into his coat. "Shrines always have back ways, the old builders were cowards about exits—"

"That's *wisdom*," Tamsin snapped, hauling me bodily toward the rear passage, because apparently in her version of love you got dragged.

Ashvane flared. For one glorious half-second the coal became a bonfire, and the nearest wall of the shrine glowed cherry-red, and the two hunters coming through the front thought better of their careers. Then the flare collapsed inward and he was small again, small and furious about it.

"I can't hold that more than once a day," he rasped. "Go. Take my ember."

"What?"

He spat a spark onto the floor, where it hung in the air like a patient star. "The Door answers to the oath, but the Door only opens where the old fire stands. Carry me. And if they take me—" his voice went dry, almost amused, "—well. I'm mostly ash anyway. Don't let them take the ember."

I picked it up. It should have burned me. Instead it settled into my palm like a bird deciding to stay, warm and weightless and trusting, and I felt something in my chest rearrange itself around the responsibility.

We ran out the back of a dragon's grave carrying his heart, and behind us the hunters came on, unhurried as weather.

* * *

The hill country behind the shrine was the kind that fights you—scree and gorse and sudden drops that appear only when you're committing your weight. Corvin knew it anyway, because Corvin knew every place that had been erased, and erased places keep their bones even when nobody remembers the flesh.

"There's a ravine," he panted, "half a mile north. If we cross before they—"

An arrow answered him, thudding into a gorse bush beside his head. The gorse, notably, did not burn. Even the plants here had learned discretion.

We ran. I ran badly, I'll admit it—sword in one hand, dragon-ember cupped in the other, Tamsin ahead of me moving with the terrible competence of someone raised on farm work and guilt. Behind us the six had become audible: boots on stone, unhurried. They weren't sprinting. Why would they? They knew the land. They knew we were going where we had to go. Every road led to the Door, and the Door was a trap with one exit.

That was the arithmetic that kept me running when my lungs filed formal complaints. Not hope. Arithmetic.

The ravine opened below us like a wound. Thirty feet across, maybe more, fog pooling in it so thick you couldn't see the bottom, which meant there probably wasn't one worth discussing.

"Bridge?" Tamsin gasped.

"There was one," Corvin said, looking at the absence where one should be. "On my map."

"On your map," I said. "Corvin. Your map shows doors in walls. Does it show bridges in air?"

"It shows what *was*," he said, and then he looked at me, and I saw the idea arrive in him the same moment it arrived in me, and we both hated it equally.

"No," said Tamsin.

"Swordspeaking isn't just destruction," I said. My voice sounded calmer than I felt, which I've decided counts as a skill. "When I spoke the fence apart, it was because the fence had forgotten how to hold together. But a bridge that *was*—" I looked at the fog, at the shape of a crossing that existed in ink and memory and nowhere else. "Maybe things that were can be again. Maybe that's the whole point of me."

"You've spoken one word successfully," Corvin said. "Once. At a fence. Fences are simpler than physics."

"Thank you for your confidence."

"Mara." He caught my arm. Under the grime and terror his eyes were steady. "If you speak wrong over that drop, you don't fall alone. You fall with us."

I looked at the ember in my palm. Warm. Patient. Trusting me, the way Ashvane trusted me, the way nobody in Vael Hollow had ever trusted me with anything heavier than festival candles.

"Then stand close," I said, "and believe hard."

* * *

I don't remember choosing the words. That's the truth of the gift nobody warns you about: it isn't casting, it's listening. I stood at the edge of the ravine and I stopped being Mara Almeida, last swordspeaker, and became instead a pair of ears in a body, and somewhere underneath the wind and my own hammering heart there was a sound like a bridge remembering itself—rope and stone and the particular creak of planks that have carried a thousand years of feet.

I said what I heard.

The fog thickened, gathered, *arranged*. Planks surfaced in it like drowned things rising, rope unspooled from nothing, and a bridge lay across the ravine—translucent, trembling, real enough that when Tamsin stepped on it with the blind faith of someone who has decided to be angry later, it held.

We crossed. Behind us the hunters reached the edge and stopped, and for the first time I heard one of them speak.

"Last heir." The voice was flat, neither cruel nor kind. A voice reading a ledger. "The Treaty thanks you for confirming the gift survives. It simplifies the paperwork."

Then, worse: "Your grandmother bargained for a century of quiet. The quiet ends tonight. Come home, girl, and die in a bed. Or run, and die tired."

I crossed the bridge and did not answer, because I had finally learned that some conversations are traps, and because my hands were shaking too hard for wit.

* * *

We made four miles before they took Corvin.

Not with arrows—they'd stopped wasting arrows, having learned I could sleep their archers mid-draw. They came at dusk through the bracken, silent as debt, and it was Tamsin who screamed the warning, and it was Corvin who did the thing I will carry until I die.

The map. They wanted the map, and Ashvane's ember, and me dead or bound. Six hunters, converging, and in the middle of it all Corvin—who had never in his life won a fight, who mapped battles rather than fought them—took the rolled vellum from his coat and held it above his head like a torch and stepped *between* me and the blades.

"Took me nine years to find this country," he shouted, and it was the loudest thing I'd ever heard him say. "You don't get to unwrit—"

A sword took him through the side. Not a duel. Nothing so honest. A passing stroke, the way you'd swat a moth, except the moth was holding the last map of a nation and the hand that struck him hesitated—one heartbeat, maybe two—because even maintenance-men hesitate when the thing bleeding in front of them is a person who chose this.

I woke three of them with a word. I put the fourth's blade to sleep in its sheath—I didn't know steel could sleep, the sword taught me right then, mid-horror. Tamsin broke a branch over the fifth's head with a war cry that was half fury and half my name.

The sixth reached for the fallen map.

And Ashvane—carried in my satchel, small as a coal, old as shame—burned. Not the polite flare of the shrine. Everything. He poured himself out through the satchel seams in a lance of white-gold fire that took the sixth hunter's arm to the bone and lit the whole hillside like judgment, and in that light I gathered Corvin up, and the map, and ran.

* * *

We didn't stop until the moon rose. By then the hunters had withdrawn—or regrouped; with those people I suspect the words are synonyms—and I knelt in the roots of an oak that shouldn't have existed in erased country and pressed my hands against Corvin's side and felt the warmth leaving him like tide.

"It's shallow," he said. "Probably."

"Probably."

"I've read accounts. People survive these. Occasionally. In very dull books."

"Stop talking."

"Can't. If I stop talking I'll think about how much it hurts, and then I'll be rude about it."

His blood was on my hands, on the map's edge, soaking into the inked coastline of a country that officially did not exist. Tamsin tore her skirt into bandages with the efficiency of long practice—farm girls know wounds the way priests know prayer—and packed the gash while Corvin made small jokes in a voice growing smaller.

Above us the sky stayed dark. Stars still missing. The moon a coin with the face worn off.

"He saved the map," Tamsin whispered. "He could have run. He stood there like an idiot and saved the map."

"He's a cartographer," I managed. "Places are people to him. I think—he'd say it the other way round."

Corvin's hand found mine, blood-slick, grip shockingly strong. "Mara. Listen. If they take me tomorrow, or the day after—you go on. The Door doesn't need me. It needs the oath and the fire and *you*." His breath whistled. "I was sent to erase the last map. Let me be the reason it survives instead. That's a fair trade for a life."

"It's a terrible trade," I said. "I reject it. Find a better offer."

For a moment he laughed, and then coughed, and then was quiet, and the quiet was worse than the coughing, and I sat in the dark holding a dying friend, a sleeping dragon's heart, and the last map of everywhere, and understood at last what my grandmother must have known every single day of her hidden life:

There is no retreat from this. There never was. There is only forward, to a door in a hillside, with hunters at our backs and the country's whole soul in my arms—

—and then, far off in the black hills, a horn sounded. Once. Low. Answering.

Ashvane's ember pulsed in my palm, and for the first time since I'd carried it, it felt afraid.
