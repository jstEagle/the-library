## 16. What I Am Not

Corvin slept the way a man sleeps when his body has been overruled—badly, in fragments, with his brow furrowed as though even unconsciousness were an argument he was losing.

I sat beside him through the long gray afternoon, in the hollow behind the shrine where Ashvane's ash had drifted into something soft as snowfall, and I did what my grandmother would have called wasting time and what I was beginning to suspect was the only honest work left to me. I thought about who I was.

Or rather—who I wasn't. Because that list, at least, had gotten longer lately.

I was not, for instance, the last swordspeaker anymore. That title had been dying for days, ever since my blade first hummed against my palm outside the wards, and it finished dying somewhere on the road north, when I spoke a fence into splinters and laughed like a girl who'd never been taught to be afraid of her own voice. Titles are strange things. You can carry one your whole life without noticing its weight, and then set it down for ten minutes and find you cannot remember how to pick it up.

I was not, either, Mara Almeida of Vael Hollow. The valley still existed—I could feel it behind me like a hand between my shoulder blades, faint and cooling—but the *Mara* that valley made did not exist outside it. That Mara rose before dawn to check the border stones. That Mara kept her gift folded up small as a funeral shroud and never named her sword-forms aloud. That Mara had been built, plank by careful plank, by a woman who loved me enough to bury me alive and call it keeping me safe.

Grandmother. Even now I couldn't decide whether to be furious with her or grateful, and I suspected the truth was that both feelings lived in the same house and shared a bed and lied to each other constantly.

* * *

The ember sat in its little iron lamp between us, burning without fuel, patient as a debt. Corvin had wrapped his coat around himself even in his sleep, and beneath the coat the wound in his side rose and fell with each breath, slow and shallow, each one costing him more than he'd admit. The treaty hunters' blade had gone deep. Deeper than I liked to think about while there was nothing I could do but wait and boil water and change dressings with hands that wanted, badly, to do something louder.

Swordspeaking doesn't heal, it turns out. It unmakes and remakes, breaks and binds, but flesh is stubbornly, mercifully ordinary. So: water, cloth, patience. The humblest magic I knew, and the only kind I'd ever learned without being taught.

His map lay rolled against the wall of the hollow, the one copy of Sarnholt that existed anywhere in the world. He'd taken a blade meant for it—or meant for the door it pointed to—and I kept looking at him and then at the map and back again, trying to understand a man who'd spent his whole career erasing places and then bled for one.

"You're staring," he said, without opening his eyes.

"You're supposed to be asleep."

"I'm resting strategically." His eyes opened, gray and tired and still faintly amused, which seemed to me a private miracle. "There's a difference."

"Strategically rest, then."

He almost smiled. Then the smile cost him something, and he stopped, and I busied myself with the dressing so he wouldn't see my face doing whatever it was doing. Grief does that. It ambushes you sideways, in the middle of a joke, and suddenly you're pressing linen to a friend's ribs and thinking about every person who ever pressed linen to yours.

"Tell me something," I said, when I'd tied off the bandage and could trust my voice. "When your guild erased a place—when they took it off the maps—what happened to the people?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer.

"They didn't die," he said finally. "That's the terrible part. They just... thinned. Ask anyone from an unwritten town what their mother's name was, and they'd look at you like you'd asked them to describe a color that doesn't exist. The name would be right there, on the tip of the tongue, and then gone. People don't need to be killed. They just need to be *unmentioned* long enough."

"And you mapped them anyway."

"I drew doors on walls," he said. "It was the only mercy my trade had."

* * *

Night came down over the shrine like a held breath. Ashvane's coal-flame guttered in the distance, orange and small, and I found I couldn't look at it too long. A dragon reduced to a spark, keeper of a door, waiting a hundred years for someone to come ask him the question I was now carrying around like a stone in my chest:

*Do I open it?*

Because here was the arithmetic nobody had warned me about. If I spoke the old words at the Door of Return, Sarnholt would live again—the fields would green, the cities would unforget themselves, the scattered people would wake up remembering. And every descendant of the first wolves, every soul bound under that ancient oath, would be bound again. Me. Tamsin. Every child not yet born in Vael Hollow. Walls forever. Safety forever. Smallness forever, dressed up in ceremony and called duty.

And if I dissolved it—

I didn't know what happened then. That was the honest terror of it. Unbinding might mean freedom. It might also mean the country died for true, all at once, and everyone who'd ever been a wolf became no one, permanently, irreversibly.

Renewal was a cage I knew. Release was a dark room I'd have to walk into blind.

I turned the blade over in my lap—the oath-blade, grandmother's letter still folded in my pocket like a splinter I hadn't worked out yet—and I tried, very carefully, to imagine myself without any of it.

Without the oath. Without Vael Hollow. Without the word *last* stitched onto everything I touched.

* * *

Here is what I found, sitting in the ash-snow with a sleeping man and a dying dragon and a map of a country that officially did not exist:

If you took away the oath, there was still a woman who checked border stones because she liked the way dawn came up gray over the hills. Take away the valley, and there was still a woman who boiled water cleanly and changed bandages gently and noticed, without being told, when a friend's breathing went shallow. Take away the sword-forms—

No. Not those. I tried to give those up in the thought-experiment and found I couldn't. But maybe that was the discovery, actually. Maybe the forms weren't the leash. Maybe they were mine before they were the oath's—a way of moving, a way of paying attention, a grammar my body spoke before anyone told it what sentences it was allowed to say. Grandmother used them to bind me. That didn't make them chains. A knife can be a cage-bar or a door-latch depending on the hand.

*Last swordspeaker.* What a small box that phrase was. Last implied ending. Implied that the story closed with me, that I was the period at the end of a thousand-year sentence. And I had worn it like an honor because no one ever told me I could wear it like a weight instead.

But if I unbound the oath—if I spoke release instead of renewal—then maybe I wouldn't be the last of anything. Maybe I'd be the *first*. First free wolf. First woman to hold these forms without them holding her back. First to say: this is who I am, and I chose it, this morning, and I may choose differently tomorrow, and that choosing is the whole point.

It sounded arrogant, thinking it. It sounded, also, like air.

"Mara." Corvin's voice, thin but clear. "You're talking to yourself."

"I'm interrogating myself. There's a difference."

"Ah." He closed his eyes again. "Carry on. Don't execute yourself; you'll want the prisoner later."

I laughed, quietly, so as not to pull his wound, and the laugh felt strange in my chest, unarmored. Somewhere in the shrine, the ember pulsed once, twice, like a heart deciding whether to keep going. Like it was listening.

And far off across the darkened fields, toward the south and the valley I could no longer feel behind me, I heard—or imagined I heard, though Corvin's head lifted too, and his eyes went sharp with something that was not imagination—a sound like many boots, moving in step, coming north along the road we'd left broken behind us.

The hunters hadn't finished their work.

Neither, I realized, standing to fetch my blade, had I.
