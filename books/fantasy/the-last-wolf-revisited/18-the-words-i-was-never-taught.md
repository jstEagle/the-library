## 18. The Words I Was Never Taught

The Door did not open so much as admit me, the way a wound admits a splinter—reluctantly, and only because something on the other side wanted to know what I was made of.

I stepped through the seam of starlight expecting a hillside's interior: dirt, roots, the smell of cold stone. Instead there was Vael Hollow.

Not the real one. The real one was behind me, its wards falling like slow snow while Tamsin wept at the threshold and Corvin bled through his bandages and Ashvane burned himself down to nothing to hold this door open. That valley was behind me. This one had been waiting for me my entire life.

It was perfect. That was the first thing wrong with it.

The barley stood gold in a light that came from no particular sky. The forge smoke rose straight as drawn thread. Somewhere a dog barked twice and stopped, because even the dog in this place knew its lines. And every face that turned toward me as I walked the lane wore an expression of mild, untroubled welcome—the faces of people who had never once needed to decide anything difficult.

"There you are," said my grandmother.

She sat on her porch with shelled peas in her lap and her hands folded over them, exactly the way she'd looked on the last ordinary evening before she died. Not the death-day version, not the candle-guttering blue version, not the woman whose letter began *If you are reading this, the country has begun to die.* This was grandmother as I'd loved her best: dry-mouthed, sharp-eyed, smelling of woodsmoke and pennyroyal.

"You're dead," I told her. "Or you're not her. One of those."

"Both things can be true," she said, and shelled a pea with her thumbnail. "Sit down, girl. You've been walking since the border stones."

And gods help me, my legs ached in exactly the way they would ache after walking since the border stones, which was a detail no illusion should have known, unless it wasn't guessing but reading—plucking the ache straight out of me and wearing it like a borrowed coat.

I didn't sit.

* * *

The lane stretched ahead of me, and I walked it because standing still felt like agreeing to something. Behind me the porch and the peas receded without hurry. Ahead, the village assembled itself out of golden light: the smithy with its familiar ring, the well with its chipped rim, the elder's hall where the council had voted to seal us all into forgetting. Everything was where it should be. Everything was *slightly too* where it should be, the way a room tidied by someone who doesn't live in it is tidy.

The stars were out overhead. All of them. Every constellation I'd watched vanish constellation by constellation on the eve of the Quiet Festival hung whole and burning, arranged more beautifully than the sky had ever managed on its own. Someone had remembered them better than the sky did.

"Mara." Tamsin's voice, from the well. She stood there with two buckets and her sleeves rolled, and she was not the Tamsin who'd betrayed me to the council, nor the Tamsin who'd ridden north to warn me. She was twelve years old, gap-toothed, the summer before everything got complicated. "You're back early. We haven't even lit the lanterns."

"I'm not staying," I said.

"You always say that." She laughed, and the laugh was perfect, and that was the second thing wrong. Tamsin's real laugh had a crack in it, a little hitch where a winter cough had scarred her throat when we were nine. This laugh had been sanded smooth. Whoever built this place had taken my memories and polished off everything inconvenient.

That's how you test an illusion, Corvin had told me once, back when he was still mostly lies and maps. Cartographers' trick. Look for what's missing. Forgeries are always smoother than originals.

I put my hand against the well's stone rim. Cold. Real-feeling cold. But underneath it, if I pressed my palm flat and listened with the part of me that had spoken a fence into splinters, there was nothing. No grain. No history. Stone that had never been rained on.

"Where is he?" I asked aloud, to whoever was listening. "The first wolf. I know this isn't your shape. Come argue with me yourself."

* * *

The village thinned around me like breath fading from glass. The golden light dimmed to gray, then to a deeper gray, and I stood in a place that was neither the false valley nor any true one—a long dark with a floor of packed earth and a ceiling of faint moving light, like the underside of a frozen river.

She came out of the dark the way weather comes.

Tall—not tall the way a person is tall but tall the way a pine is tall, vertical on purpose—and wrapped in a mantle of gray fur that might have been a cloak or might have been the last visible remnant of the wolf she'd been before the oath folded her into a woman-shape. Her eyes were amber and old and unbearably tired, and when she looked at me I understood that I was being read the way Corvin read maps: borders noted, rivers traced, blank spaces marked *here be dragons.*

"You are the last," she said. Her voice had a growl under it, worn nearly smooth by a thousand years of use.

"Allegedly." My voice cracked on it. Wry is easy when your knees aren't shaking. I planted my feet anyway. "Mara Almeida. Swordspeaker, apparently. Granddaughter of a liar. You'd be the wolf who started all this."

"I am the wolf who *ended* myself," she said, "so that others could begin. There is a difference, and you will need to learn it tonight."

"So everyone keeps telling me. Ashvane gave his speech about cost. The librarian-queen gave hers about names. Even Corvin—" I stopped, because saying his name in this place felt like handing someone a knife and showing them where I keep it soft.

"He is not here," the first wolf agreed. "Nothing you love is here. Only what you could have had. Which is heavier, in the end. Love weighs nothing until you set it down."

"I'm not setting anything down."

"No?" Something moved in her face that was almost a smile and mostly sorrow. "Then let us find out. It is the only question the Door knows how to ask, and it has waited a thousand years to ask it properly."

She raised one hand—long-fingered, human now, but with shadows of claws in the gesture—and the dark peeled open like a page turning.

* * *

The life she showed me was small, and warm, and it fit me like a glove.

I lived in the cottage on Forge Lane. I'd always lived in the cottage on Forge Lane. Grandmother had died peacefully two winters ago, in her bed, having never once lied to me about anything, and I'd grieved cleanly and planted her rowan tree and gotten on with it. The wards still held. The stars still shone—our stars, the modest handful the wards let through, filtered and safe. On festival nights the whole valley turned out and danced the sword-forms as dances, because that's all they'd ever been, patterns for celebration, and I led them because I was good at it and everyone clapped.

There was a boy—I wouldn't call him a man—who mended wheels at the smithy and had a crooked smile and asked me, in this life, to marry him in the spring. In this life I said yes without my stomach dropping. In this life I hadn't learned yet what my stomach knew.

I woke each morning to bread baking and went to sleep each night to rain on the roof, and no one had ever asked me to carry a blade north through dying fields, and no one named Corvin had ever bled onto a map for me, and no friend had ever had to choose between loving me and telling me the truth, because in this life those things had never come apart. The oath held everything together like stitches hold skin. It hurt nowhere and healed nothing and I was safe inside it, small inside it, forever.

"This is what renewal looks like," the first wolf said, walking beside me through a market day that smelled unbearably of apples. "This is what you buy when you speak the old words at the Door. Sarnholt returns. The country lives again. And every descendant—including you, including the wheelwright's children you'll have—is bound to it, walled into it, kept safe from the world and from yourselves. Your gift stays leashed. Your valley stays hidden. Your people stay asleep. Forever," she said again, gently, "is the operative word."

"It's not real," I said. "None of this happened."

"It could have. That is what makes it a test and not a trick. I am not lying to you, Mara Almeida. I am offering you arithmetic. Count what you'd gain."

So I counted. Warm bread. Rain on the roof. A crooked smile across a spring wedding. A valley that never had to watch itself be eaten at the edges. Grandmother's hands, never betrayed by a letter. I counted until my throat closed, and the terrible thing—the thing I'll never say to anyone but this page—is how far the count went before it stopped adding up.

"And what do I pay?"

"Everything outside the walls," she said simply. "Corvin dies of his wounds somewhere south, unmourned by anyone who mapped true places. Tamsin goes home and swallows her truth and calls it loyalty until it kills her quietly. Ashvane gutters out alone in his shrine, and no one ever learns what he spent himself for. And out there—" she gestured, and the market rippled, and beyond its edges I saw the gray fields, still eating, patient as rust, "—the world stays edited. Every erased name stays erased. Because the treaty only ends one way, child: either the Door opens free, or it opens bound, or it never opens at all. Renewal is not rescue. Renewal is the leash, re-fastened, with better padding."

We stopped by the well. My well, the chipped rim, except here the chip wasn't there, because in this life I'd never fallen against it at nine years old and learned that scars are just stories your body tells in its own language.

"The words," I said. "The truth-words. Teach them to me."

"They cannot be taught." She crouched, and up close her eyes were fires banked a thousand years deep. "That is the cruelty of oaths. They are written so that only willingness can speak them. Knowledge can be stolen, coerced, faked. Willingness cannot. The Door does not ask *do you know the words.* It asks *who is speaking.*"

"That's a riddle."

"It's a door," she said. "Riddles are doors that pretend to be walls. Now. One last kindness, because I was young once too, and no one offered me this."

She opened her hand, and in it lay the false valley again—but smaller, foldable, a valley the size of a coin, a valley I could keep in my pocket. The warm bread. The rain on the roof. The crooked smile. The small forever, portable, mine.

"Take it," she said. "Refuse the Door entirely, if you like. Walk away north or south or anywhere. But keep this. When the world gets loud—and it will, oh, it will—you can look at it and remember that safety exists, that it could have been yours, that some part of you chose otherwise *knowingly*, with full sight. Or take it and lose it and spend your life wondering. Or refuse it now and never wonder. Those are the shapes your freedom comes in. Choose honestly. The Door will know if you don't."

* * *

I want to tell you I refused it instantly, heroically, with a good line ready. I didn't. I stood in the gray dark with the small forever cupped in ghost-light above her palm, and I wanted it so badly my teeth ached.

I thought about the fence I'd spoken into splinters, and how for one half-second before the terror arrived I had felt *right*—the way a key feels right turning. I thought about Corvin's blood on the map, soaking through the ink of a country no one else believed in. I thought about Tamsin's face when she rode into camp, mud to the eyebrows, saying *I'm not taking you home; I'm telling you which way the gray is moving,* and how that was the most honest anyone had ever been with me, and how much it had cost her.

Grandmother's letter lay folded against my heart where I'd carried it since the chest. *If you are reading this, the country has begun to die.* She had known. She had spent her whole life building me a hiding place and hating herself for it, and her last act was to hand me the shovel and the map and the choice, because love means telling hard truths, and she'd learned it too late to say it aloud and early enough to write it down.

Safety exists, the wolf had said. Yes. And it is a room with no doors, and I have spent my whole life in one already, and I am done.

"No," I said.

I reached out and closed her fingers over the small forever, folding it shut like a letter no one would send.

"I don't want the pocket-sized version. I don't want the valley that never happened and the marriage that costs nothing and the stars somebody arranged for me. If I get a life, I want the one where the bread burns sometimes and my friends disappoint me and the world is enormous and half of it is trying to kill me and *none of it belongs to anybody.* You traded your freedom for a wall. I understand why. I even forgive you. But I'm not going to stand inside your wall and call it my life. I'm going to take the wall down."

Her eyes closed. For a moment she was only tired, only ancient, only a creature who had carried a country on her back for a thousand years and had just heard, for the first time, someone offer to put it down.

"Good," she whispered. "Oh, good. Then hear the rest, because the test was never the valley, child. The valley was mercy. Here is the truth the Door requires:"

She leaned close, and her breath smelled of snow and old smoke, and what she told me was three sentences long, and none of them were the words I'd expected, and every one of them was a word I had never been taught—

—and behind her, in the deep of the dark, the seam of starlight flared white-hot, and Ashvane's voice came through it thin and cracking as burnt paper:

*Mara. Whatever she told you—hurry. I cannot hold it much longer, and the hunters have found the hill.*

The first wolf stepped aside, making way, her gray mantle dissolving into mist that smelled, impossibly, of spring.

"Go," she said. "Be the first free wolf. And when you speak—speak like someone who means to be heard."

I ran for the light.
