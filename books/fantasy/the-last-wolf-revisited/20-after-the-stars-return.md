## 20. After the Stars Return

### Mara

The first thing I felt was the quiet.

Not the valley's Quiet Festival quiet—that performative hush of candles and held breath—but the true kind, the silence after something enormous has finished happening and the world is waiting to see if you'll apologize for it.

I was still standing at the Door when the stars came back.

They didn't blaze on all at once, the way you'd light lamps in a hall. They returned one by one, like guests arriving at a funeral who aren't sure they're welcome: a hesitant point of white over the eastern ridge, then another, then a scatter, then a flood, until the whole torn ceiling of the night mended itself overhead and I realized I had been holding my breath since Ashvane's last fire guttered into ash.

Beside me, Corvin sat up—slowly, wincing, one hand pressed to the bandages Tamsin had wound around his ribs—and looked at the sky the way other men look at their firstborn children.

"Well," he said. "That's going to ruin every map I've ever drawn."

"Good," I said.

"It's not good. Half my reputation rests on those maps."

"Your reputation rests on maps of places that didn't exist."

"And now they exist again." He grinned at me, gray-faced and blood-spotted and utterly delighted. "I'm going to have to redraw *everything*, Mara. Do you understand what that means? A lifetime of work. I've never been so happy in my life."

I might have laughed. What came out instead was something closer to a sob, which is what happens, apparently, when you unmake a thousand-year-old oath and discover your body doesn't know what to do with the leftover fear. I sat down in the wet grass beside him and let it come, and neither of them said anything about it, because that's what people do when the world ends and starts again before breakfast.

Tamsin stood a little apart, facing south, toward home.

"The wards," she said quietly.

I looked. Far off, past the gray fields that were no longer gray—more on that horror and miracle in a moment—a faint shimmer hung over Vael Hollow like heat-haze in reverse. And as we watched, it began to fall. Not shatter, not burn away, nothing so dramatic as all that. It drifted down through the dark like snow, settling over rooftops and orchards and the border stones my grandmother had walked every morning of her life, dissolving as it touched the ground, until there was nothing left between the valley and the rest of the world but air.

A century of hiding, gone in the time it takes snow to land.

"I keep waiting for it to hurt," I said. "It's supposed to hurt, isn't it? Giving something up that big."

"Ask me tomorrow," Tamsin said. "Or don't. Tomorrow I'll probably lie."

* * *

Dawn found us walking south through country I had never seen and somehow always known.

The gray fields—the dead margins where Sarnholt had been eating its own edges for a hundred years—were blooming back. Not gradually, either. As we walked, color poured into the land behind us like ink into water: grass greened, hedgerows fattened, a stone bridge that had been half-dissolved into mist the day before stood whole and mossy and dripping with morning, as though it had never been unwritten at all. Names came back with it. I could feel them, faintly, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue—*Brackenlea*, whispered the hill to our left. *The Nine Sisters*, said the river, and I swear the river said it, because this is what the world is like now, and I am still getting used to it.

Corvin stopped at the crest of a hill, took out his battered field book, and stared at the page where he'd once mapped a blank.

"It's all here," he murmured. "Every erased place. The guild told us these lands were cursed, that mapping them was heresy against the Treaty." He laughed, short and bitter. "They weren't cursed. They were *edited*. And I spent eleven years apologizing for reading the footnotes."

"You kept the one copy," I said.

"I kept the one copy," he agreed, "and it nearly got me killed, and now it's the most valuable document in two countries." He snapped the book shut. "Remind me to be insufferable about this later. When I can stand upright without making noises."

We made noises together, the three of us, walking down into a country that remembered itself, and I will not pretend I understood what any of us felt. Freedom, it turns out, is disorienting. Nobody warns you. All the songs about liberation end at the moment the chains break; none of them cover the awkward business of learning to walk with hands that have never been anything but bound.

* * *

### Tamsin

I want to set down what happened in Vael Hollow while Mara was unmaking the world, because she wasn't there, and someone has to say it, and apparently that someone is me.

The wards fell at moonrise. I know because I was standing in the square, having just finished telling the entire council, in front of everyone, that our last swordspeaker had run off to commit whatever the opposite of treason is—you can't betray a country that officially doesn't exist, I checked—and Elder Maren was mid-sentence in a speech about sealing the valley forever when the sky above the border stones began to snow light.

You could feel it go. Everyone could. Like a hand lifting from the back of your neck after years of pressing. Some people staggered. Old Wenna dropped her basket and sat down in the road and laughed until she cried, and no one asked her why, because we all knew why. We'd been carrying something invisible our whole lives and only knew its weight when it was gone.

And here is the thing nobody tells you about freedom, the thing I learned in that square while the light settled over the rooftops like ash:

Half of us grieved.

Maren stood there with her speech dying in her throat, and her face—I'll never forget her face—was the face of a woman watching her house burn down and being told the fire is a gift. Because it *was* her house. Invisibility had been our walls, our harvests, our children's safety. She had spent sixty years believing the hiding was love, that the valley's smallness was a virtue, that the Quiet Festival honored sacrifice rather than imprisonment. And in one moonrise, a girl she'd helped raise had decided otherwise on behalf of everyone.

"You should have asked us," she said to me afterward, and her voice wasn't cruel. That was the worst part. It was tired. "All of you. You should have let us choose."

"She did choose," I said. "She chose for herself. Which is more than anyone ever let her do."

Maren didn't answer that. But she didn't argue either, and from Maren that's practically a conversion.

By dawn, the village had split the way a log splits—along old grain, along lines that had always been there. Some packed their things that same morning and walked out the north road weeping with joy, touching the grass, saying names aloud like prayers. Others shuttered their windows against the returning stars and spoke of the valley as a widow speaks of a husband: with grief, and anger, and the terrible tenderness of someone who misses even the cage.

And some—most, I think—did both at once. Stood in their doorways crying without knowing which kind of crying it was.

I understand them. I stood in that doorway too, looking out at a horizon I could finally see, and I missed the fog. Just for a moment. Just long enough to be ashamed of it.

Then I went to find a horse, because Mara would need someone to tell her what her choice had cost, and she deserved to hear it from someone who loved her enough to say it plainly.

* * *

### Corvin

I have kept journals since I was nine years old, which makes this the four hundredth or so, and in all that time I have never once written the words *I was wrong*, so allow me the novelty.

I was wrong about the ember.

Eleven years I followed it, telling myself I chased a curiosity, a cartographic anomaly, a flame that burned without fuel and therefore violated everything the guild taught about the natural order. Nonsense. I followed it because it was the last living piece of a country I had helped erase, and some part of me—a part I fed sparingly, like a fire in wet wood—knew that maps are confessions, and mine was overdue.

Last night the ember went out. Of course it did. Ashvane spent his final fire holding the Door open for us, and when the oath dissolved, whatever had sustained him across a millennium of ash went with it. Mara cried. Tamsin, who had just arrived on the ugliest horse I've ever seen, pretended not to cry, which is how Tamsin cries.

And I—I found myself standing in a shrine full of cold ashes, holding a coal that had finally, mercifully, become simply coal, and feeling something I hadn't expected.

Relief. His, I mean. If a dragon the size of a mountain can be said to feel relief, curled for centuries into a flame the size of a thumbnail, keeping a door he hated keeping. He told us, in that last hour—told Mara especially—that the binding had weighed on him worse than on the wolves, because the wolves at least got to forget. He remembered everything. Every year of the hiding. Every generation born and buried inside the lie.

He asked us to bury his ashes under the eastern ridge, facing the sea he'd never seen.

So that's today's work. Mara carries the urn—or rather, an old flour jar, because prophecy rarely provides suitable vessels—and I walk beside her mapping the route, because even funerals deserve accurate cartography, and because if I stop working I will start thinking about my former colleagues, who are at this moment discovering that the Treaty of Severance is void, that their guild charter references a country that exists again, and that a disgraced mapmaker is holding the only complete record of its restoration.

Tamsin says I should worry less.

Tamsin also hit a man with a lantern on her way out of the valley, so her counsel on risk assessment may be biased.

Still. There's something in it. For the first time since the guild struck my name from its rolls, I'm not mapping what no longer is. I'm mapping what *is*. Everything I draw from this morning forward will be true, verifiable, real—and I confess the prospect frightens me more than exile ever did. Accuracy is a kind of nakedness.

But the hills have their names back. The river says its own name when you cross it. And somewhere ahead, a woman who was called the *last* of anything is carrying a jar of dragon-ash toward the ocean, and I intend to put every step of that journey on paper.

Let the guild send whoever they like. Let them come erase this map if they dare.

There isn't a blank left in the world for them to hide behind.

* * *

### Mara

We buried Ashvane at dusk on the third day, on a headland above a sea neither Tamsin nor I had ever seen, though Corvin claimed he had, once, before the guild took his name.

The wind came off the water smelling of salt and something greener underneath—the smell, I realized, of a world with no wards on it, nothing filtered out, nothing muted. I had lived twenty-six years inside a muffled room and mistaken the muffling for peace. The rawness of the open world nearly knocked me down. It still does, some mornings.

Tamsin spoke the words, since she was the one of us who'd known him longest in dragon-years, or claimed to, or simply volunteered, which amounts to the same thing with Tamsin. Corvin marked the spot on his map with a small symbol—not his usual notation for landmarks, but the one he used, he admitted sheepishly, for places worth returning to.

And I poured out the ashes and felt the last weight of the old country leave my hands.

Here is what I know now, standing at the edge of everything: my grandmother did not bind me out of cruelty. She bound me out of love, the desperate kind, the kind that mistakes preservation for protection and builds smaller and smaller rooms to keep the beloved safe in. She gave me a hidden valley, forbidden sword-forms, a life so quiet you could hear your own blood in it. She thought she was giving me everything.

She never once asked what I would have chosen.

I'm not angry anymore. I checked, thoroughly, over three days of walking, the way you probe a healing wound. What's left isn't anger. It's resolve. Somewhere behind us, half a village is grieving the fog, and half is dancing in it where it used to be, and both halves are right, and both halves will need help learning to live in a country that anyone can find on a map. Someone will have to teach the children the sword-forms as dance instead of duty. Someone will have to write down what the Quiet Festival actually commemorated, so it's never quietly repeated. Someone will have to stand in the square in Vael Hollow and say, gently, to Elder Maren and everyone like her: *the hiding was love, and it's over, and we survived both facts.*

That someone appears to be me. The universe has a sense of humor, and it is not subtle.

But tonight, the stars are out—all of them, every erased constellation restored to its post—and the sea says nothing, and Tamsin is asleep with her boots on, and Corvin is writing by firelight with the fierce joy of a man doing honest work at last, and I am sitting here at the edge of a country with its name back, thinking about a blade I still carry and a hole I intend to dig.

Tomorrow we turn south, toward home.

Tomorrow I bury the oath-blade.

But that's tomorrow's chapter, and tonight—for the first night in a hundred years, and the first night of my whole life—the stars belong to everyone, and so do I.
