## 21. The First Free Wolf

The oath-blade weighed less than I remembered.

That was the first strange thing about the morning after the world came back. I'd carried that blade in my mind for years before I ever carried it in my hand—my grandmother's voice in my head every time I picked up any sword at all: *you'll know when it's time*. And when the time finally came, and I stood in the ruins of the Door of Return with ash still drifting down like gray snow and Ashvane's last fire fading to nothing in the hillside, the blade felt almost weightless. As if it had been holding itself up all this time, waiting for permission to rest.

I suppose we had that in common.

Corvin found me there an hour after dawn, picking my way down through the shrine of ash with the blade wrapped in its old cloth. He was limping—his wound from the treaty hunters had gone from grave to merely terrible, which Tamsin assured me was progress—and he had his map case slung across his back like a bandolier, which meant he'd been drawing already. The man could not stop mapping. I think if you set him down in an empty room he'd have the floor plan sketched and three errors corrected within the hour.

"You're going to do it, then," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm going to do it."

"Want company?"

"For digging?"

"I'm told burial is a cartographical act," he said solemnly. "You're fixing a feature permanently in place. It's practically surveying."

"You're making that up."

"A little." His grin went crooked. "Come on. I want to see where you put it. Future generations will ask."

I doubted that, but I didn't say so. A year ago I would have said so. That's what freedom does to you first—it makes you rude. Tamsin says it will wear off. She's wrong, but it's kind of her to lie.

* * *

We walked up past the terraces where the valley's first spring as an unhidden place was doing alarming things to the fields. Everything was blooming early and loud, as if the land had been holding its breath for a hundred years and intended to make up for lost time in one season. The apple trees along the low wall had flowered so heavily they looked embarrassed about it. Bees everywhere, drunk and unapologetic.

The wards were gone—not shattered, not broken, just *released*, the way you open your hand. Some mornings I still reached for them out of habit, the way you reach for a stair that isn't there. But the valley didn't need hiding anymore. Sarnholt was back on the maps. Well—one map. Corvin's map. He'd spent three weeks redrawing it from memory, from Ashvane's dying descriptions, from the librarian-queen's records of names, and from the land itself, which turned out to remember everything even when no one else had been allowed to.

He called it *Sarnholt Revisited*. He wouldn't tell me why he'd chosen the title until I threatened him with a fence post, and then he admitted he'd heard me mutter it over Grandmother's letter one night and thought it fit. Writers steal. Cartographers, apparently, too.

I chose the hill above the house—the one with the view south toward the Door, or where the Door had been. It was only a seam of starlight in a hillside once, and now it was only a hillside again, grass-grown and ordinary, which is exactly what Ashvane would have wanted. Ordinary. He'd kept that door for a thousand years and the last thing he asked of us was that it be allowed to become nothing special.

I dug while Corvin sat on a rock and made notes, because of course he did.

The hole wasn't deep. Blades aren't people; they don't need much. I unwrapped the oath-blade in the morning light and looked at it properly for the last time—the wolf-tooth sigil worked into the tang, the words along the fuller that I could now read without flinching. *Wall and witness. Breath and bone.* The words of the first wolves, who had sworn themselves into the country's foundations so that their descendants could hide inside them. My gift had never been a gift. It had been a leash, and I had worn it so long I'd called it jewelry.

"You could sell it," Corvin offered. "Antique oath-blade, provenance impeccable."

"Tamsin would kill you for suggesting it."

"Tamsin would help me dig the buyer's grave, yes."

I laughed, and the sound went out over the valley, unwarded and unhidden, and nothing bad happened. Nothing bad happened. I was going to keep noticing that for the rest of my life, I thought. Good.

I laid the blade in the earth with the sigil facing up, because I wanted the wolf looking at the sky, not the dirt. Then I covered it, and patted the soil flat, and stood there feeling absurdly like I should say something.

Grandmother would have known the words. There'd have been an oath for this too—a closing-form, a binding-verse, something formal and heavy to match the blade. But that was rather the entire point, wasn't it. No more oaths. If I spoke over this grave, I'd speak plain words, mine own, unsanctioned by anyone.

"Thank you," I said finally. "And goodbye. You did your job. Now do nothing, forever. It's wonderful work if you can get it."

Corvin wrote that down. I let him.

Then I knelt and planted the sapling I'd brought up in a bucket—an oak, from the acorn I'd found in Grandmother's chest, tucked into a corner behind the letter as if she'd meant it for exactly this and hadn't dared say so. Maybe she had. Maybe she'd stood where I stood and imagined a granddaughter who would live to plant things instead of swear them. I pressed the soil around its roots and watered it from the bucket, and Corvin watched with his head tilted like a heron.

"Sword-forms?" he asked quietly.

I understood what he was really asking. Everyone who knew anything had been asking it since the Door: what happens to the last swordspeaker when there is no more sword-oath? Do the forms die with the duty? Does Mara Almeida put down the blade forever, the way the valley put down its walls?

I drew my ordinary sword—just steel, just mine, no words humming in it anymore except the ones I put there—and I answered the way I'd decided to answer, which was with movement.

The forms came easily. They always had, but now they came *lightly*, and that was the difference. Before, every form had been a sentence in a grammar I hadn't chosen, each stance a word in an oath older than my grandmother's grandmother. Now the same movements were just... language. Mine. I ran the First Form slow enough to feel the air move, and it wasn't a ward I was cutting, it was nothing, it was morning, it was the shape of a body being glad to have a body. I moved through the Turning Form and the Long Listen, and when I reached the form Grandmother forbade me to name aloud—the one I'd practiced under the dark stars on the night everything began—I named it aloud, mid-motion, laughing: "Wolf's Welcome!"

It sounded ridiculous. It sounded like mine.

When I finished, breath high, sword low, Corvin was watching with an expression I'd learned to read as *trying very hard not to draw you.*

"Dance," I said, answering his earlier question at last. "They're dance now. Anyone can learn them. Nobody has to."

"That's—" He stopped, started again. "Do you know how many people are going to come here to learn? Now that the country exists again? Now that there are travelers on the north road asking questions?"

"That," I said, wiping sweat off my brow, "is Tamsin's problem."

* * *

Tamsin, for her part, had solved the problem of what to do with a nation's worth of scattered memory the way she solved everything: with lists, and shelves, and a terrifying competence that made grown elders stand up straighter.

She'd claimed the old tithe-house on the square—the one with the good stone floors and the shutters that actually closed—and emptied it of tithes in a single afternoon. When the council sputtered, she presented them with a document. Not an oath, she was careful to say. An *agreement*. Voluntary. Revocable. Signed by thirty-one households in the first week. The word "oath" had gone out of fashion in Vael Hollow faster than winter goes out of April, and Tamsin, who had once betrayed my presence to that same council out of love, had appointed herself chief custodian of the fact that we were done with bindings of any kind.

"It's not an archive yet," she told me when I visited, walking me through rooms that smelled of fresh sawdust and ink. "It's a room with ambition. But Corvin sent the first copies of his map, and the librarian-queen sent—" she lowered her voice, reverent, "—three crates, Mara. Three crates of names. People who were struck from the record. Their names, their trades, where they went when the forgetting took them. She said Sarnholt should have its people back, even if only on paper."

"And the valley's own records?"

"Being copied. Every household ledger, every birth note, every grudge anyone ever entered in the mill book." She grinned. "Elder Brannic objected until I pointed out that if we don't write our own history, someone else will write it worse."

"He said that convinced him?"

"He said, and I quote, 'Gods forbid.'" She pulled a sheet off a new table and revealed rows of shallow drawers, each labeled in her tidy hand. "First drawer's for the oath-years. Everything about the hiding. The ward-stones, the Quiet Festival, the forms, the blades—all of it, recorded honestly, including the parts that hurt. Especially those."

"People might not want to remember," I said.

"Some won't. That's allowed too. But the ones who do deserve somewhere true." She looked at me sideways. "You'll give testimony?"

I thought about it. About standing in front of whatever passed for scholars in a nation that had spent a century learning how to forget, and saying: *this is what it cost to be safe. This is what my grandmother gave up. This is what I refused.* 

"Yes," I said. "But I'm telling it my way. Warts and wolves."

"Wolves especially," Tamsin agreed. "People will want to hear about the dragon."

"Everyone wants to hear about the dragon. Nobody wants to hear about the paperwork."

"Welcome to history."

* * *

Spring leaned into summer, and the road north filled up.

That was the strangest part, more than the stars coming home, more than the gray fields flushing green and remembering themselves into orchards and villages and towns with names again. Travelers. Actual travelers, coming down into Vael Hollow with dust on their boots and questions in their mouths. Merchants first, because merchants smell opportunity the way I used to smell ward-failure. Then pilgrims of a sort—people whose great-grandparents had come from Sarnholt without knowing it, carrying family names that had gone strange and rootless, looking for the ground those names grew from.

Tamsin met them at the archive with ledgers. Corvin met them with maps for sale. I mostly tried to stay on my hill, but the hill had acquired a reputation, because Corvin had drawn it onto the map with a small notation that read, in his infuriatingly neat hand: *site of the Unbinding; home of the last swordspeaker; ask before visiting.*

"Ask before visiting," I said, when I saw it. "Corvin. They're camping at the bottom of my hill."

"They asked first. There's a whole queue protocol now. Tamsin designed it."

"There's a *queue*?"

"You're a historical event, Mara. Try to be gracious about it."

I was not gracious about it. I went down the hill with my arms crossed and found six people and a goat waiting respectfully at the fence line, and the oldest among them—a woman with silver braids and eyes like a hawk's—asked me the question everyone asked:

"Are you truly the last swordspeaker?"

And I gave the answer I'd been practicing, the one that had taken me a whole season to grow into:

"No. There was no last one. Because there will be more."

They blinked at me. The goat chewed something judgmentally.

"The forms are free now," I said. "No oath. No duty. Come up tomorrow at dawn if you want to learn the first one. Bring bread. Leave the goat."

* * *

By midsummer I had eleven students and a schedule, which was more students than I had ever wanted and a schedule than I had sworn I would never keep. They were farmers and ferry-men and one extremely determined baker, and none of them could yet speak a fence into splinters, which was fine, because that had been an accident and I'd paid for the fence.

Teaching turned out to be nothing like wielding. When I held the blade for myself, the forms were inheritance—heavy, whether I wanted them or not. When I handed the shapes across to other bodies, they became something lighter. A gift. That was the word, and I turned it over often, because for so long *gift* had been the lie wrapped around the leash. But this—this was the real thing. Something given freely, kept freely, passed on freely. Inheritance transformed. The difference, I decided, was consent. The difference was always consent.

On the evening the first student completed the Wolf's Welcome without falling over—a ferry-man named Odell with knees like door hinges—we planted a second tree beside the oak on my hill. Tradition already. Traditions form fast when nobody's forcing them.

Corvin came up at dusk with two cups of cider and the new proof of his map rolled under his arm. He'd gotten a proper press now, down in the city of the unremembered; the librarian-queen had insisted, in the tone of a woman who does not hear the word no. He spread the corner of the sheet on his knee and showed me a detail he'd added: the hill, the oak, the buried blade beneath it, marked with a symbol I didn't recognize.

"What's that?"

"A burial cairn sign. Old guild convention. Means 'here lies something that has finished its work.'"

"Not 'here lies treasure'?"

"Treasure implies you can take it back out." He sipped his cider. "This is better than treasure. This is a thing that got to stop."

We sat there while the light went long and gold, and below us the valley did its evening business—lamplighting, supper smoke, Tamsin's archive window glowing with somebody working late over the names of the forgotten. Somewhere south, the hillside that had been the Door stood green and utterly unremarkable. Somewhere farther south, in a shrine of ash, nothing at all remained of a dragon but warmth in the stones, and that was right too. He'd spent himself holding the door open so I could choose. The least the world could do was let him rest as weather.

"Next printing," Corvin said, "I'm changing the title page."

"Oh?"

"*Sarnholt Revisited, Second Edition. With corrections.*" He glanced at me. "There's a blank space in the southeast quarter. Where the new school will go. I left it empty on purpose."

I looked at the blank space for a long moment. Empty space on a map, waiting to be filled by something no one had planned.

"Leave it empty a little longer," I said. "Let whoever builds it name it. That's sort of the whole point, isn't it?"

Corvin smiled and wrote it down, because of course he did, and the oak leaves moved overhead like a thousand small hands letting go.

Tomorrow there would be students at dawn, and bread to negotiate for, and Tamsin would arrive with seventeen objections to my teaching posture, and the world outside the valley would keep arriving in wagons and letters and rumors of roads reopening toward places that had spent a century pretending not to exist. Tomorrow the story would keep happening to other people too, which was the whole miracle of it.

But tonight the blade slept in the dark under the roots, doing nothing, forever, and the tree drank the wind, and the first free wolf sat on her hill with a cup of cider and nowhere at all she was bound to be—

—and far down the south road, small with distance, a rider was coming.
