## 22. Revisited

The travelers always come in autumn.

I've never decided whether that's coincidence or the valley's sense of humor. Vael Hollow was born in autumn—the wards fell like snow on a harvest night, the stars came back one constellation at a time as if someone were relighting lamps in an enormous house—and every year since, when the maples go red and the air gets that clean edge to it, strangers appear at the foot of the old border road with dust on their boots and questions in their mouths.

They come asking about the last swordspeaker.

I used to find this funny. Now I find it funny *and* tiring, which I'm told is what growing older actually is: the same jokes, heavier to lift.

This morning's pair arrived before the dew had burned off—a young woman with ink-stained fingers and a boy who couldn't have been more than sixteen, both of them wearing the particular expression of people who have walked a long way on the strength of a rumor. The rumor being me, apparently. Or some version of me. The versions get wilder every year. Last spring a merchant swore to my face that I had slain a dragon. I let him believe it. Ashvane would have laughed himself out if he'd had enough of himself left to laugh with.

"You're her," the girl said. Not a question. She was looking at my hands, which are scarred and ordinary and currently holding a basket of late apples. "Mara Almeida. The last swordspeaker."

Here is the thing nobody tells you about becoming a story: you stop being able to correct it. Stories don't take amendments. You can stand in your own orchard, in your own patched shirt, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and goat, and still watch someone look at you and see something taller, gleaming, already half-legend. I've stopped fighting it. Mostly.

"I'm Mara Almeida," I said. "The rest we should discuss over tea, because you've walked from the coast road and you're both about to fall down."

* * *

They sat at my kitchen table—the same table where Corvin once spread a map of a country that officially did not exist, back when I thought my whole inheritance was a sword and a silence—and the girl, whose name was Sef, pulled out a notebook that made my chest ache in a way I wasn't expecting. It was full of forms. Sword-forms. Drawn in careful ink, labeled in a hand that had clearly copied them second- or third- or fourth-hand, each one annotated with little marginal questions: *Is this right? Does anyone still know?*

"Where did you get these?" I asked, though I already knew the shape of the answer.

"Everywhere," she said. "A dancing master in Callowmere teaches three of them as exercises—he doesn't know what they are. There's a stonemason's guild in the east that uses one as a measuring pattern. My grandmother hummed one. She said it was a lullaby." Sef looked up, and there it was, the thing I remembered feeling at nineteen, standing in a dark field while the stars went out: the hunger of someone who has found a door and doesn't know yet whether it opens. "Nobody knows they're connected. Nobody knows they're *words*. Except everyone says—" she hesitated, embarrassed by her own hope, "—everyone says there was a last swordspeaker who unbound the oath, and that she'd know."

I put the teapot down. I took my time with it. Some moments deserve a small ceremony even when all they contain is tea.

"They're not wrong," I said finally. "About the knowing. But they're wrong about the rest, and since you've walked all this way, you deserve the truth instead of the legend, and the truth is shorter than you'd think."

The boy leaned forward. Sef held her breath without seeming to notice she was doing it.

"There was no last swordspeaker," I said. "There was only ever a *first*, and then a long line of people who were told they were last. That's what an oath does—it makes itself feel final. It wraps around you and whispers that you're the end of something, so you'd better carry it carefully, so you'd better not drop it, so you'd better be small and quiet and grateful for the wall you're made into." I turned my cup slowly on its saucer. "I spent my whole childhood believing I was the last of a dying thing. Turns out I was the first of a living one. The difference between those two sentences is the entire story, and I'm still learning how to say it properly."

Sef wrote that down. Of course she did. I watched her pen move and felt something loosen in me that I hadn't known was tight.

* * *

Afterward I walked them out past the orchard to the tree.

You'd miss it if you didn't know—a rowan, young-ish now, broad-crowned, planted over a patch of ground that looks like any other patch of ground. Underneath it, wrapped in oilcloth and buried deeper than any spade goes easily, lies an oath-blade that no longer hums, that hasn't hummed in years, that is just steel now, honest and asleep. I visit it the way you visit a grave that you're also grateful to. Some inheritances you keep. Some you bury and plant a tree over, and the tree is the inheritance, and the roots holding the blade quiet are doing more work than the blade ever did.

"This is where I buried it," I told them. "Not the sword. The sentence. *Last.*"

The boy—his name was Pell, and he hadn't spoken once until now—frowned at the rowan. "But you kept the forms."

"I kept everything worth keeping. That's the part the stories get backwards. Unbinding isn't throwing away. When I spoke the release at the Door, I didn't erase the oath—I *answered* it. I told the first wolf: thank you, it was enough, you can rest now. And everything true inside the binding survived the unbinding, the way a song survives being sung freely instead of under orders." I touched the rowan's bark. It was warm from the morning sun, which is the kind of miracle I can actually live with. "The forms were never chains. They were a language somebody chained. Those aren't the same thing, and confusing them is how countries get lost."

Sef was staring at me with an intensity that would have frightened me at twenty and delights me at whatever age I am now, which I decline to specify on the grounds that a woman should keep some mysteries. "Will you teach us?"

And there it was. The question. The one I get every autumn, and the one I answered badly the first few times, out of fear dressed up as humility. Because here's what I knew that first year, standing in front of the first seekers: I am not a master. I am not a lineage. I am a woman who learned a language by breaking things with it—a fence, mostly, and later, regrettably, a very nice bridge—and who has been stumbling toward fluency ever since. Every year I understand the forms a little better and understand, simultaneously, how much vaster they are than my understanding. Teaching felt like fraud. Teaching felt like the beginning of a new kind of oath, and I had sworn off oaths the way reformed drinkers swear off drink: loudly, and with occasional relapse.

But peace isn't the absence of fear; it's the presence of something louder. And what I have learned, in these years of the world being wide again, is that a gift hoarded dies quietly in its keeper, while a gift shared comes back multiplied in accents you never learned.

So I said what I always say now.

"I'll teach you what I know. Which is less than the forms deserve and more than nothing. And then you'll go teach it wrong in your own way, the way everyone does, and in fifty years the lullabies will be different and the dances will be different and somewhere a child will invent a form none of us ever imagined, and that child will be the swordspeaker too. There will be more. That's the whole answer. There was no last one, because there will be more."

Pell grinned. Sef cried a little, and pretended she hadn't, and I pretended with her, because courtesy matters.

* * *

That evening Corvin came by, which he does most evenings, ostensibly to argue about cartography and actually to eat my stew.

He's gray at the temples now, my disgraced cartographer, and his shadow keeps perfect time these days—whatever was lagging behind him healed when the country came back, as if even shadows were waiting for permission to belong to themselves. He still maps places that no longer are, except now the list is shrinking instead of growing, which he claims ruins his artistic brand and secretly delights him beyond measure. His great work sits half-finished in the archive Tamsin built—the first archive Sarnholt has had in a century, staffed by three librarians, two apprentices, and one extremely opinionated cat. Tamsin runs it the way she runs everything: with fierce love and absolutely no tolerance for nonsense. We made our peace years ago, she and I. Betrayal out of love, forgiven, becomes just love with a scar on it, and scars are only proof that something healed.

"The coastal survey came back," Corvin said, ladling stew with the concentration of a man defusing something. "There's a village two days north that remembers a fourth form. A *fourth*. Nobody recorded it. An old ferryman does it with a boat hook."

"A boat hook."

"Swordspeaking adapts to available materials. Your own principle."

"My principle was about fences."

"And bridges. Don't think I've forgotten the bridge." He passed me the bowl, and over its rim he gave me the look—that look, the one that says he's about to propose something slightly mad and wants me to pretend to resist. "We should go. Before winter. You, me, the boat hook ferryman, a fourth form that exists nowhere on paper."

I looked out the window. The stars were coming up over the Hollow, all of them, every night, in their ancient constellations and—this is my favorite part—in patterns no one had names for yet, because the sky is bigger than our naming of it and always was. Somewhere out there, a country that chose memory over law was busy becoming ordinary again, which is the most extraordinary thing a country can do.

"Tomorrow," I said. "Tonight I'm teaching two travelers their first form, and you're going to hold the lantern and complain about the light."

"That," said Corvin, settling back with his stew, "is the plan exactly."

* * *

Later, after Sef and Pell had gone to the loft with blankets and the giddy exhaustion of people whose lives changed direction over tea, I stood alone in the dooryard and did what I do every night now, and did not do for the first thirty years of my life.

I practiced.

Not the way I practiced as a girl—secretive, guilty, sword-forms named only in silence while the wards pressed down like a hand on the back of my neck. This practice is slower. It's closer to dance than duty, which was always the point, which was maybe always the point of everything. The forms move through me like words through a mouth that has finally stopped being afraid of its own voice. Sometimes, on good nights, the air hums—not with power, not anymore, not like that—but with recognition. As if the language remembers being spoken freely, and is glad.

Grandmother, if you can hear this from wherever oaths go when they're released: I'm not angry anymore. You hid me to keep me alive, and it worked, and I hate that it worked, and I forgive you, and all four of those things are true at once, which is the whole messy arithmetic of love. You bound a country to save it. I unbound it to save it differently. Between the two of us, it survived. That's not a failure of either generation. That might be the definition of one.

The rowan leaves whispered overhead. Down in the loft, Sef was practicing the opening stance in her sleep, arms moving under the blanket, learning it the way I learned it—body first, meaning after, courage somewhere in between.

Years from now, travelers will come to Vael Hollow asking about the last swordspeaker, and someone will send them up my path, and I will pour the tea and tell them the truth again, because the truth bears repeating the way a form bears practicing: not because it changes, but because *we* change, and need to hear it new each time.

There was no last one.

There will be more.

And tonight, under a sky full of named and unnamed stars, the first of them is sleeping in my loft with ink on her fingers and a language waking in her hands—and come morning, I will begin, again, gladly, to teach her how to speak.
