## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The lamb was dying again.

Hazel Dunmore had learned to tell the difference between a lamb that would die and one that only thought it might. This one — Bramble's last-born, all knobby knees and wet black nose — lay in the corner of the pen with its chin flat against the straw, breathing in shallow sips, like it was trying not to be noticed by whatever came for sick things in the night.

She crouched at the edge of the pen and watched it for a long moment before going in.

"You're being dramatic," she told it. The lamb's ear twitched. "I've seen dramatic. I once knew a woman who fainted over a broken fingernail." She didn't know any such woman. She'd made her up years ago, one of the small fictions she kept like river stones in her pocket, smooth from handling, worn of meaning. She had a whole collection of them: a woman who fainted, a house with blue shutters, someone's laugh that sounded like a door hinge. She didn't know where they'd come from. She'd stopped asking.

That was the trick of Vell. You stopped asking.

She scooped the lamb up — lighter than she liked, ribs like a washboard under damp wool — and carried it into the cottage. The fire had burned down to orange coals; she fed it a knot of pine and set the lamb on the blanket near the hearth, in the spot the goats weren't allowed but always tried anyway. From the shelf she took the brown bottle Odell had given her three winters back, when Hazel's whole flock had come down with the scours and Odell had appeared at her door with her shawl pinned wrong and her mouth set in the particular line that meant *don't ask me how I know this works*.

Half a spoonful, worked into the gum of the cheek. The lamb swallowed on instinct, which Hazel chose to take as a good sign.

Outside, the light was doing what Vell's light always did in late autumn: going thin and gray and honest, draining the color out of the hills until they looked like sketches of themselves. The valley sat cupped in the arms of two ridgelines, sheltered, self-contained, the kind of place that appeared on no map because no cartographer had ever bothered to get lost here. Ten years, and Hazel still sometimes caught herself looking up at the border stones on the northern pass — those leaning gray monoliths strung along the saddle of the ridge like crooked teeth — and feeling something in her chest lean toward them, the way a dog leans toward a closed door.

She didn't let herself wonder what was on the other side. That was the first rule of Vell, unwritten and absolute: you didn't wonder. Wondering was a crack, and cracks let things in.

"Vell keeps," the elders said, every festival, every funeral, every time a child asked why nobody ever left. *Vell keeps.* It was supposed to sound like comfort. Mostly it did.

She went back out with the milking pail, because the goats didn't care that one of their number's lambs was sulking toward death, and the morning's work didn't care either. The rhythm of it settled over her the way it always did — the squeeze and hiss of milk, the complaint of Nettle, the white goat who believed herself unloved, the smell of hay and animal warmth and woodsmoke rising off the village below. Forty rooftops, give or take. A chapel with a bell that hadn't rung in anyone's memory. Fields going gold and stubbled. Smoke standing straight up from chimneys in the windless cold.

It was, she thought, not for the first time, an unbearable kind of peace. And she loved it the way you love a hand clamped over your mouth: gratefully, and with a small wild part of you screaming.

She was carrying the second pail back up the slope when the sky changed.

Not much. Not dramatically. Just a thickening in the west, a bruise-colored smudge above the ridge where there had been nothing, and a pressure in her ears like she'd climbed too fast. She stood in the dooryard with the pail slopping her boot and stared at it, because weather in Vell came in slow fronts off the southern plains, predictable as tax day, and it never, ever built in the northwest, over the stones.

The elders said the stones did that. Kept things out. Kept things *off*, the way a roof keeps off rain.

Hazel went inside and didn't look at the sky again until dark.

* * *

The lamb worsened through the evening.

By full dark it had stopped taking the medicine, head lolling when she lifted it, breath coming fast and hot against her wrist. She wrapped it in the good blanket and held it in her lap by the fire, and she did the thing she always did at this point, the thing that wasn't prayer exactly, because prayer implied a listener and everyone in Vell agreed there were none.

Everyone in Vell was very firm about that. No gods in Vell. Whatever the lowlands prayed to — and the occasional peddler's tales suggested the lowlands prayed constantly, to saints and spirits and the Quiet God of the Church, whose name you weren't supposed to say twice — none of it reached past the border stones. Magic died at the stones. So did gods, presumably, if they'd ever lived. What couldn't cross over couldn't listen.

So what Hazel did was just talk. Words into the fire. Words into wool. The shape of pleading without the substance, a habit so old it had worn grooves into her like water wearing stone.

"Come on," she murmured to the lamb. Its eyes had rolled back to crescents. "You don't get to do this. I carried you up from the far pasture in a hailstorm, remember? You bit my neck the whole way. You owe me."

The lamb breathed. The fire popped. Outside, the wind had picked up, and it was coming from the northwest, which was impossible, and she was tired enough not to care.

"Please," she said. Just that. To no one. The word fell out of her like a coin down a well, and she heard — felt — the moment it hit bottom somewhere very far away.

The rain started.

Not on the roof. She'd have welcomed rain on the roof; the autumn had been dry and the well was low. This was rain on the *window*. One side of the window. The north-facing pane, ticking softly, steadily, drops striking glass in the dead-still night while the fire burned undisturbed and the smoke rose straight up the chimney and the south window stayed bone dry.

Hazel sat very still.

The ticking continued. She crossed the room — slowly, the way you approach a strange dog — and put her palm flat against the cold pane. Beyond it, moonlight fell silver and motionless on the dooryard, on the woodpile, on the goat pen. And directly above the cottage, in a circle maybe twenty feet across, rain fell through the moonlight and vanished an inch above her roof, as though the sky had opened only enough to weep and then thought better of it.

It lasted perhaps a minute. Then the circle thinned, unraveled, and was gone, and the night was ordinary again, and the silence afterward was so complete she could hear the lamb's quick heart against her knee.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this the way you notice someone else's hands shaking, from a distance, with mild interest. Because there was a second thing, underneath the shaking, and it was worse.

The second thing was recognition.

Somewhere in her body — in the marrow, in the old scarred places that ten years of Vell had padded over like snow over fallen walls — some part of her knew that rain. Knew its weight, its taste, the exact gray-green color of a storm-heavy sky. Some part of her had *made* rain like that before. Many times. Enough times that making it had been as ordinary as milking.

And some part of her had paid — she didn't know what, didn't know to whom, but the shape of a price hung in her chest like a hook in meat — to never know that again.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the dry windows, to the lamb. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "No. There's nothing past the stones. Nothing listens. That's the whole point of this place."

The lamb stirred. Lifted its head, wobbly, absurdly. Nosed at her wrist and gave a thin bleat, and when she checked its gums they were pink, and its breathing had slowed to something deep and even, sleep instead of dying.

Healed. Not recovering — healed, between one breath and the next, the fever gone like a snuffed candle.

Hazel sat by the fire holding a healthy lamb and felt the safe numbness of her life crack straight down the middle, quiet as ice giving way underfoot. Because prayers weren't answered in Vell. That was the entire architecture of her exile, the foundation everything rested on. And yet something had listened. Something had reached past ten years and a ring of standing stones and whatever she'd paid to forget, and answered a throwaway word from a goatherd talking to a dying animal.

Which meant the border wasn't holding.

Or — and this thought arrived last, cold and patient, and refused to leave —

Or the border was fine, and the thing that had answered had never needed to cross it at all. Because it had already recognized her voice. Because whatever she'd been before Vell, whatever had been locked away behind her own forgetting, had just heard its name spoken after a decade of silence.

And it had said, in the only language it had left:

*I'm still here.*

The fire settled. The lamb slept. Hazel sat awake until dawn, watching the north window, listening to a valley that had promised her silence — and wondering, for the first time in ten years, what she had done to deserve the quiet, and what was coming to collect the debt.
