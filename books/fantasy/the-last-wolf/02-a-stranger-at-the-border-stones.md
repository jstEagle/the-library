## 2. A Stranger at the Border Stones

The lamb died anyway.

I tell you that first because everything else that happened that day — the stranger, the map, the name I'd never heard and would never stop hearing — has a way of sounding like it mattered more than it did. It didn't. The lamb died. That was Tuesday in Vell. Things died on Tuesdays and every other day of the week, quietly, without drama, and we buried them behind the goat pen and went back to work.

So when I found the man bleeding out against the border stones two mornings later, my first instinct was not compassion.

My first instinct was to walk away.

* * *

I want to be honest about this, because everyone in Vell would tell you Hazel Dunmore is soft-hearted — she takes in strays, she cries at lambing season, she once carried old Bram's firewood up three flights of stairs with a busted wrist. All true. But the truth underneath is simpler and uglier: I take care of things because they can't ask me questions. A sick lamb doesn't wonder where I came from before Vell. A broken-wristed neighbor doesn't look at me too long.

A dying stranger at the border stones looks at you like you're the last thing he'll ever see. And there's no walking away from that. Not really. You carry it to your grave either way — his face or your shame — and I had enough faces already.

He was slumped between the second and third stones, the ones the elders call the Teeth, where the moss grows thick as carpet and nothing else does. His left side was dark with blood, soaked through a coat that had been fine once, good wool, city-made. His hand was locked around something pressed to his chest like a child holding a blanket, and even from twenty paces I could see the blood on it wasn't all his.

"Hey," I said. Stupid. He was past hearing.

I crouched. Up close he was younger than the blood made him look — thirty, maybe, sharp-faced, black stubble, lips gone gray. Fever-heat poured off him. The wound was a slash along his ribs, deep but clean, the kind that kills slow instead of fast, which meant someone had wanted him to run before he bled.

His eyes opened. Gray, wet-bright, focused somewhere past my shoulder like I was a rumor.

"Averlaine," he said.

"I don't know what that is."

"You do." His hand spasmed around whatever he held. "Everyone does. They just—" A cough took him, wet and long. "They just forgot."

I looked over my shoulder at the valley. Morning smoke rising off Odell's chimney. The bell rope hanging still. Goats on the high pasture, white dots on green. Ten years of the quietest life anyone had ever lived, and this man was lying on its doorstep talking about things nobody had forgotten because nobody had ever known them.

"Stay here," I said, which was ridiculous, and then I did the other ridiculous thing: I hauled him up. He weighed less than he should have. He'd been running for days on no food; you could feel the architecture of him under the wool, all edges. His head lolled against my neck and he whispered something that might have been *thank you* or might have been a word in a language I didn't know, and I told myself, step by step down the sheep path, that I was doing this because leaving a man to die on the stones would bring the elders asking how he got there, and questions were bad for business.

It's a good lie. I've told better ones. Just not to myself, usually.

* * *

I put him in the drying shed behind my cottage, on the racks where the lavender goes in autumn. Not in my house — I'm not stupid — but close enough that I could get to him fast, far enough that if the elders came sniffing there was plausible distance. I cleaned the wound with spirits and stitched it with the curved needle I use for torn ears, and he didn't scream, which told me he'd been hurt worse before, or that he was stubborn, or both. Probably both.

When the fever broke enough for sense, near dusk, he tried to sit up.

"Don't."

"Have to—" He got an elbow under himself, failed, lay back panting. "They'll come. If I stay in one place—"

"They who?"

His eyes found mine properly for the first time. Fever-bright, but steady underneath, and I recognized the steadiness. It's the kind you build when you've decided you're already dead and are just negotiating the date.

"The Church of the Quiet," he said. "You have them here? No. No, of course not, Vell doesn't—" He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it, and winced. "Vell doesn't believe in anything loud enough to burn."

"We don't believe in much at all," I said. "That's the local religion."

"It's a good one." He closed his eyes. Opened them. "You stitched me. Why?"

Because you looked at me like I was the last thing you'd ever see. Because the lamb died on Tuesday and Wednesday needed to be different. Because ten years ago somebody didn't stitch me, and I paid a great deal of money I don't remember earning to forget why that sentence makes my chest hurt.

"Seemed rude not to," I said.

He studied me the way you study a lock you intend to pick. Then he lifted his right hand, the one that hadn't let go since the stones, and uncurled his fingers one at a time like each cost him something.

What he held was a roll of hide. Not parchment, not vellum — hide, tanned thin and supple, and marked across its whole surface in ink gone brown-black with age. Roads. Rivers. Mountains drawn as little jagged teeth. Towns marked with tiny careful bells. And across the top, in a script that was almost ours but not quite, a single word:

**AVERLAINE.**

"That's not a real country," I said.

"No," he agreed. "Officially."

"Then what am I looking at?"

"My sister's handwriting." His voice did something complicated on the word *sister*, a small collapse and recovery, so quick I'd have missed it if I hadn't spent ten years learning to hear collapses. "She drew it from memory. Ours. She remembered better than anyone. That's why—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "That's why they killed her."

Outside, the evening settled. A dog barked twice at Odell's place and quit. I stood there with a dead country rolled up in a dead girl's ink, and felt the valley's silence press in around me like water, and understood — dimly, the way you understand weather coming — that something had just ended.

"I should get Odell," I said.

"No."

"She's the matriarch. Strangers are her business."

"I'll be dead by morning," he said, matter-of-fact, "if the elders decide what I am before I decide what to tell them. Please." The word cost him visibly. "One night. Let me be nobody for one night."

And there it was. The thing I couldn't walk away from, though God knew I tried to find the angle. A man asking to be nobody. I knew exactly what that weighed. I had carried it for ten years, and mine had been voluntary.

"One night," I said.

* * *

I fed the goats. I mended a fence rail that didn't need mending. I sat on my step with bread and hard cheese and watched the light go amber and then ash over the western ridge, and I thought about the word *Averlaine* sitting in my shed like an unexploded shell.

Here is what I knew about countries that don't exist: nothing. Here is what I knew about churches named Quiet: nothing, except that the name made my teeth ache in a way I couldn't source. Here is what I knew about myself: Hazel Dunmore, thirty-one years old or thereabouts, goat-farmer, orphan, arrived in Vell ten years ago with papers I don't remember signing and a purse I don't remember filling, and the elders had taken one look at me and agreed to forget to ask questions. Odell especially. Odell, who raised half the valley's orphans and asked none of us about our scars, and who had once, drunk on plum brandy at midwinter, gripped my wrist across the table and said, *Whatever you paid, girl, I hope it was worth it,* and then pretended till her dying day she'd said nothing at all.

I'd assumed she meant a bride-price. Some marriage fled. It was a tidy story and I'd worn it smooth as river stone through a decade of handling.

Sitting on my step, watching my own windows go dark, I realized I'd never once checked whether the story fit. That's what forgetting does. It doesn't leave holes. It leaves furniture — comfortable, familiar, load-bearing furniture — and you build your whole house on it and never think to look underneath.

There was a stranger in my shed who remembered a country the world had deleted, and some animal part of me wanted to burn that wolf-hide rather than touch it again.

I touched it again.

* * *

Lamplight in the shed, midnight. He was propped on his elbow, sweating, alive, watching me unroll the hide across the floor between us like we were children planning an invasion.

"Roads," I said, tracing one with a fingertip. "Real roads. Through mountains that aren't there. This town — Coriel? There's no Coriel."

"There was." He said it simply, no performance of grief, which somehow made it heavier. "Coriel, Vasse, the Bellmarket, the Glass Palace at Ambrun. Two million people, last census. Rivers with names. A king who was mostly a figurehead and a parliament that actually worked, which surprised everyone." He tapped the border, where the inked country just stopped, dissolving into blank hide. "And around all of it, nothing. On every map in the world, Averlaine is forest. Unmapped, unclaimed, empty. Travelers who cross it come out the other side having seen trees. Records burn easily. Memories—" his jaw tightened, "—memories burn easier, if you have a church dedicated to the burning."

"The Church of the Quiet."

"They don't call themselves that. Nobody calls themselves anything, inside. You join by agreeing to forget, and then you're not anything anymore. That's the mercy they sell." He looked at his own hands. "Some people buy it gladly. My mother nearly did. My sister wouldn't."

"What happened to her?"

"They burned the house with the maps in it. She ran. I ran. We got separated at the ford at—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "We got separated."

The hide lay between us, roads going nowhere, bells ringing in towns of brown ink. I thought about the lamb. I thought about Tuesday. I thought about how the valley's silence had always felt less like peace than like a held breath, and how I'd been grateful for it anyway, because held breath is still breath.

"Why bring it here?" I asked. "To Vell. Of all places."

"Because Vell forgets things professionally." He almost smiled. "I figured nowhere safer for a thing that needs hiding. And because—" He hesitated, and for the first time since the stones, the steadiness flickered. He looked at me the way he had at the border, like I was the last thing he'd ever see, except now it wasn't fever doing it. "Because the map says the door only opens for someone born of storm. And when you picked me up, there was rain falling on you. Only you. Six feet of clear sky all around, and rain on your shoulders like a dog that followed you home."

I laughed. It came out wrong, too short.

"It was windy," I said.

"It was," he agreed, "in a circle around you."

We looked at each other over the corpse of a country. Somewhere outside, a nightbird called, and stopped, as if listening.

"Sleep," I said finally, rolling up the hide. "If you're well enough tomorrow to lie convincingly, I'll figure out what to do with you. If you're not—" I shrugged. "Well. Vell buries strangers quietly. It's practically our sacrament."

"Hazel." I hadn't told him my name. I must have, during the stitching; fever eats days. "Thank you. For being rude about it."

I snuffed the lamp and stood in my own dark kitchen afterward for a long time, hands flat on the cool wood of the table, listening to the shed breathe.

Compassion, I've decided, is mostly a failure of discipline. You spend ten years building walls and then some bleeding fool collapses against them and you find yourself opening the gate — not because you're kind, but because some buried part of you recognizes the shape of him. Somebody running. Somebody carrying a dead country the way I carried whatever-it-was I'd paid to lose. Somebody asking, in the only language that matters, *please let me be nobody here.*

I'd hidden from the world for ten years and called it healing.

Now the world had crawled through my fence, leaking, and it had brought a name with it. Averlaine. I turned the word over in the dark like a stone found in a shoe — wrong, intrusive, impossible to ignore.

In the morning, Odell was standing at my gate.

She had a basket of early plums in one arm, which is how Odell delivered ultimatums, and her eyes went past me to the shed, and stayed there, and the look on her face was one I had never seen in ten years of knowing her.

It was fear.

"Before you say anything," she said quietly, "you'd best tell me whether the man on your floor is carrying what I think he's carrying. Because if it's the wolf-hide—" Her knuckles went white around the basket. "Girl, the last person who asked about that map, we buried behind the meetinghouse. And the Church that killed her is already on the road."
