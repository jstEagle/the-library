## 3. What the Elders Know

The stranger slept for two days in my root cellar, and I spent those two days learning what fear actually was.

I'd thought I knew. I'd thought it was the night a lamb stopped breathing and rain answered me from a sky that had no business holding anything but stars. That had been a crack. This — a bleeding man hidden under my own floor while the village went about its evening two hundred paces away, smoke rising straight from every chimney, everyone safe inside their forgetting — this was the crack widening into something else entirely.

He talked in his fever. Not words, mostly. Names. A name that might have been *Sera*, said over and over with an ache that made my chest hurt to hear it. Once he sat bolt upright in the dark, eyes open and seeing nothing, and said clearly: *"They burn what they can't unmake."* Then he lay back down and shivered until dawn.

I told myself it was none of my business. I told myself I would feed him, bandage him, and point him at the pass as soon as he could walk. I told myself a great many things, and none of them held, because on the second morning I unfolded the wolf-hide again by lamplight and looked properly at the map drawn across its skin.

Roads. Rivers with names in a script that predated anything the Church allowed. Mountains where our mountains weren't. And across the whole of it, written in faded ink gone brown as old blood: **AVERLAINE**.

I didn't know the word. That was the trouble. Everyone in Vell didn't know the word, and we all didn't know it with such completeness that the shape of the not-knowing felt like a scar. You can only miss what you've never been given, and some animal part of me missed it anyway.

I rolled up the hide before Odell's knock came at my door.

She didn't wait to be invited. Odell had never once waited to be invited in forty years of walking through Vell like she owned its stones, which in a sense she did, or at least she owned the memory of them, which amounted to the same thing here. She was small and dry as winter grass and her eyes were the sharpest thing in the valley. She carried a basket of onions she didn't need to carry, because Odell always arrived carrying something, so her hands would look busy and her intentions would look harmless.

"Your lamp burned late," she said.

"I had mending."

"You don't mend." She set down the basket. "Hazel. Girl. There were lights moving along your fence line past midnight, and Bram swears he heard voices on the road, and this morning there are tracks by the border stones that come out of nowhere and stop at your gate." She looked at the root cellar door. It was shut. It was very carefully, deliberately shut. She looked at it the way you look at a lie being told to your face.

"The tracks are mine," I said.

"Hazel."

"Odell." I used her name the way she'd taught me to use it — flat, final, a door closing. She'd taught me everything, Odell. How to salt meat. How to read weather without magic, just wind and ache in old joints. How to survive in a valley built for surviving. And one other lesson, the first one, the one underneath all the others: *don't ask questions.*

"Ten years," she said quietly, "and you've never once lied to me badly enough that I noticed. So let's not start now." She lowered herself onto my stool, joints cracking, and folded her hands. "Whatever's in your cellar, you should know what happened to the last person who brought something across the stones."

My mouth went dry. "There was a last person?"

"There's always a last person." Her voice didn't change at all. That was the frightening part. Odell's anger, if it was anger, lived somewhere below her voice, in the bedrock. "Thirty-one years ago. Before you. A woman named Ilse Vane came through the pass with a book. Just a book. Songs, we heard afterward. Old songs from a place that isn't anywhere." Odell's thumb moved over her knuckles, back and forth. "She asked questions. Gentle ones, at first. At the well. At market. *Have you ever heard a song like this? Does this tune remind you of anything?*"

"And?"

"And for three weeks, nothing happened. People hummed. People liked her. And then Marta's boy started dreaming in a language he'd never learned, and the bread turned in every oven on the same morning, and the goats wouldn't drink downstream of her house." Odell looked up. Her eyes were terrible in their steadiness. "So four men took her to the stones at night and made her cross back over, and she stood on the far side and screamed things at us in that same language her book was written in, and then she walked north toward the pass, and the snow took her. We know the snow took her because the Church told us it did. The Church sent a man to ask why we'd let her in at all."

"What happened to him?"

"Nothing." She smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd ever seen her do. "That's what happened to him. Nothing. He drank our beer, praised our silence, and left. Questions kill, Hazel. Answers kill faster. The last exile died of a *book*. And you've got whatever's in that cellar."

"It's a man," I said. My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "Wounded. Half-dead when I found him at the stones. He was carrying—" I stopped.

"A map," Odell said.

The floor seemed to tilt. "How—"

"Because the wells came back." She said it simply, the way you report frost damage. "The deep well by the chapel. Dry since before you were exiled here. This morning it was full to the brim, cold and sweet, and no rain has fallen. And Widow Cetch set three loaves' worth of dough yesterday and pulled eleven loaves from the oven, risen fat and fine, and she laughed about it, and her laughter scared me worse than the well did." Odell rose, slowly, using the table. "Bread doesn't multiply, Hazel. Wells don't fill. Prayers don't get answered in Vell. That's the entire architecture of this place. That is what we bought with our silence, all of us, generation after generation. And it's starting to fail, and the failing started at *your* fence line, and now there's a man in your cellar carrying a map of a country I have spent my whole life not remembering exists."

"You remember it," I whispered. "You just said its name."

Odell went very still.

Then, softly: "I'm old, girl. Old women misplace words the way they misplace keys. But you listen to me." She crossed the room and took my face in her dry, strong hands, and hers were shaking, and I had never once seen Odell's hands shake. "Whatever he is, whoever sent him, whatever's waking up out there — the Church of the Quiet does not send one man. They sent Ilse Vane's book ahead of her too, did you know that? No? Nobody did. That's how good they are. They will come here wearing kindness. They'll ask gentle questions at the well. And when they're done asking, they'll burn what won't forget politely." She released me. Stepped back. Became, with visible effort, the matriarch again, dry and hard and practical. "I can hold the village for a few days. Fear moves slow in Vell; it's the one crop we grow well. But you move fast. Whatever you decide about that man, decide it soon, and decide it knowing that every hour he breathes our air, the valley gets less quiet."

She picked up her onion basket. At the door she paused, back still turned.

"The lamb," she said. "Bram's daughter saw the rain. One field, one minute, clear sky above. She's seven, Hazel, and she hasn't stopped talking about it, and children in this valley learn quickly which stories get their mothers frightened." A pause. "What did you pray for?"

"Rain," I said. "For a sick lamb. It was just—it was habit. A prayer I didn't even know I remembered."

"Mm." Odell opened the door onto grey morning light. "Ilse Vane prayed too, they say. Out loud, at the stones, the night they made her leave. And the next spring, the wildflowers came up in that spot, out of season, out of soil, in a ring nobody could explain." She stepped out. "Everything costs. Even answers. *Especially* answers."

Then she was gone, and I stood in my kitchen with her warning sitting in my chest like swallowed stone.

* * *

I went down to the cellar because there was nowhere else to put my fear except near the thing that caused it.

Corren was awake. Sitting up against the wall, color marginally better than death, watching me with eyes that tracked too sharply for a man two days off a fever. Someone had trained him to wake quiet. Someone had trained him, period.

"You heard," I said.

"Some." His voice was gravel. "Your matriarch has a carrying voice."

"She wants me to get rid of you."

"I know." He didn't flinch. "It's what I'd want, if I were her. If I were you." He shifted, wincing, hand pressed to the bandage at his side. The wound had stopped seeping; the smell had improved from bad to merely wrong. "But you haven't. Two days, and you haven't."

"Don't mistake me for kind. I'm waiting to see if you're worth the risk."

"No, you're not." He almost smiled. It was a tired, cracked thing. "Kindness isn't a calculation, or I'd be dead already. You pulled me across the stones when you could have watched me die at them. Why?"

And because lying to a fevered man in a root cellar felt suddenly obscene, I said, "You were praying. When I found you. Did you know that? Crawling and bleeding and praying to gods that don't exist anymore. It was the most—" I searched. "*Familiar* thing I'd ever seen."

Something moved behind his eyes at that. Recognition, maybe. Or grief finding its sibling.

"The wells," he said. "The bread. I've seen it before, in the villages near the border. Little miracles, leaking through. It means the door is thinning." He said it like a diagnosis. Like the valley was a patient and the news was bad. "Wherever it thins, prayers start getting through. Wherever prayers get through, the Quiet follows. They can't allow it. Answered prayer is the one heresy they can't argue with, because people *see* it."

"So they'll come here."

"They may already be coming." He held my gaze, and I understood he was choosing to tell me the truth precisely because it endangered him. "Inquisitors travel as pilgrims. Grey cloaks. Soft voices. They arrive alone, humble, asking after rumors of blessing. By the time anyone connects the villages that welcomed them, those villages are ash, and the ash is called fever, and the maps are corrected." He paused. "There's a map under your bed that they burned my sister to find. Sera drew it from memory, from our grandmother's stories, because Averlaine isn't in any archive anymore. They erased it so thoroughly that even its name sounds like nonsense. But the country remembers itself. Countries do. And it's waking up, and it's reaching for anyone who can hear it."

"Why show me any of this?"

"Because of the lamb." He said it plainly. "One field, one minute. Rain answering a prayer in a valley where nothing answers. I've walked nine hundred miles, Hazel Dunmore, following exactly that sign. Because the map needs someone it fits, and I think—" His voice caught, steadied. "I think it fits you."

"I'm a goatherd."

"You're a goatherd whose prayers get answered."

"That's new. That's ten days old."

"Then you'd best decide quickly what you were before it was ten days old," Corren said softly, "because I don't think it's finished deciding for you."

Above us, faint through the earth, I heard the chapel bell begin to ring — and keep ringing, urgent and arrhythmic, the way it only rang for fire or flood or strangers on the road.

Corren closed his eyes. "Grey cloak," he murmured. "Alone. Humble."

We both listened to the bell.

Somewhere in Vell, a stranger was being welcomed in.
