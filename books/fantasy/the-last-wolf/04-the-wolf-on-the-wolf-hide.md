## 4. The Wolf on the Wolf-Hide

The fire came first, the way it always did.

Not heat — Corren had stopped feeling heat somewhere outside Carrow Bridge — but light. Orange light through shutters, light on wet cobbles, light reflected in the eyes of men who held torches the way other men held infants: tenderly, with both hands, as if what they carried was precious.

*We do not hate you,* the Quiet Men always said. *We are saving you from a lie.*

His sister's hand in his. Sera's hand, smaller than his, calloused from the loom. Running.

The dream had no order. Dreams never did. He was back in the archive under the cathedral, twenty years old and proud of it, copying out ledgers by lamplight, when he found the page that shouldn't exist. A tax roll. A province listed in a hand two centuries dead: **Averlaine, revenues remitted to the Crown, blessed be her bells.** One line. He'd copied it out of idle habit, and the copying had changed him — because once you wrote a thing down, some stubborn animal part of your brain refused to let it be unwritten again.

He'd asked the archivist about it. Just asked. That was all.

They burned the archivist on a Thursday.

Corren remembered standing in the square with everyone else, remembering that he must not remember, mouthing the litany along with the crowd — *there is no country beyond the veil, there is no door, there is nothing to forgive* — while a man he'd shared bread with went up like a bundle of straw. And the terrible thing, the thing that woke him sweating even now, ten years later, was that it had *worked*. For six days after, he hadn't been able to recall the man's face. The Church's silence wasn't cruelty. It was surgery. They cut the remembering out of people the way a barber cut away rot, and most people thanked them for it.

Sera hadn't thanked them. Sera had drawn maps.

* * *

He surfaced from the dream into darkness and the smell of tallow and goat.

For a moment he didn't know where he was. The ceiling above him was rough-hewn beams, hung with dried herbs, and someone had put a blanket over him that smelled of lanolin and woodsmoke, and there was a dog — no, not a dog, a memory of a dog, because when he turned his head there was only a stool and a basin of water gone pink.

Then it all arrived at once: the river. The stones. The woman kneeling over him with rain falling upward around her like a thing undone.

"You're awake," she said.

She was sitting against the far wall, wrapped in a shawl, a knife across her knees like she'd forgotten it was there but her hands hadn't. Dark-haired. Ordinary-looking, except for her eyes, which kept flicking toward the window every few minutes as if listening to something he couldn't hear.

"Where—" His voice came out ruined. He tried water and got half of it down before his stomach rebelled. "The village."

"Asleep. Mostly." She rose, crossed the room, sat on the stool beside the cot. Up close she looked tired in a way that went deeper than sleeplessness. "You collapsed at the border stones. You were carrying this." She held up the wolf-hide, folded. "I took the liberty. Don't make me regret being curious."

He reached for it and she let him have it, watching his hands shake as he unfolded it on his chest.

The map. Sera's map. Ten years of her life pressed into skin — not her own skin, thank every god left standing, but still. He traced the border of it without looking, the way you'd touch a scar.

"It's my sister's work," he said. Because she'd earned an answer, and because saying it aloud made it real in a way the fever hadn't allowed.

"The country on it. Averlaine." Hazel — she'd given her name only once, grudgingly, like paying a debt — said the word carefully, tasting it. "There's no such place. I checked the elders' records. There's nothing. Not even a mention of a place that *used* to exist."

"That's rather the point."

She waited. She was good at waiting; he could tell it was a discipline, not a temperament.

So he told her. All of it, or near enough, in the flat voice the telling deserved.

How the Church of the Quiet didn't just suppress magic — anyone could suppress magic, any fool with an iron rod and a license — but suppressed *memory*, systematically, generationally, until whole countries became blank spaces on honest maps. How Averlaine had refused to go quiet. Its gods had been old ones, door-gods, threshold-keepers, and their worshippers had kept remembering in defiance of three centuries of edicts. So the Church had stopped burning individuals and started burning the country itself. Razed it, salted it, and then did the worse thing: erased the record of it. Made it so that the children of the survivors grew up unable to say where their grandmothers' lullabies came from.

"And the ones who remember anyway?" Hazel asked quietly. "The ones the erasure doesn't take?"

"Depends." He smiled, and knew it was a bad smile. "If they're useful, the Church hires them. If they're not—"

"The fire."

"The fire." He folded the map closed. "My sister mapped Averlaine from inside its absence. Do you understand what that means? She walked terrain that isn't on any chart, interviewed people whose memories had been cut and stitched, reconstructed roads from folk songs and grave-marker fragments and one line in a tax ledger I stole from a cathedral basement. It took her eleven years. And the map is alive, in a manner of speaking — it only shows the roads if the walker remembers why they matter. Which is why the Church wanted it. Not to use it. To burn it, so that the last road in would close forever."

Hazel had gone very still. "Why would anyone want *in*?"

"Because the door is closing," Corren said. "The last door. The gods of Averlaine weren't killed, they were *locked out* — locked out of a world that forgot how to pray to them, starving behind a wall of forgetting. And walls don't hold forever. The prayers getting answered here — you've heard about them, don't pretend otherwise, the well, the bread—"

Her face did something complicated. "Go on."

"When a door starts to fail, everything leaks through the cracks first. Little mercies. Rain where there should be none. But the door won't fail open. Doors fail *shut*. And when it shuts for good, the gods die, truly die, and everything they touched goes with them — every song, every spring, every small kindness ever done in their names. The world gets quieter. Permanently." He looked at her. "The map says there's one person who can hold it open. Born of storm. Marked. The map even shows the mark."

"And you think—" She laughed, short and disbelieving. "You collapsed in a river, half-dead. You're in no state to go hunting storm-born saviors."

"I don't need to hunt," Corren said. "I already fell at her feet."

The room went quiet enough that he could hear the goats shifting in the shed outside, and the rain — the strange, local, wrong-headed rain — starting up again against the shutters, soft as fingers tapping.

"There's a mark on the map," he said. "A spiral, broken on one side, like a wave caught mid-curl. Storm-born. The texts call it the wolf-mark, though no one remembers why." He unfolded the hide, found the corner diagram by touch more than sight, and held it up between them. "It's drawn here next to a note in my sister's hand. Her last note. It says: *the marked one will not know what she is.*"

Hazel didn't look at the diagram.

That was the thing he noticed. Anyone else would have looked — curiosity is a reflex, like blinking. She looked instead at the window, at the sound of the rain, and her stillness acquired a new quality. Not disbelief. Recognition, arriving somewhere she didn't want it.

"I'm a goatherd," she said finally.

"Yes," he agreed. "And I'm an archivist."

The rain picked up. Outside, thunder muttered — a low, directionless grumble, like something large turning over in its sleep. Hazel flinched at it, a small motion, quickly mastered, and Corren watched her master it and understood that she had been mastering things for a very long time.

"Your sister," she said. Changing the subject the way you'd step over a body. "Where is she?"

The question landed exactly where he'd known it would land, in the place he'd been keeping it closed since the river. He had rehearsed answers on the walk south. Clever answers, deflecting answers. But he was fevered and hollowed-out and this woman had pulled him from cold water and lied to her own elders for him, and lies felt suddenly like a currency he couldn't afford.

"She's dead," he said. "Three weeks ago. The Quiet Men caught us crossing the Marlow ford. She gave me the map and made me run and stood on the bank to hold them, and she—" His throat closed. He worked past it the way you work past a cramp, mechanically, breath by breath. "She held them. Sera, who cried when our father drowned a litter of kittens. She held six armed men off a ford long enough for me to get away, and I don't know what she used, because she'd never told me she had anything to use, and then I ran, because running was the mission, and the mission mattered more than—" 

He stopped.

The rain filled the silence for him, patient, indifferent.

"She died so the map would arrive," he said at last. "So I've decided it's going to arrive. Wherever it's going. Whatever it costs." He met Hazel's eyes. "I spent ten years telling myself survival was enough. That staying hidden, staying quiet, staying *alive* was the win condition. It isn't. It's just the fee. Grief is a map too, if you let it be. Mine points one direction."

Hazel looked down at the wolf-hide between them, at the inked roads leading into a country that had been murdered twice — once in fact, once in memory — and Corren saw her hand move toward it and stop, hover, like a hand over a flame.

"The storm-born mark," she said. "Say I believed in it. Say I believed any of it. What happens to a goatherd who walks out of this valley following a dead woman's map?"

"I don't know," Corren said honestly. "But I know what happens to the valley if she doesn't. The cracks are already leaking. Your answered prayers aren't gifts, they're pressure. And the Church follows pressure the way wolves follow blood." He paused. "Inquisitors. They'll come gently, at first. They always come gently."

Something crossed her face then — fast, gone — that might have been fear, or might have been its opposite, the look of a person hearing a sentence they'd been dreading and finding it, horribly, a relief.

"You should sleep," she said abruptly, standing, taking the knife back up. "Fever talks. Tomorrow you'll want to unsay half of this."

"Fever doesn't talk," Corren said, lying back, pulling the blanket up. "Fever *listens*. Everything else is talking tonight — the rain, the stones, whatever's answering prayers in this valley. I'm just trying to hear it over my own heartbeat."

At the door she paused, her back to him, firelight making her shadow long and uncertain on the wall.

"The mark," she said, not turning. "Broken spiral. Wave mid-curl." A pause long enough for the thunder to answer somewhere far off. "I was born during a storm that killed three people and blew the roof off the chapel. My mother said the lightning struck the same field twice, both times within arm's reach of her."

She left before he could answer, which was its own kind of answer, and the door clicked shut with the soft finality of a decision not yet made.

Corren lay in the dark, fever receding, grief sitting on his chest like a cat that meant to stay, and listened to the rain fall on a valley that was supposed to be beyond the reach of all of this.

Somewhere out there, Sera's roads waited. Somewhere out there, a door was closing on the last of the old gods, and a woman who claimed to be nobody was lying awake refusing to admit she'd been born in lightning.

*The marked one will not know what she is.*

"Not yet," he murmured to the dark, to Sera, to whoever was listening. "But soon."

Outside, the thunder rolled closer — and this time, he could have sworn it sounded pleased.
