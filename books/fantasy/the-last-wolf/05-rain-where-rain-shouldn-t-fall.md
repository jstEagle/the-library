## 5. Rain Where Rain Shouldn't Fall

The storm found her on the third morning, and it was small, and it was hers.

Hazel knew this the way you know a hand is yours in the dark — not by looking but by the wrongness of doubting it. She had taken the goats up the eastern slope where the grass grew thin and sweet above the treeline, and she'd been angry about nothing in particular: about Corren sleeping in her barn for a second night, about the fever that wouldn't leave him, about Odell's face when she'd looked at the wolf-hide map, which had been the face of a woman reading her own death warrant.

She'd been angry, and then she'd said — out loud, to no one, the way you say things when you've been alone too long — *I wish it would just rain.*

And the sky over the eastern slope, and nowhere else, obliged.

It came down in a column maybe forty paces wide, a grey curtain drawn between her and the rest of Vell. She stood at its edge with her arms out and felt the first drops hit her knuckles, warm as breath. Beyond the curtain the valley lay gold in morning sun. Inside it, rain fell hard enough to flatten the grass, and the goats huddled under an alder looking at her with the flat reproach of animals who have decided their keeper is a problem.

"That's not possible," Hazel told them.

The rain kept falling. It had the particular quality of weather that has been *told* something rather than merely arrived: too neat at the edges, too obedient at the center. When she stepped forward, the curtain stepped with her. When she thought — sharply, experimentally — *stop*, it stopped, mid-fall, droplets hanging like beads on a string for one impossible heartbeat before dropping all at once around her boots.

She walked back down the slope very fast, and the little cloud came after her, low and doglike, trailing its shadow across the hillside like a leash someone had forgotten to shorten.

* * *

By noon half of Vell had seen it.

That was the problem with miracles in a valley built on forgetting: they didn't fade politely. They left tracks. The field below Hazel's croft was green in a stripe where nothing else was green yet, and Tam's mother — not the child Tam, the other Tam, the miller's wife — stood in her doorway pointing at the sky and saying the name of a god Hazel had never heard anyone say aloud in ten years. *Serevan*, she said. *Serevan who waters.* And when she said it, the well behind her house, dry since before Hazel arrived, made a sound like a throat clearing and began, slowly, to gurgle.

Hazel did not go near the mill. She went to Odell's house, because there was nowhere else, because Odell had raised her from nothing and was therefore the only person alive with the right to tell her what was happening to her.

Odell met her at the door before she knocked, as if she'd been standing behind it all morning waiting for exactly this.

"Inside," she said. "Quickly."

The house smelled of tallow and dried thyme. Odell shut the door, dropped the bar, and then stood with her back against it, looking at Hazel the way you look at a fire that has jumped the hearthstone.

"Show me," Odell said.

"Show you what?"

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip. Then Odell closed her eyes and made herself smaller, older. "Please, girl. I haven't slept. I've been sitting here since dawn trying to decide what to say to you, and I would like very much for you not to make me say any of it twice."

So Hazel held out her hand, palm up, the way you'd ask a dog for a paw, and she thought — carefully, deliberately, the way you thread a needle — *rain.*

A cloud the size of a loaf of bread condensed above her palm and drizzled into her cupped fingers.

Odell sat down. Not gracefully. She sat down the way people sit down when their legs have resigned without notice, onto the bench by the cold hearth, and she put both hands flat on her knees, and she watched the little cloud rain itself out above Hazel's hand until there was nothing left of it but a damp patch on the floorboards.

"How long," Odell said.

"Since two days ago. Since I prayed over the lamb."

"I know about the lamb." Odell's voice had gone flat and careful, a voice walking on ice. "Half the valley knows about the lamb. That's what frightens me. It used to be that things noticed you and did nothing. Now they're *answering* you."

"What things? What do you mean, they used to notice me?"

Odell looked at her for a long time. Hazel had lived in this house for six years after arriving in Vell with empty pockets and a head full of fog, and she had catalogued every expression the old woman owned: fondness, exasperation, the particular grim satisfaction she took in winning an argument with the butcher. This was none of them. This was grief wearing patience as a coat.

"You asked me once," Odell said, "why I took you in. You were nineteen and half-dead at the border stones and you had nothing — no name you could hold onto, no papers, no past. Anyone else would have turned you over to the elders and let them decide. I took you in because I recognized what you were."

"A stranger."

"You were *burning*, Hazel." Odell leaned forward. "Not your skin. Underneath. There are people who come through those stones and the stones take everything from them — the magic, whatever they carried — and they walk out the far side hollow and grateful, because hollow doesn't hurt. You came through and the stones couldn't finish the job. You were too big. They got most of it but not all, and what was left leaked. For years it leaked. Little things. Your garden came in two weeks early every spring. Your headaches came with thunderclouds. I told myself it would fade. I told myself a lot of things."

Hazel's mouth had gone dry. "You knew."

"I suspected. There's a difference, and it's the difference that's kept you alive." Odell rose, joints cracking, and went to the hearth. She reached up into the chimney flue — past the soot shelf where every house in Vell hid its valuables — and brought down a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed string, black with ten years of smoke.

"I swore I'd burn this," she said. "I swore it the day I buried the last exile, and I swore it again when you healed that lamb. And then this morning the miller's wife called Serevan by name in front of her children, and the well answered, and I understood that my oaths don't matter anymore. Something has changed on the other side of the stones. Something is reaching through." She set the bundle on the bench between them. "This reached through ten years ago. You brought it. Or it brought you. Open it."

Hazel's hands were steady, which surprised her. Inside the oilcloth was a leather satchel gone stiff with age, and inside the satchel: a strip of bark-paper covered in dense, slanted script in a language she almost recognized, the way you almost recognize a face across a market; a ring of iron, plain, unadorned, cold to the touch even in a warm room; and a sheet of heavy paper folded twice, which she opened, and which stopped her heart.

It was a drawing of her own face. Younger. Harder. Hair cropped short, jaw set, eyes furious — a stranger's fury, a soldier's fury, worn on features she shaved at every morning and saw in every reflection. Beneath the portrait, in careful block letters in the common tongue:

HAZEL DUNMORE.
STORM-CALLED. LAST OF THE LINE OF AVERLAINE.
DO NOT LET HER REMEMBER.
FOR ALL OUR SAKES.

"Averlaine," Hazel said. The word came out of her mouth shaped correctly, accented correctly, and she heard herself say it and felt the floor tilt. "I know that word. From the stranger's map. It isn't real. Odell, the elders said —"

"The elders say a lot of things." Odell sat back down beside her, close now, shoulder to shoulder, the way she used to sit when Hazel was new and woke screaming from dreams she couldn't keep. "Listen to me. Ten years ago a woman crossed our border stones carrying that paper and that ring and a head full of holes. She begged us — she *begged* us — never to let her learn her own name. She said remembering would kill people. She paid everything she had to forget, and the payment was the crossing itself, and she asked only one thing: that if she started to remember anyway, we'd stop her."

"Stop me how?"

"There are ways," Odell said quietly. "The last exile died of one of them. Before your time. He started remembering too — fragments, nothing more — and he wouldn't let it go, he dug and dug, and the elders —" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "He died. Questions are dangerous here, Hazel. They have always been dangerous here. The valley keeps its silence the way a wound keeps its scab, and people who pick at it —"

"People like me."

"People like you," Odell agreed, "and the terrible thing, the thing I have hated myself for ten years, is that I love you, and I hoped, and hope is how wounds get picked open." She took Hazel's hand. Hers was rough as tree bark and shaking. "Look at the paper again. Look at what you wrote to yourself."

*I paid everything,* Hazel thought. The drawing stared up at her, that furious younger face, and beneath it her own handwriting — her handwriting, she knew it the way she knew her hand in the dark — telling her, warning her, *do not let her remember, for all our sakes.* She tried to feel the edges of what lay under the warning. There was something there. There was something enormous there, the way there's something enormous under a frozen lake: pressure, depth, dark water shifting against the underside of the ice.

And the key was turning.

She could feel it turning.

"Why now?" she whispered. "Ten years. Why does it start now?"

"Because of him," Odell said. "Because he came bleeding through my door with a dead country folded up under his arm, asking questions I spent a decade making sure no one would ask. Because the gods are answering prayers in a valley where magic comes to die, and storms follow you around like lost dogs, and somewhere on the other side of those stones something old has remembered *you*, girl. Whatever door you shut —" Odell's grip tightened. "Whatever door you shut, it's opening. And it will open whether or not you remember why you closed it."

Outside, through the shuttered window, the light went suddenly dim.

Hazel felt it before she saw it — a pressure behind her breastbone, a gathering, the sensation of a held breath. She stood. Crossed to the window. Slid the shutter open an inch.

Over her croft, over her barn where Corren lay fevered, hung a single grey cloud. Her cloud. It had followed her home from the eastern slope and waited outside like a faithful, unwelcome thing, and now, sensing her fear, it began — gently, horribly, tenderly — to rain.

*It answers my moods,* she thought, and vertigo went through her like a spear, because that wasn't a discovery. That was a memory trying to surface. She had known that once. She had known that the way she'd known the word *Averlaine*. Someone had told her once — someone whose voice she could almost — a voice calling her name through smoke —

"Hazel." Odell's hand on her arm. "Breathe. Breathe, girl. Don't chase it. Whatever you see, don't chase it —"

But the lake ice was cracking, and under it the water was rising, and somewhere in the drowned dark below her ruined memory a tower was burning and a voice was calling *Hazel, Hazel, Hazel* in a voice that loved her and a voice that hated her and she could not tell if they were the same voice.

The cloud outside wept harder.

She shut the shutter with hands that were not entirely her own.

"Corren," she said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "He's in my barn. If questions kill people in this valley, then he's already dead, because he's nothing *but* questions. And that map —" She turned to Odell, and heard herself speak with the furious certainty of the girl in the drawing, the girl she had paid everything never to be. "That map shows roads through a country that doesn't exist. Except it does exist, doesn't it. It exists in *this* house. You knew the map the moment you saw it."

Odell's silence was answer enough.

"So either you tell me," Hazel said, "or I ask him. And if the elders come for questions, Odell, they'll find mine first."

For a moment the old woman simply looked at her — at the stranger wearing her daughter's face — and Hazel watched her weigh ten years of silence against one afternoon of rain.

Then Odell went to the door and lifted the bar.

"Not here," she said. "And not with him awake. Come at dark. And Hazel —" She paused, hand on the door, not looking back. "When you remember — and you will, God help us both, you will — remember that I loved you before you were anyone at all. That's the only prayer I've got left, and nobody's answering it."

The cloud outside had stopped raining. It hung over the barn, patient as a promise, waiting for her to feel something.

Hazel walked toward it through the bright, breaking day.
