## 6. The Church Arrives Quietly

The pilgrim came on a Tuesday, in the hour when the village smelled of bread and everyone's guard was lowest.

I knew she was trouble before I knew her face. Trouble has a walk. It comes through a place like it belongs there already, unhurried, hands open, and it watches the way other people watch — sideways, cataloguing. Most travelers to Vell watched the valley itself, the terraced fields, the border stones with their worn old carvings. This woman watched the people watching her.

She wore gray wool gone soft at the elbows, a walking staff polished by years of roads, and dust on her hem that said she'd come far on foot, which pilgrims do, which honest pilgrims do. She'd tied her hair back plainly. Her boots were good but mended, mended again, mended a third time — the boots of someone poor or of someone performing poverty so well she'd stopped noticing the difference herself.

Hazel was at the well when the pilgrim reached it, and I was in Odell's doorway pretending to mend a net I had no idea how to mend, and so I saw everything: how Hazel went still, just slightly, the way animals go still; how the pilgrim smiled at her with such open, unguarded warmth that my stomach dropped before my mind caught up and told me why.

Because I knew that smile. I'd grown up under it. The Church of the Quiet trains its kindnesses the way other institutions train their soldiers — patiently, thoroughly, until the kindness is load-bearing. Maren Sloe used to smile at novices like that right before asking them to name their doubts. *Tell me what you're ashamed of,* that smile said. *I'm safe. You can put it down here.*

Three years since I'd seen her. She'd been a different rank then, plainer title, plainer gray. But the Church doesn't change its instruments much. It tunes them.

* * *

"Water for a thirsty traveler?" she asked Hazel, and her voice carried just enough — not loud, but pitched for the ears at the surrounding doorways, the women shelling peas, the boys loitering by the smithy. Everything about her was pitched. "I've walked from the coast. I'm collecting stories, if your village will trade them. Old ones, mostly. The kind grandmothers keep."

"We don't have many of those," Hazel said. Flat. Careful. I'd learned her careful voice by then, the one she used when something underneath was moving.

"No?" The pilgrim accepted the dipper with both hands, as if it were an honor. "That's a pity. Though—" she drank, lowered the dipper, and let her gaze drift across the valley with apparent idle pleasure, "—it seems a blessed sort of place. Rain out of a clear sky, they were saying, down the road. A lamb brought back from fever. Wells that ran dry filling overnight." She laughed softly, embarrassed by her own appetite. "Forgive me. Pilgrims are greedy for miracles. It's the whole trade."

"You've heard wrong things," Hazel said.

"Probably." The woman smiled again, and this time the smile found Hazel's eyes and stayed there a half-second too long, the way you look at a mark on a map, confirming coordinates. "Most things I hear are wrong. That's why I ask so gently."

Then she moved on, staff tapping stone, and asked the pea-shellers whether they remembered any songs about rain-callers, and asked the smith's boy whether he'd ever seen lightning stand still, and asked everything in exactly that tone — wondering, warm, faintly foolish, the tone of a woman who could be told anything because she'd believe none of it anyway.

I stood in the doorway with the useless net in my hands and felt the paranoia in my chest tighten from a wire into a rope.

It was her. It was Sloe. And she wasn't here by accident, and she wasn't here for the villagers, whatever she collected from them. She was here because answered prayers are a scent, and the Church tracks scents, and somewhere behind those soft gray robes was a ledger with a wolf-hide map written in its margins.

* * *

I found Hazel splitting wood behind Odell's house, which is what she did instead of talking, and I said her name once, low, and she turned with the axe still raised, and whatever she saw in my face made her lower it.

"The pilgrim," I said. "Gray wool. Brown eyes. Left hand shorter than the right — two fingers lost at the second knuckle."

Hazel waited. She was good at waiting. Ten years of goats will teach you that or nothing will.

"Her name is Maren Sloe," I said. "Inquisitor Second Chair, Church of the Quiet. Or she was, three years ago. They burn people, Hazel. Not heretics — that would be almost respectable. They burn *rememberers*. Anyone who can recall a country called Averlaine. Priests, archivists, children who sing the wrong songs. They burn them quietly, at night, and in the morning the town agrees it never happened, because agreeing is easier than the fire."

"How do you know her?"

"I grew up in the Quiet." I heard my own voice go strange. "My father was a censor of records. My sister and I were raised on catechisms about forgetting being mercy. I memorized Averlaine the way you'd hoard food before a famine — secretly, in pieces, off my father's forbidden maps. And when the Church found out what my sister kept in her head—"

I stopped. Hazel set the axe against the wall. She didn't come closer, but she didn't leave either, and after ten years of learning her I knew that was the same thing as staying.

"She didn't survive it," I said. "The questioning. They didn't even need the fire. And I took her map and I ran, and I have been running through every country that isn't on it, and now the country that *is* on it has sent her, because of course it has. Answered prayers. Miracles. A storm that follows one woman like a dog." I made myself say it plainly, because she deserved plain. "They're not investigating Vell, Hazel. They're investigating *you*. And Sloe doesn't knock doors down. She walks in wearing humility and asks gentle questions, and by the time anyone understands what's happening, the gentle questions have names attached."

Hazel was quiet for a long moment. Beyond the fence, the valley did its ordinary evening work — smoke rising straight, goats complaining, the light going gold and then gray over the border stones. Somewhere in that ordinary evening, I knew, the gray-wooled pilgrim was lodging herself in someone's guest room and being charming at supper.

"If we run," Hazel said finally, "we confirm it."

"If we stay, we die."

"Not necessarily tonight." She picked up another log, considered it, didn't split it. "Odell will want to know. Odell knows more than she says. She always has." Her jaw worked. "And Corren — if your Church is coming for me, they'll come for the village too. For anyone who helped. For anyone who *prayed*." Her knuckles whitened on the axe handle. "I won't hand them a reason to burn Vell."

"They don't need reasons. They need witnesses. Reasons are what they invent afterward."

"I know." She said it strangely, like someone agreeing to weather. "I know better than you think."

There it was again — that locked room behind her eyes, the one I'd been circling since the border stones. I wanted to ask. I wanted it the way the starving want bread. But the light was dying and Sloe was in the village and there wasn't time for anyone's buried country, least of all hers.

"Hazel," I said. "When she finds you — and she will find you, she's the best finder they have — she won't threaten you first. She'll *help* you. She'll fix your roof. She'll sit with your sick lamb. She'll be the kindest person you've met in years, and every kindness will be a hook, and by the time you feel the line go tight you'll already be gutted." I swallowed. "Kindness is how they hunt. I need you to understand that. Whatever she offers you, however gentle she seems — she is not gentle. Gentle is the costume."

Hazel looked at me for a long moment, and something moved through her face too fast for me to read — recognition, maybe, or grief, or the memory of some other kindness that had also been a hook.

"All right," she said. "Then we watch her back."

* * *

We watched her back for three days, and it was worse than watching a blade.

Sloe was everywhere and nowhere, and she was *lovely*, that was the horror of it. She mended the widow Saret's shutter without being asked. She sat with old Bren through his coughing and knew, somehow, the exact herbs his dead wife used to boil. She listened to children the way no adult in Vell ever had — crouched down, eye level, treating their nonsense as scripture — and inside two days they adored her, trailed her through the lanes like ducklings, sang her the skipping songs that had no business surviving in a valley that forgot things.

And she asked. Oh, she asked. Always sideways, always wrapped in wonder, never twice in the same direction.

"Do you suppose," she said over supper at Odell's table — Odell had taken her in, because Odell took everyone in, because that was the trap of hospitality — "that a prayer could be answered by accident? If enough people wanted a thing in the same direction? Like water finding a crack?"

"Prayers get answered," Odell said, ladling stew, giving away nothing. "By whoever's listening."

"Mm." Sloe's spoon paused, just briefly, above the bowl. "And if no one's listening? If the listeners are all — gone?" She smiled her open smile around the table. "Forgive me. I ask foolish questions. It's how I pray, really. Asking."

Under the table, my knee pressed against Hazel's. *Don't.* She was coiled tight as a snare-trap beside me; I could feel it. Across the table, Odell's face was a closed door with the lamp blown out behind it.

Later, washing bowls, Sloe spoke to Hazel directly for the first time since the well, and I was close enough to hear it because I made sure to be.

"You carry grief like a stone in a sling," she said, gentle as falling snow. "I recognize it. I carry mine the same way. It changes the way rain sounds, grief does. Have you noticed? After loss, weather feels — addressed to you. Personal."

Hazel said nothing. Water dripped. Somewhere outside, absurdly, a goat complained.

"When I lost my sister," Sloe went on, softly, to the bowls, to the steam, "I prayed for the first time in my life. Not to God. Just — out loud, into the dark, *please, please, please.* And nothing answered." She looked up then, and her brown eyes were wet and utterly sincere and I wanted to scream, because I had seen those eyes over burning archives and they had been exactly as sincere. "But you — I think something answers you. Don't be afraid of it. Whatever it is, whatever it costs — being answered is a terrible gift, and you shouldn't carry it alone."

*I know,* I thought, gripping the doorframe. *I know, I know, that's exactly how she opens people, that's the whole liturgy — find the wound, speak to the wound, become the wound's only friend—*

But Hazel had gone very still, and her face had done something I hadn't seen before: not fear. Longing. Ten years in a valley where magic died at the stones, ten years of numbness worn like armor — and here was a stranger saying *you are not broken, you are answered,* in a voice like absolution, and I watched the hooks sink in real time and could do nothing, because the cruelest part of Sloe's method was that every word of it was true.

* * *

On the fourth night I couldn't stay in the house anymore. The walls had gotten too much like walls. I went out past the goat pen, past the border stones' pale shapes in the dark, and stood where the land started forgetting, and tried to breathe.

That was where Hazel found me. Or followed me. With her, lately, I couldn't tell the difference.

"She asked Odell about you," Hazel said, without preamble. "Tonight, after I went up. Asked whether I'd 'always lived in Vell.' Odell said yes. Odell lied smoothly, which frightened me more than the question did."

"It should frighten you. Odell lying means Odell knows what's hunting us."

"Corren." She came closer. In the dark her face was mostly shadow and the pale smear of her eyes. "What happens to a village that shelters us? Truly. No softening it."

I looked back at the valley. Lamplight in windows. Smoke smell. Someone laughing, far off, at nothing.

"Sloe writes a report," I said. "The report goes to a Chapter House. The Chapter House sends a Purification Detail. It takes months. There's a night when the village wakes to fire, and by morning there's a charred absence and a paper trail proving Vell was never incorporated, never taxed, possibly never existed. And every neighboring village agrees, because agreeing is cheaper." I made myself turn back to her. "That's what sheltering us costs. That's the price of kindness given to the wrong people in the wrong decade. So when I say run—"

"I heard you." Her voice cracked on it, barely. "I hear you. But Corren — where? Your map shows a country that doesn't exist. Mine shows a life I paid to forget. Every road out of here ends in either a lie or a lockup." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I have spent ten years building a small quiet thing worth protecting. And now the choice is give it up or watch it burn, and I don't — I don't know how to—"

She stopped. Her hands came down slowly from her face.

Behind her, up the lane, a lantern was approaching. Odell's rolling gait, unmistakable, and moving fast for a woman her age.

"She's gone," Odell said, before she'd fully reached us, breath ragged. "Packed before dawn, left a thank-you note in handwriting neat as print. Gone toward the pass." She planted the lantern between us; moths immediately threw themselves at the glass. "But before she left, she asked me one last question. Standing in my kitchen with her bag on her shoulder, smiling that smile." Odell's voice, for the first time in the ten years I'd known her, shook. "She asked me if I'd ever heard of a storm-caller named Hazel Dunmore. By name. By *full* name. And then she thanked me for my hospitality, and she said—" the old woman closed her eyes— "she said, *tell her I'll be gentle when I find her.*"

The moths battered the lantern. Down in the dark valley, the border stones hummed, very faintly, like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing.

Hazel looked at me across the light.

"Run," she said. "Now it's me telling you. Run."
