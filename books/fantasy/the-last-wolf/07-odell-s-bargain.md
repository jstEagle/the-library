## 7. Odell's Bargain

Odell didn't knock. She never had, in ten years — she came into Hazel's cottage the way weather came into a valley, without apology and with every intention of staying until it was done.

She found Hazel boiling water over the peat fire, Corren asleep in the loft above, his fever finally broken but his breathing still shallow as a bird's. The wolf-hide map lay rolled and hidden under the loose floorboard by the bed. Hazel had checked it three times since dawn.

"Sit," Odell said, though it wasn't an offer, and took the chair by the fire herself, leaving Hazel to perch on the bench like a scolded child. At sixty-eight, Odell Fenn still sat like a woman presiding over judgment. Her hands were folded on her stick, her knuckles big as walnuts, her eyes the flat grey of slate before rain.

"His fever's down," Hazel said.

"I know. I brought willowbark yesterday. I know everything that happens in this valley, girl, that's the whole point of me." She looked at the fire for a long moment. "The well at Marsh End came back up last night."

Hazel said nothing.

"Sweet water. Cold as snowmelt. Old Tammas wept over it, knelt right down in the mud and prayed, and do you know what he prayed *to*? Nothing. A name he made up out of the dark, because something answered and he needed it to have a face." Odell's voice stayed level, which was how Hazel knew how bad it was. When Odell was angry she got loud; when she was frightened she got quiet as a held breath. "And the widow Sorrel baked twelve loaves from flour that should have made four. And now there's talk. Talk, Hazel. In a valley whose entire purpose is that nothing ever talks."

"They're just small things—"

"*Small* is how it starts." Odell leaned forward, and the firelight caught the deep lines of her face, carved deeper than Hazel remembered them being even a month ago. "Small is how it started in Averlaine, if the stories are true. Small prayers answered, small wonders, and then one day the sky opens and the Church arrives with its wagons and its kind voices and its quiet little fires." She let that sit. "You know what they do. The boy told you. He told you what happened to anyone who remembered."

"He was delirious when he—"

"He was clear enough. Maren Sloe. Inquisitor of the Quiet. He named her twice in his sleep." Odell unfolded her hands and laid them flat on her knees. "So we're past pretending you don't know what's coming. Good. I hate pretending. It takes energy I don't have anymore."

Hazel stood up, mostly because sitting felt too much like surrender. She went to the window instead. Outside, the evening was doing what Vell evenings always did: going gold, then violet, then dark, in perfect unremarkable order. The goats were in their pen. Smoke rose straight from a dozen chimneys. Somewhere a dog barked once and thought better of it.

Ten years. Ten years of exactly this. She had learned to love its very blandness, its stubborn refusal of anything miraculous, the way a prisoner learns to love the shape of her cell because at least it holds.

"You want me to leave," she said.

"I want you to *live*," Odell said. "Those aren't the same thing, but tonight they're close enough."

* * *

Odell laid it out the way she laid out everything — plainly, practically, with no decoration, because decoration was for people who hadn't buried two husbands and a daughter.

There was a drover's road out of the valley, north over the shoulder of the Cwm, that the border stones didn't watch closely. An old shepherd named Brannoc owed Odell more than he could repay and asked no questions anyway, having lost most of his curiosity along with most of his hearing. He'd take a cart of late-season ewes up to the high pasture on Thursday, and two extra bodies could go with him, wrapped in sacking if it came to it, and be over the pass before anyone — pilgrim or otherwise — knew they'd gone.

"And after?" Hazel asked. "After the pass?"

"After the pass the world is wide, and the Church is busy everywhere, and two nobodies with no names can disappear into it the way water disappears into sand." Odell's voice softened by a degree, which for Odell was practically a lullaby. "You could go south. Coast towns are good for forgetting. Everyone's forgotten something there; nobody asks."

"And him? Corren? He can barely walk."

"The boy goes too, obviously. You think I'm offering to save only half of you?" Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile. "He's your trouble. I'm offering to take the pair of you off my valley's back before that woman in the pilgrim's grey finishes asking her gentle questions and starts asking ungentle ones."

Hazel turned from the window. "And Vell? What happens here after we're gone?"

"What happens anywhere. People live. People forget. That's the covenant, girl — it's why the valley exists. The founders built it so that magic dies at the stones, so that anything hunting magic would look at Vell and see nothing worth stopping for. We are a valley of *no consequence*, and our safety lives in exactly that." Odell tapped her stick once against the floorboards, a small sharp punctuation. "But the storm follows you. The prayers answer around you. Whatever's leaking through, it's leaking through *you*. Cut yourself out of the valley, and the leak stops. Vell goes back to sleep. Nobody burns."

"That's a guess."

"It's a wager. It's the best wager I have." Odell's eyes fixed on her, and there it was — beneath all the granite practicality, the thing Hazel had seen only a handful of times in ten years: fear. Real fear, for Hazel specifically, wearing Odell's face like an ill-fitting coat. "I raised you from a half-dead, half-mad child who screamed at rain. I am not going to stand here and watch some grey-cloaked ghoul feed you to her god. So swear it. Swear you'll never come back, never cross the stones again, never so much as dream toward Vell, and Thursday you ride north in a cart full of sheep and live to be old and boring somewhere the sun doesn't know your name."

*Swear it.* The words hung in the smoke-warm air between them.

Hazel heard herself say, quietly, "That's exile."

"Don't be stupid. Exile is what other people did to you. This is rescue."

"It's exile with kinder furniture." Hazel's voice came out flatter than she intended. "You're asking me to swear away the only home I've got. Again."

Something moved behind Odell's eyes — hurt, quickly buried, buried so fast and so expertly that Hazel almost missed it, and hated herself for causing it, and hated the burying for making her feel guilty about noticing.

"Home," Odell repeated. She looked around the cottage slowly, at the mended shutters and the patched roof and the goat-tallow candles Hazel made herself. "Is this home? Or is it a cell you decorated?"

"It's where I'm known."

"You're known as the quiet Dunmore woman who tends goats and keeps to herself. Girl, that's not being known, that's being *tolerated efficiently*." Odell rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and crossed to the window where Hazel had stood. For a moment they were both looking at the same view: the pen, the chimneys, the small lit windows of the village, the whole soft ordinary machinery of Vell winding down toward dark. "I've spent ten years keeping you safe by keeping you small. I'd have done it forever. But the ground's moving under us now, and I can't hold it still with love alone. So I'm doing the arithmetic. Two people over a pass alive, versus a valley full of ash. Any fool can do that sum."

"Not any fool," Hazel said. "Only a fool who's already decided I'm the problem."

"You *are* the problem!"

The words cracked out of her louder than Odell had spoken in years, loud enough that upstairs Corren stirred, the loft boards creaking. Odell closed her eyes, gathered herself back down to granite.

"Yes," she said, quieter. "You are the problem. You've been the problem since the night I found you at the stones, soaked through, with no name you'd give and no past you could bear. I chose you anyway. Don't you dare tell me I haven't earned the right to try to keep you."

The fire popped. Upstairs, the creaking settled. Hazel realized her hands were shaking, and pressed them flat against her thighs.

"I won't swear it," she said.

"Hazel—"

"No. Listen to me." And something in her chest was hardening, cooling, setting like poured wax — the strange calm that had been building in her for days now, since the lamb, since the rain, since the map. "I spent ten years being a woman with a locked room where her life used to be. I gave up whatever I was because someone — maybe me, maybe worse than me — decided forgetting was mercy. And it worked. I was *nothing*, Odell. I was comfortable. I was safe. And then a bleeding man crawled to our border carrying a country that officially never existed, and now bread multiplies and dead wells fill and the gods I apparently helped bury are knocking on the door of the world, and you want me to run south and knit?" She shook her head. "I ran once. Whoever I was, I ran, or I hid, or I paid some terrible price to stop knowing what I'd done. I'm not going to spend the rest of this life running too. Not again."

"This isn't about courage, it's about survival—"

"It's about *Vell*." Hazel stepped closer. "You said it yourself. The valley's whole purpose is to be forgotten. To be safe by being nothing. Well, I lived that, Odell. I *was* Vell for ten years — small, silent, no consequences. And I'm telling you what I know from the inside: nothing stays nothing forever. Silence isn't shelter. It's just a debt, and debts get called." She gestured toward the window, toward the village lights. "Maren Sloe is here because of me, fine. But she'll burn this valley whether I'm in it or over the pass, because burning is what the Quiet *does*, and a valley where wells rise and loaves multiply is already marked. Running doesn't save these people. It just means I won't be standing next to them when it happens."

"And what would you do instead?" Odell's voice had gone thin. "Fight her? You don't even know what you are. You don't remember what you were."

"No," Hazel agreed. "I don't. But the answers are on the other side of those stones, and I'm done letting other people decide what I pay for my own past."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The fire burned low. Outside, the first stars were pricking through the violet over the Cwm, over the pass where the sheep-cart would go on Thursday, over the whole escape route Odell had built for her out of love and terror and arithmetic.

When Odell finally spoke, her voice was the quietest Hazel had ever heard it.

"You sound like her."

"Like who?"

"Never mind." Odell picked up her stick. At the door she stopped, hand on the latch, back to Hazel — an old woman's straight back, refusing to bend. "Thursday's cart leaves at first light. If you're in it, you're in it. If you're not—" She paused. The pause stretched. "If you're not, then you were never really mine, and I'll grieve you while you're still alive to see it done."

"That's cruel."

"Yes," Odell said. "It is. Cruelty's the only honest tool left in my kit." She opened the door onto the cold blue dusk. "Think carefully, girl. Think about who you're choosing, and what it costs, and whether the person paying is you or everyone around you. Because that's the question that broke the world the first time, if the stories are true. Someone chose themselves, and a whole country paid."

The door closed behind her.

Hazel stood alone in the firelight with the words still hanging — *someone chose themselves, and a whole country paid* — and felt them land somewhere deeper than argument, deeper than defiance, in that locked room inside her where the key was turning whether she willed it or not.

Above her, the loft boards creaked. Corren was awake. Had been awake, probably, for all of it.

She climbed the ladder. He was sitting up in the dark, grey-faced, watching her with eyes that had seen a country die.

"She's wrong," Hazel said, though her voice shook. "Running won't save anyone. Will it?"

Corren was quiet a long time. When he finally answered, his voice was rough from fever and honesty.

"No. But that's not why you're staying, is it?"

Down in the village, a bell began to ring — the evening bell, ordinary, faithful, ten years of the same note — and as Hazel listened, it faltered, stumbled, and rang a second bell underneath itself, older, lower, a sound like a door remembering how to open.

She had never heard Vell's bell make that sound.

Neither, by the look on his face, had Corren.

And far off, beyond the border stones, beyond the Cwm, lightning flickered once against the clouds — silent, patient, like something waiting to be called by name.
