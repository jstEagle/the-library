## 8. Crossing the Stones

Hazel

The sermon was to be held at midday in the market square, which meant half of Vell would be pressed shoulder to shoulder beneath the new pilgrim's gentle voice while the other half stood at their doors pretending not to listen.

"She'll count us," Odell had said that morning, her hands steady as she wrapped bread and salted fish into a cloth. "That's what they do first. They count. Then they ask who wasn't there."

"Then we'll be there," Hazel said. "For the counting."

Odell looked at her for a long moment — the look she'd been giving Hazel since Hazel was nine years old and lying about a broken crock — and then she'd said only, "After the amen, you go. Not before. A missing face during a prayer is noticed. A missing face after is just weather."

Now the square smelled of dust and crushed thyme where people had trampled the herb-sellers' baskets. Maren Sloe stood on the mounting block in her plain gray dress, hands folded, and she did not preach so much as murmur, the way rain murmurs against a shutter. The Quiet God, she said, was not absent but patient. Was not silent but listening. She asked nothing of them. She only wanted, she said, to share the comfort of quietness with a valley that had earned its peace.

*Earned,* Hazel thought, and Corren's elbow pressed hers, once.

He stood beside her in the crowd wearing Odell's dead husband's coat, his wound bound under it, his face arranged into the blank devotion of a man who has been to forty sermons in forty villages. He was good at vanishing. It frightened her how good he was at it.

"Amen," said the crowd, soft as ash falling.

They were moving before the word finished, peeling away through the lane behind the smithy, past the tannery's stink, up the goat path where no one went at noon because noon was for bread and gossip. Corren walked fast despite the pull of the bandage. Hazel carried everything they owned in one sack: the wolf-hide map, three days of food, Odell's knife, and a jar of honey Tam's mother had left on Hazel's doorstep two nights ago without a note, which Hazel chose to understand as forgiveness or farewell or both.

The border stones rose out of the hillside like teeth.

There were seven of them, older than the village, older than any record, each taller than a man and leaning together as if whispering. Moss covered their faces. In ten years Hazel had never touched them. No one in Vell touched them; children learned before they could walk that the stones were not for climbing. Beyond them the land fell away into scrub and pine, and beyond that, the world.

She stopped three paces off, and her body simply refused to close the distance, the way a hand refuses a hot iron.

"You feel it," Corren said. Not a question.

"I've always felt it." She heard her own voice come out flat and strange. "Like standing at the edge of sleep and hearing someone say your name."

"That's what it is." His eyes moved across the stones, cataloguing, the way he catalogued everything — exits, weapons, lies. "A name. Yours, maybe."

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended. "Don't say things like that yet. Say them when I'm already through."

Corren nodded slowly. Then, because he was Corren, who hid grief inside practicality the way other men hid knives inside coats: "The pilgrim will finish her blessing within the hour. Then she'll walk the perimeter — she always does, first day. We want to be over and down the far slope before she rounds the north field."

"I know the plan."

"You're stalling."

"I'm *saying goodbye*," Hazel snapped, and immediately hated herself for it, because there was nothing here to point at, no house worth naming, no grave, no one standing behind her weeping. Just goats in a far field and an old woman who had told her last night, flatly: *Go. And don't come back. That's my price.*

But Odell had also packed the honey. Odell had also sewn coin into the hem of Hazel's coat while Hazel slept, badly hiding the lump of it, and neither of them would ever speak of it.

"Hazel," Corren said quietly. "Whatever's on the other side of those stones — whatever comes back to you — it doesn't change what you built here. Ten years isn't erased by ten minutes."

"No," she said. "It's *explained* by them. That's worse."

And she walked forward before she could think better of it, and laid her palm flat against the nearest stone.

Nothing happened.

That was the horror of it — nothing. No lightning, no voice, no wall of force. Just cold granite sweating under moss, ordinary as any rock in any valley. She almost laughed. All those years of flinching from these stones, all the village dread wrapped around them like wire, and here was the great boundary of Vell, dumb and passive as a fencepost.

Then she stepped past it.

The change came not from the stone but from her — something deep in her chest that had been clenched for so long she'd forgotten it was a fist. Passing the line, it opened.

Not gradually. Not gently. Like a dam going out from under.

Hazel's knees hit the turf and the world *roared* in.

It was sound first — every sound she hadn't heard in ten years arriving at once, the true texture of wind, the voices in running water, the slow conversation of roots underground. It was light second, colors splitting open like overripe fruit. And underneath all of it, rising through the soles of her boots and up her spine and out through the crown of her head, was *power*, raw and enormous and furious at having been kept waiting, flooding into her like water finding a breach—

—and memories came with it, riding the flood.

*A tower, burning from the top down though there was no fire at its base. Her own hands raised, shaking, holding back something vast. Rain turning to steam above her. A voice — not a shout, a* calling *, the kind of calling that rearranges your bones, someone screaming her name — her other name, her real name, the one she'd paid to forget — from high in that burning tower—*

"*Hazel.*"

She surfaced. Corren had her by the shoulders, shaking her, shouting her name — her exile-name, her small safe name — and she realized she'd been screaming too, that the birds had gone silent for a quarter mile, that the grass around her knees was flattened outward in a perfect ring as if a giant's hand had pressed down there.

And it was raining.

Above her. Only above her. A column of cloud maybe thirty feet wide, churning black-green, shedding fat warm drops onto the scorched hillside while the rest of the sky stayed blue and indifferent. The little storm turned when she turned, followed her gaze when she looked east, pulsed once, twice — like a heartbeat catching up after a long fright.

"It followed me," she whispered. "From the valley. Through the—" She couldn't finish. Couldn't say *the stones*. Saying it made it real, and it was already unbearably real, thrumming in her blood like a struck bell that would not stop ringing.

"The mark on the map," Corren said, and his voice had gone reverent and afraid in equal measure. "*Born of storm.* Hazel, this is it. This is what the door needs."

She looked down at her own hands. They were glowing faintly, the way coals glow after the fire seems dead, arcs of pale light crawling between her fingers like the static off a cat's fur in winter. Ten years. Ten years of numbness, of safety, of being nobody, and it had all been a held breath, and now she was exhaling and drowning at once.

The memory fragments circled her like gulls. *The burning tower. The voice calling. Hands raised against something vast.* She clawed at them, trying to make them whole, and they scattered — leaving only a feeling, huge and sickening, that she had done something once. Something enormous. Something with a price she was still paying.

"Hazel. Can you walk?"

She could. She didn't know how, but she could, because her legs remembered a body that had walked through storms all its life, even if her mind didn't. She got up. The little raincloud drifted obediently overhead, shading her, and she thought with something near hysteria: *it's like a dog. It thinks it's helping.*

They went down the far slope fast, half-running, the border stones shrinking behind them into seven gray teeth in the green. Once, Hazel glanced back toward Vell, tiny and sunlit in its bowl of hills, and felt the strangest sensation — that she was looking at a place she'd read about in someone else's book.

At the bottom of the slope, in the shade of a lightning-split pine, she stopped and put her hands on her knees and let the shaking take her properly.

Corren crouched beside her. He didn't touch her. He waited, which was perhaps the kindest thing anyone had done for her in a decade.

"There was a tower," she said finally.

"Yes?"

"Burning. I was in it. Or below it. And someone was calling my name." Her throat closed. "I don't know whose voice it was. I don't know why that frightens me more than the fire does."

"Because the fire ends," Corren said softly. "Being called doesn't. Someone wanted you, and part of you has spent ten years making sure you never find out who." He paused. "My sister prayed in a tower once. Before the Church burned it. Maybe the world is smaller than we think."

The rain-cloud settled lower over them, gentling, dropping a fine cool mist. Hazel held her palms up into it and watched the drops bend toward her skin, eager, devoted.

"Keep talking," she said. "On the road. Tell me about Averlaine. Tell me about your sister. Because I'm going to need reminding who I am, Corren, and until my memory comes back—" she flexed her glowing fingers, watching the light gutter and flare, "—you may be all of me there is."

* * *

Corren

He watched her sleep by the dead fire and understood, finally, why the Church feared the storm-born more than any plague.

It wasn't the power. Power was common enough, in the old country; the Church had burned weather-wives and bone-readers by the cartload and called it hygiene. What they feared about Hazel Dunmore — what Maren Sloe would cross the world to smother — was what he'd seen at the border stones. The way the sky *chose* her. The way a thing as vast and mindless as a storm had looked at this woman and decided, instantly and forever, to follow her like a dog.

You could kill a witch. You could not kill being chosen.

Corren pulled the wolf-hide map from the sack and unrolled it across his knees, careful of the stiff brown stains that were Sera's blood and would never wash out. By moonlight the roads of Averlaine shimmered faintly, inked in silver that only showed when it wanted to. The palace of glass at the center. The seven roads radiating out like spokes. And at the heart of the palace, drawn so small you'd miss it unless you knew to look: a door, and beside the door, a single glyph.

Sera had translated it once, sitting cross-legged on their kitchen floor, tracing it with a flour-dusted finger. *Only the storm may knock. Only the storm may open. Born of thunder, orphaned by choice.*

*Orphaned by choice.* He'd thought it meant her parents. Now, glancing across the dead fire at the sleeping woman with rain-light still crawling faintly under her skin, he wondered if it meant something else entirely. If it meant she had *chosen* to be empty. Chosen exile. Chosen forgetting.

Who pays a decade of themselves to forget? Only someone who remembers something unbearable.

He should wake her. They should be walking — Maren Sloe would find the empty chapel seats by now, would smile her terrible gentle smile, would begin asking. Every hour they sat still was an hour she gained.

Instead Corren sat in the dark and studied Hazel Dunmore's sleeping face and felt the map burn against his knees like a second heart.

Because there was one more thing on the hide, one more marking, in the corner where the border of Averlaine met the mountains. A sigil he'd shown no one, not even her. Three strokes, like a bird's footprint in snow.

Sera's mark. The mark she signed her prayers with. And beside it, in her handwriting, in their mother-tongue, four words:

*Ask her about the tower.*

He had asked. She had answered with fragments — a burning tower, a calling voice. But tonight, watching the storm-light pulse under her skin in time with some distant rhythm he couldn't hear, Corren felt the shape of a suspicion assembling itself in him, piece by piece, the way a storm assembles itself out of innocent air.

Ten years ago, the gods died. Ten years ago, Averlaine began to fade. Ten years ago, a girl walked out of the mountains into Vell and asked to be emptied.

He looked at her hands, curled in sleep, still faintly luminous. At the little cloud that had trailed them all day and now hung over her like a guardian.

*What did you do, Hazel Dunmore?*

Far off, beyond the ridge, a bell rang — one clear bronze note where no bell should be, in country that officially did not exist. Hazel's eyes snapped open, wide and grey and full of storm.

"You heard it too," she said.

It wasn't a question. And it wasn't the bell that frightened him. It was the way she said *too* — as if, somewhere behind her locked and flooding memory, she already knew exactly which bell it was, and had missed it for ten years.
