## 9. Roads That Aren't There

The map lied about the distance.

That was the first thing Hazel understood on the morning after they crossed the stones, standing at the edge of a wheat field that the wolf-hide insisted was three days' walk from where she'd slept. The field stretched to a horizon that shimmered like heat off iron, and the wheat was gold — not harvest-gold but painted-gold, too bright, each stalk identical to its neighbors as if some careful hand had drawn one stalk and then copied it ten thousand times.

"It's wrong," she said.

Corren crouched at the field's edge, turning the map over in his hands. His wound had closed overnight into an angry red seam, and he moved like a man whose body had been loaned back to him on uncertain terms. "It's right," he said. "That's the problem."

He pressed his finger to the hide. The ink — or whatever passed for ink here — shifted under his touch, roads sliding across the skin of the animal like worms turning in soil. "My sister drew this from memory. All of it. Every road, every river, every town in Averlaine. She said—" He stopped. Swallowed. "She said the country moves."

"Countries don't move."

"This one does. It exists where it's remembered and nowhere else." He looked up at her, and there was something raw in his face, something that had been bleeding since before the blood on his side had. "So we walk it the way you'd walk through someone's dream. Carefully. And we don't wake anything up."

Hazel looked at the wheat again. Up close, she could see what she'd missed: no wind touched it. Not one stalk bent. Ten thousand copies, holding perfectly still, waiting to be looked at.

Behind them, the border stones of Vell were already out of sight. Her magic sat in her chest like swallowed lightning, huge and unfamiliar, humming against her ribs every time her heart beat. She kept expecting it to fade. It didn't. Ten years of silence, and now she could feel weather three valleys away, could taste the pressure drop coming with tomorrow's front, could hear — faintly, absurdly — the conversation two hills of grass were having with the rain that hadn't reached them yet.

She'd spent a decade being nobody. Being somebody again felt like wearing a coat that didn't fit because it was cut for a taller woman.

"Keep moving," Corren said, and stepped into the wheat.

* * *

They walked all day, and the day refused to end properly.

The sun hung at the same angle for hours. Shadows stayed short. When Hazel finally called a halt — because her legs ached and because the stillness of the light had begun to press on her like a hand — Corren checked the map and went pale.

"We should be past the river by now," he said.

"What river?"

"The Aldmere. My sister drew it crossing here." He turned the hide toward her, and Hazel saw it: a blue thread winding through the gold, exactly where their feet had been treading for the last four hours. Except there was no river. There was more wheat, stretching on and on, the same wheat, the same painted light.

"The land doesn't agree with the map," Hazel said slowly, working it through. "But the map isn't wrong. You said Averlaine exists where it's remembered. So who remembers this part?"

Corren's jaw worked. "People who are dead," he said. "Mostly. People who died remembering it. Or people who—" He stopped again. He did that a lot when the truth got close. Hazel was starting to build a shape out of his silences, and she didn't like the shape.

"Or?" she prompted.

"Or people who never existed anywhere else."

They made camp without fire, though Hazel could have summoned lightning down her own arm if she'd wanted one badly enough. She lay awake watching the stars, which were also wrong — too many of them, arranged in constellations she half-recognized and then didn't, as if the sky itself had been remembered by someone who'd only ever glanced up once, quickly, and gotten most of the details from imagination.

Around midnight, she heard singing.

It was far away and wordless, a thin high sound like a child humming to fill a silence. It came from everywhere and nowhere, drifting through the wheat without bending a single stalk. Beside her, Corren sat bolt upright, eyes wide in the dark.

"You hear it too," he whispered.

"I hear it."

"That's a lullaby. From Averlaine. My mother used to sing it." His voice cracked on *mother*. "Nobody alive knows that song. Nobody except—"

The singing stopped.

In the silence that followed, Hazel became aware of something she'd been feeling without noticing: the hum in her chest, the storm-magic, had gone quiet. Not gone. Attentive. Like a dog that has heard a footstep at the gate.

Something in this country knew she was here.

* * *

Morning — if it was morning; the light came up the same way it had gone down, sideways and sourceless — brought them to the edge of the wheat.

Beyond it lay a town.

Hazel stopped so abruptly that Corren walked into her back. "That wasn't there yesterday."

"Nothing here is where it was yesterday." But his voice had changed, gone thick and strange, and when Hazel glanced at him she saw tears running freely down his face.

The town was beautiful. That was the first thing, the overwhelming thing: it was beautiful the way memory is beautiful, softened and gilded, every roofline perfect, every window glowing with warm interior light. A market square spread out before them with stalls draped in colored cloth, and the smell of bread and woodsmoke and horses drifted on air that finally, finally moved. A bell tower rose above the rooftops, white stone veined with silver, and bells hung in it — dozens of them, bronze and glass and something that might have been bone — all silent.

People filled the square. That was the second thing Hazel noticed, and the wrongness of it crawled up her spine. There were hundreds of them, buying and selling and laughing and carrying baskets, and not one of them had a face she could hold onto. She'd look at a woman weighing apples and by the time the apple changed hands, the woman's face had slid out of her memory like water through fingers. She tried three times. On the third try, she caught herself looking at a man's face and finding, instead of features, a kind of warm blur — the idea of a face, the memory of a face, rendered by someone who hadn't quite known him.

"They're not real," she breathed.

"They're remembered." Corren wiped his face with his sleeve, impatient with his own grief. "This is Marrowgate. This is — my sister used to talk about the market here. The bell tower. The bread stall with the blue awning." He pointed, and there it was: a bread stall with a blue awning, perfect down to the stitching. "She remembered it so hard it stayed."

"And the people?"

"People she saw. People everyone saw. Enough people remembered enough faces that the town keeps... filling itself in." He shook his head slowly. "But look closer, Hazel. Look how many there are. Too many. More than a town needs. Something is padding the streets."

Hazel looked. He was right. The crowd had a crowdedness that served no purpose — figures standing in doorways doing nothing, figures walking in circles, figures that repeated. She watched the same old man cross the same square twice in the space of a minute, taking the exact same steps, passing through the exact same patch of sunlight.

Averlaine wasn't just surviving on memory. It was *straining*.

"Where do we go?" she asked quietly. "The map."

Corren consulted the hide. The roads had rearranged themselves again; Marrowgate now sat at the center of everything, every route bending toward it like iron filings toward a lodestone. At the town's heart, where the bell tower stood, the map showed a mark she hadn't seen before: small, deliberate, drawn with care. A circle with a line through it.

And beneath it, in handwriting that shook — his sister's, he'd said — two words:

*Find the keeper.*

* * *

They entered Marrowgate through the market, because there was no other way in.

Walking among the remembered people was the worst thing Hazel had done since the border stones. They were warm. That was the unbearable part — when a woman selling ribbon brushed past her, Hazel felt the warmth of a living body and heard breath, real breath, in-and-out, patient as tide. The woman smelled of lavender and sweat. She smiled at Hazel with a blurred, lovely face and said, "Fine morning, miss," in a voice like a song sung by someone who'd forgotten the words and was humming the shape of them instead.

Hazel's storm-magic churned. Every instinct she had — the new instincts, the returned ones — told her these people were prayers. Not people. Prayers that had been answered so often they'd learned to walk around and sell apples.

"Don't talk to them," Corren murmured. "Don't answer them. I think if they notice us too much, it costs something. I don't know what. But it costs."

"Costs who?"

"I don't know." His hands were shaking. "I don't know, and that frightens me more than the Church does."

They moved through the crowd toward the bell tower. The streets narrowed as they went, and here the town began to fray at its edges — cobbles missing in patches, replaced by flat grey nothing that wasn't stone or dirt but simply *absence*, smooth as unglazed ceramic. Doors led nowhere. Windows opened onto white. One entire alley was only suggested, a corridor of smudge and suggestion that Hazel's eye kept sliding off, and through its middle walked a milkmaid carrying pails that flickered between brass and transparency.

"She's almost gone," Hazel whispered. "That street. That whole piece of it. Nobody remembers it well enough anymore."

"Then it's dying." Corren said it flatly, the way you say things you can't afford to feel yet. "Piece by piece. Street by street. That's what forgetting looks like when you're standing inside it."

At the base of the bell tower, the crowd thinned abruptly, as if even the remembered people preferred not to come here. The tower doors stood open — oak banded with iron, carved with wolves. Wolves everywhere, running around the lintel, curled on the hinges, howling at a moon carved into the keystone. Hazel's skin prickled.

"Why wolves?" she asked. "On everything. The map was wolf-hide. Tam—"

She stopped. She hadn't meant to say that name; she wasn't sure where it had come from. It had arrived in her mouth fully formed, the way the rain had arrived over the lamb, the way memories had detonated back into her at the stones — things surfacing from somewhere deeper than decision.

Corren stared at her. "You know the name?"

"No." Her voice came out strange. "Yes. I don't know. It's like — it's like a word I knew in a language I've forgotten."

Inside the tower, the bells hung motionless in shafts of sourceless light. And at the bottom of the stair, sitting cross-legged on the first step with their chin in their hands, was a child.

Small — seven, eight years old at most. Barefoot. Filthy in a way none of the shining townspeople were, hair matted, clothes patched with scraps of cloth that Hazel realized with a jolt were torn from banners, tapestries, funeral shrouds — pieces of Averlaine, worn as clothes. The child watched them with eyes that were the only sharp, specific, unforgivably *real* thing in the entire country. Grey eyes. Storm-grey.

"You took your time," the child said.

Hazel's heart slammed. "You can see us?"

"I can see everybody." The child unfolded, jointless as a cat, and stood on the step, head tipped back to look up at her. "That's the problem. Seeing is expensive." Those grey eyes narrowed, traveled over Hazel slowly, and stopped at her chest — at the place where the storm lived, humming behind her ribs. The child's expression changed. Wonder, yes, but underneath the wonder something older and wearier, the look of a sentry who has watched the horizon for so long that the arrival they waited for feels less like relief than like the end of a very long shift.

"You're her," the child said. "You're actually her. I thought I'd invented you. I invent lots of things. It's hard to keep track." They stepped forward, bare feet silent on stone, and looked up with terrible solemnity. "The last wolf comes home, and the bells will remember how to ring."

"My name is Hazel."

"I know." The child smiled, and it was a sweet smile, and it was the loneliest thing Hazel had ever seen. "But that's not what the country calls you. The country's been whispering about you for ten years, through every mouth it has left. *The last wolf.* The one who shut the door." The child's smile faded. "And the one who can open it."

The bells above them stirred — just slightly, just once, a single bronze throat giving a low, broken hum, like a sleeper moaning before waking.

The child flinched and pressed both hands over their ears.

"Not yet," they whispered, to the bells or to Hazel or to themselves. "Not yet. I'm not finished paying."

Hazel looked at the small, ragged figure, at the banner-scrap clothes, at the grey storm-eyes, and felt the first cold edge of understanding slide into place — an understanding of what it cost to keep a whole country alive inside one small skull, and why the child had said *seeing is expensive*.

"What's your name?" she asked softly.

The child considered her for a long moment.

"Tam," they said. "I think. I'm mostly sure."
