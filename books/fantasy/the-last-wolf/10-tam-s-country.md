## 10. Tam's Country

The country starts at a crooked fence.

That's how Tam thinks of it, anyway. There's no gate. There's no wall. There's just a moment when the grass changes its mind about being grass — when the wind comes down off the hills smelling of salt and woodsmoke instead of stone and sheep — and then you're inside, and the road under your feet is cobbled instead of dirt, and the bells are ringing somewhere ahead the way they always ring on a Tuesday morning in autumn.

Tam has heard the bells every Tuesday for as long as Tam can remember, which is not actually very long, which is something Tam tries very hard not to think about.

"Stop," Hazel says behind them. She says it quietly, but her voice has that edge it gets when she's frightened and pretending not to be. "Tam. Stop walking."

Tam stops. Turns. The woman and the man with the hurt side stand at the edge of the cobblestones like people standing at the edge of deep water. Corren is holding the wolf-hide map against his chest with both hands, the way you hold something you're afraid will be taken from you, and he is looking past Tam at the rooftops with his mouth open.

"That's the Saltmarket," he says. His voice cracks on *Saltmarket* like a boy's voice cracking. "Those are the — those roofs, they had green tiles on the fishmongers' row, my mother used to say you could tell the honest traders because their tiles were always mossy—"

"Yes," Tam says. "Green tiles." Tam points at the nearest roof. It is not mossy. It is not quite anything. It has the idea of greenness about it, the way a word remembered in another language has the idea of its meaning. "I'm still working on this one."

Hazel looks at the roof. Then at Tam. Then at the road, the crooked fence that isn't there anymore now that they're through it, the hills that have rearranged themselves into terraced vineyards going gold at the edges.

"Where did this come from?" she asks.

"Nowhere," Tam says, honestly. "It came from me."

* * *

Tam walks them into Averlaine the way Tam walks everywhere: quickly, hands busy, eyes moving. You don't dawdle in a remembered place. Dawdling gives things time to thin out.

"This is the Bellwrights' Quarter," Tam says, sweeping an arm at a street of narrow houses leaning together like old friends sharing secrets. "And that's the fountain where the fishwives argued about prices, and over there—" Tam hesitates. Over there there should be the tannery. Tam knows there was a tannery. Tam cannot remember what a tannery smells like. "Over there was a tannery," Tam finishes, weaker.

Corren makes a small sound. He's touching one of the leaning houses, flat-palmed, gentle, the way you'd touch a horse you didn't want to spook. "My sister drew these streets," he murmurs. "She drew them from memory, after. I thought she was making them up. I thought — we were children, I thought she invented them." He looks at Tam with wet eyes. "You're not inventing them either."

"No," Tam says.

"Then where—" Corren begins.

"I told you," Tam says, and hates how sharp their own voice comes out. "From me. From remembering. That's all a country is, when everyone else forgot. Somebody has to remember it hard enough to walk around in."

They go on. Tam takes them down Fishmongers' Row so Corren can see the green tiles, and spends a little extra on the tiles, making them properly mossy, properly honest. Corren laughs when he sees them — a broken, grateful laugh — and Tam feels warm all over for a whole minute afterward, the way you feel when you've given someone exactly the right thing.

Tam doesn't count the cost until later. Tam is getting worse about counting the cost.

* * *

Here is how it works, though Tam has never said it out loud before, has never had anyone to say it out loud to:

Averlaine is not gone. That's the thing nobody understands, the thing even the Church with all its burning doesn't understand. A country isn't made of ground. Ground is just where a country keeps its things. Averlaine is made of Tuesdays and bell-ringers and the smell of bread on Copper Lane and the specific way the glass palace catches the evening light so that the whole city turns the color of honey. All of that still exists. It exists in the only place it was ever really kept, which is inside people.

But the Church burned the people who kept it. One by one, year by year, the rememberers went into the fire, and each time one did, a piece of Averlaine went out like a lamp. The streets got shorter. The names wore off the shops. The bells rang less often, and then rang wrong, and then — mostly — didn't ring at all.

Except in Tam.

Tam doesn't know why Tam remembers everything. Tam was very small when the soldiers came to Tam's town; Tam mostly remembers running, and a hand letting go of Tam's hand, and after that a great deal of walking. But somewhere in all that walking, all that alone, Tam decided — or maybe Tam's head decided without asking — that forgetting was not allowed. Not one more thing would be forgotten. Not if Tam could help it.

So Tam holds it. All of it. Every street, every bell, every fishwife's argument. And when Tam walks the old roads and pays attention — really pays, the way you have to pay when nothing is holding itself up anymore — the roads come back. The buildings come back. The country comes back, soft-edged and imperfect, but real enough to shelter under.

There is only one problem, and the problem is simple, and Tam has known about it for years and has told no one, because telling someone would mean thinking about it, and Tam does not think about it:

Every piece of Averlaine Tam builds out here has to come from somewhere.

Tam only has so much room inside.

* * *

The market is Tam's best work.

Tam leads them up from Fishmongers' Row toward the great square, talking fast the whole way because talking helps, because words are also memories and spending them out loud means they get used twice. "Market day is Thursdays," Tam says. "Well. Was. The square fills up from the north end first because the north gate opens earliest, and the cheese sellers take the shady side because cheese suffers in sun, and there's a man named Bors who sells knives and sharpens anything for free because his father was a butcher and Bors says he owes the trade—"

"Bors," Hazel repeats softly.

"He had red ears," Tam says. "Ears that went red when he lied about his prices, which was always, so everybody knew."

And there he is. Tam reaches inside and finds him — finds the stall, the awning striped yellow and brown, the knives laid out in rows like silver fish, the big knuckly hands, the ears — and sets him down in the square between the cheese sellers and the wool cart, and Bors calls out his prices in a voice like gravel, lying, ears blazing red, and Corren makes a sound like he's been hit.

"He's not real," Hazel says. Quiet. Careful. Her storm-grey eyes are doing something complicated. "Tam. He's not — he's not alive. Is he?"

"He's remembered," Tam says. "It's better than alive. Alive, he'd have been dead for ten years like everyone else. Remembered, he's still selling knives."

Hazel doesn't answer. She's watching the market — the crowd, the noise, the hundred small motions of commerce, all of it built out of nothing but Tam's stubbornness — and her face has gone pale in a way that Tam doesn't like, because Hazel is smart, Hazel notices things, and Tam can see the noticing happening.

"Who taught you to do this?" Hazel asks.

"Nobody," Tam says. "You just decide not to forget, and then you don't."

"And what happens," Hazel says slowly, "to the things you decide to forget?"

Tam's stomach drops.

Because that's the question, isn't it. That's the exact shape of the trap, and Tam walked them both right up to it, showing off, spending freely, building Bors and his red ears out of whatever raw material happened to be closest.

"I don't forget things," Tam says. Too fast. Too loud. A woman buying pears turns her head, and Tam realizes with horror that the pear-seller's face is half-finished — Tam built the market crowd earlier from the easy stuff, the bright stuff, the recent stuff, and some of those faces are wearing other faces underneath, flickering, like paintings painted over older paintings.

Hazel sees it too. Tam watches her see it. Watches her look at the pear-buying woman, then at Tam, and Tam knows — knows the way you know a storm is coming, which is a thing Tam has watched Hazel do, watch the sky like she's reading a letter from an enemy — that the question has already been answered, that Hazel has done the arithmetic, that the sum is sitting there in the space between them, terrible and obvious.

"What did the pear woman look like?" Hazel asks gently. "Before."

"She always looked like that," Tam says.

"Tam."

"She always looked like that!"

* * *

Tam runs.

Not far. Just to the fountain at the edge of the square, the good fountain, the one Tam rebuilt last month with real care, real detail — the chipped dolphin on the south side, the green stain down the basin where the water's been dripping for two hundred years. Tam sits on the fountain's edge and puts their face in their hands and breathes.

This is the part Tam never gets to have. This is the part that comes after the showing-off, when the visitors — and there haven't been visitors, not ever, not once, Hazel and Corren are the first people to walk Tam's country since Tam started carrying it — when the visitors finally understand what they're looking at.

Footsteps. Hazel sits down beside Tam on the fountain's rim. She doesn't touch them. She just sits, close enough that their shoulders almost brush, and waits. Waiting is a thing Hazel is good at. Tam has noticed. Hazel waits like weather waits.

"The palace," Tam says finally, muffled through their fingers. "Do you want to see the glass palace? Everyone wants to see the palace. It's the best thing. When the light hits it at evening it goes gold, all of it, the whole city turns to honey, I told you that already, I tell everyone—"

"You don't tell anyone," Hazel says. "There hasn't been anyone."

"There's you," Tam says, and hates how young their voice sounds.

Silence. Water dripping onto the green stain. Somewhere across the square, Bors lies cheerfully about his prices, and Tam can feel him flickering at the edges already, because Tam built him shallow, quick, cheap, because Tam was excited, and cheap work doesn't hold.

"How many?" Hazel asks.

"How many what?"

"How many rooms do you have left? Inside." Hazel's voice is very quiet. "How much of yourself have you spent?"

And here is the honest answer, the answer Tam has never said, has barely let themselves know: Tam doesn't know. That's the horror of it. It's not like coins in a pocket, spend three, count seven. It's more like — like breathing out. You don't notice the air leaving until suddenly there's less of you than there was, until you reach for something ordinary and find the shelf empty. Last winter Tam tried to remember their mother's face and found the shape where a memory should be, smooth and blank, like a wall where a picture hung for so long the paint around it stayed dark.

Tam had a favorite food once. Tam is nearly sure of it. Tam cannot remember what it was.

"Some," Tam says.

"Hazel." Corren has come across the square. He stands over them both, and his face is grey with pain — the wound in his side, or the other kind of pain — and he's holding the wolf-hide map so tightly his knuckles are white. "Hazel, look at the map."

Hazel takes it. Unrolls it. And Tam watches her face change.

"What?" Tam asks.

"The map shows the roads of Averlaine," Corren says. "All of them. My sister drew every road she could remember. And I couldn't understand — when we crossed the border stones, none of it matched. The roads weren't where she drew them. I thought she'd misremembered. I thought—"

"They move," Tam says, defensive, standing up. "The roads move. They go where I've walked lately. The country follows me, that's how it works, that's normal—"

"It follows you," Hazel repeats. She's looking at the map, and then at Tam, and her expression is the worst one, the tender one, the one that makes Tam feel seen straight through, like glass. Like the palace. "Tam. How long have you been walking these roads alone?"

Tam doesn't answer.

"Years," Hazel says. It's not a question. "Alone. Keeping a whole country alive by yourself. Paying for every mile of it out of—" She stops herself. Tam watches her stop herself, watches her swallow whatever she was going to say next, and that's somehow worse, that mercy, because Tam knows what the sentence ends with. *Out of yourself.* Out of your own self, child, brick by brick, bell by bell, until there's not enough of you left to hold up the walls.

"I'm fine," Tam says. "I'm careful. I don't build the expensive things unless—"

"The palace," Hazel says. "You want to show us the palace. The best thing. The most expensive thing."

"It doesn't cost that much," Tam lies, ears going hot, and thinks absurdly of Bors, of red ears and lying, of being remembered into a lie about yourself. "It's already built. I built it ages ago. It just needs lighting up, that's all, it just needs someone to look at it—"

"Tam," Corren says, and crouches down so he's smaller than them, so they're eye to eye. His voice is rough and kind. "We'll come. We want to see it. But I need you to tell me one thing first, and I need you to tell me true, the way you told us true about the bells." He pauses. "Your sister. Did you have a sister?"

And that's the moment the tenderness curdles.

Because Tam opens their mouth to say yes — of course yes, everyone had family, Tam must have had family, there was a hand that let go of Tam's hand, there was running, there was a town — and Tam reaches for the answer and finds the shelf empty. Finds smooth blank wall. Finds the shape where something hung, the paint around it darker, the nail still there, the picture gone.

"I don't know," Tam says.

The market noise goes on around them. Bors lies about his prices. The pear woman buys pears with a borrowed face. The bells ring their Tuesday bells, and Tam can feel, distantly, the cost of each note ringing out, like blood ticking away from a cut.

"My sister prayed in Averlaine once," Corren says, very low, to Hazel, but Tam hears. "In the glass palace. That's what she told me. That's why I came all this way." His hand finds Tam's shoulder, light as a bird landing. "Then we'll see the palace," he says to Tam. "Together. And afterward—" he glances at Hazel, and something passes between them that Tam can't read, something heavy and newly decided, "—afterward, we're going to talk about how to make sure you never have to pay for any of this again."

Tam wants to ask what that means. Tam wants to ask a great many things.

Instead Tam leads them up the honey-colored hill, toward the evening light, toward the palace of glass, spending as they go — a lamplighter here, a flight of stairs there, the smell of roasting chestnuts, the name of a street, the color of a door, the sound of a song whose words Tam can no longer quite hear — and behind them, unwatched, unfelt, unnoticed by anyone but Tam, the pear seller in the emptying market puts down her fruit and stares at her own hands with a stranger's eyes, trying to remember who she is.

The bells ring on.

Tam counts them, and for the first time, counts the cost too.
