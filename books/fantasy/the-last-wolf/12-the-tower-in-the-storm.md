## 12. The Tower in the Storm

The memory came back to her on the third morning out of Vell, and it did not come gently.

They had camped in the lee of a granite shoulder, Corren asleep with the wolf-hide map folded under his head like a pillow he didn't trust anywhere else, when Hazel woke to the taste of ozone and the certainty that she had stood on this exact stone before.

Not this stone. A stone like it. A stone ten years and half a country away, wet with rain that had obeyed her.

She sat up so fast her vision swam. The fire had burned down to a red eye. Tam was a small shape curled against Corren's side, breathing the shallow, rationed breaths of a child spending themselves by the hour. Above them the sky was doing something wrong — clouds gathering over their little hollow of the world while the stars burned clear everywhere else, a storm the size of a blanket, following her like something loyal and starving.

*You called me,* the sky said, or she imagined it said. *You always used to call me.*

"Go away," she whispered, because that was what she'd been whispering to it for two days, and because for the first time she could hear how her voice sounded when she said it — not annoyed. Frightened. The voice of someone talking to a dog that keeps bringing back bones from a grave she doesn't want to look at.

But the memory was already loose. It had come up through the crack the border stones had knocked in her, through the floodwater of magic that had slammed back into her at the crossing, and now it walked through her the way water walks through a house after the dam goes: finding every room, filling every low place.

She remembered rain. She remembered commanding it — not asking, not praying, *commanding*, the way you command your own hand to close — and the rain coming because it wanted nothing else in the world but to obey her.

And beneath the rain, underneath it like a keel, she remembered a voice. Not a human voice. A voice like a bell rung inside a mountain, vast and bored and old, saying words she couldn't yet pull into the light: *they are only prayers. Let them starve.*

Hazel put her hands flat against the cold granite and breathed until the shaking stopped.

Corren woke the way he always woke now — all at once, hand going to the knife, eyes finding her first. Ten days on the road had taught him that. "Another one?"

"Another one." Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. "A tower."

He went still. "From the fragments?"

"A tower in a storm. And a voice." She looked at him across the dead fire and made herself say the rest, because he had earned the truth and because saying it aloud might make it smaller. "Corren, I don't think these memories are coming back at random. I think they're leading me somewhere."

He unfolded the map without being asked. Wolf-hide, soft as cloth, inked in a brown that both of them had agreed not to name. Roads that rearranged themselves overnight. A country that existed only where someone remembered it hard enough to keep it standing. In the northwest quarter, past the glass palace, past the bell markets, a single mark the cartographer — his sister, the sister who hadn't survived — had drawn twice, pressing hard enough to tear the hide.

A circle. A dot inside it. No label.

"I asked Tam once," Corren said quietly, "what was there. Before—" He stopped. Tam's vocabulary had lost another eleven words in the last two days; *before* was still there, but barely, and they had all learned to flinch around the words that were going. "Tam said, *the tall place where the door is quiet.*"

Hazel looked at the mark. The storm above them rumbled agreement, low and eager, like a hound scenting home.

"All right," she said.

*

They walked northwest for two days, and the land began to lie to them.

That was the thing about Averlaine no map could capture: it wasn't ruined, exactly. It was *unkept*. Roads appeared where feet expected roads and vanished where nobody bothered to remember them. They crested a hill at noon and saw below them a village of forty chimneys, all smoking; by the time they descended, the village was foundations and one stubborn wall, and Tam looked at it with an expression Hazel was learning to dread — the expression of a child checking whether a memory they'd paid for was still standing.

"The people here," Hazel said carefully, as they picked along a remembered lane, "the ones the Church erased. Are they—"

"Gone," Tam said. Then, with the terrible precision of the fading: "Gone like a word in a language no one speaks anymore. You can see the shape of the mouth it used to make."

Corren walked ahead of them both. He had been walking ahead more often since the night Hazel told him what she'd done — since she'd knelt in the mud outside the drowned chapel and confessed the whole architecture of her crime, and he had not forgiven her, and had not condemned her either, and had asked her instead to help him reopen the door *because my sister prayed there once.* That sentence had become the spine of their journey. It kept them moving. It did not let either of them rest.

On the second day the terrain stopped pretending to be anything but itself.

The hills fell away behind them and the wind rose, and the wind smelled wrong — not of pine and stone but of lightning about to happen, of a held breath. Hazel's skin prickled with static. Her hair lifted from her neck. The storm that had followed her since Vell drew in close overhead like a hand cupping a candle flame, and for the first time she felt it not as pursuit but as escort.

"There," Tam said, and pointed.

The tower stood on a tor of black rock at the head of a valley, alone, the way a tooth stands alone in an old jaw. Even ruined it was enormous — a cylinder of pale stone, its top third sheared off as if something had bitten it, its walls furred with ivy gone silver in the cold. Windows climbed its height in a spiral, most of them dark, a few holding panes of thick green glass that caught the light and threw it back bent.

Hazel stopped walking.

Her body stopped before her mind understood why, boots planted, heart slamming, and then the memory arrived — the full one, the whole one, the one she had paid ten years of herself to bury — and it did not trickle this time. It detonated.

*

Rain like a wall. Rain she had pulled down out of a clean sky because the gods below would not listen any other way.

She was twenty-three years old and she had been grieving for a season and a year, and grief had cooked down inside her into something with a cutting edge. She stood on the tower's highest stair with her arms raised and the storm screaming around her, and below her — below her, under the stone, under the world — the door.

She had gone looking for the gods to demand justice. Her family had prayed to them for generations; her mother had left bread at the border stones every solstice; her little brother had asked the storm-god to keep the wolves away and had died anyway with the wolves' work on him, and her father, and her mother, and the gods had answered with silence. Not malice. Silence. Indifference, vast as weather.

So she had climbed to the last thin place, the old door the gods used when the world was young, and she had demanded they explain themselves, and the voice had come up through the stone like cold through a floor:

*We do not owe you explanation. We do not owe you answer. Your prayers were yours. Your grief is yours. We are old, child, and tired, and we do not owe.*

And twenty-three-year-old Hazel Dunmore — storm-born, grief-mad, holding enough lightning in her raised hands to split the tor — had looked at that indifferent vastness and decided that if the gods would not answer prayers, then prayers should stop having anywhere to go.

*Then be forgotten,* she had said, and meant it with her whole ruined heart. *If you won't hold us, we won't hold you.*

She had torn the door shut with everything she was. And the price — she had known even then there would be a price; the old doors took payment, everyone knew that much of the old lore — the price had been named in the currency she had the most of. Memory. All of it that touched the deed. The tower. The voice. The door. Her own power's true shape. Her family's faces went too — collateral damage, the door taking the wound along with the weapon — and she had stood in the sudden ringing quiet of a world with one fewer god-door in it, empty as a scraped bowl, and heard hooves on the road below, and fled south before anyone could ask her name.

South, until the border stones of Vell drank the last of her magic out of her like a poultice drawing poison. South, into silence, into goats and gardens and Odell's guarded kindness. Into ten years of a numbness she had believed was exile.

It was never exile.

There had been no sentence handed down. No council, no judgment, no decree. She had walked into Vell already amputated, already paid, and the valley had simply been the place she bled out into. *Exile* was the story she'd built around the wound afterward because a story with a villain was easier than a story where the villain was the woman holding the knife.

She had sentenced herself. She had been her own church, her own inquisitor, her own burning.

Hazel came back to herself on her knees in the wet grass with Corren's hands on her shoulders and Tam's small palm pressed flat against her sternum, and the storm overhead had gone utterly silent, every cloud holding its breath.

"Hazel," Corren was saying. "Hazel. Breathe."

"I remember," she said.

His hands tightened. "Remember what?"

She looked up at the tower. Up its broken height, past the spiral windows, to the ragged stump where the top had been torn away — and she knew, with a knowledge older than memory, that the top hadn't been bitten off. She had taken it off herself, from inside, when the door slammed. The tower had cracked like a whip.

"That's mine," she said. "That's all mine. I built the storm that broke it. I sealed the door." The words tasted like copper. "Corren — there was no exile. There was never an exile. I sentenced myself. I'm not running from what the Church did to me or what some council decided. I did this. Every erased road, every forgotten name — your sister, your family, all of Averlaine going out like lamps — it started with me, on those stairs, with my hands up and my heart closed."

The silence after that had weight. Wind moved the silver ivy. Somewhere far off, thunder cleared its throat and thought better of speaking.

Tam withdrew the little hand from her chest and studied her face with the unnerving directness of the memory-spent. "You're the one who locked it," Tam said. Not accusing. Cataloguing. "The tall place. The quiet door. You're the wolf who bit it shut."

"Yes," Hazel said.

"And now you're the one crying to open it."

"Yes."

Tam nodded slowly, as if a sum had balanced. "Good," the child said. "Because I'm almost out of country to stand on."

Corren helped Hazel to her feet. His grip was careful, impersonal, the grip of a man handling something that might go off — and she couldn't blame him for it, wouldn't blame him, though it hurt worse than condemnation would have. He looked at the tower for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough.

"My sister prayed there," he said. "At the foot of that tower, the summer before the forgetting. I carried her on my shoulders the last mile. She wanted to hear the bells." His jaw worked. "Ten years I've hated the Church of the Quiet for burning the names out of the world. And it turns out they were just sweeping up after you."

"Yes," Hazel said again. She made her voice carry none of the plea rising in her. He owed her nothing. Less than nothing. "I know."

"Do you?" He turned to face her fully, and his eyes were wet and furious and steady. "Do you actually know what you did? Not the shape of it. The cost of it. Because we've walked through villages where people can't remember their mothers' faces, Hazel. I watched Tam trade the color of their own front door for a market stall last week. Every mile of that bill comes back to you."

"I know."

"Then say it plainly. Not the confession version. The true one."

Hazel looked at the tower that she had broken, at the door beneath it that she had sealed, at the storm that had loved her faithfully for ten years through a wall of forgetting. And she said it plainly.

"I killed a country because it grieved better than I did," she said. "I couldn't bear that the gods got to stay indifferent while I shattered, so I made sure everyone shattered equally. I didn't punish them. I punished everyone who ever prayed. And I hid in Vell for ten years calling it exile because the alternative was knowing I was the monster in my own story."

Corren held her gaze for three long breaths. Whatever he was looking for, he must have found some version of it, because he exhaled — a long, unsteady exhale, the sound of a man setting down a load he intended to pick back up later, but not now.

"We're here," he said finally. "That's what matters tonight. We're here, and the map says the door is under the tower, and Tam—" he glanced down, and his voice cracked and recovered, "—Tam needs us to be fast."

Tam was staring at the tower with an expression Hazel had never seen on the child's face before. Not wonder. Recognition — the recognition of a thing remembered correctly, which for Tam had become rare enough to be sacred.

"It's quieter than it was," Tam murmured. "The door. But not dead." The child's eyes came up to Hazel's, and they were the oldest eyes Hazel had ever seen in the youngest face. "Things under the door can hear the storm you brought, wolf. They've been listening since the stones."

As if summoned — as if *answered* — the tower's lowest window lit from within.

Not firelight. Not lamplight. A slow green-gold glow, the color of deep water over treasure, seeping through glass thick with age, and beneath their feet the black rock of the tor gave a single shudder, gentle as a sleeper turning toward a familiar voice.

The storm above them opened its eyes.

Hazel stood between the sky that had followed her and the door she had shut, and understood that neither of them had ever stopped waiting for her.

"Come on," she said, and walked toward the stairs.
