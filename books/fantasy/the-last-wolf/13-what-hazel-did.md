## 13. What Hazel Did

The fever broke in the night, and that was when the memories came.

Corren had learned to dread the breaking of fevers. In the Church's infirmary at Sarn Hollow, they called it the clearing — the moment a body finished its fight and the mind, left unguarded, gave up whatever it had been holding shut. He'd watched men surface from illness weeping for mothers they'd sworn were alive, for towns that did not exist on any map. The Quiet took people gently, but it took them completely, and sometimes sickness scraped against the seal and something underneath bled through.

He was lying under a lean-to behind Odell's house, wrapped in every blanket Hazel owned, and the fever was leaving him the way a tide leaves — slowly, dragging things with it as it went.

He dreamed of Averlaine.

Not the remembered country Tam carried like a lantern in their chest. Not the wolf-hide map, all roads and borders and careful ink. He dreamed of it *real*: the bell tower at Vessing with its cracked bronze bell that rang flat no matter how many times they recast it; the smell of the tanneries on the east wind; his sister Liss running the last stretch of the mill road with her skirt full of stolen apples, laughing so hard she could barely breathe, because she was eleven and eleven-year-olds could laugh like the world was a thing made entirely of gifts.

*Corren,* she said in the dream. *Corren, tell them about the apples.*

And then, the way dreams do, it turned.

He was standing in the doorway of his family's house, except the door opened onto a storm. Rain fell upward. Wind came from nowhere and everywhere, screaming around a single point in the sky above Vessing — a point where the air itself was being *torn*, peeled back like skin from fruit. Through the tear he saw light that was not sunlight. Shapes moving in it. Enormous, patient, hungry shapes, the way gods look when they are starving.

And below the tear, small and drenched and lit by her own lightning, stood a girl.

She was maybe nineteen. Her hair was plastered black to her skull. Her hands were raised, and the storm was not around her — it was *her*, radiating out of her bones, answering her fury the way water answers a broken dam. He could see her face clearly even across the impossible distance, because storms make everything clear for one terrible moment before they take it away.

Her face was grief beyond grief. It was the face of someone standing in the ashes of everything she loved, looking up at heaven and finding it empty.

*You didn't come,* the girl said. She wasn't shouting. That was the worst part. She said it quietly, conversationally, to the shapes in the light. *My mother prayed to you for three days while they burned the village. My brother prayed until his throat bled. I prayed. And you watched. You're watching now.*

One of the shapes inclined toward her. It made a sound like a bell underwater, like a word spoken too large for any mouth. Corren couldn't understand it, but he felt its meaning settle into him anyway: *We are old. We are few. Your grief is one grain among ten thousand years of grains. Forgive us our arithmetic.*

*No,* the girl said.

The tear began to close. The gods' light thinned. Whether they were retreating or being pushed, Corren could not tell — but the girl's hands turned, palms out, and the storm gathered itself into something with edges, something with intent, and he understood that she was not pushing them away.

She was closing a door. And she meant to lock it.

*Wait,* Corren tried to say. His voice wouldn't work. *Wait — my sister is down there. My family. Everyone. If you close it—*

The girl looked at him.

In the dream, across the storm, her eyes found his as if he were the only other living thing in the world, and there was nothing cruel in them, which somehow made it worse. There was only a hurt so total it had passed through rage and come out the other side as certainty.

*They stopped listening first,* she said. *I'm just finishing the sentence.*

The door slammed.

The sound was not a sound. It was an absence — every bell in Vessing cut off mid-ring, every voice, every prayer, gone as if snipped with shears. The light collapsed. The rain fell downward again, ordinary rain, and the girl stood alone in the mud with her arms at her sides, and the storm drained out of her like blood out of a wound, and Corren screamed—

—and woke.

* * *

Hazel was sitting three feet away, mending a strap on her sandal, and she was looking at him with an expression he would think about for the rest of his life.

It wasn't guilt. He searched for guilt and it wasn't there — not because she felt none, but because she didn't know yet what there was to feel guilty about. What he saw instead was fear, plain and animal: the fear of a person who has heard someone cry out in the night and does not know what name they were calling.

"You were dreaming," she said. "Badly."

"Was I?" His voice came out gravel-rough. His hands were shaking. He folded them under the blanket where she couldn't see.

"You said a place-name." Her eyes went to the wolf-hide map, rolled up at the foot of his bed. "Vessing. You said it twice." A pause, carefully casual. "Is it on the map?"

He should have said yes. It was such a small lie, and it would have bought him a day, an hour, anything. But his tongue was still wet from the dream, still tasting the moment the bells died, and what came out was:

"It's not on any map anymore. That's rather the point of it."

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, submerged, gone before he could name it. "Sleep," she said, standing. "Fever-talk doesn't count for anything here. Odell will tell you that. Half this valley talks to dead relatives when they're sick." She paused at the edge of the lean-to, her back to him. "Vell forgets easily. It's why people stay."

Then she walked down toward the goat pens, and Corren lay in the blankets and stared at the underside of the thatch, and thought: *That was her. That was her in the storm. Nineteen years old and ending the world.*

He had known. Some part of him had known since the border stones, since she'd caught him falling and her hands had been cold as a sky about to break. Tam had seen it immediately — children always saw truest — and had said *the last wolf, the last wolf* with the certainty of scripture. But knowing a thing and standing inside it were different countries, and he had just crossed the border between them, and the country on this side smelled like burnt villages.

His family had been erased. Not killed — killed he could have carried. *Erased.* When the door closed, everyone who had ever prayed to the gods behind it became unprayed-for, unremembered by the world's great ledger, and Averlaine slid out of history like a stone into deep water. The Church of the Quiet hadn't created the forgetting. The Church had merely been founded by men wise enough — or cowardly enough — to understand that if a whole country could vanish, then belief itself was dangerous, memory itself was dangerous, and fire was cleaner than questions.

His mother's name existed nowhere now but in his head. His father's hands — the hands that had taught him to mend net and shoe a horse — existed nowhere. Liss, with her apples and her flat-cracked laughter, existed in exactly one skull in the entire world, and that skull was currently lying under a goatskin blanket behind a widow's house in a valley that forgot on purpose.

And the woman who had closed the door was mending sandals thirty feet away, humming.

* * *

He didn't sleep again. He got up at grey dawn and walked, because walking was the only thing he knew how to do with a feeling too large to hold. His legs took him along the valley's eastern path, past the terraces, up to where the pines started, and he let them go because deciding anything felt impossible.

Here is what war did to a man, he thought, stopping at the tree line. Here is what the Church did. It trained you to hold two truths in your hands at once and not let either one drop.

Truth the first: Hazel Dunmore had murdered his country. Not with malice. With grief — but the result was the same as malice, the graves were the same depth, and somewhere in him a voice that sounded like Liss was screaming that grief was no excuse, that if a stranger burned your house down by accident you still didn't owe them your forgiveness or your silence.

Truth the second: Hazel Dunmore had pulled him bleeding out of the rain, lied to her elders for him, fed him from her own stores, sat up nights with his fever, and asked for nothing. Had given Tam — a feral child made of other people's memories — a place at her fire without hesitation. Was, right now, planning some half-mad walk into a country that didn't exist, because a stranger's dead sister had prayed there once, and Hazel had decided that prayer deserved an answer.

Both truths were true. That was the trap. If either one were false, he could have set it down.

"Your fever's broken, then."

Odell was standing ten feet down the path with a basket of early greens, as if she'd grown out of the hillside. Old women in Vell had a way of appearing like weather. Her eyes went over him with the frank inventory of someone who had buried three husbands and outlasted two famines.

"Mostly," Corren said.

"You slept badly."

"I dreamed."

"Mm." She came closer and stood beside him, looking out at the valley — the low stone houses, the smoke rising straight in the windless morning, the border stones glinting far off on the southern rise like a row of gray teeth. "In Vell," she said, "when a person dreams badly, we have a custom. We don't ask them what they dreamed." She shifted the basket to her hip. "Because a dream is a thing a man carries whether he wants to or not, and prying at it is like prying at a scab. You follow me?"

"I follow you."

"But I'll say one thing freely, since you're a guest and guests get one free thing." She looked at him sideways, and her eyes were sharp as awls. "Whatever you saw in that dream — whoever you saw — you'd best decide soon what you mean to do about it. Because Hazel Dunmore has spent ten years paying a bill she can't remember running up, and I have spent ten years watching her pay it, and if you intend to hand her another piece of the account, boy, you'd better be certain the giving won't kill her. Or you."

"And if I'm not certain?"

"Then you'll be like the rest of us," Odell said, turning back down the path. "Uncertain, and kind anyway. It's the valley way. It's kept us alive." Over her shoulder: "Porridge is on the hearth. Don't let Tam eat yours; the child thinks hunger is negotiable."

* * *

He watched her all day.

That was the coward's work and he did it thoroughly. He watched her haul water and curse at the nanny goat and teach Tam, patiently, the names of herbs — *this is yarrow, for wounds; this is wormwood, for worms and bad tempers both* — and every ordinary gesture landed on him now like a blow, because he knew what lived underneath the ordinariness. Storm-caller. Door-slammer. The woman whose private catastrophe had swallowed a nation.

At noon Tam climbed into his lap without asking, the way the child did everything, and held up a stick. "Draw Vessing," Tam demanded.

"I don't remember it well enough."

"Liar. Fever-dreams show true. Everyone knows that." Tam pressed the stick into his hand. "Draw it and I'll keep it. I keep everything. That's my whole job."

So he drew it, in the dirt: the crooked bell tower, the mill race, the row of houses along the tannery lane. And Tam studied the scratches with those ancient, unchildlike eyes and pointed at a small square near the water. "Whose?"

"That was my house."

"And there?" A smaller square beside it.

"My grandmother's. Then my uncle's. Then—" He stopped.

Tam waited, merciless as only children are.

"Then mine again," Corren said finally. "Averlain houses got passed around. Families blended. Nobody much cared whose walls were whose." He looked at the little square. "My sister slept in the loft there. She used to shout down through the floorboards that I snored."

"Did you?"

"Like a landslide."

"I'm keeping that," Tam declared, and pressed both palms flat over the drawing, and for a moment — Corren would have sworn it, later, though he never told anyone — the dust seemed to hold its shape against the wind, as if the child had filed Vessing away somewhere real.

That evening Hazel cooked barley and onions over the hearth, and Tam talked with their mouth full about the glass palace and the singing stairs of Averlaine's capital, and Hazel listened with her chin on her fist, and her face when she listened — open, hungry, grieving for a country she didn't know she'd killed — was almost more than Corren could bear.

*Tell her*, said the voice like Liss.

*Not yet*, said the voice that sounded like his own survival.

*She deserves—*

*Deserves?* He gripped his bowl until his knuckles whitened. *What does deserve mean, out here? She was nineteen and mad with grief and the gods themselves admitted they'd failed her. You want justice? Justice would be telling her, watching her break, and getting what back? My family won't unburn. Averlaine won't unforget. All I'd be doing is putting my pain into her hands to carry alongside hers, and she can't even carry her own.*

*Then you're a coward.*

*Yes,* he answered the voice, and ate his barley, and said nothing.

Later, when the fire was low and Tam slept curled against Hazel's side like a pup, Hazel looked across the embers at him. The orange light moved on her face.

"You dreamed of Vessing," she said quietly. "Didn't you."

It was not a question, and he realized with a lurch that she had known all day — that she'd seen it on him the way you see weather coming, that her casualness that morning had been armor, that she had spent hours waiting for him to speak and he had spent those same hours deciding not to.

"There were bells," he said. "And a storm. And a girl."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a dog barked once and thought better of it.

"The map needs someone born of storm," Hazel said. Her voice was very steady, the steadiness of a woman holding a door shut from the inside. "Tam says that's me. I've been trying to remember why a child made of other people's memories would know that. I can't. There's a room in me that won't open, and I paid — someone, something — to keep it shut, and lately the key has been turning on its own." She met his eyes across the dying fire. "If you dreamed what I think you dreamed, Corren, then you know what's in that room. And you've had a whole day to tell me, and you haven't. So I'm asking you plainly, because I'd rather hear it from you than from the storm."

The embers ticked. Tam murmured in their sleep. And Corren sat with the truth in his throat like a stone, weighing it one final time — his dead against her peace, his silence against her trust — knowing that whichever way he dropped it, something would shatter.

"Hazel," he said. "There's something you need to know about the night the door closed."
