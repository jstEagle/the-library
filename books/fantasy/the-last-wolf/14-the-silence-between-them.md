## 14. The Silence Between Them

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Hazel found her voice.

They had walked since dawn, following the wolf-hide map through country that kept forgetting itself — hills that softened into meadows, meadows that hardened back into hills, as if the land couldn't decide what it wanted to be. Tam slept between them, curled against Corren's pack like something small enough to be lost. Corren sat with his back against a leaning stone, sharpening a knife he hadn't needed to use in days, and the rhythm of it — *shhk, shhk* — was the only sound in Averlaine's half-remembered dark.

Hazel had known this moment was coming. She had felt it building for two days now, ever since the tower, ever since the memory of what she'd done had come home to her body like shrapnel working its way toward the surface. Every hour she let pass was an hour Corren might learn it some other way. From Tam, who remembered too much and said it plainly. From Maren, if the inquisitor caught them again. From the land itself, which seemed determined lately to give up its dead.

She could not bear for him to hear it from anywhere but her mouth.

"Corren."

He looked up. Firelight caught the scar along his jaw — three days old now, from the border stones, already fading to silver. He had stopped flinching when she moved suddenly. Somewhere on these roads, without either of them deciding it, they had become people who trusted each other with their backs.

That was the worst part. That made it harder.

"I need to tell you about the door," she said.

The knife went still. He didn't say anything. He just waited, the way he always did, patient as a man used to waiting out storms.

Hazel wrapped her arms around her knees and stared into the fire, because looking at him while she said it would be impossible, and she had spent ten years learning to survive impossibilities, and she wasn't sure she could spend one more.

"You asked me once why I was exiled," she said. "I told you I didn't remember. That was true. But it wasn't the whole truth. I didn't remember because I paid to forget. And the thing I paid to forget—" Her throat closed. She worked past it. "The thing I paid to forget is the reason your country is dying."

*Shhk.* The knife started again. Stopped.

"Averlaine isn't forgotten because the world chose to forget it," she said. "It's forgotten because I closed the door. Ten years ago. The gods' last door. The one place where the old gods still touched the world. I sealed it, and everything on the other side began to fade — the land, the prayers, the names. Your sister's name." She finally forced herself to look at him. "Your family were among those erased, Corren. Because of me."

The fire popped. Tam stirred and didn't wake.

"How," Corren said. It wasn't a question so much as a door held open. His voice was very level, the way it got when he was holding something down.

Hazel told him.

She told him all of it, the whole ugly architecture of it: how she had been born storm-touched in a country that worshipped the old gods, how the gods had answered every prayer but the ones that mattered, how raiders had come down out of the northern passes one winter and slaughtered her family while the gods — her family's gods, her mother's gods, the gods whose temples she had swept and whose festivals she had loved — did nothing. Nothing at all. How she had knelt in the ashes of her mother's house and prayed until her voice broke, and gotten silence, and understood at last that the silence was the answer.

"And I thought," Hazel said, "if the gods won't hold the door open for us, then the door can close on them. I was nineteen. I was grieving. I had more power than anyone born in a generation, and I aimed all of it at the one hinge that held their world to ours." Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake as if they belonged to someone else. "It took everything I had. The sealing nearly killed me, and the payment — memories are the only coin the door takes — it took my mother's face. My father's voice. My own name, almost. What was left over, the Church traded me exile for. They put me across the border stones in Vell and let the valley do the rest. Vell eats magic. It ate what remained of mine. I woke up one morning ten years later and I couldn't even remember why I was there."

She ran out of words. The silence came in behind them like water filling a hull.

Corren had not moved. The knife lay forgotten in his lap. His face, in the firelight, was doing something complicated — grief moving under it like weather, like the fronts Hazel used to read off the sky before she gave the sky away.

"My sister," he said at last. "Lira. She was seven when the forgetting reached our village. She prayed to the door every night. There was a shrine near our house, one of the old ones, and she'd leave flowers and ask the god to keep our well full and our goats healthy and—" His voice cracked, just once, and he pressed his lips together until it passed. "When she started fading, we took her to the Church. We thought they could help. That's how I learned what the Quiet really does. That's how I ended up running with the map."

"I know," Hazel whispered. "I know, and I'm—"

"Don't." The word came out sharp enough to cut. Then, softer, almost gentle: "Don't apologize yet. Let me get there."

So Hazel waited. She had offered her confession; now she offered her patience, and it was the harder gift.

Tam shifted in their sleep and murmured something — a word of Averlaine, maybe, or a fragment of a bell-song — and settled again.

"The thing is," Corren said slowly, "I've been carrying this map for four years. Four years of hiding in cellars and burning churches' records and watching people I love get unmade. And every step of it, every mile, I've been walking toward you." He laughed, and there was no humor in it. "The map says the door needs someone storm-born to open it. I found you in a goat pasture at the edge of the world, and I thought, *there, that's the one, the last wolf, the storm-caller, the person who can fix everything.*" He looked at her then, fully, and his eyes were wet and furious and something else underneath, something she was afraid to name. "And the whole time, the reason the door needs opening at all is sitting right in front of me wearing a shepherd's coat."

"Yes," Hazel said.

"I should hate you."

"You should."

"It would be easier." He dragged a hand down his face. "Gods, it would be so much easier if I could just hate you and be done with it. You'd understand that, wouldn't you? Hating is simple. Hating has somewhere to *go*."

"I know," Hazel said again, because it was true. She had lived on hatred for years. It had fed her all the way to the tower and all the way through the sealing, and then it had burned itself out, and what was left was ash and exile and a valley that forgot.

Corren stood. He paced to the edge of the firelight and stood there with his shoulders rigid, looking out at the half-real hills of his drowned country, and Hazel watched him decide something. She saw it happen in the set of his spine, the slow unclenching of his fists. When he turned around, his face had gone quiet in a way that frightened her more than fury would have.

"Here's what I keep coming back to," he said. "You closed the door. Fine. Terrible. Unforgivable, maybe. But you also crossed the border stones knowing it would tear you apart, knowing your magic would come back like a flood, knowing the memories would come with it. You didn't have to do that. Odell offered you a clean escape and you refused it. You've been bleeding power for three days straight keeping Tam anchored to this country, and I've watched you do it, and you never once complained." He crouched, bringing his face level with hers across the fire. "I don't think you're the woman who slammed the door anymore, Hazel. I think you're the woman standing in front of me trying to figure out how to open it again."

"The opening will kill me," Hazel said quietly. "Maren told you. The door needs a storm-caller's full strength. All of it. There won't be anything left."

"I know what it costs."

"Then why aren't you angry?"

"Because I am." He sat back down, heavily, like a man setting down a load he'd carried too far. "I'm so angry I can taste it. But anger doesn't open doors, Hazel. And Lira—" His breath caught. He steadied. "My sister prayed at that door for years. Little flowers, little wishes. Keep the well full. Keep the goats healthy. Keep my brother safe. She believed someone was listening, right up until the listening stopped, and I have spent four years telling myself that if the door opened again — if it *really* opened — then maybe her prayers weren't wasted. Maybe they were just waiting. Maybe somewhere in the dark on the other side, all those little wishes are still stacked up like letters nobody delivered, and opening the door delivers them. Even now. Even late. Even if she's not—" He stopped. Started again. "Even if she's gone, the prayers deserve to arrive."

Hazel felt tears on her face and realized she'd been crying for some time without noticing.

"So I'm not asking you for forgiveness," Corren said. "I don't have forgiveness to give. I'm not a priest. I don't get to absolve anybody, least of all the person who erased my sister. What I'm asking is smaller and bigger than that." He reached across the fire and took her hand — her shaking, guilty, storm-empty hand — and held it the way you hold something you've decided not to drop. "Help me reopen the door. Not because you owe me. Not because you're sorry. Because my sister prayed there once, and somebody ought to answer."

For a long moment Hazel couldn't speak. She had braced herself for the blade — for hatred, for abandonment, for Corren turning his back and walking into the half-real dark alone, leaving her to the Church and the closing country. She had offered him her life in the privacy of her own heart, ready to kneel and bare her throat if that was what justice required, and instead he was asking her for the one thing she had left to give.

Not punishment. Purpose.

"You could still hate me tomorrow," she managed. "I'd understand."

"I probably will," Corren said. "Some mornings I will wake up hating you so much I can't look at you. And some mornings I'll watch you carry Tam when your legs are shaking and remember that people are not the worst thing they ever did." He squeezed her hand once, hard, and let go. "We can both be true. That's the deal. Take it or leave it."

Hazel laughed — a cracked, wet, astonished sound that scared the birds out of a remembered tree nearby. The tree flickered, solidified, stayed.

"That's the strangest bargain anyone's ever offered me," she said. "Odell wanted me to swear never to come back. The Church wants me erased. And you want me to walk into the teeth of the thing I ruined and fix it, knowing it kills me."

"Not knowing," Corren said. "Believing. There's a difference. Believing leaves room."

"For what?"

"For the storm to surprise us." He nodded toward the eastern dark, where the glass palace waited in Averlaine's deepest memory, thin as paper at its heart, humming like a struck bell. "Tam's running low. You can see it — they lost the word for *blue* yesterday. Whatever window the door has left, it's closing faster than the map promised. We go tomorrow. At dawn."

"Dawn," Hazel agreed.

She banked the fire while Corren checked the map, his fingers tracing the wolf-hide roads toward the palace of glass. Tam slept on, small and stubborn, spending themselves even in dreams to keep the markets and bells alive. Above them, through the canopy of a country made of memory, the first stars came out — real stars, older than gods, indifferent and beautiful and finally, after ten long years, no longer the only thing Hazel believed in.

She lay down and did not sleep. Instead she listened to Corren breathing across the embers, and to the faint, far-off sound that had been growing all evening, so soft she might have imagined it: a bell, ringing in a palace of glass, tolling like something counting down.

Or perhaps, she thought as sleep finally took her — something counting *up.*

At dawn, they would walk into the storm.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, and with it, on the ridge above their camp, a line of figures in pilgrim gray.

Hazel counted eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. They did not advance. They did not call out. They simply stood at the crest of the hill in the early light, silent as a held breath, and at their center, small and still, stood a woman Hazel recognized even at that distance — Maren Sloe, hands folded, face unreadable, watching them the way you watch a door you have not yet decided whether to knock upon or burn.

Beside Hazel, Corren went very still.

"They found us," he breathed.

"No," Hazel said slowly, rising, feeling the old pressure gather behind her ribs like weather looking for a sky. "Look again. They're waiting. The Church doesn't wait."

At Maren's shoulder, one of the gray figures raised a torch against the dawn — and did not light it.

Hazel stared, and understood, and did not understand, and started walking toward the ridge anyway.
