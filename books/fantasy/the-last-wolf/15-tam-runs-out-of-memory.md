## 15. Tam Runs Out of Memory

I know what I'm spending. That's the thing nobody asks you. They think forgetting is like falling asleep — that it happens to you. It doesn't. It's a trade. Every bell I ring in the market square costs me a morning of my own. Every cobblestone under Hazel's boots is a name I used to know.

I keep a list. Not on paper — paper forgets too fast here, and ink dries into nothing by morning unless someone remembers the words it held. The list lives behind my eyes, in the last room of my head, where I keep everything that's mine.

*My mother's hands smelled like tallow and thyme.*

*The dog was called Biscuit.*

*I am ten years old. Or eleven. I was one of those when this started.*

The market holds because I remember the smell of bread. The bells ring because I remember being afraid of them, once, and then loving them. The glass palace stands at the end of the remembered road because I remember pressing my face to its gates when I was small enough to fit between the bars, before the Church came, before the forgetting came for everyone but me.

Nobody chose me. That's another thing people get wrong. There was no ceremony, no old woman tapping my shoulder with a wand of rowan. I just didn't stop looking. Everyone else in Averlaine learned to look away from the empty streets, and their looking-away ate the country bite by bite. I kept staring. And whatever gods were left, starving behind their door, they took the only child still staring and said: *then you hold it, little one. Hold all of it.*

So I do.

* * *

Hazel finds me at the fountain at dawn, which isn't dawn, because Averlaine's sky is only as real as I make it. She crouches down so she's at my eye level. She does that now. She didn't, at first. At first she talked over my head like I was weather.

"You're pale," she says.

"I'm always pale."

"Paler."

Corren limps up behind her with the wolf-hide map rolled under his arm like a soldier's bedroll. He's been sleeping badly. His sister's ghost walks with him; I can see her sometimes, standing just past his shoulder, made out of the memory he carries. She has his frown.

"We should move today," Corren says. "Maren will have crossed the river by now."

"Maren moves slow," I say. "She prays at every shrine. Even the broken ones." I don't tell him how I know. I don't tell him that I've been walking ahead of us all night in the shape of pigeons and stray dogs and one very confused goat, holding the road open mile by mile so the Church steps on stone instead of nothing. That's not the kind of thing you say to tired people.

Hazel studies my face the way she studies weather. "Tam. When did you last sleep?"

"I sleep inside the houses," I say. "That counts double."

"That doesn't answer—"

"The baker's wife hums when she kneads," I say instead, because if I keep talking I don't have to answer anything. "Did you know that? Before. Her name was Ilsette and she hummed the same seven notes every morning and the whole street woke up humming them without knowing why. I put her back. You walked past her window yesterday. Did you hear her?"

Hazel goes quiet. Corren looks at the ground.

"I heard humming," Hazel says finally. "I thought it was wind."

"It was never wind." My voice cracks on it, and I'm angry that it cracks, and I'm angry about being angry, and underneath all the anger there's a small cold fact sitting like a stone at the bottom of a well: I put Ilsette back yesterday, and I cannot, right now, remember my mother's face. Only her hands. Tallow and thyme.

The list behind my eyes is shorter than it was.

* * *

We walk. The map pulls us east through streets that exist only while we're looking at them. Behind us, Averlaine folds itself away — shutters close that were never opened, doors become blank walls, the cobbles soften back into the gray nothing the country really is now, which is less than a dream, which is a debt.

I hold the road. That's the work. I hold it the way you'd carry water in your cupped hands for miles, knowing some of you spills with every step.

By midday I've lost Biscuit. I reach for the dog — my dog, warm weight against my ribs on winter nights — and find a hole shaped exactly like him. I know there was a hole. I can feel its edges. But the middle is gone, and I sit down right there in the middle of the remembered street and cry without any sound coming out, because even crying costs something and I'm rationing.

Hazel sits down with me. She doesn't say *what's wrong*, which is good, because the answer is *everything, constantly, and I'd have to describe it to you and describing is spending*. She just puts her arm around my shoulders and lets the street hold still around us while I shake.

"What did you lose?" she asks, after a long time. She knows the mechanics now. Corren told her. Or maybe she guessed — she's the kind of person who pays attention to what things cost, because she's paid so much herself.

"A dog," I manage.

"What was his name?"

And here is the terrible arithmetic: if I say *Biscuit* aloud, the word comes out of the last room of my head and becomes hers, and I might be able to get it back from her later, but I might not. If I say nothing, the hole stays a hole forever.

"Biscuit," I say.

Hazel nods slowly. "Biscuit," she repeats, tasting it. "Good name?"

"The best. He stole a whole ham off a funeral table once and nobody had the heart to be angry."

"Then he's mine now too," Hazel says. "You don't lose him. You share him. That's different."

It shouldn't help. It helps more than anything has helped in years.

* * *

It gets worse in the afternoon.

We're crossing the great plaza in front of the glass palace — the heart of the heart, the place where the door waits thin as paper behind the palace walls — and I'm holding the plaza open, all of it, every flagstone and every statue and the enormous fountain with its carved wolves, when my mouth stops working.

Not stops. *Forgets how.* I open it to warn them — I felt the Church's presence flicker at the edge of the country like a cold draft — and what comes out is:

"Wuh. Wuh-fen. Fenn. Fennd—"

Corren turns. Hazel turns. I press my hand over my own mouth like I can push the words back in, but they're not words anymore. They're wet shapes.

*Talking,* I think, frantically, *talking is I am Tam, I am ten, the dog is Biscuit, the fountain has wolves, wolves, wuh—*

"TAM." Hazel grabs my shoulders. Her eyes are wide and storm-gray and terrified, and I realize distantly that I must look terrible, that my nose is bleeding, that the plaza around us is guttering like a candle — statues going soft, the fountain's wolves melting into suggestion — because I'm spending everything I have just staying *on*, staying lit, and there's no more left over for things like speech.

I point at my mouth. Then I shake my head.

Understanding crosses her face like a shadow crossing a field.

"No," she says. "No, no — Tam, listen to me. Stop. Stop holding the plaza. Let it go. We'll walk the rest on faith—"

I shake my head harder. If the plaza goes, the door goes. If the door goes, everything — Corren's sister, the starving gods, Hazel's whole terrible beautiful plan — all of it folds into the gray with the rest of Averlaine, and I'll have spent myself for nothing, and nothing is the one thing I refuse to have bought.

*Stubborn,* Hazel mouths. She says it out loud too, half a laugh, half a sob: "You're stubborn. Fine. Fine." She digs in the bag at her hip and comes out with charcoal and the back of an old letter — Corren's sister's handwriting on the front, I notice, and something in my chest twists — and she flattens the paper against a statue's knee.

*Talk with this,* she writes. *Save your voice. Save everything else you can.*

I take the charcoal. My hand shakes.

*How much longer,* I write.

Hazel looks at the palace doors, tall as trees, glass gone cloudy with unremembering. "Tonight," she says. "Maybe tomorrow. The door needs a full storm and a full storm-caller, and she—" she taps her own chest, grimacing, "-needs time to gather. Can you hold until tomorrow?"

I do the math I've been avoiding for three days. The list behind my eyes: mother's hands, tallow and thyme, Biscuit (shared now), seven notes of a baking song, the color of the sky on the day the Church came (yellow, it was yellow, I'm almost sure), my own age, my own—

I look up at Hazel and write: *Yes.*

I don't know if it's true. It's the truest thing I have.

* * *

Night in Averlaine is the worst, because night is when I used to be safe, and safety is expensive.

I sit in the doorway of the glass palace with my knees pulled up, holding the building lit the way you'd hold a dying fire, feeding it splinters of myself. The stars above the palace are ones I invented two years ago from a half-memory of a story about sailors. They cost less than real stars. Everything invented costs less. That's the trap of this whole country: I could make Averlaine immortal out of inventions, a city of beautiful lies, and no one would ever know except me, and eventually I wouldn't know either.

Hazel comes and sits beside me. She's been gathering all evening — I can feel it, actually feel it, a pressure in the air like the sky filling with held breath. Storm-born. The mark on her collarbone glows faintly now, a blue-white seam, and when she breathes the torches lean toward her.

"Corren's asleep," she says. "Really asleep, for once."

I nod.

She's quiet a while. Then: "Show me something. Something real. While you still have it."

I blink at her.

"So I can carry it," she says. "Like Biscuit. Like Ilsette's song. You've been alone in this so long, Tam. Let somebody else hold some of it. That's not spending. That's — putting it somewhere safe."

My eyes sting. I reach into the last room of my head, into the deepest shelf, and I take down the oldest thing there, older than the Church, older than the fear: a festival. Midsummer. The whole city in the plaza below this palace, lanterns on strings between the towers, everyone dancing wrong-footed because the drummers were drunk, and my mother lifting me onto her shoulders so I could see over the crowd, and the glass palace lit from within like a lantern itself, every window gold —

I take Hazel's hand. I don't know how to give a memory. Nobody taught me; I only ever knew how to spend them. But I press her palm flat against my chest, over my heart, and I *push*, gently, the way you'd push a boat off from shore.

Her breath catches. Her eyes go wide and wet.

"Oh," she whispers. "Oh, Tam. The lanterns. Your mother's laughing — she's — I can hear her laughing—"

*Keep it,* I try to say. What comes out is "Eep ih."

"I'm keeping it." She grips my hand hard. "I swear it on every god coming through that door. As long as I live, someone remembers midsummer in Averlaine. Someone remembers your mother laughed. You're not spending yourself into nothing, Tam. You're putting yourself into me. Do you understand? Every piece you give me is a piece that survives."

I cry then, properly, soundlessly, the way I ration it, and she holds me against her side with the storm gathering in her skin and the palace glowing faintly around us because I'm holding it, still holding it, but lighter now, somehow. Because part of what I'm holding is also being held.

* * *

Near dawn, I start losing my sight.

It comes politely, the way it all comes: edges first. The world softens at its borders, the palace doors going misty, Corren's sleeping shape by the wall becoming a smudge of warmth. I write on the letter-paper with shaking hands:

*Eyes going. Tell Corren. Keep talking to me so I know where you are by sound.*

Hazel reads it and makes a noise I've never heard from her, small and fierce, like something tearing. Then she takes both my hands.

"Listen to me," she says, and her voice is the voice from the memory, storm-voiced, certain. "Tomorrow — today — I open that door, and when I do, every god in existence is going to owe a debt to a ten-year-old who held a country together with bare hands and stubbornness. And the first thing I'm demanding — before mercy, before rain, before any of it — is that they give you back what you spent. All of it. Biscuit and the yellow sky and your mother's face. Every piece."

*Some things don't come back,* I write.

"Then they come back changed," Hazel says. "But you'll be there to see it. That's the deal. That's the whole deal. You hold on until I tear this door open, and after that, you put the weight down. Do you hear me? After tomorrow, you're just a kid. A kid with a family that includes a storm, a soldier, a traitor-priestess, and a dog named Biscuit that lives in my head now."

I laugh — a croak, barely, but a laugh — and squeeze her hands, and outside the glass palace, far across the gray plain where Averlaine ends, I feel it: the Church. Maren Sloe and whatever she's brought with her, torches and iron and hymns, marching toward the last door in the world.

And behind them, farther back, something worse. Something the Church rides ahead of like a herald.

The silence itself, coming to finish the job.

I tap Hazel's arm and point, and her face hardens into the storm.

"They're early," she says. "Then we're done waiting."
