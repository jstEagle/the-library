## 16. Maren's Choice

The order came at dusk, folded twice and sealed with gray wax, delivered by a courier who would not look at her.

Maren Sloe broke the seal standing in the doorway of Vell's empty meetinghouse, where she had been sleeping on a pallet of straw for six nights, asking gentle questions, smiling gently, being gentle the way a snare is gentle right up until it closes. The parchment was short. Church orders were always short. Brevity, the Quiet taught, left less room for mercy to hide in.

*The valley of Vell has harbored the apostate known as Hazel Dunmore. The village has witnessed and concealed acts of answered prayer. By authority of the Third Silence: let Vell be made an example. Fire is the cleanest teacher. You will kindle it. You have until the new moon.*

She read it three times.

Outside, the village was settling into evening the way villages did — smoke rising straight from chimneys in the windless air, a dog barking itself hoarse at nothing, lambs bleating their small complaints into the dark. A woman she knew as Sera waved to her from a garden gate, holding up a basket of late onions in offering, because everyone in Vell fed the pilgrim who asked such patient questions. Maren had eaten their bread for six days. She had praised their stew. An old man named Bramm had shown her his refilled well with the shy pride of a man showing off a grandchild.

*Fire is the cleanest teacher.*

Maren folded the order along its creases, carefully, precisely, the way she had been trained to fold everything — doubts included — and put it in her pocket.

Then she went to find Odell.

* * *

She found the matriarch at the edge of the goat pasture, leaning on her stick, watching the sky over the border stones. That sky had not been right for two days. Clouds gathered above Hazel Dunmore's cottage like moths around a lamp, and rain fell there when no rain fell anywhere else, soft and localized and wrong, and every soul in Vell pretended very hard not to notice, because pretending was what Vell did best. Pretending was the whole architecture of the place.

"You've read something," Odell said, without turning. "You have the face of a woman who has read something."

"You know what I am," Maren said.

"I've known since you walked in." Odell's voice was dry as August. "Pilgrims don't ask about answered prayers with that particular hunger. Pilgrims want miracles. You wanted names."

Maren came to stand beside her. Below them, in the pasture, a lamb butted its mother's flank and stumbled and tried again. Ten years ago, Maren knew, that lamb would have died of any sickness, and no one in Vell would have thought to pray about it, because prayer in Vell was a habit worn smooth and meaningless as a river stone. Now the prayers stuck. Now they landed somewhere. She could feel it herself, faintly, like heat off a stone long after sunset — the old gods pressing against the world from the far side of a door she had spent her life helping to keep shut.

"Give me the girl," Maren said. "Hazel Dunmore and the man who came with her. Give them to me quietly, tonight, and I can report that Vell cooperated. I can argue the valley down from example to warning." It was a lie, or half a lie; she did not know which parts anymore. "I can save them, Odell. Most of them."

Odell finally turned. Her eyes were pale as skimmed milk and missed nothing. "And if I say no?"

"Then I burn your village, and I burn it well, because I am very good at my work, and afterward I will spend whatever remains of my life knowing exactly what I was good at."

The silence between them stretched long enough for the first star to find its footing overhead.

"No," Odell said.

Maren exhaled. Some part of her, the part that had knelt on cold stone floors at seven years old and sworn herself to the Quiet, relaxed at the refusal — relieved, horribly, to be released from the temptation of the easy path. "Then we understand each other."

"Do we?" Odell studied her. "Girl. I raised an exile once. I know what a person looks like when they're standing at the last fork in their own road." She tapped Maren's pocket, where the order lay folded. "That paper gives you until the new moon. The girl leaves before then. What you do with the time between is the only question left in you worth answering."

* * *

Maren did not sleep that night. She sat in the meetinghouse with a single candle and took out the things she carried, the way she did when a decision would not come — laying them out on the floor like a surgeon laying out instruments, or a thief laying out loot.

Her credentials, gray-sealed. Her flint and oil flask, issued by the Church, blessed against accident. A lock of hair in a paper envelope, cut from her own head at her ordination, to be burned upon her death so that nothing of her remained unaccounted for. And beneath these, wrapped in oilcloth at the very bottom of her pack where she told herself it was not, a scrap of wolf-hide, browned with age, marked with roads.

Averlaine.

She had been born there. She was four years old when the Church came to her family's farm with kindness and documents, explaining that the country they lived in had never existed, that their memories of it were a fever, that the fever was treatable. Her mother had wept and signed. Her father had signed. Maren remembered — no. She did not remember. That was the entire point. She had a hole in her shaped like a country, and the Church had poured stillness into the hole for thirty years, and the stillness had held, mostly, and it had been bearable. Forgetting was bearable. That was the Church's one true doctrine, the one underneath all the others: *forgetting is bearable.* They sold it like medicine, and it worked like medicine, and Maren had believed in it the way the cured believe in the cure.

Until she'd stood in a farmhouse in Vell and watched a woman named Hazel Dunmore look at a map drawn on wolf-hide, and seen the hole in Maren open its eye.

She unwrapped the scrap now. Roads. Rivers. A city at the center marked with a symbol she had never been able to read and had never stopped seeing when she closed her eyes: a bell inside a circle of glass.

Somewhere out there, Hazel Dunmore was walking those roads. Corren — the smuggler, the grieving brother — walked beside her. And a child, they said. A feral child who paid for a whole country out of their own skin.

Maren thought about the child for a long time. The candle burned lower.

At some point past midnight she said aloud, to the empty meetinghouse, in the flat voice of a woman conducting an audit: "They are going to make me burn children to protect a lie I was given at four years old." She listened to the words as though someone else had spoken them. They did not improve on repetition. "And I was going to do it."

That was the worst of it. Not the order — orders were just weather, you dressed for weather. The worst was how little resistance had risen in her when she read it. Her hands had begun planning the fire lines before her heart had finished objecting. Thirty years of training, and the training worked. That was the horror of the Quiet. Not cruelty. Efficiency.

She put the wolf-hide scrap away and did not put away the question.

* * *

On the fourth day, the storm followed Hazel Dunmore through the center of Vell, openly now, a small private thunderhead trailing her like a loyal hound, and the village stood in doorways and watched it pass, and nobody pretended anymore, and the pretending — the whole ten-year architecture of it — groaned like ice in spring.

Maren sent her last report to her superiors: *Target located. Moving to intercept alone. Do not send others; the valley is volatile.* It was even true. Then she saddled nothing, packed light, and went after them, carrying her flint and her oil and her wolf-hide scrap and the folded order she should have burned.

She caught them at dusk in the hill country beyond Vell, at a place where the terrain did something her eyes kept refusing to confirm — a road appearing where the road had not been, hedgerows assembling themselves out of suggestion. Tam stood watch on a rock, thin as a winter branch, and looked at Maren with eyes gone strange and hollowed, eyes spending themselves.

Corren saw her first. He moved in front of Hazel with a knife already out, and Maren noted, distantly, that she respected it.

"Inquisitor," he said. "Run, Hazel. She's the one who—"

"I know what I am," Maren said. She dismounted — she had bought a mule in Vell, a slow honest animal, the most truthful thing about her expedition — and stood in the road with her hands visible and empty. "Hazel Dunmore. I have a warrant for your surrender. I am not going to serve it."

Hazel stepped out from behind Corren. Up close, the storm-born mark showed at her temple, faint as a scar from an old burn, and the clouds above them turned slowly, attentive. "Why not?"

"Because three days ago my Church ordered me to burn the valley you came from. Every soul in it. As collateral." Maren heard her own voice go quiet and precise, which was what it did instead of shaking. "As a *lesson*. And I want you to understand that I read that order standing in your village's meetinghouse, eating food those people had given me under false pretenses, and my first instinct was to begin planning the fire lines." She let that sit. "I'm telling you this so you'll know I'm not offering you absolution. I'm not offering anyone absolution. I'm offering you a head start, and I'm going to buy it with the only currency I have left, which is myself."

Corren's knife hand hadn't moved. "Why?"

It was the right question, the only question, and Maren had walked four days to answer it honestly, so she answered it honestly.

"Because I was born in the country you're walking toward," she said. "Because the Church erased it from me when I was four and called the erasure salvation, and I believed them for thirty years because believing was bearable. Because yesterday I understood that I don't get to choose whether that was salvation or theft — but I do get to choose whether I help commit it again, on a larger scale, against people who never hurt me." She looked at Hazel, and the looking cost her. "And because your door, when you open it, is going to change what remembering costs. And I find that I would rather die on the side of memory than live one more year as the Church's clean hands."

Tam slid down off the rock and came to stand close to Hazel, studying Maren with those spent, ancient eyes. When the child spoke, their voice was hoarse, rusted from disuse. "She smells like home," Tam said. "Underneath the burning smell. Underneath, she smells like glass bells."

Maren's knees nearly failed her. She had not known, until that moment, that a sentence could hit like a blow. "Yes," she managed. "I think it does."

* * *

She rode back toward Vell that night, and behind her the storm-clouds gathered over the hills where Hazel walked east toward a tower and a door, and ahead of her the valley lay dark and unsuspecting under a fat moon two days shy of full.

Her orders said the new moon. But the courier had not traveled alone. There was a second team, she knew — there was always a second team; the Quiet trusted no single pair of hands, least of all hers now — a burn crew moving up the south road under Preceptor Anselm, a man she had ordained beside twenty years ago, a man who sang hymns while he worked. They would not wait for the new moon if they learned the target had fled. Collateral did not require patience.

She found them camped in the beech wood above the valley, forty strong, oil barrels stacked like cordwood, and Anselm met her at the fire with genuine warmth, embracing her, calling her sister. He had always called her sister. That was the trick of the Quiet — it made the machinery feel like family, so that leaving it felt like orphaning yourself.

"Maren." He poured her wine. "Sloe herself, come down from the hills. Tell me the valley's ripe."

"The valley knows," Maren said. "Dunmore fled east days ago. The villagers are frightened and cooperative. There's no need for the example now — fear has done the work. Let me go in tomorrow and take confessions, and we close this with paper instead of ash."

Anselm smiled and shook his head, fond and immovable. "The Third Silence doesn't amend itself, sister. Ash writes deeper than ink. You know that." He squeezed her shoulder. "Rest. Tomorrow we teach."

She drank the wine because refusing would have been a confession. She sat among them by the fire while they sang, and she looked at each face in turn — young Ordwin, who still wrote letters to a mother in the lowlands; Sister Cael, who had once held Maren's head during a fever; Anselm, singing with his eyes closed — and she performed the audit one final time. These were not monsters. That was the finding, and it changed nothing, and it changed everything: these were not monsters, they were the same medicine she had taken at four years old, made flesh, come to dose a valley.

Forgetting is bearable. But it is not free. Someone always pays, and the Church had simply gotten very good at making other people pay.

Maren rose quietly near midnight, walked the picket line, and set her mind to arithmetic. Forty souls. Oil barrels at the north and south edges of the camp, per doctrine. One narrow track out through the beeches, east, toward the high road. Horses tethered in a hollow. She had, by her count, perhaps a quarter hour of advantage between the first thing she did and the last thing they would allow her to do.

It would not be enough to stop the burn. She knew that too. Whatever she did here bought hours, a day at most, and Anselm would send riders after Hazel regardless, and Maren would likely not survive the night. She stood in the dark doing this sum and found, to her lasting surprise, that the answer did not frighten her. It grieved her. Grief, she was discovering, was what truth felt like from the inside — heavy, specific, hers.

She had spent thirty years being the instrument that made forgetting bearable for other people. She would spend her final hour making remembering possible for one.

* * *

She started the fire herself.

There was a symmetry in it she did not bother to resist: her flint, her oil, her Church-trained hands, all of it turned at last against its makers. She soaked the oil barrels at the south edge while the sentries watched the valley, and when the flame caught she walked calmly to the horse hollow and began cutting tethers, and the camp woke to orange light and screaming animals and Inquisitor Maren Sloe standing in the middle of it all with a torch in one hand and the gray-sealed order held high in the other.

"The Third Silence!" she shouted, over the roar, over Anselm's bellow of her name. "Read what you're burning for! Read it! We were sent to make orphans of a valley for the crime of praying!" She hurled the torch into the second barrel stack. "I was four years old when they stole a country out of my head, and I am done being their clean hands!"

What happened after happened fast and badly, which is how such things go. Anselm did not hesitate — she had never expected him to; hesitation was another thing the Quiet trained out of you. Steel caught the firelight. She gave him her shoulder instead of her throat, took the cut meant for her neck along her collarbone, went down into the smoke, and crawled toward the treeline while the camp dissolved into chaos behind her, horses stampeding through the flames, brothers and sisters shouting each other's names, the burn order for Vell curling to ash in a fire of her own kindling.

She reached the beeches. She got a tree between herself and the camp and pressed her back against it, hand clamped over her bleeding collarbone, and watched the fire she had lit eat the machinery that had made her.

Above the eastern hills, far off, the clouds had massed into a towering black wall, and lightning flickered inside them like something struggling in a net — steady, rhythmic, insistent. Like a heartbeat. Like a bell.

Like a door being knocked upon from either side at once.

Maren Sloe laughed — a short, cracked, astonished sound — and tore a strip from her sleeve to bind her wound, and began, limping, to move east. Toward the storm. Toward the tower. Toward the last door of the old gods, where a woman born of storm was even now gathering the courage to pay the whole price of remembering.

If Maren arrived, it would be as no one's inquisitor. She had burned her rank, her name, her certainty, and quite possibly her life, and she felt — searching herself for the old stillness and finding, instead, this raw and living ache — *lighter.*

Behind her, Vell slept on, untouched. Ahead of her, the thunder counted down.

She had perhaps a day. So, she judged, did the world.
