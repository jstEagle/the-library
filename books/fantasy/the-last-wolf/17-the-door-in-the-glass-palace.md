## 17. The Door in the Glass Palace

The last stretch of road was made of bells.

Not real ones — remembered ones. Tam walked ahead of us, small and gray as ash now, and where their bare feet touched the ground, bronze bell-shapes bloomed out of nothing, hanging from remembered arches that cast remembered shadows. They didn't ring. They had rung once, ten years ago, when Averlaine still existed anywhere but inside a child's skull, and the memory of the sound hung over them like frost.

I could feel the palace before I saw it. That was new. For most of my life I'd felt nothing at all — that was the whole point of Vell, the whole point of the bargain I hadn't known I'd made. But since crossing the stones, sensation had come back into me like blood into a slept-on limb, all pins and needles and wrongness, and now it gathered in my chest and pulled me forward the way a hook pulls a fish.

"There," Corren said.

The glass palace rose out of a hillside of wheat that rippled with no wind. It was not large. That was the first cruelty of it. In Tam's stories it had been vast, its towers full of singing light, but memory is a currency and Tam had been spending it for years, and what remained was a palace perhaps the size of Odell's barn, its walls thin as church windows, its colors washed toward white. Through the walls I could see shapes moving. Not people. Not quite. Light that moved the way living things move.

"The door's in the heart chamber," Corren said. He was reciting it — his sister's voice, her map, her careful annotations in ink gone brown. "Heart chamber, west stair, under the bell called Morning."

Tam stopped walking. Their feet left no prints in the wheat.

"It's smaller than I remember," they said. Their voice had gone thin too, paper-thin. Two days ago Tam had forgotten the color blue. Yesterday, mid-sentence, they had forgotten their own name and looked up at me with blank animal eyes until I said *Tam* and something behind their face reassembled. "I'm sorry. I tried to keep it big."

"Hey." I crouched to their level. Up close I could see the fine tremor in them, like a candle flame deciding whether to stay lit. "It's perfect. It's exactly how it was."

"No," Tam said, with the terrible honesty of children. "But thank you for lying."

Corren turned away. His jaw worked. He'd been carrying his grief like a stone in his mouth for weeks — Sera, who drew maps of countries that didn't exist, whose prayer in this place had never been answered because I had slammed the door on every god who might have heard it. I knew that now. I had known it since the tower, since the memory came back whole: young Hazel, seventeen and smoking with rage, sealing the door while a god watched me with eyes like weather and did nothing, nothing, nothing.

I stood up. My knees ached. I was twenty-eight years old and my body felt like a borrowed coat.

"Let's go in," I said.

* * *

Inside, the palace smelled like rain on hot stone. Real smell — not remembered. That surprised me until I understood: this close to the door, things leaked through. Ten years of leaking. The gods on the other side had been pressing against the membrane like starving men against a window, and everything true about them had seeped into the seams of Averlaine.

The heart chamber was round and low-ceilinged, and the walls were so thin that daylight came through them colored — green-gold, like light through bottle glass, like the light of the one summer I couldn't remember until three days ago and now couldn't stop remembering. My mother's garden. The storm I called at nine years old without meaning to. The look on her face — not fear. Delight. *There she is,* she'd said. *There's our weather-witch.*

In the center of the chamber stood the door.

I had expected something grand. Iron-banded oak, maybe, or carved stone, something that matched the size of what I'd done. But the door was thin as paper. It stood freestanding in the middle of the room, tall as a man, and it was translucent, and through it I could see movement — slow, dim, enormous shapes shifting like whales seen through deep water. The surface of it shivered when I breathed.

"It knows you're here," Tam whispered.

"How can it—" I started.

"Everything here is memory," Tam said. "And you're the biggest one left."

I crossed the floor slowly. Each step made the light in the walls flicker. When I was an arm's length from the door, I raised my hand — I don't remember deciding to — and laid my palm flat against it.

Cold. Then heat. Then both at once, and underneath them a vibration like a struck bell that never finished ringing, and underneath *that*, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, voices.

Not words. Prayers. Thousands of prayers, pressed flat by time, stacked like leaves. *Send rain. Let her live. Why have you forsaken us. Thank you. Thank you. Please. Please.* Every one of them unanswered. Every one of them mine to answer for.

I took my hand back like I'd touched a stove.

"Hazel." Corren stood by the west wall, arms crossed tight. He'd been quiet since the tower, quiet in the specific way of a man holding something heavy very carefully. "Sera wrote down what the priests told her. About opening it."

"I know what it takes."

"You know what Maren *said* it takes. There's a difference."

"Maren Sloe doesn't lie anymore," I said. "That's the worst thing about her. She burned her own Church rather than lie to us about Vell. She's not going to soften this."

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. We both heard it again, Maren's voice, steady as a woman reading a bill of sale: *A door sealed by a storm-caller's full strength can only be opened the same way. All of it. Everything she has. The old texts are very clear on what happens to the vessel.*

*The vessel empties,* she had said. And then, because she was honest even in cruelty: *Most vessels break.*

"She also said there might be another way," Corren said. "A thousand doors opened by a thousand hands—"

"Averlaine is one child and a wolf-hide map. There isn't time for a thousand anything." I looked at the door, at the vast slow shapes behind it. One of them had stopped moving. It hung near the surface, near my handprint's fading warmth, and though it had no face I recognized — I recognized it anyway. I would know that indifference anywhere. It had watched me grieve and done nothing, and I had built my whole ruined life on top of that nothing.

"You hate him," Tam said quietly.

"Yes."

"And you're going to save him anyway."

I crouched down again. Tam's skin had gone almost transparent; I could see the ghost of the chamber's light through their cheek. "I'm going to open a door," I said. "What comes through it is between them and the world."

"That's a grown-up way of saying yes."

"I've had ten years of practice at grown-up ways of saying things."

Tam considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then they reached out and took two of my fingers in their small cold hand. "When you do it," they said, "don't be angry. Anger makes storms crooked. My grandmother said that. She was a bell-ringer." A pause. "I think she was. I think that's why the bells stayed."

The lump in my throat was the size of a fist. I swallowed around it.

"Then I'll try not to be angry."

* * *

We waited for nightfall, or for what passed for nightfall in a country made of memory — the remembered sky went dark on a schedule Tam's bones kept, and stars appeared in patterns no astronomer had ever charted, because Tam had never learned the real ones. Corren built no fire. We sat against the thin glowing wall of the heart chamber, the three of us, shoulder to shoulder like soldiers, and I found I couldn't stop talking.

I told them about Vell. About the goats — all their names, Pepper and Juniper and the mean one called Bishop. About Odell teaching me to salt meat and read weather signs with no magic in them at all, just cloud-shapes and bird-flight, the ordinary kind, the honest kind. About the lamb I'd prayed over in my first week in the valley, and how the prayer fell out of me like water finding a crack, and how I'd felt it die at the border stones and been grateful. Grateful. I'd spent ten years being grateful for a hole in myself and calling it peace.

"Sera prayed here once," Corren said, into the quiet after. "She was eleven. Our village well had gone bad. She walked to the shrine of the rain-mother and she prayed all day." His voice was level. Too level. "Nothing happened. And she came home and told our father the gods were dead, and he hit her for saying it, and I swore then that if I ever met one of the bastards responsible, I'd—"

He stopped.

"I know," I said.

"Do you?" He wasn't cruel. That was the terrible thing. He was just tired, and honest, and out of road. "Because I've had three weeks to decide what to do with what I know about you, Hazel Dunmore, and I still don't know. You killed my sister's whole country. You starved every god she ever might have prayed to. And you're also the only person alive who can give any of it back." He laughed, short and awful. "My sister used to say the world doesn't give you enemies and friends. It gives you people, and then it gives you information."

"What are you going to do with your information?"

"I'm going to stand next to you tomorrow," he said, "and watch you empty yourself into a door, and I am going to hate it, and I'm going to be grateful, and both of those things are true and I've decided to stop pretending they cancel out." He lay back against the glowing wall. "Go to sleep, storm-caller. You'll want your strength."

I didn't sleep. I sat with my hand on my own sternum and felt the storm in there — because it was in there, it had always been in there, a decade of weather pressed down into the marrow like flowers in a book — and I measured it the way Odell taught me to measure flour. Enough. More than enough. Enough to kill, if killing was what it did, and Maren's honest face hung in the dark telling me that mostly, it did.

*Vessel,* the old texts said. As if I were a cup. As if pouring me out were simple.

Behind the paper-thin door, the vast shape that I hated had not moved. Waiting. It had waited ten years; it could wait one more night. And I lay there hating it and loving it in the same breath, because it was the god of rain, and rain had been the first magic I ever loved, before it was the first grief, before it was the last weapon, and some traitor part of me wanted nothing in the world so badly as to hear it fall again.

* * *

Dawn in Averlaine was a slow brightening of the walls themselves, gold rising through the glass like tea through water. Tam woke first and stood at the chamber's edge, watching the remembered light, and when I joined them I saw why they'd woken.

The palace was fading.

Not dramatically. No crumbling. Just — less. The gold was thinner. The wheat beyond the windows had gone pale as salt. And Tam, standing in it, was more transparent than the morning.

"It's costing too much to keep this room solid," they said matter-of-factly. "You should hurry."

"Tam—"

"If you say something nice to me right now I'll cry," they said, "and crying costs memory too. Go stand at your door, Hazel Dunmore. I'll hold the walls as long as I can. That's my job. Yours is the loud one."

So I went.

The door waited in the center of the chamber, thin as a hymnal page, trembling faintly, and the shapes behind it pressed closer now — I could see them clearly, finally, the old gods of Averlaine worn down to shadows of shadows, the rain-mother reduced to a long gray ribbon of falling water that never reached the ground, the hearth-lord a single ember drifting in a shape that had once held shoulders and a smile. Starving. Diminished. Ten years of famine pressing their faces to the window of the world.

And above them, nearest the surface, him. The one whose name I'd screamed in a burning tower. The god of storms, who had watched a girl bury her family and offered her nothing — no comfort, no sign, not so much as a change in the wind — and so had taught her the lesson she'd built her exile on: that the gods take our prayers and keep our love and return nothing, nothing, nothing.

I put my hand on the door. It was warm now. It knew.

"All of it," I said aloud, to myself, to the vessel. "Everything I have."

The storm rose in me like floodwater finding its old banks. I felt it fill my chest, my throat, my hands — ten years of swallowed weather, every prayer I'd ever muttered into the deaf air of Vell, every hour of numbness that had really been pressure, held down, held down, held down. The walls of the palace began to sing. The door began to glow. Somewhere far away — or very close — I heard Tam's voice crack on a word they'd forgotten, and Corren shouting my name, and beneath it all the deep bell-note of the membrane thinning, thinning, ready to tear—

And the shape behind the door moved for the first time in ten years.

It pressed close. It filled the whole surface. And through the paper-thin veil, in a voice like the first thunder I'd ever loved, the god who broke me spoke my name.

*"Hazel."*

Not an apology. Not yet. Just my name — the way my mother had said it, delighted, in a garden, under a storm I'd called without meaning to.

I drew back my arm. I gathered everything. The chamber blazed white, and the door began to split along a seam no eye could find—

—and through the widening crack, cold and wet and impossibly sweet, the first drops of ten years' rain began to fall on my upturned face.
