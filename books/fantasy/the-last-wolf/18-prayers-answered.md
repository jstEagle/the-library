## 18. Prayers Answered

The storm was already in her.

Hazel felt it the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue — not as weather but as pressure, something vast and syllabic waiting behind her ribs for permission to be spoken. Ten years of silence in Vell had taught her to hold it there, to keep her chest closed around it like a fist around a coal. She had thought that was discipline. She understood now that it had been grief, compressed.

Below her, at the foot of the glass palace, the Church came up the remembered road in ranks of grey.

* * *

They should have been a rumor. Averlaine existed only where it was held in mind — Tam's mind, mostly, and Tam was running out. But Maren Sloe's soldiers were believers of a specific and terrible kind: men and women who had spent their whole lives un-remembering, and who therefore knew exactly how memory worked. They carried relics of forgetting — ash from burned archives, nails pulled from demolished churches, little lead tokens stamped with the Quiet sigil — and where they walked, Tam's country thinned. Hazel watched it happen. A market street she had crossed an hour ago, loud with phantom vendors, simply stopped being between one step and the next. The road ahead of the grey ranks was blank, white as unwritten paper, and they advanced across the blankness without slowing, because to them all countries were equally imaginary until proven otherwise.

"Hazel." Corren's voice, tight. "They're moving faster."

"I see them."

At the palace gates, Maren Sloe stood alone in their path, pilgrim's robes traded for the black-and-grey of her rank, her hands empty and raised. Even from this distance Hazel could read the set of her shoulders: a woman who had already decided how she would die and found the decision restful.

"She can't hold them," Corren said. "Not all of them. Not long."

"She doesn't need long." Hazel turned toward the doors. "She needs minutes. So do I."

Tam lay against the palace wall where they had propped them, a small shape gone nearly translucent. The child's eyes were open but seeing somewhere else, some earlier place — Hazel recognized the look. It was the look of someone reading a book only they could see, except the book was eating them as they read. Every bell in Averlaine rang faintly whenever Tam breathed out. Every breath in, and one of those bells went silent forever.

"Tam." Hazel knelt. "Hey. Look at me."

Tam looked. That much was left. "You're going to do it," they whispered. Their voice had gone thin as the country around them. "The door."

"I'm going to try."

"No trying." Tam's hand found hers — cold, terribly light, like holding a folded page. "You promised. You remember for both of us. So remember this too." The child's grip tightened with what strength remained. "Remember me angry about it. I don't want to be a story where everyone's sad and nice. I want to be a story where I was mad I didn't get more time. Say it back."

Hazel's throat closed. She said it anyway. "You're angry about it."

"Good." And Tam smiled — fierce, gap-toothed, alive in the way that mattered — and let go of her hand, and closed their eyes, and gave themselves back to the work of holding a country together.

Corren touched Hazel's shoulder. He didn't say anything. There wasn't anything left to say that they hadn't said on the road, in the silence between them, in the grace neither of them had earned but both had chosen. He just stood beside her at the doors of the glass palace, and when she looked at him he nodded once.

Inside, then.

* * *

The door stood at the heart of the palace, exactly as her returned memories had told her it would: a door made of nothing but thinness, paper-pale, hanging in a frame of storm-dark iron in a hall where the glass walls showed every season of Averlaine at once. Through its seams leaked a light that was not light — older, hungrier, the color of the sky before the first rain ever fell.

Behind it, she could feel them. The gods. Starving.

And behind them, one presence larger than the rest, indifferent even now, even dying — the god who had looked down at her burning village ten years ago and done nothing. The god whose silence had taught her what silence cost. Her hands shook. Good, she thought. Let them shake. Fury is fuel. She'd learned that young.

She put her palms against the door.

It knew her. Of course it did — she had sealed it, though the sealing-self was another woman now, a girl with smoke in her hair and her family's names still warm in her mouth. The door trembled under her hands like an animal remembering a hand that had fed it and a hand that had struck it, unable to tell which one was coming now.

"You'll need everything," Corren said quietly. "All of it."

"I know what it costs."

"That's not why I said it." He stepped back, giving her room, giving her witness. "I said it so you'd hear it out loud before you decide. Nobody here is making you."

Nobody here is making you. Odell had never said those words in ten years of careful silences. The Church certainly hadn't. Her own younger self hadn't — that girl had decided everything in one night of fire and never asked permission again.

But Corren said it, standing in his dead sister's country, in front of a door that would take from Hazel the last thing she owned, and meant it as a gift.

"Thank you," she said, and meant it as acceptance.

Then she reached into the locked room behind her ribs and opened it all the way.

* * *

The storm of her life began as a sound.

Not thunder — thunder came later, thunder came when the sky finally caught up. First it was a sound inside her, a chord struck on an instrument she'd been for ten years without knowing she was tuned. It started low, in the bones Vell had kept quiet, and rose through the years of numbness and goat-milk mornings and Odell's bread and the lamb she'd prayed over, and it gathered every answered prayer she had ever accidentally spoken, every rain she'd called without knowing, every hour of safe gray silence she'd mistaken for peace.

The glass palace filled with wind.

Outside, she dimly knew, the world was answering. Maren Sloe's voice rose over the ranks of grey — Hazel couldn't hear the words, but she heard the shape of them, the cadence of a woman reciting truths she'd been forbidden, buying minutes with her own name. Somewhere a bell that Tam could no longer remember rang anyway. The country held. The country held because a child was paying for it and a traitor-inquisitor was spending herself for it and a man who had every right to hate her chose, minute by minute, to stand witness instead.

Hazel poured all of it into the door.

Lightning first — her oldest language. It ran down her arms and into the storm-dark frame and the frame drank it like dry earth drinks the first rain after drought, and groaned, and the door bowed inward. Then wind, then hail, then the deep tectonic pressure of a storm-front miles wide, the kind of weather that has opinions. The paper-thin door split along seams no eye could find, and through the splits came the smell of the old world: rain on stone older than stone, incense, animal warmth, the green breath of forests that had worshipped something.

The gods pressed against the other side. She felt them the way a window feels birds in winter — many, weightless, desperate.

"Not yet," she gasped, though her arms screamed otherwise. "Not through a crack. All the way or not at all."

She tore.

There was no gentler word for it. She took hold of the door the way you take hold of a wound that must be reopened to heal, and she ripped it open top to bottom, and the storm of her life went through her like floodwater going through a dam — and for one endless moment Hazel Dunmore was not a person at all. She was weather. She was the whole argument the sky had ever had with the earth, funneled through one small furious body in a glass hall at the center of a forgotten country.

The door came apart into rain.

* * *

They came through slowly, the gods. That surprised her. She had expected eruption; instead they emerged the way survivors come out of a cellar after a long storm, blinking, gaunt, unbelieving.

They were smaller than she remembered. That was the worst of it. In her childhood terror they had been vast — faces in clouds, voices in thunder, presences that made harvests fail with a shrug. These were diminished things, starved down to essentials, gods worn to their cores. A river goddess with silt in her hair and her waters gone from her eyes. A hearth-god carrying his own cold fire like an orphaned child. A field mother whose hands were just hands now, calloused and empty. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Shuffling through the broken doorway into a country held together by a fading child, looking around at the remembered bells and remembered markets as if none of it could possibly be for them.

And last, filling the doorway, the tall one. The indifferent one. The god who had watched her family's village burn and turned his face — not cruelly, that was the thing she'd never been able to forgive, not cruelly but *neutrally*, the way weather turns. Storm-crowned. Rain-eyed. Her own reflection in his regard.

He looked at her, and Hazel felt ten years compress into a single held breath.

Say something, she thought. Justify it. Tell me why. Give me one sentence and I will spend the rest of my life deciding whether to believe it.

The god said nothing. Of course he didn't. Silence was his native tongue; it was the whole reason there had ever been a door to close.

Her hands were still full of lightning. She felt it coiled and ready, every volt shaped by ten years of rehearsed verdicts — she had imagined this moment in the numb gray years more often than she'd admitted, and in every version she struck him down. In every version it was easy. Gods died like anything else if you hit them with enough sky.

Maren Sloe was falling somewhere outside. Tam was thinning against a wall. Corren's sister had prayed in this country and been erased for it, and this god's silence had signed the order.

Strike him down. It would be so easy. It would be justice, or adjacent to justice, close enough that no one would argue.

The god waited. Indifferent even to his own death. That, more than anything, undid her — because she saw it plainly now, what ten years and one reopened door had taught her: his indifference wasn't contempt. It was hunger. He had gone silent because no one remembered him, and no one remembered him because he had gone silent, and the loop had eaten him hollow from the inside out until there was nothing left in him but the waiting. He was not refusing to speak.

He had forgotten how, because no one was listening.

The same crime she'd committed in Vell. The same locked room. The same self-made exile, wearing a crown.

"Oh," Hazel said aloud, and the word came out wrecked. "Oh, you *idiot*. You enormous, ancient idiot. That's all it was?"

Lightning guttered in her fists.

She had come here ready to kill a tyrant. What stood in front of her was a beggar who had forgotten he was allowed to ask. And fury, she discovered, does not die when it meets a truth like that. It transmutes. It burns just as hot and goes somewhere new.

She opened her hands and let the lightning go — up, out, harmless, spent into the high glass where it scattered into harmless gold. Then she did the one thing no version of this moment had ever contained.

She knelt. Storm-born, door-breaker, the last wolf of Averlaine — she knelt on the glass floor in front of a starving god, and she spoke to him the way Odell had spoken to frightened children, the way you speak to anything half-dead of loneliness.

"Listen carefully," she said, "because I'm only saying this once, and then I'm going to teach a whole world to say it. You are seen. You are known. You are *remembered*." Her voice cracked and she let it. "That's what you lost. Not worship. Not sacrifice. Someone to know your name. Well — I know it. I've always known it. I hated you by name for ten years, and hating someone by name is still a kind of prayer, and I am so tired of praying alone."

Around her, the lesser gods had gone still, listening — actually listening, drinking the words like water.

"So here is what I give you," Hazel said, rising, finding her feet, finding her voice. "Not a door. Doors close. I give you a people. The ones out there — the feral child holding your country together with her own life, the woman burning her Church's rulebook, the man who forgave me without my earning it, the valley full of farmers who prayed to whatever was listening and got *me*. They remember. They're learning to remember more. And I will carry every one of your names out of this place if I have to walk them home syllable by syllable."

She took the storm-crowned god's hand. It was cold. It was shaking.

"You are not dead," Hazel Dunmore told the god she had hated, "and you are not forgiven, because forgiveness isn't mine to give — but you are *welcome*. Now come and meet your people."

And the god wept, and his weeping was rain, and it was the gentlest rain Averlaine had ever known.

* * *

The door did not close behind them. She had torn it past closing — torn it into weather, into ordinary ongoing sky. It would stay open. She felt that in her bones, in what remained of her bones' acquaintance with power: the threshold was permanent now, a border rather than a barrier, and things could cross it in both directions forever.

Which meant, she realized, standing in the rain with starving gods pressing past her toward the bells and markets of a remembered country —

— which meant she could go home.

The thought arrived quietly, without triumph, the way true things do. Vell. The goats. Odell's bread. The valley that forgot, which had kept her safe by keeping her nothing, and which she had just unmade as thoroughly as she'd unmade the door — because gods pouring into the world do not respect border stones, and prayers answered in Vell would be answered by *them* now, and nothing there would ever be numb again.

She had given away the last of her magic tearing the door. She felt it plainly: the locked room behind her ribs stood empty, swept clean, just a room. Whatever she was next, she would be it mortal.

Corren appeared in the ruined doorway, soaked, singed, alive. Behind him, far below, the grey ranks were breaking — not routed, just undone, men and women staring up at rain that remembered their grandparents' names, dropping lead tokens of forgetting into the mud. Somewhere in that crowd, Hazel trusted, Maren Sloe was still standing. She intended to go find out.

"It worked," Corren said, wonder stripping his voice down to its studs.

"It worked." She laughed, and it came out half sob. "Tam—"

"Breathing. Small. Asking for you." He hesitated, and she watched him choose honesty, the hard-won habit of the last few weeks. "They won't remember you, Hazel. Not yet. Maybe not for a long time. They spent too much."

"Then I'll introduce myself," Hazel said. "I'm getting good at beginnings."

She took one step toward the door, toward Tam, toward Corren, toward the rain-washed ruin of everything and everyone she loved—

—and stopped, because the storm-crowned god touched her shoulder, and when she turned he was not looking at her with indifference anymore. He was looking at her the way you look at the person who taught you to speak.

He raised his other hand, and pointed — south, past the borders of the remembered country, past the border stones, toward a small gray valley where a woman named Odell was almost certainly standing in her doorway watching the sky behave.

"He says," Hazel translated, her heart doing something complicated, "that we should hurry. He says the valley's prayers are backing up."
