## 19. The Price of the Storm

The silence after the storm was worse than any sound.

Hazel lay on her back on the shattered floor of the glass palace, and she could not feel her hands. She could not feel much of anything. The world had gone strange and flat, like a painting of itself — colors without depth, light without warmth. Somewhere above her the dome hung open to a sky that had never been there before, and through it the gods were pouring into the world like water finding its old riverbed.

She should have felt triumphant. Everyone said that was how it went: you did the impossible thing, and then you stood in the ruin of it and felt tall. Instead she felt like an empty house. Like something had walked through every room of her and taken the furniture, the walls, the roof, and left only the shape of where they'd been.

*That's what it costs,* she thought, with the last flicker of the thing that had been her power. *That's what it always cost. I just paid it in pieces before.*

Ten years ago, she had paid in memories. Today she'd paid in everything else.

She turned her head. It was an effort like turning a millstone. Her body obeyed, but grudgingly, as if it too had been emptied and was only going through the motions out of habit.

Corren knelt a few feet away, and he was crying.

She had never seen him cry. Not when his sister's name came up, not when the map unrolled and showed him roads through a country that no longer existed, not even when Maren Sloe had put her hand on his shoulder in the market square and spoken gently about mercy. Corren's grief had always been a closed door, and Hazel had learned not to knock.

But now he knelt on the glass-strewn floor with Tam cradled against his chest, and he was sobbing like a man whose drowning had finally ended.

"Tam," he kept saying. "Tam. Tam."

And Tam — small, so small now, smaller than any child had a right to be — opened their eyes.

Hazel's breath caught. Ten minutes ago, maybe twenty — time had gone soft since the door — Tam had been fading. She had held their hand and felt the country of Averlaine draining out of them like blood from a wound: first their words, then their sight, then the memory of their own name. She had promised to remember for both of them, and she had meant it, and she had thought she was promising at a funeral.

But Tam's eyes were open, and they were new.

Not feral, the way they had been when Hazel first met the child in the reshaping hills — all edges and wildness, a creature grown strange from living inside other people's memories. And not hollow, the way they'd been at the end, spending themselves coin by coin to keep markets and bells and a palace of glass standing long enough for Hazel to reach the door.

New. Like a field after fire, before anything has decided what to grow there.

Tam blinked up at Corren, and their brow furrowed, and they said — slowly, carefully, tasting each syllable like food after famine:

"Why... are you... sad?"

Corren made a sound that was half laugh and half wound. "Because I thought I'd lost you."

"Lost," Tam repeated. They considered this. Their small hand rose and patted his cheek, once, twice, the way you'd pat a horse that was spooked. "I don't remember being lost."

"No," Corren whispered. "I don't suppose you would."

Hazel understood, distantly, what had happened. The gods had poured through the door, and the door had needed something on the other side to anchor it — or perhaps the gods, starving for ten years in the dark behind Averlaine's forgetting, had simply taken pity on the child who had paid more than anyone to hold their country together. Either way, Tam had spent themselves down to almost nothing, and what remained had been made new. Small and blank and beginning again, with no memories of markets or bells or glass palaces, and no debts either.

A clean page.

Hazel looked down at her own hands, lying pale against the broken floor, and thought: *mine is the opposite of a clean page.* Hers was a page burned past legibility. Everything written there — the storm-calling, the god-speech, the old bright terrible inheritance of her bloodline — was ash now. She reached inside herself, the way she had reached ten thousand times in the last weeks, toward the place where the weather lived.

There was nothing there.

Not silence exactly. Silence implied walls, implied a room where something might return. This was just absence, smooth and total, like the space where a tooth had been. Her tongue kept going back to it anyway.

"It worked," Corren said. He had noticed her watching. His voice was wrecked. "Hazel. The door — look at the door."

She couldn't lift her head high enough. But she could see it reflected in the glass shards scattered across the floor, dozens of little fractured mirrors, and in each one the same image: the doorway at the heart of the palace, which had been thin as paper, thin as a lie, thin as a prayer barely dared —

— and which now stood wide.

Through it came the gods.

They did not look like she remembered. That was the first thing she understood, lying there among the shards. Ten years ago, when she had slammed this door shut with grief and fury and a storm that killed the sky, the gods had been vast. Distant. Indifferent the way mountains are indifferent, the way winter is indifferent. She had begged one of them for three days over her family's bodies and received nothing but weather, and weather had seemed to her then like the cruelest possible answer.

Now they came through small.

An old woman with riverweed in her hair, wringing water from her sleeves onto stone that drank it gratefully. A young man whose skin was bark, blinking in the light as if he'd forgotten light existed. Something winged and gray that might have been a god of messages or a god of endings; it was hard to tell, and maybe it had always been both. They came limping, some of them. Came leaning on each other. Came looking around the ruined palace with expressions Hazel had never once imagined seeing on a divine face.

Wonder. Gratitude. Fear.

They were afraid, she realized. These beings who had watched empires rise and fall without blinking — they were afraid of the world they were entering. Because the world had changed. Because ten years was nothing to them and everything to everyone else. Because the people who had prayed to them were mostly dead, or scattered, or had learned to pray to quieter things.

Except.

"They're praying again," Corren said softly. "Listen."

Hazel listened. Past the ringing in her ears, past the wind still dying out of the broken dome, she heard it: far away, at the edge of hearing, voices. Villages she had walked through on the road here, villages that had woken this morning to rain falling in wrong places and wells running sweet and bread that wouldn't run out — those villages were on their knees, and they weren't screaming.

They were saying thank you.

The words drifted across the remembered country of Averlaine like pollen. *Thank you. Thank you. We didn't know who to thank. Thank you anyway.*

One by one, the small diminished gods straightened. The riverwoman lifted her head. The bark-skinned boy took an experimental step, then another, and where his feet fell, green things sprouted through the cracks in the glass — quick moss, quick grass, the first new growing this place had seen in a decade.

They were learning to be needed again. And the world, Hazel thought, was learning to need.

It should have filled her. She waited for it to fill her, lying there, watching miracles take root in the cracks. Instead she felt the way she'd felt in Vell all those years — numb, safe, sealed — except now there was nothing left to seal. The valley had given her forgetfulness. The door had taken everything else.

*Be grateful,* she told herself. *You got what you wanted. Not revenge. Mercy. You gave them mercy instead of the blade. You're allowed to rest now.*

She didn't feel allowed. She felt amputated.

* * *

Getting out of the palace took most of the day, though Hazel experienced it as a long gray blur punctuated by hands.

Hands catching her elbow on the stairs. Hands pressing a waterskin into hers. Hands — small, new, insistent — tugging her sleeve until she knelt, so that Tam could study her face from a distance of six inches with the ferocious concentration of someone meeting the world for the first time.

"You're the one," Tam said.

"The one what?"

"I don't know." The child frowned. "But everyone who comes through says your name first. Like it opens something." They tilted their head. "Are you a door too?"

Hazel laughed. It surprised her, coming out of her — rusty, cracked, real. "No, little one. I'm the opposite of a door. I'm what happens when a door closes."

"That sounds lonely."

"It was."

Tam patted her hand, solemn as a judge, and ran off toward the riverwoman, who had begun showing the child how to fold reeds into boats. Some things, apparently, survived even the spending of everything. Or maybe they didn't survive at all, and this was simply a new Tam who would make entirely different things. Hazel found she couldn't decide which possibility hurt more, and decided not to decide today.

By late afternoon they had made camp in the palace courtyard, under the glass dome with its great ragged hole, because the gods kept drifting back to look at it, and Corren refused to leave while they were still coming through. Fires burned low. Someone — the bark-skinned boy, probably — had coaxed a garden out of the flagstones, and it was already knee-high and shameless about it.

Hazel sat apart, wrapped in a blanket she didn't remember taking, and watched Corren across the fire.

He was holding Tam again. He hadn't really stopped holding Tam since the door. Every few minutes his hand would drift to the child's hair, check the warmth of their head, confirm the heartbeat — the compulsive tenderness of a man who had buried one sibling already and could not believe the universe had returned him a second chance instead of a second grave.

He looked up. Caught her watching. Held her gaze.

Here it comes, Hazel thought. The question she'd confessed to under the tower's shadow, the truth he'd swallowed whole and carried in silence ever since: that his sister had died because a grieving girl named Hazel Dunmore had slammed heaven shut. That his family, and Tam's country, and ten years of erasure — all of it traced back to her hands.

She had offered him her life at the foot of the tower. He had asked for her help instead. Neither of them had said anything more, because there hadn't been time, because Maren had come, because the door was dying, because storms don't wait.

There was time now. All the time in the world. The gods were through; the Church was scattered or burning, thanks to Maren Sloe, wherever she was, if she was anywhere at all; and there was nothing left between Corren and the question except a fire and a night.

He crossed the courtyard and sat beside her. Didn't speak for a while. The fire cracked. Somewhere beyond the walls, the winged god passed overhead, silent as snowfall.

"My sister's name was Liss," Corren said at last. "You know that."

"Yes."

"She prayed at the door the week before the Church found our village. Just a child's prayer. Rain for the barley, and for Da's cough to ease." He stared into the flames. "I used to think if the door had been open, it would have been answered. Maybe not well — the gods were never good at answering well. But answered." He paused. "Then I learned who closed it. And I've been carrying two truths in my chest for weeks, Hazel, and they weigh different amounts, and I can't make the heavier one win."

Hazel's throat was tight. "Which one is heavier?"

"The true one." He turned to face her fully. "That you were a child who lost everything, and the gods ignored you, and you broke. Anyone would have broken. I'd have broken. Maybe I did break, and that's why I ran instead of fighting." His voice roughened. "The lighter truth is that you owe me. And I keep reaching for the lighter one because it's easier to hold. But lighter isn't truer."

"So what do you do with the heavy one?"

"You carry it." He said it simply, the way he said everything that mattered. "Together, if you'll let me. Not because it's forgiven — I don't think forgiveness is a thing you finish, I think it's a thing you do, daily, like hauling water. But because Liss prayed at that door, and you opened it, and my sister's rain is falling somewhere tonight for the first time in ten years." He exhaled shakily. "I can hate you and thank you in the same breath. I've checked. It works."

Hazel discovered she was crying. It felt foreign — tears without magic behind them, salt and water and nothing else, the plainest human thing. She let them come. There was no storm to hide them in anymore. There was no storm at all.

"I'm empty," she said. "I want you to understand that. When I crossed the stones and it all came back — the power, the memories — I thought that was the worst of it. But giving it up is worse. I keep reaching for it. I reach and there's nothing. I'm just—" her voice failed, steadied— "I'm just a woman who tends goats."

Corren was quiet a moment. Then: "In Vell?"

"In Vell."

"Good," he said. "Stay a woman who tends goats for a while. The world's had enough of your storms. Let it have your ordinary days instead. I think—" and here he smiled, tired and crooked, "—I think the ordinary days are the ones the gods were jealous of all along. Why do you think they kept answering prayers? They wanted in."

Across the courtyard, the riverwoman glanced over at them — at Hazel — and inclined her head. Not worship. Recognition. One survivor acknowledging another.

Tam ran up and dropped a folded reed boat into Hazel's lap. "For you," they announced. "So you can find your way home."

"I don't know if I have one," Hazel said honestly.

Tam frowned at her with that terrible new clarity children have. "Everyone has one. Sometimes it's just on the other side of a door."

* * *

Night came down soft over Averlaine, and the stars came out through the broken dome — more stars than Hazel had ever seen, because the country remembered skies the way it remembered everything: generously, impossibly.

She slept a little. Woke once to find the winged god perched on the courtyard wall, watching her with eyes like the space between words.

"You gave us mercy," it said. Its voice sounded like pages turning. "We expected the blade. We knew your face, storm-child. We felt you coming with murder in your heart for ten years."

"I found something better to do with my hands."

"Mm." The god tilted its head. "Your power is gone. Truly gone. You know this."

"I know."

"And yet." It leaned closer, and its feathers smelled like libraries, like dust, like the ends of letters never sent. "Doors remember the hands that open them. Storms leave channels. You are empty, Hazel Dunmore, but you are not erased — and the difference matters. Something of you will always sit in the hinge of that door. When we pass through, we will feel you. When prayers rise, some small part of them will brush against whatever is left of the girl who called the storm."

"Is that supposed to comfort me?"

"No," said the god, honestly. "Gods are bad at comfort. Ask anyone. It's why you stopped praying." It spread its wings. "But it should warn you. Things that sit in hinges get visited."

It flew off into the enormous remembered dark, and Hazel lay awake a long time after, listening to the far-off sound of bells.

* * *

Dawn found them packing. There wasn't much to pack — a wolf-hide map that no longer showed roads, because Averlaine no longer needed hiding; a waterskin; a reed boat Tam refused to leave behind; the small hoard of ordinary things that three battered lives accumulate.

At the palace gate, the gods gathered to see them off, and it was so absurd that Hazel nearly laughed. Divinity lined up in a ruined courtyard to wave goodbye to a goatherd, a fugitive, and a child. The riverwoman pressed a stopper bottle of riverwater into Tam's hands. The bark-boy gave Corren a cutting that was already blooming. And the winged one — the messenger, the ender — settled briefly on Hazel's shoulder, weightless as a thought, and murmured something too quiet for anyone else to hear.

Then the road east, toward Vell, toward the border stones, toward Odell and the valley that had taught her how to live without magic and was about to learn what she'd done with the emptiness instead.

Corren walked beside her. Tam rode his shoulders, drowsing, clutching the reed boat.

"Hazel," Corren said, after an hour of silence. "When we get to the stones. What happens to you there? Your magic died on the far side of them once. Now it's dead everywhere. Crossing won't hurt you anymore."

"No," she agreed. "Nothing left to hurt."

"Then what's waiting for you?" He glanced sideways. "Odell told you to swear never to return. You refused. You chose exile over exile. So what do you walk back into?"

Hazel looked down the long road, at the hills remembering themselves green, at the thin gold line of morning.

"I don't know," she said. "And for the first time in ten years, that doesn't terrify me."

Behind them, faint and clear, a bell rang in rebuilt Averlaine — and ahead, past the ridgeline, she could see the smoke of hearth fires rising from the valley of Vell.

Too many hearth fires, she thought suddenly. Far too many for a village that size.

Something was happening at home.
