## 20. Homecoming Without Exile

I had rehearsed the speech the whole way back.

It changed with every mile, but the bones of it never did: *I'm sorry. I know what I cost you. I'll go.* I'd said it to Odell in my head a hundred times, in a hundred tones — defiant once, weeping once, mostly just tired. Ten years ago I had walked into Vell carrying nothing but a sentence and a silence I'd bought at a terrible price. It seemed fitting that I walk out the same way.

Except I wasn't walking out. The road bent east around the shoulder of the mountain, and then the valley opened below me like a hand uncurling, and my feet kept taking me down into it because there was nowhere else they knew how to go.

Corren walked beside me. Tam rode on his shoulders, half-asleep, small fingers curled loosely in his hair — smaller than Tam had been a week ago, younger, as if whatever was left after all that giving had decided to start over from the beginning. Every few minutes Corren would glance up, checking, the way you check a candle flame in a draft. He hadn't stopped doing it since the door.

"You don't have to come all the way," I said. "The stones are another two miles."

"I know where the stones are." His voice was rough. He'd been crying quietly for most of the morning and pretending he hadn't, and I was pretending not to notice, which is its own kind of tenderness between people who have confessed worse things than tears. "I want to see it."

"See what?"

"The place that kept you." He shifted Tam's weight. "You talked about it like a prison for ten years' worth of someone else's memories. I want to see what a prison looks like when it feeds its prisoners first."

"It's goats," I said. "Mostly it's goats."

He laughed — short, cracked, real — and Tam stirred and mumbled something in a language I almost recognized and didn't, some syllable of Averlaine's remembered tongue, and Corren went still with listening until the child settled again.

We came over the last rise in the gold light of late afternoon, and that was when I saw the smoke.

Not burning — cooking-smoke, hearth-smoke, the ordinary gray breath of a valley full of suppers. But above it, wrong and wonderful, rain was falling on the far fields. Just there. A soft column of silver falling through sunlight onto Odell's barley, while the rest of the sky sat clear and blue as a glazed bowl.

I stopped walking.

"Hazel." Corren's hand found my elbow. "Hazel, look at the stones."

The border stones stood in their old line across the valley mouth, gray and lichened, the ones I had touched ten years ago expecting to feel my magic die against them like a snuffed candle. Pilgrims' ribbons fluttered on the tallest one — new ribbons, dozens, bright as wounds.

And the stones were humming.

Not loudly. A sound you felt in your teeth more than your ears, a low sweet drone like a wet finger on a glass rim, and every ribbon on them trembled with it. As I watched, a woman from the village — Marta, the cooper's wife, who had once thrown mud at me for letting a lamb die — knelt at the base of the stones and set down a bowl of milk. An offering. She pressed her forehead to the ground, got up, and walked away without looking back, the way you leave a gift for something you trust will be there tomorrow.

"They're praying again," I said. My voice came out strange. "They were always too frightened to pray."

"The gods came through," Corren said quietly. "People felt it even here. Even behind your wall." He looked at me sideways. "Your wall, Hazel. You built this quiet. Maybe that's why they trust it now. It's not a cage anymore. It's a foundation."

I couldn't answer him. There was a knot in my throat shaped exactly like the word *home*, and I had spent ten years refusing to say it.

Then Odell stepped out from behind the tallest stone, and every rehearsal died in my mouth.

She was older. Of course she was; it had only been weeks since I'd seen her, but grief works fast on some faces, and mine had been missing presumed dead. She walked toward us down the pilgrim path with her stick striking the ground in her old furious rhythm, and I braced for it — the verdict, the exile, the *you brought the Church to our border and Maren Sloe burned half the ridge and now you'll take your storm and go* —

She stopped three paces away. Looked me up and down. Looked at Corren. Looked up at Tam, who lifted a sleepy head from Corren's hair and stared at her with the vast ancient patience of the very young.

"Well," Odell said. "You look terrible."

"I — what?"

"You heard me." Her eyes were wet and her voice was iron. "Ten years I fed you and clothed you and taught you to keep your head down, and you go off and tear open the sky, and you come back looking like something the storm spat out. Have you eaten?"

"Odell—"

"Have. You. Eaten."

"No," I admitted.

"Then stop standing there waiting for me to banish you." She hit my arm with the stick, lightly, the way she used to when I was slow with the milking. "I buried a girl ten years ago and mourned her every day, and last midsummer's night the rain fell in the shape of a door over my barley and I knew she'd come back to life somewhere. Do you think I dragged you in off the stones as a child to throw you out as a woman? Sit down. Both of you. Marta's making stew and there are forty people in this village who've been asking after you for a week, and if you think I'm going to let you slink past them to some martyrdom, you have learned nothing in my kitchen."

The knot in my throat broke. I put my arms around her — carefully, because of the stick, because of everything — and she held on with the grip of a woman who has hauled drowning lambs out of floods before and does not let go until they kick.

"I expected the stones," I said into her shoulder.

"The stones hum for you now, girl. Didn't you hear?" She pulled back and gripped my face in both hands, thumbs hard on my cheekbones, and looked at me the way she used to when I was small and lying about who broke the crock. "Vell doesn't forget. Vell was made to hold one thing safe, and it held *you*. That was always its work. Now the work's done, and the valley knows it." She glanced at the humming stones, the ribbons, the impossible rain on the barley. "Things are changing here. Gods walking about being thanked instead of feared. Wells that sing. Old Nan swore she saw the river god fishing in the shallows Tuesday, bold as you please, wearing a hat." She sniffed. "If we must live in a world with miracles in it, better a world that remembers whose hands opened the door."

"I emptied myself to do it," I said. "There's nothing left in me, Odell. I'm just — a woman. I can't protect anyone anymore."

"Good," said Odell, with such savage satisfaction that Tam laughed — a bright, startling, baby-bell laugh from Corren's shoulders. "Magic never made you kind. Kindness did. Come home."

* * *

The village met us in the lane, and I will tell it plainly because I still don't believe it: nobody threw anything.

Marta hugged me, mud-throwing be damned. The miller's boys fought over who got to carry Tam, and lost, because Tam refused to let go of Corren's neck. Old Nan took my hands in hers, turned them over like she was reading weather in them, and said, "Storm's passed," which from her was either a blessing or a diagnosis. Someone pressed bread into my arms, warm, and I stood in the middle of the lane holding it like an idiot while the whole valley flowed around me, and I understood that I had been forgiven so long ago that the forgiveness had gone stale waiting for me to collect it.

At supper — forty people, one long table, more food than Vell had seen since the wells refilled — Corren stood up.

He wasn't good at speeches; I could see him deciding against one. Instead he unrolled the wolf-hide map on the table, and the room went silent, because everyone in Vell had grown up on stories of questions that kill.

"This country is called Averlaine," he said. "The Church burned everyone who remembered it. Except children, sometimes, hidden in cellars. I was one." His hand rested on the hide, on the little inked bell-tower, the glass palace, the road home. "My sister drew this. She prayed at the sealed door once, and no one answered, and she died believing the gods had abandoned her. They hadn't. They were locked on the other side, starving." He looked at me. "A woman born of storm paid to open it. And now Averlaine is coming back — the roads remember themselves, the bells ring, the walls stand up out of the grass. Somebody has to be there as it returns. To build. To keep the memory honest. To make sure no child ever prays into a locked door again."

Tam, sitting beside him, raised a small solemn hand and said, clearly, in the village silence: "*I'm going to help.*

"He's four years old," Corren added, apologetic. "More or less. He says he remembers how the bridges go."

"And where do you stand in this?" Odell asked me.

"Nowhere," I said. "That's the point. I gave it all away. I'm staying."

Corren crossed the room and took my hands. Up close his eyes were red-rimmed and steady.

"I know," he said. "I've known since the tower. You're not the storm anymore, Hazel. You're what the storm was *for*." His thumb brushed my knuckles. "Family doesn't need shared walls. It needs shared remembering. So remember us. That's your whole job now. When Averlaine's bells ring right, when Tam learns to speak in full sentences again, when the market square smells like cardamom on a Saturday — I'll write it down and send it over the mountains, and you'll read it aloud in this kitchen, and that way it gets remembered twice."

"That's a stupid plan," I said, crying.

"It's the best one I've ever had." He grinned, and it was his sister's grin, and I let him have the moment.

* * *

They left three days later, at dawn, on the road through the stones.

Half the village came to see them off, which would have been unthinkable a month ago. Marta had packed enough food for a siege. Old Nan gave Tam a carved wooden wolf, and Tam held it up and declared, gravely, "This is Hazel," which delighted everyone except me, briefly, and then I decided to be delighted forever.

At the stones, the humming rose to meet them — welcoming, the way a house creaks welcome when the door opens. Ribbons stirred in no wind.

Corren hugged me hard enough to crack ribs, or so it felt, and whispered, "Write back," which meant *don't disappear again*, which meant *live*.

Tam kissed both my cheeks with the fierce precision of a child administering a sacrament, and said, "Remember the blue bridge. Nobody else remembers the blue bridge. It's yours now."

"I'll remember," I promised. "Both of us. Always."

Then they walked through, east, into the morning, and the map on wolf-hide swung from Corren's pack, and the road ahead of them shimmered faintly, the way roads do when a country is remembering itself beneath them.

Odell put her arm through mine.

"Well," she said. "Goats won't milk themselves."

"No," I agreed.

We walked home.

* * *

That evening I climbed to the top pasture, where the lambs grazed and the view ran all the way to the border stones, and I sat down in the grass with the wooden wolf in my pocket and did the hardest work of my new life, which was nothing at all.

No storm followed me. No power answered when I reached for it — and I did reach, once, gently, the way you touch the scar of a tooth that's gone. Nothing came. Just the evening wind, ordinary wind, smelling of rain that had already fallen on someone else's fields.

But far to the east, faint as memory, a bell rang.

Once. Twice. A third time, joyfully, as if practicing.

And on the border stones below, in the last light, the ribbons lifted — and this time I heard, threaded under their humming, very soft and very far away, a voice that was learning again how to speak, saying words I knew, words I had promised to carry:

*Blue bridge. Remember the blue bridge.*

I smiled, and lay back in the grass, and began — carefully, deliberately, out loud, so the valley could hear — to remember.
