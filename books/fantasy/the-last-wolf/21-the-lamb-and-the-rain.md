## 21. The Lamb and the Rain

Three springs after the door opened, I still woke before dawn out of habit rather than need.

The goats didn't care what hour it was; they cared that the gate was latched and the hay was dry and no fox had gotten ambitious in the night. I did the rounds with a lantern I barely needed anymore — my hands had learned the path to the pen the way they'd once learned the shapes of storm clouds — and I counted heads the way Odell had taught me my first winter in Vell, when I'd been a hollow-eyed girl of nineteen who flinched at thunder because some part of her knew it should answer when she called.

The lamb was sick again.

It was one of Odell's ewe's late litter, born small in a cold snap, all knobby knees and stubbornness, and for two days it had lain in the straw with its ribs working like bellows and its mother lowing over it in a voice I knew too well. I'd carried it inside my cottage the second night, wrapped it in wool, fed it from a rag dipped in milk warmed on my stove. There was nothing clever about any of it. That was the point. Everything I did now was done with hands alone — hands and patience and the ordinary stubbornness of a woman keeping something alive because she could.

I sat on the floor of my kitchen with the lamb against my chest, feeling its heartbeat flutter against mine, and I did the thing I had sworn off doing and then unsworn and then simply stopped arguing with myself about.

"Please," I said, into its wool-smelling ear. "If anyone's listening."

I hadn't asked for rain this time. I'd asked once, three years ago, over another lamb, and the sky had split open above my field like a wound, and I'd understood that whatever lived behind the door I'd torn open could still hear me — not because I had power, but because I had paid for the asking. The gods remembered their debts. It was, as far as I could tell, the only thing about them that had never changed.

The rain came anyway.

It always came as rain. Softly, this time — a fine gray hush against the roof, no more than a mist, falling only over my croft while the rest of the valley lay dry under a clearing sky. The lamb stirred against my chest. Its breathing eased. By full light it was standing on wobbly legs, butting at my knee, demanding breakfast as if nearly dying were just one item on a long list of grievances.

I stood in my doorway with the mist beading on my arms and watched it fall where it shouldn't, and I thought: *this is what I bought with everything.* Not power. Not glory. Just this — a god's attention, spent like a coin, on a sick lamb in a valley that magic had once been forbidden to touch.

Somewhere past the border stones, a bell rang. Averlaine's bells could be heard in Vell now on still mornings, though there was no honest distance between here and there that sound should cross. Nobody in the village remarked on it anymore. Miracles wear out fast when they become routine. Or maybe that's not wearing out. Maybe that's just becoming home.

* * *

Odell came up the hill at midmorning, walking with the stick she'd finally admitted she needed and refusing, on principle, to use it on the flat parts. She was smaller every year and no less immovable, like one of the border stones themselves.

"You're feeding that lamb out of your own stores," she said, by way of greeting. It wasn't a question. Nothing Odell said was ever a question.

"It'll eat you out of house and home otherwise."

"It'll eat you out of house and home," she agreed, lowering herself onto my bench with the care of a woman negotiating with her own joints. She looked at the mist still drifting over the field, thinning now, reluctant to end. "Third time this season."

"I don't ask for much."

"You don't ask at all. You mutter. There's a difference, and the difference is that muttering is how you prayed before you knew what you were." She accepted the cup of tea I handed her and held it in both hands without drinking. "The others pray properly. On their knees, with the words Tam taught them. And their wells fill, and their bread rises, and their children's fevers break. But yours come as rain. Every time. You know why."

"Because I was born of storm."

"Because you're still apologizing," Odell said. "Every drop is an apology, girl. You tore a hole in the world and then you mended it, and you've spent three years trying to decide which one of those things defines you." She drank at last. "Decide faster. You're getting wrinkles."

I laughed despite myself. That was Odell's gift — she could put a knife between your ribs and make you grateful for the handle.

"The pilgrims are back on the road," she said, after a while. "More this year. They come through the stones now instead of around, since there's nothing left to fear on either side. Mostly they want to see the market. Some want to see the glass palace." Her eyes came to mine over the rim of the cup. "One of them wants to see you."

"Me?"

"A child. Walking alone, which I don't love, but she has papers from the Averlaine road-wardens, so she's somebody's responsibility, technically, somewhere. She's asking after the last wolf." Odell set down the cup. "I told her the last wolf was a farmer with bad fences and an opinion about everything. She said she'd wait. She's waiting in my kitchen right now, eating my honey bread, and I let her because—" she paused, and for a moment the iron went out of her voice, "—because she has Tam's look about her. The way Tam looked, that night we almost lost them. Like someone who gave away more than a child should have and grew back strange."

My chest tightened. Tam was whole now — new-made, Corren wrote to me, small in spirit the way a candle flame is small, but burning. But there had been a season, that terrible season at the door, when Tam had spent themselves down to almost nothing, trading memories for walls, for streets, for a country's life. Children shouldn't know how to pay that price. This child on Odell's bench had learned it somewhere.

"Send her up," I said.

"She's already halfway up," Odell said. "I told her where you live. I'm old, not cruel — I wasn't going to make her walk back down and up again." She rose, took her stick, and paused at my gate. "Tell her true, Hazel. Whatever she asks. You owe the truth to anyone who walks that far for it."

"I know," I said. "That's the whole point of me now."

* * *

She was perhaps ten years old, sitting very straight on my bench with her boots not quite touching the floor, a satchel in her lap worn shiny at the corners from long roads. She had dark hair cut short in a practical way and eyes the color of rain over slate, and when she saw me she stood up so fast she knocked the honey bread from her knee and had to decide whether picking it up would ruin the gravity of the moment. She compromised by leaving it.

"You're Hazel Dunmore," she said.

"I'm Hazel Dunmore."

"They say you were the last wolf."

"They say a lot of things. Sit down. Eat the bread. It's better than the legend."

She sat, and ate, and studied me the whole time with the frank, unhurried stare of a child who has traveled a long way to verify a rumor and will not be distracted from the audit. I busied myself with the kettle because it was easier than being studied.

Finally she said: "I'm from Averlaine. From the city — the real one, the rebuilt one. My mother sells cloth near the bell tower. In school they teach us the Reopening, and the Church of the Quiet, and the Door in the Glass Palace, and they teach us that the gods came back because of you. But the teachers disagree about things." She frowned. "Some say you were a hero. Some say you were a witch who broke the world and then fixed it so no one would punish you. Some say the wolf story isn't true at all and there was never a wolf, it's just a symbol."

"What do you say?"

"That's why I came," she said, as if this were obvious. "To find out. Will you tell me?"

I sat down across from her. Outside, the mist had burned away entirely and the morning lay clean and ordinary over my fields, my fence, my goats. Three years ago I couldn't have told this story sitting down. Two years ago I couldn't have told it looking at anyone's face. Now I found I could do both, the way you can carry a heavy load you've carried so long it has worn a groove into your shoulders.

"Yes," I said. "But I'm going to tell it true. All of it. Including the ugly parts. If you want a hero, the road home is long, and you should start before noon."

"I walked eleven days," she said. "I'm not afraid of ugly parts."

So I began.

I told her about a girl whose family died in a war the gods watched without lifting a finger — plague and soldiers and prayers that fell upward into silence. I told her about the grief that curdled into rage, and the rage that found a door, and the night a storm-born girl with nothing left to lose sealed that door shut with the full strength of her blood, because if the gods wouldn't answer the innocent, then they could starve in the dark with the rest of us.

The child didn't blink. "You killed a country," she said. Not accusing. Checking.

"No," I said. "Worse. I made everyone forget it existed. Averlaine didn't die — it faded. Its roads unraveled. Its people scattered and lost even the grief, because grief needs a name and I took the name away. And one family —" my voice caught, and I let it, because hiding the catch would have been another kind of lie, "— one family fled the forgetting carrying a map drawn on wolf-hide, and the Church of the Quiet hunted them for it, and by the time the map reached Vell, only one brother was left alive to carry it."

"Corren," she said. "Corren Vael. We learn his name."

"He'd hate that," I said, and smiled. "He hates being a lesson."

I told her about the exile — how I hadn't been sentenced by any court or church. How I had traded my own memories to seal the door, because such doors take payment, and I had chosen to pay in the one coin I had: knowing who I was. Ten years of numbness in a quiet valley. Ten years of goats and fences and a locked room where my life used to be. "They call me the last wolf," I said, "but the wolf was the map, really. Corren's sister drew it. She marked the storm-born sigil on its corner because the door could only be opened by someone born of storm, and she died believing someone like me would come. He arrived bleeding at the border stones and I hid him from my elders and resented him for needing it, and then the sky started following me around like a dog, and everything I'd buried started clawing its way back up."

"And you opened the door," the girl said.

"With help. Always with help. A matriarch who chose defiance over safety. An inquisitor who turned her fire on her own church. A child who spent their own memories to hold a dying country together long enough for me to reach the door — and who I promised I'd remember for, both of us, everything." I looked at my hands, ordinary hands, scarred from fence wire and lambing season. "And when I got there, and the door was paper-thin, and the god I hated most was starving on the other side of it — I didn't strike him down. I wanted to. Every part of me wanted to. Instead I gave him the only thing he'd lost. A people who remember. I tore the door open and let the gods back into a world that had learned to pray again, and the storm took my magic as payment, and I have been ordinary ever since."

Silence. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere beyond the hills, faint and impossible, a bell rang mid-morning, and the girl tilted her head to listen to it the way you listen to a voice calling from a house you grew up in.

"The teachers say the sacrifice killed you," she said at last.

"The teachers say a lot of things."

"But it didn't. You're here. Farming." She looked around my kitchen — the wool blanket, the lamb's empty basket, the chipped cups — with an expression I couldn't read until she named it herself. "You're nobody special," she said, wondering. "You're just a person."

"The best thing I ever became," I said. "Listen. Here's the part I need you to carry home, since you walked eleven days for it. I wasn't a hero and I wasn't a witch. I was a grieving girl with too much power and no one brave enough to take it from me, and I did an unforgivable thing, and then I spent years earning the right to try to fix it — not earning forgiveness, because Corren never forgave me and never had to, but choosing, with him, to act as if grace were possible whether or not it was deserved. That's the true story. The guilt doesn't wash out. I carry it the way I carry the memory of Tam's hand in mine. Same weight. Both mine."

The girl was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached into her satchel and brought out a small square of oilcloth, unwrapped it, and set it on the table between us.

A bell. Hand-sized, green bronze, old — older than it should have been, with the particular shine of something that had survived being forgotten.

"This fell off the palace door last month," she said. "The real door, the one that stays open. The wardens can't hang anything that won't stay, and they can't melt it down, because it hums. So they give pieces like this to pilgrims and tell them to bring them to places that feel right." She pushed it across the wood toward me. "This place feels right."

"Why?" I asked, though my heart had already begun to answer for itself.

"Because when I touched it, it rang," she said simply. "Once. Quiet. Like it was saying hello." She stood, slung her satchel, and paused at my door with the afternoon light behind her. "Thank you for telling it true. The ugly parts especially. My teacher says history that hides its guilt makes the same mistakes twice." A pause, and then, with the sudden shyness of a child remembering her manners: "There's a festival. Midsummer. At the border stones. Everyone from both sides comes, and the stones hum all night, and they say—" she hesitated, "—they say this year the gods are sending someone to speak at it. Someone who was at the opening. Corren Vael himself, with Tam."

The bell on my table gave the faintest ring, though neither of us had touched it.

I looked at it for a long time. Then I looked up at the girl, and at the road beyond her, running down the hill toward the valley and the stones and everything I had given away and been given back twice over.

"Tell him I'll be there," I said.
