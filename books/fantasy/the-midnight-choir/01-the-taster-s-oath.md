## 1. The Taster's Oath

The king's soup arrived at his table with the same ceremony it always did, which was to say: none at all, because nobody wanted to look at the taster while he worked.

Dominic Oyelaran had learned long ago that invisibility was not something the palace granted you. It was something you performed, spoonful by careful spoonful, until even the footmen stopped seeing you. He had been performing it for nineteen years.

He sat in the small antechamber off the royal kitchens, a room the servants called the Waiting Room and Dominic privately called the confessional, because that was what tasting felt like if you did it honestly. Not eating. Confessing. Every spoonful was him saying to God, or to whatever listened in the valley where magic couldn't reach: *here is what I know, and here is what I'm willing to lose.*

The soup was pepperpot today — goat tripe, callaloo, a heavy hand with the scotch bonnet because His Majesty believed suffering built character in everyone but himself. Dominic lifted the spoon. Swallowed. Held still.

*Scotch bonnet,* he catalogued, the way other men said their prayers. *Capsaicin burn, front of tongue, nothing underneath. Tripe boiled long enough to surrender. Callaloo cut fine. And under it all—*

He paused. Set down the spoon. Lifted it again and tasted once more, slower, letting the broth sit on his tongue like a secret he was trying to coax into speech.

There it was. Faint as a rumor. A bitterness that wasn't the greens.

*Wormwood? No.* Wormwood announced itself; this kept quiet. This was polite. Something that waited for warmth — body-warmth — before it woke up. He'd read of such things in the tasters' ledger, the great cracked tome chained to the wall in Master Ferreol's day, back when there had been a master to apprentice under. *Aconite sleeps cold. Oleander shouts. But the quiet ones, boy — the quiet ones are made, not grown. Someone distilled them on purpose.*

Dominic swallowed again, a third time, deliberately, and counted his own pulse the way Ferreol had taught him: thumb to wrist, one-two-three, breathe through the nose, don't let your face do anything interesting. Nineteen years of measured doses lived in his blood like an army of small veterans, each one recruited by surviving its own war. Nightshade, met. Hemlock, met. The gray powder from the eastern trade roads whose name Ferreol had never learned and so had simply called *the Argument*, because it settled disputes. All of them stood ready.

This new thing walked past them all like it owned the house.

His fingertips went cold first. Then a ringing started behind his ears, high and silver, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass. Dominic set both hands flat on the table — no trembling, never any trembling, the kitchen boys would talk — and breathed.

"Oyelaran!" barked Cook Amara through the pass-through, her voice carrying the particular warmth she reserved for people she refused to admit she liked. "You're making faces at my soup. My soup has never done anything to deserve faces."

"It's excellent," Dominic said, and his voice came out level, which surprised him. "Pepper's bright today."

"Bright." She sniffed. "Twenty years I've cooked for this court and the man can't say anything but *bright* and *flat* and *the king prefers less salt*, which is a lie, because the king would drink brine if you told him it was fashionable." A ladle appeared through the window, gesturing like a conductor. "Sit there and finish your portion. You look peaky."

"I'm always peaky," he said. "It's part of my charm."

"It's part of your *job*, is what it is."

He smiled at her — the smile cost him something, with the silver ringing getting louder — and finished the bowl. All of it. Because that was the oath, the whole ugly arithmetic of it: a taster who spat out the poison only proved there was poison, and a proved poison meant panic, and panic meant questions, and questions meant someone would start asking why the king needed a taster at all in a valley where magic couldn't follow and half the old families pretended poisoning was a foreign custom.

Better to be quietly ill than loudly dead. That was the first lesson. The second was that there was no third lesson; everything else was commentary.

* * *

By the time he'd climbed the service stair to the royal dining hall, the ringing had faded to a hum and his fingertips had remembered they were attached to a living man. He took his place against the wall, behind the king's left shoulder, in the spot worn smooth by nineteen years of taster boots, and watched His Majesty King Osaze the Third demolish a bowl of the soup Dominic had just risked his neck for, without a single expression of gratitude.

"You're sweating," said the king, not looking at him.

"The pepper, Majesty."

"Mm." The king tore bread. "You didn't sweat over the pepper last week."

"Last week's pepper hadn't been insulted by my face."

That got the ghost of a smile, quickly killed. The court around them glittered and murmured — dukes discussing drainage rights with the passion of men discussing war, ladies comparing falconers, the Chancellor counting something invisible behind his eyes the way he always did, coins or enemies, possibly both. Candles guttered in sconces of green valley-glass, mined from the hills that ringed the kingdom like cupped hands. Outside those hills, everyone agreed, magic ran wild as weather. Inside them, nothing. The Treaty of the Quiet Valley, signed three generations ago: no gifted souls admitted, no enchantments crossing the border stones, no exceptions. The palace wore its magiclessness like a badge of virtue, though Dominic had noticed the virtue mostly benefited whoever could afford locks.

He loved this place. That was the shameful truth of it, the thing he admitted to no one, maybe not even himself on most days. He loved the kitchen's dawn-heat and Amara's terrible singing, loved the library's dust-smell and the way the corridors knew his footsteps, loved being necessary in a way that required no explanation. Nineteen years ago the crown had paid his apprenticeship debt to Ferreol, had fed him and housed him and trained his tongue like a racehorse's legs. The debt had been enormous. His parents had been weavers with more children than looms. Adaeze —

He put the thought away, the way he did every time, folding it small and sliding it into the drawer where he kept everything that hurt too much to hold. Adaeze had died of fever when he was nine. The palace had sent flowers. He'd been grateful for the flowers for years before he understood how cheap flowers were.

"Eat, Oyelaran," the king said suddenly, pushing the bread basket toward the wall without looking at it. "You hover like a crow."

"I'm not permitted, Majesty. You know the rule."

"The rule says you taste. It doesn't say you starve." But the king's hand had already moved on, tearing more bread for himself, and Dominic understood the gesture for what it was: not kindness. Inventory. The king liked knowing his possessions were in good condition.

Dominic bowed slightly anyway. In the palace, you accepted crumbs of regard the way you accepted wages — gratefully, and without examining the source.

* * *

The hum in his ears lasted two days.

He rode it out in his narrow room above the mews, drinking barley water and cataloguing symptoms in the little ciphered notebook he'd kept since Ferreol's death: *Day one, cold extremities, auditory disturbance, no nausea — interesting, most distillates announce themselves downward. Day two, faint metallic taste persisting, vision clear, pulse steady.* If it were going to kill him, it would have done it by now. His blood's garrison of survived poisons had met the intruder somewhere in the dark and ground it down, barely, the way a city survives a siege it will never speak of.

Which raised the question he'd been avoiding since the second spoonful.

Nobody grew that toxin here. It wasn't in the gardens, wasn't in the stores, wasn't in any ledger Ferreol had ever written. It came from outside the valley — from the trade roads, from the mountains, from places the treaty said nothing magical ever came from. Which meant someone had carried it in, past the wardens at the border stones, past the customs houses, past every checkpoint that existed specifically to keep such things out.

Someone with access. Someone with authority. Someone who had walked it through the front gate and handed it, directly or otherwise, into the king's kitchen.

And they hadn't put it in the king's bowl by accident. Dominic had re-tasted the recipe a dozen times in memory. The dose was calibrated — not enough to fell a healthy man outright, but precisely, *exquisitely* matched to overwhelm a body already burdened with nineteen years of accumulated toxins. A normal courtier would have gotten a headache. Ferreol would have called it a warning shot. For Dominic's particular blood, it had been a siege.

Someone had studied him. Someone had mixed that bowl the way an apothecary mixes medicine — *for the patient*.

He sat by his window on the second evening, watching the sun go down behind the western hills, and let the thought finally arrive in full: *someone in this palace wants me dead, and wants it done quietly, and knows exactly what I am.*

The strange part — the part he turned over and over like a stone found in a loaf — was that his first feeling wasn't fear. It was hurt. A small, stupid, childish hurt, the kind you'd feel if a friend's dog bit you. He had given this place his tongue, his stomach, his nineteen years of invisible service. He had swallowed its poisons like prayers. And somebody inside its walls, somebody who ate Amara's bread and laughed in its corridors, had looked at all that and decided it weighed less than whatever they wanted.

Below, the kitchens were lighting their lamps. He could hear Amara singing badly, gloriously, some old harvest song about a girl who married the moon. The sound rose up the mews wall and wrapped around him, warm as the ovens themselves, and Dominic found his throat tightening around it.

He loved the palace that would erase him. That was the whole confession, wasn't it? That was the prayer beneath all the others.

The knock came at midnight, soft, three times — a rhythm he didn't know.

He opened the door on a serving girl he'd never seen before, holding a folded letter sealed with plain black wax. No crest. No words on the outside except his name, spelled correctly, which in the palace counted as intimacy.

"From the under-steward," she said, and was gone before he could ask which under-steward, because the palace employed eleven of them and only one had ever spoken to Dominic in his life.

He broke the seal by candlelight. The handwriting was cramped, hurried, unfamiliar:

*Taster — you are not the only one who noticed the soup. Come to the old choir loft above the chapel tomorrow after compline. Ask for the woman who sang doors. Burn this.*

Dominic read it twice. *The woman who sang doors.* There hadn't been a choir in the palace chapel in twenty years, not since the purge — everyone knew that story in the shape of a rumor, the way you know a scar's history without having been there. And yet the letter knew about the soup. Knew it had been aimed. Knew enough to be afraid of the same thing he was afraid of.

Dominic held the letter over the candle flame and watched it curl into black lace. Then he sat back down at his little table, beside his notebook of survived wars, and pressed his thumb against his wrist where the pulse beat steady and stubborn.

Nineteen years of keeping his head down. Nineteen years of being furniture, of being invisible, of swallowing everything — poisons, insults, griefs — and saying nothing.

Tomorrow he would break the first rule of his survival, the rule older than the oath itself: *never ask questions you can't unknow.*

Because the letter hadn't asked him to come.

It had assumed he would. And the terrible, thrilling truth settling into his chest like a swallowed coal was that whoever wrote it had guessed right.
