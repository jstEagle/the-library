## 2. The Proclamation

The herald came on a Tuesday, which Dominic thought was a coward's choice of day. Tuesdays were soup days. Nobody in the palace paid attention to anything but soup on a Tuesday, and so the proclamation was nailed to the great oak doors while three hundred mouths were busy deciding whether the cook had finally overdone the saffron.

Dominic heard about it from a scullery boy named Femi, who skidded into the tasting chamber with his cap in his fists and his eyes enormous.

"Master Oyelaran. Master Oyelaran, they're saying—there's a paper on the doors, they're saying the crown wants—" He swallowed. "Gifted folk. Anyone with magic in them. There's a reward for naming them."

"Is there," said Dominic, and went back to his ledger.

He kept a ledger the way other men kept prayer books. *Copperleaf, faint, in the king's morning broth — antidote: honeyed milk. Widow's kiss, moderate, in the duchess's wine — antidote: unnecessary, she deserves it.* (He never wrote that last part down, but he thought it, and thinking it was its own small sacrament.) Twelve years he had catalogued poisons like psalms, swallowing each measured dose until his blood knew them the way a choir knows its parts. The valley made this possible. The valley was magic-dead, warded at every border by old spells no one living remembered casting, and inside it nothing enchanted could touch him. No curse, no hex, no gifted blade. Only honest poison, which he could out-drink.

It was, he had long ago decided, the safest life in the world. It was also, he admitted on bad nights, the smallest.

"How much is the reward?" he asked Femi.

"Ten crowns." The boy's voice dropped to a whisper, as if the number itself might inform on him. "Master, my aunt boils charms into her stew. Just little ones. To keep the weevils out."

"Then your aunt should stop boiling charms into her stew," Dominic said, "and start charging the weevils rent like everyone else."

Femi laughed, a little too loudly, the way people laugh when they are frightened, and ran off.

Dominic sat with his ledger open and did not write anything for a long time.

* * *

By evening the palace had that particular hush it got when everyone was calculating. He felt it in the kitchens, where the under-cooks stood too close together and stopped talking when he passed. He felt it in the corridor outside the tasting chamber, where Lady Veshi's maid looked at him one heartbeat too long. He was not gifted. He had spent twelve years making certain of it — a man who drank poison for wages had no room left in his body for anything supernatural; there wasn't the space. But the proclamation did not ask for proof. It asked for names, and names were cheaper than proof, and ten crowns bought a lot of bread in a hard winter.

His neighbor across the courtyard, old Mama Ekwueme, had already been visited. He saw the wardens' cart outside her door at dusk, saw her standing in her own doorway with her arms folded, arguing with two men in grey about whether the lights she set in her window at festival-time were "invocations" or simply candles. They were candles. Everyone knew they were candles. But the wardens wrote something in a book anyway, and Mama Ekwueme watched them write it with the expression of a woman memorizing faces for later.

That was the thing about hunts, Dominic thought, climbing the narrow stair to his rooms above the tannery lane. They didn't catch the guilty first. They caught the visible. And then everyone else learned to be invisible, and invisibility, he could have told the crown for free, was a terrible way to live. He had been practicing it professionally for twelve years and all it had earned him was a warm bed, a full belly, and the growing suspicion that if he died tomorrow, the palace would need less than a day to find another mouth.

His rooms smelled of dust and dried herbs. He lit the lamp, put water on for tea, and went looking for his good penknife because the lamp needed trimming — and that was how he found the loose floorboard.

He had lived here eleven years. He had never once pried up that board. But the proclamation had made the whole city jumpy, and jumpy men checked things, and the board came up easier than it should have, as if it had been waiting.

Inside was an oilcloth packet, tied with string gone brittle as pastry. His mother's handwriting on the top letter. Her handwriting. Eleven years dead, and her handwriting, addressed to him, unopened.

He sat down on the floor because his legs suggested it would be wise.

There were seven letters. Six were ordinary — complaints about his father's stubbornness, recipes for pepper soup, a running argument about whether Dominic, aged nine, had been "willful" or "possessed of convictions," which his mother had apparently considered an important legal distinction. He read them with his throat tight and his tea going cold, laughing once, badly, at her description of the neighbor's goat eating the festival banner.

The seventh letter was different. Older. The ink had faded to brown, and the hand shook.

*My son,* it began, and then stopped, and started again lower on the page, as though she had walked away from it and come back.

*They have come about the debt. The master-taster's fee, and the apprenticeship bond, and the interest, which I did not understand when I signed and understand now only too well. There is no money in this house. You know there is no money in this house.*

*But the court beneath the mountain pays in gold for souls, and they will take Adaeze's against what we owe. She is young. They say the young ones keep. They say she will serve in the halls below and it is honorable and I want to spit on them for saying so.*

*I am telling you this because you will find it someday, and I cannot bear for you to think she wandered off, or that I did not fight. I fought. God knows I fought, and lost, and signed anyway, because the alternative was debtor's prison for us both and you a beggar on the road. I chose her soul over your body and I do not know if that was love or arithmetic and I will die not knowing.*

*Do not go beneath the mountain, Dominic. Whatever you learn, whatever you become — do not go. The mountain keeps what it is given. That is the whole of their law and the whole of their mercy.*

*Your mother, who loved you both and saved the wrong one.*

Dominic read it four times. On the fourth reading he noticed his own hands were steady, which seemed obscene. Twelve years of training poisons had taught his body not to shake, and now it wouldn't even shake for this.

Adaeze. Adaeze, who used to braid flowers into his hair when he slept and swear the garden fairies did it. Adaeze, who was fourteen the autumn she "went to stay with cousins in the river country," and whose letters home had stopped after two, and whose absence his parents had wrapped in silence so thick he had eventually stopped asking. He had been eleven. He had grieved her the way children grieve — briefly, completely, and then not at all, because the world kept serving meals.

And she hadn't wandered. She hadn't died of fever, as he'd half-believed by fifteen. She had been *sold*. Priced against his apprenticeship, weighed against his future, and handed down into the dark beneath the mountain to pay for the life he was currently wasting on soup.

The lamp guttered. Outside, somewhere down the lane, a dog was barking at the wardens' cart, or at nothing, or at everything.

Dominic sat on his cold floor among his mother's letters and waited for the guilt to arrive. It did not come as a wave. It came as a detonation — silent, total, rearranging every wall of the building he had made of his life. Twelve years of careful, invisible, well-fed service, and the bill for it had been paid by a girl with flowers in her brother's hair. Every safe meal he had ever tasted had cost her. The mountain had eaten her in installments and called it *honorable*, and his mother had signed, and he had eaten the king's soup and called it *living*.

He thought: *I could do nothing.* The thought arrived wearing his own voice, reasonable, tired, practiced. *You are one man. The mountain keeps what it is given. Your mother told you not to go. The solstice feast is six weeks off and the road is hunted now and you are nobody.*

Then he thought: *But hiding is finished anyway.*

Because that was the truth sitting underneath the proclamation like a stone under snow. The hunt had ended the age of keeping your head down. Neighbors informing on neighbors; wardens writing names in books; ten crowns turning love into arithmetic in every household in the valley. A man could not be invisible in a country that had started checking shadows. Sooner or later someone would look at the food-taster who never sickened, never scarred, never flinched — who drank widow's kiss like morning tea — and wonder what exactly he was trading for his safety, and to whom.

The hunt had made hiding meaningless. Which meant the only question left was what a man did with the time between meaningless and caught.

Dominic gathered the letters, all seven, and put them inside his shirt, against his skin, where important things belonged. Then he took down the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, doing sums he already knew the answer to.

Six weeks to the solstice. One disgraced choir-mistress living in exile beyond the tanner's row, if the stories were true — a woman who had once sung the dead across thresholds, before she sang one back too far. They said she still had her voice. They said she still had her debts.

So did he.

He put on his coat, blew out the lamp entirely, and went down the stairs into the hunted night, toward the one person in the valley who might know the way beneath the mountain — and the price of the road there, which he suspected he already carried in his own blood.
