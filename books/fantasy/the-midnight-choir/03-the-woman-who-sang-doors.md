## 3. The Woman Who Sang Doors

Tanner's row smelled like everything the palace pretended didn't exist. Dominic had spent eleven years among marble and beeswax, breathing air that had been perfumed on purpose, and he'd forgotten — or let himself forget — that the valley's wealth began here, in streets where the work was honest and the stink was free.

The tanners themselves were asleep. Their dogs were not. Two of them announced him from behind a fence with the full conviction of animals who believed they were saving the world, and a third, older, grey about the muzzle, simply watched him pass with the weary expression of a creature who had long ago concluded that nothing worth barking at ever came down this road.

The exile's house stood where the row gave up and became marsh. It had been a smokehouse once. Now it leaned like a tired man against a wall that wasn't there, its roof patched with three different kinds of metal, its windows glowing faintly with a light that was wrong for candles — too steady, too green.

Dominic knocked.

Nothing. He knocked again, and from inside came a voice, low and unhurried, the voice of someone who had been expecting worse than him.

"If you're from the wardens, come around to the back where I can see your hands."

"I'm from nowhere," Dominic said. "I'm the king's taster."

A silence. Then, dry as old parchment: "Then you're from somewhere, aren't you. The worst somewhere there is." A bolt scraped back. The door opened on a chain, then all the way, and Nneka looked out at him.

She was smaller than her reputation. Stories made her a giantess who had pulled a boy out of death by the ankles; the woman in the doorway was thin, sixty-odd, wrapped in a man's coat far too big for her, with silver threaded through her braids and eyes that took his measure the way he took the measure of soup — one slow swallow at a time.

"The taster," she said. "Come in before the marsh hears you. It gossips."

Inside, the green light came from jars. Rows of them along every wall, each holding a small pale glow, like fireflies that had agreed to behave. She caught him looking.

"Threshold-lights," she said. "Souls in transit used to rest in them. Before." She said *before* the way other people said *the war*, or *my knees*. "Sit. Don't touch anything. Some of those jars still have opinions."

He sat on a stool by the table. She poured something into two clay cups without asking what he wanted, which he respected immediately.

"You've come about Adaeze," she said.

His cup stopped halfway to his mouth. "How—"

"Nobody walks past my dogs at midnight unless they're desperate, nobody desperate comes to me unless it's about the dead, and only one name has been whispered over the valley walls loud enough lately to reach even the marsh." She drank. Made a face. Drank more. "Your sister. Sold under the mountain. Solstice feast. Yes?"

"Yes."

"And you want her back."

"I want her *out*," Dominic said. "I don't know yet if back is possible. I read enough of my mother's letters to know the court doesn't return what it eats. But souls are recorded. Ledgers. Names. If a soul still has its name written somewhere, maybe it isn't fully swallowed. Maybe there's a seam."

Nneka set down her cup and studied him for a long moment. "You talk like an accountant."

"I *am* an accountant, mostly. Poison is just accounting with consequences."

That surprised a laugh out of her — short, rusty, like a door opening after winter. "All right, accountant. Here's your first lesson, free of charge: the mountain doesn't keep ledgers of names. It keeps ledgers of *songs*. Every soul sold beneath has a threshold-song, and whoever holds the song holds the soul's address. I could sing your sister's door open from here if I had her song. I don't. So we go in. And going in—" she tapped his chest once, with one finger, "—is where you get interesting."

"The breath," Dominic said. "The tunnels breathe poison. That's why the court moved under there in the first place. Everyone knows no living body crosses the deep halls."

"Everyone knows," Nneka agreed. "Because everyone who tried died beautifully. So either you're the stupidest man in this valley or—" and here her eyes narrowed, doing their accounting, "—you're the one body in the valley built to survive it."

"Eleven years," Dominic said quietly. "Measured doses. Belladonna, arsenic, hemlock, things without names in the market tongue. My teachers called it building armor. I'm starting to think they were building something else, but armor is what I have, and I'm offering it. My blood will carry us through halls that kill anyone else. In exchange, you teach me the threshold songs. Not to sing alone — I'd butcher them — but well enough to hold a note when you need a second voice. You said it yourself: songs need bodies. Choirs need mouths."

"You want to be in my choir."

"I want to be *useful* in your choir. There's a difference, and it's the difference between a soloist and a man who knows when to shut up."

Nneka got up and walked slowly along her jars, trailing one finger above them without touching, the way you'd skim water. "Do you know why I was exiled?" she asked, without turning.

"They say you sang a dead boy back too far."

"They say correctly." Her hand paused over one jar — emptier than the others, its light gone grey. "Lord Ezenwa's son. Drowned. They brought me the body while the grief was still warm, and grief makes patrons generous and singers careless. I sang him across the threshold, and then — because they paid, because they wept so *beautifully* — I sang him one verse further. And he came back. Mostly. The part that came back remembered how to eat."

Dominic said nothing. The dogs outside had settled; the marsh ticked softly against the house's foundations.

"He lasted nine days," Nneka went on. "On the ninth day Lord Ezenwa understood what he had bought, and instead of thanking me for the warning, he thanked me for the crime, because a crime could be punished and a miracle couldn't. Exile. Stripped of choir rank. My name struck from the rolls." She turned. "You know what an oath is, taster? An oath is a song you're not allowed to stop singing. I broke mine. What did you break?"

"I never broke mine. I kept it perfectly. That's what broke." He heard his own voice go flat and careful, the way it did over a suspicious dish. "Eleven years invisible. Eleven years swallowing the court's sins so the king could eat in peace, telling myself I was protecting the valley, protecting my mother, keeping my head down, keeping *safe*. And the whole time the safety was purchased. With my sister. I was the payment, Nneka. They built my immunity the way you fatten a goose — not to save me. To *prepare* me. Whatever they intend at that feast, I'm not the guest. I'm the ingredient."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Outside, the marsh sighed.

Then Nneka crossed the room, took his cup, refilled it, pressed it back into his hands.

"To broken oaths," she said. "Mine snapped. Yours rotted. Between the two of us we might have enough honest wood left to build a ladder."

They drank. The drink was appalling. It tasted like it had been poisoned on purpose, which, coming from her, might have been hospitality.

"Terms," she said, businesslike now, sitting across from him. "First: you learn the threshold songs, and you learn them properly. No humming. If your voice cracks at the wrong verse beneath that mountain, what comes through the crack won't be your sister. Second: when we find the ledger — the song-ledger — we take copies. All of them. Every stolen name. Not just hers."

"Agreed. Every name."

"Third." She hesitated, and for the first time since he'd arrived, she looked old. "If the feast takes you — if your immunity fails, if they pour something in you that your blood has never met — I finish the work without you. You swear it. Your sister gets out even if you don't."

Dominic thought about the soup, six weeks ago, the new toxin that had nearly dropped him in front of the whole court. Someone had already begun testing the limits of him. The final course was being rehearsed.

"I swear it," he said.

"Then we leave before dawn, before the hunt notices the marsh missing a ghost. Sleep in the chair. If the jars sing to you in the night, don't answer. They lie when they're lonely."

He slept badly, and woke once, deep in the dark, certain he heard voices — many voices, very soft, layered like a choir warming up somewhere under the floor. He lay still and didn't answer, and gradually the sound resolved into wind through jar-necks, nothing more. Probably.

When he rose, Nneka was already at the door with a bundle on her back and a walking stick in her hand, and the grey dog from the fence was sitting beside her like a soldier reporting for duty.

"He follows corpses," she explained. "It's a professional courtesy."

She opened the door onto a sky just beginning to bruise toward morning, and paused there, looking out over the sleeping valley — the roofs, the bell-tower, the far pale line of the palace where Dominic had given his life one measured spoonful at a time.

"They'll call us traitors by noon," she said. "Hunted by sundown. Are you ready to be erased, taster?"

"I've been erased for eleven years," Dominic said. "I'd like to try existing for a change."

Nneka smiled — a real one, brief and crooked — and stepped out into the dark, and the dog fell in behind them both, and together the three of them walked toward the edge of the only world Dominic had ever known, beyond which waited the mountain, the ledger, and whatever remained of his sister's name.

Behind them, in the dark little house, one empty jar began — very faintly, very patiently — to glow.
