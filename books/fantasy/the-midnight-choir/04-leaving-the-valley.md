## 4. Leaving the Valley

Nneka had crossed thresholds her whole life, and she had learned this much: the door is never the hard part. The hard part is the moment your foot is in both worlds at once, when you belong to neither, and something old and patient asks you—in a voice like your mother's, like your own—to choose.

The valley's edge was not a wall. It was a line of white stones half-swallowed by grass, running crooked across the hills like a stitch someone had started and abandoned. Beyond it, the land turned the color of bruised plums, and the air itself seemed to lean toward you, listening.

Dominic stood on the near side of that line with his hands in his coat pockets, looking for all the world like a man deciding whether to cross a street.

"You're stalling," Nneka said.

"I'm *appreciating*," he corrected. "There's a difference. Stalling has no dignity. Appreciation does."

She let him have his dignity. She understood what the valley was to people like him—the magic-dead ground where a taster's body could be its own quiet miracle, where poisons behaved, where nothing sang in the walls or whispered through keyholes. She had lived inside that silence for six years herself, ever since they took her voice's name away and told her she could keep the rest. The silence was a kind of mercy. It was also a kind of cage, and the worst cages are the ones you decorate.

He crouched and touched one of the white stones, the way you'd touch a sleeping dog you weren't sure liked you.

"Will it hurt?" he asked.

"Yes," Nneka said. "But not the way you think. Magic isn't going to attack you. It's going to *arrive*. All of it. Every drop of it you've been starved of since birth, coming home at once." She paused. "You've been drinking poison in small doses for years, yes? Building tolerance?"

"Measured doses. Like prayers."

"Well. Imagine every prayer you never said arriving in a single breath."

Dominic looked at the plum-colored hills beyond the stones. Then back at the palace spires behind them, small and pale in the morning haze, the place that had fed him and named him invisible and sold his sister without telling him. He looked a long time.

"I used to think I loved it here," he said quietly.

"You did," Nneka said. "That's the cruelest part."

He stepped over the stones.

* * *

It happened fast, and then it happened forever—that was how crossing always felt. One heartbeat Dominic was walking beside her, ordinary as bread; the next he made a sound like a man pulled under water and went down into the grass.

Nneka knelt over him and did the only thing she knew: she hummed, low and steady, an anchor-note, the first phrase of the threshold song. His body arched. His eyes were open but seeing something else entirely—some interior country where everything he'd been denied was being repaid at once, with interest.

Magic poured into him like a river into a dry well. She could see it happening, because she had learned to see: the faint shimmer rising off his skin like heat-haze, the way the grass around him leaned toward him as if he'd suddenly become interesting after years of being furniture. A man who had spent his whole life dead to wonder was now drowning in it, and wonder does not check whether you can swim.

"It's all right," she murmured, though it wasn't, quite. "Breathe when it lets you. Fight nothing. Let it fill you up."

His lips moved. At first she thought he was praying. Then she realized he was cataloguing—the taster's habit, the litany he used against fear. *Bitter almond. Foxglove. The gray one from the eastern jars.* Naming poisons like a man naming constellations to stay sane in open water.

After a long while—he would later claim minutes; she would swear it was most of an hour—the shimmer settled into his skin and he lay still, breathing hard, blinking up at a sky that must have looked different to him now. Richer. Louder.

"Well?" she said.

"It tastes," he said hoarsely, "like everything I ever ate, all arguing at once."

"That's normal."

"And there's a bird somewhere singing in a language I'm fairly sure is rude."

"That's also normal. Can you stand?"

He stood. He swayed. He stood again, properly, and Nneka watched him take inventory of himself the way he took inventory of a table set for the king: quick, thorough, unsentimental. But she saw the thing underneath, the thing he tried to fold neatly away. His face, for just a moment, was the face of a man hearing music after decades deaf—and grieving, already, all the songs he'd missed.

"We should move," she said gently. "Your immunity won't last. Out here, past the stones, the mountain's breath reaches further than people think, and every league we walk, the tolerance burns off a little more. Your body is spending thirty years of careful dying to keep you alive. Spend it wisely."

"Wisely," he repeated. "I'll add it to my list of things I'm bad at."

They walked.

* * *

By late afternoon the road bent down through olive terraces toward the river town of Ashford's Ferry, and that was where they heard the bells—not the good bells, the ones that called people to weddings and market, but the short, urgent triple-strike that Nneka hadn't heard since before her exile.

"Hunter-wardens," she said. "Someone reported travelers. Someone always reports travelers now."

Dominic's hand found hers without either of them deciding it should. His grip was cold. "How far?"

"Close. And we're the only two people on this road who look like trouble." She scanned the bend ahead, where the road dropped between stone walls toward the ferry landing. No cover. No trees worth the name. Just the road, and the town below, and—

Music. Faint. Drums, and a slow keening voice, and the particular shuffling rhythm of many feet moving without hurry.

A funeral procession was coming up the road from the town, bearing a bier draped in indigo cloth, a dozen mourners in white ash and wrapped cotton, walking the dead up to the burial grounds on the ridge. Old custom, older than the wardens, older than the crown's memory. Nobody stopped a funeral. Even hunter-wardens, new as they were to their cruelty, hadn't yet worked up the nerve to put their hands on a bier.

Nneka looked at Dominic. Dominic looked at Nneka.

"No," he said.

"I haven't said anything."

"Your face said it. Your face is currently composing a very persuasive argument."

"My face," Nneka said, "has been composing persuasive arguments since before you were born. Now listen. We walk among the mourners. Heads bowed. You sing or you don't—you have a decent ear, I've heard worse—but whatever happens, you do not stop walking, and you do not look at the wardens. Grief gives us a kind of invisibility. Use it."

"Grief as camouflage." He exhaled through his nose. "My sister would find that funny. She found everything about hiding funny. Said I was built like a man designed to be overlooked."

"She sounds wise."

"She sounds *dead*," Dominic said, and then pressed his knuckles briefly to his mouth, as if the words had cut him on the way out. "Forgive me. That's the grief talking. Or the poison leaving. They sound similar lately."

They joined the procession.

It was easier than it had any right to be, which is the terrible truth about funerals: sorrow makes room. The mourners took them in without question—one glance at Nneka's face, at Dominic's bowed head, and they simply shifted, making space, the way mourners do, because grief recognizes grief and doesn't ask for papers. An old woman pressed a pinch of ash into Dominic's palm. "For your dead," she whispered.

"For my dead," he whispered back, and Nneka saw his throat work, and saw him mean it.

Then the wardens came up the road.

Three of them, in the king's gray-and-gold, boots loud on the stones, halberds held with the self-conscious aggression of men enjoying their authority. The lead warden raised a hand and the procession slowed. The keening faltered.

Nneka felt Dominic tense beside her—a wire pulled tight—and began, softly, to sing. Not the threshold song. Something older and simpler, a mourning verse every soul in that province had absorbed with their mother's milk, the song sung when the dead cannot speak for themselves. Her voice, stripped of its court training, came out rough as riverstone. It didn't matter. The song wasn't about beauty. It was about weight.

The mourners picked it up. The drums deepened. And Dominic—bless him, bless his careful, cowardly, courageous heart—joined in, his voice low and cracked and true, singing a dead woman's name he'd never known, meaning another dead woman entirely.

The lead warden looked at the bier. Looked at the mourners. Looked at Nneka, who met his eyes over the indigo cloth and gave him the flat, ancient stare of a woman who has buried better men than him.

He stepped aside.

The procession flowed past the wardens like water past rocks, and no one stopped walking, and no one looked back until the road crested the ridge and the burial ground opened before them, terraced and quiet, the evening wind coming up off the river carrying the smell of rain.

* * *

They parted from the mourners at the graves with thanks and small coins, and the old woman with the ash caught Nneka's sleeve.

"You sang," she said. "Not like a mourner sings. Like a *door* sings." Her eyes, milky with age, were suddenly sharp. "I know what they say about you, choir-mistress. The woman who sang too far. My granddaughter's boy has the gift—small gift, a candle-flame of it, lighting things without touching them. He's eleven. The neighbors have been asking questions." She pressed a folded scrap of paper into Nneka's hand. An address, in a market town three days east. "If your road goes that way. If you have room in your grief for one more."

She hobbled off to her family before Nneka could answer.

Nneka stood a while among the graves, the paper in her fist, the wind pulling at her clothes. Behind her, Dominic was helping the mourners lower the bier—of course he was, the man couldn't walk past labor without joining it—and when he came back up the slope, ash still dusting his sleeves, she held out the paper without a word.

He read it. Read it again.

"Three days east," he said. "Adaeze's feast is in nine."

"I know the arithmetic."

"Every kindness costs us time." He said it evenly, testing the sentence, weighing it like a coin he suspected was false. "And if we don't arrive in time—" He didn't finish. He handed the paper back. "We take the child."

Nneka blinked. "You didn't even hesitate."

"Oh, I hesitated," Dominic said. "For about four full seconds, which for me is practically debauchery. But I spent twenty years swallowing other people's sins and calling it service. I'm not starting my new life by walking past an eleven-year-old with a candle-flame gift while the king's dogs hunt him." He looked east, toward the plum-dark hills and whatever waited beneath the mountain. "Besides. Adaeze would haunt me. Properly. With commentary."

Nneka laughed despite herself—a short, surprised bark—and folded the paper into her bodice. Below them, the mourners were filing down the hill toward the lights of Ashford's Ferry, and above them the first stars were arriving, sharp and unhidden, the way stars only look outside the valley.

"Come on, then," she said. "East. We'll need supplies, a story, and a miracle, in roughly that order."

They started down together—and that was when Dominic stopped mid-stride, one hand flying to his chest, and staggered against a grave-marker.

"Nneka." His voice had gone strange. "The poisons. They're—they're *singing*. Every one I ever swallowed. I can hear them, like names in a ledger, like—" He looked up at her, and his eyes were wide with something that was not quite fear. "They remember where they came from. Nneka, they remember *who made them*."

The wind came off the river, and somewhere far to the east, beneath the mountain, something ancient turned over in its sleep and listened.
