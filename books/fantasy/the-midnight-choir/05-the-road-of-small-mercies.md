## 5. The Road of Small Mercies

*Nneka*

The road east of the valley smelled like rain on hot stone, which Nneka had missed so fiercely during her exile that she stopped twice just to breathe it. Dominic thought she was checking for pursuers. She let him think that. A man who had spent his life swallowing poison in silence deserved a few merciful illusions.

They walked by day and slept in ditches by night, because hunter-wardens traveled by day too, in their gray coats with the crown's open eye stitched on the shoulder. Nneka had been hunted before—once, briefly, after the business with the nobleman's son—and she remembered the particular posture of it: shoulders up around the ears, hands busy with nothing, always listening to your own footsteps as though they might confess.

Dominic listened to his footsteps like a professional. Nneka found it endearing and slightly alarming.

"Your lead is gone," she told him on the third morning, when they crested a hill and saw the wardens' campfires still burning behind them, two valleys back but gaining. "The solstice is nine days off. The mountain is six days' walk if we push."

"Then we push."

"We pushed yesterday," she said. "You vomited behind a shrine."

"That was altitude."

"It was a shrine to Oshun, and she took offense." Nneka shaded her eyes and looked down at the road ahead, where something small was moving through the barley. Not an animal. Animals moved in straight lines when frightened. This thing zigzagged, doubled back, hid, darted—the movement of a creature that had learned the world was made of eyes. "Well," she said softly. "Look at that."

"What?"

"A child," said Nneka, already descending the hill, "who runs like I used to run."

* * *

*Dominic*

The child was perhaps ten years old, hollow-cheeked, barefoot, and holding a kitchen knife with the confidence of someone who had recently discovered that sharp things were a form of speech.

"Stay back," the child said. "I'm gifted. I'll do something."

"Do something small first," Nneka suggested, crouching to be less of a wall and more of a person. "So we know what we're dealing with."

The child's brow furrowed in concentration. A nearby stone lifted two inches off the road, wobbled with tremendous effort, and dropped.

"Lifting," said Dominic.

"Heavy lifting," the child corrected, with dignity.

"What's your name?"

A long, suspicious pause. "Kito."

"Just Kito?"

"Just Kito until I trust you." The knife did not lower. "The woman at the well said gifted children get taken to the mountain. She said there's bread there. She was smiling when she said it, so I ran." The child's voice cracked on *ran*, and something in Dominic's chest folded neatly in half. He knew that smile. He'd worn it himself for twenty years, serving soup to a king, being thanked for his service, being called family by people who kept ledgers.

He looked at Nneka. Nneka looked back with the expression of a woman who had already decided and was simply waiting for him to catch up.

"We can't," he said quietly.

"We can't afford not to," she answered, equally quiet. "That child will be caught within a day. And you saw how it moves—it survived three weeks alone. Gifted children are exactly what the feast wants, Dominic. We leave it here, we're carrying the mountain its next course."

"The solstice is nine days away. Adaeze is—"

"Adaeze has waited eleven years." Nneka's voice didn't harden; that was the terrible thing about her, the way truth came out of her warm as fresh bread. "Nine days more won't kill her. A warden's net would kill this one by tomorrow."

Dominic crouched beside Nneka, at knife-distance from the child. "Kito. We're going somewhere dangerous. If you come with us, you do what we say, when we say it, even when it makes no sense. Especially then. Agreed?"

The stone rose again—higher this time, a full foot, quivering. It was, Dominic understood, the child's version of a handshake.

"Agreed," said Kito.

* * *

*Nneka*

They lost their lead entirely within two days, which surprised no one except Dominic, who had apparently believed that adding a small loud child to a fugitive party might somehow make them faster.

Nneka liked Kito enormously. The child asked questions the way other people threw stones—rapidly, at everything, with no regard for consequences. Why did the valley have no magic. Why did Mr. Taster-Man smell like a medicine cabinet. Why did Nneka sing to the fire every night, and did the fire ever answer, and could it teach the stones.

"The fire does not answer," Nneka said.

"It hums," Kito insisted. "Listen."

And Nneka, who had spent forty years listening, listened, and heard what she had been pretending not to hear since they crossed the valley's edge: the fire humming back at the child. Faintly. Willingly. Magic flooding back into the world outside the valley, and finding, in Kito, a ready vessel.

She said nothing about it. Some knowledge was a burden best carried alone until it became useful or unbearable, whichever came first.

On the fifth night, sheltering in the ribs of a burned-out waystation, Nneka woke to find Kito sitting bolt upright, staring into the dark.

"There are men in the trees," the child whispered.

Nneka was moving before the whisper finished. She shook Dominic, who woke the way tasters wake—completely, silently, one hand already searching for a weapon he didn't own. She pointed. In the moonlight, shapes detached themselves from the treeline: four wardens, gray coats, the open eye catching silver.

"They've been following us since the barley field," Nneka breathed. "Not the child. Us. They let us pick the child up."

"Why?" Dominic whispered.

"Because a choir-mistress and the king's own taster running east together toward the mountain is worth more than one small stone-lifter." She was already pulling Kito behind the fallen beam. "They want to know who sent us. Or they want us alive for the feast. Either way—"

The first arrow took the doorframe above her head.

What followed was not, Nneka would later insist, a battle. It was a disagreement conducted at volume. Dominic hurled the contents of his pack—dried fish, a copper pot, a vial of something that made two wardens drop their swords and claw at their own faces, which explained a great deal about his profession. Kito lifted a boulder the size of a goat and dropped it with a shriek of effort on the third warden's foot. The fourth warden grabbed Nneka, and Nneka sang one note—a single low note, the note that unlatches things—and the man's grip opened like a flower at dusk, and he sat down on the ground looking deeply confused about his entire life.

Then they ran.

* * *

*Dominic*

They ran until dawn, until Kito's feet bled and Nneka's breath whistled, until the wardens' calls faded behind them into the ordinary noise of morning birds. They collapsed in a dry streambed under a curtain of wild fig, and Dominic lay staring at the leaves and doing arithmetic that felt like swallowing glass.

Five days to the solstice. Four days to the mountain, if nothing else went wrong. Nothing else would stay un-wronged; he knew that now the way he knew poisons, by the shape of the ache.

"I slowed us down," Kito said. Small voice. Small body curled against Nneka's side like the child had decided, unilaterally, that she was furniture now.

"You saved our lives last night," Dominic said.

"I slowed you down anyway."

"Yes," Dominic said, because lying to children was how kingdoms got built. "You slowed us down. And we're still going. Both things are true, and you'll spend your whole life learning to hold both at once. That's what being grown-up is. Nobody tells you."

Kito considered this. "Mr. Taster-Man, why do they call you that? You have a name."

"Dominic."

"Dominic." The child tried it out, chewed it. "My mother said names are doors. She said if you know somebody's true name you can call them home from anywhere." A pause. "Is that true? Is that a gift?"

Dominic's whole body went cold and quiet.

Beside him, Nneka had stopped breathing.

"Where did your mother learn that?" Nneka asked, very gently, the way you approach a bird you don't want to startle.

"She sang for the palace once. Before the mountain court bought her contract." Kito shrugged, the terrible shrug of children describing enormous things. "She said the mountain keeps a ledger. She said every soul sold there gets written down, and whoever holds the ledger holds the name, and whoever holds the name—" The child frowned, reaching for it. "—holds the door."

*Nneka's hand found Dominic's wrist and gripped it: hold still, say nothing.*

"Your mother," Nneka said, "what was her name?"

"Sela." Kito's chin lifted, suddenly fierce. "Sela of the Threshold Choir. She said I shouldn't tell anyone, because the people who took her would come for me too. But you're going to the mountain, aren't you. I can tell. You walk like people going to a funeral." The child's eyes were wet and blazing. "Take me with you all the way. I know the servant tunnels. Mama drew them for me when I was little, over and over, so I'd never forget. She said *if anything happens to me, go to the mountain, find the ledger, and read my name aloud.*"

The streambed was silent except for the fig leaves moving.

"Every kindness costs time," Nneka murmured—to Dominic, or to herself, or perhaps to some saint of bad bargains. Then she looked at Kito, and her face did the thing it did when she made a decision her bones disagreed with. "Child. Show us the tunnels."

Kito reached into the ragged shirt and pulled out a scrap of cloth, soft with handling, covered in a dead woman's careful ink. And as Dominic leaned close, his heart slamming like a fist on a door, he saw that among the winding lines of the servant ways, one passage was marked with a small drawn key—and beneath it, in Sela's hand, three words:

*The archive sleeps light.*

Four days to the solstice. Three and a half, now.

They walked.
