## 6. Beneath the Mountain

The door into the mountain was a mouth, and like every mouth Dominic had ever fed, it wanted to be paid.

Nneka sang to it. That was the toll — not coin, not blood, but a verse in a language that made his back teeth ache, a low rolling melody that sounded less like music than like someone remembering aloud. The stone listened. The stone, unbelievably, hummed back.

"Keep your hand on my shoulder," she murmured when the last note settled. "And whatever you taste in there — swallow nothing unless I nod first."

"I've swallowed worse for less grateful kings."

"You haven't." She glanced at him, and in the torchlight her face was kind in the way a blade is clean. "That's rather the point of you."

Then the mountain opened, and they went down into the court beneath the world.

* * *

He had imagined darkness. He should have known better. Courts are never dark; courts are lit like altars, because courts want to be worshipped.

The great hall of the under-mountain blazed with lamps of green glass, and everywhere — everywhere — was music. Not one song but dozens, layered: a quartet near the entrance playing something sweet and circular, a woman two terraces up singing counterpoint to herself, servants humming as they carried trays of candied figs and smoked eel. The sound came from all directions at once, and Dominic realized within a dozen steps what he was hearing.

They were paying each other. Constantly. A footman held a door; the lady passing through him trilled three notes of thanks. A lord complimented a musician's arpeggio by singing the next bar himself, and the musician bowed lower for it than any servant had bowed to any lord.

"It's currency," Dominic said quietly.

"Music is the only coin down here that doesn't rot," Nneka said. "Souls spoil fast once eaten. Notes keep."

She had told him this on the road, in her flat, unsparing way: the court beneath the mountain did not simply imprison the souls it bought. It ate them, slowly, a measure at a time, the way a miser spends a fortune so gradually he convinces himself he still has it. The nobles grew fat on borrowed voices. The souls grew thin.

What she had not managed to convey was what that looked like up close.

The first noble they passed was a man perhaps sixty years old, dressed magnificently in wine-colored silk, and he was beautiful the way a portrait is beautiful — correct, composed, faintly wrong. When he laughed, two laughs came out, pitched a third apart. When he reached for a glass, his hand hesitated mid-reach as though consulting someone about whether to complete the motion. And around his eyes, when he turned toward the light, the skin was translucent as paper, and behind it —

Behind it, something moved. Something that watched.

"Don't look long," Nneka breathed. "They take interest for staring."

Dominic fixed his gaze on the middle distance, on the tray of a passing servant, on anything, and made himself walk like a man who belonged here: unhurried, slightly bored, stomach empty by profession. It was the easiest acting of his life. He had spent twenty years being furniture with a tongue. The palace above had taught him exactly how to be invisible in a bright room.

At the head of the hall, on a dais of black marble, sat a throne that was mostly shadow and a steward who was mostly voice. This was Master Quill, Nneka had called him — the court's majordomo, its gatekeeper, its bookkeeper. It was Quill who would decide whether a taster sent ahead of the solstice feast was genuine.

Quill looked like a tall man who had been folded twice. His skin had the gray gloss of candle smoke, and when he spoke, the whole hall's music dipped respectfully, the way conversation hushes when a judge enters.

"You," he said, before they had even reached the dais. His eyes were pleasant. His eyes were the worst thing Dominic had seen yet, because they were so entirely, warmly human. "You're the one sent down from the valley. The immune one."

"I'm Dominic Oyelaran. Taster to the crown." He bowed precisely the depth the palace above would have demanded of a steward receiving a foreign envoy — respectful, not servile. "I'm told the solstice feast requires a palate that won't die of the first course."

A ripple went through the nearby courtiers — laughter, or something shaped like laughter, harmonized in too many throats.

"The valley sends us an honest man," Quill said, delighted. "How novel. Do you know, we had a wager on what you'd be? Half the court guessed a liar. A quarter guessed a coward." His smile widened without gaining warmth. "Nobody guessed honest. Honesty is such an expensive flavor."

"I'm expensive," Dominic agreed. "Ask the crown what it paid for me."

"Oh, I have read the invoices." Quill produced, from nowhere, a slim black ledger and let it fall open in his hands without touching the pages. "Apprenticeship fees. Board. A very reasonable rate for twenty years of poison training. Your king settles accounts beautifully." He closed the book, and it vanished. "Come. Walk with me while you eat. In this court, we believe a taster should be tasted."

* * *

They fed him through six courses, and he catalogued every one like prayers, because that was the oldest habit he owned.

The soup was river-eel in a broth that shimmered faintly, and underneath the saffron lay hemlock root, prepared gently, almost lovingly — a courtesy dose, a handshake between professionals. He named it silently and let it sit on his tongue and felt, distantly, the place in him where twenty years of small deaths had built a wall. The wall held. The wall always held. That was the horror of his gift: poisons arrived in his body like guests and found the house already furnished with their cousins.

The second course sang. Literally — the pheasant had been basted in rendered soul-light, Nneka had warned him, and when he cut into it the meat gave off a note, high and mournful, like a wet finger on a glass rim. Around him the courtiers leaned in to listen with expressions of genuine aesthetic pleasure.

"That one was a fiddler," said the lady to his left, unbidden. She was young, or had been young once; now her cheeks were smooth as river stones and her eyes were crowded, tenancy upon tenancy. "From the eastern villages. They bought him at auction, oh, eighty years ago. You can still hear him if you chew slowly."

"How do you know which is which?" Dominic asked, because asking was safer than screaming.

"We remember what we eat, dear boy." She smiled, and her smile had corners that belonged to other faces. "That's the whole secret of good breeding down here. Appetite plus memory. One without the other is just hunger." She patted his hand with fingers that were cold as cutlery. "Eat your pheasant. He'd want to be appreciated."

He ate it. He hated himself with a thoroughness he usually reserved for kings, and he ate it, and he noted the flavor — copper and honey and, underneath, unmistakably, a melody — because noting flavors was the only self he had left that still worked.

Through it all, Quill talked. That was the trap in the hospitality, Dominic slowly understood; the food kept his mouth busy so that Quill's voice could pour into the silence of him, soft and constant as drip-water.

"The feast will be magnificent. Nine hundred souls served across seven hours. Your king attends in effigy, of course — the mountain doesn't open for the living above, except—" the gray eyes glittered "—for special instruments. Tell me, Oyelaran. Do you dream?"

"No."

"A lie. Everyone dreams. What do you dream of?"

"Soup," Dominic said. "Large pots of it."

Quill laughed — one laugh, only one, and somehow that was more frightening than the harmonized ones — and refilled his cup himself, leaning close, and for one moment the majordomo's breath smelled of the archive: dust, ink, old paper, old names.

"Eat well these next days," Quill murmured. "Grow strong. The final course requires a healthy vessel."

* * *

It was on the fourth day, carrying a tray because a taster sent ahead works, he does not lounge, that he saw her.

He had rehearsed this moment on the road until Nneka made him stop. He had imagined running to her. Weeping. Saying her name the way you strike flint. He had imagined her turning, recognizing, the frayed memory catching fire all at once — *Adaeze, it's me, it's Dommie, you used to steal the burnt bottoms of the rice—*

Instead he was setting down cups of spiced milk among the serving choir — because of course the court kept a choir of the dead, why would it do anything else — and she was standing at the back of the second rank, holding her part of a hymn with her lips barely moving, conserving herself, spending herself carefully the way the poor spend coin.

She wore a gray gown with no sleeves, and her arms were thin, and there was a smudge of ash on her cheekbone that some older, bossier Adaeze would never have tolerated.

She looked, he thought, the way the palace kitchens looked on the night before a coronation: functioning perfectly, emptied out.

He set the cup down in front of her, and his hands were steady, because they were always steady, that was the whole cursed point of him.

"Your milk," he said. And then, softly, in the Yoruba their mother had sung over cradles, the private grammar of two children against the world: "You always drank mine too."

Her eyes came up. Brown, like his. Familiar, like a house he'd been locked out of.

For one heartbeat — one entire, enormous heartbeat — something flickered behind them. A draft through a shut window. Her brow creased, and her lips parted, and the ash-smudged face did something complicated, reaching—

"Yes," she said politely. "Thank you. You're new, aren't you? I don't remember your face." She tilted her head, courteous, blank, a hostess greeting a stranger. "You'll like it here. Everyone is very kind to us."

*Us*, she said. As if she knew what she was. As if kindness were the word for it.

The choir-master rapped his baton. The hymn resumed. Adaeze — his sister, his debt, the reason for every mile of poisoned road — turned back to the front and opened her mouth and gave her voice away, four bars at a time, to people whose skins were translucent with everything they'd stolen.

Dominic stood in the serving line with an empty tray and did not move, did not breathe, did not break, because breaking was a luxury and he had been trained out of luxuries. But somewhere under twenty years of swallowed poison, under the wall, under the professional calm, something in him stood up and began, quietly and irrevocably, to sing.

He was still standing there when a gray-glossed hand closed on his shoulder.

"Ah," said Master Quill, pleasantly, at his ear. "There you are. We've been looking for you everywhere, taster. It seems your name has come up in the ledger."
