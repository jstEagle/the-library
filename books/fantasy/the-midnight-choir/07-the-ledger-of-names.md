## 7. The Ledger of Names

The archive beneath the mountain smelled of tallow and old paper and, faintly, of something sweeter that Nneka refused to name. She had sung enough dead across enough thresholds to know the smell of souls in storage. It was like a larder. Like meat kept well.

She moved through the stacks in the grey hush between shifts, wearing the borrowed robes of an under-clerk whose real owner was currently asleep in a wine cellar, dreaming of nothing at all thanks to a lullaby Nneka had hummed into his ear on her way past. The robes itched. The clerk had been a small man. Nneka had been called many things in her life—heretic, miracle-worker, *that woman*—but *small* had never been one of them.

*Focus,* she told herself, and focused.

The archive of the court beneath the mountain was not, as she'd expected, hidden. That was the first obscenity of it. It sat in the third ring down, beside the granaries, with a brass plaque on the door: **REGISTRY OF OBLIGATIONS, TRANSFERS & PERPETUITIES**. Souls as line items. Eternity filed under *perpetuities*. Somewhere above them, nobles were eating off gold plate while their accountants kept immaculate books two floors below the kitchens.

Nneka had spent thirty years singing the dead across thresholds, and in all that time she had never once needed a ledger. A name, remembered rightly, was its own record. But this court did not trust memory. Memory could wake up. Memory could testify. So they wrote everything down, in ink, in triplicate, believing—she supposed—that paper made things permanent.

Paper made things *findable*, is what it made them.

She found the S-section just before the second bell. Shelves of ledgers bound in leather so fine it could only have come from animals that had never once been frightened, which in this valley meant they had probably been fed on hymns. She pulled the volume marked **TRANSFERS — SURFACE VALLEY — REIGN OF THE CURRENT KING** and set it on the reading table, and her hands were steady, because her hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that wanted to shake.

The entries were beautiful. That was the second obscenity. Each soul recorded in copperplate script: name, village, cause of debt, terms of sale, date of consumption where applicable. *Consumption.* They used the word without shame. A soul eaten at the spring feast. A soul eaten at a wedding—the entry noted, almost fondly, *bride's side paid generously.*

And there, halfway down a page, in ink slightly fresher than the rest:

**ADAEZE OYELARAN. Village of Ilode. Debt incurred: apprenticeship fees, brother's, outstanding fourteen years. Terms: full transfer to crown creditor. Status: HELD FOR SOLSTICE — FINAL COURSE.**

Final course.

Nneka read it three times. The words did not improve with familiarity. She thought of Dominic in the corridor outside, straightening his borrowed servant's coat for the tenth time, asking her in a whisper whether he looked like a man who belonged here, and her lying—gently, kindly—*yes.*

He had spent fourteen years believing his sister's death was a debt paid in grief alone. He had swallowed poison by the measured drop thinking he was buying safety. And all the while the ledger sat here, tidy as a hymnal, recording that Adaeze had been sold to settle *his* account, held in reserve like the last sweetmeat on a tray, waiting for the solstice.

He knew some of it. He knew she'd been sold. What he did not know—and what Nneka's fingers found now, tracing upward through the column of signatures—was who had done the selling.

Every transfer required a broker's mark. Most bore the seal of the court's steward, a fat flower of wax she'd seen a dozen times since descending. Some bore the marks of lesser magistrates, men who would be dust before their crimes finished ripening. But the Adaeze Oyelaran entry carried a different seal entirely. Larger. Pressed deep into the page, deep enough to emboss the three sheets beneath it.

A crowned mountain. And beneath the mountain, small but unmistakable, the initial **R**.

Nneka sat down on the reading stool quite suddenly, the way a woman sits when her legs have made a decision her dignity hasn't approved.

The king. The king himself had brokered the trade. Not a steward acting in his name, not a magistrate skimming from the margins—the royal seal, pressed personally, pressed *hard*. And there was more, because there was always more with kings; greed is a generous host. She turned pages backward, following the R-seal through years of entries, and a pattern emerged that made her throat close.

The king did not buy randomly. He bought *tasters*. Every food-taster in the palace's history—every man or woman sworn to swallow death in measured doses—had entered service within a year of a family member being sold below. Their debts were manufactured. Their grief was collateral. Their immunity was not a privilege granted for loyal service.

It was an ingredient, aged on purpose.

She found the memorandum tucked into the back of the ledger, folded into eighths, written in the same hand that had signed the transfers. It was brief. Bureaucratic. The worst documents always were.

*Re: the vessel question. An immune body, fed on every poison in our keeping for a span of years, becomes at the solstice a cup no other mouth may drink from. The final course requires a living chalice. Let the taster be raised to it slowly. He will believe himself protected. Protection, properly administered, is only preparation.*

*Properly administered, protection is only preparation.*

Nneka laughed. It came out wrong—a short, cracked sound in the silent archive, and she clapped her hand over her own mouth like a girl caught giggling at a funeral. Because what else could she do? It was funny, in the way that a knife in the ribs is funny if you've already died once. Fourteen years of swallowing poison to stay safe, and every drop had been seasoning him.

Dominic Oyelaran was not the king's servant. He was the king's *marinade*.

She put the memorandum back exactly as she'd found it, though her fingers wanted to tear it into confetti and eat it out of spite. Evidence mattered. She had learned that lesson the hard way, in another life, in another court, when she had sung a nobleman's son back across the threshold too far and been exiled for it while the *real* crime—the father who'd begged her, then blamed her—sat comfortably at table. The law had looked at her and seen a woman who broke rules. It had looked at the father and seen property. She had spent thirty years believing the law was merely blind.

It wasn't blind. It was *fed*. Blindness you could forgive. This was appetite wearing a wig.

* * *

Getting out was harder than getting in, mostly because of the cat.

The archive kept a cat—grey, enormous, with the self-possession of a minor deity—and it had been asleep on the warm ledger shelf when Nneka arrived and was now awake and deeply invested in her ankles. She crouched and offered it the traditional bribe: a scrap of dried fish from her pocket, carried for exactly such emergencies, because Nneka had learned long ago that in any fortress, palace, or hell, the true gatekeepers were always cats and clerks, and only one of them could be bribed with fish.

The cat took the fish, considered her further, and then—this was the unsettling part—spoke.

"You smell of thresholds," it said, in a voice like a drawer closing. "Old ones. Closed properly."

"I do that work," Nneka whispered. "Did."

"Did." The cat licked its whiskers. "The court stopped closing thresholds forty years ago. Why close a door when you can leave it open and see what wanders in?" Its eyes were the yellow of old candle-fat. "You're going to make trouble at the feast."

"I'm going to *sing* at the feast."

"Same thing, done right." The cat stood, stretched until it seemed in danger of becoming two cats, and padded toward the door. "Third ring east stair is watched. Take the cistern passage. Mind the second turn; the dark down there has opinions." It paused at the threshold—it did, pointedly, sit *on* the threshold, which Nneka chose to take as professional courtesy—and added: "The tall one who tastes. His sister asks after him. Every night. She doesn't remember his face anymore, but she remembers someone should be asked after."

Then it was gone, and Nneka stood alone in the aisle with her heart doing something complicated against her ribs.

*She doesn't remember his face anymore.*

Adaeze was fraying. Every day below ground unwove her a little more; every hour in that court's company thinned the thread. Nneka had known this intellectually—had said as much to Dominic on the road, gently, the way you tell a man his house is burning. But hearing it from a cat, in a basement, over dried fish, made it *true* in the way that matters. There was a clock on this. The clock was a girl forgetting her brother one stitch at a time, and the solstice was four days off, and the final course was already plated and walking around in a borrowed servant's coat asking whether he looked like he belonged.

* * *

She found Dominic in the servants' corridor near the feast kitchen, doing the thing he did when frightened, which was cataloguing poisons under his breath like a rosary. Hemlock, henbane, widow's-tear, the blue root they grew in the third cellar. She'd teased him about it once, early on the road—*most people count sheep*—and he'd shrugged and said sheep didn't kill anyone, which she'd privately conceded was a fair point.

"We need to talk," she said. "Not here."

They walked. Down, and around, and down again, until they found a landing where the wall-hymns—this court piped music through its stones like blood through veins—faded to a murmur. Nneka checked the corners out of habit. Empty. Probably. The dark down here had opinions, according to reliable sources.

"The trade for your sister," she began. "You knew it settled your debt."

"Fourteen years of fees." His voice was flat, pre-flat, the voice of a man bracing. "I did the sums a hundred times. I never understood how it came to that much."

"It didn't." She took his hands, because some news should be delivered holding on. "Your debt was invented, Dominic. Your apprenticeship was priced high on purpose. The king brokers these trades himself—his seal is on your sister's transfer, pressed hard enough to dent three pages underneath. And it isn't only you. Every taster the crown has ever employed was bought the same way. Grief for collateral. Service for interest."

Dominic didn't move. Didn't blink. She watched him hold very still the way he must have held still a thousand times, waiting to learn whether a mouthful would kill him.

"And my immunity?" he said finally. "Tell me."

So she told him. The vessel. The chalice. The memorandum in the back of the ledger, in the king's own hand. *Protection, properly administered, is only preparation.* She watched the sentence land in him and break something open—not the way grief breaks a thing, but the way light breaks through a shutter left unlatched. His whole life reorganized itself around the new fact. She could see it happening behind his eyes: every meal survived, every dose endured, every year of careful invisible loyalty, recast. He hadn't been the palace's shield.

He'd been the palace's *dessert*.

"I loved it," he said quietly. "That's the worst part. I loved the palace. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought if I was useful enough, invisible enough, the law would keep me. Keep her memory safe, at least. And the law was—" 

"Hungry," Nneka said. "The law was hungry. It was never neutral, Dominic. Neutral is a costume hunger wears to dinner." She squeezed his hands. "I know. I wore the exile for thirty years, believing I'd broken something sacred. I broke nothing. I interrupted a meal."

For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the wall-hymns murmured their fat, contented music, and somewhere far above, preparations for the solstice went on: garlands, glazes, guest lists. A court setting its table.

Then Dominic lifted his head, and Nneka saw the change complete itself—the despair settling, cooling, hardening into something with edges. She recognized it. It was the look of a man who had just realized that every poison he'd ever swallowed was still inside him, and that poison, as she'd told him once on the road, remembers everything.

"Four days," he said.

"Four days."

"Then we'd better teach me the rest of the songs." He smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of a cup deciding what it would hold. "Because I think I finally understand what I'm for. And I intend to be *served*. Right down the king's throat."

Above them, muffled through six rings of stone, the choir of the court beneath the mountain began rehearsing the solstice hymn—and Nneka, who had spent her life listening for what songs were really saying, went cold to the bone. She knew that melody. Everyone did.

It was a grace before meals.
