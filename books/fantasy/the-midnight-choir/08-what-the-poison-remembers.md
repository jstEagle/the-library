## 8. What the Poison Remembers

They took his boots first.

Dominic would remember that detail later, when everything else had blurred into fever-color: the small, almost courteous indignity of it. Two servants in gray livery—faces like unfinished sketches, eyes the color of tallow—kneeling to unlace his boots as though he were a guest at the end of a long evening. One of them folded the boots neatly. The other folded Dominic's ankles behind him, which was less courteous.

"Vessels travel light," said the warden who had taken him, a broad woman with a voice like gravel settling in water. "The feast provides everything."

He did not struggle. There had been a moment, back in the corridor, when he might have—when Adaeze had looked at him with those polite, vacant eyes and asked the warden, *Is this one expected?*—and something in his chest had cracked open like an egg. He had wanted to say *Adaeze, it's me, it's Dominic, you used to steal the burnt edges of the yam and say they were the best part.* He had wanted to say her name.

But Nneka's last instruction, pressed into his palm like a coin: *Don't. Not here. A name spoken in this court belongs to whoever hears it.* So he had swallowed it, the way he had swallowed everything his whole life, and let the gray servants lead him down.

The cells beneath the feast-hall were not cells. That was the first thing that unsettled him. They were guest chambers—low-ceilinged, warm, hung with tapestries that showed harvests no valley had ever grown. Comfortable. Intended for occupation. The door was oak, banded in iron, and it closed behind him with the soft finality of a lid.

* * *

He was fed.

That was the second thing. A tray came through a slot in the door: pepper soup, fufu, a wedge of pineapple sweeter than anything that had ever grown in Dominic's memory of the valley above. He stared at it for a long time before touching it, and when he did, he did what he had done at every royal meal for twenty years—he tasted, he waited, he read his own body like a ledger.

No poison. Of course no poison. You did not poison the cup you intended to drink from.

He ate anyway. That was the joke of him, wasn't it? The taster who had finally found a table where the food was safe, and the only reason was that he was the meal.

He laughed at that—actually laughed, a short ugly sound in the warm room—and then he sat on the comfortable bed and began, methodically, to take inventory. It was what he did. The world went mad, and Dominic Oyelaran catalogued.

One: they knew what he was. Not a taster sent ahead—no one sent a taster down in irons and called it courtesy. They had known, perhaps from the moment he crossed the valley's edge. Perhaps from before. Perhaps—

*Perhaps from the beginning,* said a small cold voice in him. *Perhaps the apprenticeship debt was never a debt. Perhaps you were purchased as a child and seasoned slowly, like meat in a marinade, and every measured dose of arsenic and nightshade was not protection but preparation.*

He had built his immunity the way a mason builds a wall, stone by swallowed stone. He had thought of it as armor. Sitting in the guest chamber that was not a cell, he let himself consider, for the first time, that armor and vessel were made by the same hands, and only the intention at the end differed.

Two: Adaeze had not known him.

He had prepared for hatred. For accusation, for the cold fury of a sister sold to pay a brother's debts. He had rehearsed apologies in the dark of the road while Nneka slept—*I didn't know, I would have paid it, I would have sold myself instead*—and he had even prepared for the worst version, where she looked at him with contempt and said *you lived comfortably while I was sold.*

He had not prepared for politeness. For the small frown of a woman trying to place a stranger's face. Her memory had been eaten the way the court ate everything—in courses, carefully, over years—and what remained of his sister had been served her own brother as an appetizer without knowing what she tasted.

He put his face in his hands and let the grief come, because he was alone and the door was oak and there was no one left in the world to perform steadiness for. When it had wrung itself out, he wiped his face on the fine linen—they had even given him fine linen—and took inventory again.

Three: Nneka was still free. Probably. He had to believe probably.

* * *

The souls came at midnight. He would learn later that there was no midnight under the mountain—no sun, no moon, only the feast-hall's great clocks counting down to solstice—but his body kept its own hours, and in the hour his body called midnight, the tapestries began to whisper.

Not the tapestries. The things behind them. Or in the walls. Or simply *there*, the way damp is there.

"Don't be frightened," said a voice near the foot of the bed. It was an old voice, papery, with a laugh caught in it like a fishbone. "We're told we frighten the staff, but the staff are mostly eaten themselves, so it hardly matters."

Dominic sat up. At first he saw nothing. Then, the way you see a face in fire, he saw *shapes*—thin ones, translucent ones, gathered at the edges of the lamplight like moths that had learned modesty. There were dozens. Perhaps hundreds. The warm little chamber was full to its comfortable rafters.

"You're the vessel," said another voice, younger, sadder. "They've been talking about you. The one who drank the valley dry."

"I didn't drink anything I wasn't ordered to," Dominic said, and then heard how it sounded, and added, "I'm sorry. That's not—I'm sorry."

"Oh, don't apologize to *us*," said the papery voice, delighted. "We're the only ones here who don't want anything from you. Well. Almost the only ones." A shape drifted closer, and Dominic saw it had been a man once—old, narrow, with the suggestion of spectacles. "I was a tax assessor. Forty years. Do you know what they did with a tax assessor, down here? They ate the part of me that could count. Left me the rest, like a crab left in its shell after the meat's gone. I can't remember my own ledgers, but I remember *wanting* to remember them. That's the cruelty of it. They don't take everything. They leave you hungry for yourself."

The young voice said, "I was a midwife. They took the songs."

"The songs," Dominic said. Nneka's word. *The threshold songs.*

"For the dead. So no one down here could be sung across, you see. So the eaten ones couldn't leave." The midwife-shape turned toward him, and though it had no face he felt it looking, with something like pity. "You're the one they've been feeding. All those years in the valley. Every poison, measured, in order, by the king's own physicians."

"It was to protect me," Dominic said. "To build—"

"Immunity, yes. That's the word they used upstairs too." The assessor's laugh-fishbone rattled. "Vessel, we've watched them prepare a hundred feasts. Nothing in this court is protection. Everything is *seasoning*."

Dominic sat very still. Somewhere in the walls, the clocks ticked toward solstice.

"Tell me what I am," he said. "All of it. I'd rather choke on the truth than swallow another comfortable lie. I've had a lifetime of those."

* * *

They told him through the small hours, and the terrible thing—the thing that turned his despair into something harder and stranger—was that it made sense. It fit. Every strange mercy of his life clicked into place like a lock accepting its key.

Why the crown had taken a poor boy with no family connections and trained him in the delicate art of not dying. Why the doses had been *measured*, in *sequence*, each one logged by the physicians in a ledger he had never been permitted to see. Why the valley was magic-dead—not to protect the king from sorcery, but to keep a growing vessel somewhere the souls inside him could not wake. Why the hunt for the gifted had been declared *now*, in his final year of service, when the vessel was nearly full and the last courses were being gathered.

"You are not immune," the midwife said gently. "You are *indexed*. Every poison you ever swallowed left its signature in you. Your body didn't defeat them, vessel. It *learned their names*. And a poison that has been named—" the shape drifted closer, "—can be *called*."

"Called by whom?"

"By whoever holds your ledger," said the assessor. "At the feast, they'll pour the ceremonial cup into you—the distillation of every toxin you were ever fed, the whole index at once. Your body will hold it. That's what you were built for; you're the only cup in the world that won't shatter. And every soul they've ever eaten will be bound into the wine, and poured into you, and kept. A cellar with a heartbeat." The papery voice softened. "They've been aging you for twenty years, boy. You're a very good vintage."

Dominic sat with it. He was surprised by his own stillness. He had expected—rage, maybe, or the egg-crack feeling from the corridor, the one that had opened when Adaeze's eyes had passed over him like weather.

Instead he felt something he recognized from his working life: the moment a taster's tongue catches the odd note in the soup. Bitter, faint, *wrong*—and beneath the wrongness, a signature. Because that was the secret of his trade, the thing no one at court had understood about the man who swallowed their poisons for a living.

Poison was never anonymous.

Every toxin had a maker. A recipe, a hand, a lineage—nightshade cultivated in a particular garden, arsenic refined by a particular process, the new toxin from the soup that had nearly killed him in chapter one of his ruin, which had been *new*, which meant someone had *made it recently*, which meant someone at court had a laboratory and a purpose. He had spent twenty years reading those signatures without knowing he was doing it. His body had spent twenty years writing them down.

And if his body was a ledger—

Then every poison in him remembered its maker.

The thought arrived not as hope but as arithmetic, cold and clean, and it was Nneka's voice in his head that completed it: *A name spoken in this court belongs to whoever hears it.* And a name *sung*—

"You're smiling," said the midwife, warily. "Vessel, why are you smiling?"

"Because they made a mistake," Dominic said. His voice came out steady, and it surprised him, and then it didn't, because steadiness was the one thing twenty years of the king's table had actually given him. "They spent two decades teaching my body the name of every poison in this mountain. They logged every dose. They were so careful with their ledger." He looked up at the gathered shapes, the eaten tax assessor, the songless midwife, the hundreds of hungry half-remembrances pressed against the edges of the light. "They never once asked what a ledger is *for*."

The assessor's fishbone laugh rattled, delighted and afraid at once. "And what is it for, vessel?"

"Reading," Dominic said. "And being read. On the night it matters."

He lay back on the fine linen of his comfortable cell and did not sleep. Instead he did what he had done at ten thousand royal meals: he waited, and he tasted, and he listened to his own body. And beneath the fear, beneath the grief, he began—slowly, carefully, the way he had learned every poison one measured dose at a time—to read.

Arsenic, and the apothecary's hand that refined it. Nightshade, and the walled garden where it grew. The gray tallow-eyed servants, made of something distilled. The new toxin from the soup, and—faint, bitter, recent—the signature of the hand that had brewed it, a hand he had shaken, a hand that had praised his service.

And under all of it, oldest and deepest, the first dose they had ever given him, when he was a boy of nine with a debt on his name: a poison with a signature he knew, because he had tasted it in the king's own soup a hundred times, and it was the king's own recipe, and the king had been feeding it to him since before either of them understood what was being built.

*They seasoned me with their own hand,* Dominic thought, and the terrible clarity settled over him like a second skin. *Every crime in this mountain, I have already swallowed. I am not their vessel. I am their confession.*

Far above, the clocks changed their rhythm—slower now, deliberate, a countdown he could feel through the soles of his bare feet.

Two days to solstice.

And somewhere in the mountain, a choir-mistress with a disgraced voice was looking for him, and he had a song to teach her.
