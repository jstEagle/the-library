## 9. The Midnight Choir

*Nneka*

The cells beneath the feast-hall smelled of iron and old hymns, and Nneka had never been so glad of a bad smell in her life. Bad smells meant stone that remembered being quarried. Stone that could be sung to.

She pressed her palm against the wall and hummed the first note of the threshold song—softly, the way you'd wake a child—and felt the mountain answer. Not with silence. The valley below had been silence made law, but here, deep in the dark, the rock still held every note it had ever swallowed: coronation anthems, funeral dirges, the small songs servants sang over wash-basins to keep from weeping.

*Music is currency here,* Dominic had written in his careful taster's hand. *The nobility buy it by the measure.*

Then they had been fools enough to pay their executioners in advance.

"You're certain he's alive," whispered Tobe, the child they'd sheltered on the road, crouched at her elbow in the service tunnel. The boy's gift was small and strange—he could hear names the way other people heard footsteps—but tonight Nneka thanked every saint of no particular denomination for strange gifts.

"He's alive." Tobe's eyes were shut. "He's... singing? No. Reciting. Lists. He's reciting poison lists in his head like prayers. He's scared, but he's doing it anyway."

Nneka smiled despite everything. That was Dominic Oyelaran all over. The man catalogued toxins the way other men said grace, and if death itself came for him he would probably ask it to wait while he noted its vintage.

"Good," she said. "Then he's ready."

Above them, muffled through forty feet of mountain, the feast-hall was filling. She could feel it—the press of bodies, the shimmer of half-eaten souls wearing noble flesh like ill-fitting coats. Two hundred guests. One king. One cup.

And somewhere up there, a choir without a conductor, waiting twenty years for someone to give them the downbeat.

Nneka rolled her shoulders, spat on her hands, and took up her tuning fork—a plain thing, brass, worth less than the candle beside it. The last gift from the choir she'd once led across thresholds. The one she'd been exiled for using too well.

"They told me my voice was ruined," she murmured to the dark, and the dark leaned in to listen. "They told me I sang a dead boy too far back and broke the world's rules." Her laugh came out cracked and warm. "Let's see what the rules look like when we're done."

She struck the fork against stone.

The note went up through the mountain like water through a crack, and everywhere it touched, something sleeping turned over and began, very quietly, to remember.

* * *

*Dominic*

They dressed him in white for the feast, which he found insulting. Poisoners' victims deserved better tailoring.

The ceremonial cup stood taller than his forearm, wrought of silver gone green at the rim, and the solstice brew inside it moved when he wasn't looking—not swirling, *breathing*. Forty-one compounds, by his count, though the count kept shifting because three of them were not compounds at all but distilled soul-stuff, thick as regret. He knew each one the way a priest knows scripture. He knew which lord had supplied the hemlock variant, which vintner's cellar had aged the grief-extract, which royal decree had ordered the lot.

His immunity sat in him like an armory. Every dose he'd ever swallowed on the king's behalf, filed and waiting.

*I am not their vessel,* he reminded himself. *I am their confession.*

An attendant—gray-faced, gray-eyed, wearing a smile that had been issued rather than grown—led him up the long stair into the light. And there was Adaeze.

She stood among the serving spirits near the dais, holding a tray of candied figs, and the sight of her stopped his heart the way it always did: his sister, tall and straight-backed, with their mother's stubborn chin. But her eyes went through him like weather through an open window. Twenty years of being eaten had frayed her at the edges; where her name should have been, there was only a smooth blank place, worn soft as a river stone.

She was humming, though. Faintly. A lullaby their mother used to sing, stripped of its words.

*Hold on,* he willed her. *Half a verse more. Hold on.*

Trumpets. The court rose. And the King Under the Mountain entered on a litter of black oak, carried by six souls who walked with the terrible smoothness of things that had forgotten they ever stumbled. His crown was iron. His smile was generous. His eyes swept the hall and snagged, briefly, on the taster in white, and Dominic watched recognition dawn—the pleasant recognition of a man seeing his own property arrive on schedule.

"Friends!" the king called, arms wide. "Tonight we complete what your houses began! Tonight we drink the last course, and the mountain is satisfied for another age!"

Dominic took the cup when it was offered. It was heavier than it should have been. Everything true was.

Across the hall, near the great doors, a serving woman in choir grays lifted her head. Gray hair. Brass tuning fork tucked behind one ear like a flower. Nneka caught his eye, and gave him the smallest nod—the nod of a choir-mistress counting in her alto section.

Somewhere below, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, a single pure note began to climb.

Dominic raised the cup to the king, smiled the smile of a man who had spent thirty years smiling at people who wanted his master dead, and drank.

* * *

*Nneka*

The song went through the mountain like dawn through frost.

She didn't lead it so much as aim it. The threshold song was old work, honest work—you sang a name, and the named soul remembered which side of the door it belonged on. She had sung it for the dying for thirty years. She had sung it once, disastrously, for the already-dead, and been cast out for it.

Now she stood in the feast-hall's shadowed gallery with two hundred stolen souls around her and sang every name she'd learned from the ledger, and the mountain did the rest.

*"Adaeze Oyelaran,"* she called, clear as a bell over the rising music. *"Sold in the spring of the famine year. Price: one apprenticeship, one boy's future. Remember yourself."*

The effect was not subtle.

Down on the floor, a lady-in-waiting screamed and clutched her throat as the soul inside her surfaced, dragging its memories up like a drowning swimmer. A general dropped his goblet and began weeping in a language dead two hundred years. The six bearers of the king's litter set it down—all together, all gently—and stood up straight, and looked at the king with the faces of the people they had been before he ate them.

And in the center of it all, Dominic stood with the empty cup in his hands and began to speak.

Not sing—speak, in his flat, precise, inventory-keeper's voice, and yet every word rang through the stone as if the mountain itself had decided to take dictation.

"Hemlock, third pressing, supplied by Lord Vess of the Eastern Reaches, per writ signed the third of Harvestmonth—" His voice cracked, steadied. "The poisons remember their makers. I have merely given them leave to say so."

And the poisons *did*. They rose out of him out loud, compound after compound, each one crying its origin and its orderer into the rafters, and the court heard its own crimes read back to it in the voice of the man they'd seasoned for the pot. Nneka saw nobles trying to flee and finding the doors already full of their own half-eaten pasts. She saw the king rise from his litter, iron crown sliding, shouting for guards who were busy remembering their mothers.

She kept singing. There was so much left to sing.

* * *

*Dominic*

It ended the way avalanches end: all at once, and then in echoes.

The king did not fall to any sword. He fell to arithmetic. Every soul in the hall turned to face him at once—every soul he had swallowed, now awake inside the bodies of his guests—and the sheer weight of being *remembered* pressed him down onto his own dais like a debt coming due. He opened his mouth to command them, and what came out instead was a single stolen name, then another, then a hundred, the ledger of his appetite spilling out of him faster than he could stop it, until the whole mountain rang with the inventory of his crimes and there was nothing left of the King Under the Mountain but a small, hungry, ordinary man, kneeling in the wreckage of his own feast.

The guards—who had been remembering themselves for a quarter hour now—arrested him almost as an afterthought.

Dominic stood amid the ruin, shaking, empty of forty-one poisons and fuller than he'd ever been. And then a hand took the cup from his fingers, and Adaeze was looking at him.

Not through him. *At* him. Her eyes had edges again. Her name fit her mouth again, and the first thing she said with it was:

"You got *taller.*"

Dominic laughed—or sobbed, the acoustics in the hall made it difficult to tell—and pulled his sister against him, tray of candied figs and all. Around them, souls were stepping free of their borrowed bodies, blinking in the torchlight, greeting one another across decades and centuries. An old woman embraced a young soldier and called him by a name no record held. Music rose from a hundred throats with no conductor at all.

Nneka appeared at his shoulder, hoarse as a gravel road, grinning like sin.

"Told you the rules would look different," she rasped.

* * *

*Nneka*

By morning the mountain was quiet in the way a house is quiet after a fever breaks. Souls drifted toward the upper passages, following the smell of daylight. Some would cross onward. Some would stay to testify. All of them, Nneka noticed, were humming.

Tobe found her on the steps, rubbing sleep from his eyes. "What happens now?" the boy asked. "Is there, like... a trial?"

Nneka looked out over the hall—the overturned litter, the scattered figs, the king's iron crown lying on its side like a dropped cooking pot—and thought about courts and verdicts and the slow machinery of law.

"Oh, there'll be trials," she said. "Men love their paperwork. But the sentencing's already done, child." She tapped his chest, right over the heart. "Every soul in this mountain knows exactly what happened to it, and none of us will ever be permitted to forget. That's not punishment. That's just memory, working properly."

From the doorway, Dominic's voice, dry as good wine: "If the two of you are finished philosophizing, my sister wants to see the sunrise. She says she hasn't seen one in twenty years and refuses to miss another."

Adaeze stood beside him in borrowed clothes, thin as winter grass and burning like a lamp, her hand resting in the crook of her brother's arm. Above them, at the top of the long stair, the passage to the outer world glowed pale gold.

Nneka shouldered her pack, took up her tuning fork, and gave the morning its first note.

Together, the four of them climbed toward the light—and somewhere far below, in the deepest cell of the emptied mountain, the king's small hungry voice was still calling names into the dark, and the dark, at last, declined to answer.
