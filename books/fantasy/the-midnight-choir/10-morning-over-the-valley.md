## 10. Morning Over the Valley

The valley smelled different when magic came back.

Dominic noticed it before he noticed anything else—before the light, before the crowds in the square, before the king's banners coming down from the customhouse like wet laundry. The valley had always smelled of bread and river mud and, faintly, of nothing underneath all that. A blankness you could taste if you'd spent thirty years tasting things for a living. Now there was a green note under everything, like a chord held too long finally resolving.

"Like thyme," said Nneka beside him, "but wrong."

"Like thyme that remembers being something else," Dominic agreed.

They stood on the ridge above the city with the morning fog burning off below them, and the whole valley lay open and changed. Where the spell had been a held breath, now it was an exhale. Gardens had gone feral overnight in small ways—a hedge blooming out of season, a well that sang when you drew from it. Children were already chasing lights that had no business existing this far south of anywhere.

Below them, the city was waking to the news. It traveled strangely here—not by crier but by memory. Every soul that had come up out of the mountain at dawn carried its own name back into its own house, and every household that opened its door to one of those returning dead learned what the crown had done, and told two neighbors, and so on, the way grief travels, which is faster than horses.

"They'll want someone to punish," Nneka said. She was working her hands as she talked, kneading nothing, an old habit he'd come to read as thinking.

"The court ate itself," Dominic said. "That's a start."

"And the ones who signed the papers? The ministers who brokered the trades? The neighbors who informed?" She looked at him sideways. "You're going to testify, aren't you."

"I'm going to testify."

"You could run. You've earned running. You could take your sister and go somewhere the ledgers don't reach."

"She wouldn't go." He said it gently, because Nneka still flinched sometimes at Adaeze's name, at what the mountain had made of her and what the singing had unmade. "And neither would I, apparently. Thirty years of swallowing poison for other people's safety. I find I've developed a taste for telling the truth instead."

Nneka laughed—a short, cracked sound, but real. "Terrible joke."

"I'm a taster. My jokes were never the strong suit."

* * *

He found Adaeze in their mother's old garden, which should have been impossible, since she had never stood in it while alive.

She was kneeling by the row of pepper plants their mother had kept like pets, and she was touching the leaves with both hands, carefully, the way you touch a thing you're afraid is a dream. Her soul had come back whole from the singing—that was Nneka's doing, and the choir's, and his, though his part had mostly been drinking a cup of ceremonial poison and letting every toxin in his blood call out its maker's name like a witness list. But whole did not mean unchanged. She had died at nineteen and been eaten in increments for twenty years, and what returned wore her face and her stubborn chin and also something older around the eyes, something patient and slightly sad, like a person who has read the last page of a book everyone else is still arguing about.

"You're staring," she said without turning around.

"You're weeding my mother's garden."

"Somebody has to. Your mother's letters say you inherited her thumbs and none of her talent." Now she turned, and smiled, and it was her smile—the crooked one, the one that had gotten them both in trouble as children. "Hello, Domi."

"Hello, Ada."

They looked at each other across twenty years and one solstice feast. Then she patted the dirt beside her, and he sat down in it, ruining a perfectly good coat, and for a while they just pulled weeds together like no time had passed, which was a lie they both permitted because some lies are mercy.

"You remember dying?" he asked eventually. He hadn't dared ask before. In the mountain, after the song, after the king's court came apart into screaming and remembering, there had only been time to hold her hand and walk her out through tunnels that stank of old smoke, and then the long road home, and then the valley opening around them like an apology.

"Some of it. The fever, mostly. You sitting by my bed reading me that awful adventure serial even though I was clearly dying and couldn't complain anymore." She snapped a weed cleanly at the root. "The mountain is blurrier. Like trying to remember a meal you didn't enjoy. There were years I was... furniture, Domi. Years I wasn't anywhere. And then years where I was aware enough to know I was being worn." She glanced at him. "Your poisons talked a lot, near the end. Did you know that? They remembered you."

"I know."

"One of them said you'd forgiven yourself. Which one was lying?"

"Probably all of them. Poisons exaggerate."

She laughed, and then she stopped weeding and took his hands, and hers were cold in the way that earth is cold, not unpleasantly, just honestly. "I'm not staying."

He had known. He had known since the ridge, watching the fog burn off, feeling the new-magic smell of the valley and understanding that it would feel to her like a coat that almost fit. But knowing a thing and having your sister say it are different animals, and the second one bites.

"The choir sings thresholds," she went on, before he could arrange his face into anything noble. "I've heard them now. From the inside. Nneka's song doesn't drag anyone anywhere—it opens doors. And there's a door for me, Domi. There's been a door this whole time. The mountain stole the walking-toward-it; it didn't steal the door." Her voice wobbled and steadied. "If I stay here, I'm a guest in a garden. If I go, I'm—whatever comes next. I'd like to find out. I spent twenty years finding out nothing at all."

"When?"

"At dusk. Nneka says the threshold songs sit best at dusk." She squeezed his hands hard enough that he felt it, really felt it, and marveled at that—marveled that a soul could be solid enough to bruise. "And I want the full goodbye. Not the one you gave me over my fever bed when I was half asleep. The real one. You owe me that. You owe *yourself* that."

"I'm terrible at goodbyes."

"Yes," she said fondly. "But you're excellent at showing up. Start there."

* * *

Dusk came the color of plum skins, and half the city came to watch, because word had traveled, because in a valley that had spent generations pretending magic wasn't real, the first public crossing in living memory was not the sort of thing you missed. Dominic stood at the front of the crowd with Nneka and the choir behind him—eleven singers, twelve counting the gifted child Temi, who refused on principle to be left out of anything—and the gathered souls who had chosen to stay rather than cross stood scattered through the crowd like candles, quiet, waiting.

Adaeze wore white, borrowed, slightly too big. She looked, he thought, exactly like herself and completely new, and he decided those were the same thing.

Nneka began low, a single held tone that seemed to come up through the ground, and the choir found her one voice at a time, and the air over the garden path went thin and bright, like heat shimmer, like the moment before rain. A door. That was all it was. That was everything it was.

Adaeze turned to him at the edge of it.

"Domi." She took his face in her cold honest hands. "You broke an oath of invisibility, crossed a poisoned country, drank a king's cup, and sang my name into a ledger in front of the entire court beneath the mountain. You did all that with a body full of poison and a heart full of guilt and not one ounce of training." Her thumb wiped a tear off his cheek with great efficiency, as if tidying him. "You were never invisible, you idiot. We just weren't looking hard enough."

"I miss you already."

"I know. Miss me specifically. Miss the pepper plants. Don't miss the version of me that died—that one's done, and she'd tell you herself if she hadn't gone ahead." She stepped backward into the bright air, and it held her, and she was smiling the crooked smile. "Testify loudly, little brother. Make them write it all down. And feed Nneka properly—she forgets."

Then the door closed, gently, the way a well-fed fire goes out, and the garden was just a garden again, and Dominic Oyelaran stood in the ruins of his family's evening with his heart broken open in the specific way that turns out, later, to have been a repair.

Temi put a small solemn hand in his. "Are you crying?"

"Tasting the air."

"It's salty."

"Don't be cheeky."

* * *

The trial lasted eleven days, and Dominic testified for nine of them.

He sat in the rebuilt assize hall—its roof still patched with mismatched slate, because the mountain's collapse had dropped debris on three districts and pride on all of them—and he told the truth slowly, in order, with dates. He named the brokers. He named the informers, gently, because some of them had been afraid and fear was worth weighing alongside guilt. He described the ledger room under the mountain shelf by shelf, and when the magistrates asked how he could possibly recall such detail, he said, "Because I have spent thirty years memorizing what I swallow, and I swallowed all of this," and the hall laughed in the uncomfortable way halls do when a joke is also an indictment.

Some of the guilty ran. Some bought verdicts, and discovered that in a valley where every stolen soul had come home remembering itself, there were witnesses in every parlor and no dark corner left that didn't remember being lit. Justice here was not a sword. It was a chorus, and it knew all the words.

On the last day, the head magistrate asked him what restitution he sought.

"A school of music," Dominic said. "And a pension for the returned. And for the crown's food-taster position to be abolished, permanently, with prejudice, and replaced by absolutely nothing, because a kingdom that needs its meals tested for murder should probably look at its kitchen staff instead." A pause. "Also my mother's garden plot formally reclassified as a memorial, so nobody builds on it. She'd haunt them. She's capable of it now. We checked."

* * *

Autumn found the choir in a converted granary near the river, rehearsing badly and improving.

Nneka conducted with a stick she'd stolen from the assize hall—"evidence," she claimed—and yelled at the sopranos with tremendous affection. Temi had turned out to have a voice like struck glass and the discipline of a fireworks display. They were teaching the first proper cohort: the returned who stayed, the gifted who'd crept out of attics once the hunting stopped, a retired baker whose gift was making bread that remembered being wheat and sang about it faintly while proofing, which everyone agreed was useless and delightful.

Dominic's job, officially, was administration. Unofficially, he tasted the choir's tea for poison out of habit, and Nneka let him, because some comforts shouldn't be argued with.

"You'll sing the thresholds eventually," she said one evening, watching the river go gold. "Once the immunity finishes burning off. Your throat will be ordinary. Ordinary throats can learn."

"I know. I'm dreading it."

"You're looking forward to it."

"I'm looking forward to it *with dread.* It's called being a complete person, Nneka. You should try it."

She threw her baton at him, and he caught it, and outside the granary the valley hummed its strange green note, and somewhere past the edge of hearing—but only barely, now, only just barely—a door stood open for whoever needed it next, and the choir was learning the song to walk them through.
