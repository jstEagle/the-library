## 1. Charms for the Fearful

The trick to selling magic in a city that burned witches was, Ulyana Pemberton had learned over twenty years, mostly a matter of voice.

"You'll want the blue knot, love," she said, holding it up between thumb and forefinger so the lantern light caught the faded wool. "Blue's for luck on the water. My grandmother made these same knots back in Saltmere, and her grandmother before her. Nothing in them but good wishes and patient hands."

The fishwife — Marta, third generation of Martas at the eel stall two down from Ulyana's — squinted at the knot the way she always did, as if patience and good wishes might be hiding something sharper between their folds.

"Last one you sold me kept my boat dry through the whole spring storm."

"That's the good wishes," Ulyana said solemnly. "Very hardworking good wishes."

Marta snorted, dug three coppers out of her apron, and took the knot like a woman who didn't believe in medicine but took her tonic anyway. She moved off toward the eels, and Ulyana let out a breath she hadn't realized she'd been rationing.

Three coppers for a knot that would keep a boat dry through anything short of divine judgment. If Marta ever knew what the blue thread had cost to prepare — three nights of quiet work, a whispered binding over each strand, the careful folding of intention into wool until it held its shape like a small stubborn prayer — she'd have paid ten times as much, or run screaming to the Wardens, depending entirely on which side of the fear she woke up on.

That was Cinderhollow. Everyone lived on both sides of the fear. You just tried not to notice which side your neighbors were standing on tonight.

Ulyana adjusted her shawl against the river damp and surveyed her little corner of the Gullet Market. *Stall* was generous. It was a plank table, a striped awning gone gray with age, and a hand-painted sign reading PEMBERTON'S NOTIONS — KNOTS, CHARMS, AND OTHER SMALL COMFORTS, with the last word underlined twice in a way she hoped read as *quaint* rather than *incriminating*. Behind her, in a locked box with a false bottom she'd built herself, sat the actual inventory: fever-charms braided from willow bark and thread pulled at midnight, sleep-knots stuffed with lavender that had been sung to, and one jar of true mending salve she'd never once managed to sell because she couldn't bear to part with the proof of what it could do.

The market smelled of frying onions and wet stone and, underneath everything, the river — the Graywater sliding past Cinderhollow all night long, carrying whatever the city chose to forget downstream. Above the market roofs, the mountain stood against the stars like a closed fist. Nobody in the Gullet looked at the mountain. That was another local tradition, older than the Magistrate, probably.

She was restocking her display knots — the harmless ones, the ones that were genuinely just wool and patience — when she felt the change come over the market the way you feel weather: a hush rippling outward from the lane's mouth, conversations dipping, vendors suddenly fascinated by their own goods.

Wardens.

* * *

There were four of them, torchlight on their gray lacquer armor, moving through the market at that particular unhurried pace that said *we are not here for anyone specific, which means we are here for everyone*. The tall one in front carried a brass lantern with a shuttered flame — warden-fire, the kind that burned green near unlicensed craft. Ulyana had seen one turn green outside a baker's once, over nothing but a loaf scored into a luck-pattern by an old man who'd learned his knife-work from his hedge-witch mother without ever knowing it counted. They'd taken him anyway. The bread had been very good, people said afterward, as if that mattered, as if remembering the bread was a kindness.

Ulyana lowered her eyes to her table and became, with the ease of long practice, a boring woman.

This was the deepest charm she knew, and no coven had taught it: the art of being overlooked. Shoulders slightly rounded. Hands busy with something dull. Face set to an expression of mild, apologetic competence — the face of someone you might buy twine from but couldn't describe an hour later. She'd spent two decades polishing it, this invisible woman, this Pemberton of the Notions, and some nights she was so good at it that she lost track of the woman underneath.

"Wardens," Marta muttered, arriving at the stall with a basket of eels she had no reason to carry past it. "Fourth night running."

"Mm," Ulyana said, eyes down, fingers sorting knots.

"They're looking for something."

"They're always looking for something."

"No." Marta's voice dropped further. "They've got a tracker with them now. Woman. Sergeant, by the braid. Been asking questions at the harbor gate about — " Marta paused, and Ulyana heard her choose her words with unusual care — "about *craft-sellers*. Real ones. Not notion-mongers."

Ulyana's hands did not stop sorting. Twenty years of practice lived in moments exactly like this one; her fingers went on arranging wool while her mind went white and still as a frozen pond. A tracker. Not a patrol, not a sweep, not the usual theater of gray armor meant to remind the city it was loved through fear. A *tracker*, asking about real craft, meant someone above her had received information, and information about hedge-witches only came from two places: accidents, and betrayal.

"Probably after the tallow-wife in Copperlane," Ulyana said lightly. "I hear she marks her candles."

"The tallow-wife left town a month ago."

"Then they'll be disappointed." Ulyana smiled the smile that went nowhere near her eyes and added, in a tone of perfect merchant's boredom, "Luck-knots, Marta? Half price for friends who gossip."

Marta bought a red knot she didn't need and left, and Ulyana stood alone at her table while the Wardens drifted closer, and she performed invisibility with every fiber of her considerable skill.

It was exhausting. That was the thing nobody told you about hiding — not the fear, exactly, though there was plenty of that, cold and constant as groundwater. It was the *work*. Every conversation was a performance. Every friendship was a role played so long the seams showed only to herself. Twenty years of laughing at jokes half a beat late so no one would look too closely at her face. Twenty years of pretending her remedies worked through folk wisdom and luck, of watching children she'd quietly cured grow up believing they'd simply been sturdy. Twenty years of being liked by everyone and known by no one.

The tall Warden stopped four stalls down, at Old Tam's spice table, and held up his green-flamed lantern over the saffron and pepper with theatrical diligence. Tam, seventy if he was a day, watched the flame with the serene disinterest of a man who had survived three magistrates and fully intended to survive this one.

"Anything to declare?" the Warden asked.

"Saffron," Tam said. "Pepper. Some cinnamon that's seen better days."

The lantern flame stayed orange. The Warden grunted and moved on, and Ulyana exhaled through her nose, slow and even, the way Mother Ysolde had taught her to breathe before fire-work, back in a life that belonged to a girl who no longer existed. *Breath is the first spell,* Ysolde used to say. *Panic is just breath that forgot its manners.*

She wondered, not for the first time, whether Ysolde was still alive, and immediately put the thought away like a letter slipped back into a drawer. Wondering about Thornhollow was a door you didn't open. Behind it were sisters she'd abandoned, a rite she'd refused, and a debt she'd been dodging for twenty years, accruing interest.

* * *

By the deep hours the market thinned to its bones: the onion-fryer banking her coals, the rag-and-bone man stacking his cart, a drunk singing something mournful and off-key about a girl from the salt marshes. Ulyana packed her display knots into their basket, wiped down her table, and allowed herself the small ritual that ended every market night — checking the false-bottomed box, counting her true stock by touch in the dark, whispering the three-word keeping-spell that made the lock remember her hand alone.

The words were old Thornhollow words. They tasted like home, which was the cruelest thing about them.

She was latching the box when she noticed the stranger.

He stood at the edge of the guttering market-light where the Gullet met the river stair, and he was looking at her stall — or through it, more precisely, the way a man looks through a window at a room he half-remembers. Tall, thin, dark-haired, with a coat too fine to be poor and too rumpled to be rich, and a stillness about him that was wrong for a city. People in Cinderhollow moved constantly, defensively, like minnows; this man stood the way furniture stands.

Ulyana knew every face that haunted the night market. This one she had never seen, and yet something about it snagged at her, a hook behind her sternum, some recognition arriving ahead of any reason for it. He turned, and even across forty feet of lamplight and river-damp she saw that his eyes were the color of things found at the bottom of wells.

Then he walked toward her stall, unhurried as the tide coming in, and every instinct Ulyana owned — twenty years of them, bred in the bone — said *look away, pack up, become boring.*

She stayed.

"Your sign says charms," he said. His voice was quiet and roughened, like a door rarely opened.

"Notions," Ulyana corrected, tapping the painted word. "Knots and comforts. Luck, sleep, safe travel. All perfectly ordinary."

"I'm looking for something less ordinary." He set both hands flat on her table, and she noticed his knuckles were scarred, and that he held himself carefully, as if his body were a borrowed coat. "I want a charm against forgetting."

The drunk's song trailed off. Somewhere upstream a bell rang the hour, and the sound crossed the market and died.

"A charm against forgetting," Ulyana repeated, mild as milk. "You mean a memory-aid? Rosemary sachets, I could do. Scholar's knot, maybe."

"No." Those well-bottom eyes didn't blink. "I forget. Everything, from before I was nineteen years old. There's nothing before nineteen. I wake at night tasting ash, and I don't know why, and every year I lose a little more of what's left." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "I'm told — I've been told, sometimes, by people who won't say how they know — that there's a woman at this market who sells things that aren't notions. I want my life back. Name your price."

And there it was. The exact sentence she had trained herself never to hear, spoken by a stranger under a dying lantern, and beneath the fear rising in her throat Ulyana felt something else, unwelcome and warm: *loneliness*, recognizing itself in a stranger's face like a reflection in a window you'd stopped looking into years ago.

Every rule she lived by said refuse him. Send him to an apothecary. Laugh, and call him mad, and be boring, be overlooked, be safe.

"I sell wool knots," Ulyana said, and her merchant's patter came out steady, a masterwork of blandness. "For luck, and fevers, and travelers. If you can't sleep, I recommend valerian from Tam's stall. Good night to you."

Something flickered across his face — not anger. Resignation, worn smooth as sea glass, the expression of a man who had been refused many times and had stopped expecting otherwise. "Of course," he said. "Sorry to trouble you." And he turned toward the river stair, shoulders carrying that careful borrowed-coat weight, and stepped out of the lantern light into the dark.

Ulyana stood at her empty stall with her heart going like a trapped bird, telling herself she felt nothing but relief, and knew, in the place where twenty years of hiding had left a bruise-shaped hollow, that she had just lied to the first honest thing she'd met in a decade.

She watched him go down the river stair until the dark took him.

Then she picked up her box, doused her lantern, and headed for home through streets that smelled of rain coming — and if her thoughts kept circling back to well-colored eyes and the taste of ash, well. Even a woman built on being overlooked was allowed, on the walk home, to feel seen.
