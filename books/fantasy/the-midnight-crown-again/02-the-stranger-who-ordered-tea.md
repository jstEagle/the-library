## 2. The Stranger Who Ordered Tea

There was a particular kind of customer Ulyana Pemberton dreaded more than any Warden: the honest one.

Wardens she could handle. Wardens came through the night market in pairs, boots loud, faces set to a scowl they practiced in front of their mothers' mirrors, and they looked at her stall of knotted thread and dried herbs and saw exactly what she wanted them to see—a woman selling trinkets to the gullible. Honest customers were worse. Honest customers looked at her luck-knots with clear eyes and said things like *my gran swore by these* or *I don't believe in it, but I'll take two*, and then they watched her hands too closely while she tied them, and Ulyana had to perform the whole pantomime of merchant-craft—flourishes that meant nothing, mutters that were only mutters—while keeping the actual working so quiet it was less than a whisper.

The real charm went into a knot the way breath goes into a song. Nobody watching could tell which knot it was. That was the point. That had been the point for twenty years.

It was the third night of Frostwane, cold enough that the brazier sellers were doing brisk trade and even the pickpockets worked with their gloves on, and Ulyana's stall—wedged between Old Marta's candle cart and the pie man whose name changed depending on who owed him money—was doing its usual modest business. Fever-charms for the season. A luck-knot for a fisherman who'd lost his nerve around hooks. A small jar of "soothing tea" that was mostly chamomile and mostly a lie, except for the part where she'd stirred it widdershins and asked it nicely to help.

"Evening, Penny," said the fisherman, because as far as the market knew, her name was Penny Marsh, from upriver, no family, no history, nothing interesting at all. She had spent two decades building the biography of a boring woman, and she took a small, tired pride in how thoroughly she'd succeeded.

"Evening, Tam. Knot holding?"

"Aye. Reeled in a pike big enough to eat my boot." He leaned on the stall, lowering his voice into the register men used when they wanted to seem casual about superstition. "You got anything for... dreams? Bad ones."

"Dreams are just your head taking out the rubbish," Ulyana said, in her merchant voice, bright and slightly stupid. "Try the tea."

He bought the tea. She tied a knot under the counter where no one could see, a small tightening against the dark, and slipped it into the packet, and Tam went away believing he'd been swindled gently, which was the best bargain either of them would ever get.

That was when the stranger sat down.

She hadn't seen him arrive, which bothered her more than she admitted. The night market was a river—you learned to read its currents, who flowed in from the Docks Gate, who eddied near the pie man—and people did not simply *appear* at her stall. But this man was suddenly there, seated on her second stool like he'd been using it all his life, a thin man in a grey coat gone soft at the elbows, with brown hair that needed cutting and a face that was—

Ulyana frowned.

The trouble was that his face was fine. Genuinely fine. Even features, good bones, the kind of face portraitists liked. And yet she kept looking at it the way you look at a room where something's been moved, trying to find what was wrong. It took her a moment to understand: it was his eyes. They were patient and polite and completely unoccupied. Looking into them was like looking into a house where every lamp was lit and nobody was home.

"Good evening," he said. His voice matched everything else about him—quiet, courteous, faintly echoing. "I'm told you sell charms."

"I sell knots and tea," Ulyana said automatically. "The knots are decorative. The tea is delicious. Anything else is between you and your own hopeful imagination."

"That's very neatly put."

"Thank you. It keeps the Magistrate's men bored."

Something flickered across his face—not a smile, but the place where a smile might have lived once, before the furniture was removed. He studied her stall with an odd, careful attention, the way a man studies a language he almost remembers.

"My name is Corin," he said.

"Penny."

"Just Penny? No surname?"

"Marsh. From upriver. No family, no history, nothing interesting at all." She heard herself repeat the little speech and winced inwardly; it was a thing she only did when rattled, and she made a note to be less rattled. "What can I get you, Corin-from-nowhere-in-particular?"

He turned his cupped hands over on the counter, and she saw they were steady—too steady, the steadiness of something held very still on purpose. "A charm against forgetting," he said.

The words landed strangely. Not because the request was unusual—half of Cinderhollow wanted to forget something; the other half wanted to remember—but because of the way he said it. Most people asked for charms against forgetting the way they asked for luck: loosely, hopefully, embarrassed. This man asked the way a drowning man asks for a rope. Precisely. Without embarrassment. With the terrible clarity of someone who has already tried everything else.

"Forgetting what?" Ulyana asked, and immediately wished she hadn't, because it sounded interested, and interest was a door she kept firmly locked.

"Everything," Corin said. "I forget things. Constantly. Small things first—the name of a street I've walked down a hundred times, whether I ate breakfast. Then larger ones. I keep lists. I write everything down." He produced, from inside his coat, a small notebook bound in grey leather, worn soft as cloth at the corners. "But the notes don't feel like mine. Reading them is like reading another man's diary. Useful. Uninhabited."

Ulyana's hands went still on the counter.

Twenty years of hiding taught a person to hear certain frequencies. She could hear a Warden's lie at forty paces. She could hear a curse laid on a doorstep before her boot found it. And she could hear—she was hearing now, faint and wrong, like a bell struck in another room—the sound of something missing in a person. Magic left holes. Sold magic left craters.

There was a crater sitting on her stool, asking politely for tea.

"You want a charm to make you remember," she said carefully, "or a charm to stop you forgetting? They're different workings."

"Do you know," Corin said, and for a moment the empty lamps behind his eyes flickered, "that's the first useful question anyone has asked me in a year. The tea-seller at the corner market told me to eat more fish. The apothecary sold me a tincture that gave me hives. My landlady suggested prayer." He paused. "I don't know the answer to your question. Is there a difference?"

"There's always a difference." Ulyana reached for the kettle out of pure reflex—her hands liked occupation when her mind was busy—and poured two cups of the soothing tea, though he hadn't ordered any and she hadn't offered. He accepted his with a small bow of the head, as if tea were a ceremony he remembered the steps to without remembering learning them.

"The first working finds what's lost and calls it home," she said, hearing her grandmother's cadence come into her voice before she strangled it back down to merchant-flat. "Hypothetically. If such workings existed. Which they don't."

"And the second?"

"The second builds a wall around what's left so nothing else walks off." She wrapped her hands around her cup. Steam curled between them. "The second is easier. Safer. The first—" She stopped. *The first requires knowing what was taken, and by whom, and those are questions that end with bonfires.* "The first is expensive."

"How expensive?"

"Not coin," she said, too quickly, and saw him file the word away in that uninhabited house of his, in a room marked *Penny, tea-seller, knows too much*. Foolish. Sloppy. Twenty years of careful boredom, undone by a thin man with a polite voice and a hole where his self should be.

Corin drank his tea. He drank it properly, appreciatively, and complimented the chamomile, and asked whether the honey was local, and generally conducted himself like the most courteous ghost in Cinderhollow. And underneath all that courtesy, Ulyana felt it again—that hollowed-out quality, that sense of a house swept clean. Except houses didn't sweep themselves. Something had taken a broom to this man, and whatever it was, it had been thorough, and it had not cared about the mess.

"Thank you for the tea," he said, setting down his cup and rising. "And for the question. About the difference. I'll think about it."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"No." He smiled—the full version this time, and it transformed his face so completely that Ulyana's breath caught, and she hated that it caught, and hated more the brief, absurd thought that whoever this man had been before the broom came through, he must have smiled like that often. "You haven't. But you didn't say no, either, and everyone else says no immediately. You're the first person in Cinderhollow who stopped to ask what kind of no it should be." He counted coins onto the counter—too many, she noticed; she'd have to chase him down to return them, which she supposed was the sort of trick a lonely man learned to buy himself five extra minutes of company. "Goodnight, Penny Marsh from upriver."

"Goodnight, Corin-from-nowhere."

He paused at the edge of the lamplight. "Do you know," he said, not turning around, "I have the strangest feeling that we've met before. Isn't that foolish? I'd remember. I write everything down."

And he walked away into the Frostwane dark, grey coat and careful step, and the market river closed over him as if he'd never been there at all.

* *

Ulyana did not sleep that night, which was not in itself remarkable; she slept badly most nights, in the narrow way of people who have trained themselves to wake at boots on cobbles. What was remarkable was what she did instead of sleeping, which was sit at her kitchen table with a single candle and think about a stranger's eyes.

This was dangerous. She knew it was dangerous the way she knew the stove was hot—knowledge worn smooth with handling. Interest was the first splinter. Curiosity was the crack that let the cold in. She had spent twenty years being *Penny Marsh*, who had no past worth mentioning and therefore no curiosity worth feeding, and it had worked, it had kept her neck out of the Warden's noose through three purges and a change of Magistrate, and here she was at two in the morning wondering what kind of working could scoop a man hollow and leave his manners intact.

Because that was the thing she couldn't stop circling. It wasn't theft. Stolen souls left ragged edges—she'd seen the victims of hedge-thieves in her other life, before, in the time she didn't discuss with anyone including herself. People stolen-from were frantic, jagged, grieving without knowing what for. Corin wasn't grieving. Corin was *tidy*. Whatever had emptied him had done it lawfully, contractually, with signatures and witnesses and probably tea.

Sold, then. Sold before he could object. And the buyer had taken the whole estate—the memories, the wants, the boyhood, the name—and left the furniture arranged so neatly you'd never guess the rooms stood empty.

Ulyana put her head in her hands.

She knew a great deal about leaving rooms standing empty. She had made an art of it. There was a girl called Ulyana Pemberton locked up in the back of her mind behind a door she never opened, and outside the door she had built Penny Marsh, brick by dull brick, until even she sometimes knocked at the wrong one. She knew what it cost, living in a house where you weren't allowed to be home. So why, she asked the candle furiously, why did the thought of that hollow-eyed man make her chest ache like a bruise?

*Because,* said the candle, being a candle and therefore honest, *he's the first person you've met in twenty years who's broken in exactly your shape.*

"Oh, shut up," Ulyana told it, and pinched out the flame, and lay in the dark listening to Frostwane rattle the shutters, and did not sleep anyway.

* *

For three nights afterward she caught herself scanning the market river for a grey coat.

She despised this. She despised it with the special intensity available only to people catching themselves in the act of hope. She reorganized her herb drawers twice. She over-tied a luck-knot for a pregnant goat and had to undo it before it made the kid arrive sideways. When Old Marta remarked that Penny seemed restless, Ulyana blamed the weather, and Marta said the weather was lovely, and Ulyana said exactly, that was the problem, and Marta gave her the look that old women reserve for liars they're fond of.

On the fourth night he came back.

Not to her stall. Worse—he came to sit at the pie man's cart, two stalls down, where she could see him perfectly, eating a pie with impeccable manners and writing in his grey notebook. Every few minutes he glanced over. Not staring. Just checking, the way you check a fire you're not sure you banked.

Ulyana lasted eleven minutes.

"This is harassment," she announced, appearing at his elbow with a fresh cup of tea she had absolutely no memory of deciding to pour. "Subtle, well-mannered harassment, but harassment. You return the coins tomorrow or I call a Warden."

"They'd enjoy that," Corin said gravely. "I understand they're underworked."

"Don't joke about Wardens."

"I wasn't joking. That's the difficulty." He closed the notebook. On the open page, upside down, she could read her own stall in his tidy hand—*Penny. Tea. Asks the right questions.* Underlined once. "I apologize. Truly. I'll go, if you like. But I woke last night tasting ash—I do that most nights—and I thought, perhaps, if I sat somewhere warm and ordinary, near the one person who asked me a real question, the ash might taste of something else. And it did." He looked at her with those lit-but-empty eyes, and added, with devastating simplicity: "It tasted of chamomile."

Ulyana stood there holding a cup of tea like a fool, in the cold, in the lamplight, feeling the exact shape of the trap close around her—which was not a trap at all, which was the worst part, which was just a lonely man and a lonely woman and a shared table.

"You're trouble," she said. "You're walking, talking trouble with a notebook. Whatever was done to you, untangling it will involve names and courts and the kind of attention that gets people burned, and I have spent twenty years being aggressively uninteresting, and I would like to continue."

"I know," Corin said. "You mentioned. No family, no history—"

"—nothing interesting at all, yes."

"Mm." He turned the teacup slowly in his long fingers. "May I say something impertinent?"

"You will regardless."

"When you say it, you say it the same way I write my notes. Accurately. Uninhabited." He looked up. "I don't know what happened to me, Penny-who-is-probably-not-Penny. But I know a hidden thing when I sit next to one. We recognize each other, the hollowed-out. It's the only recognition I have left."

The market river flowed on around them. Somewhere a fiddle started up badly. Old Marta laughed at something the pie man said, and the pie man's name tonight was Doran, and the cold smelled of coal smoke and cinnamon, and Ulyana Pemberton stood in the one place in the world she had built to be safe, next to the one person in the world she could not afford, and felt twenty years of invisibility shift like ice on a river.

"Drink your tea," she said at last, roughly, sitting down across from him. "And stop writing about me in that book."

"Why?"

"Because I'm not interesting. Nothing interesting at all."

Corin smiled that transformed smile, and wrote, deliberately, where she could see: *Nothing interesting at all.*

* *

She dreamed that night—or half-dreamed, in the shallow water where she did her sleeping—of a door at the back of her mind. In the dream she was dusting the corridor outside it, the way she did every day, keeping the passage neat, keeping the lock oiled. And beneath the door, faint as a bell struck in another room, she heard paper rustling. Page after page after page, turning.

She woke before dawn with her heart hammering and one thought arriving with horrible clarity, the way thoughts do when they've been waiting all night for you to hold still:

Maren's effects. Her dead mentor's boxes, still unsorted in the cellar, two years after the funeral, because sorting them meant opening the door, and she had promised Maren—no, Maren had made *her* promise, years ago, with dying hands—one thing above all others. One instruction delivered with the last of a hedge-witch's iron:

*Whatever you find in my papers, girl, burn it unread.*

Ulyana lay in the grey light and told herself she would never open those boxes.

She told herself this all the way down the cellar stairs.
