## 3. The Book That Should Have Burned

Mother Hesper had been dead for eleven days before anyone thought to open her cupboards.

Ulyana discovered this on a gray Thursday morning when she climbed the narrow stairs to her mentor's rooms above the dye-shop, carrying a broom she had no intention of using and a basket lined with linen that she intended to fill with everything dangerous. The lock was stiff with disuse — Hesper had spent her last two years mostly downstairs, arguing amiably with the dyers about mordant ratios and cheating at cards — but it turned eventually, the way old things do when you stop fighting them and simply lean.

The room smelled of dust, dried lavender, and faintly of the camphor Hesper had rubbed into her joints every winter since before Ulyana was born. It smelled, in other words, like twenty years ago, and Ulyana stood in the doorway longer than she meant to, letting it.

"Well," she said aloud, because the room deserved some acknowledgment, "you always said I'd be the one to clear this out."

Hesper had said no such thing. Hesper had said, *burn it all and let the Wardens sift the ashes if they must*, which was more characteristic. But dead mentors could not correct their students, and there was a strange comfort in that.

* * *

The trouble with clearing out a hedge-witch's rooms was that everything looked innocent and almost nothing was.

The first hour went to the easy things: three bundles of dried feverfew that were only feverfew; a jar of honey gone to sugar; forty-one buttons, sorted by color into an egg-cup, because Hesper had never met a button she could throw away. Ulyana set these aside for the market's widow-basket. Old Tansy down the lane would weep over the blue ones, and then pretend she hadn't, and that was its own small kindness done.

The second hour went to the less easy things. A knot-charm woven into the bedframe — luck-knots, her own trade, though Hesper's were cruder and older, tied in a style from before Cinderhollow had walls. A sachet of salt and iron filings tucked behind the loose hearthstone where any sensible woman kept one. A letter, half-written, addressed to *My dear fool in Thornhollow*, which Ulyana read twice, decided contained nothing actionable, and burned anyway in the cold fireplace, watching the paper curl with the particular attention she gave all fires these days. Habit. Vigilance. The posture of a life built on being overlooked: you learned to see every object twice, once as what it appeared to be and once as what it would cost you if a Warden lifted it into the light.

By noon she had filled half the basket with things to be quietly destroyed and was working through the bookshelf, which was the worst part, because Hesper's books were the kind of books that got people burned.

Not the herbals — those were legal now, barely, grudgingly, as long as you called them *remedies*. Not the almanacs or the ledgers or the six identical copies of *On the Virtues of Patience*, a gift from some long-dead suitor that Hesper had kept out of spite. It was the others. The slim volumes bound in dyer's leather with no titles on their spines. *A Catalogue of Small Mercies.* *What the River Remembers.* *Nine Ways to Close a Door.* Books that read like poetry until you understood they were instructions, and instructions until you understood they were confessions.

Ulyana handled each one like a sleeping wasp. Into the destroy-pile, one by one. She did not read them. Reading them would mean wanting them, and wanting them would mean keeping one, just one, tucked inside her shirt, and keeping one was how neighbors' daughters got dragged into Warden wagons over candle-spells. She knew the arithmetic. She had lived by it for twenty years.

She was nearly through the shelf — down to the bottom row, the books laid flat because they were too tall to stand — when she saw it.

* * *

She knew it by its cover before she knew it by anything else.

Grey leather, the grey of wood-ash after rain. No title stamped on it, but pressed blind into the front cover, so faint you had to tilt it toward the window to catch it: a crown made of coals, each coal drawn as a small closed eye.

*The Ashen Hours.*

Her hands went cold around it. Not metaphorically — she felt the chill climb her wrists the way it does when the body remembers a danger the mind has filed away, and she sat down hard on Hesper's good chair without deciding to.

She had held this book once before. She had been seventeen, kneeling on the flagstones of the Thornhollow coven-house while the elders stood over her in their grey shawls, and High Witch Maren — not Brenna, not yet, Brenna had still been a girl then too, standing at the back with her fists clenched — had put the book into Ulyana's hands and said:

*Take it to the kiln. Watch it burn to the last leaf. Bring me the ash, and do not read it.*

*Do not read it.* Which was, of course, the surest way ever devised to make an apprentice read it.

And she had wanted to. Gods, she had wanted to. She had carried it across the courtyard feeling it breathe against her ribs like a caught bird, and she had stood at the mouth of the kiln with the heat pushing her hair back from her face, and she had opened it — just opened it, just to the first page, just for a moment —

— and that was when she ran. Not from the coven, exactly. Not only. She had run from the book, from the thing she'd glimpsed in it, from whatever the elders had seen fit to warn her away from rather than explain. She had run from Thornhollow with the ash of a hundred other forbidden pages on her shoes and one page's worth of terrible knowledge already lodged behind her eyes like a splinter.

Twenty years later, sitting in a dead woman's chair in a city that hated her kind, Ulyana Pemberton turned the grey book over in her hands and understood, with a sinking, dizzy clarity, that Mother Hesper had known exactly what she was doing when she left this on the bottom shelf.

*You always said I'd be the one,* she told the empty room again. This time it felt true.

* * *

The sensible thing was to burn it. Right there, in the cold fireplace, with the same matches she'd used on the letter. No kiln needed, no ceremony. Just fire, and ash, and twenty more years of safe small life selling luck-knots under the Wardens' incurious noses.

She even took out the matches. Struck one. Held it to the corner of the cover.

The grey leather did not catch.

She struck another match and pressed it harder, and the flame bent sideways as if in wind that wasn't there, and guttered, and went out. Her hands were shaking. She told herself it was the draft from the window, except the curtain hung perfectly still.

"Oh, don't," she whispered to the book. "Don't you dare."

Because here was the truth she had built her whole adult life on top of, the way you build a house over a well: she had never stopped wondering about *The Ashen Hours*. For twenty years she had wondered. In the space between customers at her stall, walking home through the Ashen Hours themselves — the city's name for the dead hour before dawn, though few remembered why — lying awake listening to Cinderhollow's chimneys tick and settle. What had she seen on that first page? She could almost remember. Something about a crown. Something about a fire going out. And something else, something the elders had gone white and silent about, something that had made a seventeen-year-old girl choose the road over her sisters—

She opened the book.

* * *

It fell open to the middle, the way well-read books do, and Ulyana had a moment of pure animal refusal — *it wants me to look here, so I won't* — followed immediately by the admission that she was going to look there, and that she had been going to look there for twenty years, and that pretending otherwise was just another kind of hiding.

The page was dense with ink, but not with words. Or not only words. The margins crawled with annotations in at least four different hands, generations of readers whispering to each other across a century: *this is wrong, it says north but the star is south*; *M. was right, see the third verse*; *we are sorry, we are so sorry*. The central column held verses in a spidery formal script, and above them, inked with terrible care, were faces.

Portraits. Small, fine-lined, the work of someone who drew the way Hesper tied knots — old craft, older than the city.

The first face was a young man. Handsome in an unremarkable way, the way that makes you look twice precisely because you feel you've seen him before and can't place where. Short dark hair. A jaw with the ghost of a smile in it. His eyes were rendered last, most carefully, and the artist had left them slightly hollow — not empty, but waiting, like windows of a house where someone has just moved out.

Ulyana's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

She knew that face.

She had sold him a charm against forgetting eleven days ago — no. Less. A week, perhaps. The quiet man who came to her stall at dusk and ordered nothing, bought nothing, asked for a charm against forgetting in a voice like a door left ajar, and whom she had refused, because refusing strangers was survival, because charm-work for a stranger was a thread a Warden could pull until her whole hidden life unraveled. He had thanked her anyway. He had smiled the exact smile that was the ghost of a smile in this ink portrait, and he had walked away into the market crowd, and she had spent three sleepless nights telling herself she would forget him.

His face was in a century-old book of prophecies.

She turned the page with fingers that did not feel like hers.

The second portrait was a woman. Young, though the drawing had a quality that made age irrelevant — the artist had drawn her as she might be at seventeen or thirty-seven, both at once. Dark hair escaping a braid. A chin set at an angle Ulyana knew intimately, because she saw it every time she caught her reflection in the dye-shop window and flinched away from it.

Under the woman's portrait, the spidery script read:

*The witch who fled, who hid, who paid.*

Beside the young man's, on the previous page, in the same hand:

*The man whose soul was sold before his breath, whose name the court beneath the mountain holds in trust.*

Ulyana read both lines four times. Then she did the thing she had trained herself never to do in twenty years of careful invisible living: she laughed, one short cracked syllable, alone in a dead woman's room, holding a book that should have burned.

"Paid," she said to the portrait of herself. "Paid what? Paid when?" And then, quieter, in the voice she used for frightened customers: "Who are you?"

The portrait did not answer, but its inked eyes seemed, in the slanting afternoon light, to be looking slightly past her. At the door. As if expecting company.

* * *

She worked until dark. She couldn't have said afterward how much was method and how much was avoidance — the mind is a clever animal and will sort buttons forever rather than face the wolf — but by the time the dye-shop below went quiet and the lamps came up along Gullmarket Row, she had gone through the rest of the shelf and found three more things worth noting.

First: a scrap of Hesper's handwriting tucked into *What the River Remembers*, reading only, *If she comes asking, give her the grey one. She'll know which. She'll hate me. Good.* Dated the spring before last — months before Hesper's final illness, meaning this had been arranged, deliberately, patiently, like everything the old woman did. Ulyana held the scrap over a candle flame and watched it go, and hated her mentor with a fondness that surprised her.

Second: a pressed sprig of rowan between the pages of the almanac, Thornhollow rowan, the silver-barked kind that grew only within sight of the mountain. Someone had sent Hesper a message from home, recently enough that the leaves were still green at the spine. The coven did not send rowan for greetings. They sent it for summons.

Third: nothing. The rest of the shelf was genuinely only books, and this somehow unsettled her most of all, the way an empty stage unsettles before the play begins.

She ate standing at the window, bread and hard cheese from her basket, watching the market square below where her stall would stand tomorrow morning among forty others, where Tansy would haggle over luck-knots and Old Piet would complain about the price of eels and everything would be exactly, carefully, blessedly ordinary. Except that tomorrow she would be looking at every stranger's face for a jaw with a ghost of a smile in it. Except that tonight there was a book in her lap with her own face in it, drawn by a hand a century dead, captioned by someone who knew how her story ended.

Or how it was supposed to end. Prophecies were weather-forecasts, Hesper always said, not sentences. *They tell you which way the wind blows, girl, not whether you sail.* But Hesper had also taught her that the oldest prophecies were less forecasts than invoices — records of bargains struck before anyone now living was born, terms still outstanding, payment still accruing.

*Soul sold before his breath.* Sold to the court beneath the mountain. Every child in Cinderhollow knew the nursery rhyme version — *under hill, under stone, pay your debts and leave your bones* — the way children everywhere know frightening things without knowing they're frightening. She had sung it herself, once, in another life, in a kitchen smelling of rowan smoke, while Brenna drummed her heels against the bench and Maren told them both to hush.

Brenna. Twenty years, no word, and now rowan in the post. Ulyana pushed the thought down where she kept it, in the cellar with the other locked things.

* * *

The lamp guttered low before she finally admitted what she was doing, which was stalling, which was reading the same verse for the ninth time without letting it land. She made herself read it properly. Slowly. Aloud, in a whisper, because some texts will not be understood silently — Hesper's rule, and it held.

*In the ash-grey hours when the crown grows dim,*
*when Ember-that-Waits forgets its hymn,*
*the witch who fled shall meet the man*
*whose soul was sold before it began.*
*Two halves of one unpaid account*
*shall walk the road and find the mount,*
*and what was hidden, hid too long,*
*shall rise unhidden, bright and strong —*

There the verse broke off. The next lines had been scraped away — pumice-stone, from the texture of the damage, deliberate, patient scraping — and in the margin beside the erasure, in that fourth anonymous hand, someone had written:

*Removed for her protection. If you are reading this, it did not work.*

Ulyana sat very still.

Below the window, Cinderhollow settled into its evening noises: cart wheels, a drunk singing, the watch-bell marking the hour. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. The sounds of the life she had built, brick by invisible brick, on the foundation of a night she'd fled and a page she'd never read and a debt she'd assumed belonged to somebody else.

*The witch who fled, who hid, who paid.*

She looked down at the book. At the two portraits facing each other across the gutter, the hollow-eyed young man and the sharp-chinned young woman, separated by a quarter inch of century-old paper. Separated, until eleven days ago, by twenty years and a whole city. She thought of his voice: *a charm against forgetting. Please. I lose a little more every year.*

She had refused him. She had refused him because refusing was safe, and safety was the only thing she owned.

But the book — the book she'd been ordered to burn before she ever saw his face, before she ever came to Cinderhollow, before she had anything anyone could want — the book had known. Had waited on a bottom shelf for a woman with her face to find it, patient as a debt.

Ulyana Pemberton blew out the lamp, wrapped *The Ashen Hours* in linen, and did not put it in the destroy-pile.

She put it inside her coat, against her ribs, where it breathed like a caught bird, exactly as it had at seventeen. And as she locked Hesper's door behind her and started down the stairs toward the lamplit street — toward the market, toward tomorrow, toward a quiet man she now had every reason never to speak to again and only one reason to seek out — she heard, quite distinctly, from the empty room above her, the sound of a match striking.

She did not go back up to check. Some part of her understood that the room was simply finishing what she had declined to do, in its own fashion, in its own time.

The night air smelled of woodsmoke and river. Somewhere under the mountain, something waited for a payment it had been promised before either of them was born.

Ulyana pulled her collar up, became once more a plain charm-seller walking home, and began — terribly, irresistibly — to wonder where a man with no memory would go to buy tea at this hour.
