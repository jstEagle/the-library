## 4. Twenty Years of Small Magic

The night Ulyana ran from Thornhollow, the moon was a fingernail paring in the sky and the coven's great hearth had been lit for the first time in her nineteen years.

That was how she knew. The hearth only burned for rites.

She had spent all day pretending not to notice the preparations — the salt swept into rings around the standing stones, the beeswax candles rendered down from the hive they kept for solemn occasions, the way Mother Ysolde had looked at her over breakfast with an expression Ulyana had never learned to read but had come to dread. Brenna had been worse. Brenna couldn't act to save her life, which was ironic, because acting was precisely what was going to fail to save hers tonight.

"You're quiet," Brenna said, catching up with her at the well, where Ulyana was drawing water she didn't need just to have something to do with her hands.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're *quiet*," Brenna repeated, meaning something else entirely. Her red hair was braided for ceremony already, which told Ulyana everything: the rite would be tonight, and both of them were expected, and there was no more time left to be a coward in private.

Ulyana hauled up the bucket. "Brenna—"

"Don't." Brenna glanced toward the longhouse, where the elders moved behind amber windows like figures in a lantern show. "Don't say anything out loud where the stones can hear you. You know they listen."

The stones did listen. That was rather the whole problem with Thornhollow. Everything listened here — the standing stones, the yew trees, the well itself, which had opinions about bucket technique that Mother Ysolde relayed with tiresome regularity. A coven was not a place so much as a set of ears arranged in a circle, and Ulyana had spent nineteen years being heard.

"Meet me at the birches after supper," Brenna whispered, and walked away before Ulyana could decide whether that was a promise or a warning.

* * *

They met at the birches. They always met at the birches. It was the one place in Thornhollow where the ground was too soft for the roots of listening things, where two girls could sit shoulder to shoulder in the silver light and say what they actually thought, which was mostly that they were frightened and didn't want to be witches the way they were being asked to be witches.

"They'll do us together," Brenna said. She was picking bark off a birch trunk in little curls. "Side by side. So we can't refuse without shaming each other."

"That's clever."

"That's cruel, is what it is. Cleverness is usually cruelty wearing its good coat."

Ulyana almost smiled. Almost. "What did your mother say? When you were chosen?"

Brenna's mother was dead — had died giving the mountain court exactly what it wanted, a bound witch who served until her soul wore thin as old cloth. Everyone in Thornhollow spoke of such women as if they were weather, inevitable and beyond blame.

"She said nothing. She's ash." Brenna flicked a curl of bark into the dark. "Ysolde says her binding was 'fruitful.' Nineteen years of fruitful. Do you know what happens at the end of fruitful?"

"I know what they say happens."

"They lie about the end. That's the part I keep circling like a crow. Not the rite itself — I could bear the rite, maybe, the cold iron and the singing, the way they open you up like a door. It's the lying after. Every elder in that hall will tell us tomorrow that we've been honored. And every year after, when a piece of us goes down the mountain in a jar, someone will tell us that too." Brenna's voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, Ulyana could see her hating it, hating her own fear for showing.

"Brenna." Ulyana reached out and took her hand. Brenna's fingers were cold as riverbed stones. "I'm not going to do it."

The words came out smaller than she'd rehearsed them in her head all afternoon. In her head they had been thunder. Out loud they were barely a draft.

"I know," Brenna said.

"You *know*?"

"I've known since the winter you wouldn't kill the fox. Remember? Old Marrow-tooth, caught in the snare, and Ysolde told you to put it down properly, use it for the working, waste nothing." Brenna turned to look at her, and in the birch-light her eyes were wet and furious. "You cried over that fox for three days and then you went back and freed it anyway, knowing the snare would take its leg. You chose the maimed living thing over the useful dead one. You've been choosing it your whole life, Uly. Tonight they're asking you to stop, and you won't. I've known since winter."

Ulyana's throat hurt. "Then come with me."

There it was. The sentence she hadn't planned, that arrived fully formed like a bird through a window. Come with me. Two words and a lifetime attached.

Brenna laughed — a short, broken sound. "And go where? Be what? We're nineteen years old and the sum total of our education is herbs, hexes, and how to address a stone politely. There's no world outside Thornhollow for people like us except—" She stopped.

"Except hiding," Ulyana said quietly.

"Hiding." Brenna tasted the word. "Small magic. Charms under a market table. Pretending to be nothing." She shook her head. "You'd survive it. That's the terrible thing — you'd be *good* at it. You've been practicing your whole life. Being overlooked is practically your craft already. But me?" She held up her hands, big-knuckled, blunt, the hands of a girl built for loud work. "I burn too bright, Uly. I always have. If I run, they catch me inside a season, and then it's not the rite, it's the pyre, and the pyre doesn't leave anything down the mountain in a jar. It leaves smoke."

"So stay and be bound."

"So stay and be bound." Brenna said it steadily, and that steadiness was the bravest thing Ulyana had ever witnessed. "Someone has to stay. Someone has to be inside when it matters. If we both run, Thornhollow just makes more of us, and none of them ever has a friend on the outside. If I stay—" she shrugged, magnificent and miserable, "—then someday, maybe, when you're hidden and safe and hating yourself for it, there's a sister inside the walls who remembers where the birches are."

Ulyana was crying now. She let herself, briefly, because the birches didn't judge and neither did Brenna.

"The rite is at moonrise," Brenna said. "The east gate watch changes at the second bell. After that, the mist takes the road for an hour — even the stones lose you in it. Go north past the drowned chapel, don't stop, don't look back, and whatever you do, don't leave a letter. Letters are evidence. Leave nothing but absence."

"I hate this."

"I know."

"I'll come back for you. Someday. When I've found a way."

"I know that too," Brenna said. "And Uly — when you find it, make sure the way isn't paved with your own soul. Promise me that much. Whatever bargain you make, whoever you save, don't pay with the thing they're actually hunting."

It was an odd promise to ask for, and Ulyana would spend twenty years understanding why Brenna had thought to ask it.

* * *

The second bell rang across the valley like a dropped coin.

Ulyana went through the east gate carrying nothing but the clothes she stood in, a satchel of dried herbs she'd packed in plain sight for weeks (labeled *FEVER STOCK — FOR THE OUTLYING FARMS*, because no one questioned a girl doing tedious errands), and a stolen candle stub, because her grandmother had always said a witch leaving home should carry her own light and not trust anyone else's.

The mist took the road right on schedule, rolling up out of the low fields like a tide coming in over grass. For one hour, by Brenna's reckoning, even Thornhollow's thousand listening ears would hear only fog.

She made it perhaps half a mile before the hearth-fire flared.

She felt it through her boots — the coven's great hearth leaping up for the rite, a pulse of heat in the bedrock itself, and threaded through it, faint as a voice through a wall, the beginning of the binding song. The elders' voices first, low and patient. Then, unmistakably, Brenna's.

Brenna was singing her part alone.

Ulyana stopped dead in the middle of the road, in the middle of the mist, with the whole dark country ahead of her, and understood: Brenna had gone to the rite early. Had volunteered. Had stood up in front of the assembled coven and offered herself first, alone, so that when the hour came there would be no pair, no side-by-side shame, no lever to pry Ulyana with. She had dismantled the trap from inside it, at cost to no one but herself.

*Someone has to stay.*

Ulyana knelt in the wet road and pressed her palms flat against the earth and did the only magic she allowed herself, the small kind, the kind that hid: she pushed her grief down into the ground like a seed and covered it over, and told herself she would dig it up later, when there was time, when there was safety, when there was some version of the world where digging it up wouldn't kill them both.

She never dug it up. She just kept planting things on top of it.

* * *

North past the drowned chapel, as instructed. The chapel sat in its flooded hollow with water lapping at the third row of pews, saints' faces green with moss beneath the surface, and Ulyana gave it a wide berth because Brenna had once told her that places where holy things drown hold grudges against the living.

By dawn she was in country she didn't know, which was the most frightening freedom she had ever tasted. No stone knew her name. No tree had been taught to report her. She ate blackberries off a hedge and nobody appeared to tell her that taking fruit without a blessing starved the hedge's spirit, and she laughed until she cried, alone on a roadside with purple-stained fingers, at the sheer unbearable permissiveness of the ordinary world.

Cinderhollow found her the way cities find everyone: by rumor. A carter heading south mentioned wages, walls, and a night market where a person could sell nearly anything if she smiled correctly. Ulyana had three copper bits, a candle stub, and a satchel of fever-charms that were genuinely excellent, because Thornhollow might have been many dreadful things but it had never taught her badly.

The trick, she discovered in her first week, was packaging.

A charm sold as a charm got you burned. A charm sold as a *keepsake* — a luck-knot, a woven bracelet, a bit of knotted thread "the way my gran used to make them, no harm in it, missus, just string and good wishes" — got you two coppers and a repeat customer. Fever-charms became "herbal sachets, traditional recipe." Wards against nightmares became "lavender pillows, very restful." She learned the patter, the shrug, the self-deprecating smile that said *I'm nobody, I make trinkets, please forget me immediately.* She learned which Wardens took bribes and which took heads and how to tell them apart by their boot heels on cobbles.

Within a month she had a stall. Within a year she had a reputation among the market folk as a lucky seller — stalls near hers seemed to do better business, fires skipped her corner, the rain parted around her awning with what customers called remarkable consistency. Nobody guessed. Why would they? Magic, in Cinderhollow, was a pyre-thing, a mountain-thing, a story mothers used to frighten children. Nobody looked for it in knots of cheap thread sold by a tired young woman with ink-stained fingers and an unremarkable face.

Especially an unremarkable face. That had taken work too. There were small magics for the face — a dulling charm, a forgettable-ness worked into the skin like oil into leather — and using them felt like swallowing a stone that grew heavier each year. But she wore it anyway, every day, because the alternative was being seen, and being seen, she had learned at nineteen years old in a circle of standing stones, was the same as being taken.

Twenty years passed the way water passes through cloth: everywhere at once and impossible to point to.

She watched emperors rise and fall in the broadsheets. She watched Wardens' numbers grow, then shrink, then grow again. She watched children born in the tenements grow up and buy luck-knots from her for their own children, and she learned their names and forgot them deliberately, because names were attachments and attachments were weight, and a woman planning to outrun her own history traveled light.

But some nights — this was the truth she admitted to nobody, least of all herself — some nights she sat in her narrow room above the dye-shop with her one stolen candle stub (she had bought hundreds since; she still lit that first one each year on the anniversary of her running, a private foolishness) and she took out the seed of grief she'd planted in the Thornhollow road, and she didn't dig it up, but she put her hand flat on the floorboards over it, the way you'd touch a grave.

*Brenna would be High Witch by now,* she'd think. *Or ash. One or the other. Probably the first. She was always the stronger of us.*

*I'll come back for you. Someday. When I've found a way.*

Twenty years of small magic. Twenty years of knots and sachets and lavender pillows and the slow, patient art of being no one. It had worked. That was the miracle and the indictment of it — it had *worked*, it had kept her alive and free and untouched, and if sometimes she woke tasting loneliness like copper at the back of her throat, well. Freedom had a flavor and nobody warned you it was metal.

On the particular evening that ended this long chapter of her life — though she didn't know it yet, and wouldn't for another week — Ulyana Pemberton was arranging her stall for the night market, hanging her luck-knots in their careful rows, when a stranger paused at the edge of her awning out of the rain.

He was quiet-looking. Hollowed-out looking, if she was honest, like a house after the family had gone. He stared at her wares without seeing them, and then his gaze lifted to her face with an expression she hadn't seen directed at her in twenty years.

Recognition. Or something wearing recognition's coat.

"Excuse me," the stranger said. "Do you sell charms against forgetting?"

Ulyana's hands went still over her thread.

Because that was not a question customers asked. That was not a question *anyone* asked — not in twenty years of careful, invisible, unremarkable life. And somewhere beneath the stone she had swallowed at nineteen, beneath the seeds planted over the seed, the buried girl in the wet Thornhollow road stirred and whispered a single word upward through twenty years of soil:

*Finally.*
