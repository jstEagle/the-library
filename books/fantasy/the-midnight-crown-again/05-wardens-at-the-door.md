## 5. Wardens at the Door

The knock came at half past nine, which was wrong on two counts.

First, because nobody knocked on Ulyana Pemberton's door at half past nine. Her neighbors knew she kept late hours after market nights and slept like a stone with a grudge until midmorning. Second, because it wasn't really a knock. It was three measured raps, evenly spaced, delivered by someone who had been trained to knock rather than born knowing how. Ordinary knocks were lopsided things, apologetic or impatient or distracted. This one had been issued by the Magistrate's office.

Ulyana lay in bed and did not move, and in the space of that unmoving second, her mind performed the small cold arithmetic it always performed: *charms under the floorboard, false bottom, third plank from the hearth; the luck-knots are in the flour jar, which is fine, they're just knots unless you know what to look for; the fever-charm is still in my coat pocket—*

The fever-charm. In her coat pocket. Hanging on the hook behind the door. Three feet from whoever was knocking.

She was out of bed and moving before her heart finished its first frightened thump, padding across cold boards, easing the coat off its hook without letting it swing. The charm was a smooth river-stone wrapped in copper wire with a sprig of dried yarrow tucked into the winding—harmless-looking, except that it was warm to the touch, faintly, stubbornly warm, the way nothing natural should be on an autumn night. A Warden's detection-glass could spot warmth like that from across a room.

She pressed the stone between her palms and whispered the word that unmade it—not a spell exactly, more like persuading the magic to lie down and pretend to be gravel—and felt the little warmth gutter out. Then she dropped it into the jar of buttons on the mantel, where it looked exactly as innocent as forty years of other people's discarded buttons.

The knock came again. Same spacing. Same terrible patience.

"One moment," she called, pitching her voice into the flat, slightly put-upon register of Ulyana Pemberton, purveyor of knots and notions, a woman whose greatest ambition was an early night.

* * *

There were four of them on her doorstep, which was excessive even for a sweep. Three Wardens in grey-and-black with the Magistrate's flame stamped on their breasts, and behind them, holding a lantern he clearly wished someone else would carry, stood Mister Hobbes from the corner house, drafted—as all men of property in Cinderhollow were periodically drafted—into the Night Watch Auxiliary, an organization whose primary function was to stand next to Wardens looking uncomfortable.

"Ulyana Pemberton?" The lead Warden was a woman with a face like a folded ledger. Not unkind. Not anything. That was the worst kind of Warden face—the kind that had been emptied on purpose so it could hold whatever the job required.

"That's me." Ulyana hugged her shawl around herself and produced a shiver that was only thirty percent theater. "Is something wrong? Has there been a theft?"

"A sweep, mistress. Standard. May we come in?"

Standard sweeps didn't come in fours, but saying so would be a form of suicide, so Ulyana stepped back and let them fill her narrow entryway like water filling a cup. She noticed everything the way she'd trained herself to over twenty years: the woman's boots were wet to the ankle—they'd already done the houses on the far side of Tanner's Row. The younger Warden kept glancing at the mantel, which meant he was the one carrying a detection-glass, and the glass was in his satchel, not yet drawn. And Mister Hobbes wouldn't look at her at all, which meant he was ashamed, which meant he'd told them something, or failed to refuse when they asked him something.

"Mister Hobbes," she said warmly, "you're up late."

"He's assisting us," the ledger-faced Warden said. "Routine questions about street traffic. You have many visitors at night, Mistress Pemberton?"

"I sell at the night market," Ulyana said. "People visit my stall. It's how commerce works—I'm given to understand."

Something flickered at the corner of the young Warden's mouth. He smothered it. Good. There was hope for him.

The sweep itself was almost gentle. They moved through her rooms with the brisk courtesy of people who had done this a thousand times and found something only once in every hundred. The ledger-woman opened her cupboard. The young one ran his fingers along the floorboards near the hearth—he stopped, for a moment, right at the edge of the false-bottomed section, and Ulyana let her breath go shallow and began composing, mentally, the eulogy for her entire life—before moving on. The glass came out briefly, swept the room, and registered nothing but the ordinary hum of a house: lamp-oil, dried herbs, a cat-shaped absence where a cat used to sit before dying fat and beloved two winters ago.

In the flour jar, eleven luck-knots sat silent as thread. Luck-knots were legal anyway—technically—but legality in Cinderhollow was a door that only swung one way, and Ulyana had no intention of standing in its frame while anyone experimented.

"Clean," the ledger-woman said at last, and then, pausing at the door, added the sentence that made the whole night make sense: "We're working toward the Quill Street end. There was a report. Candle-work, they say. You'll hear it soon enough."

They left. Mister Hobbes lingered a moment on her step, opened his mouth, closed it, and said, "Your shutters need painting, Mistress Pemberton," which in the dialect of frightened neighbors meant *I'm sorry, I said nothing, God forgive us all,* and shuffled away with his lantern.

Ulyana shut the door and stood with her forehead against it.

Then she threw on her coat, stuffed the fever-charm back in the pocket because if trouble was coming to her street she was going to meet it armed, and went out into the dark to see who had been reported.

* * *

The trouble was the Alderys' girl.

Tam Aldery was a cooper with forearms like hams and a laugh you could hear from the fish quay, and his daughter Nettie was fourteen and made of the same wood—all grain and warmth and stubbornness. She'd been coming to Ulyana's stall since she was nine, first for candied ginger and later, shyly, for other things. Last winter Ulyana had taught her three tricks that weren't really magic at all—a knot that eased headaches if you worried it between your fingers, a rhyme for steadying hands, a way of breathing that calmed a panicked animal. Nothing that would warm a detection-glass. Nothing worth burning anyone for.

Nettie, being fourteen, had decided she was ready for more.

Ulyana reached the mouth of Quill Street in time to see the whole ugly tableau lit by torchlight: two more Wardens—it had been six, then, not four; the sweep had split—flanking the Alderys' front step, and between them, in her nightdress with a blanket thrown around her shoulders, stood Nettie Aldery, wrists already bound with regulation cord, her chin up in a way that broke Ulyana's heart precisely because it was defiant instead of terrified. Tam was on his knees on his own doorstep, hands out, voice cracking on the word *please*, and Mrs. Aldery had to be held back by a neighbor, and the candle—

The candle was still smoking on the cobbles. A stub of tallow, carved with marks Ulyana recognized because she had invented a simpler version of them herself. Nettie had tried to make a candle-spell. Something small—Ulyana could read the intent in the carving, a wish for her father's bad knee, the same knot-trick translated clumsily into wax. Fourteen years old and she'd wanted to fix her da's knee, and somewhere in the house there must have been a cousin or a lodger with a grudge or just a hunger for the Warden's informer's fee, because now there were cords on her wrists and a wagon waiting at the end of the street.

"She's a child," Tam was saying, over and over, in the voice of a man who has discovered that the word has no weight in certain rooms. "She's a child, she's a child—"

"It's not the age, it's the act," said the older Warden, wearily, the way a man recites a tariff schedule. "Candle-work carries assessment and registration. If she cooperates, she goes to the Hall of Assessment, not the pyre. Nobody's burning anybody tonight."

*Assessment.* Everyone in Cinderhollow knew what assessment meant. The ones who passed came back hollow-eyed and careful, having traded some piece of themselves for permission to exist. The ones who didn't pass—the ones whose gift ran too deep, too wild, too much like the hedge-craft the Magistrate had spent a century drowning—didn't come back at all. Nettie had a real touch; Ulyana had seen it months ago, in the way the girl's headache-knots worked better than they should, in the way sick animals went calm around her. Nettie would not pass assessment. Nettie would be *interesting* to them.

And Ulyana had taught her the first three steps of it. Had watched the girl's eyes light up and said, *that's enough, that's all you need, don't go further,* and gone home satisfied that caution had been transmitted. As if caution could be handed over like change from a coin.

She could feel the fever-charm in her pocket, warm again, waking at her pulse. She could feel the older, deeper thing underneath her skin, coiled and patient, the thing she hadn't used in twenty years. She could end this. A word to snuff the torches. A gesture to rot the binding cords. Twenty seconds of real magic and the Wardens would be staggering blind and the Alderys' family would be running for the ash country and Ulyana Pemberton would never sell another knot in Cinderhollow as long as she lived, because they would hunt her until the ends of the earth, and when they caught her—and they would catch her—there would be a pyre with her name on it, and worse, there would be reprisals, sweeps doubled, quotas filled, every hedge-touched soul on this street dragged in to pay for her flash of heroism.

That was the arithmetic. She'd done it a thousand times in the dark. Safety bought invisibility; invisibility cost silence; silence cost everyone else everything.

It had never before cost anyone standing right in front of her.

"Please," Tam Aldery said, and turned, and saw her. For one naked moment his eyes held hers—Tam, who bought her ginger wine every market night and joked about her prices and had never once suspected what she was—and there was no accusation in them, which was somehow worse than accusation. He just looked at her the way everyone looked at everyone, at a neighbor, at nobody, and turned back to the Wardens and begged.

Ulyana's hand closed around the fever-charm until the copper wire bit her palm.

The wagon rolled. Nettie looked back once, not at her parents—at the crowd, at the faces, memorizing them or saying goodbye to them, Ulyana couldn't tell—and then she was gone around the corner with the torchlight, and the street exhaled into that particular silence that follows a raid, doors opening one by one, people gathering up their children, nobody meeting anybody's eyes.

Mrs. Aldery sank down on the step beside her husband. Ulyana walked over. She had nothing. That was the whole truth of it; she had crossed the street with empty hands because everything she owned that could help was hidden in floorboards and flour jars, buried alive, useless.

"I'll ask at the Hall tomorrow," Ulyana heard herself say. "About visiting. About—what the process is. I know someone who clerked there."

It was even true. It was also nothing, and they both knew it was nothing, and Mrs. Aldery nodded anyway and gripped her hand hard enough to hurt, and Ulyana stood there being comforted by a woman whose daughter had been taken while she herself had been found clean, and the guilt went through her like a blade going through soft bread.

She walked home past her own dark windows, and inside, she didn't sleep. She sat at the table with the button jar, turning the dead fever-charm over and over, and thought about the shape of her life: twenty years of small safe hiding, each year buying the next, the bill always sent to someone else's house.

Toward dawn, she finally banked the lamp. And that was when she remembered the stranger from the market—the quiet man who had asked her for a charm against forgetting, whose order for tea had been the strangest thing she'd heard all season—and the thought arrived fully formed, unwelcome as a debt collector:

*If they can take Nettie for a candle-spell, what do you imagine they'd do with him?*

And beneath it, quieter, in the voice she used for truths she didn't want:

*And why, exactly, do you care this much about a man you refused and haven't seen since?*

She blew out the lamp and lay in the dark listening to Quill Street settle, and resolved—for the hundredth time in twenty years—that she would find out whether the Hall of Assessment allowed visitors, and whether a certain hollow-eyed stranger drank tea anywhere regular, and she told herself these were separate resolves.

They were not separate resolves.
