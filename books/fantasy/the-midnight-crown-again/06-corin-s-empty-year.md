## 6. Corin's Empty Year

The rain had been coming down for three days, and Ulyana had run out of excuses to stay indoors.

She told herself it was business. The night market didn't care about weather; the night market cared about fever-charms and dry luck-knots, and there was always someone willing to pay three coppers to keep their grandmother's cough from turning. But when she set up her stall under the awning that leaked in exactly one spot—the same spot, always, as if the canvas held a grudge—she found herself watching the tea-house across the lane instead of her customers.

He was there again. The quiet man. Third evening running.

He sat by the window with a cup of something he never seemed to drink, and he watched the street the way she watched it: not with curiosity but with inventory. Counting exits. Noting faces. She knew that look because she wore it every day of her life, had worn it so long it had worn itself into her bones like water into stone.

"Your turnips are wilting, love," said Marta from the pie stall next door, who was not actually selling pies but stolen candles, because this was Cinderhollow and nothing was what it appeared to be except the rain.

"They're decorative turnips."

"They're sad turnips."

"Everything in Cinderhollow is sad. It's called atmosphere."

Marta snorted and went back to arranging her illegal wares, and Ulyana went back to pretending she wasn't staring at a man whose face she'd seen inked on vellum in a book she should have burned twenty years ago.

*The witch who fled and the man whose soul was sold.*

She'd spent two nights telling herself it was coincidence. Faces repeated. Ink smudged. A hundred-year-old prophecy book could hardly be expected to have good references. And yet here he was, drinking tea he didn't want, in the rain, three nights running, and every time she looked at him something behind her ribs went tight and cold, like a hand closing around a key.

The rain thickened. Her customers thinned. And then the bell over the tea-house door chimed, and the quiet man stepped out into the wet, and walked—not toward the main road home, not toward any road at all, but directly across the lane to her stall.

Ulyana's hands did something clever and automatic, sweeping the more obvious charms beneath a cloth before her brain caught up.

"Evening," said Corin.

"Turnip?" she said, because her mouth was a traitor.

He looked at the turnips. "No, thank you."

"Pity. They're going fast."

"It's raining on them."

"Atmosphere."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile might live if it had ever been invited inside. "You sold me a fever-charm two weeks ago. At the market by the tannery."

"I sell a lot of charms."

"You told me it would work better if I believed in it, and then you said, 'But between us, it works fine either way, I just say that part so people stop arguing with me.'"

Ulyana stared at him. "I don't remember saying that."

"No one remembers saying things to me," he said, without drama, without self-pity, the way a man reports the weather or the price of bread. "It's all right. It's rather the point of me."

And there it was again. That cold tight thing behind her ribs.

"What can I do for you?" she asked, carefully.

He was quiet long enough that the rain filled the silence, drumming on the awning, dripping off its grudge-spot onto a turnip that did, admittedly, look miserable.

"The charm against forgetting," he said. "That you refused me."

"I remember refusing you." *Of course I do. I've thought about it every day since.* "I told you forgetting isn't my trade."

"You told me a lot of things. You told me memory isn't something you can buy. You were very kind about it, actually. Kindness is—" He stopped. Started again. "People aren't usually kind to me. They're polite, or they're frightened, or they're both. You were neither. So I've been thinking about why."

"That's a lot of thinking to do over tea."

"I drink a lot of tea."

"Do you ever drink it?"

He glanced down at his cup, which he'd carried out into the rain with him, absurdly. "No," he admitted. "I forget."

* * *

Corin had learned that the world came in two kinds of pieces: the ones before nineteen, which belonged to someone else, and the ones after, which belonged to him only loosely, like a coat borrowed from a generous friend.

He had no memories of childhood. Not faded ones, not blurred ones—none. Other people spoke of mothers, of scraped knees, of the smell of a particular kitchen, and he nodded along the way you nod at a story about a country you'll never visit. He'd constructed an early life out of other people's anecdotes the way a poor man builds a house from salvage. He was fairly sure he'd had a dog, because everyone had a dog, and it felt correct to grieve one.

He woke every night at the same hour—it varied, but the *hour* was constant, somewhere in the small dark—and tasted ash. Not metaphor ash. Actual ash, gritty on his tongue, bitter at the back of his throat, as though he'd been breathing smoke in some room he couldn't find. He would lie there until dawn with the taste fading and a feeling underneath it that he had finally, after years of searching for words, settled on:

Homesick.

For a place he had never been. Grieving a life he had never lived.

Doctors had opinions. Priests had stronger ones. A Warden clerk had once written *melancholia* on a form and charged him a fee. But none of them could explain the one detail Corin had never told anyone, the detail he carried like a stone in his shoe:

He had no reflection worth mentioning.

Oh, mirrors showed him something—a shape, a suggestion, a man-colored blur where a man should be. Barbers worked from memory and got his hair wrong every time. Once, drunk on cheap wine, he'd stood before a tavern mirror for an hour trying to catch his own eye, and the glass had given him nothing but fog and the candlelight behind him.

He had stopped looking. That was easier. There were a great many things that were easier, and all of them together added up to a life that was very quiet and very light, as though most of his weight had been removed without consulting him.

Except lately. Lately there was the charm-seller with the sharp eyes and the defensive turnips, and when she'd refused him—kindly, firmly, like a door closing on a warm room—he had felt something he hadn't felt in nineteen years:

Seen.

Not watched. *Seen.* As though she'd looked through the borrowed coat straight at whatever was or wasn't underneath.

So he'd come back. Three nights running, drinking tea he couldn't taste—another small theft, that; food barely registered either, flavor arriving secondhand like news from abroad—and working up to whatever it was he intended to say tonight, standing in her stall in the rain, holding a cold cup.

"You really don't taste anything," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd been studying him while he studied the rain.

"Bread is texture. Wine is warmth. Tea is—" He turned the cup. "Wet."

"And you wake up at night tasting ash."

His head came up sharply. "I never told you that."

"You didn't have to." Her eyes had gone strange—wide and fixed, the way a person looks at a fire they're trying to decide whether to fight or feed. "Corin. What year did you say your memory starts?"

"Nineteen. Why?"

"How old are you now?"

"Thirty-eight."

She closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet, which made no sense, because it was he who ought to be weeping, if anyone, and he had somehow never learned how.

"Come inside," she said. "Not the tea-house. My rooms, above the shop. There are things I need to show you, and I'm not doing it in the rain with Marta listening, because Marta hears everything and repeats half of it, and the half she repeats is always the dangerous half."

"Marta sells pies."

"Marta sells nothing. Come on."

* * *

Her rooms were small and warm and smelled of dried herbs and lamp oil and, faintly, of something older—woodsmoke that had soaked into the walls long ago. Books everywhere. Bundles of dried flowers hanging from beams. A cat, enormous and orange, who took one look at Corin and left the room with the air of a professional declining a case.

"Forgive Bramble," Ulyana said. "Cats know things. He'll be back when he's finished being offended."

"He seemed to object to me specifically."

"Cats object to lots of things. Sit."

He sat. She lit lamps—one, two, three—until the little room glowed amber, and then she stood in the middle of it wringing her hands like a woman about to confess to a crime, and he thought, absurdly, *she looks how I feel.*

"I'm going to tell you something," she said, "and you're going to think I'm mad, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide. Can you do that?"

"I have a lot of practice listening to things I don't understand."

"This isn't like the doctors' things."

"No," he agreed quietly. "I don't think it is."

She took a breath, let it out, and said: "There's a book. A hundred years old. Prophecies. My teacher ordered me to destroy it when I was an apprentice, and I—I kept it instead. Hid it. And two weeks ago I opened it, and I found a page with two faces drawn on it in ink that hasn't faded, and one of them is yours."

The lamp-flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"The other is mine," she went on, faster now, words tumbling like water over a broken weir. "And there's writing underneath. Old verse. It says—there are names in it, or near-names, titles—and it calls us 'the witch who fled' and 'the man whose soul was sold to the court beneath the mountain before he was born.' Before. He was. Born."

The silence after was total. Even the rain seemed to hold its breath.

Corin found that he was calm. This happened sometimes—calm arriving at wildly inappropriate moments, like an uninvited guest who brought excellent wine. "Show me," he said.

She hesitated. Then crossed to a loose stone by the hearth, and drew out a book wrapped in oilcloth, old and soft-cornered, and laid it on the table between them, and unwrapped it the way you unwrap a wound.

The page was exactly as she'd described. Two faces, inked in fine lines. His own countenance looking back at him from paper a century old—every line of it, the brow, the jaw, the particular set of the mouth he saw (or failed to see) in mirrors. Beside it, younger, rounder-faced, unmistakably her.

And beneath, in a spidery hand:

*The witch who fled shall hide her flame in plainest sight,*
*And he whose soul was sold shall walk unfelt, unlit,*
*Till ash upon his tongue leads ash to ash again,*
*And what was bought in darkness must be returned to it.*

Corin read it twice. Three times. The calm held, mostly.

"My mother," he said slowly. "Whoever she was. Someone paid something. Sold something. Before I was born."

"Soul-debt," Ulyana whispered. She'd gone gray. "My teacher spoke of it once, only once—old bargains, old courts. A family in ruin buys its survival from something beneath the mountain, and pays in the child's soul. The child grows up hollowed. Unreflected. Untasted." She gestured at his teacup, still clutched in his hand like a talisman. "*Untasted*, Corin. Your whole life has come to you secondhand because the first-hand part—the you part—is elsewhere. It's in a jar somewhere under a mountain, and it has been since before you drew breath."

Somewhere in the walls, the house ticked and settled. Bramble reappeared in the doorway, regarded Corin with yellow eyes, and this time did not leave.

"The ash," Corin said. "Every night."

"A trail," she said. "Or a tether. Something pulling you home—or pulling *it* home. I don't know. I'm not—I was never trained for this. I ran away from the training. Twenty years ago. That's the other thing you should know about me. I'm a coward with good handwriting."

"You don't seem like a coward."

"Nobody does, from the outside. That's rather the point of cowards." She laughed, a small cracked sound, and pressed her palm flat against the prophecy page, over her own inked face. "Do you know what the worst part is? All these years I thought hiding was freedom. I built a whole life on being overlooked. Small magic, small lies, small safety. And it turns out the book had my number the entire time. Some crone a century dead wrote me into a corner and I walked into it willingly, one careful step at a time."

Corin set down the cup. He reached across the table—slowly, giving her time to pull away—and laid his hand over hers, on the page, over both their faces.

"I woke up this morning," he said, "tasting ash, in a rented room, in a city where nobody remembers talking to me. And I thought: nineteen more years of this. Thirty. However long. And then I walked past your stall and you remembered something I said, and your turnips were sad, and for the first time in my life I wanted to know what happens next."

"That's a low bar. Wanting to know what happens next."

"It's the highest bar there is, if you've never cleared it."

Ulyana looked at their hands, stacked on a hundred-year-old page, at the inked faces beneath, and felt something shift in her chest—the cold tight thing loosening, or maybe just changing shape. Not dread anymore. Dread's stubborn cousin, the one that shows up at funerals and refuses to leave until something gets decided.

"Two weeks ago I was a turnip merchant," she said. "Now I'm apparently a figure of prophecy, sitting in a lamplit room with a soulless man, about to do something incredibly stupid."

"When?"

"When what?"

"When are we going to do it?"

She huffed a laugh despite everything. "I haven't even said what it is."

"You were going to. Your face does a thing when you're about to be brave. It goes very still, like a rabbit deciding."

"My face does not—"

"It's doing it now."

She pulled her hand free and covered her face with it, and for a moment she simply breathed, and he waited, because waiting was the one skill nineteen empty years had taught him well.

"There's a coven," she said at last, through her fingers. "In the mountains. Thornhollow. They raised me. They'll know what the verse means—all of it, the court, the debt, the fire. And they will be *thrilled* to see me, because I left in the middle of the night twenty years ago and broke a promise that I still don't fully understand." She dropped her hand. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely resolute. "Going back means Wardens, trackers, witches with grudges, and a mountain full of things that eat souls. Staying means knowing what I now know, and lying awake every night listening to you taste ash across town."

"So it's not much of a choice."

"It's a terrible choice. Those are the only honest kind." She stood, and began pulling books from shelves with sudden fierce purpose, stacking them on the table. "We leave within the week. I have to burn my stock, close the stall, write letters I won't send. And you—" she pointed at him, and her voice gentled despite itself— "you need to tell me everything. Every fragment. Every dream. The ash, the hour, all of it. If your soul is calling you home, then home is where we're going, and I want to arrive knowing the way."

Corin looked around the warm little room—at the books, the herbs, the offended cat now rubbing against his ankle as if reconsidering, at the woman dismantling her whole hidden life with the expression of someone tearing off a bandage in one go.

"All right," he said. "But it's not much of a story. Nineteen years of borrowed coat."

"It doesn't have to be much of a story," Ulyana said. "It just has to be true. True stories are the only kind that end properly. I read that somewhere."

"Where?"

"In a book I was supposed to burn."

Outside, the rain kept falling on Cinderhollow, patient as a creditor. Inside, the lamp-flames stood tall and steady, and on the table between them, the inked faces of a witch and a hollowed man waited in their century-old verse for the next line—

which, though neither of them knew it yet, was already being written somewhere far away, in a high cold tower, by a crow-winged courier carrying a letter sealed with black wax, addressed in a hand Ulyana would know anywhere: *Brenna.*
