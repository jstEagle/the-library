## 7. The Prophecy Read Backward

The rain came at dusk and did not stop, drumming on the shutters like a creditor who knew she was home. Ulyana lit three candles—two for light, one for courage—and set *The Ashen Hours* in the middle of her kitchen table, where it sat like a cat that had decided to stay.

She had been circling it since morning.

There were honest ways to spend a rainy day. There was mending to do, a kettle to fill, a second pot of tea already going cold because she kept forgetting it existed whenever her eyes drifted back to the book. She had dusted shelves that didn't need dusting. She had reorganized her charm-box by color, then by purpose, then alphabetically, which was a thing she had never once done in twenty years of careful, deliberate mediocrity. A woman who wanted to be overlooked did not alphabetize. Alphabetizing invited attention.

Finally she sat down, pulled the book toward her, and said aloud, to the empty room: "You are a book. Books can be burned."

It did not look frightened. Old books never do. They have outlasted everyone who ever feared them.

*The Ashen Hours* was bound in leather gone the color of wet slate, stitched with thread that might once have been red. Her mentor Maren had pressed it into her seventeen-year-old hands on the worst night of her life, with the coven's horns still sounding behind her, and had said only: *Burn this, child, and don't read it first.* Ulyana had carried it four days through the ash country, found a hearth in a waystation inn, held it over the coals—

—and hadn't been able to.

She'd told herself it was caution. A book that heavy with prophecy might not burn politely; might scream, or spark, or remember her name. That was the story she'd lived inside for two decades. But tonight, turning the cover, she admitted what she'd known all along: the fire had never been the obstacle. Her hands were.

The pages smelled of cedar smoke and something older, something like the air in a cellar before the storm comes down the chimney. The script was Thornhollow hand—cursive grown from the same root as her own—and it was written backward.

Not mirrored. Backward. The way you'd write if you expected someone to read over your shoulder and wanted them to waste their life trying. Maren's little joke, probably. Maren had loved jokes that took years to land.

Ulyana fetched her hand-mirror from the bedstead, propped it against the teapot, and began.

* * *

Reading prophecy in a mirror by candlelight is slow work, and the rain made it slower, filling the pauses with its patient gray insistence. The first verses were the ones every apprentice in Thornhollow could recite in their sleep—the ones about the crown of midnight, the fire under the mountain, the hours turned to ash. Nursery-rhyme stuff. Warnings wrapped in rhythm so children would carry them without knowing they carried anything at all.

But the middle sections were different. These weren't the public verses. These were marginalia, corrections, a scholar's furious argument with the poem itself. And in the margins, in a hand Ulyana recognized with a lurch—Maren's hand, younger, sharper—the real book lived.

She read:

*The fire is not a furnace. It is a door held shut.*

She read it twice, because the first time her mind slid off it like rain off oiled canvas.

Everyone in Cinderhollow knew the story they told children: there had been dragons once, and men had warred on them, and one dragon remained beneath Mount Cindral, sleeping on its hoard, and its breath was the warmth in the deep places of the world. A fairy tale with geology in it. The Wardens used it too, in their sermons—*the old fires must be tended by the righteous, not meddled with by witches*—which had always struck Ulyana as the Magistrate's way of claiming ownership of a thing no living person had ever seen.

But Maren's margin said otherwise, and Maren had stood closer to the truth than anyone Ulyana had ever known.

*Ask what the fire keeps out, not what it keeps in,* the margin note insisted, underlined three times, the third underline torn slightly through the paper as though the pen had been pressed in anger. *The dragon is not the prisoner. The dragon is the lock.*

Ulyana sat back. The candle flames leaned in the draft from the window, straightened, leaned again.

Beneath the mountain, then. Beneath the court whose existence the Magistrate denied and every hedge-witch whispered about. Something sealed. Something that needed a fire the size of a sleeping god to keep it sealed. And the verse that followed, in the original backward-running script, laid it out with the terrible tidiness of an invoice:

*When the last fire gutters low,*
*the hungry hours come up slow.*
*A sold soul must turn for home,*
*or all beneath shall be undone.*

She wrote it forward in her commonplace book, mirror balanced on her knee, and looked at the words until they stopped looking like words.

A sold soul must turn for home.

Her eyes went, without her permission, to the page near the end—the illumination she had seen once and then spent three days pretending she hadn't. Two figures inked in faded vermilion and gold leaf worn to a ghost. A woman with dark hair and a charm-knot at her wrist. A man whose face—

Whose face belonged to a quiet stranger who had ordered tea at her stall eleven nights ago and asked for a charm against forgetting.

Ulyana got up, walked to the window, watched the rain, and came back. This was a technique she recommended: leave the frightening thing, let it sit, return to find it exactly as frightening but slightly more familiar, the way a bruise stops throbbing and starts merely aching.

The margins around the two figures were dense. Maren—or whoever had annotated before Maren—had numbered the figures. *The witch who fled.* *The soul sold before his birth.*

"The witch who fled," Ulyana told the teapot, "could be anyone. Half the hedges in the ash country have run from something." The teapot declined to comment. She poured the cold tea out into the slop bucket anyway, a small ritual execution of denial.

Except.

Except that she had fled. Except that she had refused a binding rite that would have given her soul to the mountain court, and the prophecy's other figure was a man whose soul had been sold to the mountain court before he drew breath, and except that Corin—as she'd learned from the market gossip, that great accurate institution—was a man with no past, no family record, no reflection anyone could vouch for. Except that when he'd stood at her stall asking for a charm against forgetting, she had felt the hollow in him the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue: constantly, involuntarily, against all sense.

And now the prophecy said the seal was failing, and the fix was a sold soul coming home, and somewhere in the arithmetic of that, whether she liked it or not, she was standing in the equation holding the chalk.

* * *

She worked until the candles guttered and had to be replaced, and then until those guttered too. Around midnight she discovered why the book felt unfinished even in its finished state: the verses ran backward, yes, but some passages also ran in layers. Hold the mirror at an angle to the flame and a second text surfaced in the gutter shadows—faint brown ink, older than the black, written between the lines like roots between stones.

This older layer was not poetry. It was accounting.

*Ember-that-Waits fed: 3 souls, springtide.*
*Seal at the western throat: thinning. Patched with song, holds.*
*The queens grow careless. They take souls for ornament now, not need.*

Ulyana read the word *queens* and felt the hair rise on her arms. Plural. As if courts beneath mountains had histories, dynasties, politics—as if the thing under Cindral were not a monster in a hole but a *country*, with rulers who could be careless the way any ruler is careless: comfortably, at other people's expense.

There was a date beside the last entry. Not a year in any calendar she knew—a count. *Ninety-one years since the founding line fell.* And below it, in fresher ink, Maren's hand again, hurried:

*Ninety-nine years now. One hundred, they say, breaks the pattern. I do not know what breaks. Neither does anyone. That is the problem with patterns: they are only terrifying after they break.*

Ulyana did the sum the way you touch a sore tooth. Ninety-one plus ninety-nine. The founding line had fallen within living memory of the book's writing; the book was old; the count was now at—one hundred and ninety-something? No. She caught herself, breathed, started over. The count in the margin was relative to the entry above it. Which meant the total, tonight, sitting in her kitchen with the rain coming down, was—

One hundred and ninety-nine. Give or take a year. Give or take, possibly, a season.

One hundred, Maren's note had worried, breaks the pattern. What did two hundred do?

The candle nearest her sputtered in a draft that wasn't there. Ulyana told herself firmly that old houses settled, that rain made chimneys behave strangely, that she was forty-one years old and too sensible for shivers. Then she banked the fire, put the kettle on out of pure nervous habit, and went back to the table, because the alternative to knowing was worse than knowing, and hiding—from this, at least, here, alone, with no Wardens and no neighbors—had finally stopped working.

That was the thing about twenty years of practice at being unseen. You got very good at seeing.

* * *

Toward the small hours she found the passage she would spend the rest of her life wishing she'd found last.

It was in the layered ink, the old brown account-keeping, wedged between a note on salt-iron prices and a complaint about the taste of cave-water. Just three lines. She copied them forward carefully, mirror steady, hand less so:

*The fire eats what it is given and asks for more.*
*Given nothing, it will eat the seals instead.*
*The seals hold back the hours that were never born.*

The hours that were never born.

She sat with that a long while. Outside, the rain had softened to a hiss, the sound of a city exhaling in its sleep. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and thought better of it. Ordinary sounds. Sounds that assumed tomorrow.

Because that was what she suddenly understood, with the clarity that arrives uninvited at three in the morning wearing muddy boots: the starving things under the mountain were not creatures, not in the way a wolf is a creature or even the way a dragon is. They were *hours*. Times that should have happened and didn't—deaths postponed past their appointment, endings unwritten, debts deferred for two hundred years of borrowed firelight. Everything the dragon-fire had bought the world above, it had bought on credit. And the things beneath were the creditors. Patient ones. They had waited this long; they could wait out a dying flame the way winter waits out a bad roof.

Unless a sold soul returned home.

She looked at the illuminated page again. The woman with the charm-knot. The man with the hollow where his childhood should have been. *The witch who fled. The soul sold before his birth.*

"I don't want this," she informed the book, conversationally, the way you'd inform a landlord. "I have a stall. I have a system. I have a neighbor who thinks I'm mildly boring, which took me *years*."

The book, being a book, offered no rebuttal, which was somehow more ominous than if it had argued.

She tried the escape routes one by one, the way you try doors in a dream. Perhaps the figures were symbolic. Perhaps *sold* meant something else—metaphorical, spiritual, a state of mind. Perhaps the count was wrong. Perhaps another sold soul existed somewhere, some poor wretch in a far province who could be pointed downhill with a map and a pat on the shoulder. Perhaps—

Perhaps Corin had come to her stall for a reason, and perhaps the reason was the same one that had kept a book unburned for twenty years: that some things refuse to be destroyed, and some people refuse to be overlooked, and the two refusals were, tonight, in her small kitchen, becoming the same refusal.

She closed the book. Opened it again. Closed it. Opened it. On the final open, the last verse lay facing her, the one every Thornhollow child knew, the one that had always sounded like comfort:

*When midnight crowns the longest night,*
*the ash shall wake, the dark take flight.*

Comfort, she realized now, reading it backward through everything she'd learned tonight, was just terror with better manners. The verse didn't promise the dark would flee. It promised the ash would wake. Waking things could wake either way up.

The kettle shrieked. Ulyana jumped hard enough to knock the mirror flat, swore at it, righted it, and made tea she did not want because her hands needed employment. She drank it scalding, standing at the window, watching rain silver the empty street.

A season. The margins gave her a season, maybe two, before the count reached its breaking number and the fire sank below what the seals required. A season to decide whether a nursery rhyme and a dead woman's furious marginalia were enough to walk toward a mountain that ate souls—her soul, specifically, the one unbound, unbought, unbargained thing she had spent her whole adult life keeping off every ledger in the world.

And a season to find a hollow-eyed man who drank his tea without sugar and asked charms to help him forget, and tell him that the forgetting had a shape and a seller and a home, and that home, according to a book that should have burned, was waiting for him back.

"He'll think I'm mad," she said to the rain. "I'd think I was mad. I *do* think I'm mad."

The rain said nothing, but it had stopped hammering and begun simply falling, which felt like agreement, which felt worse.

She went to bed with the book on the chair beside her, not the shelf. In the dark she lay listening to the house settle, and just before sleep took her, uninvited and inevitable as everything else tonight, one thought arrived and stayed:

If the prophecy had found her face in its pages, then the pages had known where she'd be. Which meant the burning she'd failed to do at seventeen hadn't been cowardice at all.

It had been the first instruction she'd ever followed correctly.
