## 8. Brenna's Letter

The crow arrived on a Tuesday, which Ulyana would later decide was typical of Brenna. Her sister-apprentice had always had a gift for dramatic timing wrapped in bureaucratic packaging.

It landed on her stall's counter with the heavy thump of a bird that considered itself a courier of some importance, scattering three luck-knots and a small dish of fever-charms shaped like acorns. It then fixed Ulyana with one glossy black eye and waited, with the unmistakable patience of something that had been paid in advance.

"Oh no," Ulyana said.

The crow blinked.

"No," she said again, more firmly, as though repetition might renegotiate reality. "You are not from where I think you're from."

The crow hopped once, deliberately, onto the center of her counter—the spot reserved for paying customers—and produced, from somewhere about its person, a folded square of paper sealed with grey wax.

Ulyana looked at it for a long moment.

Around her, the night market went on with its usual clatter and steam. Old Marta was arguing with a fishmonger two stalls down about the theological implications of eels. The lamplighter's boy had fallen asleep against the cider cart again. Somewhere beyond the market's edge, Warden boots clicked past on their evening rounds, unhurried, bored, dangerous in the way that only routine danger is.

Twenty years, she thought. Twenty years without so much as a raven's feather crossing my threshold.

And now this.

She picked up the letter the way you pick up something that might be hot, or cursed, or both. The wax seal bore a sigil she hadn't seen since she was seventeen years old and running through Thornhollow's gates with her shoes in one hand and her heart in her throat: a thorned circle, open at the top. A crown made of brambles. The mark of the coven that had raised her.

The crow watched her break it.

*Ulyana.*

Just her name, at first, in handwriting that had changed and somehow hadn't—Brenna's letters still leaned too far to the right, like they were trying to get somewhere faster than the words allowed.

*I'll keep this brief, because I know you, and I know that if I give you six pages you will find a way to read none of them.*

Ulyana made an indignant sound. The crow made one back.

*The fire beneath the mountain is dying. You may have heard whispers; you have certainly heard less than we have, because unlike some people, we did not spend two decades pretending the world ended at Cinderhollow's north gate.*

"Charming," Ulyana muttered. "Two decades of not being burned alive, but yes, let's call it pretending."

*I won't pretend we parted well. I won't pretend I understood you then, either. But here is what I understand now: the seals under Thornhollow are cooling. Mother Ysolde can hear them—she says the mountain has started to snore, if a mountain can be said to snore, which according to Ysolde it can, at length, and in a dead language. When the last dragon-fire gutters out, everything the old queens locked away down there wakes up hungry. And the nearest meal to a starving sealed thing is a city full of soft, warm people who never learned a single defensive charm because their Magistrate burns hedge-witches for lighting candles wrong.*

Ulyana read that paragraph twice. Then a third time, because the fourth word of the third line seemed to be "fire," and she wanted very much for it to be something else.

*Come home, Uli. Or we come for you. Those are the options, and I'm sorry they're ugly, but the council voted, and the council includes women who remember when you left, and their memories are not kind. I argued for a month to make it a letter instead of a summons. Consider how much worse this could have been, and then consider coming anyway.*

There was more—there was a postscript about the state of the orchard, and whether Ulyana still made her plum preserves or had become one of those people who bought jam like a stranger—but the middle of the letter blurred, and Ulyana found herself standing very still behind her stall while the night market flowed around her like water around a stone.

*The fire is dying.*

She thought of Corin. Of course she thought of Corin—she'd been thinking of Corin for eleven days now, which was its own quiet humiliation. The man who ordered tea like an apology. The man who wanted a charm against forgetting, and whom she'd refused, and whom she hadn't been able to stop turning over in her mind like a coin she suspected was worth more than its face.

*A sold soul,* the prophecy book said. *Sold to the court beneath the mountain before he was born.* And the court beneath the mountain was fed by the fire, or fed the fire, or stood in some terrible relationship to the fire that Ulyana didn't yet understand—and Brenna, apparently, did.

She looked up at the crow.

"You know," she told it, "most birds just want bread."

The crow inclined its head with the weary dignity of a professional who had heard every joke in the repertoire.

*One week,* the letter finished. *The gate stands open until the new moon. After that, sister, the coven comes to Cinderhollow, and I cannot promise they will knock.*

*B.,*

*who remembers your birthday, incidentally. Every year. On purpose.*

Ulyana folded the letter along its creases. Unfolded it. Read the whole thing again, top to bottom, hunting for loopholes the way she'd once hunted for them in Warden proclamations posted on the market boards.

There were none. There was never anything with Brenna. That had always been the difference between them: Ulyana built doors into every wall, and Brenna built walls into every door, and both of them had spent twenty years believing the other one was the fool.

* * *

She took the letter home, which meant taking it to the narrow rooms above the dye-shop where she'd lived long enough to know exactly which floorboards announced her to Mrs. Halloway below and which ones let her pass in silence. She'd chosen those rooms for the silence. She'd chosen a great many things for the silence. It occurred to her, sitting on her bed with the candle unlit because lit candles invited questions, that she had furnished her entire life the way other people childproofed houses.

The letter sat on her knee.

The prophecy book sat on the shelf across from her, wrapped in oilcloth, looking innocent, which books that had survived two decades of a destruction order should not be able to do.

"The fire is dying," she said aloud, to test how it sounded in her own voice.

It sounded worse.

She thought about what she knew, which was embarrassingly little for a woman who had grown up forty miles from the mountain. The coven taught children the shape of it early: the dragon Ember-that-Waits, coiled beneath the earth since the dragon wars, burning slow and eternal, and the seals laid over the deep places by queens whose names were spoken only in winter. Keep the fire fed, Mother Ysolde used to say, tapping each apprentice on the head with a stick for emphasis, because hunger is patient but not loyal. Ulyana had assumed, at seven, that this was a metaphor about breakfast.

At seventeen, when the binding rite came due—the rite that would tie her soul to the mountain court forever, feed her years into the deep places, make her part of the machinery keeping the fire alive—she had decided it was not a metaphor about anything except being eaten politely.

So she ran. And she had told herself for twenty years that she'd run from the eating. Now, holding Brenna's letter, she wondered—with the cold, sliding discomfort of a woman discovering a draft in a house she'd thought was tight—whether she'd also run from the truth of what the eating was *for*. Whether leaving hadn't been cowardice wearing courage's coat.

"Don't," she told herself sternly. "Don't do the thing where you rewrite twenty years in one evening. You're not a historian, you're a charm-seller."

But the words kept rearranging themselves anyway. *The fire is dying.* If the fire died, the seals failed. If the seals failed, the things below woke. And the things below, whatever else they were, had bought a soul once—a whole unborn soul, purchased fair and square from somebody, delivered to a man named Corin who woke each night tasting ash.

She got up. Lit the candle after all, consequences be damned. Pulled the oilcloth off the book.

The pages fell open, because books that mean to torment you learn quickly where you're weakest, to the page with the two figures inked in faded brown: a woman with her hands full of knots, and a hollow-eyed man beside her, and between them a verse she had read so many times in the past fortnight that it had worn a groove in her:

*When the witch who fled and the sold man stand*
*Beneath the crown of embered land,*
*The fire that waits shall wait no more—*
*What closed the deep must open the door.*

"What does that even *mean*," she asked the book, as she had asked it eleven times before.

The book declined, as ever, to elaborate. Books were like crows that way.

She was still staring at it when someone knocked on the door downstairs—not her door, Mrs. Halloway's door, and then footsteps on the stairs, and then a tap at her own door, and Tam the dyer's son stood in the hallway holding a covered dish and wearing the expression of a boy delivering soup under protest.

"Marta says you missed supper."

"I was working."

"Marta says that's what you always say." He held out the dish. It was stew. It was always stew; Marta believed all sorrows were hydraulic problems. "Also there's a weird bird following me. Been following me since the market. Big black thing, keeps looking at me like I owe it money."

Ulyana closed her eyes briefly. "Don't feed it."

"Why? Is it yours?"

"It's nobody's," she said, which felt true in several directions at once.

Tam shrugged and departed with his empty dish and his stalker-bird, and Ulyana sat back down with the stew going cold and the candle guttering and the letter open on her knee, and she did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding.

One week. The new moon. Come home, or we come for you.

If the coven came to Cinderhollow—if Brenna came to Cinderhollow, High Witch now, with the council's authority and whatever else High Witches carried—they would find a woman selling luck-knots under a false name in a night market. They would find the hidden charms in the floorboards. They would find the book. And they would find, sooner or later, because Brenna missed nothing and never had, the hollow-eyed man who drank his tea in sips like he was rationing joy.

Or Ulyana could go to them. Walk through Thornhollow's gates on her own feet, seventeen years old again in all the ways that mattered, and face the women she'd abandoned and the rite she'd refused and the sister she'd left holding the door.

Neither option involved staying invisible. That was the thing she kept circling and refusing to land on. Brenna's letter wasn't a threat, exactly—it was a deadline. And deadlines, Ulyana had learned in twenty years of careful commerce, were what happened when hiding stopped working.

She ate the stew. It was good. Marta's stew was always good; it was possibly Marta's only flaw as a surrogate family member, that she was competent.

* * *

On the third morning after the crow, Ulyana went to find Corin.

He was at the tea-house, of course. He was always at the tea-house, at the corner table, at the hour when the morning fog still hung in the streets like the city couldn't quite commit to waking up. He had a book in front of him that he wasn't reading. He had a cup of tea he was drinking in the same apologetic sips. He looked up when she sat down across from him, and the look on his face—startled, wary, and underneath it, horribly, hopeful—made her feel like a thief.

"You found me," he said.

"You're the only person in Cinderhollow who sits in the same place every day. Finding you isn't a skill."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. A smile considering an application.

"I've been thinking," Ulyana said, and then stopped, because she'd rehearsed four openings on the walk over and all of them had evaporated. She tried honesty, which was unfamiliar territory and required a map. "I refused you a charm. Against forgetting. Eleven days ago."

"Twelve."

"Twelve." She exhaled. "I've been thinking about it. About why I refused. I told myself it was because memory-charms are delicate work and you looked like trouble. Both of those things were true. Neither was the reason."

Corin set down his cup. Waited. He was good at waiting; she'd noticed that early. It was the patience of a man who had learned that rushing toward things usually meant rushing away from other things.

"The reason," she said, "is that I recognized the request."

His brow furrowed.

"Not the forgetting," she said quickly. "The wanting a charm for it. Wanting magic to fix something inside instead of outside. I know that want. I've—" she gestured vaguely at herself, at the stall-keeper's apron, at the whole careful architecture of her life, "—I've been wearing one for twenty years. Just a slower kind."

The tea-house clattered on around them. A kettle shrieked; a child negotiated fiercely for a second bun; the owner shouted cheerful curses at a delivery boy. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. Ulyana reached into her bag and put Brenna's letter on the table, facedown, like a card she was ashamed of.

"There's a place I come from," she said. "A coven. People who raised me, mostly kindly, occasionally terrifyingly. I left when I was seventeen and I've spent every year since making sure they couldn't find me, and yesterday they found me anyway, via bird, which feels personal." She turned the letter over. "They say the fire under the mountain is dying. The real fire. The one the prophecies talk about. The one your—" she hesitated over the word, watching his face, "—the one connected to whatever was done to you."

Corin had gone very still. It was the stillness she'd noticed at their first meeting, the hollowed-out quality, a man built around an absence.

"And you believe them," he said quietly. "Your coven."

"Brenna doesn't lie. She considers lying a failure of planning." Ulyana almost smiled. "We weren't friends, exactly. We were sisters, which is different and worse. She was the one who didn't run when I did."

"That sounds like it hurts."

"It sounds like it hurts because it hurts. Yes." She pushed the letter an inch toward him. "Here's the thing, Corin. Here's why I'm sitting in your tea instead of burning this letter like a sensible woman. The prophecy book—the one with your face in it—it says the fire needs a sold soul to come home. Says it plainly, once you read the verses backward, which I have been doing at increasingly unsociable hours. And I keep hoping I'm wrong. I've read it nine times hoping I'm wrong."

"And?"

"And I'm not wrong." She rubbed her eyes. "I'm never wrong about the annoying things. Only the useful ones."

For a long moment Corin looked at the letter, at the broken grey seal, at the thorned circle stamped in wax. When he spoke, his voice was low and even, the voice of a man handling something fragile.

"When I was nineteen," he said, "I woke up in a wagon outside a town called Marlebridge, and the driver swore I'd hired him three days before, and I had no memory of hiring him, or of any day before that one. No childhood. No mother's face. Nothing. Just—" his hand rose unconsciously to his chest, "—just the ash taste. Every morning since. Twelve years of waking up tasting ash and being told by doctors that grief works that way sometimes, and knowing, *knowing*, the way you know a room is missing furniture, that nothing had been taken recently. Whatever was missing had been gone a very long time. Since before I had a self to miss it with."

"Yes," Ulyana whispered. "That's exactly what it looks like. What it reads like."

"So." He folded his hands on the table, neatly, like a man closing an account. "Your coven wants you home. Your book wants us both under a mountain. And I wake up tasting ash." He looked up at her, and his eyes—those ordinary brown eyes she had spent twelve days failing to stop thinking about—were steady. "What do you need from me, Ulyana?"

It took her a moment to answer, because no one had asked her that question in approximately two decades. Everyone in Cinderhollow needed things *from* her—charms, knots, fevers cooled, fortunes gently lied about—but no one asked what she needed. Needing things was for people who could afford to be seen needing.

"I need," she said slowly, "to go home. Which I swore I would never do. And I need—" the word stuck, and she pried it loose, "—I need to not go alone. Because I know exactly what's waiting at those gates, and it's a woman who remembers my birthday every year on purpose, out of spite, and I am not strong enough to face that by myself."

Something that was definitely a smile broke through Corin's wariness then, brief and crooked and real.

"Spiteful birthday remembrance," he said gravely. "Terrible. Absolutely you shouldn't face that alone."

Outside the window, on the sill, a large black crow settled itself with the air of an escort who had been waiting for exactly this development, and fixed Ulyana with one glossy eye.

She sighed.

"One week," she told it. "Fine. Tell her we're coming."

The crow, having secured its contract, helped itself to half a bun from a passing tray and flew off toward the mountains, and Ulyana watched it go and felt the ground shift under the whole careful, hidden, twenty-years-built life of hers—felt it tilt, gently and irreversibly, toward Thornhollow.

"Well," said Corin. "I suppose I should pack."

Ulyana looked back at him—at the hollow man from the prophecy, her fellow traveler, the reason the past had finally caught up with her—and discovered, to her considerable alarm, that under all the dread, some small stubborn part of her was almost glad.

"We leave tomorrow," she said. "Before I change my mind. And Corin—"

"Yes?"

"If any more birds follow you, don't feed those either. They remember kindness. It's how they get you."

* * *

That night she took inventory of her stall the way you take inventory of a life you already know you're losing: slowly, touching everything twice. The luck-knots in their basket. The fever-acorns. The little tin of sleep-charms she'd made for the lamplighter's boy when his mother was ill, though he'd never known what they really were.

Tomorrow, she supposed, she would have to burn the stock. You didn't leave hidden charms lying around for Wardens to find, and you didn't sell them to a market you were abandoning, and you especially didn't hand twenty years of careful invisibility to whoever came poking through your stall after you were gone.

Tomorrow she would grieve properly. Tonight, she sat in the dark above the dye-shop with Brenna's letter on her knee and the prophecy book open on the shelf, and read the verse one final time, and this time—this time, when she read *what closed the deep must open the door*—the book's ink shifted, faint as breath on glass.

Below the old verse, in fresh brown script that had definitely not been there that morning, one new line was writing itself:

*The witch returns, and brings the ash.*

Ulyana sat very still for a long time.

Then she blew out the candle, and began to pack.
