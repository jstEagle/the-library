## 9. What Hiding Cost

The fire took the luck-knots first.

Ulyana had chosen them deliberately, though she'd never have admitted to choosing at all. Luck-knots were her cheapest stock, three coppers apiece, braided from red thread and river-grass with a whispered word folded into the middle like a coin pressed into bread dough. They were also, if she was honest with herself in the small hours when honesty crept out from under the bed where she kept it, the most dishonest thing she sold. Not because they didn't work. They worked. But because she told people they were "merchant's patter, nothing more, a keepsake and a smile," and people bought them precisely because they didn't have to believe. That was the trick of Cinderhollow commerce: you could sell magic all day long so long as everyone agreed not to see it.

She fed them into the brazier one fistful at a time and watched twenty years of careful red thread curl into ash.

"You're early," said Marta, who kept the pickle stall two down and had opinions about everything, including, apparently, the hour at which other people burned their inventory.

"Couldn't sleep," Ulyana said, which was true in the way that most of her sentences were true—technically, conveniently, with the dangerous part amputated.

Marta squinted at the smoke. "That's a lot of thread for a woman who couldn't sleep."

"It's old stock. Thread goes brittle." Ulyana stirred the coals with an iron poker she'd bought years ago for exactly this purpose, though until this morning it had only ever been set dressing. The knots popped as they burned, tiny sounds like knuckles cracking, and each pop carried off a little wish someone had trusted her to hold: safe births, good harvests, a son home from the fishing boats, rain for Widow Cress's lettuce. She'd made every one of them properly. That was the thing nobody understood about hiding—you still did the work well, because doing it badly would get you noticed faster than doing it at all.

*You will be forgotten by noon,* she thought at the smoke. *And that is the kindest thing I can give you.*

Because that was the arithmetic of it. The Wardens counted charm-sellers the way a farmer counts crows. One or two, tolerated. A roost of them, and the torches came out. Every knot she burned tonight made the market a fraction safer, and every knot felt like pulling a tooth.

* * *

By midmorning the stall looked wrong. Stripped. She'd spent two decades building it into something unremarkable—the awning faded to a gray-beige that matched six other awnings on the row, the shelves arranged with studied carelessness, the sign hand-painted to read PEMBERTON'S NOTIONS in letters so bland they practically apologized for existing. notions, plural, meaning buttons and bootlaces and, oh, incidentally, these knotted things my grandmother taught me, no significance whatsoever.

Now the shelves held only buttons and bootlaces, which turned out to be a very small life laid end to end.

Tam came by around the time the fishmongers started shouting about the morning catch, which was to say loudly and with profanity that would have gotten him fined anywhere but here. He was eleven and mostly elbows, and he swept the lane between stalls for coppers, and he had decided years ago that Ulyana Pemberton was the best person in the market because she paid in whole coins instead of halves.

"You sick?" he asked, looking at the bare shelves with the frank alarm of a child confronting change.

"No."

"You moving?"

"No."

He waited. Tam had learned from the market that waiting got answers, eventually, from everyone except the oyster man, who was a lost cause.

"I'm closing up for a while," Ulyana said. "Traveling."

"Where?"

"North."

"That's not a place."

"It is from here."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Can I have your stool?"

"You may *buy* my stool, you extortionist."

"I don't have money."

"Then it sounds like we've reached the natural limits of negotiation." She dug in the till anyway and pressed a copper into his hand—not for sweeping, he hadn't swept yet, and they both knew it, and neither of them cared—and his face did the thing it always did, the quick bright surprise of a boy who expected less from the world than it occasionally delivered. She'd given him that face maybe two hundred times over the years. She would not be there to give it again, and he would forget her within a season, children do, and this was correct and right and it still felt like swallowing a stone.

*This is what you chose,* she reminded herself. *Chose again, this morning, with both eyes open.*

It didn't help. It helped exactly as much as it had ever helped, which was to say it kept her upright.

* * *

The fever-charms went next. Those were harder to burn, because fever-charms had names in them.

She'd learned the craft from Mother Ysolde back when learning anything from Ysolde meant kneeling on cold stone until your knees memorized the shape of humility, and the old woman had been merciless about one doctrine above all others: *a charm is a promise, and a promise has a spine.* You didn't make a fever-charm without knowing whose fever. You didn't braid protection without naming whom it protected. Magic done anonymously slid off the world like water off grease; magic done for a specific person, spoken into the work with their name and their need, bit deep and held. Which meant every charm in her stock had somebody's name folded inside it, quiet as a seed.

Burning them felt less like destroying inventory and more like reading letters out loud at a funeral.

There was the one for old Piet's cough, renewed each winter for nine winters running, and she remembered the first year he'd come to her stall wheezing and suspicious and she'd said *it's just a knotted handkerchief, sir, folk remedy, costs a penny* and he'd said *my grandmother did knots too, girl, before the Wardens made cowards of us all* and bought three. There was the birthing-charm for Sela, down the row, who was round as a harvest moon now and due within the month, and Ulyana had made it double-strength and had planned—had *planned*, God help her—to be there when it was needed, standing in the doorway with hot water and clean cloth and a lie ready about why her hands knew exactly what to do.

Sela wouldn't know why the birth went easy. Nobody would know. That had been the whole architecture of Ulyana's life: goodness with the signature torn off.

She fed the fever-charms to the coals and said each name under her breath as it went. Piet. Sela. The miller's twins. The ferryman's dog, who was not technically her patient but had eaten something dead off the flats and whose owner had paid her in eels. Twenty years of names. The smoke rose straight up in the windless morning like a column announcing her to anyone who cared to look, and she let it, because today of all days she was done pretending the column wasn't there.

"You'll want to watch that," said a voice behind her, and her heart performed its old practiced maneuver, the drop-and-dodge it had executed ten thousand times before, before she recognized the voice as belonging to nobody dangerous at all.

Widow Cress stood there with her marketing basket and her permanent expression of having been personally inconvenienced by creation. She was seventy if she was a day, sharp as a fishhook, and she had been buying charms from Ulyana since the beginning without once acknowledging that was what she was doing. Their entire commercial relationship consisted of Cress asking after "the knotted things" and Ulyana asking after "the weather," and both women finding this arrangement perfectly satisfactory.

"Lettuce," said Cress, by way of greeting. "Still coming up yellow. Third year running."

"Third year running," Ulyana agreed.

"Had a thought it might be the soil."

"Might be."

"Had another thought it might be something else." Cress's eyes, pale and unhurried, moved from Ulyana's face to the smoking brazier and back. "Had a third thought I'm not going to say it out loud, on account of the walls."

"Walls are wise," Ulyana said.

"Mm." The widow set her basket down with the decisive thump of a woman settling in for a siege. "You burning your stock."

"I'm clearing out old inventory."

"In a brazier. At dawn. With your lips moving." Cress sniffed. "I'm seventy-one years old, girl. I remember the market before the last purge. I remember what burning stock means. It means either the Wardens are close, or you're leaving, and the Wardens aren't close, because I'd smell them."

Ulyana said nothing, which in the economy of Widow Cress constituted a signed confession.

"Well," said Cress. "Damn."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize, it's undignified." The old woman's jaw worked. "Who's going to fix my lettuce?"

"Try bone meal."

"Bone meal." Cress pronounced the word as though it were a personal insult. "Twenty years of whatever-it-is you put in those knots and now it's *bone meal*." She stared hard at Ulyana, and something moved behind the fishhook eyes—not softness, exactly, Cress would have denied softness under torture, but recognition. The look of one hidden creature sighting another across a crowded room. "You were good at it," she said. "Whatever it was. The thing you never did, that I never bought. You were the best in the market at it."

"Cress—"

"Save it." She picked up her basket. Then paused, and without looking back, said: "The yellow lettuce. First spring after the war, there was a hedge-woman on Copper Lane did the same trick with knots. Wardens took her. I watched it. I was nineteen and I did nothing and I have regretted it for fifty-two years, so understand me when I tell you—" she finally turned, and her voice cracked down the middle like old rope, "—whatever you're running toward, run *well*. And if you ever send word how the lettuce turned out, I'll know it's you, and I'll know you lived."

She walked off before Ulyana could answer, which was merciful, because Ulyana's throat had closed entirely.

*They never knew me,* she thought, watching the old woman go. *And one of them knew me better than anyone alive.*

* * *

She worked through the afternoon. It was strange how much ceremony grief demanded when you gave it room. Each shelf cleared was a small funeral; each item burned or packed away was a decision made flesh. The protective wards woven into the stall frame itself—subtle things, a spiderweb of "keep clear, keep calm, nothing to see here" stitched into the wood grain—she unpicked with a bone needle, murmuring the unraveling verses, feeling the stall go naked and ordinary around her. By dusk it was just a stall. Just wood and canvas. Anyone's stall. She sat inside it and felt the difference the way you feel a tooth missing.

Corin appeared at the edge of the lamplight just as the market lamps were being lit, hands in his pockets, looking like a man who had rehearsed a speech and misplaced it.

"You heard," Ulyana said.

"Brenna's letter." He'd read it over her shoulder two nights ago, all cold ceremony and one line that had gone through Ulyana like a lance: *come home, or we come for you.* "I thought you'd want—" He stopped. Started again. "I brought tea."

He held up a paper-wrapped packet, and something about the sheer modesty of it—the way a man with a hole where his soul should be offered *tea*, as if tea were the thing that mattered—cracked something loose in her chest.

"There's no stove," she said.

"I checked. There's a chandler two lanes over who owes you for a wart charm and lets people use his back room."

"You checked."

"I check things now," Corin said. "Since the book. Since everything. Turns out I'm a person who checks things. Who knew." He smiled, and it was the smile she was already, dangerously, organizing her days around. "Come sit down for half an hour. The stall can grieve without supervision."

So they sat in the chandler's back room amid smells of tallow and salt, drinking tea out of cups the chandler's wife provided without being asked, because that was the kind of place Cinderhollow secretly was underneath all its fear—a city that kept feeding itself despite everything. And Ulyana found herself talking, really talking, for the first time in twenty years. About the stall. About Marta's pickles and Tam's elbows and Cress and her damn lettuce. About the night, seventeen years ago, when the river flooded and she'd quietly knotted every sandbag on the embankment while the men argued about whose fault it was, and the dike had held, and she'd gone home soaked to the ribs and laughing and utterly alone with the knowledge.

"Nobody thanked you," Corin said.

"Being thanked is a risk."

"Being *known* is a risk."

"Yes." She turned her cup. "But I'm starting to suspect the alternative is worse. I used to think hiding was freedom. You know what it actually is? It's a room you build around yourself, brick by brick, until one day you look up and realize you built it from the inside, and the door only locks from your side, and you've been calling the cage a garden for twenty years."

Corin was quiet for a moment. "My cage came pre-built," he said. "Somebody else built it before I was born. At least yours had craftsmanship."

She laughed—really laughed, the sound startling her—and reached across the table and took his hand, and his fingers closed around hers with the careful strength of a man holding something he couldn't quite believe was real.

"The chandler's wife is watching us through the door," Ulyana murmured.

"I know. She's been watching since the second cup."

"She thinks we're courting."

"Aren't we?"

The question landed softly, without weight, the way the truest questions do. Ulyana looked at their hands, at the steam rising off the tea, at the door-crack where a shadow that was definitely the chandler's wife pretended very hard to be a coat rack.

"Not yet," she said. "Ask me again when the world's a little less on fire."

"When will that be?"

"If the prophecy's right? Roughly never. Ask anyway."

* * *

Dawn came gray and smelled of ash and river. Ulyana stood at her empty stall one last time, wearing her traveling cloak with the hidden seams, the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against her spine where it lay against her skin like a second heartbeat. The market woke around her—shutters banging open, fish ice sliding off cart beds, Tam's broom whispering its familiar rhythm down the lane. Nobody looked at her twice. She had spent two decades perfecting the art of not being looked at twice, and this morning, for the first time, the skill felt like a scar rather than a shield.

She left the stall unlocked. Left the stool for Tam with a note that said *payment for services rendered* and nothing else. Left a jar of bone meal on Cress's corner post, labeled *for the soil, try it before you argue.*

At the mouth of the market lane she stopped and looked back once—at the awnings, the noise, the whole improbable organism of the place, the family that had fed her and teased her and never once asked her real name's worth of questions. She loved them. She was leaving them. Both facts sat in her chest like stones in a riverbed, worn smooth by now, inseparable.

"Safe travels," said Marta, passing with her pickle crates, not even slowing down.

"Thank you," Ulyana said, and meant it more than Marta could ever know.

Corin was waiting at the north gate with two packs and a map that ended in blank paper past Thornhollow, which seemed about right for how much any of them knew. The Ash Country lay beyond the wall, scarred and silent, and somewhere past it, a coven full of sisters who remembered her face and a mountain full of teeth.

"You can still turn around," he said gently. "We could go east instead. Coast towns. Nobody knows us there."

"Somebody always knows us there." She settled her pack and looked north, where the road climbed away from the only life she'd allowed herself in twenty years. "Besides. I promised a widow I'd send word about the lettuce."

She stepped through the gate, and Cinderhollow fell away behind them like a held breath finally released—

—and neither of them saw the figure in the Warden-gray cloak slip out of the shadow of the tollhouse and take the north road after them, keeping always, always, a hundred paces back.
