## 10. Road Through the Ash Country

The ash country began three miles past Cinderhollow's last gate, and it began, as everything in Ulyana Pemberton's life seemed to lately, with an argument.

"We should have taken the coast road," she said, adjusting the strap of her pack for the fourth time. The strap was fine. Her nerves were not. "It's longer, but there are villages. Inns. People."

"That's rather the problem," Corin said. "People report things. Things like a woman who sold luck-knots at the night market for twenty years and then vanished the same week her stall burned down."

"I didn't vanish. I closed with dignity."

"You fled at dawn through the fishmonger's gate wearing a borrowed coat."

"It was a *good* coat," Ulyana said. "Marta insisted. She said if I was going to abandon my entire life I could at least be warm about it."

Corin smiled—that slow, crooked smile that kept catching her off guard, appearing on his face like sunlight through a crack she hadn't known was in her. Twenty years of keeping people at arm's length, and now here she was walking beside a man whose soul had been sold before his birth, feeling more seen than she'd ever been in all her careful invisible years.

The road ahead climbed through country that did not know what to call itself. To the left lay farmland, stubborn and green, hedged and ditched and defended against the world. To the right lay the Ash Country proper—a vast grey plain where the old dragon wars had burned everything down to the bones of the earth, and nothing had grown since but a kind of pale moss that crunched underfoot like frost.

They walked the seam between them.

"The map says three days to Thornhollow if we keep pace," Corin said, patting the leather tube where Mother Ysolde's letter rode against his chest. Ulyana still wasn't entirely sure how he'd ended up carrying it. He'd simply held out his hand when she'd been fumbling with it at the gate, and somehow now it was his, the way the tea kettle at her stall had become his, the way the left side of her bed—no. That thought could stay unlit, thank you very much.

"Maps lie," Ulyana said. "Maps are written by people who assume you'll be walking in daylight with full bellies and no Wardens behind you."

"Are there Wardens behind us?"

She stopped and turned, shielding her eyes against the low grey sun, and looked back along the road they'd traveled. Cinderhollow was already lost behind a shoulder of hill, its chimney-smear just a smudge on the horizon like something wiped away.

"No," she said slowly. "Not yet."

"Not yet," Corin repeated. "You say that the way other people say *good morning*."

"When you've spent twenty years waiting for the knock on the door, you learn the difference between fear and weather." She started walking again. "Fear has a shape. Weather is just weather until it isn't."

* * *

They made camp that first night in the shell of an old waystation, one of the fortified rest-houses built during the wars and abandoned after, its stone walls still standing though its roof had long since gone to moss and sky. Someone had built a hearth in the corner once; the chimney still drew, which felt like a small miracle, or possibly a trap.

Ulyana built the fire herself, refusing Corin's help with a flick of two fingers—not magic, just habit, the gesture of a woman who had done everything alone so long that accepting assistance required conscious effort. She noticed him noticing. She noticed him deciding not to comment, which was, she was beginning to understand, one of his particular kindnesses.

"Tell me about Thornhollow," he said, settling onto the far side of the fire with his back to the wall, facing the door. He always sat facing doors. She'd started cataloguing these things about him: faced doors, slept light, ate like someone who expected the meal to be taken back.

"What do you want to know?"

"Whatever you haven't told me. You've told me the parts from books. Coven of hedge-witches in the mountains, old as the wars, keeps the old songs." He turned his hands toward the flames. "You've never told me why you left."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, wind moved through the dead country, making a sound like a great slow breath.

"My sister-apprentice and I were raised there from children," Ulyana said. "Brenna. We were inseparable—we finished each other's charms, we got into trouble in stereo. And when we came of age, there's a rite. A binding. Every witch gives a piece of herself to the mountain court—it's how the coven pays its ancient debts, keeps its protections, all of it. Brenna went first. She came out of the rite shining, glowing, *herself* but more, and it was her turn to hold my hand while they—" She stopped. Started again. "I ran. That night. No note, no goodbye. Just me and the dark and a twenty-year head start."

"Do you regret it?"

"Every day," she said, and then, because lying to him had become strangely difficult, "and not at all, and both of those are true, and I don't know how to make them fit in the same mouth."

Corin considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, as though words were knots he refused to leave loose.

"They feel the same size to me," he said finally. "I can't remember anything before nineteen. So every day I grieve a life I can't find, and every day I'm grateful for the one I woke up into. Both at once. They don't fight each other. They just... sit together. Like old dogs."

Ulyana laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and felt something in her chest come loose by a degree she hadn't authorized.

"Old dogs," she said. "That's exactly what it is. You're strange, Corin. Has anyone ever told you?"

"Once or twice." His smile went soft at the edges. "Usually right before they asked me to stay anyway."

* * *

The second day, she saw the rider.

She caught it without meaning to—the old market habit of reading a landscape for threats, the vigilance that had kept her alive and cost her everything. A figure on the ridgeline behind them, half a mile back, moving the way soldiers move: not hurrying, not falling behind. *Keeping pace.*

She didn't tell Corin immediately. She spent an hour telling herself it was a drover, a courier, a pilgrim. Then the figure dropped below the ridge and reappeared a quarter mile closer, having gained ground during the exact minutes it was out of sight, and she stopped lying to herself. It was a skill she was getting practice at.

"Corin." She said it quietly, not turning her head. "Don't look back. There's a rider behind us. Grey cloak, military seat, been matching our pace since the ridge."

He didn't look back. She felt him go carefully still instead, the stillness of a man whose body remembered things his mind had lost.

"How long?"

"Since mid-morning, maybe longer. I think I missed them earlier because I wanted to miss them."

"And now?"

"Now we stop being travelers and start being quarry." She scanned ahead. The road bent east toward a river valley—water, cover, other footprints to hide among—but bending east meant showing their backs to the rider for a full turn of the path. "There's a village called Marlow's Rest at the river crossing. If we can reach crowds before they reach us—"

"Wardens don't operate well in crowds," Corin said. "Too many witnesses."

"You've read the Warden field manual?"

"No," he said. "But I've spent six years being watched without knowing why. You develop theories."

* * *

They cut off the road an hour later, following a shepherd's track down through a fold of hills, and that was where the Ash Country showed them what it actually was.

The track wound between formations of fused glass—great twisted flows of it, green-black and smooth, where the dragon-fire had melted stone itself during the wars. In the evening light they glowed faintly, holding the sunset the way a cupped hand holds water. Ulyana had heard about the glass fields her whole life; the coven's oldest songs called them the Dragon's Tears, and warned that nothing that lived in them lived *kindly*.

Halfway through, Corin stopped walking.

"Ulyana." His voice had changed. Flat. Careful. "How many riders did you see?"

"One. Why?"

"Because there are hoofprints here." He crouched beside a patch of ash where the moss had been churned to paste. "Two horses. Recent. And they didn't pass us—they came from the east. From *ahead* of us."

The cold that went through her then had nothing to do with the evening air.

"A pincer," she said. "Someone who knows the country. Someone who guessed we'd leave the road." She stood very still, listening to the wind move through the glass formations, making them sing faintly against each other—a high, thin sound, almost like voices. "We need to move. Now. Off the track, into the deep glass, somewhere a horse can't—"

The whistle came from the western ridge.

One note, clean and carrying. Not a hunting call. An announcement. *I see you.*

And then, from the eastern rise, a voice—a woman's voice, level and unhurried and carrying the particular authority of someone who has done this many times:

"Ulyana Pemberton. Sergeant Ilse Vane, Magistrate's Wardens. I've been tracking you for two days, and I'll tell you plainly: I'm very good at this, and you're very tired. Come out with your hands visible and no one needs a fire tonight."

Ulyana's hand found Corin's without either of them deciding it. His grip was steady. Hers was not.

"There's another way," she breathed. "Through the glass. It's dangerous, but horses can't follow, and the songs say the deep fields confuse pursuit—"

"The songs also say the glass fields sing people to sleep," Corin murmured. "Permanently."

"Yes, well." She was already pulling salt-iron from her pack, feeling the old shapes wake under her fingers for the first time in twenty years—real craft, not luck-knots and fever-charms, but the work she'd been trained for and had sworn off. "I've slept badly for two decades anyway. Stay behind me."

"Ulyana—"

"*Stay behind me.*"

She stepped forward to the edge of the glass field, and the singing rose around her like the beginning of a name.

Behind her, boots crunched on ash. Ahead, the green-black glass gleamed with the trapped light of a war a century dead. And somewhere in the deep fields, she was almost certain, something that was neither Warden nor witch had heard the shouting, and was beginning, patiently, to move toward the sound.

* * *

Sergeant Ilse Vane sat her horse at the field's edge and did not dismount, because her mother had taught her that a tracker who loses the high ground loses everything else shortly after. Below her, the witch and the hollow-eyed man were small figures at the lip of the glass, and beyond them the Dragon's Tears stretched away into dusk, singing their thin sweet deadly song.

She'd had them, she thought. Two days of patient work, reading a market woman's habits like a book, cutting the angle to the river crossing—and now they were going to do the stupid brave thing and go into the glass, and half her job would be done for her, except for the half where her orders said *bring the woman alive*.

Her orders had not mentioned the man at all. Which was interesting. Which was the most interesting thing about this entire assignment, and Ilse Vane had survived nineteen years in the Wardens by finding the interesting things and pulling on them gently.

Below, the witch raised her arms, and even at this distance Ilse felt it—a pressure change, a taste like struck flint on the air. Real magic. Not market-charm trickery. Something older.

"Hold position," she said quietly to the corporal beside her. "Nobody fires. Nobody follows. We watch where they go, and we mark it, and we take them tomorrow when they stumble out the far side."

"And if the glass takes them first, Sergeant?"

Ilse watched the two small figures—hand in hand, gods help her—step into the singing light.

"Then the Magistrate's questions die with them," she said, "and I intend to be the one asking my own instead."

She touched her spurs to her horse and began to ride the edge of the field, keeping pace with the two small lights of them moving deeper into the glow, while all around her the Dragon's Tears sang their century-old song—and beneath the song, faint but unmistakable now, something answered.
