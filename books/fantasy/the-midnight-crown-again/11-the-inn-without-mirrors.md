## 11. The Inn Without Mirrors

The inn announced itself three ways: a sign of painted tin that read THE HONEST FACE in flaking gold letters, a smell of woodsmoke and onion soup strong enough to pull travelers off the road like fish on a line, and a hand-lettered card nailed beside the door that said, in careful block capitals:

**NO MIRRORS. NO EXCEPTIONS. NO ARGUING.**

Ulyana stopped on the threshold and read the card twice.

"Charming," she said. "A place for people who've given up on their hair."

"It's not unusual out here," Corin said. He had gone very still beside her, the way he did whenever something about a place snagged against whatever it was that lived where his memories should be. "Ash Country inns. Mirrors are supposed to show things that aren't you."

"Superstition."

"Most superstition is just memory wearing a costume." He said it absently, already looking past her into the common room, and Ulyana felt the words settle somewhere under her ribs and stay there.

Three days out from Cinderhollow, they had developed a rhythm she neither planned nor trusted. She talked; he listened with an attention so complete it felt like being read aloud to herself. He carried the packs without being asked, remembered how she took her tea (honey, no milk, and don't say it's because of her grandmother, everyone said that), and woke each dawn tasting ash he never mentioned unless she asked first. Somewhere between the burned orchards and the second night of sleeping in shifts, she had caught herself watching him across a fire and thinking—not *he is handsome*, which was true in the way weather is sometimes fine, but *I would know his face anywhere.*

Which was, she reminded herself grimly, rather the point. A book had told her so. She was building her life around a book's opinion, and she hadn't even liked the book.

Inside, the Honest Face was warm and low-ceilinged and crowded with the particular silence of people minding their own business very loudly. The walls were hung with everything except glass: old horseshoes, bundles of dried yarrow, a child's embroidered sampler reading GOD SEES ME SO I NEEDN'T. Behind the bar stood a woman built like a rain barrel, forearms like hams, who looked at them both exactly once and said:

"Room's six coppers. Soup's two. No reflections, no questions, and if either of you starts glowing after dark, you sleep in the stable."

"We're not—" Ulyana began.

"Everyone says that first." The woman—Marda, according to the chalkboard behind her—slid two bowls down the counter with the economy of long practice. "Glowers get the stable. It's a good stable."

Corin leaned toward Ulyana and murmured, "Do we glow?"

"You have a soul-shaped hole where your reflection should be, so I'd say the odds are not nothing."

He considered this. "The stable can't be that good."

It was, as it turned out, a very good stable. But that came later.

* * *

They ate by candlelight at a corner table, and Ulyana watched Corin watch the room—or rather, watched him fail to do what every other person in the room was doing, which was avoiding every polished surface with the practiced ease of habit. The brass fittings on the bar had been dull-waxed into blindness. The windowpanes were smoky glass, more suggestion than mirror. Even the soup was served in wooden bowls, not pewter, and when Ulyana noticed this she felt something cold walk up her spine on small feet.

A whole inn, arranged so no one had to meet themselves.

"They come through every few months," Marda said, appearing with bread and refilling their cups unasked. "Folk running from something. Folk running toward something. Mostly folk who caught sight of themselves one bad morning and didn't care for the company." She set the loaf down. "You two aren't running from anything."

It wasn't a question, but Ulyana answered it anyway, because twenty years of practice had taught her that the safest answer to any observation was agreement wrapped in blandness. "Just passing through."

"Mm." Marda looked at Corin a moment too long, then at Ulyana, and whatever she saw made her voice drop half a register. "Third floor. End room. There's a washstand but no basin—I'll leave the pitcher. Don't pour it out over anything silver." And she was gone, back into the crowd of shoulders and steam, before Ulyana could decide whether she'd been warned or threatened or simply given housekeeping advice.

"She knows something," Corin said quietly.

"Everyone in this building knows something. That's what the building is *for*."

* * *

It happened near midnight, when the common room had thinned to three snoring drovers and one insomniac priestess of some small god playing solitaire with a deck that kept cheating.

Ulyana came downstairs for water—it was honest thirst, mostly—and found Corin standing at the bar, alone, holding the polished brass bell Marda used to call last orders. Someone had left it out. It was the brightest thing in the room.

He was looking into it.

From across the room, Ulyana saw only his back, the set of his shoulders, the tilt of his head—the posture of a man checking whether his collar sat straight, or his eyes were bloodshot, or his face still belonged to him. Ordinary. A thousand men a day did exactly that.

But he stood there too long. And then longer. And when she crossed the floorboards and came around beside him, she understood why, and the cold thing on her spine grew claws.

The bell showed the bar. It showed the candles, the chalkboard, the shelf of bottles with their wax-blinded labels. It showed Ulyana—her tired face, her wind-burned cheeks, the gray coming in early at her temples that she pretended wasn't there.

Where Corin stood, the bell showed the bar.

Not a blur. Not darkness. Just the bar, uninterrupted, as though the space he occupied were politely declining to be seen. She lifted her hand and waved it through the space where his reflection should have been, and the candlelight passed through it clean, and Corin made a small sound—not a gasp, something quieter and worse, like a door closing very gently on a room someone had hoped was furnished.

"I thought," he said. His voice was level. He worked hard at level; she was learning the seams of it. "I thought maybe I just hadn't been looking properly. In windows, in water. I thought maybe I was doing it wrong."

"Corin—"

"There's a word for this, isn't there? You'd know it." He turned to look at her directly, and here was the horror sharpening the tenderness: even now, even with the proof hanging in the brass between them, her first instinct was to reach for him. To fix it. To lie if lying would help. "What's the word?"

"Soulless," she said, and hated the word in her mouth. "No. That's wrong. That's what the Wardens would say. The old craft calls it *elsewhere-housed*. Your soul isn't missing. It's somewhere else. Being kept."

"Being kept," he repeated.

"The prophecy said sold. Before you were born." She set her hand flat on the bar, steadying herself against the grain of the wood, against the absurd domestic reality of spilled salt and a knife scar and a ring-mark from a hundred thousand mugs. "Someone sold it before you drew breath, and you've been walking around for thirty-odd years as the nicest empty house I've ever met."

That got a breath of a laugh out of him, which had been perhaps the entire purpose of saying it that way. Gentle humor, Mother Fen used to call it, back in Thornhollow, before gentle humor was a thing Ulyana had to smuggle across borders along with fever-charms. *If you must break terrible news, put a joke in the wrapping paper. They'll remember the kindness, not the knife.*

"The nicest empty house," Corin said. "You've met worse?"

"My aunt's cottage. Haunted, drafty, and the ghost rearranged the cutlery passive-aggressively."

He laughed again, properly this time, and then the laugh ran out of road and he was just a man standing in front of a brass bell that refused to admit he existed. Ulyana did the thing she'd been wanting to do since the river crossing, since the burned orchard, possibly since the night market, if she were honest, which she was trying very hard lately to become.

She took his hand.

His fingers were cold. They closed around hers carefully, the way a man handles something he's afraid of breaking, and she thought: *he doesn't know what he is worth. He has spent his whole life not knowing, and the world has confirmed it nightly, in every window and every still pond, that he is a man-shaped absence.* Fury rose in her—not the hot kind, the slow kind, the kind that keeps.

"Listen to me," she said. "You are not an absence. Absences don't make tea correctly. Absences don't remember that I hate sleeping facing a door. Absences don't—" She stopped. The next words were dangerous ones, and she was not yet brave enough for all her truths in one night.

"Don't what?" he asked softly.

"Don't hum while they cook," she finished, retreating, and he let her retreat, because he was kind, which was its own unbearable quality.

Above them, a floorboard creaked. Then another. Footsteps descending, uneven, dragging slightly on the left side. Marda appeared at the end of the bar, took in the bell, the two of them, the joined hands, and did not scold. Instead she reached over and turned the bell face-down with one ham of a hand.

"Some folk need to see it," she said. "Some folk need to see it once and never again. Which are you?"

"The second one," Corin said.

"Mm. Thought so. You've got the look." She wiped the bar where the bell had sat, an entirely unnecessary gesture performed for the comfort of having something to do with her hands. "There's a woman upstairs. Room four. Hedge-witch, out of Kettering way, came through ten days ago coughing up things I won't describe over food. She's asking for anyone with craft. Real craft." Her eyes rested on Ulyana, mild and knowing and utterly unsurprised. "Says she's got something to trade before she goes. Something about doors."

Ulyana's mouth went dry. "Doors."

"Back doors," said Marda, "are her specialty."

* * *

Room four smelled of camphor and beeswax and something underneath both, older, mineral, like the air inside a cave that had once been angry. The hedge-witch lay propped on pillows, a small woman worn down to essential parts, with white hair cropped close to the skull and eyes that were still fiercely, insultingly awake.

"Well," the woman rasped, when Ulyana closed the door behind them. "Two travelers and a soul-shaped vacancy. Sit down. I haven't got the breath for standing visitors and you haven't got the time for comfortable ones."

"You know what he is?" Ulyana asked.

"I know what the absence of him is, girl, which is nearly as useful." She coughed, wet and long, and Corin moved instinctively to pour water from the pitcher, and the hedge-witch watched him do it with an expression Ulyana couldn't parse until the woman spoke again. "Kind hands. Funny, isn't it. The court buys a soul and leaves the manners behind. Like selling a piano and keeping the stool."

Ulyana went cold. "You know about the court."

"I know about doors." The woman fumbled beneath her pillow and produced a folded square of leather, cracked with age, tied shut with a cord of braided hair—three colors, three donors, or one donor across three years. "My name was Ottalie. Before the coughing, I was a door-finder. Back ways. Servants' entrances. The mountain court has a front door, and it has a gate, and it has a throat, and none of those will have you. But it also has a *back stair*, and nobody watches a back stair that requires crawling through a dead queen's chapel to reach."

"Seraiva," Ulyana breathed.

Ottalie's eyebrows climbed. "So you've heard the name. Good. Saves me the dramatic recitation, and I'm rationing my breath for swearing." She pushed the leather square across the blanket. "Take it. Map's inside. Drawn by my mother, corrected by me, cursed lightly by both of us so it burns if anyone but a witch reads it."

"Why give it away?" Ulyana asked, though her hands were already reaching. "Why not sell it?"

Ottalie smiled, and it transformed her ruined face into something briefly luminous. "Because I sold things my whole life, girl. Charms, favors, silences. Died rich in coppers and poor in everything else." She looked at Corin, and her voice gentled into something almost shy. "Let the last thing I do be a gift. Humor a dying woman. We're sentimental. It's the camphor."

Corin took her thin hand in both of his and bowed over it, formal as a courtier, absurd and perfect, and Ottalie laughed until she coughed until she laughed again.

"One condition," she managed. "One. When you're down there—and you will go down there, I can see it written on you both like a laundry mark—when you're down there, if you happen to pass a shelf of bottled souls, third gallery, past the throne, the ones with the blue wax seals—" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "My sister's name was Wren. If you see a jar with Wren scratched under the seal. You don't have to free her. Just tell her Ottalie kept the map safe. Tell her I remembered the way home the whole time."

The room was very quiet. Downstairs, faintly, the cheating solitaire priestess swore at her cards.

"We'll find her," Corin said. "And we'll tell her. Word for word."

Ottalie nodded, once, and closed her eyes, and was asleep before they reached the door—or something near enough to sleep that they let it be that.

* * *

In the third-floor end room, by the light of one candle and the map unrolled between them, Ulyana traced the route with a fingertip: the drowned chapel, the crawl-space behind the altar, the stair cut into living rock, marked in faded ink with a warning in Ottalie's mother's hand: *THE STAIR LISTENS. STEP IN RHYTHM OR NOT AT ALL.*

"A drowned chapel," Corin said. "Of course. Why wouldn't it be."

"You could stay here," Ulyana said, not looking up. "The stable's apparently excellent. Marda seems fond of you. You could live out your life as a beloved local mystery."

"And miss the chance to crawl through a dead queen's chapel in rhythmic single file with you?" He touched the edge of the map, careful, as if it might wake. "Ulyana. My soul is in that mountain. Whatever's left of me walks around down here humming while I cook. I want the rest."

She looked up at him then—at the face a century-old book had promised her, at the man whose emptiness was kinder than most people's fullness—and the tenderness in her chest and the horror in her gut finally agreed on something: *this is real, and it is going to cost, and you are going to pay it gladly.*

"Then we go at first light," she said. "Before Vane picks up our trail again. She's half a day behind us, no more."

"How do you know?"

"Because I know trackers," Ulyana said, folding the map along Ottalie's creases, "and because the soup downstairs has been salted for soldiers, and Marda started boiling twice as much water tonight."

Outside the shuttered window, far off across the ash flats, something howled—or sang—or did whatever it is that dying dragons do when they dream of fire.

Neither of them slept much after that.
