## 13. Thornhollow Gates

The road to Thornhollow smelled the way memory said it would: wet stone and woodsmoke and something green underneath it all, like the forest was whispering *we kept your place* in a voice Ulyana didn't trust for a moment.

She had been rehearsing the whole way from Cinderhollow—three days of it, muttered into her collar while Corin pretended to sleep by the fire. She had opening lines. She had dignified silences. She had, at one point, a devastating exit line involving the phrase *"and that is why I never wrote,"* which she suspected she'd only deploy if things went very badly or very well.

None of her rehearsals had accounted for the gates.

They were not as she remembered them. The twin pillars of black granite still stood where the coven's founders had raised them four centuries ago, but someone had grown a living arch between them—hawthorn and rowan braided so tightly together they had become one tree with two opinions—and its branches hung heavy with small bells made of folded birch-bark. As they approached, every bell rang at once.

Every bell. At once.

"Subtle," Corin murmured at her shoulder.

"They know we're here." Ulyana stopped at the edge of the shadow the arch threw across the road. "They knew we were here two days ago, probably. Possibly before we left Cinderhollow. Possibly before I decided to leave Cinderhollow."

"Is that a joke or a genuine fear?"

"With this coven? Yes."

Corin looked up at the arch. In the three days since the river crossing—since she had torn a hole in the world with her own two hands and stepped through it carrying him—he had changed in small ways she kept noticing sideways, the way you notice weather. He stood straighter. He flinched less. And when he looked at things now, he looked at them like a man who half-remembered having seen them somewhere before and couldn't decide whether that was comforting or horrifying.

"The bells are watching us," he said.

"Bells don't watch."

"These do." He pointed without touching. "There's a knot in each one. Same knot. It's the one from your stall—the luck-knots."

Ulyana went cold from the boots up. "That's my knot. I invented that knot. I invented it because I needed something the Wardens wouldn't recognize, something no coven taught me—"

"And now it's hanging on their gate," Corin finished quietly. "Ulyana. They've been reading your work for years."

She stood there a long moment, twenty years of careful invisibility rearranging themselves in her chest like furniture after a flood. Then she laughed—one short, unlovely bark of a laugh.

"Of course they have. Of course. I spent two decades hiding from them and they've been collecting my homework."

* * *

The path beyond the gate wound up through terraced gardens gone slightly wild in the way of gardens tended by people who believed weeds had feelings too. Ulyana remembered hauling water up these paths as an apprentice, eleven years old and furious about it, and Brenna beside her turning the chore into a game, into a song, into a story about a water-sprite with a grudge against buckets. She had spent twenty years remembering Thornhollow as a prison. Walking back through it, she kept tripping over evidence that it had also been a home, which was somehow worse.

Corin walked close enough that their shoulders bumped on the narrow turns. He didn't say anything. He had gotten very good, in three days, at saying nothing in a way that felt like company instead of absence. It occurred to her that a man with no memories had probably practiced that skill his whole hollow life.

Halfway up, the garden itself reacted to her.

It was nothing dramatic. A patch of feverfew turned its white heads to follow her passage. A stand of nettles—which she had once charmed, badly, at thirteen, to stop stinging her—parted like courtiers making room. Small things. But magic remembers, and Thornhollow's magic had clearly been keeping a scrapbook.

"They missed you," Corin said.

"Gardens can't miss people."

"You're a witch. Don't lie to me about what gardens can do."

She was spared answering by the arrival of the welcome committee, if you could call it that.

Three witches stood at the top of the last terrace, arranged with the studied casualness of people who had definitely been waiting there since dawn. Two were strangers—young, robed in the grey-and-green of full sisters, regarding Ulyana with the open curiosity of women who had grown up hearing her name as a cautionary tale. The third—

The third was Brenna.

Twenty years should have changed her more. Her hair had gone iron-grey at the temples and she wore it braided back with silver wire now, and there were new lines around her mouth, the particular geography of a woman who had spent two decades being responsible for other people. But her eyes were the same: dark, quick, and currently doing an absolutely terrible job of looking cold and ceremonial, because they kept doing this thing—this flicker, this catch—every time they landed on Ulyana's face, like a hearth-fire spitting despite the damp.

"Ulyana Pemberton," Brenna said, in a voice pitched precisely for official record. "You return to Thornhollow after twenty years of absence, without summons—" a pause, the barest flicker of a glance at the crow-shaped absence where a certain letter had been delivered, "—without *invitation*, accompanied by an unknown man, bearing—"

"Brenna."

"—bearing, I am told, a book that was ordered destroyed before either of us could grow breasts, and—"

"*Brenna.*"

"What?" Brenna snapped, and then caught herself, visibly, jaw tightening, hands folding into formal positions at her belt. High Witch posture. She had learned it well. "Forgive me. Sisters, this is Ulyana of Cinderhollow, returned to us. See her safely quartered. Ulyana—the elders will convene at moonrise. Until then, you will be treated as a guest of the coven." A beat. Her eyes did the hearth-flicker thing again. "A *guest*. Not a prisoner. I want that noted by everyone, including whatever the garden told you on the way up, because I know it talks."

One of the young sisters coughed in a way that suggested the garden talked a great deal.

Brenna turned on her heel and strode toward the hall, robes snapping. Then, ten paces distant, without turning around, without breaking stride, in a voice pitched for exactly one listener:

"You look terrible, Ula. Eat something before moonrise."

* * *

They gave her the apprentice room.

Not her old apprentice room—that door, she noticed, was shut, and someone had laid fresh rowan sprigs across its threshold, which was either reverence or a seal and she genuinely could not tell—but a smaller room at the end of the east corridor, with a sloped ceiling and a window overlooking the herb terraces and a bed exactly the width of a person who was expected to sleep alone and like it.

Corin took one look at it and said, "I'll take the floor."

"You don't have to—"

"Ulyana." He sat down on the window seat, which creaked in protest, and looked at her with that disarming directness of his, the one that came from having no history to hide behind. "For three days you've been rehearsing arguments with a woman you haven't seen in twenty years. You need rest more than you need a chaperone. And I need—" he paused, choosing honesty, which was a habit of his she was starting to find dangerous, "—I need to be somewhere quiet tonight. This place is *loud*. Underneath the loud. Like a hundred conversations happening in a room I can't see."

Her stomach dropped. "You're hearing the coven's workings."

"I'm hearing *something*." He rubbed his arms. "And it keeps almost saying my name."

She wanted to argue, and didn't. Instead she dug through her pack, found the last of the fever-charms she hadn't burned back in Cinderhollow—a small knotted thing smelling of rosemary and stubbornness—and pressed it into his hand.

"It won't stop dreams. But it'll keep them from sticking. You'll wake up knowing you dreamed, not drowning in it."

He turned it over in his fingers. "This is your knot."

"I'm aware."

"It's on their gate, Ulyana. Why is your knot on their gate?"

"Because apparently I've been writing letters to this place my entire exile without knowing it." She sat down beside him on the window seat, both of them ignoring the creak. "Every charm I ever sold—I built them from scratch. From instinct. Because I refused to use anything the coven taught me, and it turns out instinct has a bloodline. Everything I made was Thornhollow craft wearing a disguise. They must have collected every luck-knot that came out of Cinderhollow's night market and read them like correspondence."

"So you never actually left."

"I left *geographically*."

Corin huffed something that was almost a laugh, then went quiet, thumb tracing the charm's knots. When he spoke again his voice had dropped into the register he used for the things that scared him.

"When the bells rang today. All of them at once. For half a second, I heard them ring *backward*. Like the sound being taken in instead of given out. And something under the mountain heard it too. I felt it turn over." He looked at her. "We're close, aren't we. Whatever's down there—it knows we're coming."

"Yes," Ulyana said, because lying to him had stopped working sometime around the river crossing. "And it's known longer than we have."

* * *

She did not eat before moonrise. She told herself it was nerves; she knew it was spite; she suspected Brenna knew it was spite too, because when Ulyana arrived at the great hall, she found a covered bowl of stew waiting just inside the doors with a note pinned under the lid in Brenna's unmistakable handwriting:

*Eat or I will make the moonrise meeting about your nutrition. — B.*

Ulyana ate. It was venison and barley and far too much thyme, exactly the way Cook Maretta had made it twenty years ago, which meant either Maretta was still alive or her recipe had been canonized. It tasted like being eleven. She hated how much she needed it.

The hall itself had changed less than she expected. The long trestle tables remained, scarred by centuries of knife-work and one memorable incident involving a summoned goose. The rafters still held the drying bundles of herbs, and the great hearth still burned with the low blue-edged flame that never went out—coven-fire, older than any witch alive, the flame they said had been lit from the first dragon-fire itself.

Around the high table sat seven elders, robed in grey, faces composed into various flavors of disapproval. Mother Ysolde was among them—older, smaller, but with eyes like awls—and when she saw Ulyana she smiled with exactly one corner of her mouth, which from Ysolde was a standing ovation.

And at the center, in the High Witch's chair that had been old Mother Casilde's, sat Brenna, and Brenna was *different* here. Ulyana watched it happen in real time: the woman who had told her to eat something became a wall of ceremony, spine straight, voice level, every word weighed on scales calibrated for judgment.

"Sisters," Brenna said. "Ulyana Pemberton stands before you. Twenty years ago she fled this coven on the eve of her binding rite. She abandoned her sisters, her oaths, and her obligations. She carried our craft into a city that burns witches, hid it behind market-stalls and merchant patter, and let the world believe her nothing." Around the table, heads nodded; one elder's mouth thinned. "She broke faith with us."

"She did," Ulyana said.

The hall went silent. It had not been the answer anyone had scripted.

Ulyana stepped forward, heart hammering, and discovered—to her enormous irritation—that twenty years of selling charms to frightened strangers had given her a skill she'd never named: she knew how to speak to a hostile room without flinching.

"I broke faith. I ran. I chose my own skin over my sisters, and I have paid for it in ways none of you can see, in small coin, every day, for twenty years. I am not here to apologize for surviving." She held Brenna's gaze. "But I'm not here to pretend I didn't run, either. Both things are true. I've learned you can carry both."

An elder to the left—Vessa, maybe, or Vessa's student with Vessa's frown—began, "The rite you fled—"

"Is why I'm here," Ulyana said. "I know. The fire beneath the mountain is dying. Your letter said it. My book says worse." She set 'The Ashen Hours' on the high table, and even wrapped in oilcloth it seemed to darken the candles near it. "And the prophecy that started this names the man standing at your door as a soul sold to the court before he was born. So ask me your questions, sisters. Judge me however you need to. But do it quickly, because we have a season, maybe less, and I did not walk through the ash country to admire your architecture."

Silence again. Somewhere in the rafters, the dried herbs rustled though there was no wind.

Then Mother Ysolde laughed—a dry, papery sound like pages turning—and said to the table at large, "Twenty years away and she walks in and reorganizes the meeting in under a minute. Casilde would have loved her. Casilde *did* love her, which is why she cried for a week after the girl ran, though she'd have hexed me for telling you."

"That's enough, Ysolde," Brenna said, but her voice cracked on the name of their dead teacher, just barely, just once, and her eyes—her terrible, honest eyes—flickered hearth-ward again.

The meeting dissolved into logistics after that: wards to be renewed, the book to be examined, Corin to be questioned gently by sisters skilled in listening. Ulyana answered what she could and deflected what she couldn't, and through all of it she tracked Brenna the way you track weather—aware of every shift in pressure, every front moving in behind the official face.

It ended near midnight. The elders filed out. The young sisters extinguished the extra candles. And Brenna said, to the room, to no one, in her ceremonial voice: "Ulyana. A word. In my study."

Which was High Witch for *follow me or else*, and so Ulyana followed.

* * *

The study was small and cluttered and smelled of ink and dried lavender, and it had been Casilde's study once, and before that someone else's, and the desk still bore the burn-mark Ulyana herself had made at fourteen with a carelessly aimed cantrip that Brenna had taken the blame for. Ulyana looked at that mark and felt twenty years collapse like a telescope.

Brenna shut the door. Stood with her hand on it. Didn't turn around.

"You look terrible," she said again, quieter this time. "I said it earlier, but it bears repeating. You look like a woman who hasn't slept properly since the river."

"I haven't."

"Mm." Brenna turned. And the ceremony fell off her like a cloak she'd been holding up with both arms, and what was underneath was just Brenna—tired, fierce, terrified Brenna, who crossed the room in four strides and grabbed Ulyana by the shoulders and shook her, once, hard.

"Twenty *years*, Ula. Twenty years I wrote to you and got nothing. Twenty years I sent crows that came back empty. Do you know what I thought? I thought you were dead for the first five. I *mourned* you. I lit the mourning candle, Ula, I sat vigil, I—"

"I know. I got the letter. The one that said come home or you come for me. Very tender."

"That was the *official* letter! I wrote you forty others and burned them because every single one sounded like—" She stopped. Breathed. Her hands were shaking on Ulyana's shoulders. "You could have written *once*. One line. *Alive, well, don't look for me.* One line, Ula."

"I know." Ulyana's throat hurt. "I know. I'm sorry. I thought—if I reached out, they'd find me, and if they found me, they'd finish the binding by force, and I would rather die unfound than—"

"Be owned." Brenna released her, stepping back, laughing in a way with no humor in it at all. "Yes. I remember. You said that at fourteen, about chores. You said it at sixteen about marriage. You said it the night before the rite like the world was going to bend around your principles." She dragged a hand through her iron-grey braid. "And then you ran, and the world bent, and I stayed, and I took the rite, and I have been *bound* for twenty years, sister. Bound to that court. I feel it every hour. A leash made of light, hooked under my ribs, running down into the dark. And every year Mother Casilde grew older and the coven grew smaller and the fire under the mountain grew dimmer, and every year I thought: *my sister is out there free, and she hates me, and at least one of us gets to hate me from somewhere warm.*"

"Brenna—"

"I reported you." It came out flat, fast, like ripping off a bandage. "Fourteen years ago. I told the coven's contacts in Cinderhollow where you were. I gave them your street."

The floor tilted. Ulyana gripped the edge of the desk—the desk with her own burn-mark—and stared at her.

"You—what?"

"Not the Wardens. Never the Wardens. Our own watchers, so we could keep an eye on you, so that when the fire began to fail—and it was already failing, Ula, we've known for a decade—we'd know whether you were alive to help." Brenna's chin came up, defiant and miserable at once. "So yes. I found you years before that crow ever flew. I've known about your stall, your knots, your little life, for fourteen years. I read your luck-charms when our traders brought them back. I kept one. It's in the drawer. Right there." She pointed at the desk drawer Ulyana was gripping. "Second knot from the left, slightly crooked, because you always rushed the third loop."

Ulyana opened the drawer with numb fingers. There it was. A cheap luck-knot, faded, crooked third loop, kept for fourteen years like a relic.

"You watched me," she whispered, "the whole time. My whole invisible life. I wasn't invisible at all."

"No," Brenna said softly. "You were just hiding from everyone except the one person who loved you. Which, honestly, Ula, is the most witchy thing you've ever done."

Ulyana laughed, or sobbed, or produced some sound that contained both, and Brenna crossed the room again and this time it wasn't a shake, it was an embrace, fierce and crushing and twenty years overdue, and they stood in Casilde's old study holding onto each other like the building might sway.

"I'm angry at you," Ulyana managed into her sister's shoulder.

"Good. Stay angry. Anger keeps better than forgiveness."

"Why did you give me the cold ceremony? Out there, at the gate, in front of everyone—"

"Because half those elders think you're a deserter and the other half think you're a weapon, and if they saw me greet you like—" Brenna pulled back, and her eyes were wet and furious, "—like *this*, they'd start managing you, maneuvering you, using you, before you'd eaten a single meal. Ceremony is armor, Ula. I've had twenty years to learn that lesson from watching you learn it wrong."

Ulyana wiped her face with her sleeve. "Corin. The elders want him questioned."

"The elders want him *measured*." Brenna's voice cooled several degrees, and something protective and sharp moved behind her eyes. "Some of them—Vessa especially—have been saying since your letter arrived that a sold soul is a key, and keys get *used*. That perhaps the best use of a hollowed man is as bait, or payment, or a bargaining chip with the court below."

"He's not a chip."

"I know that."

"Do they?"

Brenna was quiet a moment. Outside, wind moved through the gate-bells, and faintly, distantly, they rang—not all at once, this time, but one by one, like something counting.

"Come to the morning circle," she said finally. "Where we explain the rite. The true rite, not the story you were told at sixteen. You need to understand why we bind ourselves, Ula—what the binding actually *does*, and why the fact that yours failed, that you slipped it, makes you the only witch in this coven who can walk into that court and walk back out." She held Ulyana's gaze, and beneath the weariness in her eyes there was something older and sadder, something that had waited twenty years to be understood. "Because tomorrow, when you hear what I agreed to at eighteen—when you understand what's hooked under my ribs—you're going to realize the rite you ran from was the kindest lie this coven ever told. And you're going to wish, really wish, that you'd let them finish it."

Beyond the window, the last bell stopped ringing.

The silence it left behind felt, to Ulyana, exactly like something beneath the mountain drawing breath.
