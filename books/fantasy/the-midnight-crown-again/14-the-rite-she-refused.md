## 14. The Rite She Refused

The guest chamber they gave Ulyana in Thornhollow was the same one she'd slept in as an apprentice, and someone had clearly tried to make it feel like a kindness. The shutters had been freshly oiled so they wouldn't squeal the way they used to. A fire had been laid and lit before she arrived, which meant Brenna had known exactly when she'd cross the threshold, which meant Brenna had probably been watching the road for two days with her arms crossed, pretending she hadn't.

There were even flowers on the sill—yarrow and dried lavender, arranged with more enthusiasm than skill.

"They still smell like headaches," Ulyana said to the empty room, and found, to her surprise, that she was smiling.

Corin had been given the room across the hall, and through the wall she could hear him moving about—the careful, restless pacing of a man trying to figure out whether he was a guest or a prisoner. She didn't blame him. Thornhollow did both equally well.

She'd been back less than four hours when the knock came. Two knocks, a pause, then one more. Brenna's knock. It had been Brenna's knock twenty years ago too, when they were girls sharing a dormitory and Brenna wanted to sneak down to the kitchens for honey bread without waking Mother Ysolde.

Ulyana opened the door. Brenna stood there holding a clay jug of mulled cider and wearing the expression of a woman arriving to have a conversation she had rehearsed eleven times on the stairs.

"Your room," Brenna said. "It hasn't changed."

"You changed it. You put flowers in it. You've never put flowers anywhere in your life."

"I put them there because Ilsa the younger grows them now and she asked me who used to sleep here, and I said no one, and then I felt like a liar." Brenna pushed past her into the room, set the jug on the table, and glared at it as though it had betrayed her. "Sit down, Ulyana. Please."

*Please.* Brenna had said please maybe six times in the entire span of their girlhood friendship, and every one of those times had preceded either an apology or an argument. Ulyana sat.

* * *

Brenna poured the cider with great concentration, as if the mug might bite her. Then she sat across from Ulyana, knees together, hands wrapped around her own cup, and stared into the steam for so long that Ulyana finally said, "You're stalling."

"I'm gathering."

"You've been gathering since I walked through the gate. You gathered all through supper. You gathered at me over the roasted parsnips."

"The parsnips were incidental." Brenna looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed, and she was fighting it the way she always fought things—jaw set, spine straight, like grief was a Warden she could out-stare. "I owe you an accounting. Of why we do it. Why we did it to you."

"The rite."

"The rite." Brenna nodded slowly. "You ran because we tried to bind you. That's the shape of it as you've carried it for twenty years. So let me tell you the shape of it as we carry it."

Outside, the evening bells rang from the coven tower—seven strokes, the hour of lessons. Somewhere below, children's voices chanted a counting rhyme that was secretly a ward-song, the way all the best children's rhymes in Thornhollow were. Ulyana had been one of those voices once. The thought landed strangely, like a stone dropped down a well she'd thought was filled in.

"The mountain court," Brenna began, "feeds on souls. You knew that much even then—you heard it in the market tales, the ones about pale folk who trade in bottled lives. What the tales don't tell you is *how* they feed. They can't take a soul by force. Not wholly. A soul fights, and a fighting soul spoils the way meat spoils—they get something sour and thin and it doesn't last. So they take souls by *consent*. Twisted consent, tricked consent, desperate consent—but consent nonetheless. A bargain freely entered. A debt freely assumed."

"That's what the binding rite is," Ulyana said quietly. It wasn't a question. She'd understood it even at fifteen; she simply hadn't had the words, or the courage, or anyone willing to confirm it.

"The rite binds an apprentice's soul to the court's ledger. We give them a claim—a lien, Mother Ysolde calls it, though she uses fancier words. In exchange, the court leaves the coven alone. Leaves the town alone. Leaves the *valley* alone." Brenna's voice went flat and careful. "Every witch bound at sixteen buys, with her own self, another year of safety for everyone who isn't bound. That was the arithmetic they taught us. That was the arithmetic I swallowed whole, because I was sixteen and obedient and terrified, and you—" her mouth twisted, "—you stood up in the middle of the circle and told Mother Ysolde the court could eat its arithmetic."

"I remember being dragged out by my ear."

"You remember being dragged out by your ear, and then you remember running. What you don't know—" Brenna set down her cup, and her hands weren't quite steady, "—is what happened after."

* * *

"After," Brenna said, "the elders came to me. The night you fled. I was seventeen hours from my own binding—I was next in line after you—and they sat me down in this same chair, more or less, this same fire, and they told me the rest of it. The part nobody tells apprentices until it's too late to matter."

Brenna rose and went to the window, looking out at the dark bulk of the mountain, its crown invisible against the stars.

"A bound soul can't enter the court freely. That's the cruelty of the design—it's not a flaw, it's the *feature*. Once you're in their ledger, you belong to them wherever you go, but the doors only open inward for the bound. You can be summoned down. You can be taken down. But you cannot walk down of your own will, and—this is the important part, Ulyana, so hear me—you cannot walk back *up*. The bound go down and stay down. That's where the coven's sisters go, generation after generation. Down. Into the soul-market. Bottled or bled or worn, depending on the mood of whatever queen sits the throne of teeth."

Ulyana felt cold despite the fire. "And the unbound?"

"The unbound are useless to them. Worthless as food, worthless as leverage—except for one thing." Brenna turned. "An unbound soul can enter the court and leave it. Walk in, walk out, no claim, no chain. There has never been more than a handful alive at any time. Right now, in all the world, as far as any of us knows, there are three: Mother Ysolde, who is too old and too wise to waste; a fisherwoman in the Saltmarshes who wants nothing to do with any of this; and you."

The fire popped. Down in the yard, someone laughed—one of the young ones, bright and unafraid, and the sound of it made Ulyana's chest ache in a way she couldn't name.

"So when you refused the rite," Brenna said, "when you ran rather than be bound—you didn't just save yourself. You became the only weapon the valley had that couldn't be confiscated. And none of us knew it yet. None of us understood what you'd done until years later, when the first of our bound sisters stopped writing home."

"Brenna—"

"Let me finish. Twenty years, Ulyana. I have been High Witch for nine of them, and every year I have signed the ledger page sending another girl down, and every year I have hated myself more efficiently. I got good at it. That's what being High Witch is—getting good at hating yourself on schedule." She laughed, short and bitter. "You think I stayed angry at you for running? I stayed *grateful*. Grateful and furious about being grateful, because gratitude toward someone who abandoned you is a very uncomfortable thing to carry."

"I didn't abandon—" Ulyana started, and stopped, because the word was true and they both knew it. She'd run. She'd left Brenna to stand alone in that circle, left her to be bound alone, left her to inherit a machine she'd refused to help build. "I was afraid."

"Yes," Brenna said simply. "You were. And you were right to be. And I have spent twenty years wishing I'd been brave enough to run beside you." She came back to the chair but didn't sit. "So now you know the shape of it. The court feeds on consent. The bound feed it forever. The unbound are the only ones who can go down and come back—and the fire under the mountain is dying, and Mavrosa's court is getting hungrier and more reckless, and the seals are held by a dragon that is starving to death while it works. That is why we sent the letter. That is why I threatened you. Because I needed you here, and threatening you was the only language I was sure you'd believe."

"It worked," Ulyana admitted.

"I know." Brenna almost smiled. "You always did respond well to ultimatums. Remember when I told you if you ate the last honey bun I'd never speak to you again?"

"You spoke to me within the hour."

"Because you split it. Even then. You always found the third option." Brenna finally sat, and leaned forward, and for a moment the High Witch of Thornhollow fell away and it was just Brenna, her sister-apprentice, her almost-sister, tired and human in the firelight. "I'm sorry. For the cold ceremony at the gate. For the letter. For every year I let you believe you were a coward for saving yourself."

Ulyana discovered her eyes were burning. She blinked hard and pretended to be interested in the cider. "You're forgiven. Imperfectly. Don't push it."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

They drank in silence for a while, and it was the good kind of silence, the kind they'd shared over stolen honey bread a lifetime ago, before ledgers and thrones and teeth.

Then Ulyana said, "There's something else. You've been circling it since supper."

Brenna's cup paused halfway to her mouth. "Have I."

"You get a crease. Between the eyebrows. You've had it since you introduced Corin to the elders." Ulyana set down her mug. "What do they want with him?"

* * *

Brenna did not answer immediately. Outside, the ward-bells finished their seventh stroke and the mountain wind picked at the shutters.

"He's a sold soul," Brenna said at last. "Sold at birth, whole and clean, never claimed. Do you understand how rare that is? Most of what the court holds is fragmented—shaved, sipped, traded in pieces. A whole soul, intact, still attached to a living body? The court would value it beyond crowns. Beyond queenship. It's the single most precious object in their entire ledger, and it's currently sleeping down the hall from my chambers."

"So the elders want to use him."

"The elders want to *bargain* with him. Or with his soul, which amounts to the same thing and is precisely why you're going to hate it." Brenna rubbed the bridge of her nose. "Mavrosa will trade anything for a chance to reclaim a whole soul—power, passage, perhaps even concessions on the feeding. Elder Maren wants to offer Corin to her. Dangle him. Let the court open its gates wide to close their hands around him, and in that moment of opening, send in the only person alive who can walk back out again."

"Send in *me*."

"With him as bait." Brenna met her eyes, unflinching, because she was Brenna, and she had never once flinched from a truth she deserved to deliver. "The council votes tomorrow night. I am telling you tonight because you deserve to hear it from me and not from Maren's smug face across a council table. And because I need to know, Ulyana—before I walk into that room and fight for you—what you intend to do about it."

Ulyana looked into the fire for a long moment. She thought of Corin tasting ash each morning, of a jar somewhere beneath the mountain labeled with a name he didn't know was his. She thought of a circle, twenty years ago, and a girl who'd learned—too late—that running wasn't the same as escaping.

When she looked up, Brenna was watching her with something that might have been hope.

"Tell the elders," Ulyana said, "that the third option is mine to find. And that if anyone dangles Aldous Vane like a lure, they'll learn what an unbound witch does with her freedom."

Brenna exhaled slowly. "There she is," she murmured. "Twenty years late, but there she is."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the mountain hummed—low and hungry, like a belly waiting to be fed.
