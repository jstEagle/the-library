## 15. Coven Politics

The Thornhollow coven kept its council chamber in the hollow of a great dead oak, which Ulyana had always found to be the coven in miniature: something once alive, now hollowed out, and full of people arguing.

She stood at the base of the spiral stairs with Corin at her side and Brenna a step ahead, listening to the murmur of voices filter down through the bark like bees in a wall. Twenty years since she'd last climbed these steps. She had hoped the smell would be different—beeswax and cedar and the particular green rot of old spells—but of course it wasn't. Smell was the most loyal of all the senses. It waited for you.

"Nervous?" Corin asked quietly.

"I'm about to walk into a room full of witches who remember me as the girl who ran," Ulyana said. "Nervous implies I think it might go well."

"That's fair." He glanced up the stairwell. "For what it's worth, I've spent my whole remembered life being looked at by people who wanted something from me. I'll manage."

"I know you will. That's rather the problem."

Brenna turned on the stair above them. "Whatever they say in there—and they will say terrible things, because that is what elders *do*—remember that I called this council to help you, not to hand anyone over. Say your piece and let me do mine."

"When have I ever been good at letting other people do things for me?"

"Never," said Brenna. "It's one of your more exhausting qualities. Up you go."

* * *

The chamber was round, ringed with thirteen seats carved from the oak itself, nine of them filled. Mother Ysolde sat at the far end looking as though she'd been sitting there since before the oak died, which was possibly true. The others were faces half-remembered: Sister Maren, who had taught Ulyana herb-lore and whose hands still trembled slightly; Elder Cassian, broad and grey-bearded, with the patient expression of a man who did arithmetic while other people talked; three sisters Ulyana couldn't name at all, which stung more than she expected. And near the door, young-faced and watchful, a witch she didn't recognize at all—clearly placed there to observe the outsider.

They observed her now. All of them. Nine pairs of eyes doing sums of their own.

"Sisters," Brenna said, taking the empty tenth seat. "You know why we're gathered. This is Ulyana Pemberton, returned after twenty years, and this is Corin—the sold soul named in *The Ashen Hours*. He is the key to the mountain court, and the fire beneath it is dying. We have perhaps a season."

"We know what the book says," said Cassian mildly. "We've had the book's contents memorized longer than either of these two has been alive. What we don't yet have is agreement on what to do about it." He turned his gaze on Ulyana, unhurried, appraising. "Welcome home, runaway. You look well, considering."

"Thank you. You look exactly the same, which is either flattery or an accusation."

A ripple of something—not quite laughter, but its cousin—moved through the room. Ysolde's mouth twitched. Ulyana counted that a small victory, and took it, because she suspected she wouldn't get many today.

"You bring us a soul the court has owned since before he drew breath," Cassian continued. "A soul the prophecy says must return 'home.' And you bring him here first. Why? If the fire needs him fed to it, every day he spends above ground eating our food and asking questions is a day closer to everything beneath Cinderhollow waking up hungry."

"Because I'm not feeding him to anything," Ulyana said. "He goes down there *alive*, with his soul restored to him, or not at all."

"And how exactly do you propose to restore it?"

"That's what Seraiva is for."

The name landed in the chamber like a dropped glass. One of the unnamed sisters actually gasped, which Ulyana privately found delightful. It was nice to learn that some things could still shock a coven that had survived dragon wars, purges, and presumably several centuries of its own meetings.

"The exiled queen," Cassian said carefully. "You mean to bargain with Seraiva the Undrowned."

"She can open the way down. She claims no one else can. I believe her."

"You *believe* her." Cassian laced his fingers. "Child, do you know what queens are? A queen is a hunger with a crown on. Seraiva ruled that court for four hundred years. Whatever she asks of you, be certain it will cost less than what Mavrosa would ask, and be certain it will not cost nothing."

"I'm aware. We've met her price."

Ysolde spoke then, for the first time, her voice like dry leaves. "And her price was?"

Ulyana hesitated. Beside her, Corin shifted. "That's between us," she said. "It's paid already, or it will be. It doesn't concern the council."

"It concerns the council if it weakens our position," said a sharp-faced sister near the window—one of the unnamed ones, deciding to become named by force of sheer disapproval. "You come back after twenty years of—what shall we call it? Selling luck-knots to farmers?—and you want us to treat you as though you understand how negotiations with immortal powers work."

"Maren," Brenna started—

"No, let her finish," Ulyana said pleasantly. "I'd love to hear what twenty years of selling luck-knots to farmers qualifies me for. Because I'll tell you what it qualified me for. It taught me what fear looks like on ordinary faces. It taught me what people will pay when they're frightened, and what they'll forgive, and what they won't. Four hundred years ago Seraiva was a queen. Right now, tonight, she's a woman hiding in a drowned chapel because her own court ate her throne out from under her. I've spent two decades reading frightened women in market squares. I know exactly what I'm looking at."

The sharp sister opened her mouth. Ysolde raised one finger, and the room went quiet with the obedience of long habit.

"Charming," Ysolde said. "Rude, but charming. You were always rude. It's why I liked you." She folded her hands atop her cane. "But rudeness isn't a plan, child, and neither is faith in a deposed queen. So let us speak plainly about plans. Cassian. Tell them."

Ulyana's stomach dipped. She knew that tone. Every teacher she'd ever had used it right before the lesson became a trap.

* * *

Cassian laid it out the way a man lays out cards he's already won with.

"The court feeds on souls. Bottled lives, traded among pale courtiers—that's the engine of the place, yes?" He waited for Ulyana's grudging nod. "And the fire is starving because the supply has thinned. Fewer souls coming down, less fuel for the flame, weaker seals. Now. Here is what interests me." He tapped the arm of his chair. "A soul the court *already owns*. Sold before birth. Not stolen, not taken—a debt, legally contracted, owed to the throne of teeth. Mavrosa cannot pretend otherwise. Her own law binds her."

"So?" Ulyana said, though she already hated where this was going with her entire being.

"So we do not send Corin down as an offering. We send him down as a *claimant*. He walks into that court as property demanding restitution—or better, as bait. The court will not destroy him. They'll want to hold him, examine him, perhaps even restore him, because a soul that walked free above ground for nineteen years is a curiosity, and courts collect curiosities. While they gather around him, marveling—" Cassian spread his hands, "—our people follow. We take stock of the seals. We count the courtiers. We find where the fire is fed and how. Perhaps we find where the bottled souls are kept. Knowledge, sisters. Knowledge is worth more than any single rescue."

"Our people," Ulyana repeated. "Meaning who, exactly?"

"A party of bound sisters. Witches tied to the court by the rite—they can enter and leave freely, as the rite allows."

"Can they?" Ulyana laughed, and it came out sharper than she intended. "Forgive me, but the rite lets bound witches pass through the doors. It does not let them lie, cheat, spy, or leave with anything the court considers theirs. I've read the terms. My mentor made me read them three times before I refused to sign. The court is *generous* with passage and merciless with fine print."

"The court also hasn't seen an incursion in centuries," Cassian said. "Complacency is a crack in any wall."

"And you'd drive that crack open using a man who didn't consent to any of this." Ulyana turned to face Corin, deliberately, making the room look at him too. "Go on. Tell them what you told me. Tell them about the year you don't remember. Tell them what it's like waking up tasting ash and knowing some part of you is already down there, in a jar, on a shelf, labeled."

Corin was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice was steady, which somehow made it worse.

"They're offering strategy," he said. "I understand that. Truly. If I thought it would work—if I thought I was bait and nothing worse—I might even volunteer. But I've met what owns me. I dream about it. There's a throne down there with teeth in it, and something sits on it that knows my name, and it has been *patient* for nineteen years. Bait works when the fish doesn't know the hook exists." He looked around the chamber. "This fish built the hook."

Silence. Somewhere far below, wind moved through the oak's roots with a sound like breathing.

"Then we are at an impasse of sentiment," Cassian said at last, not unkindly. "You love him. Love makes poor strategy."

"Yes," Ulyana said. "And survival logic makes monsters, and I've watched this coven make them before. I ran rather than sign that rite. I am telling you, as someone who read the contract and fled it: anything you send into that mountain belongs to it within a day. Including him. *Especially* him. You'd be delivering the court its own collateral, gift-wrapped, and calling it cunning."

"The alternative," said the sharp sister—Maren's neighbor, the one with the disapproving mouth—"is that the fire dies, the seals fail, and everything sealed under Cinderhollow wakes up under Cinderhollow. Have you considered the arithmetic of that, runaway? Thousands of souls above ground against one soul below. Even you should be able to do that sum."

"Oh, I've done it." Ulyana's voice went quiet, which those who knew her well understood to be the dangerous register. "I did it every night for twenty years. Every time I hid a charm instead of helping someone. Every time a Warden dragged a neighbor away while I polished my stall and looked invisible. Do you know what I learned? The sum always comes out the same. One soul, traded for thousands. And then next month it's one soul again, and then another, and the thousands never notice, because the ones paying are always the ones nobody counts. That's how the Magistrate runs Cinderhollow and it's how Mavrosa runs her court and it is *exactly* how this coven proposes to run this rescue. I won't be part of the machine. Not again. Not ever again."

The words rang in the hollow wood. Ulyana realized her hands were shaking and made them stop through sheer spite.

* * *

It was Ysolde who broke the silence, and she did it by laughing—a dry, rustling sound, like pages turning.

"There it is," the old woman said. "Twenty years gone and not one ounce of it tamed. Sit down, child, before you fall down; righteous fury burns blood-sugar terribly."

"Mother Ysolde," Cassian began, "the girl is letting feeling—"

"The girl is letting *accuracy*," Ysolde snapped, and Cassian subsided, because everyone in that room had learned long ago which elder outranked whom, regardless of seats. Ysolde levered herself upright with her cane and crossed the chamber slowly, so that all of them had time to feel whatever it was she intended them to feel. She stopped in front of Ulyana and studied her face with eyes gone milk-pale but apparently missing nothing.

"You refused the rite at sixteen," Ysolde said. "Do you know why I let you refuse? Why none of us forced the binding on you, when forcing it would have been simple?"

"Because Brenna hid me in the root cellar for three days."

"Because Brenna hid you in the root cellar, *and* because I told the council you were unstable and would likely botch the rite and shame us all." The old woman's smile was thin and ancient. "I lied to protect you, and you've spent twenty years thinking you escaped by running. But hear me now, Ulyana Pemberton, because this is the truth of it: unbound witches are the only souls in this world who can walk into the mountain court and walk back out *whole*. Bound sisters pass freely, yes—but freely means the court may keep whatever pieces of them it likes. You alone can go down, deal with queens, defy thrones, and return with everything you brought. That is why the prophecy names *you*. Not Brenna. Not me. The witch who fled."

Ulyana felt the floor tilt very slightly. "That's not—you're saying I'm useful *because* I ran."

"I'm saying the wound was the making," Ysolde said simply. "Most gifts arrive dressed as catastrophes. It's the world's oldest joke and I've never found it funny myself." She turned to the council. "Sisters. The proposal before us is to use the sold soul as bait. I say nay. Not from sentiment—from craft. You do not dangle the only claimant the prophecy recognizes in front of the thing that owns him. You send the unbound witch, armed, prepared, *with him beside her as witness and anchor*, and you trust the woman who has already survived twenty years of a life designed to kill her slowly."

"And if she fails?" asked the disapproving sister.

"Then we are no worse off than your bait plan, except wiser and with cleaner consciences," Ysolde said. "Vote."

They voted. Ulyana watched hands rise one by one—Brenna first, then Maren, then Ysolde, then, after a pause long enough to contain an entire internal war, Cassian himself, followed grudgingly by the rest. Six to three. The motion carried: no bait. The unbound witch would lead.

Ulyana let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since the stairs.

"Don't celebrate," Brenna murmured as the council began to break apart into knots of muttering sisters. "Six to three means a third of this coven thinks trading Corin is reasonable, and the three loudest of those are the ones who control our stores, our wardings, and our ravens. Tonight's vote ends the argument in this room. It does not end the argument."

"Arguments never end in this room. They just get adjourned."

"True." Brenna's mouth curved, tired and fond. "You haven't changed."

"You have, a little. You're scarier."

"I've been High Witch for eleven years. Scary is the job description." She squeezed Ulyana's arm, brief and fierce. "Get some sleep. Both of you. Tomorrow you start training with Ysolde, and I promise you—" her eyes flicked toward the old woman, who was watching them with an expression of profound anticipation, "—she has been waiting twenty years for this, and she intends to enjoy it."

* * *

The coven put them in the guest cell in the east trunk, a small round room with a bed, a candle, and a window that looked out over the ash country they'd crossed to get here. Ulyana sat on the bed's edge while Corin stood at the window, watching the dark.

"Cassian's plan wasn't madness," Corin said at last. "I want you to know I saw the sense of it. If I were braver in the wrong direction, I might have agreed."

"If you'd agreed I'd have voted against the whole council and taken you back over the ash country myself."

"I know." He turned. In the candlelight his face had that quality she was learning to read—the hollowness behind his eyes flickering, filling, flickering again, like a lamp low on oil. "That's the strange thing. Nineteen years I've been nobody's. No family that claimed me, no court that wanted me, no self I could account for. And now there's a room full of immortals and witches debating my value like grain futures, and the person arguing hardest that I'm not currency at all is a woman who sells luck-knots."

"Sold. Past tense. I burned the stock, remember."

"I remember." He smiled, small and real. "What happens tomorrow? Really. Brenna said training, but with Ysolde it sounded less like lessons and more like sentencing."

"Fire-magic," Ulyana said. "The kind I abandoned. The kind I ran from." She rubbed her palms together absently; they ached in cold weather, an old ache with an old story attached. "She's going to make me relive the worst night of my life and then set my hands on fire. Probably in that order."

"And you'll do it?"

"For you? For the fire? For everyone sleeping above that starving seal?" She lay back on the narrow bed and stared at the ceiling beams, where somebody—some apprentice, decades ago, maybe Ulyana herself—had scratched a tiny sun into the wood. "Yes. Apparently I'll do it. Being brave turns out to be awful. Nobody warns you."

Down the corridor, faint through the living-dead wood of the great oak, Ulyana heard voices—low, urgent, three of them, moving away from the council chamber toward the stores. Toward the ravens.

Toward, unless her instincts had rotted along with everything else, the west trunk, where the coven kept its fast-flying birds and its older, less official agreements.

She closed her eyes and listened until the footsteps faded, and made a note to check the raven loft at dawn.

Some arguments, it seemed, had already been adjourned to somewhere else.
