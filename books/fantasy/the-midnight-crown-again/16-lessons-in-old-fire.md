## 16. Lessons in Old Fire

Mother Ysolde's cottage sat at the far edge of Thornhollow, past the bee-skeps and the rowan trees, in a hollow where the moss grew green even in the dead of winter. Ulyana had been sent there as a child, once, to deliver a basket of dried nettle and an apology from Brenna for something neither of them could now remember. She remembered only two things about that visit: the smell of woodsmoke and honey, and the old woman's hands, which were spotted like a plover's egg and moved with the unhurried certainty of someone who had never once been afraid of fire.

Now those same hands were holding a cup of willow-bark tea under Ulyana's nose with the gentle insistence of a woman who had raised forty witches and buried thirty-nine excuses.

"Drink," Ysolde said. "You've gone grey around the mouth. In my day that meant either fear or bad conscience, and I'm told you've plenty of both."

"I've been told my whole life I'm stubborn," Ulyana said, taking the cup because refusing it would take more energy than she had left.

"And were they right?"

"About the stubbornness? Entirely."

"Good." Ysolde settled into the chair opposite, joints crackling like green kindling. "Stubborn witches live longest. It's the agreeable ones who burn." She said it mildly, sipping her own tea, and let the words sit there in the smoky little room until Ulyana understood that she was not speaking generally at all.

The cottage was exactly as Ulyana half-remembered and wholly dreaded. Bundles of herbs hung from every beam. A kettle muttering over the hearth. And above the mantel, mounted on a plank of black oak, a single candle stub no longer than a thumb — burned down to almost nothing, yet still alight after what must have been fifty years. Its flame was the size of a grain of barley and burned without flickering, without consuming, patient as a held breath.

Ulyana caught herself staring at it and looked away too quickly.

"You remember the candle," Ysolde said.

"Everyone remembers the candle."

"Mm. And does everyone remember *why* there is a candle?"

Ulyana drank her tea and did not answer. The truth was that she remembered the lesson but not the point of it — the way you remember the shape of a door you walked through once and never again. Fire-magic lessons. The first year apprentices kneeling in a circle on the cold flagstones while Ysolde lit a single candle and told them: *Fire is the only magic that asks to be loved before it obeys. You cannot trick it, hide from it, or bargain with it. It knows what you are the moment you reach for it.*

Ulyana had reached for it exactly once. She is reaching for it again now, twenty years later, because the last dragon-fire beneath the mountain was guttering out, and guttering fires apparently required witches who could do more than tie luck-knots and lie charmingly about fever-charms.

Brenna had put it less poetically. "Ysolde taught all of us everything we know about flame," she'd said, pressing a jar of salve into Ulyana's hands for burns Ulyana hadn't earned yet. "And she's the only one in this coven who won't pretend you didn't run. Go to her. Let her be angry. Anger from Ysolde is worth more than kindness from anyone else."

* * *

The first lesson began at dawn, which Ulyana felt was unnecessarily dramatic, since nothing good had ever happened to her at dawn except sleep ending.

They stood outside, in the frost-hardened garden, where Ysolde had arranged three things on a flat stone: a candle, a bowl of water, and a dead sparrow laid out with terrible dignity.

"Water first," Ysolde said. "Show me your charmwork."

Ulyana knelt and did what she had done ten thousand times in Cinderhollow's night market — coaxed the water into shapes, into small soft movements, a ripple here, a slow spiral there. It was honest work, quiet work, work that could pass for sleight-of-hand if a Warden squinted. Her fingers knew it the way they knew braiding hair. The water rose in a thin ribbon and turned a lazy loop, glittering in the early light.

Ysolde watched with her chin tucked into her shawl like a tortoise assessing weather. "Competent," she said at last. "Cautious. Small." She said *small* the way other people said a name, with weight and history behind it. "Now the candle."

Ulyana looked at the candle. The wick was unlit. Twenty years of careful, deliberate, self-erasing caution stood between her and a piece of string.

"It's just a candle," Ysolde said.

"Yes."

"You've lit thousands."

"With matches," Ulyana said, "which are a triumph of civilization and ask nothing of my soul."

That got the ghost of a smile. "Light it."

So Ulyana reached for the fire-magic — or rather, she reached toward the place where it used to live, the way you reach in the dark for a bedside table that has been moved. And there it was, exactly where she'd left it, coiled and waiting and *hungry*, and the moment her attention touched it, the candle flared so hard the wax spat, the flame standing up tall and furious as a struck match in a powder store.

Ysolde pinched it out between finger and thumb, unhurried. "Hm," she said. "You didn't lose it. That's something. Most who flee the craft come back empty-handed." She flexed her scorched fingers absently, as though burns were correspondence she answered daily. "But you came back carrying it like stolen goods. Like something you're ashamed to be found with. Fire doesn't tolerate shame, girl. It answers it. Every time."

"So what do I do?"

"The same thing you'd tell any frightened creature." Ysolde nodded at the treeline, where some small animal was rustling through the frost. "You stop treating it like a monster and start treating it like a thing that wants to be *known*. Sit. We're going to be here some hours, and I warn you now, I nap after lunch whether you're enlightened or not."

* * *

By midday Ulyana had lit the candle four times and extinguished it involuntarily eleven times, which by Ysolde's arithmetic meant she was "improving at a pace best described as geological."

The trouble wasn't the magic. The trouble was that every time the flame caught, something in her chest flinched — a reflex older than thought — and the flinch traveled down her arm and strangled the fire before it could settle. It was like trying to hold water while being startled. It was like trying to sing while remembering every audience that ever laughed.

"You're not fighting the fire," Ysolde observed from her chair, wrapped in three blankets against the cold she claimed not to feel. "You're fighting the night you left. Every single time. I can see it in your shoulders. You carry that evening like a sack of wet wool."

"There's nothing to fight," Ulyana said. "I made a choice. It's done."

"Child." Ysolde's voice went soft in a way that was infinitely worse than sharp. "I have taught this craft for sixty years. Do you imagine I can't smell an unfaced memory? You didn't choose to leave. You *fled*, and you've spent twenty years calling it a choice because 'choice' has dignity in it and 'fled' does not."

The words went in like a splinter — small, clean, deep. Ulyana opened her mouth to argue and found, to her horror, that her eyes were stinging, which was absurd, she was nearly forty, and crying in front of one's teacher was a humiliation she had specifically budgeted against.

"Tell me about it," Ysolde said. "Or better — don't tell me. Show me. That's what the fire's for. It holds what we can't."

"I don't—"

"Ulyana." For the first time the old woman leaned forward, blankets sliding, and her eyes — pale, watery, ancient — were anything but frail. "In a season, you intend to walk into the court beneath the mountain, where the oldest and hungriest fire in the world is dying, and pour yourself into keeping it alive. If you go down there flinching, it will find the flinch, and it will use it, and you will die badly and take others with you. So." She gestured at the candle. "We face it now, in my garden, with tea afterward. Or we face it later, in the dark, alone. Your call. I'd recommend the tea."

* * *

She showed her the candle-flame, and the flame showed her back.

That was how it worked, though no one had ever explained it to Ulyana that way. You looked into the fire and let your memory lean forward into it, and the fire — generous, greedy, curious — took the memory and gave it back to you whole, with all its edges. Not remembering. *Reliving.* Ysolde had warned her: "It will feel like it's happening. That's the price and the gift. Fire doesn't deal in summaries."

And so Ulyana was nineteen years old again, kneeling on these same flagstones in this same garden, on the night before the binding rite.

The coven had dressed the courtyard with lanterns. Brenna had braided her hair — Brenna, sixteen and solemn, her fingers quick and sure, humming the binding-song under her breath because she'd already memorized it, because Brenna memorized everything, because Brenna had decided years ago that the rite was an honor and had never once asked whether Ulyana agreed.

*Tomorrow,* Brenna had whispered, tying off the braid. *Tomorrow we belong to something bigger than ourselves. Isn't that wonderful?*

And Ulyana had smiled and said yes, because saying yes was easier, and then she had gone to her narrow bed and lain awake listening to the mountain breathe.

Because that was the thing nobody talked about. The mountain breathed. At night, when the wind was right, you could feel it through the floor — a long slow pulse from below, like a heart under the world, and threaded through the pulse, faint as a whisper under a door: *voices.* The court beneath. The ones the binding rite would give her to.

The elders called it service. Called it the covenant that kept the dragon-fire fed and the seals shut and the world turning. Called it glory. But Ulyana had crept down to the archive that night — she'd never admitted this to anyone, not even Brenna — and read the oldest accounts, the ones written before the language got polite. And the accounts said *given*. Said *taken*. Said, in one shaking hand from four hundred years ago: *they do not return what they take, and what remains walks and speaks but is not the girl I bore.*

She had gone back to her room. She had packed nothing, because packing made noise and taking nothing meant leaving everything, which was its own terrible clarity. And at moonset she had climbed out the scullery window, past the sleeping skeps, down the frost-slick path — and at the gate she had stopped, because the gate was where the wards began, and crossing them uninvited would ring through every witch's bones in Thornhollow like a cracked bell.

She had stood there in the freezing dark with her hand on the latch, and she had done the thing she was most ashamed of, the thing she had never told a living soul:

She had used fire-magic to open the gate. Quietly. Carefully. Melting the ward-seal's frost-lock with a thread of heat so fine it made her teeth ache. The first real magic she had ever done alone, unsupervised, and she had spent it on *leaving* — and the seal had sung a single low note as it parted, and somewhere behind her a window had lit, and she had run. Down through the ash country, half-starved, ears ringing with the memory of that note, certain that any moment the coven would come for her.

They never did. She had told herself for twenty years that this meant she'd gotten away clean. Now, kneeling in Ysolde's garden with tears freezing on her cheeks, she finally understood what it actually meant: they had let her go. All of them. Even Brenna. They had watched a terrified girl run from the rite and chosen not to drag her back — and she had repaid that mercy by spending two decades pretending none of them existed.

"That's it," Ysolde said quietly, beside her, in the present, in the frost. "There she is. There's the wound."

"She let me go," Ulyana whispered. "Brenna saw the window light and she let me go."

"Of course she did. She loved you. Love lets people run, even when running breaks your heart. Especially then." Ysolde's hand settled on her shoulder, light as ash. "Now look at the candle."

Ulyana looked. The flame stood steady — truly steady, for the first time all day, burning straight and calm as if it had all the time in the world.

"Do you see?" the old woman said. "It was never angry with you. It was waiting. Fire is the most patient thing there is, despite its reputation. It waits inside wood for years. Inside stone for ages. Inside frightened girls for decades." She squeezed Ulyana's shoulder once and released it. "Shame makes fire stutter. Resolve makes it sing. You have carried the shame long enough. What would you like to carry instead?"

Ulyana stared into the small bright flame and found, to her surprise, that the answer was already there — had perhaps always been there, folded up small and hidden in the same place she'd hidden everything else.

"Not the girl who ran," she said slowly. "The woman who went back. On her own terms. With her own name."

Something kindled in the old teacher's face. "Then tomorrow," Ysolde said, "we begin teaching your hands what your heart has finally learned. And I promise you nothing about the process except that it will hurt less than hiding did, and considerably more than tea."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon and into the blue dusk. By the time the first stars came out, Ulyana could light the candle with a thought and keep it lit through a sneeze, which Ysolde declared "adequate for a beginner and insulting for someone your age," which Ulyana had decided was the highest praise the woman gave.

Her arms ached. Her eyes burned. Something loose and long-dislocated had shifted back into place behind her ribs, and she kept touching the spot the way you touch a tooth that has finally stopped hurting, not quite trusting it.

Corin found her there as the light died, coming up the garden path with his collar turned up against the cold and that particular careful expression he wore — the one of a man perpetually apologizing for occupying space. He had taken to walking the coven's boundaries each evening, he said. Getting the measure of the place. Ulyana suspected he simply couldn't bear to be indoors when the dusk came on; there was something in him that listened for the mountain, the way a compass needle trembles near iron.

"You're limping," she said, standing, brushing frost off her skirts.

"I sat on a bee."

"A bee. In winter."

"He was very committed." But he was looking past her, at the candle she'd left burning on the stone — burning steady in the open air, untouched by wind, and his face changed. Not fear. Something closer to recognition, or grief's well-dressed cousin. "It doesn't move," he said softly. "Even the big fires in the hearth move. That one just... stays. Like it's listening."

"Old fire does that," Ulyana said. "Ysolde says it's the closest thing to a soul that isn't attached to a person."

He was silent a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped, gone careful, the way it did when he was approaching something dangerous from downwind. "Ulyana. Can I tell you something strange? Even stranger than usual?"

"Always."

"Since we came up the valley — since we got close to the mountain — I've been dreaming. Same dream, every night. There's a hall, and the walls are..." He stopped. Started again. "The walls are teeth. And there's a throne made of the same, and on it sits a queen, and when she speaks—" His jaw worked. His hands had closed into fists at his sides, and the candle-flame between them bent toward him like a listener leaning in. "When she speaks, Ulyana, she sounds like my mother. A mother I don't remember having. She calls me by a name that isn't Corin. And last night—" His voice frayed. "Last night, she said, *Come home, Aldous. The fire misses you.*"

The flame straightened. Somewhere down the valley, faint and vast and patient beneath the frozen earth, the mountain breathed.

Ulyana took his cold hand in hers, and together they stood in the garden and listened to it.
