## 17. Corin Remembers Something

The dreams began three nights after they crossed into the ash country, and they did not come gently.

Corin woke the first time with his hands pressed flat against the dirt floor of their shelter, fingers spread wide, as if he'd been trying to grip the world and keep it from sliding. His heart was hammering. His mouth tasted of cinders, which was nothing new—he had woken tasting ash every morning of his remembered life—but this time there was something underneath it, something sweeter and far more frightening.

A voice. He could almost hold on to it. A woman's voice, warm as a kitchen in winter, singing something low and half-familiar, and he had the terrible sense that if he could just remember the words, everything else would come flooding back behind them like water through a broken dam.

He sat up in the dark. Across the small room—half-collapsed barn, honestly, but it kept out the wind—Ulyana slept sitting upright against the wall, her cloak pulled to her chin, one hand resting near the little knife she'd taken to sleeping beside ever since Sergeant Vane's hounds had howled somewhere south of them two nights past. Even asleep she looked vigilant. Even asleep she looked like someone who had spent twenty years practicing not being noticed and had gotten very good at it.

Corin envied her that, a little. He had never once managed to be unnoticed. It was hard to be unnoticed when you weren't entirely sure you existed.

* * *

"You talked in your sleep," Ulyana said the next morning, handing him a strip of dried apple with the particular briskness she used when she was worried and wanted very much not to be. "You said 'not the teeth, Mother, not the teeth' about eleven times. Then you laughed. I didn't care for the laugh."

"I don't have a mother," Corin said. "That's rather the point of me. No memories before nineteen. No family. I walked out of a ditch outside Cinderhollow with my boots on and my head empty, like a parcel somebody forgot to collect."

"Yes." She chewed her own apple slowly, watching him with those gray eyes that missed nothing and forgave, he suspected, even less. "And yet you called for her."

"Maybe I was calling for a mother generally. A hypothetical mother. People do."

"People who are empty inside don't call for anyone," Ulyana said quietly. "I've been empty-adjacent myself for twenty years, Corin. You learn what emptiness sounds like. It doesn't dream."

They broke camp in silence after that, but the silence had changed texture. It wasn't the comfortable silence of two people who had learned each other's habits over long miles—the way she always walked on the windward side, the way he counted fence posts under his breath without realizing he did it. This silence had teeth in it. He wondered, not for the first time and with considerably less denial than before, whether the prophecy book was right about him, whether some essential piece of him really had been carried off before his birth and bottled in a court beneath the mountain, and whether whatever was dreaming its way back toward him through his sleep was a rescue or an invasion.

By midday the mountain itself had appeared on the horizon, and Corin's headaches began.

* * *

They were not ordinary headaches. Corin had known ordinary headaches—had, in fact, built a whole quiet life around avoiding loud noises and strong smells and the particular greenish light that came before storms, because all of these made his skull feel like a cracked bell. But this was different. This was directional.

The closer they walked toward Thornhollow and the black shoulder of the mountain beyond it, the worse it got, and the pain always sat in exactly the same place: behind his eyes, slightly to the left, as though something in there were leaning against the inside of his skull and pushing.

"It's pulling," he told Ulyana on the third day, stopped in the middle of a dead orchard, trees twisted into shapes like screaming. "The headache. It's not spreading, it's—it's tugging. Toward the mountain."

Ulyana had gone still in that way of hers, the stillness of a small animal deciding whether the noise in the grass is dinner or death. "Show me," she said, and took his face in her hands, and closed her eyes.

Her magic, when she let him feel the edges of it—which was rare, and always felt to Corin like being allowed to watch a deer drink—was small and precise and warm. She was a woman made entirely of luck-knots and fever-charms, tiny mercies knotted out of string and whispered words, and he had once thought that was all she was. He no longer thought so. There were moments, usually when she forgot herself, when something much older and much larger moved underneath her careful smallness, the way something enormous moves under ice.

Now she stood holding his face for a long moment, and then her eyes opened and she looked frightened, which she almost never permitted herself to do.

"There's a thread," she said. "Running out of you. Out through your chest, thin as spider silk, going east. Going down." Her thumb traced a line across his sternum without quite touching. "It's taut, Corin. Something on the other end has noticed we're getting close, and it's reeling."

"That's the least comforting sentence anyone has ever said to me."

"I'm a charm-seller, not a comfort-seller." But her voice wobbled on the joke, and she covered it by shouldering her pack and walking faster, which was her other method of covering things.

* * *

That night they sheltered in the ruins of a waystation, one of the old dragon-war garrisons, walls half-melted into glassy stone that caught the moonlight and held it wrong. Ulyana salted the doorways and sang a low warding song under her breath—one of Mother Ysolde's, she admitted, taught to her long ago and never forgotten—and Corin lay down knowing he would dream again, because the dreams had become as regular now as his heartbeat, and twice as loud.

The throne of teeth came first, as it always did.

He stood in a hall vaster than any cathedral, its vaulted ceiling lost in darkness, and at the far end rose a throne built from what could only be teeth. Thousands of them. Millions. They were not all the same size—some were small and white and human, some curved and yellowed like a boar's tusks, and some were longer than Corin was tall, serrated, ancient, dragon's teeth gone ivory-pale from age. They had been set into the stone with a kind of loving artistry, arranged in whorls and spirals, so that the whole throne looked less like a weapon and more like a garden grown from the mouths of the dead.

He knew, in the way one knows things in dreams, that every tooth had been freely given. That was the horror of it. Nobody had built that throne by force. The court beneath the mountain asked, and something in the asking made you want to say yes.

Seated upon it—or draped upon it, or perhaps simply wearing it—was the queen.

She had his mother's voice.

That was the thing that undid him every night. Her face shifted when he tried to look at it directly, running like wax, but her voice never changed. It was warm and low and amused, and it sang the same half-song he'd heard the first night, and when she spoke it was in the tone every orphaned child knows, the tone of someone who has been waiting for you at a window:

*"There you are,"* she said. *"You're late, darling. We've kept your chair."*

"I don't have a chair," Corin said, or dreamed he said. "I don't have anything. That's the whole trouble with me."

*"Oh, but you do. Bottled and labeled and dusted weekly. Mavrosa keeps it on the high shelf, next to the vintages."* The wax of her face ran and re-formed, ran and re-formed. *"Do you know what you taste like? I do. I remember. Sweet, with an undertone of—"* she paused, savoring, and Corin felt the dream lean toward him like a wave deciding to break— *"—apples. Dried apples, shared on a cold morning with a woman who lies beautifully."*

He woke gasping.

The waystation was dark. The moon had moved. And Ulyana was sitting bolt upright across the room, staring at him, her hand white-knuckled around her knife.

"What did you see?" she demanded.

"How did you know I—"

"Your thread," she said. "It glowed. Just now. Like a filament in a lamp. Corin—" and here her voice did something he had never heard it do, dropped all its armor at once, went young and scared— "it *pulled*. I watched it pull. Whatever's down there just took a look at you, and I think it liked what it saw."

Corin sat in the glassy moonlight with his heart slamming and found, to his genuine astonishment, that he was smiling.

"What," Ulyana said dangerously.

"It knew you," he said. "In the dream. It mentioned dried apples. It said—" he stopped, because saying it aloud would make it real, and some stubborn animal part of him wanted to keep one corner of this nightmare private. But she was looking at him with her whole face, unguarded, and he had spent his entire remembered life unable to lie to her for longer than four seconds anyway.

"It quoted you," he finished. "The queen. She said I taste sweet, with an undertone of dried apples shared on a cold morning with a woman who lies beautifully. Which is accurate, incidentally. You lie constantly and beautifully. You told that innkeeper in Ashford Crossing you were my wife so we could get the married couple's rate."

"That was economy, not lying," Ulyana said automatically, and then went pale in the moonlight, and he watched her arrive at the same conclusion he had: the queen had been *watching*. Through him. Through the taut silk thread of his sold soul, the court below had been peering out at their campfires, counting their supplies, listening to their jokes.

"She can see us," Ulyana whispered. "Through you. Every night. Every—"

"Not every night," Corin said slowly, working it through, the way he worked through everything, fence post by fence post. "Only when I dream of her. Only when the thread goes taut." He looked at Ulyana, and the smile had gone out of him, replaced by something colder and more useful. "Which means I control when she sees. If I can learn to dream on purpose. If I can learn to open the thread instead of being opened by it."

Ulyana stared at him. "That is either the bravest or the stupidest plan I've ever heard, and I grew up in a coven, so the competition is fierce."

"My mother used to say something similar," Corin said, and then stopped dead.

The words hung in the air between them. *My mother used to say.* He had no mother. He had no *used to*. He had nineteen years of nothing and a handful of borrowed habits.

But the sentence had come from somewhere. From behind his eyes, slightly to the left, where something leaned against the inside of his skull. And it hadn't sounded like the queen at all—it had sounded dry, and tired, and fond, and utterly unlike anyone he had ever met.

"Corin," Ulyana said carefully, "what else do you remember?"

He closed his eyes. And there, at the very bottom of the ash-taste, underneath the throne and the teeth and the singing, he found it waiting for him like a coin left in a pocket: a kitchen. A small kitchen with a crooked window. A woman with flour on her hands and a scar through one eyebrow, laughing at something, saying, *your brother will be the death of me, Aldous, he'll argue with the Magistrate himself someday—*

"Aldous," Corin said aloud, and the name fit him like a key fits a lock that had been rusted shut for years, painful and exact. "My name might be Aldous."

* * *

They did not sleep again that night. They sat in the ruined waystation drinking bitter tea from Ulyana's dented kettle, and Corin turned the name over and over like a stone found on a beach.

"Aldous," Ulyana repeated. "Where would that have come from? The prophecy doesn't name you. The book only shows your face."

"I don't know. It came with the kitchen. With her." He rubbed his eyes. "There was a brother, too. In the memory. Someone I argued with. Someone who—" he frowned, chasing it, and the thread behind his eyes pulled taut enough to make him wince— "someone who left. Or was left. It's like looking at a painting through frosted glass. I can see colors. Shapes. Not faces."

"Then we go slowly," Ulyana said, in the voice she used on frightened customers at the stall, the voice that had probably talked a hundred fevered children into drinking their medicine. "One shard at a time. Memory's like a charm-knot, Corin—you don't yank it undone, you find the right thread-end and ease it loose. And if it hurts, we stop."

"When did you get wise?"

"Nineteen years ago, allegedly. Ask my sister." She refilled his cup. "Drink. And then tell me about the kitchen."

So he did. The crooked window. The flour. The scar through the eyebrow. A dog, he thought, some shaggy brown thing under the table. Rain on the roof, and a smell of bread, and a feeling so violent and simple that he had to set the cup down when it hit him: *he had been loved*. Whoever Aldous had been, whoever had sold him, however the bargain had been struck before his birth—there had been a kitchen, and a woman, and love in it, enormous and specific and his.

"They took that from me," he said quietly. "Whoever bottled my soul. They didn't just take me. They took her son away from *her*. Somewhere out there is a woman with a scar through her eyebrow who raised a boy and then one day he was—" he gestured at himself, at the hollow where a childhood should be— "*this*. Empty. Walking around with my face."

Ulyana reached across and put her hand over his. It was a small gesture, quick and matter-of-fact, the way she did everything that mattered, as though tenderness were too valuable to make a fuss over.

"Then we'll find her," she said. "After. When your soul's back in you and the fire's lit and the world's gone mad in all the usual ways. We'll knock on every door in the ash country until we find a kitchen with a crooked window." She squeezed his hand once, hard, and let go. "But first we survive my family, and I want you to understand that the second one may be harder."

Despite everything—the headache, the thread, the throne of teeth—Corin laughed. Really laughed, until his ribs ached, until Ulyana's mouth twitched despite her best efforts, until the ruined waystation felt for one moment less like a tomb and more like a kitchen with a crooked window.

* * *

The last dream came just before dawn, and it was different from the others.

There was no throne this time. No hall, no teeth, no queen with her wax-running face. Instead Corin stood in darkness that was not empty but *attentive*, the darkness of something vast turning its attention toward him, and far away—or very near, distance meant nothing here—something breathed. Long, slow, labored breaths, each one carrying a faint smell of smoke and old iron and, impossibly, roses.

*"Little bottle,"* said a voice that was not the queen's. It was older than hers. Tired beyond hers. It came from everywhere, the way heat comes from everywhere. *"Little stolen bottle, walking around on two legs. You carry my smell in you. Do you know why?"*

"No," Corin said, and his voice in the dream was very small.

*"Because you were meant to feed me."* A pause, long as a season. *"Not you yourself, child. What's in the jar. But jars rattle, given time, and mine has been rattling for nineteen years, and now it walks toward me wearing your face, and the one who sold you thinks I haven't noticed."* The breathing hitched, and in the hitch Corin heard something that stopped his heart: hunger. Enormous, patient, bottomless hunger, held back by will alone. *"Tell the witch who lies beautifully that the fire is guttering faster than any of them know. Tell her the seals won't last the season. And tell her—"* the voice dropped, became almost intimate, almost gentle— "*tell her the debt always comes due, and I am so very hungry, and she smells of twenty hidden years, and I have waited a long, long time for someone to pay."*

Corin woke with dawn graying the broken windows and Ulyana already awake, already watching him, already knowing.

"The dragon," he said hoarsely. "It spoke to me. It says the fire is dying faster than the prophecy says. It says the seals won't last the season." He swallowed. "And it says you smell of twenty hidden years, Ulyana. It says it's been waiting for someone to pay."

Ulyana went very still. Outside, far off across the ash flats, a crow called once—a harsh, deliberate sound, nothing like a bird's ordinary noise—and swooped down toward their camp, black against the paling sky, something glinting at its leg.

"Brenna," Ulyana said softly. "That'll be Brenna."
