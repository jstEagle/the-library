## 18. The Queen in Exile

The map the dying hedge-witch had pressed into Ulyana's hands was drawn on the back of a funeral invitation, which told her something about the witch's sense of humor and something else about her honesty. *Follow the water that remembers being holy,* it said, in a cramped hand, *and mind the third bell.*

They found the water easily enough. A stream ran down from the high country toward the mountain's shadow, and where it went underground through a cleft in the rock, old stonework began — not Cinderhollow masonry, all soot and practicality, but carved arches furred with moss, their faces worn to suggestion. Corin walked beside her with his hood up and his hand never far from the knife at his belt, though what he thought a knife would do against whatever lived out here, Ulyana couldn't guess.

"You've been quiet," she said.

"I'm always quiet."

"Quieter than usual, then. Which is saying something. Talking to you is like corresponding with a well."

The corner of his mouth moved — not quite a smile, but the place where one kept its coat. "I dreamed again last night."

Ulyana stopped walking. The stream chattered past their boots, indifferent. Since the mountain had come into view two days ago, gray-shouldered and patient as an executioner, Corin's dreams had been getting louder. She could tell without him saying. He slept like a man lying next to a lit window.

"The throne?" she asked.

"Worse." He looked at his own hands. "There was singing. Someone singing a lullaby, and I knew the tune, Ulyana. Not remembered it. *Knew* it, the way you know your own heartbeat. And when I woke I was humming it, and I have no idea where a man who has no childhood learns a lullaby."

The words sat between them. Ulyana wanted very much to say something comforting and had nothing that wasn't a lie, so instead she said, "The third bell," and pointed ahead.

It hung in the ruined archway over the flooded nave: a bronze bell the size of a dinner plate, green with age, swaying slightly in a wind she couldn't feel. Below it, the chapel — because that's what this had been, before whatever war or flood or forgetting had come for it — lay half-drowned. Black water filled the nave to the depth of a man's chest, still as poured glass. Candles burned along the submerged sill of the altar window, underwater flames that did not gutter, casting light upward in long amber ropes.

"Well," said Ulyana. "That's either welcoming or a warning, and my whole life has taught me those look identical."

"Your whole life has taught you to check anyway," Corin said, and waded in first.

* * *

The water was warmer than it had any right to be, and it did not soak them. That was the strangest part. It rose around their legs and closed over their shoulders, and they came through it dry — dry except for a fine mist that clung to their hair like breath. Ulyana felt charms stir under her shirt, the little knots she'd sewn herself waking one by one, whispering their small warnings. None of them screamed. One of them, the fever-charm she'd made for a market woman named Doss who'd died three winters back, actually hummed contentedly, which was either reassuring or deeply unsettling depending on how much one trusted dead women's fevers.

The pews were still there beneath the water, soft with silt. Fish — pale, blind, unhurried fish — drifted between them. At the far end, on the altar steps, someone was sitting.

She was beautiful the way winter is beautiful: precisely, and with intent. Silver hair fell loose past her waist, and she wore a gown that might once have been court-gray and was now the color of deep water, hem darkening into actual water where it touched the flooded floor. She was doing something with her hands — threading light, or unthreading it; it was hard to say which — and she did not look up until they stood at the foot of the altar, dripping mist.

"You took your time," she said. Her voice was low and even, with an accent Ulyana didn't know and somehow understood perfectly. "A hundred years, give or take. But I suppose you walked here at human pace, so I shouldn't complain."

"We're looking for Seraiva," Ulyana said.

The woman smiled. It was a lovely smile and it reached nowhere near her eyes, which were the color of the water behind her, if the water had opinions. "You're looking at her. Or near enough. Most of me is here. Some of me is still under the mountain, keeping doors shut that my dear Mavrosa would rather have open." She set aside whatever she'd been weaving and folded her hands. "And you are the witch who fled, and this is the sold soul. The book travels with you, I assume. It would be rude to come all this way without it."

Ulyana's hand went, before she could stop it, to the satchel where 'The Ashen Hours' rode wrapped in oilcloth against her spine. Seraiva watched the motion with evident satisfaction.

"It reads people coming," Ulyana said. "Doesn't it. The book. That's how the prophecy knew your faces — it was reading forward the whole time."

"Reading sideways, more accurately, but yes. Prophecy is just history that got impatient." Seraiva rose, and rising turned out to be a longer process than it should have been, unfolding upward in increments, until she stood above them both by half a head. Up close she smelled of cold stone and, faintly, of woodsmoke — dragon-fire, Ulyana realized, old dragon-fire, soaked into her like perfume into cloth. "Sit. Both of you. Negotiations go worse standing; everyone ends up looming."

There were no dry chairs. They sat on the altar steps anyway, which seemed to amuse her.

"Now," Seraiva said. "You want into the mountain court. Through the front door is impossible — the gates answer to Mavrosa's blood, and blood answers to nothing else. Through the throat-tunnels, perhaps, if you enjoy being digested by cooling lava for nine days. There is a third way. My way. I built the back door myself, when I still wore the crown, in case I ever needed to leave quickly. As it happens, I needed to leave quickly." Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice — grief, maybe, or its expensive cousin, regret. "I can open it. Only I can open it. Mavrosa has hunted that door for sixty years and found nothing, because it doesn't open for power. It opens for payment."

Corin shifted beside Ulyana. "What kind of payment?"

"Oh, the usual." Seraiva waved a hand gracefully. "Souls. Memories. Names. First-born children, if you're traditional, though I've gone off those; they cry too much." She let the joke hang in the flooded air, unsmiling, watching them both with the patience of something that measured time in reigns. "For a door of this size? For a door that opens onto the throne room's antechamber, past every seal and sentry? The price will be steep. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise, and I won't name it yet. Naming prices before you understand your customers is how queens end up exiled."

"And how do we know," Ulyana asked quietly, "that opening your door isn't exactly what Mavrosa wants? That we're not carrying the key to her enemy straight to her gate?"

Seraiva regarded her for a long moment. Then she laughed — a real laugh, short and surprised, as if it had been in storage a hundred years and was dusty coming out.

"Oh, I like you. You hide beautifully, little witch, but underneath the hiding there's a mind like a fishhook." She leaned forward. "Here is what you should know about me, since you're deciding whether to trust me. I ruled that court for four hundred years, and in four hundred years I never took a soul that was not freely traded. Freely traded, freely priced, freely grieved. When Mavrosa came — with her teeth and her shortcuts and her appetite dressed up as necessity — she called me sentimental. She was right. I was. Sentimental about consent, about the shape of a bargain, about the difference between a gift and a theft." Her eyes flicked to Corin, and something in them hardened. "Your soul, sold man, was stolen. Bottled before you drew breath, by people who wanted protection and didn't ask whose body would pay. I intend to see it returned. Not because it profits me. Because it was *wrong*, and I have spent a century in a drowned chapel with nothing to do but catalog wrongs."

The candles under the water flared as she spoke, all together, and settled.

Corin's voice came out rough. "You know who sold me?"

"I know what the ledger says." For the first time, Seraiva looked away from them, out the broken window at the mountain. "Ledgers are not the same as truth. But they rhyme." She stood, and the water around her ankles stirred as if bowing. "Rest tonight. The chapel keeps out trackers and weather and most regrets. Tomorrow I'll show you the door, and tomorrow night I'll tell you my price, and you'll hate it, and then we'll discuss it like civilized monsters."

She glided up the aisle — literally glided, Ulyana noted; the water carried her like a rumor — and paused at the archway beneath the third bell.

"One more thing, witch. The Warden woman following you lost you at the ridge this afternoon. She'll find the stream by morning. The chapel will not admit her — it knows soldiers — but she'll wait outside, and waiting women learn things." Seraiva glanced back, silver hair swinging. "Whatever she wants with you, it isn't simple, and neither are you. Sleep lightly."

Then she was gone into the dark beyond the arch, and the bell gave a single soft note, though nothing had touched it.

* * *

They made camp on the altar steps, which felt sacrilegious until Ulyana remembered that whatever god this chapel had housed had presumably also allowed it to drown, and gods make their choices.

Corin lay staring at the ceiling, where faded frescoes showed figures that might have been saints or might have been dragons; at this altitude of decay, the difference was academic.

"She knew about Vane," he said.

"Seraiva knows everything within a mile of that mountain, I suspect. Probably everything within a hundred years of it, too."

"No. I mean—" He sat up. In the candlelight his face was all angles and shadow, the hollowed-out face she'd first seen across a tea stall, except that these days the hollows had begun to fill in. With what, she didn't want to examine too closely. "She said the ledger rhymes with the truth but isn't it. She knows who sold my soul, Ulyana. And she looked at me when she said it like she was sorry for me twice over."

Ulyana pulled her cloak tighter. Outside, somewhere up the valley, an owl called, and then another owl answered in a voice that was almost certainly an owl and almost certainly only an owl.

"When she names her price tomorrow," she said carefully, "we'll hear it. All of it. And then we'll decide. Together. That's the whole advantage of not being a queen — nobody expects us to decide anything alone."

Corin huffed something that was nearly a laugh. "You're planning to haggle with an exiled fae monarch."

"I've run a market stall for twenty years. I once talked Old Marta down from four coppers to two for a luck-knot, and Marta could smell fear through stone walls." She lay back beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and the touch steadied her more than any charm she'd ever sewn. "If anyone can haggle with a queen, it's a woman who survived Cinderhollow pricing."

Above them, the third bell trembled in the windless dark and rang once more, very softly, like something counting down.

And in the water below the altar, among the blind pale fish, the drowned candles burned on — and one of them, Ulyana noticed as her eyes finally closed, had begun to burn blue, the exact color of the flame she'd seen once in a memory she no longer owned, in a kitchen she couldn't name, in a life someone was going to ask her to pay for.

Seraiva's voice drifted down the nave, barely audible, almost gentle:

"Sleep well, Ulyana Pemberton. You'll want your strength tomorrow. Twenty years is a great deal to carry, and a greater deal to put down."
