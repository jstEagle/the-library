## 19. Seraiva's Price

The drowned chapel smelled of pond-water and old incense, and Seraiva made tea in it the way queens do—which is to say she made it look like a ceremony and let you believe the ceremony had always existed.

"You're staring," Ulyana said.

"I'm deciding how much truth you can hold without spilling." The exiled queen poured three cups from a chipped brown pot that had no business being in the hands of someone with a crown-shaped scar on her brow. "Most people can hold very little. You, I suspect, have spent twenty years practicing."

"Practicing what?"

"Holding." Seraiva set a cup before her. It was willow-bark and bog-myrtle, bitter enough to make Ulyana's eyes water, and it was, unmistakably, kindness. "Drink. Negotiations go poorly on dry throats."

Corin sat on a fallen pew near the doorway, half in shadow, watching the water lap at the chapel floor through gaps in the stones. Since they'd found Seraiva here—since she'd traced one finger along his jaw and said, *yes, this one belongs below*—he'd barely spoken. He drank his tea like a man taking medicine.

Ulyana wrapped both hands around her cup. "You said your price was steep."

"I did."

"And I'm not agreeing until I hear it."

"No," Seraiva agreed pleasantly, "you're not. But you'll want to sit down for the hearing of it, and you're already sitting, so we may proceed." She settled onto a pew opposite, folding her hands, and for a moment the drowned light through the broken windows caught her face and Ulyana saw what the court beneath the mountain must have seen: something beautiful arranged around something very old and very hungry. Not hungry for food. Hungry for *settling*. For debts paid. For accounts closed.

"The way into my former home is not a door," Seraiva began. "Doors are for guests. I would be opening a wound in the mountain's skin, and wounds admit only what the mountain believes has a right to be there. Salt-iron will get you through the throat-tunnels, yes, your coven's charms will keep the dark off, yes. But passage itself—the right to descend, to arrive, to *be received*—that requires payment. Everything below requires payment. That is the first law of the court, and it is the only law Mavrosa never broke, because breaking it would have unmade the court entirely."

"What kind of payment?"

"Value," said Seraiva simply. "The mountain weighs what you offer against what you want, and if the scales balance, you pass. If they don't, the mountain keeps the difference out of your hide. Literally. I've seen men arrive at the threshold offering gold for their lives and leave offering gold *and* their shadows."

Corin shifted. "And me? What do I weigh?"

"You, dear boy, are not paying. You are *owed*. A sold soul returning home is not a purchase; it is a repossession. The mountain will part for you like water." Her gaze slid back to Ulyana, and there it was again—that terrible gentleness. "It's you who must pay. You want to enter carrying intent: to free him, to reach Ember-that-Waits, to meddle in the oldest machinery under the world. That's a heavy want. And you—" she leaned forward slightly, "—are a woman who has been hiding her value for twenty years. Which means you are sitting on a fortune you've never once counted."

Ulyana's mouth had gone dry despite the tea. "Say it plainly."

"I intend to." Seraiva lifted her cup, drank, set it down with a small ceramic click that echoed strangely in the flooded nave. "I want your hidden years. All of them. The memories themselves—not sealed, not borrowed, not held in trust. Given. Every day you spent invisible. Every charm sold behind merchant's patter. Every name you didn't give, every glance you dodged, every night you went home to your little room above the dyer's shop and locked your magic away like a shameful letter you couldn't bear to reread." She paused. "Twenty years, Ulyana Pemberton. I want them out of your head and into my keeping, and then they will be mine, and you will walk into the mountain as a woman whose survival cost exactly what it was worth."

The chapel was very quiet. Water dripped somewhere in the dark behind the altar, patient as a clock.

"That's not payment," Ulyana said finally. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which surprised her. "That's theft with extra steps."

"It is absolutely payment." Seraiva's smile didn't waver. "The mountain will weigh it and find it sufficient. More than sufficient. Hidden years are dense, witch-girl. A year lived openly is worth its weight in days. A year lived hidden—every moment double-wrapped, doubled-checked, disguised—is worth tenfold. Your twenty years of careful nothing are worth more than most souls in that court below. Mavrosa hoards kings' lifetimes that weigh less."

"Why?" The question came out rawer than Ulyana intended. "Why would you want them? What does an exiled queen do with a hedge-witch's Tuesdays?"

Seraiva considered her for a long moment, and then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a real laugh, short and startled, like a bird flushing from cover.

"Do you know, no one has asked me *why* in ninety years? They ask *how much*, they ask *must I*, they ask *is there another way*. You ask why." She rose and drifted along the pew, trailing her fingers over the waterlogged wood. "Very well. Honesty for honesty. Three reasons. First: memory is currency below, and I am a queen without a treasury. Your twenty years would buy me allies, information, leverage. I intend to take my throne back, and thrones are bought with other people's yesterdays."

"That's charming," Corin muttered from the shadows.

"Second," Seraiva continued as if he hadn't spoken, "hidden things are my particular affinity. Did you wonder why I live in a drowned chapel, girl? Because water hides. Because no mirror shows this place, no map records it, no Warden's ledger lists it. I am the Queen of What Is Concealed—or I was, before Mavrosa took even that. A gift of twenty concealed years would restore strength I haven't felt since my exile. You wouldn't be paying me in trinkets. You'd be paying me in my own native element."

"And third?"

Seraiva stopped walking. She turned, and the playfulness drained out of her face so completely that what remained looked almost like grief.

"Third," she said, "is that I want to see whether you'll do it. Whether any of you surface-dwellers will ever choose *truthfully*. Everyone who comes to me wants passage and wants to keep everything too. They want the mountain opened and their selves intact. They want the fire fed and their hands clean. And when I name a price that actually costs, they flinch, and they bargain, and they hate me, and some of them pay anyway with their eyes shut." Her voice dropped. "But no one has ever paid with open eyes. I confess a curiosity. Ninety years is long enough to develop one."

* * *

They walked afterward—Ulyana and Corin—out along the shore of the black mere where the chapel stood knee-deep in its own reflection. The ash country had given way here to reeds and grey water and stunted birches, and the evening came down soft and smelled of rain that hadn't arrived yet.

"She can't be serious," Corin said. "She wants to scoop out twenty years of your head like marrow from a bone."

"That's roughly the shape of it."

"And you're *considering* it." He stopped walking. In the flat grey light his face did the thing it sometimes did—the hollowness showing through, like looking at a lit window from outside in winter. "You've spent two decades building those years. Hiding was hard, Ulyana. I watched you sell luck-knots for sixpence while Wardens walked past. I watched you flinch when a neighbor learned your name. Those years aren't empty. They're full of *you*."

"They're full of me pretending not to be me." She kept walking, because if she stopped she might sit down in the reeds and not get up. "That's the horrible arithmetic of it, Corin. She's not asking for the best of me. She's asking for the *buried* of me. And the worst part—" Ulyana laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle, "—the worst part is I don't know which parts of those years are which anymore. If she takes them all, what's left? Do I still know how to charm a fever? Do I still know why I ran from Thornhollow? Do I still know—" she gestured vaguely between the two of them, at the space that had been quietly filling up since a man ordered tea and asked for a charm against forgetting, "—do I still know you?"

He was quiet for a few strides. Then: "You'd know me. You met me eleven weeks ago. That's inside the line."

"Eleven weeks ago you were a stranger who wanted tea and forgetting-charms. Everything that matters happened after." She shook her head. "No. That's not true either. It matters because of what came before. Because I recognized something in you, and I could only recognize it because I'd spent twenty years learning the shape of hiding. Take the years, and maybe I meet you and feel nothing. Maybe you're just a sad man at a market stall and I sell him a fever-charm and forget him by morning." Her throat tightened. "Maybe the whole reason I love you gets sold to a dethroned queen for ferry fare."

Corin stopped again. This time he reached out and took her hand, and his grip was careful, the grip of a man who had learned that things could be taken from him without warning.

"Then don't pay it," he said. "Find another way. There's always another way."

"There isn't. You know there isn't. Ysolde's maps show one path down, and it runs through Seraiva, and Seraiva doesn't haggle." Ulyana looked out over the mere. Far across the water, the mountain stood up against the sky like a closed fist, and somewhere inside it a dragon was starving, and somewhere deeper than that the seals were thinning, and somewhere in a cellar of bottles a soul named Aldous Vane waited in glass for someone to come and carry him home. "There's no other way. There's only the price, or the world ending politely while we refuse to look at the bill."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Nobody told us it would be fair. Well—Brenna probably told us. I wasn't listening. I was nineteen and furious."

A heron lifted off the shallows, unhurried, ancient-looking, and Corin watched it go with an expression Ulyana was beginning to catalog: the look of a man seeing things for what might be the last time, because he'd learned early that last times didn't announce themselves.

"If you do this," he said slowly, "promise me one thing. Not that you won't. I don't have the right to forbid it, and honestly—" his mouth twisted, "—I've been on the losing end of soul-accounting my whole life. I know how these bills work. But promise me that whatever she takes, you check what's left. Before you come down the mountain. Before you step into the dark for me. Make sure there's still enough of you left to *want* to save me. Because a rescue by a stranger is just charity, Ulyana. I don't want your charity. I want—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Twenty years of hiding had made Ulyana fluent in unfinished sentences.

"I promise," she said. "I'll inventory myself like a shopkeeper closing for the night. Count everything twice."

* * *

She went back alone at moonrise.

The chapel was darker now, the mere gone silver-black, and Seraiva sat where she'd sat before, as though she hadn't moved in a century—as perhaps she hadn't. She didn't look surprised. Queens who trade in hidden things are rarely surprised.

"You've come to answer," Seraiva said. "Or to bargain. Everyone bargains first."

"I came to ask a question, and then I'll answer." Ulyana stood in the aisle, refusing the offered seat. It felt important to stand. "When you take them—the years. Do I feel it? At the taking?"

"Yes."

"Just like that? No comfort in it?"

"There is no comfort in honest accounting, witch-girl. There is only accuracy." Seraiva studied her. "You will feel each year leave. Some will go easily—they were heavy anyway. Some will fight. The year you buried Marla the candle-seller and couldn't weep where anyone could see—that one will hurt going out. The year you taught yourself to smile at Warden patrols—that one will *scream*." She tilted her head. "You asked whether I'm serious. I am telling you the invoice. Most sellers thank me for the warning by trying to run."

"I'm not running." Ulyana's heart was pounding so hard she could feel it in her teeth, and beneath the fear—she was ashamed to find—was something else. Something that had been waiting a long time to be asked this exact question. "I want to understand what I'm buying with them, first. Tell me what happens below. Truly. No queen's poetry. If I pay, what do we get?"

"You get passage through the wound in the mountain. Safe arrival at the court's edge. My word—and my word is iron, it's the one possession Mavrosa never managed to steal—that I deliver you to Ember-that-Waits alive and whole. Beyond that?" Seraira spread her hands. "Beyond that is dragons and usurpers and the oldest debt under the world, and no payment buys certainty. You know this. You've read the book."

*The Ashen Hours.* Ulyana had carried it against her ribs since Cinderhollow, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a second heartbeat. *The witch who fled and the man whose soul was sold.* The prophecy hadn't mentioned the fee structure. Prophecies never did.

"One more question," she said. "Why memories? You could have asked for blood, for years of service, for my craft. Why the one thing that makes me *me*?"

And Seraiva—for the first time since the drowned chapel, since the tea and the laughter and the ledger-voice—looked tired. Old in a way that had nothing to do with years.

"Because hidden years are the purest coin there is," she said. "Blood regrows. Service ends. Craft can be retaught. But a hidden year, once given, is *gone*, and everyone knows it, and so the payment means something. Below, we say: a price that costs nothing proves nothing." Her eyes were steady and dark and, Ulyana thought suddenly, lonely beyond measuring. "You surface folk think the court beneath the mountain is cruel because it takes. It isn't. It's cruel because it *weighs*. And the weighing never stops, and nothing is exempt—not queens, not dragons, not girls selling luck-knots in a night market. The only mercy the mountain offers is this: it tells you the price in advance. Would that the world above did the same."

Ulyana stood in the flooded nave a long moment, listening to the water breathe against the stones. Twenty years. The dye-shop room with the crooked shutter. The market family who knew her as the quiet luck-knot woman. Brenna's face at nineteen, furious and betrayed. The smell of fever-charms. The taste of tea she'd shared with a hollowed-out stranger. All of it, weighed, priced, payable.

What was survival worth?

Apparently: everything it had cost. To the penny.

"I'll pay," she said. "On one condition. We go soon. Before I have time to grow wise about it."

Seraiva smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting sight. "Wise sellers renege. I accept your condition gladly."

They shook hands, queen to witch, and the chapel lights guttered, and somewhere far beneath them, in the dark under the mountain, something enormous stirred in its sleep—hungry, hopeful, and waiting.

They broke camp at dawn. And it was as they were saddling the mules that Ulyana saw the figure on the ridge road: a woman in a Warden's grey coat, alone, unarmed, hands raised and empty, walking toward them through the ash-grass with the careful tread of someone who had decided that today she was done hunting.

"Sergeant Ilse Vane," the woman called, when she was close enough. Her voice was gravel and exhaustion. "And I've come to make you a proposition, witch. One you're going to hate."

Behind Ulyana, Corin's hand went to the salt-iron at his belt—and stopped, because Ilse Vane was crying as she walked, and soldiers who cry on approach are not carrying orders.

They're carrying something worse.
