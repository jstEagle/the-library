## 20. Vane's Bargain

Ilse Vane had been demoted twice in her life, and she had learned something important both times: the difference between disgrace and dismissal is paperwork.

Dismissal meant a hearing, a tribunal, a formal stripping of rank and badge. Disgrace—the kind that followed letting a hedge-witch and a hollow-eyed man slip through your fingers at the Grayford crossing while half your squad watched you get knocked flat by what witnesses described as "a wall of hot wind" —that was quieter. That was a letter delivered at dawn. That was Sergeant Vane being reassigned to "administrative duties pending review," which everyone understood to mean: sit in this office, Sergeant, and think about what you've done while filing other people's successes.

So Ilse sat. And filed. And thought.

The administrative office of the Cinderhollow Warden garrison smelled of ink, cold tea, and the particular despair of men who had joined a paramilitary order to chase witches and now spent their days cataloguing confiscated candlesticks. Ilse had been there eleven days. She had alphabetized the evidence locker twice. The second time, purely out of spite, she had done it wrong on purpose—filed everything under first letters of *descriptions* rather than names—just to feel the small electric thrill of sabotage. It had not helped.

"You're doing it again," said Corporal Fenwick from the next desk, not looking up from his ledger.

"Doing what?"

"The face. The one where you're deciding whether a man deserves to be arrested or merely yelled at."

"I'm always deciding that," Ilse said. "It's called leadership."

Fenwick snorted. He was twenty-three, earnest, and the only person in the garrison who still brought her tea, which told her either that he liked her or that he hadn't yet learned how quickly kindness could be career-ending. She suspected the latter. Youth was like that—a long hallway of doors you didn't know were locked until you walked into them nose-first.

The truth—the truth she could not put in any report—was that she wasn't thinking about the river at all. Or rather, she was thinking about it constantly, but not the way the review board imagined. They assumed she was replaying her failure, hunting for tactical errors. Where had the perimeter thinned? Why had she committed her reserves too early? Should she have shot the witch when she had the angle?

No. What Ilse replayed, over and over, in the grey hours before dawn in her narrow bed, was the moment the fire-wall went up between them. The heat of it. The color of it—not orange, not gold, but a deep red like embers seen through a keyhole—and the way Ulyana Pemberton had looked standing inside it. Not frightened. Not triumphant, even. *Relieved*, almost, the way a woman looks who has been holding her breath for twenty years and finally, catastrophically, lets it out.

Ilse knew that look. She had worn it once herself, a lifetime ago, in an office very much like this one, across a desk very much like this one, from a man very much like the Magistrate himself, when she signed the paper.

She did not let herself finish the thought. She never let herself finish the thought. Finishing the thought led to places with no exits.

* * *

On the twelfth day, the summons came.

Not from the review board—from higher. A courier in Magisterial grey arrived at the garrison at dusk, handed Ilse a folded card sealed with black wax, and left without waiting for a reply. Fenwick watched her break the seal with the expression of a man watching someone open a trap he'd heard about but never seen.

"It'll be about the river," he said. "They can't blame you entirely. Everyone knows Pemberton's craft ran deep. Her mentor trained under—"

"Fenwick."

"Yes, Sergeant?"

"If they wanted to comfort me, they'd have sent wine." She stood, buttoned her coat, and tucked the card into her breast pocket. "Watch my desk. If anyone asks, I've gone to be yelled at with dignity."

The address on the card was not the Magistrate's palace. It was a townhouse in Candlewick Row—one of those tall, narrow houses with iron balconies and windows darkened by law, where the city's second-tier power lived and entertained and quietly ruined each other. Ilse knew the street. She'd made arrests on it. She knew which house belonged to whom, which hosted the tax-farmers, which the shipping magnates, which the retired Wardens who drank too much and talked too loudly about the old days when the burning grounds had been busier.

The house she stopped before bore no nameplate. But the door opened before she knocked, and the servant—a thin man with the careful blankness of someone paid extremely well to see nothing—showed her up three flights to a study lined with books behind glass.

The Magistrate was not there.

Instead, a woman rose from behind a desk that was too large for the room, the way furniture is too large when its owner wants you to notice it. She was perhaps sixty, silver-haired, dressed in dove-grey wool with a single jet brooch at her throat. Her hands were folded. Her eyes were the pale, patient blue of winter sky, and Ilse recognized them immediately, because she had spent thirty years learning to read eyes and these were the eyes of someone who had already decided how this conversation would end and was simply curious how Ilse would get there.

"Sergeant Vane," the woman said. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please. Would you like tea?"

"No, thank you, ma'am."

"Good. Tea takes time, and we haven't much." The woman sat back down. "You may call me Mistress Corvel. Or nothing at all. Names are a formality in rooms like this."

"What is this room, ma'am?"

Mistress Corvel smiled as though Ilse had asked exactly the right question, which, Ilse would reflect later, was probably because she had. "This room," Corvel said, "is the part of the Magistracy that does not appear on any organizational chart. We concern ourselves with problems the regular Wardens are not equipped to solve. You've encountered one such problem recently. At Grayford."

"The Pemberton woman."

"And her companion." Corvel opened a folder on her desk—thick, well-used, its edges soft from handling—and turned it so Ilse could see. A sketch of Corin stared up at her. Beneath it, notes in a cramped hand. *No records prior to age nineteen. No family. No debts. No history.* "We have been aware of him for some years. A curiosity. Now, apparently, a priority."

"He's nobody," Ilse said. "I've run his description through every registry. If he's got papers, they're better than mine."

"Oh, his papers are excellent," Corvel agreed pleasantly. "That's part of what makes him interesting. Nobody is that unremarkable by accident." She closed the folder. "But I didn't bring you here to discuss him. I brought you here because I've read your file. All of it. Including the parts marked sealed."

Something cold moved down Ilse's spine and settled in her stomach, familiar as an old injury. "Ma'am, I don't know what—"

"Vane. Please." Corvel's voice didn't rise; if anything it softened, and somehow that was worse. "I'm sixty-one years old and I have buried two husbands and one career. Do not insult us both with the performance. You were born in Ashfall parish. Your father died in the mill collapse when you were nine. Your mother took in sewing until her hands failed. There were four children. There was a winter—I believe you remember the winter—when the choice came down to selling something, or burying someone."

The clock on the mantel ticked. Somewhere below, a carriage passed, iron wheels on cobblestone.

"I don't know what you think you—" Ilse began.

"You sold a soul," Corvel said gently. "Your brother's. Infant. To the mountain court, through a broker whose name I won't say aloud in this house, though I will note that he is no longer among the living, and that his death was ruled natural, and that I have never believed a ruling in my life. In exchange, your family received enough coin to survive the winter, and your mother received a Warden's protection bond that kept the rest of you untouched for fifteen years." She paused. "And then you joined the Wardens yourself. At seventeen. The youngest recruitment in Cinderhollow's history at that time. You have served without blemish for twenty-eight years. You have burned forty-one hedge-witches. You keep a list. I know, because I have read it, and I noticed that you write their names, not just their case numbers. Every one. Even the ones the tribunal assigned numbers to."

Ilse's hands had gone numb in her lap. She looked at them—her father's hands, big-knuckled, miller's hands—and found she had nothing to say that wouldn't be a confession.

"Why," she managed at last, "are you telling me this? Blackmail doesn't usually come with a full accounting."

"Because blackmail implies I want something you'd rather not give," Corvel said. "What I want is something you've been dying to give someone for thirty years. I want you to stop carrying it alone."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a floor tilts when you've stood too long. Ilse gripped the arms of the chair.

"You don't know anything about me," she said, and hated how it sounded. How young it sounded.

"I know everything about you," Corvel said. "It's my profession. And here is the thing I know best of all, Sergeant Vane, the thing that made me send for you instead of one of the dozen more decorated officers I might have used: you have spent your entire adult life hunting magic because magic took something from you, and you have never once—not once, in twenty-eight years, I checked—looked at a captured witch and asked her where the broker was. You never asked where the court beneath the mountain keeps its jars. You never followed the thread home. Do you know why?"

"Because it's not—"

"Because you were afraid," Corvel said, "of what you'd find. Because some small, stubborn, sensible part of you understood that the thread leads down, and down, and down, and at the bottom of it there is a shelf, and on that shelf there is a jar, and in that jar—" she spread her hands, delicately, "—there may be a boy who never grew up. Who never learned to walk. Whose whole life is sitting in the dark, labeled, waiting. And you could not bear to look. So you burned witches instead. It's honest work. Someone has to do it. But it was never the work you wanted, was it?"

The clock ticked. Ilse became aware that she was crying—silently, without permission, tears simply arriving on her face as though they'd been queued for decades and the door had finally opened. She wiped them with the heel of her hand, furious at them, at Corvel, at the gentle terrible accuracy of it all.

"What do you want?" she said hoarsely.

Corvel slid a single sheet of paper across the desk. Ilse looked down at it. It was an order of release—drafted, unsigned—for one *Corin, also known as Aldous Vane.*

The name hit her like cold water. She read it again. Read it a third time. The paper blurred and sharpened.

"That's—" Her voice failed. She started over. "That's not possible. That's a coincidence. Registry error. Some clerk's joke."

"Aldous," Corvel said softly. "A good old family name. Common enough in Ashfall parish, particularly among the mill families. Born the same season as your brother. Disappeared from every record at the age of nineteen—no, wait. Forgive me. Appeared at the age of nineteen, walking out of nowhere with excellent papers and no past, tasting ash every morning, dreaming of teeth." She leaned forward. "Sergeant. When the court sells a soul, it doesn't destroy it. Souls are currency. They're traded, invested, held in reserve. Sometimes—rarely—they're returned to the body they came from, when the debt runs out or the owner tires of the asset. Your brother's soul has been in the market for twenty-nine years. Something has changed down there. Something has set the accounts moving. And that man traveling with Pemberton woke up empty at nineteen and has been walking toward the mountain ever since, whether he knows it or not."

Ilse stared at the unsigned order. Her vision swam. Twenty-nine years. A jar. A label. And a man with her mother's chin walking beside a hedge-witch through the ash country, and Ilse had raised a crossbow at him. Had given the order to loose. Had missed only because fire got in the way.

"Why show me this?" she whispered. "Why give me this?"

"Because I need the prophecy book," Corvel said, and here at last her voice changed—lost its warmth, gained something harder underneath, the sound of a woman who had done arithmetic no one should have to do. "'The Ashen Hours.' Pemberton has it. It contains verses concerning the mountain court that the Magistracy cannot permit to circulate. Panic, Sergeant. Riots. Every hedge-witch and charm-seller in six provinces suddenly convinced they know how the world ends. We need the book. Quietly."

"And Pemberton?"

"Pemberton is a complication. The Magistrate wants her. I, frankly, do not care what happens to her." Corvel folded her hands. "But you care what happens to him. I can see it—you've been able to see nothing else since you read that name. So here is the bargain, and I'll make it only once, and only to you, because you are the only officer in this garrison with a reason to keep it."

She tapped the release order.

"Deliver 'The Ashen Hours' to me. In exchange: the man goes free. Full pardon, clean papers, his choice of city. No Warden touches him again. Pemberton's fate is not part of this arrangement—that is above even my pay—but the book buys his freedom, and his freedom, I suspect, buys yours. Both kinds."

* * *

Ilse walked back across the city in the dark, and the city looked different.

It had looked different for twelve days, actually—since the river, since the fire—but tonight the difference had a shape. She had spent her whole life believing the world divided cleanly: the law above, the magic below, and honest people in the middle keeping their heads down. Tonight she saw the seams. The darkened windows of Candlewick Row. The garrison lanterns burning oil taxed from parishes like Ashfall. The burning grounds beyond the walls, cold for weeks now, waiting.

She thought about her mother, dead eleven years, who had never once spoken of that winter except to say, near the end, gripping Ilse's hand with ruined fingers: *You were the strong one. You did what had to be done. Don't you dare carry it like a sin.* And Ilse had promised, and had carried it like a sin anyway, because her mother had been wrong—it was not a sin, it was a wound, and wounds don't care what you call them.

She thought about the list. Forty-one names in her own handwriting, kept in a locked drawer, reviewed on the anniversary of each burning, because forgetting felt like the greater crime. She had told herself for years that the list was penance. Standing now under a gas lamp at the mouth of Tanner's Bridge, she admitted what it actually was. It was a ledger. Debts she hoped someone, someday, would collect from her. Because punishment, at least, was a form of the thread leading somewhere.

And now Corvel had offered her a different thread. One that led down. All the way down. To a shelf. To a jar. To maybe—*maybe*—a man walking around above ground wearing her brother's missing twenty-nine years like a coat he couldn't take off.

She reached the garrison near midnight. Fenwick was asleep at his desk over the ledger, pen still in hand, ink drying on a blot. She stood looking at him for a moment—young, kind, doomed to learn things slowly unless someone taught him faster—and felt an unexpected tenderness, and beneath the tenderness, decision, arriving with the quiet click of a lock turning.

She climbed to her narrow room. She took out the map of the ash country that she'd drawn herself, from memory, after Grayford—the route Pemberton would take toward Thornhollow, because a woman running home always runs home, and Ilse had known coven geography since her first posting. She took out her travel cloak. She took out her crossbow, and then, after a long moment, set it back on its pegs. Whatever happened next, she was tired of meeting witches armed.

Then she sat down at the little table by the window and wrote two letters.

The first was addressed to Mistress Corvel, and it said: *Terms received. Consider the book negotiable. I will require proof of the claim regarding Aldous Vane before delivery. Meet me alone, or not at all.* It was, she reflected, the first time in twenty-eight years she had negotiated upward with the Magistracy, and the sensation was so strange and so fine that she had to put the pen down for a moment and breathe.

The second letter had no address. It was to a hedge-witch named Ulyana Pemberton, and it was four drafts in before Ilse found the words, because how did a woman write to the person she'd tried to kill and say: *I believe the man with you is my brother, and I believe you are the only person alive who knows how to reach him, and I am prepared to betray everything I have spent my life serving, if you will let me stand beside you when you do it.*

She settled, finally, on the plainest version. She sealed it with plain wax, no crest—she had a feeling crests were about to become a liability—and she gave it to the garrison's night messenger with instructions and coin, and then she stood at her window watching the messenger's lamp dwindle toward the east gate, toward the ash country, toward the road where a woman who had spent twenty years hiding was learning, day by day, what it cost to be seen.

Below, the city slept. Beneath the city, Ilse knew now, something vast and starving lay in the dark, keeping the seals shut with borrowed souls, and one of those souls might be wearing her family's name.

"Hold on, Aldy," she said to the glass, to the mountain beyond it, to the boy who never got to grow up. "I'm coming. And I'm bringing the whole rotten system down on the way."

Somewhere far off, thunder rolled—or perhaps it was only the mountain, shifting in its sleep, counting its debts.
