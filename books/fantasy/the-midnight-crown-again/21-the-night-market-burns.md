## 21. The Night Market Burns

The letter from Brenna had been sitting in her coat pocket for three days, folded and unfolded so many times that the creases had gone soft as cloth. Ulyana carried it everywhere—out of habit, out of dread, out of some superstitious feeling that if she set it down on the table where she ate her supper, it would somehow become more real.

She was at the stall before dawn, as always, setting out her luck-knots in their careful rows, when Old Tammer came up the lane with his hand pressed to his side like a man carrying a wound.

"You heard?" he said.

"Heard what?"

Tammer was a fishmonger with a face like a dropped pie and a heart the size of the harbor. He'd never once asked what she actually put in the fever-charms he bought for his grandchildren every winter. He just paid his copper and said *keep it quiet, love* and went on his way.

"The Wardens," he said. "They've been asking questions at the Tanner's Gate. Asking who buys charms."

Ulyana's hands went still over the luck-knots. "Who's been telling them?"

"Nobody knows. But Ilsabet's boy saw a Warden writing in a little book. Names." Tammer lowered his voice, though the lane held nothing but gulls and the smell of last night's fish. "Your name might be in it, love. You want to think about not coming tomorrow."

*Tomorrow.* As though there were a long bright shelf of tomorrows stretching out ahead, and she could simply choose to step off one and onto another. She thought about Brenna's letter in her pocket. *Come home, or we come for you.*

"I'll be here," she said, because what else did one say.

Tammer gave her a look that was far too knowing for a man who sold mackerel. "Course you will," he said. "Stubborn as a barnacle, you are." And he shuffled off down the lane, and Ulyana stood alone among her little knots of colored thread, each one tied to carry a whisper of luck to someone who would never know her name.

She should have left then. That was the thought that would come back to her later, in the dark, wearing its sharpest shoes: *you should have left that morning.* She could have packed the stall, burned the stock herself, walked out the Tanner's Gate and been two miles gone before the sun cleared the chimneys. Instead she sold eleven luck-knots, two fever-charms, and a birth-blessing ribbon to a young couple who blushed at each other the whole time. Instead she laughed at Widow Cress's joke about the mayor and his second chin. Instead she watered the little pot of marjoram on the corner post of her awning because it looked thirsty.

Habit is a kind of cowardice dressed in comfortable clothes. She didn't know that yet. She would learn it by nightfall.

*

The fires started at the far end of Lantern Row, and for one merciful moment the whole market thought it was an accident—a lamp knocked over, a cookfire left careless, the ordinary small disasters of a city built mostly of wood and wishful thinking.

Then the second fire started. And the third. Three points, spread wide, lit almost together. That wasn't accident. That was arithmetic.

Ulyana was locking her coin box when the first scream went up. She turned and saw the sky over Lantern Row turn the color of a bruise, orange bleeding into black, and beneath it people spilling out of doorways into the street with their arms full of whatever they'd grabbed first—a child, a cat, a bread loaf, a portrait of somebody's dead mother.

"Fire!" Someone seized her sleeve. It was Pip Halloway, the pie-seller's daughter, fourteen years old and missing most of one eyebrow from her own experiment with flambéed eel. "Miss Pemberton, the Row's alight, they're saying it's the Wardens, they're saying—"

"Go home," Ulyana said. "Pip. Go home now, get your mother, get out through the Tanner's Gate, don't stop for anything."

"But my pies—"

"*Pip.*"

The girl went. Ulyana stood in the middle of the lane with her heart going like a hammer on sheet metal, doing sums she already knew the answer to. The Wardens hadn't found her. Not yet. But they'd found the market—the whole soft, illegal ecosystem of it, the hedge-touched and the charm-buying and the quietly lucky, all of them clustered together like moths in a jar because moths in a jar are easier to count.

Because of her. Or near enough. Vane had come to her stall. Vane had seen her face. A woman like that wrote things in little books.

She ran toward Lantern Row instead of away from it, which told her something true about herself that twenty years of careful living had kept politely hidden.

*

Lantern Row was a river of fire.

It ran along the shopfronts and dripped off the awnings and pooled in the gutters, burning greenish at the edges where the Warden-fire had been laced with salt to keep it hungry. Salt-fire. That was deliberate. That was doctrine—she remembered the coven teaching it, back when she was small and Thornhollow was the only world there was: *salt-fire eats magic faster than it eats wood. It's how they burn us without wasting good timber.*

The heat pushed at her face like a huge rude hand. People formed bucket lines that accomplished nothing except giving everyone something to do while their livelihoods died. Ulyana shoved through them, looking—though she couldn't have said for what until she found it.

Old Tammer was on his knees in front of his shop, which was fully involved, flames standing up out of the windows like yellow grass. He wasn't screaming. That was the worst part. He was just kneeling there with his cap in his hands, watching forty years of fish and family history go up in salt-fire, and beside him his grandson—little Ferren, six years old, the one whose fever-charm Ulyana renewed every winter without ever charging full price—was coughing, doubled over, coughing in that wet tearing way that meant smoke in the lungs and worse coming.

Ulyana didn't decide anything. Her body simply moved, the way bodies do when the mind has finally stopped interfering. She knelt in the gutter next to the boy, pulled his head against her shoulder, and laid two fingers against his throat where the pulse beat thin and fast.

She had not worked real magic in twenty years. What she did then wasn't real magic. It was the smallest thing—a breath-charm, a mother's trick, barely a spell at all, a whisper of craft so old and so humble that even the Magistrate's books probably didn't bother listing it. Cool air moved through the boy's lungs. The wet tearing eased. His breathing smoothed out like a wrinkled shirt under a warm iron.

Ferren looked up at her with streaming eyes. "Thank you, miss," he wheezed, and then, because children are honest creatures: "You smell like smoke."

"So do you," she said. "We match."

Tammer gripped her wrist hard enough to leave marks. His eyes were red-rimmed and furious and grateful all at once, and he said, low, "Was that you? Did you just—" and Ulyana shook her head once, sharply, *not here, not ever,* and he swallowed whatever else he'd been going to say. Twenty years of unspoken understanding between a fishmonger and a charm-witch, compressed into one look.

Behind them, the shop groaned and let go of its roof.

*

They fought the fire until dawn, which is to say they lost to it slowly, dignity intact, everything else gone. By gray morning Lantern Row was a row of teeth—blackened walls, empty sockets—and the market square was a field of ash drifts and trampled cabbages and people sitting on their ruined goods like gulls on a wreck.

Wardens lined the Tanner's Gate. They weren't helping. They were watching, and writing in little books, and when Widow Cress spat at their boots one of them struck her across the mouth with a gauntleted hand, casually, the way you'd swat a fly, and the widow sat down hard in the ashes and began, very quietly, to cry.

Ulyana helped her up. She wiped the blood from the old woman's lip with her apron, and Widow Cress gripped her arm and whispered, "They knew where the charms were sold. Every stall. How'd they know that?"

"I don't know," Ulyana said, which was true, and also a lie, because she suspected she knew exactly how, and the knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

Marta of the dye-works had burned hands, wrapped in wet linen. The Halloways' cart was a black skeleton; Pip stood beside it with her jaw set like her mother's, refusing to cry in public, crying anyway, silently, tears cutting clean tracks through the soot on her face. The young couple who'd bought the birth-blessing ribbon yesterday huddled under a tarp with a bundle Ulyana realized, with a lurch, was a baby. Their room above the chandlery was gone. Everything was gone. Eleven stalls, four homes, one chandlery, one pie-cart, one fish-shop with forty years of family in it.

And here was the arithmetic no one wanted done aloud: one woman had spent twenty years being invisible, and invisibility had protected precisely no one. Not Tammer. Not Marta. Not the baby under the tarp. All those careful years of smallness, all that diligent self-erasure—and the fire had come anyway, and the only thing her hiding had purchased was this: that when the blow fell, she was standing safely to one side of it, close enough to watch.

Her safety hadn't been innocence. It had been seating.

She sat down on the curb next to Tammer and Ferren and shared out the last of the water from her jug, three swallows each, and nobody spoke, because there was nothing language could do that sitting shoulder-to-shoulder couldn't do better.

Near midmorning, a Warden sergeant worked down the line of survivors with his little book, taking names. When he reached her, Ulyana gave him the name she'd used for twenty years—Pemberton, Marigold Pemberton, seamstress—and watched him write it down, and understood, with a strange floating clarity, that the name was dead now. Whatever happened next, Marigold Pemberton of Cinderhollow was finished. The Wardens had made sure of it. Perhaps that was the point. Burn the market, and the witch has nowhere to hide in it.

Sergeant Ilse Vane was not among them. Ulyana looked for her twice without meaning to. Absence, she was learning, had a shape too.

*

Corin found her at noon.

He came through the Tanner's Gate walking fast, ash-gray to the elbows, and when he saw her sitting on the curb his whole face changed—not dramatically, nothing so useful as drama, but the way ice changes when warmth first touches it. He crossed the ruined square without looking at any of the destruction, which took a peculiar kind of discipline, and sat down beside her, and said nothing for a while, because Corin had the rare gift of knowing when words were furniture and when they were intruders.

Eventually he said, "I brought bread." He produced a loaf from inside his coat, slightly crushed, slightly ash-dusted. "It was all the baker had left. He wouldn't take money. He said—" Corin's voice did something complicated. "He said to give it to whoever needed feeding most."

"And you chose me."

"You look like you need feeding most."

She took the bread. It was warm still, in the middle, a small impossible mercy. She broke it and gave half back and they ate in silence while the market smoked around them like a dying hearth.

"I keep thinking," she said finally, "that I could have warned them. Yesterday. Last night. I could have told Tammer to move his stock, told the Halloways to sleep elsewhere. One word and half this ruin doesn't happen." She turned the bread crust over in her fingers. "But warning them means admitting what I am. Means becoming visible. And so I stayed quiet, because staying quiet is the only skill I've practiced since I was nineteen years old, and it turns out I'm very good at it, and it turns out it was worth nothing. Less than nothing. My hiding cost other people their houses."

"That's not fair," Corin said. "That's not how it works. They hid too. Everyone here hid. The whole city hides. You can't hold the weight of a Magistrate on your own shoulders."

"No," she agreed. "But I can feel it there."

He was quiet a moment. Then he said, in the careful voice of a man approaching a stray dog, "The letter. From your sister-apprentice. Have you decided?"

Ulyana looked out at the smoking square—at Tammer hauling salvage, at Pip comforting her mother, at Widow Cress asleep upright against a wall with her split lip crusted brown. At the little pot of marjoram, somehow untouched, sitting on its scorched corner-post like a small green miracle or a small green accusation.

"They burned my market," she said. "My friends. Because somewhere in a Warden's ledger there's a line that starts with me." She brushed ash from her skirt. "Brenna said come home, or they come for me. And I've spent twenty years running from that sentence. And today I finally understand that there is no direction I can run where the sentence doesn't follow. So."

She stood. Ash sifted off her like snow.

"So I'm done hiding," she said. "Not brave. Not noble. Just—" she searched for it and found it, simple and terrible, "—done. If the world insists on seeing me, then let it see all of me. Let it see what I can actually do."

Corin rose beside her. He didn't touch her—that was another of his gifts, knowing the difference between comfort and claim—but he stood close enough that she could feel the strange cool hollow of him, the place where a soul ought to press warm against the world and didn't.

"Then we go to Thornhollow," he said.

"We go to Thornhollow." She picked up her satchel. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, lay *The Ashen Hours*, patient as a knife. "Tonight. Before the Wardens finish their lists and start their knocking."

They walked out through the Tanner's Gate together, past the Wardens who watched them go, past the guards who didn't know yet that the woman in the ash-dusted coat was the reason for all of it. Behind them Cinderhollow smoked and wept and began, in the stubborn way of cities, to pick itself up.

At the gate's threshold, Ulyana paused and looked back once.

Old Tammer caught her eye across the ruins. He raised his hand—not waving goodbye. Holding something up. A scrap of charred thread, blue, one of her luck-knots, half-burned and still knotted tight. He closed his fist around it, and nodded to her, slow and solemn, the way sailors nod to a departing ship.

She nodded back.

Then the gate passed behind her, and the road to Thornhollow opened ahead, gray and long and full of everything she'd spent twenty years avoiding—

—and somewhere behind them, unnoticed, a Warden clerk finished writing a name in a little book, and underlined it twice.
