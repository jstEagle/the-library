## 22. Descent Preparations

The armory at Thornhollow had once been a chapel.

Ulyana could still see the shape of it beneath the renovations—the arched windows bricked to arrow-slits, the altar now a long workbench scarred by decades of knife-work and solder, the saint's niche where Mother Ysolde kept her collection of teeth in labeled jars. As a girl she had knelt on these flagstones learning her letters. Now she stood at the workbench while Brenna laid out instruments of war as gently as if they were sleeping things: rods of salt-iron blackened with soot, coils of copper wire threaded through dried rowan berries, small clay beads that clicked when shaken and hummed when thrown.

"Salt-iron," Brenna said, setting a short blade beside Ulyana's elbow. "It burns the pale ones like sunrise burns a moth. Don't stab yourself with it, which I know sounds obvious until you're frightened."

"I've been frightened before."

"Not like this." Brenna's hands did not pause in their work, but her voice went quieter. "You were frightened of Wardens. Of being caught. That fear has edges you can see. What waits under the mountain—" She shook her head. "The fear down there is round. It has no edges. You can't tell where it ends and you begin."

Ulyana picked up one of the humming beads and turned it over. "You've been down there?"

A silence. Ysolde, dozing in her corner chair with a shawl over her knees and a cup of cold tea going bitter beside her, made a sound that might have been a snore or might have been commentary.

"The coven sends someone every generation," Brenna said finally. "To look at the seals through the mountain's skin. To listen. I went eleven years ago, with Marta and old Fen. Only I came back." She took the bead from Ulyana's fingers and set it down, aligning it precisely with the others. "So believe me when I tell you that the song-charms matter more than the iron. Iron will save your body. The songs save the rest."

She pressed a small wooden whistle into Ulyana's palm—carved from yew, worn smooth at the mouthpiece by other mouths before hers.

"Ysolde made this for you specifically," Brenna said. "Three notes. Low, low, high. It opens a door that isn't there and closes a door that is. She says you'll know which is which when the moment comes." A crooked smile. "I said that sounded unhelpful. She hit me with her cane."

"She always liked me better."

"She always liked everyone better than me. It's my most consistent achievement."

Ulyana laughed despite herself, and something in the room loosened—a knot she hadn't known she was carrying, easing by a single thread. This was the strange new weather between them, three days into her homecoming: not warmth exactly, not yet, but the thaw that comes before it, everything softening and dripping and unstable. They had spent twenty years not speaking, and now they spent their days side by side over weapons, trading jokes like people who had never stopped, and neither of them mentioned how strange it was because mentioning it might break it.

Brenna held up a coil of braided thread—red, black, and a third strand that seemed to move when Ulyana wasn't looking directly at it. "Wearing-cord. Ties your shadow to your body. If anything down there tries to take your shade and wear it home, the cord pulls it back. Uncomfortably."

"You've thought of everything."

"I've had three weeks and a great deal of guilt to work with." Brenna said it lightly. Too lightly. Her hands went very still over the workbench, and Ulyana watched the joke die on her sister-apprentice's face like a moth hitting a window.

"Brenna?"

"There's one more thing you should be armed with." Brenna didn't look up. "And it isn't mine to give you nicely, so I'll give it to you badly, the way I do everything that matters."

* * *

They walked out to the well behind the kitchen garden, where the coven's oldest apple tree leaned over the stones like an old gossip. Evening was coming down purple and gold across the ash country beyond the walls, and somewhere below them, deep under miles of cooling stone, a dragon was starving.

Ulyana remembered this tree. She had climbed it at nine years old and gotten stuck, and Brenna—seven, furious, loyal—had stood underneath shouting instructions for two hours until Ysolde came out and fetched them both down by their collars. The tree had been smaller then. So had everything.

Brenna sat on the well's edge and did not speak for a long moment. Ulyana let her have the silence. She was learning, again, after twenty years, how Brenna's silences worked—how they were not emptiness but pressure, like water behind a dam, and the trick was never to tap the stone but simply to wait beside it.

"When you ran," Brenna said at last, "I told myself you'd died. It was easier. Dead girls don't choose to leave. Dead girls don't get to be free while I stayed and took the rite and learned what bound wrists feel like." She turned a ring on her finger—the High Witch's band, iron set with a chip of mountain glass. "I hated you for four years. Properly. With craft behind it. There are hexes in the eastern records under my name that would curl your hair."

"I don't doubt it."

"And then, in your ninth year gone, a Warden tracker came to the gates asking after a hedge-witch matching your description, working charms in a night market in Cinderhollow." Brenna's voice stayed flat, careful, the voice of a woman reciting a confession she'd rehearsed until the rehearsal wore thin. "They'd found your name in a peddler's ledger. Ulyana Pemberton. Selling luck-knots."

Ulyana felt the ground tilt. Not literally—she knew what literal tilting felt like, and this was worse, interior, a slow rotation of everything she had believed about her own survival. "That was six years ago."

"Yes."

"How did they find me, Brenna? I changed the name on everything. I spelled the stall. I was careful for years."

"I know you were." Brenna looked up then, and her eyes were terrible and steady and full of the thing she had come here to say. "Because I gave them the rest. Your mother's name. Your birth village. The shape of the charm-knots you tie—left-handed, thumb-over, the way Ysolde taught us both. I handed them enough to find the left-handed witch in Cinderhollow who tied her knots like our dead teacher."

The evening kept happening around them. Birds. Wind in the apple tree. The smell of bread from the kitchens. None of it reached Ulyana; she stood inside a small cold space where there was only her sister's face and the words coming out of it.

"You brought the Wardens to me."

"I sent them *near* you." Brenna was on her feet now, hands open, the gesture of a woman showing she holds no weapon—which was itself a kind of lie, because Brenna's weapon had always been truth, deployed at close range. "Listen to me. Listen. Because I have only ever been able to say this once, and I am saying it now: the coven had received word from the mountain court that year. Mavrosa's courtiers had begun collecting unbound witches—not killing them, worse than killing, *collecting*—because an unbound soul is loose coinage down there, spendable anywhere. Two witches from the Sea Reach coven vanished that autumn. Their High Witch got them back in pieces. In jars." Her breath shuddered. "If the court found you first, Ulyana—if they'd traced the unbound apprentice who refused the rite—they wouldn't have burned you. Burning is quick. They'd have bottled you and drunk you slow for a century."

"So you fed me to the Wardens instead."

"The Wardens burn hedge-witches. Quickly. Publicly. With fire that destroys the body and scatters whatever's left to the wind." Brenna's jaw tightened. "I did the arithmetic nobody should ever have to do, and I chose the fire, and I have woken up every morning since choosing it again, and some mornings the choice tastes like mercy and some mornings it tastes like what it is, which is me spending my sister to buy time. And then Ilse Vane found you instead—and didn't report you. For months. I couldn't understand it. A Warden sitting on a live witch like a hen on an egg. And then I read the prophecy book Ysolde recovered, and I understood that the mountain had plans for you both, and that Vane had plans of her own, and that the world was moving whether we pushed it or not."

Ulyana's hands had curled into fists without her permission. She looked down at them—her mother's hands, charm-maker's hands, the hands that had tied ten thousand knots for strangers so that no one would ever look too closely at the witch tying them.

"All those years," she said quietly. "Every night I checked the shutters twice. Every market day I scanned the crowd for gray cloaks. I built a whole life out of watching doors, Brenna. And one of the doors was already open. You'd already let them in."

"I know."

"You could have *told* me. A letter. A warning. 'Run north, sister, they're coming.' Anything."

"A letter travels," Brenna said. "A warning can be intercepted. And a warned witch runs, and a running witch leaves traces, and traces reach the court faster than they reach us." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped to something raw and unarmored. "There was no clean way to keep you alive. There was only the least dirty one. I have never forgiven myself for it and I am not asking you to forgive me either. I'm telling you because you're walking into the mountain in two days, and if you go down there believing the last person who loves you betrayed you for nothing, the court will smell that wound on you and pour itself into it. They feed on grievance the way Ember feeds on souls. So: hate me if you must. But hate me *knowing* why."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the house, Ysolde was banging a pot—dinner, and also, probably, a pointed comment about sisters who conducted difficult conversations outside where the whole valley could watch them posture.

Ulyana found that she was crying, which infuriated her, and that Brenna was crying too, which made it marginally more bearable.

"You let me think the Wardens found me because I slipped," Ulyana said. "For six years I thought I wasn't careful enough. Do you know what that does to a person? To build your whole self around being invisible, and then fail at even that?"

"I know exactly what it does." Brenna wiped her face with her sleeve, graceless, sniffling. "I watched you do it."

"I should hit you."

"You'd miss. You were always rubbish at the striking forms."

"I was *better* than you at the striking forms, you cheated with the binding-cantrip—"

"—which is strategy, not cheating—"

Ulyana swung, half-heartedly, and Brenna caught her wrist the way they'd done a thousand times in the practice yard, and then neither of them moved. Standing there, wrist in grip, twenty years and one confession between them, Ulyana felt something settle in her chest that was not forgiveness—forgiveness was a finished thing, a knot pulled tight, and this was not that. This was messier. This was a knot tied loosely, on purpose, so it could be retied tomorrow, and the day after, by two women who intended to keep having tomorrows together.

"I forgive you the way you taught me the binding-cantrip," Ulyana said. "Badly, and while calling it strategy."

Brenna laughed—a wet, cracked, real laugh—and pulled her into a hug that smelled of woodsmoke and salt-iron and the particular soap of the Thornhollow kitchens, unchanged in twenty years, and Ulyana held on and let herself be held, and did not say *thank you* because some things go without saying, and did not say *I understand* because she didn't, not fully, maybe never—but she stayed, and staying was its own answer.

* * *

Later, past midnight, Ulyana sat alone in the armory packing her satchel, because sleep had become a country she could visit but not stay in. Salt-iron blade. Humming beads. The yew whistle. Brenna's wearing-cord, coiled soft as a sleeping snake. At the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth: *The Ashen Hours*, its pages warm against her fingertips even now, patient as a debt.

Footsteps. Aldous came in carrying two cups of Ysolde's dreadful chicory, handed her one, and lowered himself onto a crate with the careful movements of a man still learning which parts of being alive came naturally.

"Your sister cornered you tonight," he said.

"She confessed to a felony against my peace of mind, yes."

"Mm. She cornered me yesterday too." He blew on his tea. "Told me about the jars. About what the court does with collected souls. Then she told me that if I hurt you, there are hexes in the eastern records under her name that would make me pray for the Wardens." He paused. "I found the whole speech oddly touching."

"That's Brenna. Love expressed exclusively through threat assessment." Ulyana leaned her shoulder against his—deliberately, openly, the way she was practicing doing things now, visible and unhidden. Down the hall, Ysolde's snores rattled the door in its frame, keeping imperfect time like a drum in a parade.

Aldous drank his chicory and grimaced and drank more. Then he said, quietly, "I dreamed about the throne again tonight. Teeth. And this time—" his hand flexed around the cup— "this time there was a door in the throne. Behind the teeth. And I knew, the way you know things in dreams, that the door opens from our side. That it's meant to be opened from our side. Soon."

Ulyana looked at him—at the hollowed place behind his eyes filling in by degrees, at the man who had been a jar on a shelf and was now sitting on a crate drinking bad chicory in her sister's armory—and felt the prophecy book warm against her hip like a hand pressing her forward.

"Two days," she said. "Then we knock."

Outside the arrow-slit windows, far to the north, the mountain stood black against the stars—and for the first time since she'd arrived, Ulyana could have sworn she saw a light in it flicker. Once. Twice. Like a lamp running out of oil.

Like something down there, in the dark, beginning to cough.
