## 23. Into the Mountain

The mouth of the mountain did not look like a door. It looked like a wound.

Seraiva stopped at the edge of it and let her lantern light play across the rock—a ragged seam in the mountainside where the stone had cooled in ropes and folds, black glass gone dull with centuries of ash-fall. It might once have been a river of fire pouring out of the world's throat. Now it was a tunnel, and the tunnel breathed.

Ulyana felt it before she understood it: a slow exhalation of warm air against her face, then a pause, then a slow inhalation that tugged at her hair and her cloak hem like a curious child.

"It breathes," Aldous said quietly.

"Everything down here breathes," Seraiva said. "That's the first thing you must remember. The mountain is not a place with things living in it. The mountain is *alive*, and the court lives inside it the way lice live inside a coat." She glanced back at them, and even by lantern light her smile was lovely and unsettling. "Try not to be lice."

"That's reassuring," Ulyana said. "Thank you. I feel much braver."

"You feel terrified," Seraiva said. "Good. Terror is honest. Bravery is just terror that's been taught manners."

* * *

They had made camp the night before in a hollow below the tree line, where the ash country gave way to grey scree and the last stubborn pines grew sideways out of the wind. Ulyana had barely slept. She'd sat up watching the mountain instead—the true mountain, Mount Vhalen, rising out of the dark like something kneeling—and had felt the same pull she'd felt since Thornhollow, since the drowned chapel, since the moment Seraiva had pressed one cold finger to the map and said, *Here. This is how we go in without being announced.*

Now, standing before the entrance, she found herself doing what she always did when frightened: checking her supplies. Salt-iron in its pouch—check. Ysolde's song-charms, three of them, wrapped in waxed linen against her ribs—check. The Ashen Hours, wrapped oilcloth-tight and strapped across her back where no one could take it without taking her shoulder too—check.

Aldous watched her do it with an expression she was beginning to know well. Not quite amusement. Not quite worry. Something in between that he probably would have called *fondness* if either of them were brave enough to use words like that yet.

"The book will still be there in ten minutes," he said.

"I'm not checking the book."

"You're checking everything, which includes the book."

"I'm *inventorying*," Ulyana said with dignity. "It's a merchant's habit. Twenty years of selling luck-knots teaches you to count your stock before you walk anywhere dangerous."

"And what's your stock?"

"One witch, slightly used. One man with a soul on layaway. One deposed queen of questionable motives." She buckled the flap closed. "I've sold worse inventories."

Seraiva, ahead of them, snorted—an unexpectedly human sound from someone who otherwise moved like poured mercury. "You two bicker like an old married couple. It's disgusting. Keep doing it. Fear goes quieter when there's talk over it."

* * *

The tunnel narrowed within the first hundred paces.

It began wide enough for the three of them to walk abreast, the ceiling lost somewhere above their lantern glow, walls ribbed with ancient flowstone. Then the passage bent, and bent again, and the walls drew in like hands closing, until they were walking single file through a corridor barely wider than Ulyana's shoulders, the ceiling low enough that Aldous—tall, long-limbed, built for open fields and city streets—had to fold himself forward as he walked.

Ulyana heard his breathing change behind her. Shorter. Shallower.

She remembered the inn without mirrors, the way he'd stood in front of the blank wall where a mirror should have hung, and she understood: for a man who had spent his whole adult life hollowed out, a passage this tight was not just uncomfortable. It was a reminder. There wasn't room in here for anything but exactly who you were.

"Talk to me," she said over her shoulder. "Tell me about Cinderhollow. The night market. What did you sell, before all this?"

"Rope," he said. His voice came out flat and careful. "Hemp rope. Tarred line. It's not a romantic trade."

"Everything's romantic if you tell it right. Go on. Rope."

A pause, filled only by dripping water and the mountain's slow breathing. Then, grudgingly: "There was a woman who came every market day to buy exactly three feet of tarred line. Never more, never less. Every week for four years. I never asked what for. I liked her. I think about her more than is reasonable for a customer."

"Why three feet?"

"I don't know. That's why I think about her." A breath. A slightly steadier one. "You can build whole friendships out of mysteries, if you don't insist on solving them."

Ulyana smiled at the wall in front of her nose. "That may be the wisest thing anyone's said in this tunnel, and a queen said something earlier."

Ahead of them, Seraiva lifted her lantern higher. "Flattery noted, hedge-witch. Mind the floor here—the glass is thin. Step where I step or lose a boot."

* * *

An hour in—or what Ulyana guessed was an hour; time went strange when the sky was gone—the tunnel dropped.

Stairs, of a sort. The old lava had pooled and terraced as it cooled, leaving a descent of broad glassy shelves, each one slick with condensation. Seraiva went down them like water finding its level. Ulyana went down them like a woman who had spent twenty years avoiding ladders, which was to say slowly, swearing softly, both palms flat against the wall.

Aldous descended behind her with one hand hovering at her elbow—not touching, just *there*, the way he'd learned to be. She'd noticed it over the road. He never grabbed, never steadied without asking. He simply arranged himself so that if she fell, she would fall into him.

"My grandmother climbed these," Seraiva said conversationally, from below. "Well. Not these. These didn't exist yet. But she climbed into the mountain the hard way, through the fire, when it was still fire."

"Your grandmother came in through *lava*?" Ulyana said.

"She was the dragon's first friend. Fire doesn't burn what trusts it." Seraiva paused on a shelf and looked up, lantern light carving her face into planes and shadows. "That is also how you should think of the court, when we reach it. Do not trust it. But do not flinch, either. Flinching is a scent down there. They follow it."

"Wonderful," Ulyana muttered. "Anything else? Should I bleed attractively? Whistle a specific tune?"

"If you meet anything pale and polite, agree with it."

* * *

* * *

The second hour brought the heat.

Not the dry furnace-heat of a forge—something older, wetter, a warmth that rose out of the stone itself and wrapped around them like a blanket pulled by patient hands. Sweat prickled at Ulyana's hairline. Her charm-satchel, hanging at her hip, began to smell strongly of the dried herbs inside it: wormwood and rue and salt-mustard seed, released by the warmth like a confession.

And beneath the heat, beneath the drip and settle of cooling stone, there was the sound.

She noticed it the way you notice your own heartbeat—suddenly, and then unable to stop noticing. A vast, slow rhythm under everything. Not the mountain's breathing. Deeper than breathing.

"What is that?" she whispered.

Seraiva had gone very still, lantern raised, her face turned toward the wall as though listening through it. When she answered, her voice had changed—all the silk gone out of it, leaving something raw.

"The fire," she said. "Ember-that-Waits. Its heartbeat carries through the stone. We are walking down its throat toward its heart." Her hand flattened against the rock, and Ulyana saw—actually saw—the queen's fingers tremble. "It is slower than it was. When I was young, it beat like a drum in festival. Now—" She stopped. Started again. "Now it beats like something trying to remember how."

Ulyana put her own palm against the wall.

Warmth. Stone. And underneath, faint as a word spoken in another room: *boom ... boom ... boom.* Long pauses between. Too long. She thought of her mother—no, she couldn't, she'd traded those years, she thought of nothing, she thought of the gap where a memory should be—and pressed her forehead briefly to the rock.

"Hold on," she told the mountain, absurdly, quietly. "We're coming. We're slow, but we're coming."

Behind her, Aldous had stopped too. His eyes were closed. His lips moved around something soundless, and when he opened his eyes they were wet.

"Ash," he said. "I taste ash. Same as every morning." He swallowed. "But this time I almost—there was a voice under it. Saying a name."

"Whose name?"

"I don't know. Mine, maybe. Or the name mine used to be." He looked at her, and in the lantern light his face was stripped of everything except the question underneath it. "What if I go down there and I remember, and I'm someone you don't—"

"Stop," Ulyana said.

She reached back without looking—found his wrist, wrapped her fingers around it, and kept walking, towing him gently along the glassy floor.

"Whoever you were," she said, "you bought tea from a witch who refused to serve you, and you came back anyway, and you learned to braid luck-knots badly, and you held my hand at a river crossing while I set the water on fire. That person exists. Whatever else is buried under the ash, that person grew *on top* of it, and I have met him, and he is worth knowing." She squeezed his wrist. "So we'll go down, and we'll find whatever's left of Aldous Vane, and we'll introduce them. They'll get along. They're both stubborn."

He laughed—a cracked, wet, wonderful laugh—and followed her down.

* * *

The tunnel ended, and began again, and ended.

They ate crouched in a wider chamber where a side-passage exhaled cool air, eating hard bread and salted plums while Seraiva slept sitting upright with the lantern on her knees, one eye open, the way cats sleep. Ulyana checked her charms again. Aldous pretended not to watch her check her charms, which was its own kind of tenderness.

"Do you think it'll work?" he asked finally. "What we're doing. Any of it."

"No."

He raised his eyebrows.

"I think," Ulyana said, chewing, "that the prophecy has been right twice, and prophecies that are right twice get arrogant about the third time. I think queens lie, including beautiful ones sitting right there pretending to sleep. I think the coven armed me with salt-iron because they love me, and with song-charms because they expect me to need rescuing, and both of those things can be true." She brushed crumbs from her knees. "But I also think a dying dragon has been holding up the ceiling of the world for a thousand years on borrowed souls, and somebody owes it better than that. So. We'll see."

"That's not a plan."

"It's better. Plans break. This just bends."

From her corner, without opening her eye, Seraiva said: "The hedge-witch should rule something someday. Remind me to arrange it."

* * *

* * *

The third hour was the worst.

The passage shrank. Truly shrank—not narrow-and-unpleasant but *wrong*, a crawl of smooth-worn glass where the ceiling met the floor and the only way through was on hands and knees, elbows scraping, lantern held out ahead in one fist, the light showing five feet of black polish and nothing beyond.

Ulyana went first, because she was smallest, and because if she let Aldous go first she would spend the entire crawl imagining the ceiling falling on him.

Halfway through, the mountain inhaled and did not exhale.

The air went still. Utterly, horribly still—the kind of stillness that has weight. Ulyana froze, cheek against warm glass, and felt the walls press in around her like a held breath, like a fist deciding, like the whole weight of the mountain above her suddenly *noticing* her, a grain of grit in a great sleeping throat—

*Cough us out,* she thought wildly. *Cough us out or swallow us, but choose.*

Panic rose in her chest like floodwater. She knew this panic. She knew it from the Warden raid, from the cellar she'd hidden in at nine years old while boots crossed the floor above, from every closet and every silence and every small safe life she'd ever built out of being overlooked. Small spaces. Hidden places. Her whole survival had been built of them, and now here she was, in the smallest, most hidden place of her life, and it wanted to kill her, and there was nowhere to be overlooked *into*.

Then Aldous's voice came through the dark behind her. Low. Steady. Talking about rope.

"—so the trick with tarred line, you have to coil it wet or it cracks, everyone does it wrong, and there was this apprentice I trained, Denny, sweet boy, absolutely incapable of coiling anything—" a breath, slightly ragged, controlled anyway— "and I used to tell him, Denny, the rope doesn't hate you, it just wants to be held properly. Everything wants to be held properly. You just have to ask it right."

Ulyana laughed—a shaky, airless laugh, half sob—and laid both palms flat against the glass.

"Please," she whispered to the mountain. "Hold us properly. That's all. We're coming to help your fire. Ask us right and we'll ask you right."

The mountain exhaled.

Warm air rushed past her, ruffling her hair, carrying the smell of hot iron and, impossibly faintly, of woodsmoke and cinnamon—someone's hearth, a thousand years ago, folded into the stone. The pressure eased. The passage was just a passage again.

She crawled on, laughing and crying at the same time, and when she emerged into the wider dark beyond and turned to haul Aldous through after her, he took one look at her face and said, "It worked, then. Asking nicely."

"Don't you dare be smug while covered in glass dust."

"I'm always smug when I'm right."

"He held the rope wrong," Seraiva observed, sliding out behind them, immaculate despite everything, because of course she was. "For what it's worth. The mountain prefers to be sung to. Next squeeze, sing."

"I'd rather fight it," Ulyana said.

"Yes," Seraiva said. "Most people would. That's why most people die in there."

* * *

* * *

Near what Ulyana judged must be midnight—if midnight still existed without a sky—they smelled the court before they saw any sign of it.

Perfume. That was the horror of it. After hours of stone and sulfur and hot iron, the air ahead suddenly bloomed with something floral and cold: white roses and snowmelt and something underneath like copper coins left in rain. It drifted up the tunnel toward them, elegant and wrong, a guest arriving early.

Seraiva raised a hand. They stopped.

Beyond the next bend, the tunnel opened—she could feel it, a widening, a roominess after hours of constriction—and from that opening came light. Not lantern light. A pale, sourceless luminescence, the color of moonlight strained through pearl, flickering faintly as though the whole cavern beyond were lit from within by something slow and enormous turning over in its sleep.

And beneath the perfume, beneath the light, the heartbeat. Louder now. *Boom ... boom.* Each beat rolling through the floor and up through the soles of Ulyana's boots like a tide deciding.

"We are here," Seraiva said softly. She smoothed her travel-worn clothes with both hands—an oddly vain, oddly human gesture—and when she turned to them, her beauty had assembled itself into something formal and terrible. "Past that bend is the outer gallery. From there, the ways down to the soul-market. The court will already know we've entered—the mountain tells them everything—but they will not yet know *what* we are. That ignorance is our only coin. Spend it carefully."

"And if they ask what we are?" Aldous said.

Seraiva smiled. It did not reach her eyes, which had gone bright and distant, fixed on the pearl-light ahead like a woman looking at a house she'd been thrown out of.

"Then I will remind them who wore the crown before the thief took it," she said, "and you two will look appropriately awed. Can you manage awe?"

"I've practiced my whole life," Ulyana said, "at looking like less than I am. Looking like more should be easy."

For the first time since the drowned chapel, Seraiva laughed genuinely—a short, startled, real laugh—and Ulyana decided, provisionally and against all her better judgment, that she liked the exiled queen.

Then they rounded the bend together, into the light.

The gallery opened around them like the inside of a geode the size of a cathedral—walls glittering with crystal seams, vaulted far overhead into darkness, and everywhere the pale radiance pulsing gently with each distant beat of the dying fire. And filling the gallery, drifting between its pillars in robes the color of frost, pale and courteous and already turning to look—

Dozens of faces. All smiling.

All waiting.

"Remember," Seraiva murmured, lips barely moving, "agree with anything pale and polite."

One of the figures detached itself from the rest and began gliding toward them, smiling wider, and Ulyana's hand crept toward the song-charms at her ribs as the pulse of the dying dragon rolled through the floor beneath her feet—slower now, she was sure of it, slower than yesterday, slower than an hour ago—

*Boom.*

*Boom.*

*... boom.*
