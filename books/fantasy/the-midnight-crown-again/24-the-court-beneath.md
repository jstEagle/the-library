## 24. The Court Beneath

The tunnel changed its mind about them three times before they reached the bottom.

First it narrowed to a crawl, the walls pressing close enough that Ulyana could feel the mountain's heartbeat through her palms—slow, enormous, and wrong, like a drum played by something that had forgotten why drums existed. Then it opened without warning into a gallery of black glass where their footsteps rang back at them a half-second late, as if the mountain were repeating what they said for its own amusement. And then, finally, it began to warm.

"Warm is bad," Seraiva said over her shoulder, in the tone of someone reporting weather. "Cold means the fire's far away. Warm means you're standing on top of it."

"Is there a temperature that means we should turn around?" Aldous asked.

"All of them," Seraiva said pleasantly. "That's rather the point of exiles."

Ulyana had stopped counting the hours somewhere past the second sleepless rest against warm stone. The lava tunnels wound down and down, sometimes smooth as poured candle-wax, sometimes jagged as a broken promise. Salt-iron charms clicked softly against her collarbone with every step—Mother Ysolde's work, each one knotted with a verse Ulyana was only permitted to know half of. *The other half keeps you safe,* Ysolde had said. *The first half keeps you brave. I could only fit one in the thread.*

She had thought about that a great deal in the dark.

Aldous walked beside her, close enough that his elbow brushed hers on the turns. He had been quieter since the dreams started—the throne of teeth, the queen with his mother's voice—but quiet in him had a different texture now. Less hollow. More like a man holding a door shut from the inside.

"You're doing the thing," he murmured.

"What thing?"

"Where you count my breaths."

"I am not counting your—" She was, a little. "I'm checking that you're breathing. There's a difference, and it's called concern."

"It's called counting," he said, but his hand found hers in the dark and stayed there, and neither of them mentioned it again, which was its own kind of conversation.

Ahead, Seraiva raised one pale hand, and the column behind her—two coven sisters named Merrin and Osla who had volunteered with the grim enthusiasm of people who had never been underground before—stilled.

"We're here," the exiled queen said. "Whatever happens next: I speak. You listen. If anyone addresses a courtier directly, they will answer honestly, and their honest answers will be used to purchase things. Do you understand?"

"Purchase things how?" Merrin whispered.

"With *you*," Seraiva said, and smiled the way winter smiles.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain did not announce itself. It simply arrived—one moment tunnel, the next an opening so vast that Ulyana's eyes gave up trying to find the ceiling and settled instead for the walls, which were veined with slow rivers of ember-light, red and gold and dying orange, like the inside of a banked hearth the size of a cathedral.

And the sound. That was the worst of it, or would have been, if she'd had time to decide about worsts. The whole cavern hummed—a low, sweet, endless chord, like ten thousand voices humming in their sleep—and underneath the hum, fainter, a second sound: the tick and clink of commerce.

They stood at the mouth of a market street.

Ulyana had spent twenty years in markets. She knew them the way she knew her own hands: the haggling rhythms, the way light fell on cheap tin versus good silver, the small theater of a seller pretending not to watch a buyer linger. So it took her only three heartbeats to recognize this place for what it was, and another five for her stomach to understand it, and those last five heartbeats were the longest of her life.

Stalls lined both sides of the street, carved from obsidian and lit by jars of cold blue flame. And on every stall, in ranks and rows and glittering pyramids—

Bottles.

Small ones, fat ones, some no bigger than a thumb and some tall as a wine flagon. Each held a soft glow that moved. Not flickered—*moved*, the way a sleeping person moves, turning over, reaching for something. Some glowed gold. Some green. One, near the front of the nearest stall, glowed a stubborn, cheerful pink, and Ulyana found herself unable to look away from it, because whatever was inside that bottle had been, at some point, a person who liked pink.

"Souls," Aldous breathed beside her. His face had gone the color of the tunnel stone. "These are all—"

"Bottled lives," Seraiva said. "Fresh-decanted, mostly. The courtiers trade them among themselves like spices. Older vintages go to the fire." She said it flatly, the way one reads a menu, and Ulyana understood suddenly that the flatness was armor, worn so long it had grown into the skin. "Keep walking. Don't touch anything. Especially don't *want* anything. Wanting is currency here."

"That seems," Osla began, "deeply—"

"Walk," Seraiva said.

They walked.

The courtiers themselves were pale—not white-pale but *unlit*-pale, like figures seen through frosted glass. They drifted between stalls in robes that moved too slowly, trailing ribbons of shadow, and their faces were beautiful in the way that empty houses are beautiful: everything symmetrical, nothing home. When they spoke to one another, their voices joined the great hum, harmonizing with it unconsciously, and Ulyana realized with a lurch that the hum wasn't architecture or magic.

It was the souls. All of them, together, singing in their bottles because singing was the only thing left they could do.

One courtier paused at a stall, lifted two bottles, and held them to the blue flame the way a jeweler holds stones. Inside one, the light pressed desperately against the glass. Inside the other, the light had gone thin, diffuse, patient. The courtier shook the first gently, listened to something Ulyana couldn't hear, and set it back with a small disappointed tilt of the head.

*It rattled,* Ulyana thought wildly. *He didn't buy it because it rattled.* And then the absurdity cracked something open in her, and she had to press her knuckles to her mouth to keep from making a sound that was half laugh and entirely sob, because it was her market, wasn't it? It was the night market in Cinderhollow, translated. The same stalls, the same weighing and comparing, the same sellers' patter. Only here the goods were people, and the haggling never ended, and nothing was ever carried home.

Aldous's grip on her hand had gone tight enough to hurt. She didn't loosen it.

"There." Seraiva nodded down the street, to where the stalls thinned and the ember-veins in the walls converged like tributaries. Beyond them, filling the far end of the cavern the way a mountain fills a valley, lay the hoard.

* * *

Ulyana had expected gold. Everyone expected gold; dragons were gold the way winters were cold. But the heap that rose out of the cavern floor was coins only at the edges. At its heart, gleaming dully in the ember-light, were years.

She knew them when she saw them, the way you know a face across a crowded market even after decades. Coins of every mint and century, yes—but also rings, keys, buttons, wedding bands, baby teeth, a child's tin soldier fused by heat to a woman's locket, a hundred thousand small bright objects that had each meant *a day* to somebody once. Spent time, traded in, hoarded. A dragon's bed made of everything people had given up to stay alive, or keep others alive, or simply because the alternative had been worse.

And on top of it, folded like an old dog in winter sun, lay Ember-that-Waits.

The stories had prepared her for vastness. Nothing had prepared her for *dimness*. The dragon's scales, which must have burned like a forge once, now glowed the way coals glow an hour after the fire's abandoned—beautiful, still, and going out. Her wings lay slack across the hoard. Her ribs rose and fell in long, labored swells, and with each exhale a fine gray ash sifted off her flanks and drifted down between the coins.

But her eye was open.

One eye, vast as a cellar door, amber shot through with fading veins of red—and it was watching them. Had been watching them, Ulyana realized, since the moment they stepped out of the tunnel. Perhaps longer. Perhaps since Cinderhollow. Perhaps since the day twenty years ago when a frightened apprentice ran from Thornhollow with a book under her arm and a debt in her chest, while deep beneath the world a starving creature felt the ledger shift.

"She knows us," Merrin whispered. It wasn't a question.

"She knows *everyone*," Seraiva said. "She has eaten everyone's years. You are, to her, a flavor she remembers fondly. Walk slowly."

They came down the slope of spent time, boots crunching on centuries. Halfway there, Ulyana stepped on a hairpin and went cold all over, because the hairpin was shaped like a thrush in flight, and Brenna had worn one just like it, and either it was a different hairpin or it was the same hairpin, and both possibilities were unbearable.

Ember-that-Waits spoke without moving anything but the air.

Her voice arrived the way heat arrives—not heard so much as *felt*, settling into the bones, into the teeth, into the spaces between thoughts. It was a very old voice, and very tired, and gentler than any voice had any right to be, coming from a creature that could have swallowed their entire party the way a cat swallows a moth.

*Little unbound one.* The amber eye turned fully on Ulyana, and being looked at by it was like being read aloud. *You smell of salt-iron and fear and someone else's cooking. Twenty years of someone else's cooking. How was it?*

Ulyana's mouth was dry. "How was what?"

*Hiding.*

"Oh," Ulyana said. And then, because lying to a creature made of truth and fire seemed unwise: "Warm. Mostly. Lonely in the middle."

*Mm.* The exhale stirred ash across the hoard. *I remember warm. I invented it.*

Beside her, Aldous made a small sound. The dragon's eye shifted to him, and something in the vast faded gaze *softened*, the way a grandmother's face changes when a grandchild enters the room.

*And there you are,* Ember said. *Sold before you drew breath. Carried twice. Once in your mother, once in a bottle. Tell me, boy—do they still sing to you at night?*

Aldous's jaw worked. "The ash," he managed. "Every night. Tasting ash. Is that—is that the singing?"

*No.* Something moved through the great body then, a shudder that set the whole hoard chiming softly, thousands of small metal griefs ringing at once. *What tastes like ash is me. You were bottled within reach of my fire, little twice-carried. Some of my smoke got into the seal. When I gutter, you'll taste it first. I am sorry for that. It was not done kindly, and it was not done by me, and it will not be undone by anyone polite.*

Seraiva stepped forward, and for the first time since the drowned chapel, the exiled queen looked uncertain. "Ember. Mother-of-Coals. We've come to—"

*I know why you've come.* The eye swept them all. *Everyone comes. Every generation, someone comes down the throat of the world to ask the old question. Shall I tell you what I tell them?*

Nobody answered. The hum of the soul-market filled the silence, ten thousand bottled voices singing their one long note, and Ulyana realized the dragon had timed her pause deliberately—to let them hear it. To make the market the answer.

*I am the lock,* Ember said. *The seals beneath me are woven of my fire, and my fire is woven of what I am fed. Willing years burn brightest. Stolen ones smolder. Bottled ones—" she gestured, wearily, back toward the market street, "*—bottled ones are gruel. They keep me alive the way bread and water keeps a prisoner alive. Your queens have fed me gruel for four hundred years and called it salvation, and the seals hold, barely, badly, thinner every winter. I am not a monster on a hoard, little witch. I am a wall that eats. And the wall is starving.*

"And if you starve?" Ulyana asked quietly.

The ash-fall thickened. Somewhere far below them—or perhaps not below, perhaps *beneath everything*, beneath the idea of below—something shifted its weight in the dark, and the mountain's heartbeat stuttered.

*Then the things your coven sings lullabies about stop being songs,* said Ember-that-Waits. *You have until the thaw. Possibly less. Your Magistrate's fires have heated the stone above me strangely this year; hunger moves faster through warm rock.*

"The thaw," Ulyana repeated. "That's—that's weeks."

*Yes.* The great eye closed briefly, and when it opened again, the fading fire in it had arranged itself into something that took Ulyana a long moment to name, because she had last seen it in a mirror over a charm-stall in Cinderhollow, on the nights she let herself count what her safety cost.

It was pity.

*So,* said the dragon, gently, terribly. *Now you know what the wall eats. The question—the only question, the one your queens never dared ask aloud—is whether anyone down here is willing.*

* * *

They withdrew to the mouth of the tunnel to argue in whispers, because apparently even at the end of the world, people formed committees.

Merrin wanted to send word to the coven immediately. Osla wanted to take a bottle—just one, a small one, the pink one—as evidence, and was voted down so fast she didn't finish the sentence. Seraiva stood apart, arms folded, staring at the market with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she hated, and Ulyana caught enough of it to guess the sum: *the throne, the feeding, the machine, and whether breaking it kills more than it saves.*

Aldous touched Ulyana's elbow. "You've gone quiet."

"I'm always quiet. It's my defining feature. Twenty years of practice."

"You've gone quiet *differently*."

She looked back down the street of stalls, at the blue jar-lights, at the courtiers drifting with their beautiful empty faces, at the pink bottle glowing stubbornly in its row. "When I was nineteen," she said, "I ran from a rite because they told me my soul would belong to something else. I thought that was the most horrible thing a person could do to another person. Sell them." Her voice caught on the last word and she pushed through it. "And then I spent twenty years buying my own safety with other people's arrests, telling myself invisibility was harmless, and I come down here and find an entire *economy* built on exactly that lie, scaled up until it props up the sky. Corin—I keep thinking, the only difference between me and these courtiers is inventory size."

He was quiet a moment. "For what it's worth," he said, "the courtiers don't look like they hate themselves."

"No. That's the frightening part."

"I hated myself for years without knowing why. Empty year, ash mornings, the whole miserable catalogue." He shrugged. "Turns out it's hard to feel like a person when part of you is on a shelf. So I'd say—" he tipped his head toward the market, toward the humming, "—they know exactly what they're doing. That's worse."

Ulyana looked at him—at the man whose face had been inked in a burning book a lifetime before she met him, whose soul sat somewhere in this cavern in glass, labeled with a name that belonged to someone else's guilt—and felt the prophecy settle onto her shoulders like a coat cut precisely to her measurements.

*Aldous Vane.* She hadn't told him yet. She'd found the label in the vision the dragon's presence had stirred, or perhaps she'd known since the inn without mirrors, and either way the words were in her chest now, heavy as a hoard coin, and every hour she kept them was a small theft of her own.

Before she could decide—before she could be brave or cowardly, whichever way the words came out—the hum changed.

It faltered. All of it, the whole vast chord of ten thousand bottled singers, stumbling at once like a choir missing its cue. The blue jar-lights guttered. Down the length of the market street, courtiers turned their beautiful heads in perfect unison toward the far end of the cavern, toward the hoard, toward something entering from the opposite tunnel with the unhurried confidence of ownership.

Seraiva went rigid. "Mavrosa," she breathed. "She's felt the fire dip. She's come to feed it early—"

The new arrival resolved out of the ember-light: a figure in a gown of layered black silk that moved like smoke deciding to be cloth, crowned with a circlet of teeth—real teeth, dozens of them, none matching, all polished. Pale courtiers sank to their knees along both sides of the street in a ripple, the way wind goes through wheat.

And Mavrosa, Queen of Teeth, mistress of the market, keeper of the gruel, paused halfway down the aisle of bottled lives, lifted her head, and looked directly at the mouth of the tunnel where six intruders stood frozen in the dark.

Her smile was warm as a hearth, and as hungry.

"Well," she said, and her voice carried without effort, sliding into the hum as though it had always been part of the song. "Visitors. How thoughtful. I do so hate to shop alone."
