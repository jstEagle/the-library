## 25. The Queen of Teeth

The throne room announced itself long before we reached it, the way a storm announces itself by the smell of rain and the behavior of birds.

First came the sound—a low, wet chiming that echoed down the lava tunnels like a thousand glasses being stroked at once. Then the warmth. The tunnels had been cooling for hours, the stone going from painful to merely hot to something almost comfortable against my palms as we crawled and climbed through passages shaped like the inside of a throat. But now the air thickened again, sweetened, until every breath felt like drinking.

"Ambergris," Seraiva said. She moved ahead of us with the unhurried grace of someone returning to a house she'd been evicted from. "They scent the halls with it when there's trade being done. Trade days were always the worst. Everyone smiling."

"You've been gone long?" Aldous asked. He said her name carefully—*Seraiva*—as if testing whether the syllables would cut him. Since the drowned chapel she had taken to walking close beside him, closer than I liked, studying him sidelong whenever he spoke, and he pretended not to notice, which meant he noticed completely.

"Two hundred years," she said. "Give or take a decade I spent in a bog." She glanced back at me, and her smile was all edges. "Your coven's records are very thorough, witch. I'm told they list me under *catastrophes*."

"I wouldn't know," I lied, because in fact I'd read her entry twice on the road. *Seraiva, called the Drowned Queen. Deposed. Do not bargain without salt.*

The tunnel opened.

I have seen the night market at its fullest, lanterns strung shoulder to shoulder, the whole street glowing like a spilled jewelry box. It looked like a funeral next to what lay below the mountain.

The cavern was vast enough to have weather. Clouds of gold dust hung in slow spirals beneath a ceiling lost in darkness, lit from beneath by veins of ember-light running through the floor like rivers of slow blood. And everywhere—the sound explained itself—everywhere were shelves. Terraces of them, rising in tiers toward a dais at the far end where a throne sat that I would later learn was carved, genuinely carved, from teeth. Thousands of them, fused and polished, white as fresh bone. Courtiers stood among the shelves in gowns the color of candle-smoke, each of them holding bottles, and each bottle held a light.

I knew what the lights were before anyone told me. You spend twenty years selling charms to frightened people; you learn what souls look like when they're small enough to hold.

"Oh," Aldous whispered beside me. His hand found mine without either of us arranging it. "Oh, that's—" He swallowed. "That's where I am. Somewhere in there. That's *me*."

"Not yet," Seraiva murmured. "Stay behind your eyes, sold one. They can't take what you don't offer."

"And you," said a voice from everywhere at once, "shouldn't be here at all."

The court went silent the way a held breath is silent.

She descended the steps of the tooth-throne slowly, and I understood immediately why the old books spent so many words on her. Mavrosa was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful—perfect, inviting, and built entirely over something dark and deep and patient. Her gown was black shot through with veins of ember-gold, as though she wore the mountain's own fire as trim. Her crown was iron, unadorned, which somehow made everything worse. And her face—

Her face was delighted.

"Sister," Mavrosa said, and the word dripped honey and arsenic in equal measure. "You *came home*. Two hundred years of sulking in chapels and bogs, and all it took to bring you crawling back was—" she paused, savoring it, "—a hedge-witch and a jarless man. How wonderfully small your ambitions have become."

"Mavrosa." Seraiva didn't bow. Around us, courtiers began drifting inward, silent as smoke, forming an elegant closing fist. "You're wearing my crown wrong. It sits better tilted left."

"It sits however I say it sits. That's rather the point of taking things." Mavrosa reached the bottom step and stopped, her attention sliding past Seraiva entirely—and landing on me. On the satchel at my hip. Something in my chest went cold and small.

"And you," she said softly. "Ulyana Pemberton. Of Cinderhollow. Seller of luck-knots, mender of fevers, the little mouse who slipped out of Thornhollow with her whiskers intact." She smiled. It did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "Do you know how rare you are? An unbound witch who walked away clean. We've kept your page marked in our ledger for twenty years. There's a whole shelf reserved."

"I'm flattered," I managed. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "I usually only rate a footnote."

That got a laugh—not from Mavrosa, but from the courtiers, a ripple of silvery, terrible amusement, quickly smothered. Mavrosa's smile widened by a degree that made me wish I'd stayed quiet.

"Oh, I like this one," she said to the court. "Bring them."

* * *

They brought us not to cells—that came later, though I didn't know it then—but to a receiving hall hung with tapestries woven from something that wasn't thread. Silk of memory, Seraiva breathed to me as we were escorted; the court wove its history in stolen recollections, and the walls showed flickering half-scenes of faces and fires and festivals no one living remembered. Guards of pale glass walked us between them, their bodies translucent, their interiors swirling with dim lights that might have been bottled souls doing the work of muscle. I tried not to look too closely at the one holding my elbow. Looking closely at things down here had a way of making them worse.

We were given chairs. Actual chairs, cushioned, arranged around a low table set with tea.

"This is a trap," Aldous said flatly.

"Everything is a trap," Seraiva replied, settling into hers with the composure of a woman who had attended two hundred years of hostile tea. "The question is whether the trap has good refreshments. This one does. Drink the amber kind, refuse the red."

Mavrosa arrived alone. That was the most frightening part, I decided—the queen of the court beneath the mountain dismissing her guards to pour tea herself, lifting the pot with her own ringed hands, playing at hostess while the walls watched us with woven memories.

"To old acquaintances," she said, filling four cups. "And new debts."

"We haven't come to bargain," I said.

"Everyone who comes below comes to bargain. Some just don't know their price yet." She passed me a cup. Her fingers brushed mine—cold, cold as the chapel water Seraiva had been hiding in—and her gaze dropped, briefly, to my satchel. To the shape of a book beneath the oilcloth.

My stomach turned over.

"The Ashen Hours," Mavrosa said, conversationally, as though remarking on the weather. "Every copy accounted for. Burned, buried, dissolved. All but one. All but the one a nervous apprentice was supposed to feed to a fire twenty years ago and—didn't." Her eyes lifted to mine, and there was nothing frozen about them anymore; they burned. "Where is it, little mouse? In that bag? Under your bed? Swallowed in pieces?"

I put my hand over the satchel. A stupid gesture, protective as a child guarding bread, and I knew it, and she saw me know it, and her smile deepened.

"There it is," she whispered. "There it *is*."

"She hasn't agreed to anything," Seraiva said sharply.

"She doesn't need to. Look at her face, sister. She's already negotiating with herself. I can wait; I've had two centuries of practice." Mavrosa leaned back, cradling her cup, and let the silence do its work. It was very good work. The hall hummed faintly, the tapestries rippling with their half-born scenes, and somewhere far above—or perhaps far within—I felt the faint, arrhythmic pulse of the dragon-fire, slower than any heartbeat ought to be.

Aldous drank his tea. Amber kind, per instructions. When he lowered the cup, his hands were trembling.

"Why me?" he asked suddenly. "Of all the souls in all those bottles—why does everyone keep looking at me like I matter?"

Mavrosa regarded him with something that, on anyone else, I might have called tenderness.

"Because you were expensive," she said. "Do you know what your soul cost, Aldous Vane? A Magistrate's favor. A family's survival. A debt paid in full on the day you were born, by a girl barely older than you are now, who signed you away to save the rest." She said it gently. That was the horror of her—she wrapped every cruelty in kindness like a gift. "You've been ours since your first breath. And now here you sit, drinking our tea, asking why we care. Sweet thing. You're not a person to us. You're an heirloom."

His face went white. I reached under the table and took his hand.

"Don't," Mavrosa said, watching us with interest. "Don't do that. It makes the next part harder for both of you."

"What next part?" I asked.

She set down her cup. The porcelain click rang through the hall like a struck bell, and every tapestry stilled.

"The merciful part," she said. "The part where everyone gets what they want and no one has to burn."

* * *

Here is the thing about temptation dressed as mercy: it fits so well that you check yourself for seams and find none.

Mavrosa laid it out simply, the way a merchant lays out cloth. She rose, crossed the hall, and stood before Aldous, and from the air itself she drew a bottle—no, not drew; it was simply *there*, as if it had always been waiting in her hand, a small vessel of smoked glass with a stopper of iron. Inside it, a light. Not a dim, guttering scrap like the ones on the shelves outside. This light was whole. Round. Warm. It pressed against the glass like a face against a window.

Aldous made a sound I will hear until I die. Not a word. Just a small, broken recognition, the noise a man makes when he sees his own heart beating outside his chest.

"Yours," Mavrosa said softly. "Kept safe. Kept *whole*—I never let them drink from this one; it amused me to preserve it. Your memories, your name, your mother's lullabies, every year you lost. One stopper." She turned. Her eyes found mine over the top of the bottle. "One book."

The hall went very quiet.

"No," Seraiva started.

"Hush, sister. This isn't yours to haggle over." Mavrosa hadn't moved. The bottle glowed between her hands like a lantern. "Think of it, Ulyana Pemberton. No rite, no fire, no desperate crawl through dying tunnels. The dragon-fire is my problem; I have fed it for two hundred years and I will feed it for two hundred more. It is not your burden. It was never your burden. You were a girl who wanted a small life, and you *earned* one—you paid for it with twenty years of fear, and I am offering to let you keep the change." Her voice gentled further, became almost maternal. "Give me the book. Take him upstairs whole. Let him remember his name, his brother, his life. Let him wake tomorrow and taste nothing but breakfast. And you—" the smile again, soft as banked coals, "you go back to your stall. Back to your knots and your fevers and your lovely invisible years. Everyone walks away. Isn't that mercy? Isn't that *kindness*?"

It was. That was the poison of it—it was genuinely kind. Every word true, every promise plausible, every alternative worse. Give up the book and Aldous lives whole. Keep the book and he stays a hollowed man chasing ash-tastes through his dreams forever, and the fire dies, and the things beneath the seals wake starving, and none of it—*none of it*—would be because I was selfish. It would be because I chose a book over a man.

My hand was already on the satchel strap.

I want to be honest, since honesty is the one currency I have left: I undid the knot. My fingers worked it the way they'd worked ten thousand charm-knots, quick and practiced, and I had the oilcloth folded back and the worn leather cover of *The Ashen Hours* in my hands before I even understood I'd moved.

The book was warm. It was always warm. It pulsed against my palms like a second heartbeat, and the prophecy pages rustled though there was no wind, and somewhere in their ink Corin's face waited beside my own, and the last unwritten line waited after both.

"One condition," I heard myself say.

Mavrosa's eyebrows arched, pleased. "Name it."

"He remembers choosing. When you give it back—he remembers that I gave you the book for him. He remembers all of it."

Something flickered across the queen's face—surprise, maybe, or the first crack of genuine feeling I'd seen in her. "You want him to carry the weight of what you traded?"

"I want him to know he was worth trading."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment—I believed she might actually laugh, or weep, or do anything human. Instead she extended the bottle toward Aldous with ceremonial slowness, and he rose from his chair like a sleepwalker, eyes fixed on his own soul, tears cutting clean lines down his face—

And Seraiva stood up and threw her teacup at the wall.

The porcelain shattered. The red tea—it was not tea—hissed where it struck, and ate a smoking hole through a tapestry of woven memories, and every glass guard in the hall snapped toward the sound at once, and in that single heartbeat of chaos Mavrosa's hand closed around the bottle and her expression curdled from mercy into fury.

"You *drowned cow*," she snarled, and the temperature of the hall dropped thirty degrees. "Two hundred years and you're still ruining my negotiations."

"They were never negotiations." Seraiva stepped in front of me, arms spread, gown smoking where the spilled tea had splashed it. "They were a purchase. And she was about to sell the only weapon in this mountain that isn't yours."

"*Weapon?*" Mavrosa laughed, high and sharp. "It's a book of poems, sister. Old verses and moth-eaten prophecy. What could a hedge-mouse possibly—"

The book flared in my hands.

I hadn't done it. I want that on the record, whatever record survives down here—I hadn't cast, hadn't sung, hadn't done anything but stand there clutching a decision I already regretted. But the ember-veins in the floor brightened, and the distant pulse of the dragon-fire stuttered like a startled heart, and every bottle on every shelf in the cavern beyond sang one clear note, and Mavrosa's head snapped toward the throne room with an expression I recognized from the mirror, once, twenty years ago, on the night I ran from Thornhollow:

Fear.

"Guards," she said quietly. "Take them to the cellar. Separately." Her eyes came back to mine, and the fury in them had re-frozen into something far more patient. "Especially the witch. Put her somewhere she can think about what she almost gave away. And put the book somewhere *I* can think about it too."

Glass hands closed on my arms.

They dragged me past Aldous, and he fought the guards holding him, shouting my name, shouting *don't*, though I couldn't tell anymore whether he meant don't trade the book or don't fight or simply don't leave me. Our fingers caught for half a second and slid apart.

The last thing I saw before they pulled me down the cellar stair was Mavrosa standing alone in the ruined hall, turning *The Ashen Hours* over in her hands, reading it the way one reads a rival's letter—with hunger, and growing, dawning disbelief.

Then the door shut, the torches died, and the dark of the cellar closed over me like cold water—thick with the smell of glass and wax and, underneath it all, faintly, unmistakably, the sound of hundreds of small lights whispering behind their stoppers.

One of them, I realized as they pushed me into a cell, was calling a name.

Not mine.

*Aldous.*
