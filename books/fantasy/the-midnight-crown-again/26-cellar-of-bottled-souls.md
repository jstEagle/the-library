## 26. Cellar of Bottled Souls

The cell was not what Ulyana had expected, and she had expected very little good.

She had braced herself for a dungeon proper—dripping stone, iron rings, the theatrical misery that tyrants were supposed to provide. Instead, Mavrosa's people had put her in a room that could only be described as *tasteful*. Smooth walls of dark basalt, warmed from somewhere below by the dragon-fire's dying breath. A low bed with actual linens. A shelf. A carafe of water that tasted faintly of honey and iron.

It was a very comfortable prison, which was its own kind of insult. The court beneath the mountain had been stealing souls for a thousand years; apparently they'd gotten quite good at hospitality too.

"They're fattening you," Seraiva had said before the guards—pale, courteous, faintly apologetic things in gray silk—had separated them. The exiled queen had smiled her beautiful, terrible smile. "Don't eat the honeyed things past midnight, witch. And don't sign anything."

Then they'd taken Corin away, and Ulyana had been alone with a carafe, a shelf, and two days of silence.

*Two days.* Or she thought it was two days. There was no window. Time down here moved like syrup poured uphill. She marked it the only way she knew how: she braided. She'd pulled threads from the hem of her blanket—coarse wool, but wool—and knotted them into luck-charms out of pure habit, laying them on the shelf in a row like tiny hostages to hope. Seven knots for seven days. If she ran out of blanket, she'd start on the pillowcase.

She was knotting the ninth when she decided she was done being polite.

* * *

The door had no lock she could see, but it had hinges, and hinges were just joints, and joints could be coaxed. Mother Ysolde's song-charm was still sewn into the lining of her sleeve—a small braid of thread and salt-iron that hummed against her wrist when she pressed it there. It wouldn't open doors. It did something better: it made her *uninteresting*.

Ulyana pressed her thumb to the charm, breathed the first three notes of Ysolde's walking-song—softly, so softly, more felt than heard—and opened the door.

The guard outside was a tall gray figure with a face like a candle left burning in an empty room. It looked directly at her. Its eyes slid off. It looked at the wall instead, frowned at nothing, and resumed whatever passed for sentry duty among people who no longer needed to blink.

Twenty years of practice. Twenty years of being overlooked, of being the woman behind the stall, the one whose name customers never asked. She had hated that skill with her whole heart for two decades. Tonight, for the first time, she blessed every tedious, invisible hour of it.

She walked out of her cell the way one walks through a market crowd: unhurried, unremarkable, already forgotten.

* * *

The corridors of the court were carved from the mountain's own bone, and everything in them was pale—the walls, the floors, the courtiers drifting through them in their gray silks. Pale hands held pale goblets. Pale mouths laughed at jokes Ulyana couldn't hear, because sound down here traveled strangely, arriving half a heartbeat late, as if the mountain were thinking about whether to repeat it.

And everywhere, everywhere, the jars.

They lined the corridors in niches cut into the rock, hundreds of them, thousands—glass vessels the size of wine bottles, each glowing faintly with a light that wasn't quite light. Some glowed gold. Some blue. One near the stairwell pulsed a sickly green, and when Ulyana passed it, she heard—late, half-swallowed—a child laughing.

Her stomach turned over. She kept walking. Unhurried. Unremarkable.

She found the cellar by following the cold.

Everything in the court was warm with borrowed fire except one downward passage where the air turned sharp and clean, like the first breath after snow. The stairs spiraled down through rock that grew older as she descended—carved stone giving way to raw granite giving way to something glassy and black, older than the city above, older maybe than the Magistrate's whole shining kingdom.

At the bottom: a vaulted cellar, long as a church, and colder than either.

The racks went back farther than she could see. And the jars here were different—not trinkets, not the petty bottled lives of the corridors above. These glowed steadily, deeply, each one holding what looked like a slow golden storm. Whole souls. Complete ones. Labeled in a fine spidery hand on tags of vellum.

Ulyana told herself she was looking for anything useful—leverage, exits, weaknesses. That was a lie, and some honest corner of her knew it within ten steps. She was looking for *him*. For the hollow place she'd felt the first night he ordered tea and asked for a charm against forgetting. Somewhere in this cold, orderly horror was the piece of Corin that had been taken before he drew his first breath, and if it existed anywhere in this mountain, it would be here, filed away like tax records.

She moved down the rows, reading labels.

*Maren of the Fen Houses, sold in the year of the Third Frost.*

*Aldric Thorne, purchased from his father, price: three winters without plague.*

*The Miller's Daughter, unnamed at sale. See also: regret of the seller, archived separately.*

That last one made her stop. *Regret of the seller, archived separately.* The court kept even remorse on its shelves. She wondered which rack held hers—the regret of a mother who'd signed away a son before he was born, trading his soul for a debt. Somewhere in these rows was the paperwork of a family's ruin, catalogued and dusted by patient gray hands.

Keep moving. Keep reading.

*Ilse Vane,* said a tag, and her blood went to ice water.

She snatched the jar off the rack before her fear could catch up with her hands. The soul inside burned white-gold, fierce and bright, and the label beneath the name read:

*Aldous Vane. Sold at birth by next of kin. Price: continuance of the Vane line. Purchased for the Court Reserve. Note: unusually vigorous. Recommend retention.*

*Aldous Vane.*

Not Corin. Not Corin at all.

Ulyana stood in the cold with a stolen soul cradled in both arms and felt the world quietly rearrange itself around her.

Vane. Sergeant Ilse Vane, who had hunted them across the ash country, who had cornered them at the river crossing with a Warden's blade and a tracker's patience, who had been disgraced for letting a hedge-witch slip through her fingers. Ilse Vane, whose face Ulyana had spent weeks learning to hate.

Whose brother was sleeping in her arms in a jar.

"Oh," Ulyana said aloud, to nobody, in the smallest voice she owned. "Oh, that's—that's *awful*."

Because it wasn't just awful in the ordinary way. It was awful in the way that explained everything. A tracker who hunted supernatural answers the way other people hunted game. A woman who'd stared at Corin across the river with an expression Ulyana had taken for hatred and now recognized, with sickening clarity, as recognition. Ilse had looked at Corin's hollow face and seen a shape she knew. Maybe she hadn't known why. Maybe she'd spent her whole life feeling a missing piece and never once been shown the shelf it sat on.

And the prophecy. *The man whose soul was sold to the court beneath the mountain before he was born.* She'd read those words a hundred times since Thornhollow. They had never once mentioned that the seller might still be alive. Still hunting him. Still—stars help her—possibly looking for him, all these years, without knowing what she was looking *for*.

The stakes had detonated, right on schedule, exactly the way her life worked: not with a villain's cackle but with a label in fine spidery handwriting.

"Unusually vigorous," she read again, and despite everything, despite the cold and the dread, a wet laugh escaped her. "Yes. He is, rather. Stubborn as a mule with opinions about tea."

The jar was warm in her hands. Warmer than the cellar had any right to allow. When she tilted it, the golden storm inside swirled toward her fingers, pressing against the glass like a cat leaning into a familiar hand.

She felt it notice her.

There was no other word for it. Something in that light became aware of her—of Ulyana Pemberton, unbound witch, twenty years hidden—and leaned closer, and a warmth spread up her arms that had nothing to do with magic and everything to do with the specific, unmistakable feeling of being *known*.

"You're him," she whispered. "You're really him. All of him."

The light flared, soft and gold, and for one impossible moment she smelled woodsmoke and rain on hot stone—the night market, the first night, the stranger who ordered tea. Memory without memory. Recognition without a mind to recognize with.

Somewhere above, in a tasteful cell, a body was waking each night tasting ash. And here was the reason. Here was everything missing, patient as a seed, waiting out a nineteen-year winter in a bottle while its owner walked around with a hole shaped exactly like this.

"I'm going to get you out," Ulyana told the jar. Her voice cracked in the middle, and she let it. "I don't know how yet. I'm going to steal you out of the most heavily guarded cellar in creation, reunite you with a man who doesn't know his own name, and then somehow explain to a Warden sergeant that I've been carrying her brother around like a bottle of plum wine." She wiped her nose on her sleeve. "I want it noted that I never wanted adventure. I wanted a stall. I wanted Tuesdays."

The soul pulsed warmly, which she chose to interpret as sympathy, though it might equally have been amusement.

* * *

Getting back up was harder than getting down.

The song-charm's notes were fraying—Ysolde's braids weren't made for repeated use, and Ulyana could feel the salt-iron growing warm and thin against her wrist, a candle burning at both ends. Halfway up the spiral stair, a pair of gray courtiers drifted past her going down, and one of them paused. Turned its head. Frowned at the space beside her with the expression of someone who has lost a word mid-sentence.

Ulyana held perfectly still and thought *unremarkable* thoughts with the desperation of a woman praying. *I am nobody. I am the woman behind the stall. I am the seventh luck-knot on a shelf of nine. You have never seen me. You will not remember seeing me. Please, please have a short attention span.*

The courtier's frown deepened. Then its companion touched its arm—some pale courtesy—and they continued down into the cold, and Ulyana exhaled so hard she nearly dropped the jar.

She made it back to her cell. She laid the jar on the shelf, between the luck-knots and the carafe, where it glowed like a captive sunrise.

Then she sat on the edge of the bed and shook, quietly, for a long time.

* * *

She talked to it. She wasn't ashamed—or rather, she was ashamed, and did it anyway, because the alternative was silence, and silence in this mountain curdled fast.

"So here's the situation," she said, sitting cross-legged on the floor, chin on her fists, addressing the jar like a stall customer who couldn't answer. "Your sister thinks you're dead. Or worse—she probably doesn't think about you at all, because thinking about you would be unbearable, and people are marvelous at not thinking about unbearable things. I know. I was one for twenty years."

The light swirled slowly, listening, or doing whatever light does instead.

"She sold you. Before you were born. To save the rest of your family, presumably—which doesn't forgive it, but it *explains* it, and explanation is where forgiveness usually has to start, in my limited experience." She picked at a loose thread on her knee. "My experience is limited because I ran away from my family instead of bargaining with devils, and I've spent two decades telling myself that made me the brave one. But Brenna stayed, and Brenna fought from inside, and Brenna is High Witch now while I'm sitting in a cellar talking to a bottle." A pause. "So maybe neither of us gets the medal."

The jar flared gently. Comforting. Or possibly bored. Souls probably didn't have much use for a witch's midlife crisis.

"And here's the part I can't say to anyone else," Ulyana went on, quieter. "When I find him—when I give you back—I won't get him. Do you understand? The man I know is built around the hole where you should be. Every odd thing about him, every gentleness and strangeness, grew in that empty space like flowers through a cracked wall. And when you go back in..." She swallowed. "Corin goes away. The man who asked me for a charm against forgetting—he'll be forgotten. By himself. Possibly by everyone. Maybe by me."

The light in the jar dimmed a little, the way a fire dims when someone speaks a hard truth beside it.

"But he'll be *whole*," she said firmly, because she refused to cry in front of a bottle, or rather, because she'd already cried once tonight and had a reputation to maintain. "Whole beats happy. I've believed that since I was sixteen years old and too stupid to live by it." She reached out and rested two fingers on the glass. "So we're agreed. Both of us. We get you home."

The warmth that answered spread all the way to her elbows, and she sat there a long while, in the dark of a stolen cell, keeping watch over a sunrise in a jar.

* * *

On what she judged was the third day—three knots unpicked, the braid unraveling under her fingernails—they came for her.

Not the polite guards. Gray silk, yes, but these wore silver chains across their chests, and they moved with the terrible smoothness of things that had practiced being graceful for centuries. They did not look past her this time. They looked *at* her, and their gaze stuck, and Ulyana understood that somewhere upstairs, someone had finally remembered she existed.

"Lady Pemberton," said the foremost, bowing with exquisite irony. "The Queen of Teeth requests the pleasure of your company. She wishes to discuss... terms."

"Terms," Ulyana repeated. Her eyes flicked—just once, she hoped invisibly—to the shelf.

The courtier followed her glance. Of course it did. It smiled, showing teeth that were slightly too regular, like piano keys.

"Yes," it said. "Terms. You may bring your... keepsake."

Her heart stopped, stumbled, restarted. *It knows.* Of course it knew. Nothing in this mountain was unwatched; she'd walked through its veins believing herself invisible, and perhaps she had been—but invisible things still cast shadows, and the court had a thousand years of practice reading shadows.

The courtier gestured toward the corridor, gracious as a host offering wine.

"After you," it said. "Do hurry. Her Majesty dislikes waiting almost as much as she dislikes losing things."

Ulyana lifted the jar from the shelf and held it against her chest, and the soul inside burned steady and bright against her ribs like a second heartbeat, like an answer, like a promise being kept in advance.

She walked out of the cell with her chin up, a witch carrying a stolen name through the halls of the thing that stole it, toward a throne of teeth and whatever mercy dressed as temptation was waiting there.

Behind her, far above, through miles of stone, she could almost hear the sound of boots on a road—a sergeant riding hard toward a mountain, chasing answers to a question she'd carried her whole life without knowing she was asking it.

But that was a story for tomorrow. Tonight, Ulyana Pemberton had a delivery to make.
