## 27. What Ilse Lost

The winter Ilse Vane turned eleven, her mother taught her the difference between a debt and a wound.

"A debt," Maren Vane said, counting coins into a tin that had once held throat lozenges and now held the family's entire future, "is something you can pay back. A wound is something you carry." She said it in the flat voice she used for arithmetic, which was how Ilse knew it was bad. Her mother only used the flat voice when the numbers had stopped making sense.

Their father had been a Warden before them — Third Cohort, records division, a man who could find any name in any ledger in Cinderhollow if you gave him an hour and a lamp. That was the trouble. He had found a name he wasn't supposed to find, a name attached to a shipping manifest that shouldn't have existed, and three weeks later he was dismissed without pension for "clerical irregularities," and six weeks after that the Magistrate's auditors came to the house on Tanner's Row with papers that said the Vanes owed nine years of back-taxes on income their father had never earned, in a business he had never owned, and would someone please explain the discrepancy.

There was no explaining a discrepancy like that. Ilse understood, even at eleven, that the discrepancy *was* the explanation. Someone upstairs wanted the Vane family gone, or broken, or both, and paperwork was simply the polite way of doing it.

* * *

"You can't sell what isn't yours to sell," Ilse said. She was eleven and knew everything.

Her mother didn't look up from the tin. "No. But you can sell what they tell you is yours."

The broker came on the last night of the year, because of course he did; the courts beneath the mountain liked thresholds, liked endings, liked the exact moment when one thing became another. He arrived through no door at all, simply was suddenly standing in the kitchen where a draft had been a heartbeat earlier, and he was pale as paper and polite as a funeral, and he set a small glass jar on the table between the salt cellar and the bread.

Inside the jar, something moved. It moved the way light moves under water, the way a sleeping child breathes: slowly, sweetly, without knowing itself.

"What is it?" Ilse whispered.

"Potential," said the broker. "A life not yet lived. The Magistrate has debts of his own, madam, and certain parties below are willing to settle them for — well." He tapped the jar. "For this."

Maren Vane looked at the jar for a long time. Then she looked down the hallway, toward the small room where the crib stood, where her son — eight weeks old, red-faced, furious at everything, named Aldous for her own grandfather — was finally, blessedly asleep.

Ilse watched her mother do the arithmetic. She watched her mother use the flat voice without saying anything at all.

"He'll never know," the broker said gently. "That's the mercy of it. A soul sold before it's worn in leaves no hole behind. No dreams lost. No memories missed. He'll grow up whole as far as he can tell, and the family keeps the house, and the debts die tonight, and everyone lives."

"And him?" Ilse asked. "What happens to him?"

The broker smiled at her the way adults smile at children who have asked precisely the right question and will be punished for it someday. "Clever girl," he said. "Nothing happens to him. Nothing at all. That's rather the point."

Her mother signed. Not with ink — with blood, pressed thumb to page, because the mountain court had never trusted ink, ink being a thing of the surface and the sun. The jar went into the broker's coat. The papers on the table went soft and gray and crumbled like old ash, every debt in the household dissolving into nothing, and outside the window the first snow of the year began to fall, fat and slow and indifferent.

Her father wept that night, quietly, in the washroom, where he thought no one could hear. Ilse heard. She was eleven and she heard everything, and she decided — standing barefoot on cold floorboards, listening to her big brave Warden father cry like a kettle left too long — that she would never be the one who wept. She would be the one who counted. Counting kept the tears in their tin.

* * *

Here is what nobody told the Vane family, and what Ilse spent the next twenty-two years learning one splinter at a time:

The broker had lied about the mercy.

Not entirely. Not cruelly, even. Aldous grew. He ate, he walked, he learned his letters and then his sword-drills, he laughed at the right times in stories and cried at funerals the way other people did. But there was a — Ilse searched for the word for years — a *thinness* to him. A hollowness, like a bell cast slightly wrong, that rang true enough until you put your ear close. He slept badly. He woke some nights tasting ash and couldn't say why. He had no memory before nineteen, none at all, a blank so smooth it frightened doctors and amused priests, and the family learned to build around the gap the way a tree grows around a nail: completely, lovingly, never once mentioning it.

And Ilse remembered the jar.

She remembered it the way you remember a sound — the faint wet shift of something alive inside glass, turning over in its sleep. She was eleven. She had watched her mother trade her brother's soul to save four lives, and the mathematics were unimpeachable, and her mother died seven years later still insisting the sum had been correct, and Ilse had stood at the graveside doing the sums again and getting a different answer every time.

Four lives saved. One soul sold. And the interest — oh, the interest accrued in places ledgers don't reach.

* * *

She joined the Wardens at sixteen, because it was the family trade and because the uniform came with access. Records division first, like her father — she had his gift for names, for the little hooks that connected one ledger line to another. Within two years she had read every file the Magistrate kept on the mountain court, which was to say she had read three pages of official denial and forty years' worth of clerical footnotes where frightened men had written things like *payments rendered below per arrangement* and *see prior manifest (missing)* and once, memorably, in a dead archivist's hand, *the jars are real, I have seen the jars, God forgive us the inventory.*

They burned that archivist. Ilse read the execution order herself. Hedge-witch, the file said, trafficking with subterranean powers. His crime, as far as she could reconstruct it from the margins, had been counting correctly.

So she transferred out of records. Into enforcement. Into the burning trades. Because — and she was honest with herself about this, brutally so, in the way of people who cannot afford illusions — the fastest way to learn what the court beneath the mountain wanted was to catch the people who dealt with it. Every hedge-witch she dragged in had fragments. A charm against soul-sickness. A map to a door that opened inward. A rhyme about a queen deposed, a fire guttering, a debt coming due.

She collected fragments the way other women collected lace. She told herself it was procedure. Procedure, procedure, procedure — the word was a coin she paid herself daily, and some nights it almost covered the cost.

* * *

"You know what they say about you," said Sergeant Brakke, over cards, the winter Ilse made lieutenant. "Vane the Flame. Burns 'em clean. Never smiles, never wavers, never takes a bribe." He laid down his hand and lost, again. "I've seen you read a witch's confession like a grocery list. Doesn't it ever — " he waved his cards vaguely — "get to you?"

Ilse considered lying to him. It was easier, and kinder, and she was good at both.

"It gets to me exactly as much as it should," she said instead, "which is the amount required by regulations."

Brakke laughed and dealt again, and she let him believe that was a joke, and she won the next four hands because Brakke always folded when he thought she was sad, and she needed him not to think she was sad. This was the whole art of Ilse Vane's life, though she'd never have phrased it so plainly: keep the men comfortable, keep the ledgers balanced, keep the jar turning over in its sleep at the center of everything.

Once — only once — a captured hedge-witch, an old woman with flour still under her fingernails from whatever innocent bread she'd been charming, looked up at Ilse from the holding cell and said, without hatred, with something worse than hatred, with recognition:

"They took somebody from you too, didn't they? I can smell it on you. You're not hunting us, girl. You're hunting the taker."

Ilse wrote the woman up as unrepentant. Recommended standard sentence. Signed the order in her flat, beautiful, regulation hand.

Then she went home and drank half a bottle of plum brandy and got out the shoebox under her bed where she kept the fragments — forty scraps of forbidden knowledge arranged with a precision that would have gotten her burned alongside the witches if anyone saw it — and added the old woman's words to the collection, written out in full, because recognition deserved preserving. Because somewhere under the uniform and the brandy and the arithmetic there was still an eleven-year-old girl standing barefoot on cold floorboards, listening to her father cry, promising the darkness in the kitchen: *I will find where you put my brother.*

* * *

The years stacked up like cordwood. Ilse rose. Ilse burned. Ilse built a reputation so clean and so terrible that junior Wardens flinched when she cleared her throat, and she told herself the flinching was respect, and most days she believed it, and the days she didn't she worked later.

She found the broker. Or a broker — the pale ones all wore the same face the way crows wear the same feathers. She cornered one in a wine cellar off Coopersmith Lane with a drawn blade and twenty-two years of questions, and the creature had looked at her with mild, papery amusement and said, "Ah. The sister. We wondered when you'd come asking."

"Where is it," she said. "The jar. My brother's soul."

"Sold onward, long ago. Souls are currency, Lieutenant; currency circulates." The broker examined its nails. "Though I'll tell you a secret, since you've been such a diligent customer of ours over the years, all unknowing. Yours was a notable vintage. An infant of good stock, unspoiled, freely — well. Freely *adjacent.* Such things drift downward. Toward the throne. Toward the hoard. If your brother's soul still exists, it exists *below*, keeping company with the very oldest fires." It smiled. "Would you like to know the name it travels under now?"

"I know his name."

"You know the name you gave him." The broker leaned close, and its breath smelled like the inside of a locked room. "Things that live in jars get given new ones. Inventory requires labels, Lieutenant Vane. You of all people understand labeling."

It stepped backward into a shadow that hadn't been deep enough to hold it and was gone, and Ilse stood alone in the wine cellar among bottles older than her grief, and for the first time in twenty-two years she sat down on the floor of her life and put her head in her hands.

* * *

Afterward — after the brandy, after the long gray dawn, after she'd washed her face and pinned her hair and become Lieutenant Vane again, armor over wound over armor — she made herself a new ledger entry. She was, above all else, a bookkeeping soul; it was the inheritance her father actually left her.

*Asset: one soul, male, infant-vintage, whereabouts below.*
*Liability: one family, ruined to purchase same.*
*Outstanding question: what, precisely, does one burn to balance this account?*

Because that was the joke the universe had been telling for two decades, the joke with teeth: Ilse Vane, terror of hedge-witches, scourge of the small magics, had spent her whole career burning the wrong people. The charm-sellers, the fever-healers, the luck-knot women — they were kindling, and she had known it in her bones for years, and she had burned them anyway because the alternative was admitting the fire should be pointed elsewhere, upward, at the polite men in the Magistracy who signed manifests, or downward, at the pale accountants of souls who filled them. You could not arrest the Magistrate. You could not subpoena the mountain. So she had arrested grandmothers, and called it duty, and the duty had held her together the way a stitch holds a wound — tightly, painfully, and only ever temporarily.

But now the ledger had changed. Now there was a number in the liability column she could actually pursue.

* * *

Word reached her in early spring, in the flat bureaucratic language she loved best: a field report, flagged urgent, regarding a stallholder from the Cinderhollow night market — a charm-seller, quiet, careful, twenty years invisible — who had slipped a Warden cordon at Miller's Crossing using fire-magic of a grade nobody in the lower ranks had seen outside of execution records. Traveling companion: male, mid-thirties, no record, no history, no memory before age nineteen.

Ilse read the description twice.

She read it a third time with her finger resting on the words *no memory before age nineteen*, and the tin-lozenge arithmetic of her childhood turned over in its sleep inside her chest, and something that had been sealed for twenty-six years cracked along its label.

She had hunted the supernatural for half her life on procedure, on duty, on the respectable fiction that she was protecting Cinderhollow from the dark. She had told herself the story so long she'd nearly believed it. But every story has a final verse, and hers had just walked into her jurisdiction wearing her brother's face and traveling beside the one witness who might know where the jars were kept.

Ilse Vane closed the report, straightened her collar, and gave the order that would end her career or redeem it, quite possibly both:

"Saddle the horses. We're going to the mountain — and this time, nobody burns until I say so."
