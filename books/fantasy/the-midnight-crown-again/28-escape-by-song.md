## 28. Escape by Song

The song-charm was a small thing—a flat disc of river-clay no bigger than a coin, pressed with Mother Ysolde's thumbprint and wrapped in waxed thread. Ulyana had carried it against her sternum for three days, tucked into the binding of her shift, feeling it warm and cool with her breath like a second small heart.

*When they take you,* Ysolde had said, pressing it into her palm in the last unguarded minute before the court's guards swept them apart, *sing the first line of the lullaby I taught you. Not aloud. The charm will hear what your throat wants to say. It will make the walls forget you are there.*

*Walls can forget?*

*Child,* Ysolde had said, with the terrible patience of a woman who had buried three coven-masters and outlived two Magistrates, *everything forgets. That is how prisons work. They only hold what insists on being remembered.*

So now, in the dark of her cell beneath the Court of Teeth, Ulyana lay on cold stone that smelled of copper and old smoke, and she did not insist on anything at all.

She sang silently. The lullaby was four notes long—Ysolde had never believed in complicating a spell—and as she breathed them out through the clay disc, she felt something loosen in the room, like a knot slipping in wet rope. The cell didn't open. The cell simply became less certain of itself. Somewhere above her, she heard a guard's footsteps falter, slow, and continue on past her door without the little hitch of attention a passing jailer always gave a prisoner. She was becoming background noise. She was becoming the hum of the mountain itself.

Ulyana had spent twenty years practicing this. Being overlooked was a craft, and she had been its finest artisan in Cinderhollow. She knew exactly how to be nothing: the downcast eyes, the merchant's patter, the luck-knots sold cheap so no one looked too closely at the knots' true knots underneath. She had built a whole life on the principle that the safest place in a burning city is inside someone else's indifference.

She had never expected to need the skill here, in the dark, with her hands shaking.

Because the truth—the truth she'd been carrying since Mavrosa's guards had taken them in the throne hall, since Corin had been dragged one way and she another, his eyes finding hers over a pale courtier's shoulder with an expression she would spend the rest of her life trying to describe—was that this escape was not meant to save everyone.

It was meant to save almost no one.

She had worked it out during the first night, lying awake in the copper-smelling dark, doing arithmetic she hated. Seraiva was in a cell somewhere near—she'd heard the deposed queen's voice through stone, low and melodic, singing to herself in a language Ulyana half-remembered from childhood lessons about the mountain court's old days. If anyone could reopen the way out, could unseal passages that Mavrosa had locked behind her own claim, it was the queen who had once ruled these halls.

Corin was not near. Corin was *valuable*. Mavrosa kept valuable things close to her body, the way a miser keeps coins in a boot. Which meant Corin was up, in the living heart of the court, behind whatever doors even Ysolde's forgetting-song could not make indifferent.

And Ulyana had one charm. One song. One window of maybe a hundred breaths before the walls remembered themselves and every guard in the mountain remembered her too.

One charm could carry two people out slowly or one person out quickly. It could not carry three.

She turned the arithmetic over and over those first two nights, looking for a kinder sum, and there wasn't one. There was never one. That was the lesson of her whole life, written again in a new hand: safety was a thing you bought, and the currency was always somebody.

The difference, this time, was that she intended to come back for him.

* * *

On the third night—or what she guessed was night; the court kept no honest hours, only a dim amber gloom that seeped through the lava-tunnels like light through honey—she heard Seraiva stop singing.

That frightened her more than shouting would have. A queen who sang to keep her courage up, going quiet, was a queen running out of courage.

"Seraiva," Ulyana whispered to the wall. Sound traveled strangely down here; the tunnels were throats, and throats carry voices. "Deposed Queen of the Mountain Court. Formerly radiant. Currently imprisoned. Can you hear me?"

A pause. Then, dry as old paper: "Formerly radiant. You flatterer. Who is back there?"

"Ulyana Pemberton. Hedge-witch. Unbound." She hesitated, then added the word that mattered most in this place: "Free."

Another pause, longer. When Seraiva spoke again, the melody had gone out of her voice entirely, replaced by something sharp and hungry. "Unbound. Do you know what that makes you down here, little witch?"

"Trespassing."

"It makes you the only creature in this mountain who came in without owing anything." Ulyana heard the scrape of movement, a body shifting against stone. "Mavrosa cannot compel you. Her claim runs along debts, along bindings, along souls that were signed away. You are a hole in her ledger. How did you get a charm past her searchers?"

"A very old woman with very steady thumbs."

"Ysolde." Something that might have been laughter, quickly strangled. "Of course. That woman has been smuggling songs into this mountain since before I wore the crown. Listen to me, Pemberton. My cell is sealed by my own abdication-oath—Mavrosa bound me with my own word, which is the cruelest lock there is. But oaths have seams. And if you are truly free, truly unclaimed, then when you touch the seal, it will have to reckon with you. It will have to ask whether you count."

"Do I count?"

"You are standing in a prison talking to a wall," Seraiva said. "You tell me."

Ulyana closed her eyes. Pressed her palm flat to the stone between their cells, where the wall sounded thinnest. She sang the lullaby's four notes into the charm, and then—because Ysolde had also taught her, years ago, in a kitchen smelling of bread and herbs, that magic was mostly just paying very close attention—she listened.

The seal was there under her fingers like a scar in the stone, a ridge of cold that hadn't been cold anywhere else. And when her unclaimed skin touched it, she felt the strangest sensation of her life: the mountain pausing. Considering her. A vast attention turning over, finding no debt attached to her name, no signature, no chain—and moving on, baffled, the way a clerk moves past a page left blank.

The ridge softened. Crumbled. On the other side of the wall, stone ground against stone, and Seraiva stepped through a gap that hadn't existed a moment before, blinking in the amber gloom like a woman emerging from deep water.

Up close, the exiled queen was smaller than Ulyana had imagined, and more worn. Her beauty was still there, but it had the look of furniture in an empty house—fine, and unused. Her eyes went straight to Ulyana's face, and something flickered across them that was not gratitude.

"The witch who fled Thornhollow," Seraiva said softly. "Brenna's letters mentioned you. The one who wouldn't sign."

"That's me. Professional blank page."

"Mavrosa will want you very badly by morning." Seraiva flexed her fingers, wincing; centuries—no, decades, Ulyana corrected herself—of imprisonment had stiffened even a queen's hands. "We should be leagues away before her ledger remembers it has a hole in it."

"We're getting Corin first."

Seraiva went very still. "The sold soul. Aldous Vane's brother's jar, the one Ilse Vane has been circling this mountain after for twenty years without knowing why." She said it matter-of-factly, but watched Ulyana's face while she said it, cataloguing. "He is in the Queen's Keep. Above us. Behind the Court of Teeth proper. You understand what that means."

"I understand I have one charm and it's already half-spent."

"Then let me say plainly what you are refusing to say." Seraiva's voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. "You cannot reach him. Not tonight. Possibly not ever, from inside. What you can do is get out, get to your coven, get to Vane, get to anyone with an army, and come back. He is leverage, yes—but leverage stays valuable precisely because it isn't spent. Mavrosa needs him alive. His soul is the key to the fire, and the fire is the only thing keeping her kingdom from being crushed under everything it seals. She will not break her own key."

"And if she decides she doesn't need the key anymore because she's found another way?"

"Then we are all dead regardless, and I'd rather die having tried than having stood politely in a cell."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere far above, faint as memory, Ulyana thought she heard the great slow breathing of Ember-that-Waits, the dragon whose hunger held up the world.

"He waited twenty years without knowing what he was waiting for," Ulyana said finally. "I can wait a week. That's the bargain I'm making with myself, and I'd thank you not to argue with it, Your Majesty, because I've heard how queens negotiate and I'm in no state."

Something warmed briefly in Seraiva's worn face. "You'll do," she said. "Come. I know the way out. I built half of it, before my sister put me in a box made of my own promises."

* * *

They went up through service-tunnels that Seraiva navigated by touch, her fingertips reading the stone like a blind woman reads a face. Twice they passed alcoves where bottled things sat in rows—souls, Ulyana realized with a lurch, souls in glass, each one glowing faintly with the color of the life it held. She kept her eyes forward. She couldn't afford to read the labels. Not tonight. Tonight every jar she stopped to mourn was a breath off her charm's budget.

The trouble came at the third junction, where the tunnel opened onto a gallery overlooking the Court of Teeth itself—and there, below, lit by the amber gloom, stood Mavrosa, speaking quietly with a courtier, and beside her, wrists bound in silver cord, hair matted, upright anyway, stubborn as a fencepost:

Corin.

Alive. Whole. Glancing around the hall with the wary, hungry attention of a man trying to memorize exits, and Ulyana's heart broke cleanly in two, one half soaring and one half sinking, because he was right there, thirty feet away, and she had never in her life been so close to anything she could not touch.

He turned. For one impossible moment his gaze climbed the gallery and found hers in the shadows.

He didn't call out. He understood faster than she could have asked him to—he saw her, saw Seraiva, saw the shape of what her presence meant, and he lifted his chin a fraction. Just a fraction. *Go,* the gesture said. *I'm all right. Go.*

Then a courtier followed his glance upward, and the alarm bells of the Court of Teeth were not bells at all but a rising chorus of voices, every pale mouth opening at once—

Seraiva grabbed Ulyana's wrist. "Now or never, witch!"

They ran. Down, and down, and down, through throats of cooling stone, the chorus swelling behind them, and Ulyana sang the lullaby into the charm until her lips cracked, spending the last of Ysolde's gift in great reckless handfuls, making walls forget, making doors grow uncertain, making herself and the deposed queen into rumor, into draft, into nothing—until at last they spilled out through a crack in the mountain's shoulder into real night, real wind, real stars, and collapsed gasping on ash-country scree like two fish thrown from a net.

Above them, the mountain's peak glowed dull red. The fire was guttering. She could feel it even from here, like a heartbeat heard through a dying man's chest.

Behind them, sealed inside, Corin was spending his nights alone in a cage, holding the door shut with his value.

Ulyana knelt in the ash and shook, and did not let herself cry, because crying was insisting on being remembered, and she wasn't done being forgotten yet.

"One week," she whispered to the mountain. To him. "Hold on. I'm coming back for you. I'm bringing everyone."

Beside her, Seraiva rose, brushed ash from her ruined gown, and looked east toward Thornhollow with an expression Ulyana was beginning to recognize as the exiled queen's version of hope—which is to say, arithmetic.

"Then we'd better move fast, little witch," she said. "Because I know my sister. And by the time you've gathered your army—" her voice dropped, grim and quiet, "—she'll already have sent hers."

Far off across the ash country, torchlight bloomed at the edge of the dark. A column of them. Marching toward Cinderhollow.

Marching toward the market.
