## 29. Ember-that-Waits

The tunnels stopped pretending to be tunnels somewhere past the third hour.

Ulyana had been following Seraiva's map through a throat of cooling stone that narrowed and widened like the inside of some enormous sleeping animal—which, she was beginning to suspect, it rather literally was. The walls had gone from black rock to something with a grain to it, striated in slow rings, and when she pressed her palm against them they were not cold. They were warm the way a great dog's flank is warm. Warm the way something alive is warm.

"Corin," she whispered, though whispering felt foolish when there was no one to hear but the mountain itself. "Corin, I think we're walking through its ribs."

He turned back to her, lantern raised, and for a moment his face did the thing it always did now—the flicker, the almost-recognition, as if he were remembering a room he'd only ever dreamed. "I know," he said. "I've known since the last fork. It's why I keep wanting to apologize."

"To whom?"

"I don't know." He smiled, small and crooked. "That's the worst part. The apology is just *there*, sitting in my mouth like a word on the tip of the tongue."

They walked on. The heat grew gentler as they went deeper, which made no sense and yet felt exactly right—like stepping closer to a hearth and finding not scorching but comfort. The air tasted of cedar smoke and old rain and something sweeter underneath, something Ulyana couldn't name until she realized it was the smell of her mother's kitchen, or what she imagined a mother's kitchen would smell like if she'd had one. The dragon's breath, she thought. Even dying, it exhaled home.

The passage opened without warning into a cavern so vast her lantern-light simply gave up, swallowed by darkness the way a shout is swallowed by snowfall.

And there, at the bottom of the world, lay Ember-that-Waits.

* * *

Ulyana had expected terror. She had spent two decades being afraid of things much smaller than a dragon—the Warden's knock, the neighbor's too-long glance, the creak of her own floorboards—and she had assumed that standing before the last dragon in the world would be fear perfected, fear distilled, fear with nowhere left to hide.

Instead she felt grief. Immediate, enormous, personal grief, the kind you feel at a deathbed, because that is precisely where she was standing.

The dragon filled the cavern the way winter fills a valley. Its body was a landscape of dull copper scales, each one broad as a cart-wheel, and along its spine ran ridges that might once have blazed like a festival bonfire but now glowed only faintly, guttering, the way coals glow an hour after the party has gone home. Its wings were folded tight against its sides, tattered at the edges like old banners retired from service. One enormous eye was open—a slit of amber light in the dark—and it watched her descend the slope of broken scale-stone with an expression she could only describe, absurdly, as *polite attention*.

"Well," said Ulyana, because someone had to say something. "Hello."

The eye blinked. The blink took four full seconds and produced a wind that smelled of every hearth fire that had ever been lit in Cinderhollow.

Beside her, Corin made a sound—not words, just a soft exhale, the sound of a man setting down a weight he hadn't known he carried. "It knows me," he said quietly. "Or—it knew Aldous. There's something in its look. Like looking at your grandmother's portrait after everyone who loved her is gone."

"You remember your grandmother?"

"No," he admitted. "But I remember how portraits are supposed to feel. Isn't that strange? All the feelings, none of the facts. That's been my whole life."

Ulyana reached over and took his hand. His fingers were cold; hers weren't much better. They stood together at the edge of a dying god's bedroom and held hands like two children at the ocean's edge, daring each other to go in first.

The dragon moved.

It was not a dramatic movement—nothing so crude as rising. It simply shifted its head, slowly, with the immense courtesy of a very large creature in a very small-feeling space, until its snout rested on the stones perhaps twenty feet from where they stood. This close, Ulyana could see the truth of it written plainly across that ancient face: *starvation*. Not the dramatic starvation of famine illustrations, but the long, patient, dignified starvation of something that had decided complaining was beneath it. The scales around its jaw had gone grey. The nostrils, vast as cellar doors, barely moved with each breath.

"You're hungry," Ulyana said. It wasn't a question. She didn't know how she knew, except that she knew the way you know a stranger is crying in the next room—not by seeing, but by the shape of the silence.

The dragon's eye brightened by a degree. When it spoke, its voice did not boom. It arrived the way warmth arrives through a wall on a cold day—from all directions at once, already inside you before you noticed it coming.

*Little witch. You are not what I expected.*

"What did you expect?"

*A soldier. A queen. A thief with a clever plan.* The amber eye regarded her, and there—in the depths of it—was something that startled a laugh out of her, a laugh that came out wobbly and surprised. Humor. Dry, exhausted humor, the humor of someone who had been keeping watch so long that watching had become the whole personality. *You look like a woman who sells luck-knots and apologizes to furniture when she bumps into it.*

"That's—" She flushed. "That's accurate, actually."

*I have been alone with my thoughts for three hundred years,* the dragon said. *One learns to be observant. And to find the small funny things, or go mad from the large sad ones.*

Corin stepped forward. "Ember-that-Waits," he said, and his voice shook. "My name—I think my name is Aldous Vane. I think my soul was sold here before I was born. Do you—" He stopped. Started again. "Do you know me?"

The great head tilted. For a long moment there was no answer but breath, the slow bellows-sound of failing fire.

*I knew your soul when it arrived,* the dragon said at last. *It was very small and very loud. They all are, at first. New souls scream like kittens.* Something that might have been fondness moved behind the amber. *I held it a while, little Aldous. Before they bottled it. That is my work, you understand. Or it was. I am the bank where they deposit. I am the vault. Every soul this court has stolen in five hundred years passed through me first—warmed in my fire, pressed flat as coins, made portable. And then I gave them back out again, one by one, to keep myself burning.*

Ulyana's stomach dropped. "You *eat* them."

*I burn them,* said the dragon, with terrible gentleness. *There is a difference, though I understand how it may not seem so from where you stand. A soul burns longer than wood. Cleaner than coal. And the seals beneath me—the starving things your Magistrate's grandfathers never learned about, because the queens kept the ledger secret—they require fire. Not metaphorical fire. Not courage or love or any of the pretty words. Actual fire, unceasing, for as long as the mountains stand. So the queens bring me souls, and I burn them, and the seals hold, and the city above sleeps soundly and burns hedge-witches in the square because it cannot bear the thought that anything real might be living under its floorboards.*

The silence afterward was enormous.

Ulyana sat down. Her legs simply declined to continue participating, and she found herself seated on a warm stone with her knees drawn up, staring at a creature older than the city, older than the Magistracy, older probably than the concept of being tired.

"The Wardens," she said slowly. "The burnings. The raids. Twenty years of women like me hiding in cellars—"

*Are candles arguing about whose light is legal,* said the dragon, *while the furnace below runs on stolen lives.* It did not say it cruelly. That was somehow worse. *I do not excuse the queens. Mavrosa enjoys her work; I have heard her laugh down the centuries and wished my ears could close. But understand what you are walking into, little witch, so that you may hate the right things accurately.*

"And you?" Corin asked. His voice was very quiet. "Do you enjoy yours?"

The eye closed entirely. When it opened again, it was wet.

*No,* said Ember-that-Waits. *I am so tired, Aldous Vane. I have burned ten thousand souls and every single one I felt. Do you know what it is to be a hearth? Everyone loves the hearth. Everyone comes to the hearth. No one asks the hearth whether it wants to burn today. Five hundred years I have held up this ceiling, and I am starving anyway, and the queens keep bringing me less and demanding more, and I can feel the seals thinning like ice on a spring pond, and above me a million people sleep, and not one of them—not the Magistrate, not the queens, not the gods if any still bother to listen—has ever once asked me how I fare.*

Ulyana discovered she was crying.

She wiped her face angrily with her sleeve, the way she did everything—as though the emotion were slightly embarrassing and best handled discreetly—but the tears kept coming, and eventually she gave up and let them. Two decades of hiding, of smallness, of making herself a creature no one looked twice at, and here at the bottom of the world was the largest, most visible thing that had ever lived, and it was *invisible*, utterly invisible, carrying everything while everyone above argued about candle-spells.

"I see you," she said. It came out thick and stupid and completely sincere. "For whatever that's worth. I see you."

The dragon was silent a long moment.

*That,* it said finally, and its voice had gone rough as banked coals, *is worth more than you know. Three hundred years, and you are the first to ask how I fare. I shall treasure the moment. I will put it somewhere safe in myself, where the hunger hasn't gotten to yet.*

Corin laughed wetly. "It flirts."

*I am dying, not dead,* said Ember-that-Waits with dignity. *There remains time for manners.*

* * *

They stayed. Neither of them suggested leaving; the suggestion would have been obscene, like suggesting you leave a funeral early because the biscuits were running low.

Ulyana climbed down onto the dragon's foreleg—it was like climbing onto a hillside that tolerated her—and walked, absurdly, up to the great shoulder, where the scales were warmest and the heartbeat came through the stone-flesh like a slow drum in another room. Corin followed. They sat together in the curve between neck and wing, two small warm-blooded creatures nestled against a mountain that breathed.

"How long?" Ulyana asked, eventually. "How long do you have?"

*If nothing changes? A season. Less, if the queens grow frightened and start feeding me dregs.* The amber eye rolled upward, regarding her from below. *You carry the book. The Ashen Hours. I can smell the prophecy on you like smoke in wool. Shall I tell you what it says, or would you prefer the pleasure of deciphering it yourself?*

"It says a sold soul returns home," Ulyana said. "To rekindle the fire."

*Mmm. Prophecies are like recipes—accurate, but only if you already know how to cook.* The dragon's breath stirred her hair. *A sold soul returning will indeed rekindle me. Briefly. A week of strong flame, perhaps two. Then the hunger resumes, worse, because hope burns hot and fast. Your friend's return is a log thrown on a dying fire. Welcome. Necessary. Not salvation.*

Ulyana's chest tightened. "Then what—"

*Ah-ah.* Something moved through the dragon's voice that was almost, almost playful. *I am an old creature, little witch, and I have learned that the answer to "then what" is never survivable all at once. It must be taken in doses. Let me give you instead the part you came for.*

The warmth beneath her deepened. Not painfully—more like sunlight finding skin—and suddenly Ulyana was seeing.

She saw the court as the dragon saw it: five hundred years compressed into a single long look. Queens in masks descending with their bottles. Pale courtiers trading years like spices. The first queen, weary and honest, weeping as she made the first bargain: *Burn these, great one, and hold the door shut, and I will make you a crown of ember-light every midwinter.* The second queen, less honest. The third, cruel. Mavrosa, clever and hollow, feeding the vault with one hand and skimming it with the other. And beneath everything, always, the seals—the vast cold lids pressed down over things that scratched, patiently, endlessly, the way water scratches at a dam. She saw the starving things for one horrible half-second: shapes like unfinished thoughts, mouths like regrets, patient as arithmetic.

She gasped and came back to herself, gripping Corin's arm hard enough to bruise.

"Now you know," the dragon said softly. "The whole ugly machine. Souls in, fire out, seals held. And every person who ever profited from the arrangement told themselves a story about necessity. Even the kind ones. *Especially* the kind ones—the kind ones are how the machine stays oiled. Cruelty makes people rebel. Kindness makes them compliant."

"So what do we do?" Corin whispered. "If freeing me isn't enough—if it's all going to gutter out anyway—"

*That,* said Ember-that-Waits, *is the "then what" I mentioned. And I confess, little Aldous, that I do not fully know. But I know this much:* the great head turned, and both eyes now fixed on Ulyana with an intensity that made her spine prickle, *the prophecy chose you both, and prophecies are lazy creatures. They point at the likeliest shape and call it fate. Which means the true answer is likely to come not from the shape they pointed at, but from whatever that shape decides to do that no one predicted.*

"I don't follow."

*Good. Following is for courtiers.* The dragon settled deeper into its hoard, and Ulyana heard the clink of it—thousands upon thousands of spent years, ground fine as sand, the ash of every soul ever burned. *Rest now. Eat, if you brought food—I can smell bread, and I am jealous of you in a way I haven't been jealous of anything in a century. Sleep in the warm. Tomorrow the mountain gets difficult, and Mavrosa will have felt your arrival by now. The queen of teeth does not tolerate uninvited guests. She tolerates nothing. It is her single accomplishment.*

Ulyana laughed despite herself—a real laugh, cracked and surprised. "You really have been alone too long."

*Three hundred years of material,* said the dragon, closing its eye. *Every joke unrehearsed. Pity the first audience.*

They made camp in the shelter of its jaw, absurd and tender, sharing bread and watered wine while the last dragon of the world breathed slow fire-dreams around them. And sometime in the deepest hour of the night, Ulyana woke to find the amber eye watching her—not hungrily, not hopefully, just *watching*, the way you watch something you're trying to memorize before it's taken away.

"What is it?" she murmured.

*Nothing,* said Ember-that-Waits. *Only—when you go up there tomorrow, and Mavrosa offers you her bargains, and the fear comes, and the clever survival-instinct that kept you hidden for twenty years whispers its excellent advice—remember that I asked how you fare, and you answered. Remember that being seen did not kill you. It is the only weapon I can give you, little witch. But it is the one that matters.*

Far above them, muffled by miles of stone, something began to ring—deep and rhythmic, the sound of the mountain court waking, of bells made from bone and brass, of a queen who had felt two uninvited hearts enter her house.

Ember-that-Waits sighed, a wind of cedar and sorrow.

*Ah. There she goes. Dressing for guests.* The amber eye gleamed. *Sleep fast, Ulyana Pemberton. Tomorrow, the teeth.*
