## 30. The Deal Refused

The dragon's light followed Ulyana out of the deep gallery like a dog that didn't want to be left.

She felt it on her skin the whole way back through the lava tunnels—warmth without source, a glow that came from inside her own chest now, as though Ember-that-Waits had pressed something small and burning into her hands and trusted her not to drop it. *They are starving anyway,* the dragon had said, in a voice like a hearth remembering itself. *Every queen before Mavrosa fed me, and every queen told herself it was mercy.*

Ulyana climbed toward the throne hall with that warmth and with Seraiva's silence beside her, and by the time the tunnel mouth opened onto the great vaulted dark of the court beneath the mountain, she had almost convinced herself she knew what she was going to do.

Then she saw what Mavrosa had done while she was away, and every plan she'd built turned to ash in her mouth.

* * *

They had set up a table.

That was the first absurdity of it—a table. A long one, black wood veined with red, dragged somehow down into a cavern the size of a cathedral and dressed for company. Silver candlesticks. Two chairs. A tea service, white porcelain painted with tiny gold dragons, steam curling from two cups as though someone had poured them moments ago and known exactly when she would arrive.

Around the table, at a respectful distance, stood the courtiers. Pale as peeled birch, tall as grief, their faces smooth except where mouths were needed. They did not breathe. They watched her the way moths watch a flame they have already burned themselves on once.

And behind them, ranged along the far wall in rows, were the bottles.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Souls in glass, each one glowing its own private color—the blue-green of a fisherman's last summer, the dusty rose of a grandmother's patience, the fierce gold of a child who had never learned to be afraid. They pulsed slowly, all together, like one enormous heart deciding whether to keep beating.

Corin sat at the table.

Alive. Whole-seeming. His hands wrapped around one of the teacups as if someone had placed it there and he hadn't had the will to refuse. When he saw her his whole face changed—not relief exactly, but the thing underneath relief, the thing relief is made from—and he started to rise, and Mavrosa's hand settled on his shoulder like a bird alighting, and he sat back down.

He was wearing different clothes. That was the detail that made Ulyana's stomach turn over. Someone had dressed him. In fine grey wool, well-cut, a silver clasp at the collar. He looked like a gentleman. He looked like a man who had never sold charms out of a night market stall or slept in ditches in the ash country or woken tasting cinders. He looked *arranged*, the way flowers are arranged, the way lies are arranged.

"Ulyana." Mavrosa's voice filled the cavern the way cold fills a cellar. The usurper queen wore a gown the color of deep water over drowned stone, and her crown—if you could call it that—was a circlet of teeth, each one long as a finger, none of them animal. "You've been below. You've seen him." She tilted her head toward the direction of the dragon's gallery. "You've seen what he wants from you."

"He doesn't want," Ulyana said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "That's rather the tragedy of him. He waits."

"Mm." Mavrosa pulled out the second chair. Sat. Folded her hands. Behind her, the bottled souls pulsed, pulsed, pulsed. "Sit, then. Have some tea. I'm told it's terrible. The court has never understood tea—they make it the way they make everything, too much and too long—but the ritual of it is useful. Rituals are how you get people to agree to things without noticing they've agreed."

"I've noticed everything I've agreed to since the day I was born," Ulyana said, and did not sit. "Which is more than most people here can say."

Something flickered across the queen's perfect face. Not anger. Interest. It was worse.

"Seraiva," Mavrosa said pleasantly, "you may go. Or stay. It hardly matters; you're decorative either way. That's why my sister lost her throne, you understand—" this to Ulyana now, conversational, intimate, as though they were old friends catching up over bad tea, "—she thought a crown was about love. Love is what you give people *after* you've secured the borders. First you secure the borders."

Seraiva said nothing. But Ulyana felt the exiled queen go very still beside her, the stillness of a drawn blade pretending to be a stick.

"You've come to offer me a deal," Ulyana said. "Get to it. I'm tired, I'm frightened, and I've recently been emotionally devastated by a dragon, which I recommend no one try."

That startled a sound out of Corin—half laugh, half sob—and Mavrosa's eyes cut to him with something almost tender, which was the most frightening thing Ulyana had seen all day, and she had spent the morning looking into the heart of a dying god.

"Yes," Mavrosa said. "A deal."

* * *

She laid it out the way a jeweler lays out stones—one at a time, letting each catch the light before the next appears.

"The book," she began. "*The Ashen Hours.* Ilse Vane carried word of it to me weeks ago, did you know that? Before your little river adventure. The Magistrate's trackers are thorough people. She described it, and I knew it at once—I was there, in another life, when it was written. So here is the shape of things." She lifted one finger. "Give me the book. All of it, every page, and I will burn it myself in front of you, and with it goes the prophecy, and with the prophecy goes the *necessity*. No sold soul returning home. No fire rekindled by choice. The machine continues as it has continued for four hundred years. Quietly. Efficiently. Nobody above ground ever knows there is a mountain under their feet holding up their sky."

Second finger. "Corin—Aldous—gets his soul back. Today. Whole, restored, memories intact, the ash taste gone from his mornings forever. He walks out of here a complete man, and neither of you owes me anything. I release the claim freely. You have my oath, and whatever you may think of me, my oaths hold. They're load-bearing." A thin smile. "Everything down here is load-bearing."

Third finger. "And you, Ulyana Pemberton. This is the part I want you to listen to."

Mavrosa leaned forward, and for a moment—just a moment—her face did something complicated. Something almost human. Ulyana would think about that moment for a long time afterward, trying to decide whether it had been real.

"Twenty years," the queen said softly. "Twenty years of luck-knots sold as 'lucky knots,' fever-charms passed off as herbalism, wards disguised as embroidery. Twenty years of flinching when boots hit cobblestones. Twenty years of watching other women burn so that you wouldn't." She spread her hands, and the courtiers behind her stirred like reeds. "I am offering you all of it back. Every memory, every kindness, every face you've memorized in the night market—the baker's wife who always overpaid, the boy who bought a courage-charm before his first job interview and never knew what actually got him through it. Your stall. Your street. Your small, safe, invisible life, exactly as it was, exactly as you built it, preserved whole. And more than that: I'll make it *permanent*. No Wardens will ever find Cinderhollow interesting again. No raid will ever sweep your lane. You will be overlooked, blessedly, eternally overlooked, until you die warm in your bed at ninety with your secrets intact and your hands unburned."

She sat back.

"That is the deal. The book for the boy, and the boy for the life. Everyone walks away. The world stays exactly the shape it is—which, I grant you, is an ugly shape, but it is a shape that has held for four centuries, and shapes that hold are worth more than beautiful ones that shatter."

The candles flickered. Somewhere far below, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the dragon-fire guttered.

"And if I refuse?" Ulyana asked.

"Then we proceed as you were planning to proceed," Mavrosa said, pleasant as frost. "You fight your way to the fire, you pour yourself into it, and perhaps it works and perhaps it doesn't, and either way Aldous spends eternity as a jar on my shelf, and your twenty years buy nothing but a few seasons' delay. And I will still have the book eventually, because everyone brings me the book eventually. They always do. Hope makes such excellent delivery service." She picked up her teacup. "But you won't refuse. Sit down, witch. Drink your terrible tea. Let me tell you what you're really being offered."

* * *

And here is the thing Ulyana would carry out of that room like a splinter under the skin:

It was tempting.

Not in the way stories say temptation works—not a devil's whisper, not a seduction. It was tempting the way a warm bed is tempting when you've been walking for days. It was tempting because every single part of it was true.

Her stall. Her market. Marta's bread stall three down, and the way Marta hummed when she kneaded. Old Fenwick who bought a luck-knot every month for his fishing boat and called her "girl" like an insult and meant it like a blessing. The specific quality of Cinderhollow lamplight in autumn, amber through cheap glass. Twenty years of small safeties, and she had mourned them already, had burned her stock and closed her shutters and grieved them properly—and here was a woman offering to undo the grief. To hand it all back. To make the hiding *holy*, protected, permanent.

No more fear. Ever. That was the real merchandise. Not the stall, not the street. The end of fear.

And Corin—Aldous—whole. Sitting right there, breathing, watching her with those hollowed-out eyes that had finally, over these weeks on the road, begun to fill with something. If she took the deal, he'd wake tomorrow knowing his mother's voice, his brother's name—no. Not his brother's. *His* name. Aldous Vane. He'd know why he woke tasting ash. He'd know everything, and he'd be free, and none of it would cost him a thing, because the cost was hers, and the cost was only—

Only everything she'd spent twenty years building.

"Say it," Ulyana said quietly. "Say the part you haven't said yet. There's always a part you haven't said."

Mavrosa's teacup paused halfway to her lips.

"The seals," Ulyana said. "Ember-that-Waits showed me. The fire isn't decoration. It's a lock, and the lock is failing, and your family has been feeding it stolen souls for four hundred years to keep it turning, and it's starving *anyway*, and you know it. You know the machine is dying. You're not offering me a deal to save your kingdom—you're offering me a deal so you don't have to be the queen who was sitting on the throne when it broke. Because if I walk away and take the book, and the fire gutters in a season, and the things under the seals wake up—" she let the sentence hang, let the bottled souls pulse their slow pulse behind her, "—then it happened on your reign. And queens have been deposed for less."

The silence went on so long that one of the courtiers' mouths opened slightly, breathing air it didn't need.

"There it is," Mavrosa said at last, and set down her cup with a small precise click. "Ysolde's student after all. I wondered if any of Thornhollow's lessons had stuck." She rose, and the teeth in her crown caught the candlelight, and she was suddenly very tall and very old in a way the beauty had been hiding. "Yes. The fire is dying. Yes, I know. My mother knew, and her mother knew, and every queen for six generations has poured more souls into the furnace and prayed to outlive the arithmetic. You think I don't lie awake doing sums? You think I don't know that the deal I'm offering you is a bandage on a wound the size of the world?" Her voice dropped. "I am offering you the bandage, witch, because the alternative is the wound, and I have seen the wound. I was *raised* on stories of the wound. The things beneath those seals ate an entire age of the world, and the only reason you've never heard of that age is that there was no one left to write it down."

"So we feed it," Ulyana whispered. "Forever. Bottles and bottles and bottles. Fishermen and grandmothers and children. Forever."

"We feed it," Mavrosa agreed, "until someone finds a better answer, and if no better answer comes, then we feed it until the sun burns out, and we call that victory. That is what ruling *is*, Ulyana. That is the whole secret of every crown ever worn: you choose which horror happens on schedule, and you sleep, or you don't, and either way you choose again tomorrow." She extended her hand across the table, palm up, elegant, patient. "Or you refuse, and you become the horror that happens unscheduled. Choose."

* * *

Ulyana looked at Corin.

He was looking at her hands, not her face—she realized he'd been doing that for a while, tracking her fingers the way sailors track weather. And when she caught his eye, he shook his head. Once. Small. Clear as speech.

*Don't,* he mouthed. *Whatever it costs—it's not worth—*

"It is worth it," Ulyana said aloud, and watched him flinch. "That's the horrible truth of it. It's worth it, Corin. Your whole soul for my twenty hidden years? I'd pay that trade drunk. I'd pay it backwards in a snowstorm. If those were the only terms, I'd sign in blood and thank her for the pen." She turned to Mavrosa. "But those aren't the terms, are they? The terms include the book. The terms include walking away while your machine keeps eating people. The terms include Marta's grandson growing up in a city where the girl next door gets burned alive for a candle-spell, and me selling luck-knots ten streets over and telling myself the market family was safe because *I* was quiet." Her voice cracked and she let it crack; she was done spending strength on smoothing it. "You're not offering me my life back. You're offering me my cage back, gold-plated. And you're right that I chose that cage. For twenty years I chose it every single morning, and I was *good* at choosing it, I was so good at being nobody that even I stopped being able to find me." She laughed, wet and short. "Do you know what the dragon said to me? Down there? The oldest creature in the world, four hundred years of souls in its belly, holding up the sky on a broken wing—and do you know what it said?"

"Enlighten me," Mavrosa said.

"It said it was sorry. It apologized. To *me.* For being hungry." Ulyana wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, furious at the tears, done hiding the fury too. "Even the monster down here has better manners than the monster above. No. Here's my counteroffer, Your Majesty. Keep your tea. Keep your table. Keep your beautiful, load-bearing oath. I am going to walk past you, and I am going to take Aldous Vane's soul-jar off that shelf, and I am going to the fire, and I am going to pay what it actually costs—not the discounted price, not the bandage price, the real one. And when the seals hold—or when they break, if they break—it will not have happened because a woman in a nice dress made a tired witch an offer she was too frightened to refuse."

Mavrosa studied her for a long, long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—and it was the first honest expression Ulyana had seen on her, and it was almost kind, and it was absolutely terrible.

"There it is again," the queen murmured. "That thing Ysolde used to do. That *refusal.* Do you know, I offered your Mother Ysolde a very similar bargain, eighty years ago. She refused too. It's why I respect Thornhollow witches and would exterminate every last one of them without losing sleep." She withdrew her hand. Snapped her fingers, once, and the courtiers moved—and the nearest shelf of bottles swung open on silent hinges, and a dozen pale hands reached for the jars—

"No!" Corin was up, chair crashing, and Ulyana's magic answered before her mind did—twenty years of banked heat roaring up her arms, salt-light spilling off her fingers, and every bottle on that shelf *rang*, one clear bell-note, as if the souls inside had all turned to look.

Everyone froze. Even Mavrosa. Especially Mavrosa.

"Well," the queen said softly, and there was something new in her voice now, something that had not been there before, something that sounded almost like hunger meeting its match. "There she is. Under all that hiding. Hello, Ulyana."

Behind the queen, at the far end of the great hall, the doors boomed—once, twice—struck from the outside by fists in gauntlets, by boots, by a voice Ulyana knew from a river crossing and a burned market and twenty years of nightmares, shouting orders in the flat cadence of the Warden corps.

Ilse Vane had found the mountain.

And she had not come alone.
