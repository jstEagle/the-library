## 31. Choosing to Be Seen

The throne room beneath the mountain was built for echoes.

Every word spoken there came back twice — once in the voice of the speaker and once in the voice of the room itself, a cold silver twin that made even whispers sound like decrees. Ulyana had learned this in the first minute of her capture, when Mavrosa's herald had announced their names and the walls had repeated *Ulyana Pemberton* in a voice like coins dropping down a well. She had flinched at her own name. She realized, standing on the cold mosaic floor with Corin's hand warm in hers, that she had been flinching at it for twenty years.

Mavrosa sat upon the throne of teeth — an actual throne of teeth, which Ulyana had privately decided was the single most insecure piece of furniture she had ever seen, and she had once sold a chair to a man whose house was actively on fire. The teeth were enormous, yellowed with age, set in rows that curved up and around the seated queen like the rib cage of some vanished leviathan. Dragon teeth, probably. Ember-that-Waits's ancestors, perhaps. The courtiers below the dais were pale as candle wax and dressed in gauze the color of drowned things, and they did not blink, which Ulyana found rude.

Mavrosa herself was beautiful in the way that frost on a window is beautiful: precise, temporary, and utterly indifferent to what it covered. She had made her offer while leaning forward, elbows on knees, almost conspiratorial, as though they were two merchants haggling over cloth rather than a monarch offering a witch the restoration of everything she had ever lost.

"Your old life," Mavrosa said again, patiently, the way one repeats an offer to a slow child. "The market stall. The luck-knots. The little fever-charms your neighbors never thanked you properly for. You would have all of it back, whole and untouched, and no one — not a Warden, not a coven sister, not a single soul above or below — would ever know you were anything but what you appeared to be. A quiet woman who sells knots. And him." Her gaze slid to Corin, and something in it made Corin's grip tighten. "He walks out of here with his soul returned, his memories restored, twenty-six years of a life handed back to him like a coat from a cloakroom. He will remember his mother's voice. His brother's name. Everything the court took."

"And all I have to do," Ulyana said, "is give you the book and walk away."

"Give me the book, forget this place, forget him — briefly, only until you're safely above; memory is such a fragile thing, we can arrange the forgetting however you like — and walk away." Mavrosa smiled. It was, Ulyana had to admit, a very good smile. Practiced. Warm around the edges. The kind of smile that had talked a hundred frightened people into worse bargains than this one. "You have spent twenty years perfecting the art of being unseen, Ulyana Pemberton. I am offering to make it permanent. Most people would call that mercy."

The echo said *mercy*, *mercy*, and the courtiers' gauze sleeves whispered as they shifted, and somewhere far above them — so far above that it might have been imagination — Ulyana thought she heard the mountain groan.

She looked at Corin.

He was looking at her, and his face did something complicated. She had gotten good at reading his face over these weeks on the road — the ash-tasting nights, the inn without mirrors, the dreams that were not dreams but memories leaking upward through whatever seal the court had poured into him. Right now his face said: *I want you to say yes.* Not because he wanted her gone. Because he wanted her safe, and he had spent his entire hollowed-out existence learning that safety was the only currency worth anything, and he could not yet believe in any other economy.

And underneath that — quieter, steadier — his face also said: *but I know what you're going to do.*

"You should say yes," Corin said quietly. "Ulyana. Twenty years. You could just *have* them. All of them back."

"I wouldn't," Ulyana said.

"You don't know that."

"I do, actually." She turned back to the throne. "That's the funny thing about hiding, Your Majesty. Everyone assumes the person doing it wants to keep hiding. Like the hiding is the prize. But it isn't. It's the *cost*. I paid it every day for twenty years and called the paying 'freedom,' because the alternative was admitting I'd been robbed."

Mavrosa's smile did not waver, but the temperature of the room did. One degree colder. Two. "Careful."

"No," Ulyana said. "You know what? No, I don't think I'll be careful. I've been careful for twenty years. I've been *exhaustingly* careful. I have hidden charms in loaves of bread and sewn sigils into hem-stitches and lied to every kind person who ever bought a luck-knot from my stall about who they were buying it from, and do you know what I got for it? Nothing. I got nothing. The Wardens raided my street anyway. Marta's daughter was dragged off anyway, for a *candle-spell*, a child's spell, the kind of thing I could have taught her to do safely if I hadn't been so busy being invisible." Her voice was climbing now, and she let it climb. "My hiding didn't protect anyone. Not them. Not me. It just meant that when the bad thing finally came, I was alone, and practiced at being alone, and very, very good at pretending that was the same thing as being free."

The echo carried it all around the chamber, *alone, alone, free, free,* and the pale courtiers stirred like reeds in water.

"You are making a speech," Mavrosa observed, "instead of an answer."

"I'm getting to the answer. It's a long way coming. Twenty years long." Ulyana drew a breath. "Here is what you don't understand about me, Majesty, and I think maybe you've never understood it about anyone, because you sit on a throne of stolen teeth and trade in bottled souls and you think that wanting is the same as weakness. You offered me invisibility because you believe everyone wants it. That everyone's secret dream is to be overlooked forever. But I didn't hide because I loved it. I hid because I was *afraid*, and fear is a terrible landlord. It charges rent forever and never fixes the roof."

Something flickered across Mavrosa's face — quick, quickly buried — and Ulyana thought, with a strange lurch: *she knows. She knows exactly what fear-charged rent looks like. She's paid it too, to sit on that throne.* Exiles did not become usurpers by being brave every day. Somewhere behind those frost-beautiful eyes was a woman who had also once decided that disappearing was safer than being seen, and had then discovered that the only way back up was through other people's souls.

It didn't make her sympathetic. But it made her legible. And Ulyana had spent twenty years reading the small print of frightened people, and she knew a fellow signatory when she saw one.

"So here is my answer," Ulyana said.

She let go of Corin's hand.

She stepped forward — one step, then another, out ahead of him, out from behind anyone at all — and she stood in the middle of the great mosaic floor where every pale eye in the court could see her, and she felt the old reflex rise up in her throat like bile, the reflex that said *smaller, quieter, plainer, be the knot-seller, be nobody, be smoke* — and she looked at that reflex, and she said, internally, with enormous tenderness: *thank you. You kept me alive. We're done now.*

"My name," Ulyana said, and her voice rang against the teeth, "is Ulyana Pemberton."

The echo caught it. *Pemberton. Pemberton.* But something was wrong with the echo. It sounded different this time. Brighter. Warmer. As though the walls themselves were surprised.

"My mother named me for her grandmother, who was a hedge-witch in the fen country and could charm a storm into sitting down like a tired dog. My father was a cooper and smelled of oak shavings and thought magic was nonsense right up until the day he watched me cure his best barrel of rot by singing to it, and after that he still thought it was nonsense, but he brought home honey cakes whenever I sang, which is its own kind of faith." Her voice grew stronger. "I was raised by the Thornhollow coven. I was apprenticed to Mother Ysolde, who taught me fire-magic and patience in roughly equal measure and despaired of me in both. I refused the binding rite because I would not sell my soul to this court, and I ran, and I survived, and I told myself for twenty years that surviving was the same as living."

A courtier near the dais made a sound like wet paper tearing. Another stepped backward. The gauze of their sleeves trembled.

"I am a witch," Ulyana said. "Not was. Am. I mend fevers and untangle grief and I can find lost things, which I always thought was a small useless gift until I understood that what I'd really been finding, all along, was myself. I sell luck-knots. Good ones. Fair prices. I cheat nobody, not even customers who deserve it, although I have *wanted* to." Someone above — Corin — made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled by tears. "I am afraid of Wardens and deep water and my own sister-apprentice's disappointment, and I am done letting the fear speak for me. My name is Ulyana Pemberton. My craft is small magic done honestly. I am *seen*."

She spoke the last word the way Mother Ysolde had taught her to speak fire-words — not loudly, but *completely*, filling the word edge to edge the way you fill a bell.

And the room broke.

Not literally — although three of the teeth on the throne's left side cracked clean through with sounds like icebergs calving, which Ulyana chose to take as a compliment. What broke was subtler and vastly more important. The echoes changed. Where before every word had come back doubled — speaker and silver twin — now the echo came back *singular*. Just her voice. Just the truth of it, ringing in stone, owing nothing to the room.

Because that was what Mavrosa's power had always been, Ulyana understood suddenly, watching the pale queen rise half out of her seat with an expression that had stopped being beautiful. The court beneath the mountain held dominion over hidden things. Bottled souls. Buried names. Sold births and forgotten years. Every bargain struck in shadow fed it. Every person who lived a lie unknowingly paid it rent. The whole architecture of the court — the soul-market, the seals, the starving dragon — was built on the premise that people would rather hide than be seen, and that their hiding could be harvested.

But a thing declared openly belonged to no one but itself.

"You cannot—" Mavrosa began, and her voice cracked on the second word, and the crack ran outward through the room like a fault line. Around the chamber, the courtiers were clutching their sleeves, their throats, their faces, as though something they wore were slipping. "You cannot unmake the terms of this court with *words*—"

"Not words," Ulyana said. "Truth. There's a difference. You'd know that if you'd ever tried one."

Corin came to stand beside her. Of course he did. He didn't announce himself — that wasn't his way; he was a quiet man, a tea-ordering man, a man whose soul had been sold before he was born and who had nonetheless, somehow, remained gentle — but he stood beside her in full view of the throne and he said, in his ordinary, unhurried voice:

"My name is Aldous Vane."

The room shuddered.

"My mother called me Doss. My brother is Ilse Vane, and she thinks I'm dead, or worse, and she has spent her whole life hunting the thing that took me without knowing it was hunting me. I taste ash when I wake because part of me has been burning down here since before I had a name to burn with. I am not empty. I was *robbed*. And I am standing in the place that robbed me, saying so, out loud, where everyone can hear." His hand found Ulyana's again. "Seen."

The mosaic floor lit under their feet.

Ulyana felt it happen — felt the declaration travel outward through the stone like warmth through a floor in winter, seeking every sealed and bottled and buried thing in the mountain and knocking politely on each one. Down in the cellar of jars, she would learn later, a hundred labels had blurred at that moment, ink swimming, true names rising up through the glass like bubbles through honey. In the soul-market, the pale traders had dropped bottles. On the hoard of spent years, the dimming bulk of Ember-that-Waits had lifted its head.

And on the throne of teeth, Mavrosa screamed.

It was not a regal scream. It was the scream of a landlord discovering the tenants unionized. "SEIZE THEM—"

The courtiers did not move.

They stood in their drowned-colored gauze, blinking — actually blinking now, Ulyana noticed, fast and desperate, like people surfacing — and not one of them took a step. Because every courtier in that room was itself a hidden thing, a bound thing, a soul on layaway, and Ulyana's declaration had rung through all of them like a bell through a fog, and bells are terribly hard to unhear.

"They can't," said a voice from the back of the chamber, high and wondering. A young courtier, hardly more than a girl, staring at her own hands as though they'd just been returned from the cleaners. "I can't — I can't remember why I couldn't leave—"

"*Guards!*" Mavrosa's composure was in ruins now; she looked less like frost and more like a thaw. "The seals— I hold the seals—"

"You hold *bottles*," Ulyana said. "There's a difference, and I'm told it matters."

What happened next happened quickly and messily and was, in later tellings by both participants, heavily embellished in opposite directions. Mavrosa came down from the throne of teeth with power gathering around her hands like black water, and Ulyana — who had not used real fire-magic in twenty years and had only recently remembered how, thanks to a river crossing, a furious Sergeant, and a very patient dead teacher's training regimen — raised her palms and met her.

The collision was not elegant. Fire-magic against shadow-magic in an enclosed space mostly looks, from the outside, like an argument between a bonfire and a thundercloud conducted entirely in sparks. Ulyana's braid came half-undone. Something singed. She would find ash in her hair for days. But every spark she threw was thrown *openly*, named as she threw it — *"Ember-word, Ysolde's gift, mine by study!"* — and named fire burns hotter, and Mavrosa's shadows were made of other people's secrets and had begun, badly, to leak.

The queen staggered back against her own throne. A tooth fell out of it and rolled across the floor and came to rest at Ulyana's feet, huge and yellowed and suddenly, pathetically, just a tooth.

"Twenty years," Mavrosa rasped, propped against her crumbling seat, and for one moment the frost fell away entirely and Ulyana saw the exiled girl underneath, the one who had once been afraid, the one who had chosen the throne instead of the truth. "Twenty years building this. You think one declaration undoes it?"

"No," Ulyana admitted. She was breathing hard. Her hands were shaking. She had never felt more alive in her life. "One declaration starts it. The rest takes work. That's usually how undoing goes."

Above them — far above, muffled by miles of cooling stone — came a sound that none of them expected and all of them would remember.

Trumpets. Human trumpets. The particular sour brass of Cinderhollow's Warden guard, playing the advance-call that preceded a raid.

Mavrosa started laughing, ragged and relieved. "Well," she said, wiping her eyes with a gauze sleeve. "It seems your Magistrate's dogs have found the back door after all. Whatever you've broken in here, witch, they've come to burn the rest. I do hope you have a plan for soldiers."

Ulyana looked at Corin. Corin looked at Ulyana. And then they both looked toward the tunnel mouth, where torchlight was already beginning to flicker against the far wall, growing brighter, accompanied by the ring of salt-iron boots on stone.

"Ilse," Corin breathed.

"Ilse," Ulyana agreed grimly. She picked up the fallen dragon tooth, weighed it in her hand, and tucked it into her satchel, because a witch who has just declared herself does not stop collecting useful things.

"Stay close to me," she said. "And whatever happens next — whatever she shows you, whatever she says — remember that we're already seen. There's nothing left for her to use."

The torchlight grew. The boots came on.

* * *

They met the Warden column forty paces from the tunnel mouth, in the wide antechamber where the lava tunnels opened into the first vaulted hall of the court. Torches on iron staves. Thirty soldiers at least, in grey-and-salt tabards, and at their head, gaunt and grim and holding a drawn blade that smoked faintly with consecrated oil —

Sergeant Ilse Vane.

Her eyes found Corin first. Ulyana watched it happen — watched the recognition move through the tracker's face like a crack through ice. Watched her stare at the shape of her brother's jaw wearing another man's softer years, at the eyes that were their mother's eyes, at the small scar above the brow that Ilse herself had given him falling out of a pear tree when he was six.

The sword point dipped. Rose again. Dipped.

"Sergeant," Ulyana said carefully, stepping slightly forward, hands open and empty. "Before anyone burns anything — I need to show you something. It's about your family. It's about what you've been hunting all these years."

Ilse Vane's voice, when it came, was scraped raw.

"What," she said, "could you possibly have that I haven't already lost?"

Behind her soldiers, in the dark of the throat of the mountain, something vast and old and nearly starved turned its attention upward — and waited.
