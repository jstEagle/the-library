## 32. Ilse Turns

*Ilse*

The tunnels breathed.

Ilse Vane had led Warden raids for eleven years—tanneries where love-potions were brewed in vats that smelled of rotten plums, attics full of forbidden almanacs, one memorable cellar in Dockside where a hedge-witch kept a salamander in a soup pot—but she had never led men underground before, and she found she hated it with her whole body. The passage down from the drowned chapel sloped through rock the color of old blood, and the walls sweated warmth, and every twenty paces the whole mountain exhaled against the back of her neck like something deciding whether to swallow.

Behind her, forty Wardens carried lanterns and fire-oil. Ahead of them, according to the map she'd wrung out of a dying woman at an inn without mirrors, lay the court beneath the mountain—the soul-market, the bottled lives, the throne. The Magistrate's orders were simple and had been repeated to her three times, each repetition more insulting than the last: *burn it.*

She had not slept since the river. She had not slept properly since she was seven years old and overheard her parents arguing in numbers instead of words—*forty crowns, Ilse, they wanted forty crowns or the boy*—and she did not expect to sleep again until this was finished.

"Vane." Corporal Hesk, at her shoulder, wiping his palms on his coat. "You hear that?"

She heard it. Beneath their boots, far below, something was singing. Not words. A long, low note, patient as a held breath, and around it other voices wove harmonies that made her back teeth ache.

"The witch's charms," Hesk said, wanting it to be charms.

"It's not charms," Ilse said, and kept walking.

*Ulyana*

"They're coming," Seraiva said, without looking up from the basin of dark water. Her voice had the particular flatness of someone who has said *they're coming* many times across many centuries and has stopped finding it interesting. "Forty-one souls above us in the throat-tunnels. Forty carrying fire-oil. One carrying grief."

"One carrying what?" Ulyana asked.

The exiled queen looked at her. In the drowned chapel's borrowed light her eyes were the amber of resin about to trap something. "Grief. Can't you smell it? It smells like cold iron and a name said once a year."

Ulyana thought of Ilse Vane at the river crossing, rain streaming off her hat brim, the tracker's patience, the terrible competence. And underneath it—now that Ulyana let herself remember, now that she let herself look—that flicker in the woman's face when Corin had shouted his own lack of a past at her. *You know what it is to be missing something,* he'd said, and Vane had flinched as though struck.

"She's here for the book," Ulyana said. "And for him."

"She's here because someone told her the truth," Seraiva said, rising, water sliding off her sleeves without wetting them. "That's worse. Truth makes people reckless."

Brenna came up the chapel steps two at a time, salt-iron staff first, which was very Brenna—lead with the pointy end, apologize never. "Ysolde's song-charms are distributed. The sisters are holding the upper galleries. Ulyana—" She stopped. Looked at Seraiva's basin, then at Ulyana's face. "You've got an idea. I can tell because you look guilty. You always look guilty when you've got an idea."

"I need a jar," Ulyana said.

*Ilse*

They broke into the great cavern at moonset, if there was such a thing as moonset under a mountain, and forty hardened Wardens of the Cinderhollow Magistracy stopped walking all at the same moment, which Ilse would later learn was the most coordinated thing any of them did that night.

The cavern was vast beyond sense. Stalactites hung like the ribs of some drowned god. And filling the middle of the floor, tier upon tier upon tier, glowed ten thousand bottles—clay jars and glass flasks and stoppered gourds, each one lit from within by a soft gold light that pulsed, gently, rhythmically, all of them together, like a city of sleeping hearts.

"Oh," said Corporal Hesk, in a small voice entirely unlike a corporal's.

"Fire-oil," Ilse said. Her own voice sounded wrong in her ears. "On my mark."

Because this was the enemy. This was the seat of everything that had taken her brother—everything her family's forty crowns had fed, everything she'd spent her life clawing toward through warrants and ledgers and burned hedge-cottages. Burn the market, burn the court, burn the whole rotting apparatus, and maybe somewhere in the ashes there'd be—

There was a woman standing on the lowest tier of shelves.

Small, brown-haired, dressed in a merchant's coat with singed hems, holding a clay jar in both hands the way you hold something that might break or might bite. Behind her stood a tall man whose face Ilse knew from eleven years of reports, and half a dozen robed witches, and a pale woman who could only be the exiled queen, radiating the specific menace of royalty in exile.

"Sergeant Vane," the small woman called, and her voice carried impossibly well in the cavern, the way voices do in churches and tombs. "Before you give the order—I want to show you something."

"Fire-oil," Ilse said again, but her arm didn't move. Nothing moved. Ten thousand little lights pulsed, and Ilse's heart had somehow fallen into rhythm with them.

"You came all this way," the woman said. "You crossed the ash country. You cornered us at a river and lost us and followed anyway. Don't you want to know why a tracker like you couldn't stop following?"

"Give me one reason not to drop the torch," Ilse said.

"You already have one," the woman said. "You just haven't looked at it in thirty years." She lifted the jar.

Even at fifty yards, even across a cavern full of sleeping souls, Ilse saw it. A plain clay jar, cork stopper sealed with green wax. And painted on its side in careful white letters:

**ALDOUS VANE — SOLD IN INFANCY — PROPERTY OF THE COURT BENEATH**

The world went very quiet and very large.

Her torch was still lit. She was distantly aware of it burning down toward her fingers. Around her, forty men waited on her word, and below her, a woman she had hunted across half a kingdom held her brother's soul in cupped hands like an offering, or an accusation, or—Ilse genuinely could not decide—a gift.

"Aldous," she said. Her voice cracked in the middle of the word, right down the grain, like old wood.

"He answers to Corin now," Ulyana Pemberton said gently. "But it's him. It was always him. He's been down here the whole time, Sergeant. Your family never sold him to the Magistrate's mercy. They sold him *here*. And the Magistrate—" she paused, and Ilse watched her choose honesty over comfort, watched her do it deliberately, "—the Magistrate has known about this place for a hundred years. Why do you think hedge-witches get burned but the nobles' soul-brokers get licenses?"

The torch burned lower.

Hesk shifted beside her. "Sergeant? Orders?"

Ilse Vane looked at the jar. At the light inside it pulsing in time with ten thousand others, in time, she realized, with her own heartbeat, because it was her blood in that light, her mother's nose and her father's stubbornness and a boy who had once bitten a tax assessor. Thirty years of hunting, and the quarry had been here the entire time, shelved like a preserve.

She dropped the torch into the snowmelt channel at her feet. It went out with a hiss that sounded almost like relief.

"Stand down," she said.

*Ulyana*

Later, Ulyana would try to explain the next hour to Brenna and fail completely, mostly because it involved a Warden sergeant sitting down on the floor of hell, taking the jar in both hands, and saying, in a voice like a child's, "He used to hum when he ate. Did anyone—does he still—"

"He hums," Corin said. Aldous. He crouched in front of her, careful not to touch, careful the way people are careful with things they're afraid will vanish. "I don't know why. There's no music down here. But I hum while I eat, and it drives Mavrosa's courtiers mad, and I've never known where it came from."

"Your grandmother's kitchen," Ilse said. "Porridge days. She hummed, you copied her, drove everyone mad." She laughed, and it came out broken in half, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Thirty years. I sold my soul to the Magistrate's service looking for you. I burned cottages. I dragged a girl away for a *candle-spell*, I—" She stopped. Looked up at Ulyana, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely level. "I did terrible things in your direction too."

"You did," Ulyana agreed. "We can discuss reparations after we survive the night. I'm thinking formal apology, public, plus you owe me a new stall."

"My men burned your market."

"And you'll help rebuild it. With your hands. In front of witnesses."

"That's—" Ilse blinked. Something that might have been the ancestor of a smile tugged at her mouth. "That's fair, actually. Hesk! Hesk, are your men standing down or do I need to stand them down the hard way?"

From across the cavern, Corporal Hesk's voice floated back, wobbling somewhere between terror and wonder: "Sergeant, one of the jars just giggled at me and Private Ommel sat down, sir, ma'am, nobody's doing anything, everybody's just kind of... looking at the lights."

"Good enough," Ilse said. Then, quieter, only to Ulyana: "Mavrosa will know within minutes that her siege became a congregation. What's the plan? Please tell me there's a plan. I didn't come all this way to die planless."

"There's a dragon," Ulyana said.

Ilse stared. "A what?"

"A dying dragon. Under the hoard. It holds up the seals that keep the starving things outside the world, and it's starving itself, and Mavrosa's answer is to keep feeding it stolen souls forever until either it dies or the rest of us run out." Ulyana shrugged. "So. Dragon first, tyrant second, tea sometime after, if we live."

"That's not a plan, that's a list of disasters in order."

"Yes," said Seraiva pleasantly, drifting past with the serenity of a woman who had been attending lists of disasters for six hundred years. "Welcome to politics."

*Ilse*

They moved through the court in a strange procession: two witches, an exiled queen, a restored-almost man, and a Warden sergeant whose forty soldiers trailed behind in a daze, carrying their unlit torches like pilgrims' candles. Courtiers scattered from their path—pale, beautiful things with ledger-eyes—and Ilse found she could no longer look at them without seeing clerks. That was the horror of it, now that the grief had cracked her open: not monsters. *Bookkeeping.* Her brother, filed under V.

At the edge of the hoard—the mountain of spent years, coins and rings and buttons, each one a life skimmed thin—the heat changed. It rolled up from below in slow waves, and the singing she'd heard in the tunnels swelled until it lived in her sternum.

And there, coiled in a hollow of its own shed centuries, the dragon.

It was vast and dim, its scales the grey of banked embers, and only along its spine did a thread of orange still glow, pulsing faintly, in time—she understood now—with the ten thousand jars, with everything, with the whole sick machinery of the place.

Ember-that-Waits opened one eye. It was the color of dawn.

It looked at Ilse Vane a long moment.

"*The sister,*" it said, and its voice was the low note itself, the one from the tunnels, the one she'd been hearing all night without knowing she was hearing it. "*You carry a debt older than your grief.*"

"Yeah," Ilse said, because apparently she addressed dragons now, casually, like a neighbor over a fence. "I've got a few. Where do I start paying?"

The dragon's eye crinkled, which Ilse would not have believed possible, and which she would absolutely be describing incorrectly to everyone for the rest of her life.

"*Start by keeping your soldiers from burning my hoard,*" it said. "*Then attend. The witch is about to do something foolish and brave, and those are always worth watching.*"

Everyone turned to Ulyana.

Ulyana Pemberton was standing at the lip of the hollow, looking up at the dimming spine-fire, and her face wore the expression of a woman adding up a bill she'd been dodging for twenty years. Beside her, Corin—Aldous—took her hand.

Above them, far above, through miles of stone, a bell began to ring in the throne hall. Once. Twice. Then continuously, urgent, ugly.

"Mavrosa knows," Seraiva said softly. "And she knows exactly what the witch is thinking of doing. Run, little thief of hearts. We have a usurper to disappoint, and I have waited six hundred years to see the look on her face."

*Ulyana*

Ulyana squeezed Aldous's hand once, let go, and started down into the ember-light—toward the dragon, toward the price, toward the last line of a prophecy she'd meant to burn.

Behind her, boots followed. Warden boots, witch boots, and the steady tread of a sergeant who had finally, after thirty years, stopped hunting and started marching *with* someone.

The bell rang on. Somewhere ahead, a queen of teeth was waiting.

Ulyana smiled in the dark, and went to meet her.
