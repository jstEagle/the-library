## 33. The Throne of Teeth Falls

The throne room of the mountain court had no ceiling that Ulyana could see—or perhaps it had every ceiling at once. The vault above them was a slow churn of stone and ember-light, like smoke given architecture, and somewhere in its turning she could hear the dragon breathing: vast, arrhythmic, the sound of bellows worked by a dying man.

She had imagined this moment a hundred times on the road—inns, riverbanks, Ilse Vane's grim face across a campfire—and in none of those imaginings had Mavrosa been *laughing*.

But the Queen of Teeth was laughing. She sat upon her throne—actual teeth, thousands of them, set in dark stone like a reef of ivory—and her laughter rolled down the long hall and made the bottled souls on their shelves shiver in their glass.

"You brought me *Seraiva*," Mavrosa said, wiping her eye with one pale, elegant finger. "The drowned queen herself, paddling back to my hall with a hedge-witch, a soulless man, and—" her gaze found Ilse Vane, standing very straight among the Warden soldiers, "—a *Magistrate's officer*. Oh, this is the finest gift the upper world has sent me in three hundred years."

"She's not here as a gift," Ulyana said.

Her voice came out steadier than she felt. That was the trick of it, she was learning—the trick Mother Ysolde had spent those terrible lessons hammering into her bones. Courage wasn't the absence of shaking. It was simply doing the thing while you shook.

"No?" Mavrosa rose from the throne of teeth. She moved like water running backward, wrong and lovely. "Then what is she here as? Speak your purpose, little witch who hid for twenty years. I do so admire a coward who finally finds her spine. It saves me the trouble of growing one for her."

Beside Ulyana, Seraiva drew breath—and the whole hall changed temperature. The exiled queen's grief and grace had been stripped away somewhere in the lava tunnels; what walked beside Ulyana now was something older, colder, crowned already by sheer refusal.

"I come for my throne," Seraiva said.

"Your throne." Mavrosa smiled, and her smile was a collection. "Darling, thrones aren't property. They're appetites. You lost yours because you were too gentle to feed it properly."

"And you've fed it so well," Seraiva said softly, "that the fire itself is starving. Have you looked at Ember lately, usurper? Have you actually *looked*?"

For the first time, Mavrosa's laughter stopped.

It didn't die gradually. It cut off, clean as a snipped thread, and in the silence Ulyana heard the thing she'd been hearing all along without naming it: the great breathing overhead had a rasp in it now. A catch. Like an old woman climbing stairs.

"The seals hold," Mavrosa said. "That is all that matters."

"The seals hold *today*."

"They have held for nine hundred years—"

"On the backs of stolen children." Seraiva's voice cracked through the hall like ice splitting on a lake. "Bottled lives. Sold souls. A market built on theft, feeding a fire that grows hungrier every century because *theft is thin food*, Mavrosa. Even you must know that. Even you can taste it."

Ulyana glanced sideways. Aldous stood near her left shoulder—Corin still, in her heart, though his name had come home to him down there in the cellar of jars—and he was watching Mavrosa with an expression she'd never seen on him before. Not fear. Not even anger, exactly. *Recognition.* Somewhere under nineteen empty years and one reclaimed lifetime, some part of him knew this court. Had heard its music before he could walk.

Mavrosa's eyes flicked to him too, and her smile returned, thinner now.

"And there's my little investment," she said. "Aldous Vane. Sold at birth, delivered on schedule, wandering loose in the world with his soul in my pantry. Do you know, most sold souls never amount to anything? They shuffle. They forget. But you—" she descended one step from the throne, then another, "—you came *back*. Souls always try to come home. It's the one law even queens can't repeal." Another step. "Unfortunately for you, little Aldous, home is mine."

"Don't," Ulyana said.

Mavrosa paused. "Don't what?"

"Just—don't monologue your way toward him. I've read about queens like you. In books. The ones who explain themselves are always the ones who lose."

Someone behind her made a sound that might have been a suppressed laugh—it sounded suspiciously like Brenna—and Mavrosa's composure fissured, just slightly, just enough.

"You dare instruct me on—"

"I dare lots of things lately," Ulyana said. Her heart was slamming against her ribs hard enough to hurt, and she kept talking anyway, because that was the other thing Ysolde taught her: once you start telling the truth out loud, it gets easier, and easier is dangerous, and dangerous is exactly what this moment required. "I dared to say my own name in front of you last week. I dared to unmake your claim over hidden things. And here's what I know about hidden things, Your Majesty—you keep them because they're useful, and yours are about to stop being useful."

She reached into her coat and drew out 'The Ashen Hours.'

The book had changed since the night market. Since the declaration. Its cover, once gray as old ash, now showed faint veins of red, like coals waking under cinders. When she held it up, the light in the hall bent toward it—all of it, every guttering lamp, every ember in the churning vault above—as if the room itself wanted to read.

Mavrosa went very still.

"That book," she said, and her voice had lost all its music, "is not permitted here."

"It's a prophecy book. Prophecies don't ask permission." Ulyana opened it. The pages turned themselves, eager, to the place she'd marked with a ribbon of Brenna's weaving. "Shall I read aloud? There's a verse about a queen of teeth. It doesn't end well for her. It ends with—" she squinted theatrically, "—huh. The ink's still drying on this part. Funny how that works."

"*Give it to me.*"

"Come take it."

And Mavrosa—queen, usurper, keeper of the soul-market, terror of nine centuries—came off the throne of teeth and took three strides down the hall, and that was when everything happened at once.

* * *

Later, Ulyana would struggle to put the fall of the Throne of Teeth into proper order. Memory sorted it into pieces afterward, the way a storm sorts itself into thunder after the lightning:

Seraiva moving first—not attacking but *unveiling*, dropping the glamour of the drowned chapel, the wet rags, the weariness, until she stood in the full terrible regalia of a true queen of the mountain court, and half the courtiers in the hall dropped to their knees on pure reflex, bodies remembering fealty their minds had sold.

Ilse Vane shouting a single word—"NOW!"—and forty Warden soldiers raising not guns but salt-iron lanterns, the kind the Magistrate used for burning witches, repurposed, their hungry light turned inward on the shelves of bottled souls, holding the courtiers back, holding the *market* hostage in reverse: touch anyone, and we shatter everything you own.

Brenna and the coven sisters singing—Ysolde's song-charm grown to cathedral size, a chord that slid under the skin and loosened grip, loosened will, loosened the strange gravity by which Mavrosa held her hall together.

Aldous walking forward into all of it, unarmed, unhurried, straight toward the throne, because—he told her later, with the ghost of a shrug—*his soul had been kept in that room for nineteen years and he wanted to see where he'd lived.*

And Mavrosa lunging for the book, for Ulyana, her fingers closing on empty air as Ulyana spoke—not a spell, nothing so tidy, just her name again, her true name, spoken like a key turning—and the Queen of Teeth's power, built on hiddenness, on secrecy, on the ownership of buried things, slid off Ulyana like rain off oiled wool.

Mavrosa screamed. It was not a dignified scream. Ulyana took a certain private, guilty pleasure in that.

Then Seraiva's hand closed on the back of the usurper's neck—not striking, just holding, the way you hold a cat that has decided to be foolish—and she said, in a voice that carried to every corner of the hall and down every tunnel and, Ulyana suspected, all the way to the dimming hoard below:

"This court is ended. Long live the court."

And Mavrosa, Queen of Teeth, maker of markets, thief of infants' souls, was dragged by her own kneeling courtiers to the dungeons she had designed so lovingly for others, screaming verses of prophecy back at them the whole way down, which Ulyana thought was probably the most honest thing she'd ever done.

* * *

The crowning was not grand.

That surprised Ulyana most, somehow. She'd expected ceremony—oaths, ancient words, the weight of centuries settling onto shoulders. Instead Seraiva simply walked to the throne of teeth, looked at it for a long moment, and said, "Take this apart. Every tooth goes back to whoever it was taken from. If we cannot find whose it was, bury them with honor in the ash country, under open sky. No one sits on people."

"But then—" a courtier began, wringing his pale hands, "what will Your Majesty sit upon?"

"A chair," said Seraiva. "I'm told they're comfortable."

They found her one within the hour—an ordinary wooden chair, salvaged from some clerk's alcove, slightly too small for her regalia, entirely wrong for her dignity. She sat in it anyway, and something in her posture suggested she would sit in nothing else ever again, and Ulyana found her eyes stinging and couldn't have said exactly why.

Perhaps because she recognized the gesture. Twenty years of small magic, small life, small safe hiding—and here was a queen choosing the same thing from the top of the world instead of the bottom of it.

"You're staring," Seraiva said, without looking up.

"You're sitting on a clerk's chair."

"I am sitting on a *throne that doesn't require feeding*." The new queen finally raised her eyes, and they were the gray-green of deep water, and very tired. "You'll understand soon, witch. Everyone wants to give me advice about the fire. Let's hear yours."

Ulyana hadn't prepared advice. She'd prepared for a battle, and battles had a way of ending cleanly compared to what came after them. So she said the truest thing she had:

"Don't feed it anymore stolen souls."

The hall around them had begun, cautiously, to breathe again—courtiers whispering, Warden soldiers standing awkwardly amid the pale elegance like cats at a formal dinner, Brenna directing the dismantling of the tooth-throne with the brisk efficiency of a woman reorganizing a kitchen. At Seraiva's words, though, the nearest whispers stopped.

"No," Seraiva agreed quietly.

"Even though—"

"*Even though.*" She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the regalia fell away from her voice and she was just a woman who had been exiled three hundred years and was very tired of being polite about it. "Do you know what I did, the last year of my reign, before Mavrosa came for me? I read the ledgers. All of them. Nine hundred years of intake records for the fire. Do you know what they show?"

Ulyana shook her head.

"They show a fire that eats more every century and shines less. They show diminishing returns written in children's names. My predecessors solved nothing—they *postponed*. Every soul poured into that dragon bought a decade, then a year, then a month. The curve only points one way." She sat back. "I refused to sign the last ledger. That refusal cost me my crown. I'd sign it again tomorrow."

"So the seals—" Ulyana began.

"Will fail," Seraiva said plainly. "Eventually. Perhaps in fifty years. Perhaps five hundred. Perhaps next spring, if the fire gutters faster than the ledgers suggest. And beneath us are things that were sealed away when the world was young and crueler than it is now, and when they get out, no throne and no chair and no clever hedge-witch will matter much." She spread her hands. "So. That is the state of my kingdom, freely given, since you asked me not to lie to you. I will not feed the machine with stolen lives. I would rather the world end honestly than continue dishonestly. Now everyone in this hall believes I'm mad, and the worst part is that I might be."

Silence. Overhead, the great breathing rasped on.

It was Aldous who broke it. He had drifted to the edge of the dais during all this, drawn—as he was always drawn now—to the places where his missing years had lived, and he spoke without turning around.

"There's a third option."

Every head in the vicinity turned toward him. He still wasn't looking at any of them. He was looking at the far archway, the one through which, hours ago, Ulyana had first seen the dragon—a glimpse only, a vast dim shape curled on a hoard of spent years, like a coal wrapped in grey cloth.

"You said the ledgers show theft is thin food," Aldous said. "You said the curve only points one way. But the ledgers only record what was *taken*. Has anyone ever recorded what was *given*?"

Seraiva went very still. "Freely-given souls are rare enough that—"

"I'm not talking about souls." Now he turned, and his eyes found Ulyana's, and there was something in them that made her stomach drop—not dread exactly, more the feeling of standing at the top of stairs in the dark and misjudging the first step. "I'm talking about time. Years. Life freely poured rather than stolen. The witch downstairs—the old one, Ysolde—told me about it on the march down. Old magic. Older than the market. The kind nobody uses because nobody *has* anything worth giving." His voice stayed level, but Ulyana saw his hands weren't quite steady. "Somebody should ask the dragon. Somebody should ask Ember what it actually needs, instead of asking ledgers what it's been force-fed for nine hundred years."

"That," said Seraiva slowly, "is either the wisest thing said in this hall today or the most dangerous."

"Those are usually the same thing," said Brenna, arriving at Ulyana's elbow with the expression of a woman who had recently forgiven her sister and intended to make the forgiveness as inconvenient as possible. "In our family, certainly."

* * *

They went down to the dragon that evening.

Not all of them. Seraiva insisted on coming; Brenna refused to be left behind; Aldous would not have stayed above ground if they'd physically restrained him. The rest—the soldiers, the courtiers, the freed souls still blinking their way through their first hours of liberty—remained in the hall, and Ulyana was glad of it. Some things should only be witnessed by the few.

The way down was a stair of cooling stone, spiraling through rock that still remembered being lava, and the air grew warmer and stranger with each turn. Ulyana felt her charms stirring against her skin—not warning-charms, for once, but the luck-knots, the fever-knots, all the small crafts of her small life, humming like struck tuning forks. As if the magic itself knew where it was going.

The hoard opened around them like the inside of a hill made of years.

Spent time, Ysolde had called it. Bottles and jars and vials by the ten thousand, stacked in drifts, piled in dunes, glittering dully in the ember-light—every one of them emptied, drained, the lives inside used up as fuel. Ulyana had seen the soul-market above and thought she understood. She hadn't. This was the market's *conclusion*, its final arithmetic, and walking through it felt like wading through a graveyard where the graves were labeled with birthdays.

And at the center, curled around the oldest and emptiest bottles like a hen gone grey over eggs that would never hatch, lay Ember-that-Waits.

Up close, the dragon was smaller than the breathing had promised and sadder than anything Ulyana had ever imagined. Its scales had gone the color of banked charcoal, dull except where faint veins of red still pulsed—slowly, so slowly, like a heartbeat counted in geological time. One enormous eye opened as they approached. It was the color of the last coals of a fire everyone has gone to bed and left untended.

"Well," the dragon said.

Its voice was not what she expected. It was tired, and dry, and—gently humorous, somehow, in the way of very old people who have outlived the need to impress anyone.

"Well," said Ulyana.

"You're the one who declared herself. The hidden witch. I felt it from down here—like someone opening a window in a house I'd forgotten had windows." The great eye regarded her. "Thank you for that, incidentally. It's been stuffy."

"We've come to ask you a question," Seraiva said, and for once her queen's voice softened into something almost human. "The ledgers say you eat souls. Nine hundred years of ledgers say it. But ledgers are written by the thieves. So we're asking *you*. What do you actually need, Ember-that-Waits?"

The silence went on so long that Ulyana wondered whether the dragon had fallen asleep with its eye open.

Then, quietly, in a voice that made the spent years around them seem to lean in and listen:

"No one has asked me that in nine hundred years."

* * *

The answer, when it fully came, was not a single word but a showing—the dragon breathed out, not flame but memory, and the hoard lit up around them with images: the sealing of the starving things, ages ago; the first desperate bargain, when the founders of the court offered the dragon their own remaining years, *freely*, to fuel the fire that fed the seals; and how that gift had blazed brighter than any theft ever managed, bright enough to seal the deep things for a thousand years—

—and how, one generation later, someone had done the arithmetic, and decided that free gifts were expensive, and stolen goods were cheap, and surely the fire wouldn't notice the difference.

The fire had noticed.

The fire had been noticing for nine hundred years, swallowing thin stolen food and burning dimmer and dimmer, keeping the seals shut on fumes and stubbornness, waiting—always waiting—for someone to remember the original terms.

"Freely given," the dragon said, as the visions faded and the ember-light settled back to its long, slow pulse. "Time. Years. Life, willingly poured. Not souls—that was the thieves' shortcut, and look what it bought them. A soul freely given would work, yes, but it's a clumsy instrument. What I starve for is simpler and harder." The ancient eye fixed on Ulyana, and there was no menace in it at all, which was somehow worse. "There is a witch standing in front of me carrying twenty years of hidden life inside her like a jar of preserved summer. I have smelled them since you walked in. Every hour you spent unseen, unfound, unsung—hoarded, not wasted. Hoarded time is the richest food there is."

The hoard was very quiet. Brenna's hand found Ulyana's and gripped it hard.

"You're asking her to—" Brenna started.

"I'm asking nothing." The dragon's voice was infinitely gentle. "Asking is what thieves do politely. I am only telling you the truth, which is more than this court has ever done: the fire can live again. The seals can hold for another age. But the bill has come due, and it must be paid *willingly*, and I will not pretend otherwise, and I will not pretend there is an easy version." The great eye blinked, slow as a season turning. "Think on it. I have waited nine hundred years. I can wait a little longer for an honest answer."

Ulyana stood in the drifting ember-light, her sister's hand crushing hers, Aldous's eyes on her face full of a fear he was trying valiantly to hide, and twenty years of invisible mornings and unseen kindnesses and small safe days rising in her like a tide.

Somewhere above them, the seals groaned in their sleep.

She opened her mouth to speak.
