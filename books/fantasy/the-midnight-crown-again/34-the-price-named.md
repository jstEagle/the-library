## 34. The Price Named

The dragon's chamber was warm the way a hearth is warm when you've come in half-frozen—except that this hearth was the size of a cathedral and breathing.

Ulyana stood at the edge of the hoard and tried to understand what she was looking at. Not gold. Not coin or crowns or cups. Years. Poured out in glittering drifts like sand from an hourglass the size of a hill: bottled decades cracked open and spilled, the spent lives of centuries of courtiers who had fed themselves to their own machine. And in the middle of it, dim as a banked coal, lay Ember-that-Waits.

Up close, the dragon was not the vast winged terror of the old woodcuts. Up close, the dragon was *thin*. Scales that should have gleamed like forge-iron had gone gray as ash. The great ribs rose and fell in a rhythm too slow, too shallow—the breath of something conserving its last strength for a job it could not put down.

"You're starving," Ulyana said quietly. "You've been starving for three hundred years."

The dragon opened one eye. It was the color of cooling lava—not the fierce orange of living fire but the deep sullen red of something going out—and in it Ulyana saw herself reflected, small and absurd, a woman in a borrowed coven cloak standing on a mountain of dead time.

*And yet the seals hold,* said a voice that was not sound so much as warmth pressed against her thoughts. It had a texture to it, dry and patient, like pages of a very old book turning. *Three hundred years I have held them. Do you know what holds a door shut, little witch? Weight. Only weight.*

"I'm sorry," Ulyana said, and meant it, and felt how useless the words were.

*Sorry.* Ember-that-Waits made a sound that might have been laughter if laughter were made of embers settling. *The queens were never sorry. They were efficient. Mavrosa's grandmother fed me forty souls on her coronation night and called it mercy, because the alternative was letting the things below wake up and eat everyone at once. Efficiency. That is what they called feeding children to a furnace so the house stays warm.*

Ulyana's throat tightened. She thought of the cellar of jars. Of Aldous—Corin—labeled in careful courtier script, his true name inked beside him like a shipping mark: *Aldous Vane. Paid in full.* Somewhere above them, Ilse Vane was holding Warden soldiers at bay at the mountain's throat, having turned her whole life inside out in the space of one look at a jar. Somewhere behind her, Seraiva sat on a stolen throne she didn't want, trying to build a court out of the word *consent*.

And here she was, having tea with the furnace.

"Tell me what you need," she said. "Honestly. Not what the queens told you to need. What you actually need."

The dragon's eye regarded her for a long moment. When the warmth-voice came again, it was quieter. Careful. As though it had not been asked an honest question in a very long time and did not quite trust the experience.

*Hunger is simple,* Ember-that-Waits said. *I burn. To burn I must be fed. But there is feeding and there is feeding. Souls taken by force are ash before they reach me—all the heat torn out in the taking, all the screaming. I have been eating smoke for three hundred years and calling it bread. What I need is what no queen ever offered because it cannot be seized. A soul given. Freely. Knowing the price.*

"How much?" Ulyana asked. "How much soul?"

*All of it,* the dragon said simply. *Or enough of it that what remains cannot tell the difference. There is no partial payment for a fire this old. Half a loaf does not warm a city.*

---

She climbed down from the hoard on legs that felt borrowed from someone else and found Brenna waiting at the mouth of the tunnel, arms crossed, jaw set in the particular way that meant she'd already done the arithmetic.

"No," Brenna said, before Ulyana had said anything at all.

"You don't know what I was going to say."

"Twenty years of sisterhood, Ulyana. I know exactly what you were going to say." Brenna pushed off the wall and came toward her, and up close Ulyana could see she'd been crying, which she would deny under torture. "You were going to say you'll do it. You were going to say it in that calm little market-stall voice you use when you're about to do something completely insane."

"That voice has kept me alive for two decades," Ulyana said. "Show some respect."

"It kept you *hidden*. Those aren't the same thing." Brenna caught her by both shoulders. Her grip was strong; coven work did that. "Listen to me. There are four hundred witches in Thornhollow. There are bound souls in this court who'd leap at the chance—"

"To give themselves entirely? To be erased? That's not sacrifice, Brenna, that's just dying with better manners." Ulyana gently pulled free. "The dragon said it plainly. It needs a soul freely given. Not a soul thrown away out of despair. Not a soldier's duty-death. Given." She looked back toward the hoard, where the dim red glow breathed like a sleeping animal. "And it has to be *strong*. Unbound. Whole. Do you know how rare that is? Everyone in this mountain is either sold, bound, or broken. Except—" 

"Don't."

"—the woman who refused the rite," Ulyana finished softly. "The woman who spent twenty years keeping herself whole without knowing why. Turns out I wasn't hiding, Brenna. I was *saving up*."

Brenna stared at her. Then she laughed—a short, awful, wet-edged laugh. "Of course. Of course. Twenty years of running, twenty years of luck-knots and fever-charms and being nobody, and it was all leading here. The Ashen Hours wrote it a century ago and we're all just *walking through our lines*, aren't we? Is that what prophecy is? Just fate with good penmanship?"

"I've been asking myself that since chapter three," Ulyana said, and then, because Brenna looked like she might actually strike her: "Sorry. Gallows humor. It's a coping mechanism. I have many."

"You have *one*, and it's insufferable." Brenna wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, like swatting a fly. "There has to be another way. Ysolde is searching the oldest vaults. Seraiva swore the court's founding records—"

"Seraiva also swore the court ran on consent now, and she's only been queen for six hours." Ulyana took her sister-apprentice's hands instead of her shoulders, which changed the shape of the argument somehow, made it smaller and more human. "Brenna. If the fire goes out, everything below wakes up. Everything. And then it comes up here, and then it goes to Cinderhollow, and the last thing I will ever have done with my life is hide while it happened. I've hidden enough for one lifetime. I'm owed exactly none of it back."

"That's not—" Brenna's voice cracked. "That's not how debts work, you idiot. You don't get to decide you're paid up. That's what family is *for*. We argue."

"We're arguing."

"No, *you're* monologuing nobly and I'm supposed to be moved by it. This isn't arguing. Arguing requires two people with viable positions." Brenna squeezed her hands hard enough to hurt, which Ulyana understood was Brenna's way of hugging. "One more day. Give me one day to tear this court apart for an alternative. Promise me."

Ulyana looked at her—at the girl she'd left crying at the gates twenty years ago, now grown into a woman who reported her to save her and lied to protect her and loved her in a dialect made entirely of criticism—and found she couldn't lie to her anymore. It was a strange feeling. She rather liked it.

"One day," she said. "But Brenna—if the day ends and there's nothing—"

"There'll be nothing," Brenna said flatly. "Because I'll find something. Go tell your man. He deserves to hear it from you before he hears the walls gossiping about it. This entire mountain is one enormous ear."

---

Aldous was waiting in the cellars—or what had been the cellars. The soul-market had gone strange in the hours since Mavrosa fell. Courtiers drifted through the racks of bottles like people wandering a house after a funeral, touching things, unsure whether to pack or grieve. Some of the freed souls had already been sent up the lava-tunnels toward daylight; others waited, labeled and shelved, for names to be read out. Seraiva had ordered every jar catalogued by morning. *No one goes home unidentified again,* she'd said, and her voice had shaken only once.

Aldous had appointed himself to the work. He stood among the shelves with a ledger, reading labels aloud in a low voice, and Ulyana watched him for a moment before he noticed her—watched a man who had spent thirty years as a stranger to himself give strangers back their names, one bottle at a time, with the patience of someone handling eggs.

He looked up. Smiled. And Ulyana felt the thing she always felt now, the new thing, the terrifying thing: that this face was not a stranger's face. It was the face she'd fallen for across a market stall over a charm against forgetting, and it was also a boy's face from a mother's stories, and it was *his*, whichever name he ended up choosing, and she loved all of its versions.

"You have your dragon-face on," he said.

"My what?"

"The face you wore coming back from the hoard the first time. Like you'd seen something beautiful and terrible and were deciding which one to tell me it was." He set down the ledger. "Which is it this time?"

Ulyana opened her mouth to deploy the market-stall voice—the calm patter, the deflection, the joke first and truth later, maybe never—and heard Brenna's words in her head: *that isn't arguing, that's monologuing.*

So instead she walked over, sat down on an empty crate beside him, and said: "It wants a soul. Freely given, entirely. And mine is apparently the strongest one left, because I was rude enough to spend twenty years refusing to be eaten by anybody."

The silence that followed was long. Aldous didn't gasp or grab her or do any of the dramatic things she'd braced for. He just went very still, the way he did, the hollowed-out quiet that she'd once mistaken for emptiness and now understood was depth.

"Okay," he said finally.

"*Okay?*"

"Okay. Let's talk about it. Because I've been doing sums down here too, and I want to compare figures." He turned to face her fully, and his eyes—they were ordinary brown eyes, she'd always thought, but they saw her with a steadiness no one else's ever had—were fierce. "First sum: 'strongest soul left.' Says who?"

"Says a dragon older than the Magistrate."

"Says a starving creature three days from collapse, whose judgment of 'strongest' may be a judgment of 'nearest.'" His voice stayed gentle even as the logic sharpened. "Second sum: you gave twenty years to invisibility. Fine. Terrible trade, I'm still angry about it on your behalf. But those years taught you to survive *without* spending yourself. That's not strength, love. That's thrift. Third sum—" and here his voice did waver, just slightly, and he let it, which she knew cost him— "third sum: I have spent my entire life missing a piece of myself. I know precisely what it is to be a fraction of a person. I will not stand in this cellar and help you become one."

"Aldous—"

"And fourth sum." He took her hands. His were warm. They were always warm, and she had once thought that was odd for a man with no soul, and now she understood the warmth had leaked upward from somewhere, from whatever stubborn remainder of him had refused the theft. "Fourth sum: the prophecy named us both. Both, Ulyana. The witch who fled *and* the man whose soul was sold. I am not the plot device in your story. Whatever the ending is, it has my name in it too."

Ulyana laughed despite everything—a real laugh, startled out of her—and leaned her forehead against his. "When did you get so good at arguing?"

"When I got my memories back, I inherited my mother's temper and my father's stubbornness and absolutely no sense of proportion between them. It's very inconvenient." He kissed her knuckles. "We have until the fire gutters. Days, maybe. Let's not spend them pre-writing your elegy."

Above them, faint through leagues of stone, came a sound like a vast slow heart skipping a beat. Both of them went quiet. Both of them felt it in their teeth: the fire, breathing shallower.

Then boots on stone—quick ones—and Brenna appeared at the cellar door with Mother Ysolde behind her, and Ulyana knew before anyone spoke, because Brenna had found something and Ysolde's ancient face wore the expression of a woman carrying news she wished she could drop down a well.

"You said one day," Brenna began. "We didn't need it. We found the founding records, and there *is* another way—there's a verse in the first covenant, something about the fire accepting a *partial* gift if—"

"If what?" Ulyana asked.

Ysolde answered, and her old voice was gentle as falling ash. "If the gift comes from a soul that has already chosen, publicly and by true name, whom it belongs to. The fire honors love, child. It always has. That was the one thing the queens never understood, and the reason their offerings fed it so poorly." She paused. "But there is a condition. There is always a condition. The declaration must be witnessed by the fire itself, at the hoard, with both souls present—and the one who is claimed must be able to claim you back, freely, or the gift means nothing."

Ulyana turned to Aldous. Aldous turned to Ulyana. And somewhere far below them, the dying dragon exhaled a thread of smoke that curled up through the dark like a question mark.

"Well," said Ulyana, "it's a good thing I rehearsed a speech."

"It's a good thing," Aldous said, "that I've decided to interrupt it."
