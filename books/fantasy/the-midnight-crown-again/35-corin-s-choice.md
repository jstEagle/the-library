## 35. Corin's Choice

The soul went back into him the way rain goes into dry ground—slowly at first, then all at once, and then there was no telling where the boundary had been.

Aldous Vane opened his eyes in a cellar of bottled lives and knew his own name.

It was not *Corin*. That was the first thing. The name arrived like a key turning in a lock he'd forgotten he owned: *Aldous Merrick Vane, born on the longest night of a hard winter, son of Marta and Josiah Vane of Cinderhollow's Tanner's Row, sold before he drew breath.* Nineteen years of memories poured in behind it—the smell of his mother's bread, the sound of his father coughing through the last winter, a sister with sharp eyes and a sharper silence, a dog named Bristle who had loved him without reservation and died without him.

He lay on cold stone with Ulyana kneeling over him, her hands still glowing faintly from the unsealing, her face wet and lit from below by a hundred jars of other people's stolen years.

"Say something," she whispered.

"I had a dog," Aldous said, because that was what came out. "Bristle. He was mostly ears." His voice cracked in the middle of it, an old crack, a nineteen-year-old crack, and he found to his astonishment that he was laughing and weeping in the same breath, which was apparently something a whole person could do. "Ulyana. I remember everything."

She made a sound that wasn't quite a word and folded around him, and for a long while the cellar of souls held nothing but two people holding each other among the shelves, like the world's saddest and happiest library.

* * *

Later—hours later, or minutes; time down here moved like syrup—they sat against the wall between two racks of bottled lives, and Aldous turned his hands over and looked at them as if they belonged to a stranger, which, until this morning, they had.

"Do you know what I remember most?" he said.

"The dog?"

"Bristle is high on the list," he admitted. "But no. It's smaller than that. I remember being four years old, sitting on my mother's kitchen counter while she kneaded dough, and she let me press one finger into it, just one, and she said—" He stopped. Swallowed. "She said, *that's yours now, Aldie. Whatever you touch, you make your own.*"

Ulyana was very quiet beside him.

"And then they took it," he said. "All of it. Before I could even walk, someone looked at my life and put a price on it, and my family paid their debt with me, and I spent nineteen years walking around in a body with a hole where I should have been. Filling the hours. Tasting ash every dawn. Being *Corin*, whoever that even was—a name I picked up like a coat off a fence post because I needed something to answer to."

"You were still you," Ulyana said gently. "Even hollowed out. I knew you three days before I ever saw your name in that book."

"That may be the kindest thing anyone has ever said to me," Aldous said, "and I need you to know that I am going to ruin it completely in about a minute."

Her brow creased. "Aldous—"

"Because I've been thinking," he said. "Down here. In the dark. With nothing to do but think, which as you know is my least favorite activity, ranking just below dancing and slightly above being set on fire. And I've been listening, too. To the courtiers. To the dragon's breathing. And I've done the arithmetic, Ulyana, and I hate the arithmetic, but somebody has to do it."

"Aldous." Her voice had gone flat and careful, the way it did when she was frightened and refusing to show it. "Whatever you're building up to—"

"It's you," he said. "Isn't it? What Mavrosa wants. What the fire needs. A soul freely given, and yours is the strongest one left, and everyone in this miserable mountain has been looking at you all day like you're a loaf of bread and they haven't eaten in a week."

"They haven't asked me yet," she said.

"They will," he said. "And when they do, you're going to say yes, because you are the single most infuriating person I have ever met, and I say that with tremendous love. You'll do the sums yourself. You'll decide you're worth less than the world, because that's what twenty years of hiding teaches a person—that they're safest when they cost nothing. Well." He stood up. His legs shook and he stood anyway. "I'm not letting you pay."

Ulyana rose too, slowly, and faced him down in the jar-light. She was half a head shorter and had been running on terror and tea for days, and she still managed to look like the immovable object in any argument.

"Aldous," she said. "I love you. But the fire doesn't take arguments."

"No," he agreed. "It takes souls. Which brings me to my point, which I warned you I'd ruin the moment for." He drew a breath that hurt all the way down. "The fire already took mine. Nineteen years of it. It drank me before I had teeth. Every dream I can't remember, every birthday, my father's funeral—I wasn't there, Ulyana, I was *here*, or some sliver of me was, leaking into that hoard like water through a cracked jug. The fire owes *me*. It has been in arrears since before I was born, and I'll be damned if I stand by and watch it collect a second debt from the woman I love just because she happens to be standing closer."

"That's not how it works—"

"Then we'll find how it works!" His voice rang off the jars and came back at them. "You told me yourself what Ember showed you. The queens fed it souls for centuries because feeding it was easier than asking what it actually needed. Easier than changing anything. Well, I have spent my entire existence as the easy answer to somebody else's problem, and I am *done*. If that dragon needs a soul so badly, it can start by giving mine back with interest."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere far above, muffled by stone, came the deep slow pulse of the fire itself—a heartbeat with a cough in it.

Ulyana reached out and took his hand. Hers was rough with charm-work and shaking only slightly.

"You beautiful, stubborn man," she said. "That's not a plan. That's a grievance with scenery."

"I've been told my grievances have excellent views," Aldous said, and she laughed despite herself, wet and startled, and pressed her forehead against his chest, and he held her among the stolen years and thought: *whatever it costs, not her. Not her.*

* * *

They brought word up through Seraiva, who had taken the throne's antechamber like a woman trying on an old coat and finding it fit worse than remembered. She listened to Aldous's demand with an expression of polite fascination, the way one listens to a child explain that the moon should be delivered to the doorstep.

"The fire does not negotiate," Seraiva said. "It endures. There is a difference, and the difference is measured in centuries."

"Then it can endure a conversation," Aldous said. "Everything else in this mountain talks. The queens talk. The seals talk—I heard them singing through the walls last night, did you know they sing? The courtiers certainly never stop talking. Only the fire is mute, and only because everyone decided for it that it should be."

Seraiva studied him for a long moment, and something shifted behind her eyes—not warmth exactly, but recognition. "You argue like my grandmother," she said finally. "She argued with the mountain itself, once. The mountain won, but it respected her afterward." She turned to Ulyana. "Your mate is either a fool or a marvel."

"He's both," Ulyana said. "It's his best quality."

"Mm. Then hear the shape of the thing, marvel, and tell me if your arithmetic improves." Seraiva spread her hands, and pale light gathered above her palms, showing shapes: a dragon coiled on its hoard, dim as banked coals; the seals beneath it, thin as old ice. "Ember-that-Waits is not hungry the way a wolf is hungry. It is hungry the way a lamp is hungry. It burns fuel. For six hundred years the fuel has been stolen souls, and stolen souls burn fast and dirty, which is why the fire gutters and why the seals wear thin. A given soul—freely offered—burns clean and long. One such soul could hold the seals for a generation. Yours, witch, unbound and hidden and *chosen*, would burn longer than any soul collected in the history of this court."

"And his?" Aldous said. "Restored, returned, freely his own again? What does his burn like?"

Seraiva paused. "No one has ever asked," she said slowly. "Souls are taken, or bargained away by others. No one has ever stood in front of the fire and *offered terms*."

"Someone has to be first," Aldous said. "Might as well be the man with nineteen years of back interest owed to it."

* * *

Mother Ysolde, when they climbed back up to the coven's camp at the mountain's root, heard the whole scheme out with her chin propped on her knuckles and her ancient eyes closed, as though listening to a song rather than a plan.

When Aldous finished, she was silent for so long that Ulyana started to speak, and the old woman raised one finger without opening her eyes.

"Hm," Ysolde said at last. "Hm hm hm." She opened her eyes and fixed them on Aldous. "Boy. You want to walk up to a starving god and invoice it."

"When you put it that way it sounds ridiculous," Aldous said.

"It *is* ridiculous," Ysolde said. "Which is why it might work. Fire-magic is older than sense, child. The first witches didn't command flame—they *bargained* with it, gave it wood and got warmth, gave it grief and got light. The theft began later. Theft is always later." She pushed herself upright with a groan like a ship changing course. "A soul that comes to the fire demanding repayment instead of begging mercy—that is a thing the fire has not tasted since the first crowning. It may refuse him. It may eat him out of spite. Or it may listen."

"And if it eats him?" Ulyana said sharply.

Ysolde shrugged. "Then we're no worse off than if it ate you, except sadder, and better-looking in mourning."

"That's *awful*, Mother Ysolde."

"I'm four hundred years old, girl. Everything I say is either awful or obvious, and usually both." But her hand, when it settled on Ulyana's shoulder, was gentle. "Let the boy try. Love that only flows one direction isn't love—it's charity. Let him spend himself on you a little. It's the only way he'll ever believe he's allowed."

Aldous felt something go through him at that, quiet and enormous, and caught Ulyana watching him with an expression he was learning to read: tenderness with the safety catch off.

* * *

That night—or what passed for night beneath a mountain—he couldn't sleep. Ulyana could, curled against his side with the boneless trust of someone who had spent twenty years sleeping with one eye open and had finally, briefly, set it down. He lay awake and let the memories move through him like fish through reeds.

His sister's face. Ilse, nine years old, solemn as a magistrate, teaching him to whistle through a blade of grass. *Ilse,* who had grown up into Sergeant Vane, who had come down the mountain with Warden steel intending to burn this place and had seen a jar with a name on it and turned her sword around mid-swing. He hadn't spoken to her since. There had been no time, and also—he admitted it to the dark—no courage. What did you say to the sister whose childhood debt had been paid with your soul? *Sorry you sold me* seemed insufficient. *Thank you for the rescue* seemed grotesque.

He was still turning it over when he heard footsteps, and Ilse herself appeared at the edge of the firelight, armor off, hair loose, holding two tin cups of something steaming.

"You're awake," she said.

"I'm told that's typical of me. Was. Will be again." He sat up carefully, disentangling from Ulyana, who muttered something about luck-knots in her sleep and did not wake.

Ilse handed him a cup and lowered herself onto the stone beside him, stiff, as if the sitting were a formal act. They drank in silence for a moment. The stuff was terrible—coven herb-brew, tasting of pine and penance.

"I remember you," Aldous said quietly. "All of it. The grass whistle. You used to make me be the audience while you practiced speeches arresting imaginary criminals."

"You were a terrible audience. You always applauded too early." Her mouth twitched, and then stopped twitching, because Ilse Vane's face had been trained out of softness by harder tutors than he'd ever known. "Aldous. When you go up to that fire—"

"If it takes me—"

"Don't." The word came out like a blade leaving a sheath. "Don't apologize to me. Don't thank me. Just—" She stared into her cup. "Just come back. I carried you for nineteen years without knowing I was carrying you. I'd like to find out what it's like to carry something that answers back."

Aldous set down his cup and leaned his shoulder against hers, the way he suddenly remembered doing when they were children on the porch steps, watching storms come in off the ash country.

"I'll come back," he said. "I owe the fire a bill, and I intend to collect it in person."

* * *

They went down at dawn—which was to say, at the hour the court's pale lamps were trimmed, because the sun had never once reached this deep—and Ember-that-Waits filled the cavern ahead of them like weather. Up close, the great dragon was worse and better than the stories: scales gone dull as old coins, wings folded like a tent fallen in on itself, one eye opening to track them across the floor with the weary patience of a lighthouse keeper counting ships.

Behind it, the hoard of spent years glittered and guttered. Above it, the seals hung in the dark like veins of frozen lightning, thin now, humming their thin song.

Ulyana's hand found his. Her fingers were ice.

"You don't have to do this," she murmured. "One word and we run. I'm very good at running. Twenty years of practice."

"I know," Aldous said. "And I love that about you, and I'm not running." He squeezed her fingers once and let go, and stepped forward alone onto the black glass floor, and lifted his voice—a small human voice in a vast stone throat, and it shook, and he let it shake, because Ysolde had told him that fire trusts what trembles honestly more than what stands proud.

"Ember-that-Waits!" he called. "My name is Aldous Merrick Vane. You drank nineteen years of me before I could walk, and nobody asked my leave. I've come to settle accounts!"

The great eye widened, fractionally. The fire in the cavern's heart—the true fire, the one that was the dragon and was more than the dragon—flared once, dim orange, attentive.

"You want a soul freely given?" Aldous said, and his voice steadied as he went, gathering heat of its own. "Fine. Here are my terms. You will take no more stolen years—you'll burn clean or not at all. You will release every soul in this mountain that was taken without consent, starting with the ones in that cellar. And you will keep your seals up for a hundred years on the strength of what I offer now—my restored self, my whole memory, my choice, freely made—if, and only if, you give me back the years you stole. All nineteen. I don't want them to *keep*. I want to live them forward. Consider it restitution."

Silence, vast as the cavern. Behind him he heard Ulyana stop breathing.

Then Ember-that-Waits laughed.

It was not a sound any of them were prepared for—a sound like a hearth catching after a wet week, like ten thousand kettles agreeing, like the mountain clearing its throat after six hundred years of holding its tongue. The dim eye kindled. The dull scales flushed, faintly, with ember-light, spreading outward from the heart like dawn coming up wrong-way-round through stone.

"*Little debtor,*" said the fire, in a voice that was every hearth Ulyana had ever warmed her hands over, "*you are the first creature in six centuries to address me as anything but a furnace or a god.*"

"Get used to it," Aldous said, heart slamming, grinning like a fool. "There's more where that came from."

The fire laughed again, and the seals overhead brightened, and Ulyana's hand found his once more, gripping hard enough to leave marks he hoped he'd keep forever.

"Terms accepted," said Ember-that-Waits, "*conditionally.* Come closer, Aldous Merrick Vane. There is a formality. There is always a formality—and I fear you will not like what it asks of the witch who loves you."

Behind him, Ulyana went very still.
