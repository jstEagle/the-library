## 36. The Giving

The dragon's breath smelled like the last coal of a hearth on a winter morning—the kind you huddle over because you know it's going out and there's nothing to be done about it except be near it while it lasts.

Ulyana knelt on the hoard of spent years, her knees sinking into coins that were not coins, into bottles that were not bottles, into all the pale glittering sediment of centuries of taken lives. Ember-that-Waits lay before her with its head lowered, so that one vast eye—amber going gray at the rim, like tea left too long in the pot—could consider her at close range.

*You smell of hiding,* the dragon had said, an hour ago, in a voice that came less through her ears than through her ribs. *Twenty years of it. It has soaked into you like smoke into wool.*

She had not argued. Arguing with a creature the size of a cathedral seemed unwise, and anyway the dragon was right.

Now she stood at the edge of the fire-pit where Ember-that-Waits curled around its own dwindling light, and Aldous gripped her hand hard enough that she could feel his pulse—or what passed for pulse in a man three days restored to his own soul. He was still learning how to be inhabited again. Sometimes he flinched at sudden sounds, as though his skin were a coat he'd only just gotten back and hadn't yet found all the buttons.

"Don't," he said. That was the whole sentence. He'd been saying versions of it since the throne room fell: *don't, don't, don't.* "There has to be another way. There's always another way. I've spent my whole life being someone else's way."

"I know." She turned her hand in his so their fingers laced properly. His palm was warm now. Three days ago it had been cool as river stone, and she had hated it without knowing why. "That's exactly why it has to be me. Everyone else's way is taking from someone who didn't choose it. I'm choosing."

"Choosing isn't the same as surviving, Ulyana."

"No," she agreed. "But it's the same as living. And I've only just started doing that."

Above them, on a ledge of black glass, Seraiva watched with her ruined crown tilted at an angle that suggested she was trying very hard to look bored and failing. The exiled queen's hands were folded. Her eyes were not dry.

"The seals are already singing," Seraiva said quietly. "You can hear it if you listen. The mountain is holding its breath."

Ulyana listened. Beneath the groan of cooling stone, beneath the whisper of the hoard shifting, there it was—a thin high note, like a wineglass about to break. The starving things beyond the seals had begun to sing back.

"All right," she said to no one in particular. "All right."

* * *

Mother Ysolde had told her once—it felt like a hundred years ago, though it was barely two months—that fire magic was never really about fire.

"It's about *naming what you burn,*" the old woman had rasped, poking the coven's kitchen hearth with an iron rod older than the Magistrate. "Amateurs throw power at flame and wonder why it eats them. Fire keeps accounts, girl. You give it something true, it gives you warmth. You give it a lie, it gives you ash. You give it yourself—" she'd shrugged, "—well. Best know which part of yourself first."

Ulyana closed her eyes and took inventory.

Her soul was hers; that much was settled, that much Mavrosa had raged over in the last hour of her reign—that Ulyana Pemberton was *unbound, unbought, unspent,* the rarest coin in the court below, and worth more than the whole hoard. But a soul was a large thing, unwieldy, and she did not intend to hand over the whole of it. She intended to be precise.

Because here was what twenty years of hiding had actually been, when she laid it out honestly: not one great wound but ten thousand small ones. The charm-songs sung under her breath at the night market so no Warden would look twice. The false name given to the baker's wife. The birthday she'd celebrated alone in her rented room with a candle she couldn't even wish on. The way she'd learned to laugh one note too quietly. The years of being *the woman at the stall,* loved by everyone and known by no one—not Brenna, not the market family, not herself.

Every year of it had a weight. Every year had a taste. And every single one of them belonged to her, freely given or freely kept, and she would choose which ones went into the fire.

"You're smiling," Aldous said suspiciously.

"I'm pricing myself," she said. "It's harder than it looks. Do you know what I charged for luck-knots? Four coppers. Four! Twenty years of craft and courage at four coppers a knot, and I *undercharged.*"

His laugh cracked in the middle and turned into something else, and he pulled her against his chest and held on. She felt his heart doing its new-old work, steady and astonished at itself.

"If this takes your memories of me," he said into her hair, "I want you to know that I will simply tell you everything. Every day. I'll be insufferable. I'll start with the tea. I'll do voices."

"You do terrible voices."

"They're getting better."

"They're really not." She stepped back, and looked up at him, and let herself memorize him—not with magic, just with the ordinary human machinery of love, which turned out to be the strongest enchantment she owned. Gray eyes, ink-stained fingers, a small scar through one eyebrow he still couldn't explain because the memory of earning it had been sold before he was born. "Whatever the fire takes," she said, "you'll tell me the rest. That's the bargain. Not with the dragon. With you."

"With me," he said. His voice held. Barely.

She turned to the dragon.

* * *

Ember-that-Waits opened one eye fully when she approached, and the gray rim of it flickered, hopeful and ashamed at once. A starved animal looks at food that way. So does an old friend who knows they've eaten too much of your bread.

*I do not wish to take from you,* the dragon said. Its thought-voice was quieter now, frayed. *I have taken so long that taking is all I remember how to do.*

"That's why I'm not letting you take," Ulyana said. "I'm giving. There's a difference. My mentor used to say—" she stopped. Blinked. "Huh. Mother Ysolde said it. Or maybe I read it somewhere. Anyway: fire keeps accounts. So let's keep them properly."

She knelt at the edge of the ember-bed, close enough that heat pressed against her face like a huge slow hand, and she began to work.

The magic rose in her the way it had by the river, when Sergeant Vane's crossbow bolts had come out of the dark and something twenty years asleep had answered in her blood—but gentler now, deliberate, a door opening instead of a wall coming down. She gathered the years the way she'd once gathered charms off her stall at closing time, counting them, naming them.

*This is the year I learned to walk softly on the third stair so the landlady wouldn't wake.*

*This is the winter I sold fever-charms during the coughing sickness and saved eleven people whose names I never learned.*

*These are the four thousand mornings I woke alone and made tea for one and called that freedom until I almost believed it.*

Each year came up out of her glowing, a small bright knot of time, and she held them in cupped hands that shouldn't have been able to hold years at all, and she fed them one by one into the dying fire.

It was not painless. She had expected pain and got something stranger: each year left the way a word leaves your mouth after you've said it—you could remember having said it, but not the shape of saying. The first year went and she gasped, because suddenly there was a hole in her twenties shaped like a market stall, and she knew she had run a market stall, knew it the way you know a fact from a book, and the *living* of it was gone.

The fifth year took the faces of the market family. Marta with her honey stall. Old Piet who cheated at dice and lost gracefully. The children who bought luck-knots with stolen coppers and thought she didn't notice. She knew their names. She could no longer see their faces, and the grief of that was enormous and clean, like a bell struck once.

The twelfth year took the sound of her own laughter as it used to be, careful and quiet, and she found she didn't mourn that one much.

Aldous stayed beside her the whole time, kneeling in the heat, saying names aloud whenever her face went blank—*Marta, Piet, the blue awning, the rain barrel, the night we met, you refused to sell me a charm, you had flour on your sleeve—* feeding her own life back to her in fragments, a man bailing out a boat with his cupped hands.

And the fire grew.

Slowly at first—a coal remembering it had once been a tree. Then faster. The gray rim of the dragon's eye burned away to gold, then to white-gold, then to a brightness that made Seraiva shield her face on her ledge and the very rock of the mountain begin, faintly, to hum. Heat rolled across the hoard and the bottled souls of the freed prisoners, stacked in crates awaiting transport to the surface, caught the light and glowed like lanterns at a festival.

*More,* the dragon whispered—and then, horrified at itself: *Forgive me. No. No more. You have given enough—*

"One more," Ulyana said. Her voice came out strange, young and old at once. Because she had counted, in the beginning, twenty years of hiding, and she had fed the fire nineteen, and there was one left—the last year, the year of the book and the tea and the crow-delivered letter and the road through the ash country and the inn without mirrors and the river and the descent and him.

The best year of her life.

She held it up. It blazed between her palms like a captured star, and she found she was crying without any particular sorrow, the way you cry at weddings.

"This one I give glad," she told the dragon. "Not hidden at all. Spent wide awake, in front of witnesses. Take it and *burn,* old one."

She let it go.

The world went white.

* * *

When her sight returned, she was flat on her back on warm stone, and the cavern was full of light the color of dawn, and above her—filling the cavern, wings half-spread in impossible joy—Ember-that-Waits burned.

The dragon was *young.* Not in body—the vast scarred frame was the same—but in the fire of it, in the way the light poured off every scale, in the great trumpeting cry that shook dust from the ceiling and sent echoes rolling down through the sealed deeps like a promise. The seals sang back, loud and strong and shut. Somewhere far below, the starving things fell silent, listening to a fire they had hoped was a rumor.

Seraiva slid down the glass ledge with more enthusiasm than dignity and stood staring, her mouth open, her ruined crown crooked, looking—for the first time since Ulyana had met her—entirely human.

"It worked," the queen breathed. "Twenty years. One witch's hiding, and it worked. Mavrosa fed it thousands of souls a century and it *starved,* and you—" she laughed, a wild disbelieving sound. "You gave it *boredom.* Twenty years of small safe invisible life, and it burned like a coronation. Why? How?"

Ulyana, still on her back, considered the question with the seriousness it deserved.

"Because they weren't wasted," she said. "They were *kept.* Every year I hid, I chose kindness anyway. Small kindnesses, secret ones, four coppers at a time. Nobody saw them. They were real anyway." She sat up slowly. The world felt lighter than it should—there were gaps in her where furniture used to be, rooms missing walls, and she'd have to go through them all later, with him, with patience. "Fire keeps accounts. Apparently it pays interest on honest ones."

Aldous knelt beside her and took her face in his ink-stained hands and searched her eyes with a terror he wasn't bothering to hide.

"Do you know me?" he asked.

And she did—she knew the shape of him, the scar, the terrible voices, the tea he'd ordered on the first night—but the *first night itself* was gone, dissolved into gold light along with nineteen other years, and she grieved it even as she smiled.

"I know you now," she said. "Tell me the rest. Start with the tea."

"You ordered *me* tea, technically. You thrust it at me and said, 'Drink, you look haunted,' and then you pretended not to watch whether I did." His voice wobbled. "You had flour on your sleeve. You always have flour on your sleeve. I don't think you've ever once noticed."

"I notice now." She touched his wrist. "Aldous."

"Aldous," he repeated, wonderingly, as if hearing it in her mouth for the first time. "Say it again sometime when we're not lying in a dragon's bedroom."

"Promises, promises."

Above them, Ember-that-Waits lowered its blazing head, and its voice, when it came, filled their bones with warmth instead of dread.

*Witch-who-fled, witch-who-came-back. Your years burn well in me. I will carry them the way mountains carry rivers—every day, without dropping one. And I owe you a truth in return, freely given, as all debts in this place must now be paid.*

Ulyana looked up into an eye like a sunrise. "What truth?"

*The seals hold. They will hold for a lifetime, perhaps two. But hear the rest of it:* the dragon's gaze moved past her, up toward the distant shaft of gray daylight that led to the surface, to Cinderhollow, to the world above where a new accord was being drafted in ink that still smelled wet. *Your Magistrate's men are coming down the throat of the mountain even now. Led by the sergeant. And she carries something that was yours, and something that was never hers, and she is weeping, and she does not know yet whether she comes as a sword or as a sister.*

Ulyana got to her feet. Her knees held. Nineteen years lighter, she found, weighed almost nothing at all.

"Well," she said, brushing ash from her skirt, feeling—for the first time in her remembered life—no need whatsoever to hide. "Then let's go meet her."
