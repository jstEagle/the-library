## 37. Seals Renewed

The first thing Ulyana felt was the heat.

Not the punishing, greedy heat of the throne room, nor the dry rattle of the lava tunnels, but a warmth like standing near a hearth on the first cold night of autumn—the kind of warmth that seemed to be warming you *on purpose*.

Ember-that-Waits blazed above the great cavern like a second dawn. Fire poured from her in slow ribbons, curling down through channels in the rock that Ulyana now understood had been built for exactly this: rivers of flame running along ancient seams of stone, feeding seals carved so deep into the mountain's bones that they predated every queen, every coven, every name anyone remembered. The seals drank the light and glowed faintly gold, and beneath them—far beneath, deeper than the soul-market, deeper than the cellar where she had found a jar labeled ALDOUS VANE in careful courtier's script—something shifted its weight once, tested the bars of its long imprisonment, and settled back down to wait.

It did not sound angry. It sounded *patient*. Somehow that was worse.

"It holds," Seraiva said beside her.

The deposed queen stood at the edge of Ember's light with her hands folded, watching the fire run through the mountain like a woman watching rain fall on land she'd nearly given up on. Her gown was still ruined from the drowned chapel, her crown nowhere in evidence, and she looked, for the first time since Ulyana had met her, entirely unguarded.

"How long?" Ulyana asked.

"Centuries, if we are wise. Decades, if we are fools." Seraiva turned to look at her, and there was something new in her face—not softness exactly, but the absence of calculation, which for Seraiva amounted to the same thing. "You gave it twenty years. Twenty years of a life. I have watched souls fed to this fire by force for four hundred years, witch, and I never saw one given freely do half so much."

"It wasn't the whole soul," Ulyana said. "Just... the part I wasn't using."

Seraiva made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't gone out of style in the court before Ulyana was born. "Modest to the end. No wonder Mavrosa couldn't hold you. She only ever understood what people wanted. Never what they were willing to lose."

Ulyana looked up at the dragon. Ember-that-Waits hung in the air on wings that were not wings, vast and slow, her scales no longer dull ash but burnished bronze shot through with veins of living red. She had not spoken since taking the gift—had not needed to. But as Ulyana watched, one enormous eye rotated toward her, ancient and amber, and held her gaze for a long moment.

There were words in it. Not spoken ones. Something older: *I remember all twenty years, little thief. Every charm. Every market morning. They were good years. I am sorry they are gone.*

Ulyana pressed her hand flat against her own sternum, where the hollow lived now. It didn't hurt. That was the strangest part. It simply *wasn't*, like a room in a house she'd once owned.

"Thank you," she whispered, and the dragon's eye closed, satisfied.

* * *

Reforming a government, it turned out, took considerably longer than toppling one.

The throne of teeth came apart piece by piece over the following days. Seraiva insisted on it being done publicly, in the great hall, with every courtier assembled—and there were more of them than Ulyana had guessed, pale and elegant and watching from alcoves with expressions ranging from terror to cautious hope. When the last tooth was pried from the obsidian seat and carried out under guard, Seraiva sat down upon the bare black stone and looked out at her people.

"No more bottles," she said simply. "No more ledgers of names. The market is closed. Whatever this court becomes, it will not be a place where lives are bought."

A murmur ran through the hall. One courtier—a thin, silver-haired creature who had spent centuries as the court's chief appraiser of souls—raised a trembling hand.

"And what," he asked carefully, "shall we *do*?"

It was, Ulyana thought later, the most honest question anyone asked during the entire reformation. Because the truth was ugly and simple: the court had been a machine, a terrible machine, but a machine nonetheless, and it had kept the seals fed and the starving things below asleep for longer than human memory. You could not simply smash it and walk away. Someone had to build something in its shape.

So they built. Slowly, clumsily, with arguments that lasted days and compromises that satisfied no one completely—which, Brenna observed, meant the compromises were probably honest.

Souls would no longer be taken; they could only be *given*, and only by adults, and only with the full knowledge of what giving meant, witnessed by three courtiers and recorded in a public book. In exchange, the giver's family would be protected, honored, and—when the fire finally failed ages hence, as all fires must—remembered. It was not a perfect system. Ulyana suspected no system built atop a prison for world-ending horrors ever would be. But when she compared it to the old way—infants sold by desperate families, trackers hunting their own brothers—it felt less like a compromise and more like a small revolution.

"You realize," Aldous said one evening, watching Seraiva argue with the former appraiser about the wording of the consent clause, "that I am technically the first beneficiary of the new order. My soul was returned under the old law. There's no precedent for me."

"There's precedent for everything in this place," Ulyana said. "Four hundred years of it. Most of it dreadful."

"Yes, but I'm going to insist on being cited anyway. *In re: Vane.* It has a nice ring." He was smiling, but his fingers drummed against his knee, and Ulyana had learned to read that drumming. He was still adjusting. A soul returned after thirty-one years did not settle in all at once, like water finding its level. Some mornings he woke reaching for memories that had been someone else's, or flinched from his own reflection, which had recently begun to show him things he didn't expect—a crease between his brows he'd earned rather than inherited, the particular way his mouth went crooked when he was trying not to hope.

"The courtiers keep staring at me," he admitted quietly. "Like I'm a curiosity. The man who came back."

"They stare at me too."

"Yes, but you enjoy being stared at now. You've decided it's your new hobby."

Ulyana laughed—really laughed, the sound surprising her with its ease. He was right. After twenty years of practicing invisibility, being *seen* had turned out to be an intoxication she had no intention of recovering from.

* * *

Ilse Vane stayed three days after the fall of the throne, and then she left without saying goodbye—or so everyone believed until Ulyana found her in the cellar of bottled souls, standing before the shelf where a jar labeled ALDOUS VANE had sat for thirty-one years.

The shelf was empty now. Ilse was looking at the empty space as though she could still see the jar, and perhaps she could. She had her brother's soul in a leather satchel at her hip, wrapped in wool, guarded the way she'd once guarded warrants and writs—with everything she had.

"He used to hum," Ilse said, without turning around. "When he was small. Before. All the time, tuneless, drove our mother mad." Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a sergeant delivering a report. "I don't know if the humming was in the bottle. I haven't opened it yet. I'm afraid that whatever comes out won't know me."

Ulyana came to stand beside her. Below them, the great fire roared softly through the mountain, steady as a heartbeat.

"He'll know you," Ulyana said. "Souls remember what bodies forget. Ask any hedge-witch." She paused. "Or ask me. I lost twenty years of memory and my sister-apprentice still knew me across two decades and a war grudge. Recognition isn't in the head, Ilse. It's lower than that. Deeper."

Ilse was quiet for a long moment. Then, in a voice that cracked precisely once before she wrestled it back under control: "What do I say to him? Thirty-one years. What does anyone *say*?"

"'You're late for supper,'" Ulyana suggested. "'Your soup's gone cold.'"

Something flickered at the corner of the tracker's mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of one.

"I hated you," Ilse said. "On the riverbank. When you did—" she gestured vaguely, the gesture encompassing the escape, the magic, the entire impossible woman beside her. "I'd spent my whole life believing craft was theft. That every witch was just a prettier version of the things in jars down here. And then you burned my warrant out of my hands and I thought, *there it is, proof.*"

"And now?"

"Now I think magic is a tool, and tools don't care who swings them, and I have spent thirty-one years swinging some very sharp tools at very wrong targets because it was easier than asking what actually happened to my brother." She straightened her shoulders—the old parade-ground posture, but wearing it differently now, like a coat taken up voluntarily instead of issued. "The Magistrate will want a report. I intend to give him one. All of it. What's down here. What the seals are. What the fire costs and why it's worth paying."

"They'll call you a traitor."

"They called me diligent for thirty years, and look how much good that did." She picked up the satchel, held it against her ribs like a child. "I'm going home, Pemberton. I'm going to open a bottle, and make soup, and see if my brother hums." At the doorway she stopped, and did not turn around. "If it goes badly—if he doesn't know me—I'm going to come back here and ask your dragon whether souls can be given *back*. So don't leave town."

"I never manage to," Ulyana said, and heard, fading up the stairs, the ghost of that almost-laugh again, freer this time.

* * *

The freed souls went home in ones and twos at first, then in dozens.

Seraiva opened the old pilgrim road through the mountain's shoulder—the one the court had sealed centuries ago, back when the court had decided that what happened below should stay below. Now the gates stood open day and night, lit by torches that burned with Ember's borrowed fire, and the freed dead-and-not-dead walked up through them blinking in daylight many of them hadn't seen since before the Magistracy existed.

Some of them were very old souls indeed. One gentleman in courtier's dress emerged squinting into the sun and announced, with genuine bewilderment, that the last thing he clearly remembered was a harvest festival in the reign of King Aldric the Second, and could someone please tell him whether Cinderhollow had gotten larger or whether he had gotten smaller. (Both, he was told. He took it remarkably well.)

Others were recent. Heartbreakingly recent.

The market prisoners came up on the third day—eleven of them, taken in the Warden raids, including Tam's daughter Pip with her candle-spell and her furious, tear-streaked defiance. Ulyana was waiting at the gates when they emerged, and Pip stopped dead at the sight of her, and for one horrible moment Ulyana braced for the anger she deserved.

Instead Pip threw her arms around her and held on hard enough to bruise.

"You came *down here*," Pip said into her shoulder, muffled and accusing. "You absolute lunatic. Mam said you'd run off with your young man and we'd never see you again, and I told her no, Mrs. Pemberton doesn't run, she just hides—but look at you, you're not hiding at all—"

"No," Ulyana said, holding the girl tightly. "I appear to have misplaced the habit."

They walked up together out of the mountain, the eleven freed prisoners and one former prisoner-of-invisibility, and the daylight on the other side was the plain gray light of a northern afternoon, and it was the most beautiful thing Ulyana had seen since the dragon caught fire.

Her friends from the market were waiting beyond the gates. Of course they were. Tam stood at the front with his arms crossed and his jaw set in the expression he used when negotiating prices, which meant he'd already prepared his remarks and would not be deterred.

"Twenty years," Tam said. "Twenty years I bought luck-knots off you, and fever-charms, and that one time a love-potion you swore was 'just tea,' Ulyana, and you never once—never *once*—told us."

"I told you it was just tea."

"You told me it was just tea *while winking*, which in hindsight raises questions!" But his voice broke on the last word, and his arms uncrossed, and he pulled her into a hug that smelled of charcoal and cinnamon like the market itself. Around them the others crowded in—Old Marta who sold dried fish, the twins from the lamp-stall, quiet Hesper who'd never learned Ulyana's real name and now, apparently, refused to learn anything else. "No," Hesper declared firmly, when Aldous began to introduce himself. "You're Tea Man. That's settled. Mrs. Pemberton and Tea Man. It's got a ring to it."

"I have a name," Aldous protested.

"Not anymore you don't."

Later, sitting on the mountainside with the gates glowing behind them and the freed souls making camp like festival-goers waiting for the road home to clear, Marta lowered herself onto the grass beside Ulyana with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions.

"The girl," Marta said, nodding toward Pip, who was showing Aldous how to skip stones with markedly limited success. "She was in the cells down there. Weeks. I heard what happens in those cells." She was quiet a moment. "You went and got her."

"We all went and got all of them."

"Mm." Marta picked a stem of grass and chewed it. "You know what I keep thinking? All those years you sold me charms and told me they were nothing, just knots and patter, and every winter my fevers broke faster than the neighbors', and my fish never spoiled, and I *knew*, somewhere under my skull, I knew, and I let myself not-know because not-knowing was comfortable." She spat out the grass. "Comfortable's worth less than I priced it at. That's the lesson, I suppose. Cost me a raid and a scare to learn it, and it cost you—" she waved a hand at all of Ulyana, the hollow included, "—considerably more."

"It cost the right amount," Ulyana said. "I'm told that's what a fair price feels like. I wouldn't know personally. I always charged too little."

Marta snorted, and leaned her shoulder against Ulyana's, and together they watched the sun go down behind Cinderhollow's chimneys, far below, smoke rising from ten thousand ordinary fires.

* * *

On the last night before the road home, Brenna found her at the gates.

Her sister-apprentice stood in the torchlight with her High Witch's mantle over her shoulders and her hands jammed in her pockets like an apprentice playing hooky, and for a while neither of them said anything, because they had already said everything, twice, sometimes shouting.

"The elders are staying," Brenna said at last. "Someone has to liaison with the new court, and Mother Ysolde claims the mountain air is 'good for her rheumatism,' which is a lie, she just likes arguing with Seraiva. They argue like sisters, those two. It's horrifying. I can't look away."

"You could stay too."

"I could." Brenna looked out at the dark road winding down toward Cinderhollow, where the lights of the city were coming awake for the evening. "But Thornhollow needs a High Witch who remembers both sides of it. And besides—" her mouth twisted, wry and warm, "—someone has to teach the next generation that hiding is not a strategy, it's just a slower way of disappearing. I intend to be extremely annoying about it. You've inspired a lifetime of lecturing. I hope you're proud."

"Immensely."

Brenna dug in her pocket and produced something small, pressing it into Ulyana's palm: a luck-knot. Sloppily tied. Terrible workmanship, honestly. The kind of knot an apprentice makes on her first day.

"You were better at this than me even at eight years old," Brenna said. "I've resented it for thirty years. Keep it. Consider it back-payment on every fever I faked so you'd nurse me instead of going to lessons."

Ulyana closed her fist around it. The string was cheap and the knot was crooked and she would keep it until the day she died.

"When you get home," Brenna said, and her voice gentled into something almost shy, "write to me. Not coven business. Just—letters. Tell me about the market. Tell me about him. Tell me everything I missed while you were busy not-existing." She paused. "And Ulyana? If you find any of the missing twenty years floating around somewhere—you know. In a dragon's hoard. On a shelf. Send word. I remember them too, some of them. I could lend them back."

"I'll write," Ulyana promised. "Every week. Whether you like it or not."

"That's the spirit. Annoyance as devotion. We Pembertons—" Brenna stopped, corrected herself with a small, real smile. "We Lockridges. We Thorne-sisters. Whatever we are. We do it thoroughly."

They embraced at the gate, and the torchlight of the new court burned steady at their backs, and the road down the mountain waited, long and dark and entirely unhidden, leading to a city that was learning, chimney by chimney, street by street, that the world had changed while it slept.

Ulyana took Aldous's hand, and they started walking.

Behind them, deep in the mountain, the fire roared on—fed now by consent instead of theft, tended by a queen who remembered what theft felt like, guarding seals that would hold for centuries if the world stayed wise.

Ahead of them lay Cinderhollow: the market to reopen, the neighbors to teach, the frightened and the curious lining up outside a certain stall for charms that would no longer need to pretend to be tea.

And in Ulyana's pocket, riding home against her heart, the copy of *The Ashen Hours* lay warm as a sleeping bird—its pages quiet, its prophecies fulfilled, all but one.

Because that morning, for the first time since she'd found it in her mentor's trunk, the book had written something new.

One line. Fresh ink. Waiting to come true:

*In the year's longest night, the crown shall pass to willing hands.*

Winter was coming, Ulyana realized. And this year, for the first time in her life, she planned to stay awake for it.
