## 38. Aftermath in Cinderhollow

The first frost of the season came on a Thursday, and with it the first customer who asked Ulyana Pemberton for magic out loud.

She was setting out her stall in the night market—same corner of Tanner's Row as always, same crooked table, same striped awning that she'd patched so many times it was more thread than cloth—and a woman was standing at the front of it holding a feverish child and looking at her with an expression Ulyana had spent twenty years making sure no one ever wore in her direction.

Hope. Aimed at her. In public.

"You're the witch," the woman said. Not a whisper. Not a question shaped like a whisper, which was how people had asked for charms for two decades, sliding coins across the table while looking elsewhere and saying things like *a luck-knot, if you happen to sell such a thing, though of course you don't.* Just: *you're the witch,* plain as asking the price of turnips.

"I'm the charm-seller," Ulyana said, out of pure habit, and then heard herself, and winced.

The woman's face began the slow collapse of someone bracing for a door to close. Her name was Marta, Ulyana realized. Marta Ostrow, whose husband mended boots two stalls down, whose daughter had once paid for a fever-charm with three buttons and an apology. The child in her arms was small and flushed and breathing in that shallow, whistling way that made a mother's spine go rigid.

"The accord," Marta said. "They posted it on the Warden house door. It says—" She stopped, as if saying it too plainly might unmake it. "It says craft isn't a crime anymore."

"It does say that," Ulyana agreed.

"And you're—" Marta's voice cracked down the middle. "You're *her*. The one from the mountain. They're telling stories in the taprooms. About the fire under the city, and the queen, and the witch who gave twenty years—"

"Marta." Ulyana reached across the stall and took the child's wrist, gentle as anything, two fingers over the pulse point where she'd taken a thousand pulses and never once been seen doing it. "Your boy is burning up, and we're standing here talking about me. Sit down."

Marta sat. The child—Tam, four years old, furious with the whole arrangement of being ill—glared at Ulyana from under a mop of dark hair.

Ulyana looked down at her own hands. They were the same hands they'd always been. That was the strange part. She had expected, somewhere in the weeks since the mountain, since the fire roared back to life and the bottled souls came streaming up through the stone like spring water, that she would feel different in her own skin. She didn't. Her hands still smelled faintly of knot-grass and beeswax. She still woke before dawn out of habit and lay there listening for boots on cobbles, and found none, and didn't quite believe it, and listened again.

But her hands could do this now in daylight, and so she let them.

The working was a small one—a drawing-out charm, the kind she'd sold wrapped in paper and plausible deniability for years. But she did it openly, murmuring the words at full volume instead of mouthing them behind a cough, letting the warmth gather visibly in her palms until it glowed amber against the child's brow. Tam sighed, the whistle in his breath loosening, and his eyes drooped.

"There," Ulyana said. "Fever breaks by morning. Keep him off sweets for two days—he'll tell you I said he can have them, and he'll be lying."

Marta was crying. Quietly, the way people cry when they've been frightened a long time and have only just noticed it.

"He'll be all right?" she whispered.

"He'll be all right."

"And what do I owe you?"

Ulyana thought about it. Twenty years of prices quoted low enough to be forgettable, high enough to look like honest trade. Twenty years of watching mothers count coins and deciding which children got charms and which got sympathy.

"Come back tomorrow," she said, "and tell me your neighbor's name."

Marta blinked. "My—Tilde. Tilde Brannock. Why?"

"Because tomorrow I'm going to teach anyone who wants to learn how to make a fever-draw themselves, and I want to know who to expect." Ulyana smiled, and felt her own face do something unfamiliar with it—not the merchant's smile, not the careful pleasant nothing she'd worn like a mask for two decades, but the real thing underneath, a little rusty at the hinges. "Go home, Marta. Put the boy to bed."

* * *

The trouble with reopening a stall you'd spent twenty years keeping invisible was that everyone suddenly wanted to see it.

By the third evening there were so many people crammed into Tanner's Row that Old Fenwick from the pickle cart had to move his barrels twice, and complained about it both times in the tone of a man deeply pleased to have something to complain about. They came for charms, at first—the luck-knots and hearth-blessings that had always sold, except now people said the word *charm* out loud and paid in full view of their neighbors, and some of them laughed while doing it, giddy as children skipping church.

Then they started coming for the lessons.

It had begun as a joke, almost. Marta told Tilde, and Tilde told her sister, and by week's end there were a dozen people standing around Ulyana's stall after closing with the wary expressions of adults about to embarrass themselves, and Ulyana, who had never taught anyone anything except how to lie convincingly about the contents of a paper packet, heard herself say:

"All right. Fever-draws first. It's the safest working there is—you can't hurt anyone with it, you can only make them comfortable, and if you get it wrong nothing happens at all. Which is more than I can say for most things in this city."

They were terrible at it, that first night. Wonderfully, hilariously terrible. The baker's widow, Grethe, produced a heat so fierce it singed her own sleeve and had to be doused with pickle brine by Fenwick, who had opinions about it for a week. A young dockhand named Piet got the words backwards and filled his own boots with cold. And little Sefi Kessler, nine years old and missing two teeth, got it exactly right on her first try—a soft golden glow cupped in her palms like a captured candleflame—and burst into tears.

"Oh, love," Ulyana said, crouching down. "What is it?"

"I thought—" Sefi hiccupped. "I thought I was broken. My gran said our family doesn't have it anymore. She said it went away."

And there it was. That was the thing the Magistrate's fires had actually burned, Ulyana thought. Not the witches—they'd missed plenty of those, herself chief among them. What the fires had burned was the knowing. Generations of families teaching their children the small safe crafts, and then stopping, and then forgetting there had ever been anything to teach, until a girl like Sefi grew up thinking the warmth in her hands was a fault in her, a brokenness, a thing to hide even from herself.

"It didn't go away," Ulyana said. "It went underground. Where I kept it too, for a long time." She closed the girl's fingers back over the light. "This isn't broken, Sefi. This is the least broken thing about you. Now—again. Slower this time. You're not catching a moth, you're warming your hands by a hearth. The fire's already lit. You're just letting yourself stand near it."

* * *

Aldous came most evenings, when the harbor work allowed.

He still flinched sometimes when people used the old name—Corin—and she caught him correcting himself now, *Aldous, I'm Aldous,* with the careful patience of a man relearning his own mouth. Twenty-seven years of memories had come back to him in the mountain's deep places, and most of them were good ones, and some of them were Ilse Vane's face at seven years old watching soldiers carry her brother away, and those were the ones he set down carefully each night, the way you set down something heavy, before he could pick up the lighter things again.

He sat on an overturned crate beside her stall these nights, ostensibly helping—sorting knot-grass, winding twine—and actually serving as the market's favorite entertainment, because Aldous Vane, restored soul, brother of the infamous Sergeant Vane, had turned out to have a dry streak of humor a mile wide and no sense whatsoever of when to deploy it.

"That one," he murmured, as yet another hopeful soul approached the stall, "is going to ask you to bless his fishing boat. Watch. Three... two..."

"Could you bless my fishing boat?" said the fisherman.

"You see? I should take wagers."

"I am not taking wagers on my customers, Aldous."

"Fenwick would hold the stakes."

"Fenwick would *skim* the stakes." But she was smiling, and the fisherman was smiling, and a year ago the idea of a queue of strangers watching her do magic and cracking jokes about it would have sent her heart slamming against her ribs like something trying to escape. Now it was just Tuesday.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, she was learning. Freedom didn't arrive like a thunderclap. It arrived like weather, gradually, until one day you noticed you'd stopped flinching at shadows and couldn't pinpoint when.

Although—not entirely. Some evenings a Warden's cloak went by at the end of the row and her hands went still without her permission. The Wardens still walked their rounds; the accord hadn't disbanded them, only changed what they were for. Most of them had taken the change with the grace of men handed new orders they didn't fully believe in. One or two had resigned rather than serve alongside the very things they'd burned.

And one had come back in a new uniform entirely—gray instead of black, the mountain court's colors, worn by a tall woman who stood at the edge of the crowd most evenings and watched Ulyana teach with an expression that might have been envy, or grief, or simply the particular hunger of a person who had sold her own family's magic away and was watching other people get theirs back.

Ilse Vane never approached the stall. But every few nights she left something on it before dawn—a jar of good honey, once; a coil of salt-iron wire, another time; once, a small carved wooden bird of no magical significance whatsoever, which Aldous turned over in his hands for a long moment before setting it on the shelf where they kept things that mattered.

"She's learning too," Aldous said quietly, the night of the bird. "In her way. She asked Mother Ysolde to teach her."

"Ysolde?"

"Song-craft. The binding songs." He turned the bird over once more. "She says she wants to know everything the court knows. Everything that was done with souls. So it can't be done again quietly."

Ulyana watched the gray-cloaked figure at the end of the row, straight-backed and alone amid all the warmth and noise, and felt something twist gently in her chest—not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but recognition. Two women who had survived by hiding, in opposite directions. One hidden in plain sight among the crowds; one hidden inside her own duty, so thoroughly she'd helped bury her brother's name.

"Tell her," Ulyana said, "that the lessons are open to anyone. Even Wardens. Even Vanes." She hesitated. "Especially Vanes, maybe. He'd want—"

"I know what he'd want." Aldous's voice went rough. "He'd want her to stop apologizing with honey and sit down at the damn table."

"Well then." Ulyana squeezed his hand. "Tell him's sister that."

* * *

The letter from Brenna arrived on a gray morning, carried by a crow that landed on the awning pole with the smugness of a creature who knew exactly what it was delivering.

Ulyana fed it bacon rind because you didn't refuse a crow, and broke the wax—Thornhollow green, sealed with the coven's thorn-and-moon—and read it standing in the cold with her breath steaming.

*Sister,* it began, and the word hit her the way it always did now, complicated and warm. Brenna's handwriting had gotten worse, or perhaps more honest; the letters leaned like drunks.

*The elders are insufferable in victory, which I suppose means they are themselves. Mavrosa's fall has reached every ear in the craft, and half of them want to declare you a saint and the other half want to declare you a cautionary tale, and Ysolde tells them all to shut up and mend the seals, which is the correct answer.*

*I write because Seraiva writes to me—did you know that? The Queen of Teeth sends letters now, actual letters, asking questions about consent-law and soul-contract reform as though she were a magistrate and not a woman who ate a usurper's throne last month. The world is stranger than any book, including yours. Including hers.*

*Mostly I write to ask: are you well? Truly? Ysolde says the giving took more than you admit. She says the hidden years don't just vanish—they leave a shape behind, like a hand lifted off wet clay. If there are holes, sister, fill them however you can. And if you cannot fill them, let someone else hold the lamp.*

*Also, the apprentices ask constantly about "the witch of Cinderhollow" and whether you will ever visit. I tell them the witch of Cinderhollow is busy. I do not tell them she is teaching fever-draws to bakers' widows, because they would all apply to transfer and I would lose my best students to a pickle cart.*

*Come at midwinter. Bring him. Bring the crow if it will come; it likes you better than me, which stings.*

*B.*

Ulyana read it twice and then folded it into the pocket where she kept things that mattered, next to the wooden bird and a pressed sprig of knot-grass from a stall that had burned and been rebuilt.

The holes. Yes. There were holes.

She'd known it since the first morning after the mountain, when she'd woken in a borrowed bed in Cinderhollow and reached for the shape of her life the way you reach for a stair in the dark, and found gaps where stairs should be. Twenty years poured into a dying dragon—and what remained was strangely intact and strangely hollowed at once. She knew the market. She knew her neighbors' names, mostly, though some faces arrived with a delay now, recognition swimming up from somewhere deep like fish rising to feed. She knew her craft entire—that lived in her hands, in her bones, deeper than memory.

What she'd lost were the small things. The texture of two decades. She remembered that she had been lonely; she could no longer remember the specific flavor of the loneliest night. She remembered that a neighbor had once left bread on her step during a bad winter; she could not, no matter how she reached, recall which neighbor, or which winter, or whether she'd thanked them. Whole kindnesses had happened to her and were gone, spent to feed a fire that held up the world, and some mornings that bargain felt like nothing at all and some mornings it felt like reaching into her own chest and finding a room she'd furnished once, long ago, now empty and swept.

Aldous had appointed himself the keeper of what remained. Every evening he told her pieces of her own life back—carefully, without pity, the way you'd read a favorite story aloud. *Tuesday you argued with Fenwick about barrel placement and won. You keep your spare twine in the blue tin because the red one has a hole. You hum when you work; you don't know you do it.* And each piece settled into her like a stone returning to a wall, imperfectly fitted, held by mortar she was still mixing.

*Let someone else hold the lamp,* Brenna wrote. Well. Someone already was.

* * *

Midwinter's eve came, and with it the lesson that Ulyana would remember longest—if she remembered anything, which was no longer a given, and which she had decided to stop treating as a tragedy and start treating as weather.

Sefi Kessler stood before the assembled class of the Tanner's Row night school—twelve adults and three children crowded between the pickle barrels, breath fogging in the cold—and cast her first warding-light unsupervised. It wasn't much. A pale glow, wavering, the size of a hen's egg. It guttered twice.

It held.

The crowd applauded. Grethe wept, as she did at every lesson now, having decided that tears were simply part of her contribution. Fenwick pretended not to watch and then loudly critiqued Sefi's stance, which from Fenwick was a knighthood.

And Ulyana stood at the center of it all, on the corner where she had sold luck to the fearful for twenty years without one single person knowing her name, and felt the strangest sensation of her long strange year: not triumph, exactly. Something quieter. Like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known she'd been standing against.

Belonging, she thought. Nobody grants it to you. You build it, plank by plank, lesson by lesson, fever by fever, until one evening you look up and the thing exists and you live inside it.

"Again," she told Sefi, grinning. "Slower. You're not fighting it, you're—"

"—standing near the hearth," Sefi recited. "I know, I know."

The crow arrived then, out of the north, earlier than any letter ought to come. It dropped a scrap of parchment into Ulyana's hands and stood there radiating self-importance.

She unfolded it. Brenna's leaning scrawl, only a line and a half:

*Change of plans. Come early. Seraiva has found something in Ember-that-Waits's hoard—something the dragon hid even from its queens. She says it has your name on it.*

*Not the name you use. The one you were born with.*

Ulyana read it twice. Around her the lesson went on, the warding-lights bobbing like lantern-festival floats, Aldous laughing at something Fenwick said, the whole warm ordinary miracle of it continuing—and she stood quite still in the middle of her earned life, holding a scrap of parchment, feeling the ground shift beneath the foundation she had only just finished building.

The crow waited. It knew her better than she knew herself, lately.

"All right," she told it softly. "One more trip, then."
