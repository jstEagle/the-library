## 39. What She Cannot Remember

The first thing Ulyana forgot was the smell of bread.

She didn't know she had forgotten it, of course. That was the cruelty of it. You cannot mourn a smell you no longer remember existed. She only knew that some mornings she stood in the market outside her stall—her stall now, legal and licensed and stamped with the new accord's wax seal—and felt a grief with no name attached to it, like a coat that fit a person who no longer lived there.

It was Aldous who told her about the bread.

"Old Marta's bakery," he said. They were walking home through Cinderhollow at dusk, past stalls being shuttered for the night, past children chasing each other between the lamplighters' carts. "Two streets over from your old one. She used to save you the heel of the honey loaf every seventh day, because you'd once mended her grandson's croup with a fever-charm and refused payment."

"I don't remember that."

"No," he said gently. "You wouldn't."

"What else don't I remember?"

He was quiet for a long moment, long enough that she stopped walking and turned to face him. The lamps were coming on around them, gold pooling in the gutters of the street where she had spent twenty years being nobody.

"That," he said finally, taking her hand, "is going to take a while to answer. There were a lot of years."

* * *

They had developed a ritual by then, though neither of them had named it. Every evening after supper, Aldous would pour two cups of tea—the good black tea from the mountain traders, bitter enough to keep them both honest—and tell her one thing she had lost.

Not all at once. He'd learned quickly that drowning her in it did nothing but leave her weeping into her sleeves with nothing to show for it but wet wool. One thing per evening. He kept a list, written in his careful clerk's hand, folded into the breast pocket where he used to keep the scrap of prophecy that had started everything. He never showed her the list. He said it felt too much like reading from an inventory of the dead.

Tonight's item was a woman named Odile.

"You met her your second winter in Cinderhollow," Aldous began. "You were twenty-three. You'd been selling charms at the night market for barely a season, and you were terrible at the patter. Truly awful. You'd blush when anyone haggled."

"I still blush when anyone haggles."

"You do, and it's very effective, which I suspect is why you kept doing it." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "Odile sold fish. Smoked eel, mostly, and lampreys out of the Graywater. She had hands like knotted rope and a laugh that carried three stalls down. And every market night for nineteen years, she saved you the best eel and pretended she couldn't sell it because it was 'too crooked.' You never once asked what she did with the rest of the crooked ones."

Ulyana laughed despite herself, and then the laugh caught on something sharp halfway up her throat. Nineteen years. A woman had fed her twice a week for nineteen years, and Ulyana could not summon her face. She could not have picked Odile out of a crowd if her life depended on it.

"Does she still sell fish?"

"She does. Third row, near the well. She asked after you last week. I told her you'd been ill, and that the illness took some things." He hesitated. "I didn't tell her what things."

"Why not?"

"Because she cried when I said you'd been ill," Aldous said, "and I didn't want to watch her cry again while I explained that the woman she fed for nineteen years doesn't know her name."

Ulyana set down her cup because her hands were shaking, and if she didn't put it down she was going to throw it, and the cup had been a gift from Brenna and throwing gifts was poor manners even by Pemberton standards.

"I want to see her," she said. "Tomorrow. Take me to her stall."

"Are you certain? It might be easier to let it—"

"Aldous." She heard her own voice go flat and hard, the voice she'd used on Warden patrols for twenty years, the voice of a woman pretending to be furniture. Then she made herself soften it. "I gave those years away so a dragon could live and a city full of frightened people could stop burning each other. I'm not sorry. But I am not going to sit in this kitchen being politely sad about them either. If I can't remember Odile, then I will meet her again. That has to count for something."

He looked at her across the table with that expression she was learning to read—the one that wasn't pity and wasn't quite awe but lived somewhere in the neighborhood of both.

"You know," he said, "in the market, before any of this, I watched you refuse to sell me a forgetting-charm. You looked at me like I was asking you to help me disappear, and you wouldn't do it, and I thought: there is the stubbornest person in Cinderhollow."

"And now?"

"Now I think I underestimated you considerably."

* * *

Meeting Odile again was worse and better than Ulyana had feared, in roughly equal measure.

Worse, because Odile came around the stall wiping her hands on her apron and said, "There she is! My favorite customer!" with such unguarded delight that Ulyana felt like a thief standing in the middle of someone's open grief.

Better, because halfway through the conversation—Odile talking, Ulyana listening, Aldous quietly holding her elbow—Odile paused, squinted at her, and said, "You've gone strange, girl, but you've not gone different. You still stand like you're waiting for someone to shout at you. We'll fix that. Sit down. Have an eel. They're crooked today."

And Ulyana sat, and ate crooked eel, and began, haltingly, to learn a friendship the way you learn a language as an adult—with effort and embarrassment and small triumphs. By the end of the afternoon Odile had told her six stories about herself, four of which were demonstrably exaggerated, and Ulyana had laughed until her ribs hurt at a story involving a lamprey, a tax collector, and a bucket, whose details she would forget by morning but whose shape she suspected would stay.

Walking home, Aldous said, "Well?"

"It hurt," Ulyana said. "And I'm going back Thursday. Both things are true."

"That's usually how it goes."

* * *

Some evenings the ritual went badly.

One gray morning in late autumn, Ulyana woke with tears already on her face and no idea why. She lay in the dark cataloguing herself—name, trade, city, the man breathing softly beside her—and everything checked out, everything accounted for, and still the sorrow sat on her chest like a cat that had decided to stay.

Aldous woke to find her sitting up in bed, staring at the wall.

"The dream?" he asked.

"There was a door," she said. "Green paint. Peeling. And someone was laughing behind it, and I knew her—I knew her the way you know your own hands—and now it's gone. Just the door left. Green paint." Her voice cracked on the last words. "That's all I get, Aldous. A door."

He sat up and pulled her against him, and for a while neither of them spoke. Outside, Cinderhollow was waking—the carts, the bells, a fishmonger shouting prices in a dialect that predated the Magistrate by two hundred years. The city went on being the city, indifferent and alive.

"Brenna," he said at last. "That was Brenna's door. At Thornhollow. The apprentice quarters—you shared a room with green doors, hers peeled worst because she picked at the paint when she was thinking. She used to laugh at her own jokes before she finished telling them. Still does. I've seen her do it."

Ulyana pulled back to look at him. "How do you know all this?"

"Because I've been to Thornhollow three times since we came home. I've been writing letters to your sister-apprentice asking her to tell me everything she remembers about you." He had the grace to look slightly sheepish. "I've filled two notebooks. She says you once turned Elder Maren's hair blue for a fortnight and blamed a hexed comb."

"I did not."

"She says you absolutely did, and that you were smug about it for a month, and that the smugness was the only reason anyone figured out it was you."

Ulyana opened her mouth to deny it again and found—to her genuine astonishment—that somewhere beneath the missing years, something warmed. Not a memory. Nothing so complete. Just a heat, like a hearth in a distant room, the sense of having once been a girl who turned hair blue and enjoyed it.

"I think I was happy there," she said slowly. "Before the rite. Before I ran. I think I was actually happy, and I've spent twenty years telling myself the whole thing was just fear."

"Fear and happiness can share a house," Aldous said. "Mine certainly did. Nineteen empty years and my mother's voice in my dreams, and still—" he paused, choosing the words with care, "—still there must have been mornings I liked. Tea. Rain on windows. Whatever a soul does with itself when nobody's watching it."

Ulyana wiped her eyes and blew her nose with a sound that would have embarrassed a trumpet.

"Read me another one," she said. "From your notebook. Tell me something awful I did."

"You want awful?"

"I want true. Awful is fine. I'm tired of grieving a saint I don't remember. She sounds exhausting."

Aldous grinned and reached for the notebook on the bedside table, and by the time the light came properly through the shutters, Ulyana was laughing again—a little wetly, but laughing—at the tale of young Ulyana Pemberton charging a farmer triple for a luck-knot because he'd insulted her hat, then sneaking back the difference disguised as a refund from "the charm guild," an organization that did not exist.

* * *

Winter came early that year, the first snow falling soft and businesslike on Cinderhollow's rooftops, and with it came a letter from Brenna, sealed with the Thornhollow knot-work.

*Come for midwinter,* it read. *Mother Ysolde asks after you, which in Ysolde means she has prepared three lectures and a pie. Bring the clerk. Bring your appetite and your stubbornness; you'll need both. And Ulyana—there's a box here you should collect yourself. It's yours. It always was.*

"A box," Ulyana said, turning the letter over as if more instructions might be hiding on the back.

"Every coven keeps apprentices' things," Aldous said. "When they run."

"When they run," she repeated. Twenty years ago a girl had run out of Thornhollow in the dark with nothing but terror and a stolen cloak, and somewhere in a green-doored room, someone had gathered up what she left behind and kept it. Kept it through two decades, through the coven politics and the elder schisms and the dying fire and all of it. "Did Brenna say what's in it?"

"No. She said—" he glanced at the letter again— "'she'll know it when she sees it.'"

They traveled up to Thornhollow as the snow deepened, and Brenna met them at the gates with cold ceremony and warmer eyes than her words allowed, exactly as she always did, and hugged Ulyana hard enough to crack a rib before the ceremony got fully underway.

The box was waiting in Ulyana's old room. The door was freshly painted green. Someone had sanded it and repainted it in the summer, by the look of it, and when Ulyana ran her hand along the frame she found the wood warm under her palm, or imagined she did, and decided not to investigate which.

Inside the box: a cloak, moth-eaten. A wooden comb. A bundle of practice knots tied by an eleven-year-old's clumsy fingers, preserved like relics. A hairbrush. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, a small book—not a grimoire, not a prophecy, just a diary, the handwriting round and childish and getting steadily less childish page by page.

Ulyana sat on the narrow bed that had been hers and read her own life by candlelight while snow ticked against the window. Entries about lessons. About Brenna. About Mother Ysolde's terrifying kindness. About the rite, and the fear, and the last desperate night. And near the end, an entry dated the day before she fled:

*I don't want to be brave. I want to be invisible and safe and I hate myself for wanting it. If I ever come back, whoever I am by then, I hope she forgives me. I hope somebody tells her it wasn't cowardice, it was just being twenty and alone with a choice too big for one person.*

Ulyana closed the book and held it against her chest and wept—not for the lost years, not entirely, but for the girl who wrote that, whom she had abandoned on a road in the dark and never once gone back for. Aldous sat beside her without speaking, his shoulder against hers, and let her.

When she could speak again, she said, "I remember her now. A little. Not everything. But I remember her."

"That's more than most people get," Aldous said. "Most people never meet themselves at all."

* * *

They stayed for midwinter. On the longest night, the coven lit the old fires in the great hearth, and Mother Ysolde delivered two of her three lectures (the third was apparently reserved for emergencies), and the pie was excellent, and Ulyana fell asleep in a chair by the fire with her head on Brenna's shoulder like she had done when she was twelve, and no one moved her, because Brenna threatened the first person who suggested it.

On the road home, three days later, Aldous rode beside her in silence for a long stretch of ash-country road before he said, quietly:

"There's something I haven't told you. About the list."

Ulyana turned in the saddle. His face was doing something complicated.

"Every evening I give you back one thing," he said. "One memory, one story. And it helps, I know it helps, you've laughed again, you've gone back to Odile, you've got Brenna back. But I've been keeping the last page of the list to myself. Because it isn't a loss, exactly. It's a question."

"What kind of question?"

"The kind I've been afraid to ask for weeks." He brought his horse closer, close enough that their knees nearly touched, and looked at her with the expression of a man standing at the edge of something. "Twenty years, Ulyana. You poured them into Ember-that-Waits. Every hidden year, every small safe day. And the dragon lives, and the seals hold, and I wake up next to you every morning grateful past all describing. But I keep thinking about what Seraiva said at the court. What the fire takes, it keeps. Which means your twenty years aren't gone. They're *in there*. Burning. Being burned."

Ulyana felt the cold of the road settle into her bones.

"So here's my question," Aldous said. "Seraiva owes us. The queen who reformed the whole soul-market owes you more than she can ever repay. And dragons, according to a certain book of prophecies, can give as well as take." He swallowed. "What if we asked for them back?"
