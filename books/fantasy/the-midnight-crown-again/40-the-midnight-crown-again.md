## 40. The Midnight Crown Again

The year's longest night arrived the way all important nights do—quietly, and with too much to carry.

Ulyana stood at the mouth of Ember's Hollow with her arms full of lanterns she didn't need. The dragon's fire lit the cavern better than any flame humans had ever invented, a deep amber glow that turned the lava tunnels into corridors of honeyed glass. But the market women of Cinderhollow had insisted. "You don't go to a coronation without lights," Marta had said, pressing three paper lanterns into Ulyana's hands like it was scripture. "Even if the one being crowned could swallow the sun."

So Ulyana carried them anyway, because that was what you did for people you loved, even when the people were dragons and the rules were nonsense.

"You know," Aldous said beside her, his voice low in the warm dark, "in my dreams of this place—the ones from before—I never once imagined anyone *bringing anything*. It was always just the court, and the teeth, and the hunger. I never thought about lanterns."

"What do lanterns mean?"

"That somebody expected there to be a morning." He smiled at her, and twenty years of hiddenness and one year of hollowness sat behind the smile like old friends who'd finally learned to wait outside. "I like being the sort of person who expects mornings."

The tunnel opened.

Ember-that-Waits rose before them in the great vaulted heart of the mountain, and Ulyana still, after weeks, had not grown used to it. The dragon was vast beyond sense—a cathedral given scales, a mountain given breath—and where once she had lain dimming on her hoard of spent years, gray as ash and nearly as still, now light moved under her skin like weather. Like dawn deciding whether to bother. Her ribs expanded in slow, enormous rhythm, and each breath stirred the air so that the whole chamber breathed with her.

She was awake. Truly awake, for the first time in a century, and everyone in Cinderhollow knew whose doing that was, which was why Ulyana could no longer buy bread without someone pressing an extra roll into her bag and refusing payment with a ferocity that suggested the refusal was the point.

"She's watching us," Aldous murmured.

"She's always watching us. She thinks we can't tell."

"I *can't* tell. You can. One of us should be unnerved by this."

"I've been unnerved since I was sixteen," Ulyana said. "I'm retired from it."

*Little thief of quiet,* rumbled through the stone—not sound exactly, but meaning arriving the way heat arrives from a hearth. *You brought the small fires.*

"They're gifts. From the night market. Apparently you're famous now." Ulyana set the lanterns down at the edge of the hoard, absurd little paper things glowing against mountains of coin and memory-glass. "Marta says you're to have the blue one especially. She made it herself."

*Tell Marta,* said Ember, with what Ulyana could only describe as dignity, *that blue suits me.*

Behind them, boots on stone announced the rest of the party, and Ulyana turned to see them come: Seraiva, the exiled queen crowned again at last, wearing her authority like a garment she'd finally decided fit; Brenna, High Witch of Thornhollow, carrying the coven's oldest salt-iron staff and pretending she wasn't nervous; Mother Ysolde, ancient as the seals themselves, humming the song-charm under her breath out of pure habit; and Ilse Vane, former Sergeant of the Wardens, current something-harder-to-name, walking with her chin up the way she always did, as if daring the world to comment on her presence here.

And trailing them, carrying the book, came the person Ulyana had been both dreading and longing to see all day.

"The Ashen Hours" looked smaller than it had in her mentor's effects, smaller than it had in her nightmares. A century-old book of prophecies, bound in gray leather gone soft as moth-wing, its pages inked with faces before those faces were born. For twenty years it had been the thing she'd been ordered to burn. Then it had been the thing that ruined her hiding. Now it was simply a book—and tonight, according to Ysolde and every scrap of prophecy-logic they'd puzzled through together, it wanted to be finished.

"Everyone's here," Brenna said, looking around the vault with an expression Ulyana recognized from their apprentice days—the look Brenna wore when she was cataloguing everything that could go wrong and deciding to proceed anyway. "Seraiva holds the throne. The seals are singing. The fire is—" she glanced up at the dragon, and her voice went briefly, uncharacteristically soft, "—the fire is *bright*. So I suppose all that remains is the part nobody understands."

"The crowning," Seraiva said. "Yes. Let us be honest with one another, sisters and sergeant: none of us knows precisely what the last verse commands. Only that it must be done freely, by the two named in the opening lines, on the longest night, with ember-light for a crown." Her gaze found Ulyana, and there was something in it that might have been apology or might have been envy; with Seraiva it had always been hard to say. "Prophecy is a door. It does not tell you what the room looks like inside."

"It never has," Ulyana agreed. "That's rather been the theme."

Ysolde hobbled forward, took Ulyana's hands in hers—hands like paper wrapped around wire—and studied her face with eyes that had watched a hundred apprentices flee or break or burn. "Child," she said, "when I taught you fire, you hated every lesson. Do you remember why?"

"Because fire doesn't hide. Because it announces itself. Because—" Ulyana stopped. The answer had changed somewhere in the last year, and she let the new one finish itself. "Because I thought being seen would kill me."

"And now?"

"Now I think never being seen already did, a little. Every day. In very small installments."

Ysolde nodded slowly, satisfied, and squeezed her hands once, hard. "Then you are ready. Not ready the way I wanted you ready at sixteen. Ready the way only a woman who has paid for her choices can be. That is better currency."

* * *

They prepared the crown as the night deepened toward its longest hour.

It was not a crown, precisely—or rather, it was, but not one any goldsmith could make. Ember-light, the prophecy called it: a circlet drawn living from the dragon's own flame, held in shape by will and song and the consent of the one who wore it. Ysolde had spent weeks teaching Ulyana the shaping of it, and Ulyana had burned her fingers more times than she cared to admit, until Aldous had taken to sitting beside her during practice with a bowl of cool water and a running commentary that was half encouragement and half terrible jokes.

"Left hand higher," he'd say. "You're making a hat, not a wreath. Though honestly, either would be an improvement on the Magistrate's actual crown, which I'm told looks like a cheese wheel with ambitions."

Now he stood beside her at the hoard's edge while the others withdrew to the cavern's rim, and the dragon lowered her head—slowly, majestically, the way a mountain might bow if mountains were capable of tact—until her great eye was level with them both. Up close, her fire-skin moved like the surface of the sun seen through smoked glass, and her warmth fell over them like a blanket.

*Ninety years I slept dim,* Ember said, and the words settled into Ulyana's bones. *Ninety years I ate what they fed me and told myself it was enough. Bottled years. Stolen souls. The queens called it keeping the world safe, and perhaps it was, and perhaps it was only keeping *me* tame. But your twenty hidden years, witch—they burned different. They tasted like a life. Freely given, remember, always freely given.* The vast eye blinked, molten and gentle. *So ask me what I want, before you crown me. It is the question no queen ever asked.*

Ulyana swallowed. The whole chamber waited. Even the seals beneath the floor, she thought, had gone quiet to listen.

"What do you want?" she asked.

*I want to be a fire,* said Ember-that-Waits, *and not a cage. I want the court above me to feed me nothing it has stolen. If the seals must thin, let them thin honestly, and let the people above learn to mend them with songs instead of souls. I am old, and I am tired of being the excuse for every cruelty done in the dark. Crown me, and I will hold the dark—but I will hold it as a promise kept, not a hostage taken.*

Silence, then, of the kind that feels less like absence and more like attention.

"Well," said Aldous into it, his voice slightly unsteady, "that's the most reasonable thing anyone in a position of power has said in about four hundred years."

*Flatterer,* said the dragon, and her amusement came off her skin in waves of extra warmth that made the paper lanterns flutter.

Ulyana raised her hands.

The shaping was harder than practice—it always was, with real things—but it came, the way her magic had come back at the river crossing, the way her name had come back to her in Mavrosa's throne room: not smoothly, but *completely*. She drew the ember-light up out of the dragon's breath the way you'd draw thread from a spindle, and it resisted, and then it sang, and then it became. A circlet of living gold-red fire, turning slowly above her palms, throwing light across the hoard, across Aldous's upturned face, across the far walls where her sister-witches stood hand in hand.

Her hands shook. She let them shake. Twenty years ago she would have hidden the shaking, would have buried the whole moment under merchant's patter and a smile designed to be forgotten.

"Ember-that-Waits," she said aloud, and her voice carried in the great vault without effort, the acoustics of a place built for declarations. "Keeper of the seal. Last fire of the old wars. I crown you not with stolen years but with this light, freely drawn, freely given—and I name before all present that you hold it by consent, and may lay it down whenever the burden grows false."

She set the crown of ember-fire upon the dragon's head.

The light went through everything.

Not painfully—more like the moment at dawn when the sun clears a rooftop and suddenly the whole street is a different street. Fire ran the length of the dragon's spine in a bright wave; her wings, folded so long they'd seemed like architecture rather than limbs, spread halfway, tentatively, like an old woman stretching in a doorway; and the song of the seals beneath the floor rose from a drone to something almost joyful, chords within chords, the sound of locks turning healthy and oiled.

And Ulyana felt it pass through her too—the crown's light, the dragon's gratitude, the whole enormous machine of the deep world acknowledging her—and she understood, standing there with singed fingertips and a full heart, that this was what her mentor had tried to teach her at sixteen and she hadn't been able to hear: that magic was never about power. It was about *witnessing*. About saying *this is true, and I am here for it.*

Beside her, Aldous was crying without any apparent awareness of it, tears catching the ember-light, and Ulyana reached over and took his hand and did not say anything, because some things are better left unpatterned.

*It is done,* said Ember, and her voice had changed—lighter, somehow, the way a room changes after a long illness leaves it. *The verse is fulfilled.*

"Not quite," said Ysolde, from the cavern's rim, and Ulyana turned.

The old woman was pointing at the book.

* * *

Aldous had set "The Ashen Hours" on a flat stone at the hoard's edge, as the ritual required—open to the final page, the page that for a hundred years had ended mid-line, the ink stopping as though the author had been interrupted, or as though the ending refused to exist until someone lived it.

The page was writing itself.

Ink crawled across the parchment in letters fine and unhurried, appearing the way frost appears on a window—not written so much as *remembered into being*. Everyone drew closer. Even Ember lowered her crowned head, firelight and prophecy-light mingling.

The new line read:

*And the witch who fled laid down her hiding like a coat too heavy for the season, and the man twice-born stood witness, and the fire chose its own crown; and the last line of this book belongs to no fate, for it was chosen, and what is chosen cannot be prophesied—only lived.*

Below it, the ink paused. Hovered. And then, in a smaller hand, almost shy:

*Here ends the Ashen Hours. Begin the others.*

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Brenna laughed—one short, disbelieving bark of a laugh—and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand in the most undignified manner ever employed by a High Witch. "A century of dread," she said, "a hundred years of coven elders whispering about doom and sacrifice and the price of fire, and the wretched book ends by telling us to *start another one*."

"That's writers for you," Ulyana said. "Always leaving threads loose so you'll buy the sequel."

Seraiva touched the page with one pale finger, careful as a woman handling a sleeping animal. "Chosen, not prophesied," she murmured. "Do you understand what that means, witch? It means the future is no longer written. It means everything after this page is ours to botch magnificently." She smiled, and for once the smile held no teeth at all. "I find I prefer that arrangement. Thrones are much easier to govern when heaven isn't taking notes."

Ilse Vane said nothing. She was looking at the book the way she'd once looked at the soul-jar labeled *Aldous Vane*—with grief and relief fighting openly on her face. Then she straightened, squared her shoulders in that way she had, and said, "The Magistrate's council meets at month's end. The accord needs signatures, and the signatures need arguing for. Half the council still wants to burn every charm-seller in Cinderhollow out of habit." She looked at Ulyana. "They'll listen better if the hedge-witch who rekindled a god stands in front of them and refuses to be polite about it."

"You're asking me to testify."

"I'm asking you to be *seen*, Pemberton. Loudly. In daylight. It's apparently your special talent now."

"Careful," Aldous said mildly. "Keep flattering her and she'll start charging admission."

* * *

Later—much later, when the witches had gone up the tunnels toward sleep and the dragon's breathing had settled into the deep rhythm of a creature finally resting easy—Ulyana and Aldous sat together on the warm stones at the hoard's edge, sharing a flask of tea Marta had sent along ("dragons get offerings, escorts get tea, that's the economy"), watching the ember-crown turn its slow circles against the dark.

"Do you miss it?" Aldous asked eventually. "The hiding? I know it cost you. But it was also yours. Twenty years of a life."

Ulyana considered the question honestly, because he deserved honest answers and because she'd promised herself, coming down the mountain, that she would never again give anyone the smoothed-over version.

"Sometimes," she said. "I miss the version of it that was peaceful. The early mornings at the stall before the market woke up. The particular silence of a street when you know nobody's looking at you." She turned the flask in her hands. "But that silence was never really empty. It was full of everything I wasn't saying. And now—" she gestured vaguely at the dragon, the crown, the book lying closed and finished on its stone, "—now the streets are loud, and Marta hugs me in public, and children follow me around asking to see charms, and yesterday a Warden *bought a luck-knot*. Paid full price. Asked if I did wedding blessings."

"Do you?"

"Marta's already booked me for spring. I told her I'd need to consult my schedule." She leaned her head against his shoulder. "I have no schedule. I have never had a schedule. I'm going to spend the rest of my life inventing reasons to seem busy and it's going to be wonderful."

Aldous laughed—that laugh she'd learned first as Corin's, careful and rare, and had slowly heard change into something freer, someone with a childhood and a mother's lullabies and a sister waiting awkwardly in Cinderhollow for him to work up the courage to visit. Ilse had gone to him twice already, Ulyana knew. Twice, and stood in the doorway both times, and left. Some doors take longer than prophecies.

Above them, faint and far, the mountain hummed its seal-song, steady as a heartbeat.

"Last year tonight," Aldous said quietly, "I woke up tasting ash and didn't know my own name. Tonight I'm sitting on a dragon's doorstep wearing her warmth like a blanket, next to the woman the book was waiting for." He shook his head. "If someone had put that in a prophecy, I'd have called it sentimental nonsense."

"The book agrees with you. It refused to write it."

"For exactly that reason, probably."

They sat a while longer, and the longest night rolled slowly past its deepest hour and began, minute by invisible minute, to climb back toward morning. Ulyana found she was watching the tunnel mouth, where the first hint of the upper world's grey would eventually arrive, and realized she was doing what she'd done every night since the crowning: expecting the dawn. On purpose. As a discipline. As a small daily act of faith that the world after the last page was worth getting up for.

It was. It kept proving it was—in bread pressed into her hands, in seals that sang instead of screamed, in a sister's gruff weekly letters, in a dragon learning, breath by breath, what it meant to be warm without being hungry.

"Aldous," she said, as the darkness began, at last, to soften at its edges. "The book said 'begin the others.' Plural. Other books. Other stories."

"I heard."

"You know what that means."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "Somewhere out there is another book with someone's face in it. Someone who's currently hiding, or forgetting, or paying for a debt they never agreed to." His hand found hers in the ember-light. "And someday a crow is going to land on their windowsill, or they're going to open a dead mentor's trunk, or a stranger is going to order tea at their stall and look at them like a mirror."

"And they'll have a choice," Ulyana said. "Like we did."

"Like we did." He squeezed her fingers. "Terrifying thought."

"Wonderful thought." She watched the first grey touch the tunnel mouth, thin as a charm-thread, certain as morning. "Come on, then. Dawn's coming, and I intend to be insufferably visible in it."

They walked up out of the mountain together, side by side, leaving the fire burning bright behind them—and ahead of them, at the top of the long stairs, the ordinary, unwritten, entirely theirs day was already beginning.
