## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The trouble with stealing memories is that nobody thanks you for it.

They pay you, mind. They count out coin with shaking hands and look at you like a priest who's just granted absolution, and for about an hour they feel lighter than they've felt in years. Then the hour passes, the grief grows back like grass through floorboards — grief always does; I've never met a memory that couldn't be regrown from its roots if you watered it with regret — and what's left is a hollow where something used to live, and me, three days' ride away with their sorrow in a glass vial and their coin in my boot.

I'm not proud of the trade. But pride is for people who can afford it, and on the smuggler roads of Thornfell, winter is coming down off the high passes like a debt collector, and debts are the one thing this mountain range has never once forgiven.

* * *

The Salt Gate waystation sat at the mouth of the Kestrel Cut, where the trade road narrows to a throat and every cart going north or south has to pass close enough to spit on the walls. Twenty years ago there'd have been forty wagons queued there on any autumn morning, mule trains stacked nose to tail, the air thick with salt-smoke and cursing in four languages. Now there was my horse, half-dead already and too honest to admit it; a fuel-seller's hut with its shutters nailed against the wind; and one wagon, painted merchant-blue, standing alone by the cold fire-ring like the last guest at a funeral.

I knew the wagon. Everyone on the roads knew Odile Ferrant's blue wagon, because Odile Ferrant had been the richest woman between Cindralle Gap and the Aurelian border towns back when "between" meant anything worth being rich over. She'd built her fortune moving imperial silk through the Cut at prices that would make a bishop blush, and she'd done it by remembering everything — every toll, every favor, every face that owed her. Memory was her ledger.

Which is why it struck me as a particular kind of joke when she sent word ahead that she wanted to forget something.

* * *

She received me inside the wagon, which was smaller than its reputation. A stove ticking away in the corner, shelves of ledgers gone soft with damp, and Odile herself sitting very straight on a bench with her hands folded like a woman attending her own trial. Sixty-odd years old, built like a door hinge — all angles and rust-colored determination — with the kind of face that had stopped pretending to be kind sometime around the second empire census.

"Voss," she said. "You look terrible."

"You're paying for the memory, not the messenger."

"I'm paying for silence, speed, and no questions. You're two of those things." She nodded at the bench opposite her. "Sit."

I sat. On the table between us: a purse heavy enough to make my teeth ache, a stoppered glass vial of Thornfell workmanship — grey glass, leaded seams, made to hold what shouldn't be held — and a single candle burned down almost to nothing.

"The candle's for timing?" I asked.

"The candle's for him." Her voice didn't change at all, which was how I knew we'd arrived. "You'll want the details. Thieves always do. Fine. My son's name was Tomas. He'd have been twenty-six this spring. He drowned in the Vell at Ashford Crossing eight years ago, hauling cargo he shouldn't have been hauling for a rate I should never have agreed to." She said it the way you'd read a shipping manifest. Flat. Weighed. Paid in full. "I've carried it eight years, Voss. Every morning it's the first thing I reach for, before the ledgers, before the light. And every morning it costs me the whole day. I'm done paying."

I looked at the purse. It was more money than I'd see again until spring, if spring came, which on the Thornfell side of the mountains was a theological question rather than a calendar one.

"There are cheaper ways to stop hurting," I said. "Drink. Distance. Hitting your head on a low beam."

"Those leave the hole anyway. This takes the hole out." She pushed the vial toward me with one finger, precise as a clerk. "I'm told you take the whole thing clean. Not dulled. Not blurred. Gone. That true?"

"It's true."

"And it doesn't come back?"

"Not from me. What you grow back afterward is your own garden."

Something moved behind her eyes — not doubt exactly. More like a merchant checking a weight twice. "And you keep it? The memory. After."

"I keep it in the vial. Vials get sold, traded, sometimes broken. I don't sit around watching other people's worst days for pleasure, if that's what you're asking."

"What I'm asking," Odile Ferrant said, "is whether some night, twenty years from now, some drunk in a border tavern is going to buy my son's drowning and laugh at it."

The stove ticked. The wind found the wagon seams and worried them, a thin whistling note like a dog that wouldn't stop asking.

"No," I said, because it was mostly true, and because a lie that size deserves to be told looking someone in the eye.

She believed me. That was the worst part. She counted out the terms like freight rates — full sum now, half again if I was gone before dawn, a lifetime ban from her routes if anyone ever learned — and I sat there taking a dead woman's son out of her head for coin, and both of us pretended this was commerce.

Maybe it was. On Thornfell, everything is commerce. The mountain taught the valley that lesson with avalanches.

* * *

Here's how the theft works, since you're wondering, and since you'd wonder anyway.

There's a moment in every person's memory — the ones that matter — where the whole of it stands gathered like water behind a dam. Grief is good for this. Grief is a memory that refuses to disperse; it pools, it waits, it presents itself whole whenever the mind goes looking. Love scatters. Joy leaks. But grief holds its shape, and that makes it easy to lift, if you know the grip.

I put two fingers of each hand to her temples — her skin was cool and papery, like autumn fruit — and I asked her to think of Tomas. Not to fight it. Just to hold him up in her mind's window and let me look.

She held him up.

He was sixteen in her strongest memory of him, standing on the loading dock at Cindralle Gap with his sleeves rolled up, laughing at something a dockhand had said, holding a rope he was absolutely not qualified to hold. Sunlight. Sawdust. The specific golden stupid happiness of a boy who doesn't yet know the river has his name in its book. Around that image, the whole architecture of her loss rose up — the message from Ashford Crossing, the ride south through rain, the river itself, brown and indifferent, the small crowd, the wrong-sized boots on the wrong-sized body, the sound she'd made that she'd never made before or since —

I took it.

It came away the way a splinter comes away — one long clean slide, resistance, then nothing. The vial drank it in with a faint amber flicker, the glass warming in my palm, and Odile Ferrant sat across from me with her hands still folded and her face suddenly, terribly empty. Not peaceful. Empty. There's a difference, and I've seen enough of both to write a field guide.

"Well," she said, after a while. "That's — hm."

"Ma'am—"

"Tomas." She frowned, testing the name like a loose tooth. "I have a son. Had. There's a gap here. Like a missing stair in the dark." She looked at me, and her eyes were dry, and that was worse than weeping. "Was he young?"

"He was young," I said. "He laughed a lot."

"Did he now." She turned it over. "Well. He must have gotten that from somewhere." And then she straightened, hinged herself upright, and became a merchant again, because that was the only self she had left that didn't hurt. "Half bonus if you're over the pass before dawn, Voss. Don't let me see your face on my road again. No offense."

"None taken."

* * *

I rode north out of the Cut with Tomas Ferrant's last sunny afternoon riding in my saddlebag next to hard cheese and a knife, and I did the thing I always do after a job, which is to try very hard not to think about it, and failed the way I always fail.

Because here's the part of the trade nobody puts on the price list. To steal a memory clean, you have to carry it for a while. Minutes, usually. Long enough to lift it whole without tearing the edges. And during those minutes, you're not yourself. You're the boy on the dock with the rope, laughing. You're the mother in the rain learning what the river took. You live somebody else's worst day as your own, with all the nerves and blood of your own body doing the feeling, and then you bottle it and hand it over and go back to being nobody in particular.

Most thieves in my line drink after a job. I never understood that until recently. Now I understand it completely and refuse to do it, on principle, because principle is the only furniture I've got left.

See, the dirty secret of Vesper Voss — and I'll thank you to keep it between us and the vials — is that I don't hoard memories because I'm disciplined. I hoard because I'm poor. There's nothing left to sell. Somewhere in the last ten years of surviving the Thornfell roads, I spent myself down to the studs. I know this the way you know a room you've lived in: by the absences. There's a shape in me where a mother used to be. A draft where brothers might have stood. I have skills with no history, opinions with no origin, a laugh I don't recognize when it gets out. Ask me my earliest memory and I'll tell you a mountain pass in fog, age indeterminate, and a hunger that predates the fog.

Everything else went into glass and got sold to fences and grieving widows and one very persistent collector in Aurel who pays scandalous prices for childhoods. I told myself it was fine. I told myself the past is just inventory, and inventory exists to move.

But some nights, riding alone above the tree line with the frost coming down like ash, I catch myself reaching for something — a face, a smell, bread, a voice saying my name in a particular way, the way people say it when they've known you since before you were anything — and finding the shelf bare, and the bare shelf looking back at me.

Odile paid me to take a hole out of her. Nobody's ever paid to put one in me. There isn't enough coin.

* * *

The pass was closing early this year. Everyone said so. The shepherds said it, the fuel-sellers said it, the crows said it by leaving. Three weeks, maybe four, and the Kestrel Cut would seal itself under snow until thaw, and every soul on the wrong side of it would spend the winter eating whatever they'd been smart enough to stockpile and whatever they'd been desperate enough to take.

Which meant the roads were dying, and a man whose entire trade is other people's memories needs roads, and other people, and reasons for either to exist. The Empire's garrisons were pulling back to the warm side of the range — I'd watched their supply columns go south all week, banners snapping, leaving the high settlements to God and gravity. When the legions leave Thornfell, they don't leave order behind. They leave a vacuum, and vacuums on these roads fill fast with teeth.

So I had a plan, such as it was. Sell the Ferrant job to Maren Quill in Lowmark — she pays best for grief-work, don't ask me why, maybe grief keeps better than joy — winter somewhere cheap and defensible, and come thaw, reassess whether the memory trade had a future or whether I should finally take up honest banditry like everyone else.

It was a fine plan. Plans usually are, right up until the mountain introduces amendments.

I was two miles below the Salt Gate, riding through the first real snowfall — fat, deliberate flakes with the patience of something that intends to stay — when I saw the firelight in the trees off the road. Off the road matters. Nothing honest burns off the road in Thornfell in October.

I should have ridden past. Any sensible thief rides past. But the firelight was wrong somehow — unsteady, guttering, the light of a fire nobody's feeding — and my horse, that cowardly animal with the survival instincts of a turnip, was already turning toward it, because horses can smell dying men the way I can smell grief, and apparently mine had decided tonight was a teaching night.

I found him against a boulder, propped up like a man posing for his own monument. Older than me, maybe fifty, dressed in clothes too fine for the woods and too torn for anywhere else. Blood down his left side in a quantity I associate with bad endings. Frost in his beard. And in his fist, held out toward me with the last of his grip, a scroll of parchment sealed in black wax with a mark I didn't recognize — seven points, like a crown drawn by someone who'd only heard crowns described.

"Voss," he said.

I hadn't given him a name. That should have been my first warning.

"You're Voss." His lips were grey. His eyes were the worst part — not afraid, not pained. *Relieved.* Like he'd walked a hundred miles to a door and it had finally opened. "They said — the fence in Lowmark — said you were on the road. Said you could—" A cough folded him nearly in half, wet and deep. "Take it. Take it north. The map knows the way. The map will show you."

"It's a map," I said, because apparently my contribution to this scene was going to be stating the obvious to a dying man.

"It's the door." He pressed it into my hand, folding my fingers closed over it, and his hands were freezing and strong, both at once, which shouldn't be possible. "Cindralle isn't gone, Voss. They only wrote it that way. The door remembers. Tell it—" Another cough, longer this time, and when he surfaced from it his voice had dropped to a whisper with gravel in it. "Tell it I'm sorry. Tell the door I kept the key safe. Tell it—"

He died on the fourth *tell it*, which felt rude of the mountain but typical of the season. I knelt there in the falling snow with a stranger's blood cooling on my gloves and a sealed scroll in my fist, listening to his fire tick itself out, and I did the arithmetic I always do: what's this cost, what's this worth, who's coming to collect.

The answer to the third question arrived before I'd finished counting.

Down through the trees, below the road, torches. Four of them, five, moving fast and in formation, which meant soldiers, which meant Empire, which meant whatever this scroll was, men had died for it and more men were climbing a freezing mountainside in the dark to get it back.

I am many things, most of them unflattering. But I am not a man who dies in the snow holding another man's parcel.

I killed his fire with snow, took the scroll, and ran.
