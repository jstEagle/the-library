## 2. A Stranger at the Salt Gate

The Salt Gate waystation was the kind of place that existed only because the Empire had never gotten around to demolishing it. A stone shell with a roof that leaked in three places and a hearth that smoked in all of them, perched at the throat of the Kestrel Pass where the Thornfell mountains squeezed travelers thin enough to rob. I'd been holed up there for two days waiting out a squall, eating hardtack that could have doubled as roof tile, and telling myself that the itch between my shoulder blades was just the damp.

It wasn't the damp.

You learn things on the smuggler roads after enough years. You learn that mules go quiet before rockslides. You learn that when the birds stop arguing over the pass, something worse than weather is moving through it. And you learn — this one took me longest, being a professional liar even to myself — that the itch between your shoulder blades is almost never about what's behind you. It's about what's coming.

I was feeding the fire a splintered chair leg when I heard him on the switchbacks below: not footsteps, exactly. The suggestion of footsteps. The sound a body makes when it has stopped caring whether it arrives anywhere at all.

I put out the fire first. Habit. Light draws eyes, and eyes draw soldiers, and soldiers in the high passes in late autumn mean either deserters or a hunt, and both of them want whatever's in your pack.

Then I did the stupid thing. I went to the door.

He came up out of the dark like something the mountain had chewed and returned. Tall, or he would have been standing; he moved like a man folded around a wound. His cloak had been good once — Aurelian gray, officer's cut, though someone had torn the rank insignias off with more anger than care. Blood had soaked through his left side and frozen in the wind, so it cracked when he walked, a soft creaking rhythm like an old door opening over and over.

"Waystation," he said, as if confirming it for himself. Then he saw me, and I watched a man make a decision in real time. His hand twitched toward a knife that wasn't there anymore. He decided against dying fighting.

"You're Voss," he said.

Now, there are maybe six people in the Thornfells who know that name and mean *me*, and I keep the list short on purpose. "You've got the wrong mountain," I said. "Voss froze to death last winter. Very sad. Everyone says I look nothing like him."

He laughed. It cost him. He leaned against the doorframe and left a smear of red on the stone, and said, "Still doing that thing where you talk instead of running. Sera told me you'd do that."

The name landed somewhere under my ribs before my head caught up with it. Sera. There was only one Sera who'd have cause to describe me to anyone, and she wore imperial gray and carried a warrant with my face on it, and I had spent four years making sure our roads didn't cross.

But this man wasn't wearing a uniform. Not a whole one.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"A courier," he said, which was true, and then, "a thief," which was also true, and then he looked at me with eyes going glassy at the edges and said, "the last honest man in Cindralle, if you can believe it," which I couldn't, because no such thing had ever existed.

He came inside because his legs made the decision for him. I got him to the hearth — relit the fire, since hiding from a dying man seemed beside the point — and cut the cloak off his left side, and did the arithmetic every road-doctor does. The blade had gone in under the ribs and come out lower down, which meant it had gone through something important on the way. He'd been riding with that for a day, maybe two, in freezing rain. The blood he'd already lost wasn't coming back, and the blood still leaving him wasn't asking my permission.

"You need a surgeon," I said.

"I need ten minutes and a man who listens." He caught my wrist. His grip was astonishing for a dead man — cold fingers, iron intent. "They'll be here within the hour. Soldiers. Not pass-patrol. Warrant-men, inquisitor's detail. They know I came through Kestrel."

"How do you know I'm—"

"Sera Aldane described you." Something flickered across his face — pain, or the ghost of amusement. "'Talks instead of running. Will lie about his own name. Watch the hands; he steals without looking.' Her words. She wrote them in an official report, Voss. Do you understand? Your oldest friend is hunting you with the full weight of the Aurelian Empire behind her pen."

"I don't have an oldest friend," I said. "I have debts. Some of them are people."

He smiled at that, briefly, and then coughed, and the cough brought up red, and we both pretended we hadn't seen how much. I pressed a fresh pad of cloth against the wound and he let me, mostly because it kept my hands busy while his mouth worked.

"The map," he said.

"What map?"

"The map you're about to wish you'd never touched." He fumbled inside his shirt, and I swear on every saint the smugglers pretend to worship, my hands moved to help him before my brain issued the order. That's the thing about obligation — it gets into your muscles faster than your judgment.

What he pulled out was a roll of parchment the length of my forearm, sealed at the center with wax the color of dried wine. The seal bore a device I didn't recognize: a crown, but wrong — drawn as if by someone who'd only had it described to them. Midnight-colored metal, if metal it was, set into the wax like a splinter of night.

"It doesn't open," he said. "Not for me. Not for anyone but—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, and I understood he was rationing his secrets now, spending each one where it bought the most. "It's keyed. It wants memories. It takes them. And everything it shows you will cost you something you didn't agree to lose."

"Wonderful. Does it come with a warranty?"

"Voss." My name again, and this time he said it like a man setting down something heavy. "Cindralle isn't gone."

"I know. It's a story grandmothers tell. Kingdom erased from history, yada yada, the Empire writes the books now—"

"It isn't gone. It's *hidden*. There's a difference, and the difference is a door, and the door remembers everything the Empire paid to forget." He pushed the map against my chest with the last of his force. "Take it east. Take it to the valley past the Weeping Stones. And whatever it shows you — whoever it makes you remember — don't trust it to love you back. Maps like this were built to use people. I was built to carry one. You were built to survive carrying it. That's all any of us are, in the end. Built."

"That's a terrible philosophy and also you're bleeding on me."

"I know," he said gently. "I'm sorry. I chose you because you're the only thief I ever heard of who keeps nothing. They'll tear apart everyone who might hide it. But you — you hoard no past, you keep no letters, you sleep in a different ruin every night. You're the safest man in the mountains, Voss, precisely because you're the emptiest."

*Emptiest.* I've been called a great many things by a great many people, most of them accurate, several of them actionable. But that word went into me like a splinter of its own.

"I keep things," I said. It came out smaller than I intended.

"Do you?" He looked at me with real curiosity, the way scholars look at interesting ruins. "Name one."

I opened my mouth.

And here is the thing I will carry until someone steals it from me: I couldn't. Oh, I had facts. I knew I'd been born somewhere in the low Thornfells. I knew my mother had existed, in the abstract way one knows a debt existed — I'd sold her face to Maren Quill years ago, or rather I'd sold the memory of it, and what remained was the receipt. I knew there had been a girl named Sera who'd shared a rooftop and half a stolen loaf with me one endless summer, because I'd sold those too, one piece at a time across two lean winters, until all I owned of her was the word *Sera* and a shape of warmth with no features in it.

I kept coin. I kept tools. I kept my skills, because you can't sell the hand that does the stealing without losing the stealing.

"Exactly," the stranger said, reading my silence like a ledger. "So it can't take anything from you that you'll miss. Do you see? That's why it has to be you. That's the whole cruel joke of it. The Empire spent twenty years teaching its subjects to forget Cindralle, and the only men who can carry its memory safely now are the ones who were never allowed to keep anything of their own."

Outside, the wind shifted, and beneath it — faint, far down the pass — came the sound I'd been dreading all evening. Iron. Boot-iron on wet stone, many boots, marching in step, because warrant-men march in step even where marching is idiotic.

His eyes cleared for one moment, sharp as a hawk's, and he gripped my collar.

"They must not read it," he whispered. "If they complete it, they won't restore Cindralle — they'll *own* it. The crown. The claim. Everything." Blood ran from the corner of his mouth and he spoke faster, spending words like a gambler down to his last chips. "The door remembers. Tell them the door remembers. Whoever holds the door holds the truth, and the truth is the only weapon they never learned to counter—"

Boots. Closer. A voice below, barking orders, echoing off the pass walls.

"Go out the back," he said. "There's a scree slope behind the woodpile. It looks like death and it isn't, mostly. Take the map. Don't open it. Don't let it open *you*." He sagged back against the hearthstone, and the frost-cracked cloak settled around him like a shroud deciding to get a head start. "Voss. One more thing."

I should have gone right then. Every survival instinct I owned was screaming the arithmetic: one dying stranger versus a squad of warrant-men, and the stranger had already given me the goods. Leave. Climb. Live.

Instead I heard myself ask, "Your name. What's your name?"

Something moved behind his eyes — surprise, maybe, or grief for a courtesy arriving too late to matter. "Corvane," he said. "Lieutenant Corvane. Formerly of the Fourth Column. Currently of nothing at all." And then, with the ghost of that dry smile: "Tell Sera the kindness worked. She'll know what I mean. She always was kinder than her job."

The door burst inward.

I went through the back window — not the scenic scree-slope exit he'd advertised, because I'd spotted the window earlier and scree takes time — and dropped into the dark behind the station as the first lantern-light spilled through the front doorway. Behind me I heard the crash of furniture, a sergeant's voice snarling *"Where is he,"* and then a pause, a long one, the particular quality of silence that means a roomful of armed men has found exactly what they were hunting and it's too late.

Then a single shot from an arquebus, flat and final in the cold air.

I didn't look back. Looking back is a luxury item and I've never kept much stock.

* * *

The scree slope nearly killed me anyway, which would have been an embarrassing epilogue. Twice the shale gave way under me and twice I arrested myself on exposed roots, hanging over a drop that ended in a frozen streambed, the map crushed against my chest under my coat like a second heartbeat. Below and behind, torchlight crawled along the pass road. Voices called to one another in the clipped cadence of a search line. Once, very close, a soldier stood not ten feet above me on the rim of the slope, silhouetted against the snow-glow, and I lay in the shadow of a boulder and practiced being a stone myself — a skill, incidentally, that I have never once managed to sell, though gods know I've tried.

They searched the waystation until well past midnight. I heard them turn it inside out. I heard their frustration build. And near the end I heard a woman's voice arrive — low, controlled, carrying that particular authority that doesn't need volume — and every soldier's tone change to answer it.

I didn't need to see the gray cloak or the warrant case at her belt. Four years of careful avoidance hadn't dulled my ear. Sera Aldane had come to the Salt Gate herself.

She spoke to her men for a long time. I caught fragments. *"He passed through here two days ago — the ferryman saw him." "Search the slope. He's a thief, not a mountaineer; he'll have taken the road."* And once, quieter, almost to herself, in a tone I couldn't parse at all:

"He was always here. Why is he always *here*?"

Then the torches withdrew down the pass toward the ferry crossing, taking the light with them, and the mountain closed its dark hand around me again.

I climbed the rest of the way down, crossed the streambed where the water ran black under ice, and put six miles between me and the Salt Gate before I let myself stop. Found a hollow under a leaning granite slab that some earlier fugitive — centuries ago, judging by the soot — had used for the same purpose. No fire. I chewed hardtack and watched my breath fog and finally, with the reluctance of a man drawing a knife he suspects is cursed, took out the map.

Wax seal, midnight crown, parchment the color of old bone. I held it up to the starlight. Blank. Utterly blank — no roads, no borders, no watermark. Just paper pretending with tremendous confidence to be worth killing for.

Which, granted, describes most paper.

"Door remembers," I said to it. "That's it? That's the whole briefing? Dying-man wisdom comes with no refund, you know."

The map said nothing, being a map, but I felt — and I want to be precise here, because I traffic in precision — I felt something on the edge of perception, like a room next door where someone has just stopped talking. Attention. The specific weight of being *considered*.

I wrapped it away fast and told myself the crawl up my spine was the cold.

Here's what I'd resolved, lying there under the granite: deliver it east, to the valley past the Weeping Stones, collect whatever gratitude exists in a dead kingdom (none, historically), and be done. A courier run. I'd carried stranger cargo for worse pay. The fact that a lieutenant of the Fourth Column had died buying me a night's head start — that fact I filed where I file all inconvenient facts, in the drawer marked *survival requires selective arithmetic.*

Except.

Except Lieutenant Corvane had known Sera. Had spoken of her the way you speak of someone whose goodness you witnessed at close range and never recovered from. *Kinder than her job.* And the only version of Sera I had left — the featureless warm shape, the word and no face — had stirred in its drawer when he said her name, turning over in its sleep like a dog hearing its owner's step on stairs I can't remember building.

I don't want it back. That's the professional position. Memory is inventory, inventory is risk, and the empty man walks through checkpoints lighter than any smuggler's load. I have told clients this. I have told myself this across a hundred cold campsites, and mostly I believe it.

Mostly.

Dawn came up gray over the eastern peaks, and with it came the itch between my shoulder blades again — except now I knew its name. Somewhere behind me, Sera Aldane was reading a report that included a dead courier, a missing map, and my description in her own handwriting. *Watch the hands.* She knew me better than I knew myself, which these days was a low bar cleared by nearly everyone.

I stood, brushed the frost off, and turned east toward the Weeping Stones.

The map sat against my ribs, quiet as a held breath, patient as a debt.

Waiting to be opened. Waiting, I suspected, to open me.
