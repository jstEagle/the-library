## 3. What the Map Refuses

The map refused me for three days.

I tried everything a thief knows. I held it to candlelight looking for watermarks. I held it to firelight looking for heat-marks. I held it to moonlight, which is the light smugglers trust most, and got nothing but the same blank cream-colored nothing, smooth as a liar's conscience. No ink. No creases worth reading. Just the wax seal at its corner, unbroken, dark red as a wound that had decided to scab over in style.

"You're a beautiful piece of nothing," I told it, on the second night, in a waystation loft that smelled of goat and old smoke. The goats below regarded me through the floorboards with the flat judgment of their kind. "Some dying man bled out in the snow so you could waste my time."

The map said nothing, being parchment, but I felt — and I want to be careful with this word because I don't use it lightly — I felt *seen*. Which is not something paper should do. Paper should lie there and take what's coming to it, like everyone else in Thornfell.

Let me back up.

After the Salt Gate, after the blood and the whispering and the soldiers with their torches carving orange holes in the mountain dark, I had run the only way a memory-thief runs: badly, then well. Badly for the first hour, crashing downslope through scrub pine with my heart trying to exit through my ribs, every snap of a branch behind me a crossbow bolt finding its target early. Then well, because fear burns off fast when you've spent your whole life renting out pieces of yourself, and what's left over is a kind of cold arithmetic. Cold arithmetic said: they're hunting the map, not the man, so long as the man doesn't act like he has it. So I rolled it into the hollow core of my walking staff, sealed the ends with pitch, and walked — *walked*, gods help me, casual as Sunday — down the shepherd track toward the low passes while two imperial outriders clattered past me asking after a bleeding stranger.

"Didn't see him," I said, which was true, in the sense that dead men are hard to see.

They looked at my staff. I let them look. There's an art to letting soldiers look at a thing; you hold your face bored and your body tired and you think about something genuinely dull, because men who hunt for a living read faces the way I read memories, and the best place to hide a stolen thing is inside a mind that looks empty. I thought about goats. It worked. They rode on toward the pass, and I turned the other way, up into the high country where winter comes early and questions come never.

Three days of that. And the whole time, the staff against my shoulder, I could feel the map the way you feel a coin sewn into a coat lining — a small wrong weight, a presence with opinions.

Here's the other thing I should confess, since apparently we're being honest now, this chapter and I. A dying stranger gives you something, and there are two ways to take it. You can take it as a gift, or you can take it as a debt. I took it as a debt, because in my trade everything is a debt. Every memory I've ever lifted was owed to someone — the grief I drained from Maren Quill's clients, the joys I sold to lonely officers, all of it moving hand to hand like coin, and none of it, none of it ever mine. I have traded away pieces of my own past to cover shortfalls. That's the dirty secret of my profession: we steal memories because we can't afford to keep our own. Somewhere in my head there are gaps shaped like my mother's face and my tenth winter and the name of the first person I ever loved, gaps I filled with other people's weather.

So when the stranger pressed this thing into my hands and died saying *the door remembers*, I understood one thing clearly: whatever the door remembered, I would end up paying for it.

* * *

On the third night the storm came down off the peaks like something with intent, and I holed up in a shepherd's hut — stone walls, turf roof, a hearth that some considerate dead shepherd had left stocked with peat. I built a fire, ate the last of the smoked fish I'd bought at the waystation with the merchant's coin, and took the map out to insult it properly by firelight.

That's when I noticed the detail I'd missed, and I'm telling you this so you understand I'm not entirely a fool, merely mostly one. The blankness wasn't uniform. Under the fire's flicker, the parchment's surface moved. Not obviously. Not the way ink moves. But there was a depth to it, a faint shimmer beneath the surface like light on water too deep to see the bottom of. Blank parchment doesn't shimmer. Blank parchment is just dead skin off a tree. This was more like —

More like a surface. Like the top layer of something.

And here is where I did the stupid thing, the thief's instinct, the same instinct that makes us touch the locked box even when the locked box is probably trapped. Because I am a professional, and professionals know that some locks aren't locks at all, they're invitations with teeth. And because — I'll say it plainly — three days alone with a mystery in a storm will make anyone stupid, and loneliness is the cheapest key there is.

I pulled off my glove and touched it barehanded.

* * *

Cindralle.

I need you to understand that word arrived in my head whole, before anything else, the way a name arrives before the person. Cindralle. And with the name came bells — bronze bells ringing from towers I could see, could *feel* the cold stone of under my palms, because I was leaning on a balustrade and the balustrade was real and my hands were someone else's hands and I didn't care, I didn't care, because the city below me was —

Lanterns. Ten thousand lanterns going up along the river terraces at dusk, street by street, district by district, a tide of warm gold climbing the hillsides. The river itself ran silver-green and thick with barges hung in colored silk, and the barges were singing — no, the people on them were singing, a harvest song in a language that my borrowed throat knew the way you know your own pulse. Terraced gardens cut into the valley walls, fig trees and pomegranate, and above them the white walls of the palace catching the last sun, and above *that* the crown-spire, slender as a needle threaded with light, where —

Where the Midnight Crown sat. I knew that without being told. The way you know your own house in the dark. The crown that was crowned only at midnight, once a year, by whoever the city chose, and the choosing was the whole government, the whole law, the whole point — a kingdom ruled by consent renewed annually like a lease, and everyone laughing at the Aurelians across the mountains who needed armies to feel legitimate.

I stood on that balcony in someone else's body and smelled woodsmoke and cardamom and the green smell of the river in autumn, and somewhere below a child was shrieking with joy about lanterns, and I thought, with a clarity that had nothing to do with me: *this is what they took. This is what isn't anywhere anymore.*

Because I knew that too, suddenly, the way the memory knew things — knew it the way you know the ending of a song even mid-verse. I knew the lanterns would go out. I knew the barges would burn. I knew the white walls were white because they hadn't been smoked yet, and the crown-spire would come down, and the language my borrowed mouth sang in would be beaten out of children in schools with imperial names. Not because Cindralle lost a war. Because Cindralle would be *unwritten* — decreed never to have been, its records burned, its names struck, its very valley folded into somebody else's map until even grief had nowhere to stand.

The memory turned then, the way memories do when they're old ones — it went soft at the edges, went golden, went *home*. And underneath the wonder, faint as a heartbeat under a pillow, I felt the other thing. The thing I almost missed because the lanterns were so bright.

Hunger.

Not mine. The map's. The memory wasn't being given. It was being *spent* — paid out to me like coin from a purse, and the purse wanted refilling. I felt it lean against the inside of my skull with the patient pressure of a beggar who knows you'll come back to the same doorway tomorrow. It had shown me this because showing is how it feeds. Somewhere in that shimmer under the surface was a ledger, and ledgers in my experience always balance, and I had just eaten without paying.

I tore my hand off the parchment.

I sat back on my heels in the freezing hut, breathing like I'd run the pass again, firelight jumping on the walls, and the map lay on the stones between us, blank again, innocent again, wax seal gleaming. My hands were shaking. Not from fear. From *want*, if I'm honest, and honesty is a habit I'm told is fatal in my line of work. For thirty seconds I had been a man from a city that doesn't exist, standing in evening air that smelled like home, and my own life — the debts, the huts, the hired-out grief — seemed thin as the parchment in comparison.

"That," I said aloud, to the map, to the goats of the world generally, "is the most dangerous thing you could have done."

Because I know hunger when I feel it. I've made my living inside other people's hunger for years — their hunger for dead sons' laughter, for first loves, for the faces of mothers. I know exactly what a hungry thing sounds like when it offers you warmth. It sounds like a bargain.

The map fed on memories. Stolen ones, kept ones, it didn't care — it had drunk from the dying stranger somehow, or he had poured himself into it, and now it had shown me a taste. A sample. The first taste is always free in this trade. Everyone knows that. I *am* the free first taste, professionally speaking.

And it wanted more. I could feel it wanting, the way you feel a hook wanting a fish. More memories, richer ones, and it didn't specify whose, and that silence was the loudest part.

I wrapped the map around my bare hand anyway — carefully, cloth between us, a professional courtesy — and that's when I found the second thing hidden inside the roll. Tucked against the wax, folded small, a slip of heavy imperial vellum, the kind warrants are printed on, with the black-and-gold stamp of the Inquisitor's Office pressed deep enough to feel with a thumbnail.

I should have burned it unread. Note that I considered it. Note also that I am who I am.

I opened it.

*By order of the Viceroy of the Western Reaches, all checkpoints, garrisons, and licensed informants are instructed to apprehend for questioning:* — and then a description, and the description was mine. Not approximately mine. Mine. *A male of middle years, lean, dark-haired, scarred left brow, known in the Thornfell trade under various names.* Various names. They'd listed four of them. Two I hadn't used in a decade. One I'd only ever used with Maren Quill.

Maren. Someone had sold me. Or someone had sold the road, and I'd simply been standing on it.

But that wasn't the part that stopped my breath. Below the description, in a different hand — smaller, precise, a hand that wrote the way surgeons cut — a notation:

*Subject is known to the undersigned. Approach with minimum force. He will come if asked kindly. — S.A.*

S.A.

I sat in that hut with a dead kingdom blooming behind my eyes and a piece of vellum in my fingers, and I read that line five times, six, and each time it landed worse. *He will come if asked kindly.* Not a threat. Worse than a threat — a *recipe*. Somebody in the Inquisitor's Office knew me. Knew me well enough to know that kindness works on me better than irons, which is a humiliating thing to have written about you in an official document, and a true thing, which is worse.

I tried to remember who. I reached back through my own head the way I reach through other people's, hunting for an S, for a face attached to those initials — and hit the gaps. The smooth, rented-out gaps where my childhood used to be. I had sold those years long ago, piece by piece, to cover debts I barely remembered incurring. Whatever face went with S.A., whoever had once asked me for things kindly enough that I came — that person lived in a part of me I'd spent.

The map lay on the stones, blank and patient, full of lanterns and hunger. Outside, the storm screamed down the valley like it had a warrant of its own. And somewhere south, following kindly, was S.A.

I banked the fire, rolled the warrant into my boot, the map into the staff, and lay down staring at the turf roof, doing the coldest arithmetic I knew.

The empire wanted the map. The map wanted memories. And I was the only man alive carrying both prices in the same pair of boots.

Sleep, when it finally took me, tasted like cardamom and woodsmoke from a city that had never existed — and somewhere in the dark of me, something whispered back, patient as a ledger: *the door remembers. What will you remember?*

In the morning, I'd start walking toward Maren Quill. Thieves' arithmetic: when everyone wants what you carry, find the fence who'll tell you what it costs.
