## 4. The Inquisitor's Kindness

The smuggler's name was Doss Halloway, and he lied the way other men breathed — constantly, automatically, and without any expectation that anyone would notice.

I noticed. Noticing was my trade.

"Never heard of any Vesper Voss," he said for the fourth time, spitting into the sawdust at his feet as if punctuation required expectoration. "Name doesn't mean anything to me."

"That's strange," I said pleasantly, because I had learned long ago that unpleasantness made liars dig in while pleasantness made them relax, and relaxed men made mistakes. "Because your wife says otherwise."

Doss's face did something complicated. Grief, fear, and arithmetic all fighting for the same real estate.

"She wouldn't," he managed.

"She would and she did. She's frightened, Doss. Frightened people tell me things. It's practically a law of nature." I let the silence stretch, then added, gently: "She also told me where you keep the second ledger. The one with the names of everyone you've moved through Salt Gate this season. Including, on the ninth of Frostmonth, a man traveling alone who paid in *memories*."

Nobody paid in memories except memory-thieves. And there was exactly one memory-thief working the Thornfell roads this autumn whose description matched the man on my warrant down to the crooked way he held his left shoulder — an old break, badly set, from a fall neither of us had ever talked about.

Doss looked at his own hands like they'd betrayed him. In fairness, they probably had. Hands always do eventually.

"I want it understood," he said, "that whatever I say next, I say under duress."

"Noted. Duress is my favorite context." I pulled up the room's only other stool and sat, which surprised him; inquisitors were supposed to loom. Looming was for amateurs and theater. Sitting put us eye to eye, and eye to eye was where I did my best work. "Tell me about the ninth of Frostmonth, Doss. What did he look like?"

"You've got the description."

"I've got the Empire's description. I want yours. The Empire writes about men. I'm asking what a man looks like when he's trying not to be seen."

That landed. Doss blinked at me, and for a moment the smuggler fell away and just a tired middle-aged man was sitting in sawdust, missing his wife, wishing he'd never gotten into the moving-people business at all.

"Tall," he said finally. "Thin the way winter makes you thin. Dark hair going gray early at the temples. Kept looking at the door without looking at the door, if you take my meaning. Polite. Too polite. The kind of polite that means he's counting exits." He swallowed. "He paid for passage with a memory. Sold it to old Maren Quill right there in my back room. I didn't see what it was. But after, he stood by the window a long time with his hand over his face, and Maren — who has no heart, I've watched her cheat widows — Maren couldn't look at him either. So whatever it was, it was something that mattered."

Something that mattered.

I wrote that down even though I already knew it, because writing things down is how you keep yourself honest, and honesty was the one tool in my kit that hadn't gone dull yet.

"Where was he headed?"

"He bought passage south. Toward Cindralle country." Doss said the name carefully, the way everyone did now, like handling a knife by the blade. "Though between you and me, lady, nobody goes *to* Cindralle country. There's nothing there. Never was anything there. Just valleys full of—" he waved vaguely, "—old stone. Farmers. The Empire's been settling it forty years and there's still more ghosts than people, far as anyone can say."

"There are no ghosts," I said. "There are only things the Empire hasn't finished explaining yet."

"Amen to that," said Doss Halloway, with feeling, and then remembered who he was talking to and went pale.

I let him go two days later, once his testimony stopped changing. That's another thing they don't put in the ballads about inquisitors: we're supposed to release people when we're done with them. Most don't bother. I bothered, because I remembered being someone the Empire could have simply discarded, and having been that person once makes it very hard to be careless about others later.

Not impossible. Hard.

* * *

Here is what the Empire knew about Vesper Voss, according to the warrant folded inside my breastplate, worn soft at the creases from reading:

Memory-thief. Unlicensed. Operating along the Thornfell smuggler roads for approximately eleven years. Method: skin contact, duration unknown, victims report loss ranging from trivial to catastrophic. Considered nonviolent but dangerous. Reward offered for capture alive. *Alive* was underlined twice, which told me somebody important wanted him breathing, and nobody important wants a thief breathing unless they want something out of his head.

And here is what I knew about Vesper Voss, which the Empire did not, because the Empire had never shared a blanket with him in a cellar in Thornfell while the wind tried to peel the roof off the world:

His real name wasn't Vesper. His real name was Ves. He hated the dark so much he slept facing doorways. He could pick a lock before he could read. He cried only once a year, on Founding Day, when the matron made the orphans sing the Aurelian anthem, and he never told anyone why, and I never asked, because I understood without asking that some griefs need to stay unexplained to stay survivable.

We were eight years old together in the Thornfell foundling house, which was less a house than a habit the Empire had fallen into and never broken. The Empire collected orphans the way it collected provinces: efficiently, without malice, and without ever once wondering what it intended to do with them. There were thirty-one of us that winter. By spring there were twenty-six, and nobody held a service, because services cost candles and candles cost coin and the Empire's ledger had no column for grief.

Ves and I survived by dividing the labor. He stole; I watched. He was small and quick and invisible, and I had a face that made adults believe whatever I told them, which turned out to be the single most valuable talent either of us ever developed, though it took me years to admit it. Between us we ate through two winters that should have killed us. He taught me which guards pocketed bribes and which pockets were worth picking. I taught him to read, using a stolen hymnal, sounding out words of praise for an empire that had forgotten we existed.

When the recruitment wagons came — and they came every few years, sweeping the foundling houses for children tall enough to carry a spear or sharp enough to carry a grudge — they took eleven of us. They took me because I tested well at obedience with initiative. They did not take Ves because he bit the assessor, and the Empire does not waste rations on children who bite.

I remember standing in the wagon bed, holding the rail, watching him watch me go. He wasn't crying. He was doing that thing with his face — the flattening, the shuttering, the whole boy folding himself up small behind his own eyes. I shouted something at him. I have spent twenty years unable to remember what I shouted, and I've begun to suspect that's because I never actually shouted anything, and what I remember as a shout is just the shape of everything I wish I'd said.

They trained me. That's the word they used. *Trained*, like I was a hawk. And I suppose I was — a hawk is a fine thing to be, if you never think too hard about whose wrist you sit on.

I hunted deserters first, then smugglers, then seditionists, and I was good at it, and the promotions came, and each promotion came with a slightly larger office and a slightly smaller soul. Somewhere around my thirtieth year I noticed that I had become the exact sort of person the eight-year-old version of me would have picked clean in an alley without a flicker of remorse, and instead of troubling me the way it should have, the thought arrived with something adjacent to pride.

You can build an entire life out of not thinking about certain things. I recommend it. It's efficient. It works right up until it doesn't.

For me it stopped working on a Tuesday, three months ago, when a courier handed me a folder containing the warrant, and I read the name, and the office walls went distant and quiet, and somewhere underneath twenty years of imperial discipline a girl in a cellar started screaming *that's Ves, that's Ves, he bites assessors and sleeps facing doorways.*

They assigned me the hunt because I knew the territory. That was the official reason. The unofficial reason, which I understood perfectly and they never wrote down, was that I was expendable in exactly the ways that mattered: competent enough to succeed, provincial enough not to be missed if I failed. The Empire spends its children the way Thornfell spent firewood — gratefully, and until it runs out.

* * *

The trouble with hunting someone you love is that you know all their tricks, and worse, they know all of yours.

Every smuggler I squeezed gave me the same picture: a thief who worked the roads alone, who never took more than he needed, who had a reputation — an actual *reputation* — for refusing to steal certain kinds of memories. Children, mostly. First loves, sometimes. One fence told me, laughing into her cups, that Voss had once returned a memory he'd taken by accident rather than sell it, and had taken a beating for his scruples.

A thief with standards. A predator with a conscience. The Empire's file called him *dangerous*. My sources described a man who apologized to his marks.

Both things could be true. Both things usually are, with people. But only one of them fit the boy who'd once given half his bread ration to a smaller orphan and then threatened the smaller orphan into silence about it, because charity was weakness and weakness got you dead in Thornfell, and Ves had understood at seven years old what the Empire still pretended not to know: that kindness and survival are natural enemies, and every act of decency is a small defection.

He'd been defecting his whole life, in other words. Quietly. One stolen memory at a time.

Which meant that when I caught him — and I would catch him; I had never failed to close a hunt, and I told myself the certainty felt professional rather than hungry — I would be catching a man mid-defection. A man running toward something. And the warrant said *alive*, and the underline said *important*, and none of it said what would happen to him afterward, because warrants never do. Warrants are the Empire's way of starting stories without admitting how they end.

So I constructed a mercy. You have to understand, I genuinely believed it. Believing your own mercy is what separates an inquisitor from a butcher, and I clung to that distinction the way a drowning woman clings to driftwood that is, upon honest inspection, part of the wreck.

The mercy went like this: Vesper Voss was wasting away out here. Thieving memories piece by piece, spending his own past like a drunkard spends wages, until there'd be nothing left of him but competence and appetite. I had seen what thieves became at the end of that road. Hollow men in warm coats, smiling at nothing. The Empire, by contrast, offered structure. Purpose. Perhaps — if he cooperated, if the people who wanted him alive wanted him for reasons short of vivisection — a pardon, a post, a life. I would bring him in. I would vouch for him. Sera Aldane, who tested well at obedience with initiative, would spend her accumulated credit on one reckless withdrawal, and the boy from the cellar would get what neither of us ever had: a future with walls that kept the weather out.

It was a beautiful mercy. I polished it nightly. And if there was a voice in the back of my skull — small, flat, eight years old, watching from a wagon bed — that pointed out that the Empire erased kingdoms and called it peace, and might manage the erasure of one inconvenient thief without breaking stride — well. I had twenty years of practice not listening to voices.

* * *

Frostmonth deepened. The passes began to close. I worked my way south along the salt road, and the trail warmed under me like a blooded thing.

At a waystation called the Kettle, a hostler swore he'd seen a lone traveler heading toward the high valley routes — the ones that skirted Cindralle country — carrying nothing visible but walking like a man loaded down. At a shrine two days further on, a lay sister mentioned a traveler who'd asked odd questions. Not about roads. About *history*. Whether anyone remembered, before the Empire, what the valleys had been called.

Whether anyone remembered anything at all.

I stood in that cold little shrine with its imperial sun hammered into the lintel, and I felt the first hairline crack run through my beautiful mercy, because there was only one reason a memory-thief starts asking historians' questions.

He wasn't spending what he stole anymore.

He was *looking* for something. Following it. Which meant something out there in the blank white reaches of Cindralle country — the country of nothing, the country of never-was-anything — had gotten its hooks into Ves Voss, and whatever it was, it had scared him badly enough to make him polite, and determinedly enough to make him walk toward the one direction every sensible criminal in these mountains was fleeing.

I requisitioned horses, provisions, and two auxiliaries whom I did not trust and did not pretend to, and I rode south into the closing winter after a man I loved, carrying a warrant with his name on it and a mercy in my chest that was starting, despite all my polishing, to smell faintly of iron.

On the third night, camped under a ledge while sleet ticked against the tarp, one of the auxiliaries asked me why the Empire wanted this particular thief alive. He was young. He meant it as small talk.

"Because he's useful," I said.

"To who?"

"Someone above my pay grade."

He nodded and went back to his ration, satisfied, and I lay awake a long time listening to the sleet and thinking about the ninth of Frostmonth, and the second ledger, and a man standing at a window with his hand over his face while a heartless fence couldn't meet his eyes.

Whatever he'd sold that day, it had mattered. Whatever he was chasing, it had teeth. And I was riding toward both with two men I didn't trust and a story I told myself at night to sleep.

In the morning, the pass ahead of us stood open exactly long enough to let us through — and then, behind us, with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, it closed.

The snow took our back-trail. All of it.

Ahead lay Cindralle country, and whatever waited there, and no way home but forward.

"Well," said the young auxiliary cheerfully, stamping his boots. "Nothing for it, eh, ma'am?"

Nothing for it. I looked south into the white, where a boy who bit assessors was following a ghost through the one kingdom the Empire had decided to forget — and I wondered, for the first time, whether I was bringing him the mercy.

Or whether he'd been sent out here to bring it to me.
