## 5. Thieves' Arithmetic

Maren Quill kept her counting house in the belly of a dead mine, three switchbacks above the last honest tavern in Thornfell, and she liked to say the only reason she hadn't been robbed yet was that everyone who knew where it was owed her money. I was no exception. I owed her enough that the arithmetic had started to feel personal.

The tunnel mouth breathed cold air at me as I ducked under the collapsed lintel, and I did what I always did: counted my exits. One behind me, half-choked with scree. One ahead, through the old ore shafts, if you knew the sequence of turns — which Maren assumed I didn't and I made a point of never confirming. Two lamps burned at the far end of the passage, guttering on tallow that smelled like a candle factory's apology.

"Voss." Her voice came out of the dark before her face did, the way it always did. "You look like a man who's been running."

"I've been walking briskly."

"With imperial soldiers behind you?"

"Behind *somebody*," I said. "I'm just traveling in the same direction as the somebody."

She stepped into the lamplight. Maren Quill was sixty if she was a day, built like a root vegetable, with hands that had counted more coin than most banks and washed less often than most priests. She wore a coat lined with fox fur that had been fashionable when Thornfell still had trade routes instead of rumors of trade routes. Around her neck, on a cord, hung a dozen small glass vials, each one stoppered with wax, each one holding a curl of grey mist that moved when you weren't looking directly at it.

Memories. The fence's fence. The woman who bought stolen years by the ounce.

"You brought me something," she said. It wasn't a question. It was an invoice.

"I brought myself," I said. "Which is nearly the same thing, given current market rates."

Her eyes went to my left hand, where the map rode wrapped in oilcloth against my palm, and something flickered across her face — quick, professional interest, the look of a fisherman seeing an unusual shadow under the boat. I shifted so my body blocked the light from it. Old habit. New instinct. Something about the way strangers' eyes kept finding the thing made me want to keep it close and also, increasingly, to throw it off a cliff.

"Not that," I said. "That's not for sale."

"Everything's for sale. That's not cynicism, boy, that's bookkeeping." She turned and led me deeper into the mine, past shelves of goods whose provenance would have made a customs officer weep: salted pork, arrowheads, a crate of Aurelian prayer books with the pages hollowed into hiding slots. "Sit. Tell me what you need. Then tell me what you're paying. In that order, because I enjoy watching your face fall."

I sat on a barrel that complained under me. The lamp between us threw our shadows up the rock wall, two crooked shapes like a bad omen drawn by a child.

"Safe passage," I said. "South road, past Kettlewatch, down to the lowlands before the passes close. A guide who won't ask questions, horses that won't die, papers that will survive one inspection but preferably none."

"That's three things."

"That's one thing wearing three coats."

"And winter's coming early this year," Maren said, pouring something amber into two cups that were, generously, clean. "The shepherds say the birds left a month ago. The smugglers say the roads will be shut by Frostmoon. You want to travel *now*, with every checkpoint between here and the plains doubled because some poor bastard bled out at a waystation with an imperial seal in his pocket."

News traveled fast in Thornfell. Faster than mercy, certainly faster than me.

"I heard about that," I said carefully.

"Mm." She pushed a cup toward me. I didn't drink it. Poisoning clients was bad business, but Maren liked people to know the cup was hers to fill. "The soldiers pulled four bodies out of that station. Three of them were theirs. The fourth they carried out wrapped like a relic. And now there's a warrant going down every road with a description on it." She recited, flat as a toll notice: "*Male, late twenties, dark hair, scar through left brow, memory-thief by trade, travels alone.* They got your profession right, Voss. That's the part that stings, isn't it? Not the face. The résumé."

"The scar's new," I said. "It'll heal wrong and confuse them."

"Everything about you heals wrong." She drank. Set the cup down. Folded her hands, which meant the negotiation had begun and the friendship — such as it was, a load-bearing wall of mutual contempt — was taking its boots off. "Passage south. Guide, horses, papers. I can do it. Cost is steep."

"Name it."

"Coin's no good to me anymore. Coin buys goods, and goods don't move in a dying kingdom, and I am," she said, with the pride of a woman announcing a rare disease, "the last buyer of the only commodity that still holds its value on this mountain."

My stomach performed a small, controlled collapse. "Maren—"

"You owe me eight hundred crowns of principal and eleven years of interest, and you're going to pay me in *stock*." She tapped one of the vials at her throat. It chimed faintly, glass against glass, like a tiny bell tolling for someone else. "A memory. A real one. Yours."

"I don't sell my own."

"You've never *had* anything worth selling. That's been the arrangement. You steal other people's pasts and hoard the empty space where yours should be, and I've always wondered why." She leaned forward, lamplight pooling in the seams of her face. "But I saw your hand move when you covered that parcel. I saw how you held it. Like it was warm. Voss, either that map is worth a fortune, or it's already taken something from you, and either way I'm buying into that story today."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being a memory-thief: the theft is easy. Any hedge-witch can pull a recollection loose with a copper needle and a whispered word; children in the smuggling camps do it to each other for dares. What's hard is the keeping. What's hard is standing inside someone else's grief — a merchant's dead son laughing at a kitchen table, snow in his hair, bread burning on the stove — and pulling it out whole without letting any of it stick to you. You learn to hold memories the way you'd hold a hot coal: firmly, briefly, and with total commitment to putting it down.

You learn, above all, not to think about the shape of the hole it leaves behind. Because a memory doesn't come out clean. It takes a little of the room with it. Take a man's wedding day and he keeps the fact of the marriage but loses the smell of his wife's hair, and he'll spend the rest of his life knowing he loved someone without remembering why her. Take a mother's face —

Take a mother's face and you keep the knowledge that you had one. You keep the word. You lose everything the word used to open.

I'd done it to others a hundred times. I had never once stood on the selling side of the table.

"There has to be another way," I said, and hated how young my voice sounded.

"There's always another way. There's the soldiers' way. There's the snow's way. There's the way where you stay here and we wait together to see which finds you first." Maren produced a small brass scale from beneath the counter, the kind apothecaries use, and set it between us with a click that sounded horribly final. "Eight hundred crowns, Voss. One strong memory clears half. Two clears it all, plus passage. Or give me one *good* one and I'll call it even and throw in the guide."

"One good one," I repeated.

"Something with weight. Childhood. Family. Love, if you've ever had any, though looking at you I'd guess no." She said it without cruelty. That was the worst part. Maren Quill dealt in human souls the way a butcher deals in sides of beef, and she was gentle about it in exactly the way a butcher is gentle: efficiently, and with respect for the meat.

I thought about what I had left. It wasn't much, and that was by design. Years ago I'd started feeding my own past to the trade — not selling it, just... spending it, in small increments, the way a man stranded in winter burns his furniture. A memory of my father's voice, spent on a border bribe when I was nineteen. A memory of my sister's laugh — did I have a sister? I believed I'd had a sister; the belief was all that remained, a nail hole in a wall where a picture used to hang — traded away in Cindralle's last free port, back when Cindralle still pretended to be free. Each time I told myself the same lie: *this is the last one. After this, I keep the rest.*

And then the map. The blank parchment that had flooded me with a dead kingdom's morning — bells, cedar smoke, a woman's voice singing in a language I somehow knew and had never learned. It sat against my ribs like a second heartbeat, patient as debt.

When I'd touched it barehanded, it hadn't just shown me things. It had *taken* the showing as payment, a taste, a deposit — and I'd felt the absence afterward like a tooth missing. Whatever it wanted from me, it had already begun collecting.

Maybe that was why I said yes. Maybe I figured the map was going to eat my past anyway, and better it should pass through Maren's ledger on the way, and buy me a horse.

Or maybe — and I'm honest enough, in the parts of myself I haven't sold, to admit this — maybe I simply couldn't stand the thought of the soldiers catching me with it, and this was the fastest door.

"One memory," I said. "My choice which."

"My choice whether it's sufficient." Maren uncorked a vial and set the empty glass beside the scale like a priest setting out a reliquary. "Whenever you're ready. And Voss — don't fight it on the way out. Memories tear. Torn ones are worth half, and I'll know."

* * *

I closed my eyes and went looking for my mother.

This is harder than it sounds. You'd think a man would know where his own childhood lives inside him, but memory isn't stored, it's grown — roots tangled through everything else — and mine had been root-pruned so many times that finding one living branch meant walking a garden of stumps. I passed the place where my father's voice should have been and found smooth nothing. I passed the shape that might have been a sister, or a neighbor, or a story I invented to keep myself company on cold roads. The mind is kind that way. It fills holes with plausible weather.

But she was there. She was always there. Not a face — the face was gone, had been gone longer than I could remember, which is a sentence that stops making sense the moment you read it twice. What remained was a constellation of details orbiting an absence: flour on her forearms. A hummed tune, three notes of it, looping. The feeling of being picked up — the specific vertigo of small hands gripping a neck that smelled of woodsmoke and rosemary. The sound of her saying my name.

Not *Vesper*. Something before Vesper. Something shorter, softer, worn smooth like a river stone from saying.

I stood in that constellation for a long moment, in the dark behind my eyes, and I want to tell you I hesitated. I did hesitate. But hesitation is a luxury priced in seconds, and seconds were the one currency I'd never managed to steal.

*I'm sorry,* I said, to whoever she had been. *I'm sorry it's me.*

Then I took hold of it the way I take hold of other people's memories — gently, at the root — and pulled.

It came out whole. Flour on her forearms, three notes of the song, rosemary and woodsmoke, the vertigo, the name. For one impossible instant I held it outside myself and *saw* it — and here was the cruelty the trade never advertises: in the moment of extraction, the memory was more vivid than it had ever been while I owned it. I saw her. Brown skin, a scar on her chin, eyes the color of cheap beer and just as warm, laughing at something I couldn't hear because the hearing belonged to a boy whose head I was no longer in. I saw my mother for exactly as long as it took to lose her forever, and she was beautiful, and she had a scar on her chin, and I will never know that again.

The vial drank the mist. The scale, metaphorically, tipped.

When I opened my eyes, the mine was unchanged. The lamp. The barrel. Maren Quill holding the filled vial up to the light with the expression of a jeweler finding no flaws, and me sitting in a silence that had a new hole in it, shaped like a woman I could no longer picture.

"Well?" My voice came out steady. Thieves' trick: spend the trembling somewhere it can't be seen. "Sufficient?"

Maren looked at me for a long moment over the rim of the vial. And I'll say this for the old monster — she knew exactly what she was holding. I watched her decide not to gloat about it.

"Sufficient," she said quietly. "Half your debt, cleared. Passage, guide, papers, horses. You leave tonight, ahead of the snow and behind the soldiers' supper." She stoppered the vial and hung it on her cord with the others, where it swung against its neighbors, and I had to look away from the small grey curl turning slowly inside the glass. *Flour on her forearms. Rosemary. A scar on her chin.* Somewhere in that mine, in a glass jar, my mother hummed three notes to a boy who would never find her again.

"Voss." Maren's voice pulled me back. "One piece of professional advice, free of charge, which you know I hate."

"Then charge me."

"The thing in your wrap." She nodded at my hand. "I've fenced relics, charms, cursed coins, a saint's finger that bit people. I know hunger when I see it sitting across my scale. That map of yours isn't a map. Maps don't make men pay in advance." She tapped her temple. "Whatever door it's promising you, ask who's collecting the toll. Because in my experience there are only two kinds of magic in Thornfell, boy. The kind you steal. And the kind that steals you."

* * *

She was true to her ledger, as she always was. By nightfall I had papers in the name of Corvin Ashe, wool factor, dull enough to be believable; a guide named Tammas who asked no questions because he was mute and communicated entirely in gestures of profound skepticism; and two mountain ponies with the combined charisma of furniture and roughly triple its endurance.

We rode out of the mine's valley under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, the first flakes coming down fat and lazy, scouts for the army marching behind them. South road. Past Kettlewatch. Down before the passes closed.

Somewhere around the third mile, riding through snow that was beginning to mean it, I took out the map. Unwrapped it. Held it barehanded, because I needed to know what I'd bought my escape with, needed to see the waypoint bloom where my mother's face used to live.

The parchment warmed against my fingers. Ink crawled up out of nothing — not appearing so much as *remembering itself* — lines drawing rivers, ridgelines, a thread of road stitched south through the mountains. And there, where the thread bent west into a fold of hills below Kettlewatch, a mark bloomed: a small circle, precise as a seal, with a single word inscribed around its rim in that language I shouldn't have known and did:

*The Hollow Crown.*

Below it, smaller, in a different hand — hurried, human, the dead stranger's hand — a line of Common Tongue, pressed deep into the parchment like a warning scratched after the fact:

*Bring what it asks. It always asks.*

The ink faded. The map went blank again, smug as a closed ledger. Against my ribs, that second heartbeat settled, satisfied, fed.

It had drunk my mother's face the way I'd once drunk a stranger's grief — professionally, completely, leaving nothing on the bone.

I wrapped the map away and rode south through the thickening snow, repeating three notes of a song to myself, over and over, trying to hold a tune whose owner I had sold — and by the time we made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, I could remember that there had been a melody.

I could no longer remember how it went.

Tammas watched me across the fire with his eloquent silence, and I realized I was mouthing the shape of a name I no longer knew, and that the snow was falling harder, and that somewhere behind us, following my trail down every road on the mountain, came a woman with a warrant and a kindness I wouldn't recognize until it was too late.
