## 7. Ashes on the Ledger

The Imperial Archive Beneath the Ice keeps its secrets the way a miser keeps coins: counted, wrapped, and buried where no honest hand will ever warm them.

I had been down there three days before I understood that I was not the one doing the searching.

That is a strange sentence to write, and stranger to have lived, so let me begin properly — with the lie I told myself at the top of the stairs.

* * *

The Archive sits beneath the Viceregal Palace in Cael Amure like a heart that stopped beating two hundred years ago and was never buried. To reach it you descend nine flights of stairs cut from mountain granite, past sentries who check your warrant four times — once with eyes, once with seals, once with a lamp held close to your face as though treason might be written there, and once by simply looking at you for a long moment, which is the only check that ever finds anything.

I am good at that last one. I have been looked at by experts my whole life, and I know what they see: Sera Aldane, Inquisitor Third Rank, thirty-one years old, straight-backed, plain-spoken, loyal as a hound and twice as tireless. The Empire loves a woman who can be described in four lines. It means she can be filed.

My warrant that morning read, in the Viceregal secretary's careful hand: *Investigation of fugitive Voss, V. — supporting documentation, historical division.* Which is bureaucratic language for *go find out who this smuggler used to be*, which is itself bureaucratic language for *the Viceroy wants leverage and does not care what it costs you.*

I knew Vesper Voss had been born somewhere in the Thornfell corridor. So did every file I'd pulled. Thornfell births were recorded loosely even before the Empire standardized its censuses, and after annexation the mountain parishes burned half their own records out of spite and lost the other half to damp. What I needed was the other end of the thread. Not who he was — who we were. The orphanage records. The foundling rolls. The name of the matron who fed us barley porridge and called us both *little sparks* because we could never stop striking against each other.

I had not thought about the orphanage in years. That is not quite true. I had thought about it constantly and remembered almost nothing, which is a different ailment entirely. Memory in the Empire is a matter of filing. You keep what serves the report. I kept Vesper's laugh, the shape of his thin shoulders under a coat too big for him, the winter he gave me his boots and swore he'd won them gambling. I did not keep the name of our village. I checked myself on this, descending the ninth stair: I did not keep it because it had never mattered, or because it had mattered too much. With me, those two reasons have always worn the same coat.

The historical division smelled of cold stone and older paper. A clerk named Ossory met me — a small man, gray as dust, with the particular patience of someone who has watched important people come and go for forty years and expects to outlast them all.

"Inquisitor Aldane." He said my name as if tasting an old wine gone slightly off. "Thornfell materials. You're the second request this season."

"Who was the first?"

"The first was no one." He smiled without warmth. "This way."

* * *

They gave me a reading room with one lamp and one chair, because the Empire believes scholars work best in conditions resembling punishment. The boxes arrived on a cart wheeled by a boy too young to wonder about their contents. Annexation-era records, Thornfell administrative district. Census supplements. Tithe ledgers. And, at Ossory's suggestion when I mentioned foundlings, a box marked only **C-7** in faded ink, with no district designation at all.

I should have asked about C-7 immediately. Instead — and I record this honestly, because honesty is the one luxury left to me now — I started with the comfortable things. Tithe ledgers. Grain shipments. The dull arithmetic of occupation, which reassures you that the world is governed by men who count sacks.

Vesper would have laughed at me. He always said I loved rules the way other people loved sweet wine — too much, and against my own interest. He said it the summer we were twelve, the day I turned him in for stealing bread from the factor's wagon. He got the lash; I got a commendation from the garrison captain, who patted my head and told the matron I had *the makings of something useful*. Vesper didn't speak to me for a month. When he finally did, standing at the well in the gray dawn light, he said: "You'll go far, Sera. They'll carry you far. Just don't look down at what you're walking on."

I had forgotten that until this moment, sitting in a basement with a dead empire's paperwork in my hands. Memory is a matter of filing. Apparently some files open themselves.

* * *

It took me a full day to understand what C-7 was.

The label meant nothing. The contents were chaos at first glance: parish registers, militia rosters, letters between administrators, all Thornfell, all spanning roughly sixty years around the annexation. I worked through it the proper way, chronologically, cross-referencing, building the little scaffolds of fact I was trained to build. Foundling rolls for the year of our birth — there, the matron's hand, cramped and honest: *Boy, age approx. 4, found at Salt Gate, no kin claiming. Girl, same year, same circumstance.* Two entries among dozens. Two little sparks.

And then, interleaved between the registers, in a different ink, a different century, a different hand entirely — documents that had no business being in a Thornfell box.

The first was an order of settlement. Standard imperial form, granting land rights in the Thornfell corridor to Aurelian colonists. I nearly skimmed it. I am glad I did not, because the land grants referenced parcels by survey numbers, and beside each survey number someone — long ago, in pencil, in haste or guilt — had written a second set of figures.

I spent another hour proving to myself that the second figures matched the holdings of Cindrali freeholders recorded in the pre-annexation parish books. Every parcel granted to a colonist corresponded to a working farm already occupied by a Cindrali family. Not empty land. Never empty land. The Empire had not settled a wilderness. It had evicted a nation and then, on paper, renamed the eviction *settlement*.

That was the first crack. I want to be precise about what I felt, because precision is the discipline I have instead of faith, and I am losing that too. I did not feel horror. Horror is for people who expected better. I felt the specific vertigo of a clerk who finds a sum that doesn't balance — the professional nausea of arithmetic betrayed. The Empire, I had always believed, was brutal the way weather is brutal: impersonal, enormous, occasionally cruel, but ultimately lawful. Here was a ledger that lied. Weather doesn't lie. Weather has no need.

I told myself it was local corruption. Frontier administrators overstepping; headquarters unaware. There is comfort in that story. I drank it like brandy and kept reading.

* * *

The second document was thinner, and it broke me.

It was a memorandum, internal, restricted, dated nineteen years ago — the year of the last Cindrali rising, the one they don't teach children about because officially there was never anything to rise. The memorandum concerned the disposition of persons following the suppression of hostilities. Its language was exquisite. Bureaucracy writes murder in calligraphy. Persons of the former Cindrali governing class were to be *resettled*. Records of the former kingdom were to be *consolidated*. And item four, initialled by three separate officials whose names I have since memorized down to their middle initials:

*Regarding the surviving claimant of the former royal house: subject to be retained under supervision. Public record to reflect no surviving line. Subject's existence to remain a matter for the Office of the Viceroy alone.*

No surviving line.

Except there was a line. One living girl, nineteen years old now, retained under supervision, her existence known to the Office of the Viceroy — and erased from every public record, every schoolbook, every honest history, so that the Empire could go on telling its colonies that Cindralle died childless and deserved its death.

I sat in that cold room with one lamp and understood, with the clarity that comes only after the thing you feared is already true, that I was not holding evidence of frontier corruption. I was holding the machine itself. The erasure hadn't been a consequence of conquest. It had been the instrument. You do not defeat a country and then forget it. You defeat a country, and then you unmake it — its farms reassigned, its records consolidated, its heir locked in a drawer like a document too dangerous to file and too useful to burn.

And my superiors knew. Three sets of initials. Three men with salaries and gardens and portraits in the ministry corridors, men I had saluted, men who had told me that bringing in fugitives like Vesper Voss was *mercy* — mercy for them, rescue from a life outside the law, the warm arms of the Empire offered to the lost. All the while their own signatures sat in a basement confirming that the lost were made lost on purpose, catalogued, maintained, and hidden.

I thought of Vesper at the well. *Don't look down at what you're walking on.*

Too late.

* * *

Here is what I did next, and I ask whoever reads this — no one reads this, there is no one to read this — here is what I did, and let it stand as the record of exactly the kind of woman I am.

First, I copied the memorandum. Word for word, initial by initial, into my field notebook, in my own shorthand, which no one in this palace can read. My hands were steady. I remember noticing that they were steady and being ashamed of it, as though grief ought at least to tremble.

Second, I reboxed everything. C-7 went back on the cart in the same order, same orientation, same dust. If Ossory noticed anything, it would be only that the Inquisitor had spent two days with tithe ledgers and gone away bored. I thanked him. He looked at me a moment too long — that fourth kind of check, the one that finds things — and said, "History is heavy lifting, Inquisitor. Most prefer not to touch it." And I said, "Most prefer not to know what's under them," which was more truth than I intended, and he almost smiled, and I climbed the nine flights with my heart going like a smuggler's feet.

Third — and this is the part that matters, the part where loyalty finally cracked clean through like ice on the Thaw River — I wrote my report.

The report was flawless. I recommend flawless reports to anyone planning treachery; nothing covers it better. I documented the annexation-era records thoroughly, confirmed the fugitive's probable origins in the Thornfell corridor, noted that further archival work was unlikely to yield operational intelligence, and requested transfer of the investigation to the field. Pursuit, not paperwork. Let me chase Vesper through the mountains. Let me be far from this basement and these initials and this lamp.

I sealed it. I addressed it. I carried it to the dispatch room.

And then I stood there for a quarter of an hour, holding the fate of my entire life in one envelope, doing the arithmetic I had been avoiding since item four.

If I sent it, the machinery continued. The Viceroy's office would learn that an Inquisitor had been in the historical division asking about Thornfell foundlings, and eventually — not soon, but inevitably, because machines are patient — someone would connect my name to box C-7 and ask why Inquisitor Aldane had copied a restricted memorandum into shorthand. I had seen what the Empire did to loose ends. I had *been* what the Empire did to loose ends, twice, proudly.

And if I did not send it — if I delayed it, lost it, rewrote it — then I was no longer an instrument. I was a decision. I had never been a decision before. Instruments don't get tired. Decisions do.

There was a version of me, the version they built in that orphanage with commendations and head-pats, who sent the letter. She was a good soldier. She believed the Empire was weather. She is the woman I have been for thirty-one years, and I stood in that dispatch room and let her die quietly, without ceremony, because ceremonies attract attention.

I pocketed the report. I told the dispatcher my seal had smudged and I'd recopy it in the morning.

In the morning I requested leave to pursue the fugitive personally, citing prior acquaintance — a detail I had previously concealed, now offered up deliberately, because the best place to hide a fire is inside a larger one. They approved it within the hour. The Viceroy's office wants Vesper Voss very badly, and I intend to learn exactly why before anyone else learns what I know.

* * *

One more thing, and then I ride north.

Last night I took out the notebook and read my own shorthand until the words stopped looking like words. Then I did something I have not done since I was a child: I tried to pray, or whatever passes for prayer in a woman who has just discovered her whole life was built on a forged foundation. Nothing came. What came instead was memory — real memory, unfiléd, the kind they can't consolidate — of a boy giving me his boots in the snow and lying beautifully about winning them at cards, and me knowing he was lying, and taking them anyway, because I was cold and he was Vesper and some transactions don't need honesty to be true.

He is out there in the Thornfell passes with a stolen map and a stolen past, running from me. The Empire calls him a fugitive. The archives say the Empire made fugitives of everyone, starting with a kingdom and ending, if I'm careless, with me.

I am riding north to catch him. That part is still true, and must remain true in every report, every conversation, every carefully observed expression. But somewhere on that road, between here and the mountains, I am going to have to decide what I am catching him *from* — and for the first time in my life, I am not certain the answer is justice.

The snows are coming early this year. Good. Snow hides tracks — his, mine, and the Empire's.

I leave at dawn.
