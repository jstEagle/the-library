## 8. The Toll Collector

The second waypoint was a bridge.

Of course it was. The map had a sense of humor, or its dead maker had, and both of them found it funny to send me back down out of the high passes — three days of climbing I'd have paid good silver to skip — only to deliver me at the edge of the Vellwater Gorge, where the empire had thrown a single stone span across the dark like a man testing ice with one cautious boot.

The bridge itself wasn't the problem. Bridges are honest. A bridge says: *here is a gap in the world; here is how we pretend otherwise.* The problem was what stood on the far side of it.

A tollhouse. Imperial gray, slate-roofed, with a chimney smoking fat and comfortable against the grey sky and a flag hanging limp as a wet rag. And beside the tollhouse, a sign, freshly painted, letters so crisp they practically squeaked:

**TOLL: ONE SILVER PER HEAD. TWO PER PACK ANIMAL. FIVE FOR UNDECLARED GOODS.**

Underneath, smaller, in a different hand:

**NO EXCEPTIONS.**

I'd been robbed by better liars than this sign, but never by one with such nice penmanship.

* * *

Here's what the map had shown me when I pressed my palm to the wax seal that morning, huddled under an overhang while sleet ticked off the stones: not a place this time, but a *man*. A face rendered in that strange borrowed light the map used — memory-light, the color of things as someone loved or hated them. Square jaw going soft at the jowls. Grey coming in at the temples in two tidy wings. Eyes the brown of river water after a flood, carrying everything and settling nothing.

And beneath the face, written into the parchment in ink that seemed to rise up from underneath rather than sit on top, a single line:

*The keeper of the crossing keeps more than the toll.*

Which was the map's way of saying: there's something in that tollhouse worth stealing. Something the waypoint needed. And because the map was a hungry thing wearing parchment as a disguise, it also meant: you'll pay for it yourself, Vesper Voss. You always do.

I've told you how this works before. Or I think I have. Some days the telling of it blurs — which is, I suppose, the point. I steal memories from other people. The map takes payment from me. An exchange of goods, no refunds, no credit. Thieves' arithmetic. Maren Quill would have appreciated the elegance of it, if Maren Quill had ever appreciated anything except the weight of your purse.

So I did what any sensible thief does when faced with a fortified position: I walked up to the front door and bought a cup of tea.

* * *

Governor Dain Ferro — not governor, as it turned out, but he'd had the title painted on the door anyway, and who was I to argue with a man's furniture — received me in a room that smelled of woodsmoke and lamp oil and, underneath it all, faintly, of money. Not coin. Money as an idea. The smell of paper that has changed hands too many times.

He was bigger than his portrait on the map. Broader. A soldier gone comfortably thick around the middle, the way men do when they've traded marches for desks. He wore a civilian coat cut military, buttons polished, and he looked at me with exactly the amount of attention a toll-keeper gives a traveler: enough to price him, not enough to remember him.

"One silver," he said. "Two if that bag's got trade goods in it."

"It's got socks," I said. "Grieving ones. Their whole family died."

Something flickered behind the flood-water eyes. Not amusement. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing a joke he used to make himself, twenty years ago, before he stopped making jokes.

"Tea's on the stove," he said, instead of charging me double. "Sit. Nobody crosses the gorge till the wind drops anyway. Bridge ices."

I sat. He poured the tea himself, which surprised me. Toll collectors with freshly painted signs don't usually pour tea for sock merchants. But then, I'd come to learn that Dain Ferro had a great many habits left over from a man he no longer was, the way a snake keeps the shape of its last meal.

"Road's quiet," he said, settling into the chair across from me. It groaned. So did he. "Used to be caravans every day through here. Thornfell wool, Cindrali glasswork—" He stopped. Corrected himself, smoothly, the way a man corrects a slip in his own ledger. "Mountain glasswork. From before."

"Before," I agreed, and drank my tea.

You want to know the trick of stealing a memory? It isn't force. Force is for amateurs and inquisitors. The trick is *proximity*. You get close enough that your mind and theirs are breathing the same air, and then you find the seam. Every memory has a seam — the place where it's stitched into everything else a person is. Bribery memories have the loosest seams of all, because nobody wants them stitched tight. Nobody wants to be able to find them easily.

Dain Ferro had kept his seams loose for thirty years.

I didn't even have to work for it. That's the part I keep circling back to, the part that made my hands unsteady around the teacup. All I had to do was sit in the warmth of his fire, match my breathing to his, and *reach* —

— and the memory took me like the gorge takes rainwater.

* * *

A young soldier stands in the rain at this same bridge, thirty years younger, and the bridge is new and so is his uniform, and his name is Dain Ferro and he is *proud*.

That's the thing you have to understand first. He was proud. He stood his first watch on this crossing with his chin up and his boots planted, because the empire had given him a post and a purpose and a wage to send home to a mother with bad lungs in some lowland town whose name even the memory was already forgetting. He checked the papers of every traveler. He charged the exact toll. When a glass-seller from the hidden kingdom — because everyone knew there was a kingdom in the mountains, whatever the maps said, the way everyone knows there's a draft in a room without being able to point to it — when a glass-seller couldn't pay, Dain Ferro waved him across anyway.

*First offense,* the ledger would later say, though there was no ledger yet, only the memory of doing it and the warm shame-warmth of having done right.

Then came the year the orders changed. Quietly. The way empire orders always change, arriving not as commands but as *understandings*. The tolls went up. The lists of taxable goods grew longer than a winter night. And a caravan master with a fat face and a fat purse leaned close to young Sergeant Ferro at the end of a long day and said, not offering, just *observing*: "Be a shame if your mother's medicine got expensive, the way prices do."

And Dain Ferro stood there in the rain where he'd once waved a glass-seller across for free, and he thought about his mother's cough, and he took the purse.

*Second offense.* Except this time there *was* a ledger, and his name went in it, and so did the caravan master's, and the two entries were bound together forever like prisoners sharing chains.

After that it was arithmetic. It was always arithmetic, with Dain Ferro. One bribe to cover the medicine. Another to cover the silence about the first. A third because the second had made him valuable, and value in the empire is a debt that collects interest. I watched it happen the way you watch a landslide — each stone reasonable, each stone alone, the whole mountain moving all at once. He married. He buried a wife. He raised the tolls because his superiors raised their expectations, and skimmed the difference because his superiors skimmed theirs, and somewhere in the middle decades the young soldier who waved a glass-seller across for free became a fat man in a borrowed title pouring tea for strangers, and the terrible thing — the thing I carried out of that memory like a splinter working toward my heart —

— the terrible thing was that he never once felt like a villain.

Every step had been survival. Every compromise had been *necessary*. He could trace the line from the free crossing to the fresh paint on the sign, and every point along it had a reason, and the reasons were all true, and the line still ended where it ended. In a tollhouse that bled travelers dry. In a valley where villages paid him twice — once in silver, once in silence — for the privilege of pretending the empire hadn't eaten their roads alive.

He remembered his mother. That was the last clean room in the house of himself. He'd spent thirty years of dirty silver keeping her medicine stocked, and she'd died anyway, years ago now, and he kept paying anyway, into accounts that led nowhere, because stopping would mean admitting what the payments had been for.

*Survival*, the memory whispered, in his voice, in the voice of every man who ever fed a monster one reasonable stone at a time. *It was all survival.*

I came back to myself with the teacup rattling against the saucer.

* * *

Dain Ferro was watching me with an expression I recognized, because I'd worn it myself in mirrors: the look of a man who suspects he's just been seen, and can't decide whether to reach for a weapon or another cup of tea.

"You went somewhere," he said.

"The tea's strong," I said.

"My wife used to say that." His mouth did something that had once been a smile. "She's dead. Everything's dead. The road, the trade, the glasswork." He said *glasswork* deliberately this time. Testing me. Watching my face the way I'd watched his. "There was a kingdom in these mountains, once. Whatever they tell you in the schools."

"Was there?"

"There was." He leaned forward. The chair groaned again, and this time it sounded less like furniture and more like a confession. "I've held this crossing for thirty years, friend, and I'll tell you what I know that isn't in any report. Twice — twice — I've seen people walk out of the western valley who weren't on any road. Came over the ridge like they'd stepped out of the air. And the imperial surveyors chart that gorge every five years, and every five years their maps show nothing in that valley but trees, and I have *stood on this bridge* and seen walls. Towers. A whole city sitting in the mist like it was waiting for permission to exist."

My heart was doing something unsporting against my ribs.

"And you reported it?" I asked.

"I wrote it up. First time." He laughed, short and joyless. "You know what came back? A form. Asking me to verify my observations against standard survey data. Second time, I didn't write anything. Third time, I started taking a cut from smugglers to lose their cargo manifests, and one of those manifests listed a client buying *old Cindrali stonework*, and I burned the manifest, because burning it was cheaper than asking questions." He spread his hands. Thick fingers, ink-stained. A soldier's hands gone clerk. "See how it works? Thirty years, and every choice small, and here I am. Keeper of a crossing to a country that doesn't exist, paid to forget it does."

The map, folded against my chest, felt suddenly warm. Hungry. It knew what it wanted. It had wanted it since I sat down.

Because here's the ugly arithmetic I hadn't let myself do until that moment: the waypoint needed what Ferro knew. The map needed the *memory* of it — not the knowledge, which he'd give me free over tea, but the living weight of it, the thirty years of complicity and grief and rot, the whole seamed-together mass of a decent man's long surrender. That was the toll. The map didn't pay tolls. The map collected them.

And I was the collector.

"So," Ferro said quietly, watching my face. "What are you, really? Smuggler? Thief?"

"Worse," I said. "A businessman."

He almost laughed. And in that almost-laugh, in the loosening of him, in the old soldier's habit of trusting a man who makes him laugh — I reached.

* * *

I won't describe what I took. Not because I'm squeamish. Because I've described thefts before and they all sound the same in the telling: a warmth leaving, a hollow opening, a man blinking at the sudden absence of something he can't name. What I will tell you is what it cost *me*, because the map collects from both ends of the transaction, always, and I felt it go the moment the stolen weight settled into the parchment against my chest.

It took my memory of the smell of bread baking.

Not bread in general. One specific bread — flat, seeded, baked on a hearthstone in a kitchen I can no longer picture, by hands I can no longer see, on a morning I can no longer locate in any year of my life. I know the smell existed. I know it was *home*. I know that whoever I was before Thornfell's orphan rows and the smuggler roads, that person woke some mornings to that smell and thought, *this is mine, this is safe, this is where I belong.*

I stood in Dain Ferro's tollhouse holding a stranger's thirty years of corruption, and I could not remember why bread should matter, and something in my chest tore along a seam I hadn't known was stitched.

Ferro blinked slowly, twice, like a man surfacing from deep water. His eyes drifted around his own room with mild confusion, as if checking that everything was where he'd left it. Then he frowned at the window, at the gorge, at the mist sitting heavy over the western valley.

"Strange," he said. "I was just thinking — there's something I ought to be remembering. Something important." He rubbed his jaw. "Old man's absent-mindedness. Tea?"

"I've had my cup," I said, and my voice came out steady, which felt like the day's one small mercy.

"Crossing's open, then. Wind's dropped." He fumbled a key off a hook and slid it across the table to me, frowning slightly, as though the gesture had been decided somewhere below thought. "Bridge ices after sundown. Go on across. No charge." A pause, and then, quieter, almost puzzled at himself: "No charge. Imagine that. Thirty years, and I've never once let anyone cross free."

He shook his head at his own generosity, bewildered by it, and I picked up the key before he could reconsider, and I walked out into the cold with a stolen life pressing against my ribs and one fewer reason in the world to know what bread was for.

Behind me, through the tollhouse window, I saw him settle back into his chair and stare at the fire, searching his own face the way you search a room for something misplaced — knowing it was there a moment ago, knowing it mattered, unable to name it.

I crossed the bridge. The stones were slick and the gorge roared below, and halfway across I stopped, because the map against my chest had grown warm again — warmer than payment-warm, warmer than hunger. It was *showing* me something.

I unfolded it there, in the wind, over the drop.

The blank parchment was filling. Ink rising from beneath the surface like drowned things coming up for air, drawing lines I recognized — the gorge, the bridge, the tollhouse — and then lines beyond, westward, into the valley Ferro had seen and been paid to forget. Roads. Walls. A citadel crowned with a spire like a needle. And at the city's heart, a mark I hadn't seen before: not a waypoint this time, but a *door*, drawn standing open, with light pouring out of it in strokes of gold so fine they looked like they were still falling.

Beneath it, in that rising ink, four words:

*One toll remains. Home.*

I stood on the bridge between the empire and the erased kingdom, holding a map that had just told me the price of the next crossing, and understood at last that it had never been a map at all.

It was a ledger.

And I was running out of currency.
