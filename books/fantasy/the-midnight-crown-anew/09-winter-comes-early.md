## 9. Winter Comes Early

The snow didn't fall so much as arrive — one moment the pass was open and gray, the next the sky had decided to empty itself onto the Thornfells like a creditor settling accounts. I'd been walking for three days since the governor's toll-house, carrying a stolen memory of bribery that sat in my chest like a swallowed coal, and when the whiteout came down I stopped pretending I was choosing my direction. The mountain chose. The mountain always chooses.

I smelled woodsmoke before I saw anything else. Then a dark shape in all that white — low, turf-roofed, hunkered against the slope like something that had learned humility from the weather. A shepherd's hut, the kind that dotted these high valleys in better years, back before the Empire's roads strangled the trade routes and everyone who could leave had left.

Knocking on the door of strangers while carrying an imperial warrant's worth of trouble in your pack is not, generally, a survival strategy. But the alternative was lying down in the snow and letting it do what snow does, and I've never liked other people deciding things for me, even weather.

I knocked.

* * *

The door opened on a face that did something complicated.

An old man stood there, wool-wrapped to the eyes, holding a fire-iron like he meant to introduce it to my skull. Behind him: a small round room, a peat fire, a woman stirring a pot, two children staring at me with the frank rudeness of the young. Warmth poured out of the doorway and hit me like a physical thing.

And then the old man's face went through its complication and arrived somewhere I didn't expect. His grip on the fire-iron loosened. His mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

"*Vessan?*" he said. "Vessan of the Ashline? Gods below — boy, you're *alive*."

I want to be clear about what happened next, because I'd like to claim I corrected him immediately. That I said, sorry, old timer, you have me confused with someone else, my name is Vesper Voss and I am nobody's kinsman, least of all yours. That would have been the honest move. That would have cost me nothing but the warmth of the hut, which is to say it would have cost me everything, because outside the temperature was dropping toward the place where breath freezes mid-fall and men become interesting shapes for spring to find.

"Vessan," I said instead. "Yes."

Liar. Thief. Both true, both insufficient. Let me explain how I got there, because even now I can't decide whether it was weakness or the most honest thing I've ever done.

* * *

Here's the mechanism, as best I understand it. The map drinks memories — mine, mostly, when I pay; other people's, when I steal. But apparently it doesn't just take. It *gives*. Everything it has fed on sits in me like sediment in a riverbed, and some of it leaks. Somewhere between Cindralle's ghost-capital and the toll-house, I'd absorbed enough borrowed Cindrali that I carried it in my body now: the way I said certain vowels, the little touch-two-fingers-to-brow greeting I did without thinking, a lullaby I hummed when I wasn't paying attention that I had never once heard in my life.

To this old man — whose name, I learned within the hour, was Davor Ashline — those leaks added up to a nephew. His sister's son, gone south before the Empire came through burning, presumed dead these six years. And here he stood in his doorway, tall and hollow-cheeked and speaking Cindrali-inflected trade-talk, doing the brow-touch without knowing why.

What were the odds? Don't ask. I've stopped believing in coincidence. I believe in maps now, and maps have intentions.

"You don't remember us," Davor's wife — Sefa, broad-faced, sharp-eyed — said quietly, later, watching me over the stew pot. Not accusing. Worse than accusing: understanding. "You were at the capital when it fell. We heard what they did there." She touched her own temple. "The forgetting. Some folk came back with holes where their lives used to be."

"That's it," I said, and the ease of the lie frightened me. "Holes."

It was even true, in the way that matters least. I have holes where my life used to be. I just know exactly how much each one sold for.

* * *

I should describe the family, because I'm going to betray them shortly and you should know what they look like.

Davor, sixty-some winters, hands like tree roots, a man who had spent his whole life moving sheep between high pasture and valley and now couldn't do either because the pastures burned and the valley belonged to Aurelian resettlement officers. He laughed easily, which is a thing I associate with people who haven't yet understood what's been taken from them, or maybe with people who've understood it perfectly and decided laughter is the only retaliation available.

Sefa, who missed nothing. Who watched me the way I watch marks. I kept waiting for her to catch the fraud, and part of me — a large part, if I'm honest — wanted her to.

The children: Pell, ten, all elbows and questions; and Issa, seven, who had decided within the first hour that I hung the moon and several of its more decorative stars. Issa climbed into my lap by the fire that first night as though she'd been issued a standing reservation, and asked me to tell her about the capital, about the glass bridges and the singing towers, and I told her — God help me, I *told her* — because the memories were right there, someone else's childhood rendered vivid as yesterday, and it was easier to recite them than to admit I was a stranger reading from a stolen book.

"The bridge sings when the wind comes off the water," I said, "like a hundred cups being filled at once."

"Mama, did you hear? *Cups.*" Issa looked up at me with an expression of pure belief, and I felt something crack in me along a line I hadn't known was there.

Because here's the arithmetic of it, and I am very good at arithmetic: every minute of warmth in that hut was purchased with goods I'd lifted from dead men. Their homeland lived in me like coin in a purse, and I was spending it, coin by coin, on stew and firewood and a child's adoration. The real Vessan Ashline — wherever his bones lay, under whatever collapsed tower — had nothing left to give these people, and I was spending his inheritance in front of them, and they called me *kinsman*, and the fire was warm, and the stew had actual meat in it, and I hated myself with a thoroughness that surprised me.

You'd think hatred would spoil the warmth. It doesn't. They coexist fine. That's the worst discovery of the week.

* * *

Three days the storm held us. On the second night Davor brought out a bottle of plum brandy he'd been saving for a wedding or a funeral, whichever came first, and poured four fingers into a cup and put it in my hands.

"For the ones who didn't come back," he said.

We drank. He talked. He talked about his sister — my supposed mother, a woman named Lira who sang badly and planted too many beans every single year out of optimism that six decades of failed bean crops had somehow not cured. He talked about the fall of the capital, which he'd seen from the hills: the towers going dark one by one, not burning so much as *dimming*, like lamps carried away down a long corridor. He talked about the year after, when the Empire's clerks came through with ledgers and ink and unmade the country — struck names from rolls, reassigned farms, declared that no such kingdom had ever existed, thank you for your cooperation, here is your new tax district.

"They took our words," Davor said, "and gave us receipts." He laughed his easy laugh, and it had a broken sound in it that night. "But you'd know all that. You were there."

"I was there," I said.

And the terrible thing — the thing I keep turning over like a stone looking for the clean side — is that I *was*. Not as Vesper. As the merchant's grief I once stole, as the governor's decades, as whoever's lullaby I hum. The map has made me a country of immigrants; everyone who ever died near me lives here now, undocumented. When Davor mourned, I mourned with material that wasn't mine and grief that was. Where does the theft end? Where does anything?

Sefa found me outside at dawn, third day, checking the sky. Snow had stopped. Light came off the drifts hard enough to cut.

"My husband thinks you're a miracle," she said. No warmth in it. "Six years praying, and his nephew walks out of a blizzard to his door. He'll tell that story until he dies."

"And you?"

She looked at me a long time. Wind moved her hair around a face that could have been carved for judging. "I think miracles come with holes in them," she said. "I think whatever walked out of that blizzard, it isn't entirely the boy who went south. And I think a woman who lost her son to the Empire's machines doesn't get to choose what the gods send back through her door. She only gets to choose whether to feed him."

My throat did something embarrassing. "And if what came through your door is worse than nothing? If he's a thief wearing her son's—"

"If you finish that sentence," Sefa said, "I will feed you anyway, and you will carry the shame of it out of my house on your own feet. That's how it works." She turned back inside. At the threshold she paused. "Issa slept well last night. First time since the resettlement. Whatever you are, boy, the stories you tell are hers now. Try to be worth the borrowing."

* * *

The storm broke on the fourth morning. Blue sky, brutal light, the pass still choked but walkable if a man was stupid in the correct direction.

I packed in the grey dawn, quiet as I could, which wasn't quiet enough. Issa appeared at my elbow the way children do, fully dressed, wearing a knitted cap with ear-flaps and an expression of absolute intention.

"I'm coming with you."

"No."

"Papa says you're going to find the others. The hidden ones. The people who remember." Her voice dropped to the register children use for secrets. "Take me. I remember good. I remember the bridge song *all of it.*"

For a moment — a bad, bright moment — I considered it. Considered having her. A witness, a guide, a living thread to a country I'd only ever known secondhand. The map would probably love her. The map loves everything it can drink.

"No," I said again, and crouched to her height. "Listen. What I'm carrying — it eats remembering. It's the thing that made the holes in me. You stay here, where your remembering is safe. Promise me you'll keep it safe. All of it. Even the sad parts. *Especially* the sad parts."

She frowned at me with her grandmother's judgment settling early onto young features. "You're going to give it back," she said. Not a question. Children always think the world is fixable if the grown-ups would simply do the obvious thing. "The country. You're going to give it back."

I looked at her — at the whole impossible faith of her — and I could not say yes, and I could not say no, so I did the brow-touch, the two fingers, the old greeting I never learned, and said, "Watch the sheep, Issa Ashline."

Which was, I realize now, the first promise I've made in years that I intend to keep.

* * *

Davor walked me as far as the drifts allowed, which was far enough to see the pass and the dark line of the road beyond it, heading east toward the capital, toward the puppet court, toward the girl the map was dragging me to whether I willed it or not.

He gripped my arm with root-hard hands. "When it's done," he said, "come back. There'll be beans to plant. Badly. It's tradition."

"When it's done," I said, and we both let the lie stand, because some lies are load-bearing.

Then I turned east, and the snow crusted over my boot-prints behind me, and by the time I reached the ridgeline there was no evidence I'd ever been warm.

Except — I checked, the way you check a wound — except the memory of it. Issa's weight in my lap. Sefa's terrible mercy. Davor's toast to the dead. Those were mine now, weren't they? Earned, not stolen. Paid for with a lie, but paid for.

The map shifted against my ribs, patient as winter, and I had the sudden ugly certainty that it disagreed — that it had already filed those four days under *assets acquired* — and that somewhere ahead, in the capital, in the cold marble cage where a drugged girl wore a crown she couldn't feel, the final entry was waiting to be written.

I pulled my collar up against the wind and started down.

Behind me, faint on the wind, I could almost hear a child humming a song about a bridge that sings like cups being filled — and I knew, with the last honesty left in me, that someday soon she'd sing it to her own children, and none of it would be mine, and all of it would be true.

That, at least, the Empire never figured out how to tax.

Yet.
