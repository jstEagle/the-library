## 10. The Second Face of Sera Aldane

I had been waiting at the Graywater crossing since before dawn, and by the time the sun hauled itself over the Thornfell ridgeline I had learned three things about ambush work that no one tells you in the inquisitor's academy.

First: it is mostly sitting. Sitting on cold stones, sitting in wet brush, sitting with your hand pressed flat against a horse's neck to keep her quiet while she decides whether the morning smells like danger or just like morning. My mare, Verity — named by a younger and more sentimental version of myself — had opinions about both.

Second: you think about all the wrong things. I meant to rehearse what I'd say to Vesper when he came down to the water. I had a speech. A good one, drafted over four nights in waystation taverns, revised more times than I'd care to confess even under truth-oath. *You remember me. You knew me before either of us had titles or warrants.* Something like that. Instead I spent the hours cataloguing every way the Empire could have already gotten to him ahead of me, and then cataloguing why that thought made my chest hurt, and then being annoyed at my chest for having opinions about a smuggler's survival.

Third: an ambush is only as good as your certainty that the man you're ambushing is still the man you're ambushing.

I was very certain about Vesper Voss. I was increasingly uncertain about everything else.

* * *

Let me be honest, since honesty is apparently the theme of this particular disaster: I did not come up the mountain for the map alone.

The warrant said otherwise. Warrants always say otherwise; they are documents written by people who need other people to be simple. The warrant bore the double-crown seal of the Aurelian Inquisition, my name, my rank, and a description of Vesper Voss so thorough it read like a love letter written by someone terrified of their own handwriting. *Subject is of medium build. Subject moves quietly. Subject is known to trade in memories and may attempt to steal yours if approached within arm's reach.*

They were right about the last part. When we were nine years old, Vess stole the memory of my mother singing — not from me, from a neighbor woman who'd known her — and gave it back to me wrapped in a scrap of cloth like it was something precious instead of something stolen. He said, "You shouldn't have to not-have this." He was a thief even then. He was also the only person in the orphanage at Coldharrows who understood the difference between taking and giving.

That distinction has been the whole problem of my life, if I'm being truthful, which I keep promising myself I will be.

We grew up in the same drafty dormitory above the imperial wayhouse, children of the Empire's leftovers — mine dead in a fever season, his simply gone, direction unknown, cause unrecorded. The matrons taught us the catechism: the Empire feeds you, the Empire shelters you, the Empire is the shape of order itself. I swallowed it whole. Vess chewed and picked out the gristle. I used to think that was cynicism. It took me twenty years and one sealed archive room to understand it might have been the opposite.

He left at fifteen. Walked out into the Thornfell winter with nothing but a coat two sizes too big and a grin he wore like armor. I joined the Inquisition at seventeen because I wanted to make the world the kind of place where nobody had to walk out into winter. These are both answers to the same question. Only one of us, I was beginning to suspect, had answered correctly.

The dispatches had been strange for a month. My superiors wanted Vesper found, yes — but the language kept shifting. At first it was *apprehend*. Then it was *intercept and secure the artifact*. Then a courier came up from the capital with new orders in a cipher I wasn't cleared for, and the garrison captain at Salt Gate — a decent man named Oren Hale who'd once shared his fire with me during a blizzard — stopped meeting my eyes entirely.

Then Captain Hale turned up dead in a ravine, officially a fall, unofficially wearing bruises in shapes that falls don't make.

So when I took this post at the Graywater crossing, I took it knowing two things: that I would find Vesper Voss there or near there, and that I might be the only person hunting him who wanted him breathing at the end of it. That is a strange kind of loneliness. You stand in the cold holding a weapon, and you realize the weapon is pointed at everyone except the person you're actually afraid for.

* * *

He came down to the river a little after midmorning, and I nearly laughed at how well I knew him still.

He moved the way he'd moved through the orphanage kitchens at midnight — weight on the balls of his feet, head turning in small increments, reading the landscape like a page he expected to lie to him. Thinner than the boy I remembered. There was gray coming in at his temples that seemed unfair; we weren't old enough for gray. He crouched at the crossing, tested the water with two fingers, winced at the cold, and I felt a ridiculous urge to call out that he should dry his hands before the wind got them, the way I used to nag him about wet sleeves when we were small.

Instead I waited until he was halfway across the ford, committed, boots in the current, and stepped out of the brush with my hand raised.

"Sera Aldane," I called. "Inquisition. Don't run, Vess. Please."

I want it recorded that I said *please*. Nobody else would believe it of me.

He went very still in the water. And here is the thing that I will carry to whatever grave is allotted to me: he looked at me the way a stranger looks at a stranger. Not fear exactly — calculation. His eyes went from my face to my badge to my horse to the treeline, doing arithmetic, and nowhere in that arithmetic was there any flicker of recognition. None. I had prepared for anger, for bargaining, for that old crooked grin deployed as a shield. I had not prepared to be nobody.

"You know me?" he said. Careful. Flat.

"Since we were seven." The words came out smaller than I intended. "Coldharrows. You stole my mother's voice off a neighbor woman because you couldn't stand watching me try to remember it."

Something moved behind his eyes — not memory, just the shape where memory should go, like pressing on a bruise that isn't there anymore. "That's a story," he said. "People tell stories."

"I do," I said. "It's practically the family trade now. Yours and mine."

And that was when the kill-squad came out of the trees.

* * *

There were six of them. Later, going over it the way I go over everything, I counted six and thanked whatever stubborn god watches over liars that it wasn't eight. They came down the north slope in loose file, grey-cloaked, no colors, which told me everything before they fired the first bolt: imperial wetwork doesn't wear badges. Badges can be traced.

The crossbow bolt meant for Vesper's back hit the water a foot behind him because I put my knife through the shooter's wrist instead. I don't fully remember deciding to do it. I remember the sound — that flat wet crack of steel finding the narrow gap between glove and vambrace — and I remember thinking, with absurd clarity, *well. That's that, then.* There is no walking back from cutting a brother of the silent retinue. The retinue does not forgive, and more to the point, the retinue does not forget faces.

"Down!" I shouted, and Vesper — to his credit, and I mean that genuinely, because trust from a hunted man is expensive — dropped into the river without asking why, which put the second volley's bolts hissing over both our heads.

Verity, bless her contrary heart, chose that moment to decide the morning did indeed smell like danger, and screamed, and kicked, and caught one of the squad across the jaw hard enough to redecorate it. That bought seconds. Seconds are the whole currency of surviving an ambush, and I had spent years learning to count them.

"In the water, stay low, move!" Vesper was already moving, low and fast, using the current's noise to cover the sound of his own progress, angling downstream toward the willow tangle on the far bank. Good instincts. Terrible plan — the willows funneled right back up toward the trail, where two more of them were repositioning.

"The bank, not the willows," I shouted, wading after him with my sword out, and he actually adjusted course, which stung a little. Twenty years of estrangement and he trusted my tactical judgment instantly while looking at me like I was a stranger. Men.

The fight itself I'll give you briefly, because fights are brief when they're real. The squad leader was good — better than good, capital-trained, the kind of soldier whose movements have been sanded down until nothing wasted remains. He came at me with a short blade and no speech, which is the retinue's whole philosophy in miniature. I fought him in the shallows where footing favored neither of us and my longer reach favored me. I took a cut along my forearm for the privilege. He took my pommel across his temple, and then the river, and rivers in the Thornfell in late autumn are not survivable places to be unconscious.

Vesper handled the two on the far bank in a manner I found deeply unsettling and would spend the evening trying not to admire: he didn't fight them so much as *unmake* them. A touch to the wrist of one, and the man simply forgot which end of his blade was which, stood blinking at his own weapon like a child handed a strange tool. The second he sent sprawling with a thrown stone to the knee and then — I saw this clearly, I wish I hadn't — leaned close and whispered something, and the man's face went slack with terror at whatever memory Vesser had just handed him, some borrowed horror worn for a moment like a mask.

The remaining two broke and ran when their leader went into the water. Retinue discipline holds until it doesn't, and then it runs downhill very efficiently.

Silence, after. River noise. Verity blowing hard on the bank. My forearm bleeding in a way that would want stitching. And Vesper Voss standing in the shallows ten feet from me, soaked to the ribs, staring at me with those careful strangers' eyes.

"So," he said. "Inquisitor. Are you going to arrest me, or was the rescue a long con?"

* * *

We made camp that night in a shepherd's bothy upstream, because he wouldn't let me pick the location — fair, since I'd technically lured him into a kill zone — and because neither of us could walk farther. My arm was stitched with thread from his kit, clumsily, by him, because he refused to let me use my sword arm and refused to let me do it one-handed and refused, with escalating sarcasm, to explain how he'd learned field surgery on the smuggler roads. Some things apparently survive memory-theft. Hands remember.

He sat across the small fire with the wax-sealed map case held against his chest like a child holds a thing they've been told not to hold, and finally asked the question I'd been dreading.

"Why did you save me?"

Because the true answer was long, I started with the true answer anyway. "Because the people who sent me to find you don't want you found. They want you erased. There's a difference, and lately I've become pedantic about differences."

"They sent you to find me and someone else to erase me."

"Yes."

"And you figured that out how?"

"Captain Hale at Salt Gate. He tried to warn me — or warn someone — and fell down a ravine that had never once fallen on anyone in forty years of service." I fed a branch to the fire. "Vess— Voss. Before the archive at Coldharrows burned, I pulled records. Cindralle. The kingdom that supposedly never existed."

His head came up sharply. "You know that name?"

"It's in sealed files I was never cleared to see, describing an annexation that officially never happened, signed by chancellors who officially never served. The Empire didn't conquer Cindralle, Voss. It *unwrote* it. Whole generations of colonists settled lands that were emptied first — and I don't think most of them know it. I didn't know it, and I was raised on the border of those lands." The fire popped. "My entire career is built on enforcing a lie so large it looks like the ground."

"That's a heavy accusation for someone who works for them."

"I've been reconsidering my employment." I looked at him across the fire and made myself say the rest plainly, because he deserved plain, whatever else he deserved. "I came up this mountain to bring you in. I won't pretend otherwise — you'd smell the pretense anyway, you always could. But somewhere between Hale's funeral and that riverbank, the mission changed. I'm not taking you to the capital. I'm getting you away from everyone who wants what you're carrying, including — especially — the people who gave me my orders. Which makes me a traitor with a badge, and you a thief with a treasure, and I suppose that means we're colleagues."

He was quiet a long time. The firelight moved on his face and I searched it, helplessly, for the boy, and found only the man, and hated the Empire a little more for the difference.

"There's a word you keep almost saying," he said finally.

"I know."

"You're not going to say it."

"Not yet. You look at me like I'm a stranger with a knife. If I say it now, it'll just be one more thing you can't feel." I pulled my cloak tighter around the stitched arm. "When you're ready to hear it, I'll say it. Until then, call me Aldane."

"Aldane," he repeated, testing it, and something in his expression flickered — not recognition, but the ghost of a door rattling in its frame.

"One more thing," I said. "The squad today. They weren't sent because of the map alone. I checked the cipher traffic before I left the garrison. There's a standing order attached to your name — older than the warrant. *Loose end.* Someone in the capital has known about you for a very long time, Voss. Since before the stranger died at the salt gate. Maybe since—" I stopped myself, because the next words were *since Coldharrows*, and I wasn't ready to open that door with him standing on the far side of a fire looking at me like weather.

"Since when?" he said.

"Since childhood," I said. "Someone has wanted you dead since we were children. And I intend to find out who, and I suggest you decide quickly whether the map is worth more to you than the question of why."

He looked down at the map case, and the firelight caught the wax seal, and for just a moment — I would swear this later, under no oath, to no one — the seal seemed to darken, the way a pupil dilates.

Behind us, far down the valley, a horn sounded. Once. Long. The retinue's signal for *regroup*, or its signal for *reinforcements*, and from the distance, I honestly could not tell which.

"We move at first light," I said. "North. There's a valley past the shepherd passes that doesn't appear on any imperial chart I've ever seen, and after today I have a suspicion about why."

Vesper looked at me then with something that was almost — almost — a smile. "Lead on, Inquisitor. Apparently you're the only person in the Empire I can trust."

"Don't say it like a joke," I said. "It's the saddest sentence I've heard in twenty years."
