## 11. Two Thieves, One Road

**Vesper**

There is an art to traveling with someone who has spent the last three months trying to arrest you.

The art, mostly, consists of not turning your back on them. I'd broken that rule twice before noon and survived both times, which told me either Sera Aldane had gone soft or she genuinely meant this uneasy truce of ours. Given that she'd put a knife through the throat of an imperial kill-squad officer to save my life two days ago, I was leaning toward soft. Or something stranger than soft. Something I didn't have a name for yet.

We walked the high shepherd road above the Vale of Sorrows — the Empire's name for it, naturally; everything they named, they named like landlords. The snow had stopped but the clouds hadn't decided anything, hanging low and gray as unwashed wool. Sera walked a pace behind me and to the left, which was inquisitor formation, the position you take when you want your sword arm free and your subject's hands in view. She probably didn't even know she was doing it anymore. Twelve years of service wears grooves into a person, and people roll along them like water finding channels.

"You're limping," she said.

"I'm favoring my left leg."

"That's what limping is."

"It sounds better when I say it."

She made a sound that might have been a laugh if it had been strangled at birth. We kept walking. The map rode against my chest inside my coat, wrapped in oilcloth, quiet as a held breath. It had been quiet since the river crossing. Quiet maps worry me more than loud ones. Loud ones are honest about their appetites.

"Camp at the next bend," Sera said. "The wind's going to drop after sundown, and we'll want fire before it does."

"You're giving orders now?"

"I've been giving orders since I was nine years old. You were there."

And there it was. The thing she kept saying, casually, the way you'd mention weather — *you were there* — as though she were handing me something small and obvious across a table. *You were there when the orphanage burned.* *You were there the winter we ate pine bark.* *You were there when they took the boys to Thornfell and left the girls to the sisters.*

I was never anywhere. That was the trouble.

"The bend," I said, "yes. Good idea. Very tactical."

She looked at me too long. Inquisitors are trained to look at people too long; it's how they find the seams. But this wasn't interrogation looking. This was the other kind, the kind I'd been dodging for days now, the kind that wanted something from me I couldn't sell because I didn't own it.

---

We made camp under an overhang of granite where the firelight wouldn't show. Sera built the fire small and neat, soldier-neat, while I sawed at hard sausage and pretended my leg wasn't screaming. The map stayed silent against my ribs. I'd started thinking of it as a sleeping animal, which was stupid, because animals don't plan.

"Eat," she said.

"I'm eating."

"You're performing eating. There's a difference." She tore her own ration in half and passed half to me without ceremony, which annoyed me more than generosity would have. Generosity I could refuse. Practicality just sat there being reasonable until you hated yourself.

"Sera." I said it before I could stop myself. Her name felt strange in my mouth, like a coin from a country that no longer existed. "The things you remember. About us. About the orphanage. About—" I gestured vaguely at the fire, the mountains, the whole shape of the past. "You keep talking about them like they're mine too."

"They were yours. That's rather the point of shared childhoods."

"Well." I turned the sausage over. "They're not. Not anymore."

The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, a rock cracked in the cold with a sound like a far-off shot, and we both went still out of old habit, then both pretended we hadn't.

"What do you mean, not anymore?" she said quietly.

I could have lied. Lying is my trade; I'm very good at it. But there's a particular exhaustion that comes from lying to someone who saved your life, and I was carrying enough weight already. So I did the thing I almost never do.

"I sold them," I said. "Years ago. My memories. All of them — Thornfell, the orphanage, my mother's face, whatever came before. Sold them piecemeal to fences and buyers who pay better for a poor boy's grief than the boy can earn any other way." I shrugged, which cost me something, though I tried to make it look cheap. "You're the only one left who remembers us. Congratulations. Terrible inheritance."

Sera stared at me. And here's the thing about inquisitors — I'd expected fury, or pity, or that awful professional compassion they train into them like a second spine. What I got instead was worse. She looked *grieved*. Not for herself. For me.

"How much?" she said.

"Does it matter?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I want to know what you thought you were worth." Her voice stayed level. Her eyes didn't. "What price a man puts on his mother's face. I want to know if it was insultingly low, so I can be angry about it, or insultingly high, so I can be angry about it differently."

That got a real laugh out of me, short and surprised. "Low," I said. "It's always low. First memories go for coppers. Nobody wants a child's happiness; it's thin wine. They want the grief later, when it's aged. Grief sells by the pound." I fed a stick to the fire. "My mother's face went for eight marks. Enough to eat for a month and buy boots. Good boots, actually. I still have one of them."

"One boot."

"The other died heroically in a flood."

She shook her head slowly, and for a long moment neither of us spoke, and the silence between us was the strangest thing I'd ever occupied. It wasn't hostile. It wasn't comfortable either. It was the silence of two people standing on opposite sides of a hole in the world, looking down at the same absence.

"All those times I found traces of you," she said finally. "Over the years. Reports, rumors, witnesses. I used to think you were hiding from me specifically. That you'd changed your name, your face, your trade, to stay ahead of *Sera Aldane*." A bitter breath escaped her. "You weren't hiding from me at all. You couldn't have hidden from yourself harder if you'd tried. You sold the self that knew me. Of course I never caught up with him. He doesn't exist."

"There's a version of that that's comforting," I offered.

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't do the thing where you make it funny so nobody has to feel it. I watched you do it with the smugglers, with the governor's men, with everyone. You make it funny and then you walk away and the joke stays behind like a tip." She leaned forward, firelight moving on her face. "Vesper. Do you know what I remember? Tell me if any of it lands. Anything. Even an echo."

So she told me. She talked into the dark like a woman paying out rope, and I listened the way you listen to a story about strangers who happen to wear your name.

She remembered the winter the orphanage roof collapsed and we slept in the chapel for a month, all the children in one heap for warmth, and how I — some earlier version of me, some boy whose face I'd never see — used to invent stories about the saints in the windows, giving them scandalous private lives to make the little ones laugh. She remembered the matron's bell. She remembered the day the Aurelian recruiters came, and how I stood in front of the younger kids so they'd be taken last, and how I cried afterward where I thought no one could see, and she saw anyway, and never told anyone until tonight.

None of it landed. That was the terrible part. Every word arrived clean and foreign, a traveler's tales about a boy called Vesper Voss. I felt nothing where my childhood should have been. Nothing at all — except, curiously, grief about the nothing. A bruise around an empty socket.

"No," I said at last. "Nothing. I'm sorry. You gave me all that and there's nowhere in me for it to go."

"Then I'll give it again," she said. "As many times as it takes. Memory isn't property, Vesper. It doesn't get spent when it's shared."

"Careful," I said. "Talk like that gets you reassigned."

"Talk like that got me exiled from everything I was two days ago. I'm committed."

---

**Sera**

He slept badly. I know because I slept worse, taking first watch, watching the man I had chased across three provinces sit hunched by a dying fire like a beggar outside his own house.

I had imagined this conversation a hundred times during the pursuit. In most versions, he confessed to crimes. In several, he begged. In none of them — not one — did he tell me, in a voice stripped of all its cleverness, that the boy I remembered was a sum of money long since scattered among strangers' pockets.

Eight marks for his mother's face. I lay awake doing the arithmetic I couldn't stop doing: every memory he'd ever sold, itemized. The Empire pays its inquisitors well. I could have bought him back, piece by piece, years ago, if I'd known what he was selling and to whom. I could have reconstructed him like a burned archive, fragment by fragment, and handed him himself as a gift. Instead I'd spent those years reading warrants with his description on them, learning his habits from informants, knowing everything about him except the only true thing: that he had amputated his own past to survive, and called it trade.

The Empire calls memory-thieves parasites. I had written reports using that exact word. *Parasite, feeding on the grief of others.* And all along, the truth was that he was the host, and everyone else had fed on him, and the wound where his childhood used to be had never once been allowed to close.

Near midnight I woke him for his watch, and he took it without complaint, and I lay listening to the wind and thinking about the vault.

Because that was where the map pointed next. He'd shown me — grudgingly, with the air of a man showing a cellmate the tunnel — the third waypoint marked in lines of light that surfaced when his bare skin touched the parchment. Beneath the ice, north past the Shearwater pass. An imperial archive, decommissioned, unlisted. I knew the designation; every inquisitor did. Cold Vault Nine. Where the Empire keeps what it cannot destroy and dare not display.

Including, if my training served, the crown of Cindralle.

I should have felt dread. I felt something closer to purpose, which frightened me more. Three weeks ago I would have arrested him at that fire, taken the map, written a report, gone home to quarters I barely lived in. Now I lay on frozen ground planning infiltration of my own former employer alongside a man who didn't remember me, and the strangest part — the part I'd take out and examine later, when there was time — was that it didn't feel like betrayal.

It felt like coming home by a door I'd never noticed.

Before sleep finally took me, I said it into the dark, quietly, not sure if he heard:

"When we're done — if we live — I'll tell you everything again. Every year. As long as it takes."

From across the dead fire, after a long pause, his voice came back dry as ever, but softer underneath the dryness, like a blade wrapped in cloth.

"Start saving up, inquisitor. I'm expensive."

---

**Vesper**

Dawn came gray and mean, and we broke camp and climbed.

Somewhere past midmorning, crossing a shelf of scree below the pass, I stumbled — the leg, honestly, the leg this time — and threw a hand out to catch myself, and my bare palm met exposed stone, and through the stone, faint as a pulse under skin, I felt the map wake.

Not hunger. Not the flooding pull of stolen memory. Something else. Something almost shy.

It was Sera's voice, still living somewhere behind my sternum where I'd filed it overnight. *Memory isn't property. It doesn't get spent when it's shared.*

I understood then what the map had done in the night, while we slept, while she talked into the dark and gave away her childhood for free. It had tried to drink her gift. It had pressed itself against everything she poured out — the chapel, the saints, the boy crying where no one could see — and found it could not swallow. Because it feeds on theft, on loss, on the price paid. A freely given memory carries no debt, and the map is nothing but debt wearing parchment.

It had refused her. And I felt, in the momentary contact, its frustration — a starved thing licking a closed hand.

Which meant something else too. Something that made me stop on the scree and turn, and look at Sera climbing steadily behind me with frost in her hair, offering herself across the hole in me again and again, knowing it wouldn't take, giving anyway.

If gifts can't feed it, I thought — if the freely given is the one currency it cannot spend —

Then maybe that's not just a limitation.

Maybe that's a seam.

"Keep moving," Sera said, catching me staring. "You can brood at altitude like everyone else."

"Onward," I agreed, and climbed, and said nothing yet.

But all that day, as the pass narrowed and the cold deepened and the map hummed its low hungry note against my chest, I turned the thought over and over like a coin I wasn't ready to spend: the vault beneath the ice held a crown, and crowns are given, not taken — and everything the Empire ever stole, it stole by calling it law.

Three days north, Cold Vault Nine waited under the glacier, holding the last gold of Cindralle.

And for the first time since the salt gate, I wondered whether the map had brought me here to complete itself —

— or whether I'd brought *it*, and the difference mattered more than either of us knew.
