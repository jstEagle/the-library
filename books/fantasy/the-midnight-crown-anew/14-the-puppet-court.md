## 14. The Puppet Court

There are three ways to enter Aurel's Reach if you're not supposed to be there. You can bribe the gate sergeant, which costs coin and leaves a trail of receipts. You can go over the walls, which costs broken bones and leaves a trail of blood. Or you can walk in through the front gate wearing the uniform of an inquisitor whose warrant no one at this end has ever bothered to verify — because the Empire's great secret is that its invincibility runs on paperwork, and nobody checks paperwork that looks tired enough.

I chose door number three. I've always been frugal.

The sergeant at the River Gate glanced at my credentials, glanced at my face, and decided both were somebody else's problem. "Inquisitor Aldane," he read off the seal, and something flickered behind his eyes — not recognition, exactly, more like the way a man flinches from a debt collector even when the debt isn't his. Inquisitors have that effect. We are the Empire's conscience, and consciences make everyone nervous.

"Business?" he asked.

"Court business," I said. Which was true, though not in the way he'd have understood it.

He waved me through, and just like that I was inside the city where Cindralle had been unmade — or rather, where the unmaking was dressed up in bunting and called a homecoming.

* * *

Aurel's Reach sits on the bones of a Cindrali city whose name appears in no surviving document. I know this now because I spent three nights in the imperial archive beneath the Viceroy's palace, reading by candle-stub, learning what my superiors hoped I would never learn: that the erasure was engineered, itemized, and signed. There was a ledger entry for every burned library. Somebody had costed out the ink.

The city above the archive wears its theft comfortably. Aurelian columns bolted onto Cindrali foundations. Imperial banners hung over doorways carved with mountain-lions, the old royal sigil, defaced just enough to pass for decorative stonework. The colonists here are fourth-generation now; they were born on top of someone else's grave and told the ground had always been empty. You can't blame them for believing it. I believed worse things for longer, and I got paid for it.

My orders, transmitted in cipher from the Office of Ecclesiastical Inquiry, were simple: confirm the status of the claimant, assess the security perimeter, and prepare the province for the *Rite of Confirmation*, wherein the returned Queen of Cindralle would formally petition the Emperor for incorporation. The Empire loves a petition. It lets them call conquest a favor.

What the orders did not say — what I had to dig out of the archive myself, cross-referencing a supply requisition against a physician's invoice — was what the claimant actually was.

I found the answer on my fourth night, and it fit on a single line:

*Somniferous tincture, 40 minims daily, administered under seal. Dosage per directive of the Office.*

Forty minims. Enough to keep a grown man docile. For a girl of sixteen, enough to keep her smiling at strangers and forgetting them by morning.

I sat in the archive cold until the candle drowned in its own wax, and I thought about how many reports I had written in eleven years of service, and how many of them had been true only because I had declined to look underneath them.

* * *

The presentation was held in the Hall of Reconciliation, which had been the throne room of a dead kingdom and was now a theater with better upholstery.

They put her on a dais under the restored banner of Cindralle — the midnight crown stitched in silver thread, the mountain-lion rampant — and they let the crowd come up in shifts to look at her. That was the whole ceremony. A viewing. Like a reliquary. Like a prize hog at the autumn fair, except the fair at least let you bid.

Elira Vael-Cindralle, last living heir of the erased kingdom, sat on a chair too large for her and smiled.

It was a good smile. That's what made it obscene. It was warm and slightly shy and it reached her eyes, and I knew — because I had read the physician's invoice, because I had done the arithmetic on forty minims a day — that none of it belonged to her. The drugs didn't hollow her out; they curated her. They took whatever the girl might actually have felt and replaced it with a gentle, compliant pleasantness, the emotional equivalent of wallpaper. The Empire hadn't stolen a queen and propped her up. It had stolen a person and installed a product.

The crowd loved her. An old woman in a faded Cindrali shawl wept openly and pressed her forehead to the edge of the dais, murmuring a name — a family name, I'd have guessed, some name the erasure had missed, surviving the way names survive, in kitchens and cradles where the archives can't reach. And Elira looked down at her with those curated, wallpaper eyes and said, in a voice pitched to carry, "I am so happy to be home."

The old woman wept harder. The guards smiled. The Viceroy's chamberlain, standing beside me with his hands folded like a man at a funeral he'd organized, leaned slightly toward me and said, "Remarkable, isn't it? After two hundred years. Providence."

"Providence," I agreed.

I want to be precise about what happened in me at that moment, because it wasn't rage. Rage would have been cleaner. What happened was smaller and colder: I heard myself say *providence* in the same tone I'd used in a hundred interrogations, the tone that means *the conversation is over*, and I realized I had been using that tone on the truth itself for eleven years. Every uncomfortable fact, filed and closed. Every crack in the edifice, papered with procedure.

The girl on the dais was sixteen years old and chemically prevented from being a person, and she was the load-bearing wall of an entire colonial government, and somewhere upstairs men in good coats would drink to her health tonight.

I looked at her hands. They rested on the arms of the chair, palms up, fingers loose — the posture of surrender, taught to her so thoroughly it had become rest. And then, for perhaps two seconds while the chamberlain was turning to greet someone behind us, the smile dropped off her face entirely. Not into grief or fear. Into nothing. Just a face with nobody operating it, waiting for the puppeteer to take up the strings again.

Then the crowd shifted, and the smile came back, and the old woman in the shawl touched her heart and whispered thanks to ancestors who had died believing their kingdom would someday need them.

I left the hall before I did something that couldn't be filed.

* * *

Here is what I did instead, over the following six days, with the methodical patience that once made my superiors call me the best investigator in the western provinces:

I confirmed the security perimeter. Four rings, rotation schedules copied, guard strength noted. I assessed the claimant's handlers: a governess who was also a warden, a physician who was also a jailer, a chaplain who reported separately to the Office, because even her prayers were double-booked. I drafted the assessment of the Rite of Confirmation, complete with recommendations.

And then I sat down to write my report to the Office of Ecclesiastical Inquiry, and I found that I could not lie, and I could not tell the truth, and the space between those impossibilities turned out to be exactly wide enough for resignation.

The report went through seven drafts. I still have the drafts. I keep them the way other people keep love letters.

Draft one was honest. *The claimant is sedated daily with imperial sanction. She is displayed to the public in a state of chemical compliance. The Rite of Confirmation is a fraud performed upon both peoples, and I decline to certify it.* I burned that one in the hearth of my lodgings and watched the words curl, thinking: this is the version that gets me hanged and changes nothing. They'd file my execution under *regrettable irregularity* and find another inquisitor by spring.

Draft two was professional. All findings confirmed, all protocols satisfactory, recommendation to proceed. I got four lines into it before my hand stopped working. Not trembling — just refusing, the way a lock refuses a key that's been cut wrong. Eleven years of writing lies in service of a truth I'd been promised existed, and I discovered that my loyalty had been spending itself all along without my noticing, like coins from a purse with a hole in it, and the purse was finally empty.

Drafts three through five were negotiations. With whom, I'm not sure. God, possibly. The girl on the dais, probably.

Draft seven, the one I sent, began like this:

*To the Office of Ecclesiastical Inquiry, from Inquisitor Sera Aldane, Warrant 4471. By your instruction I have completed my assessment of the claimant and the province. My findings follow. So does my resignation, which you will find is the same document.*

I laid it out the way I'd lay out any case. Evidence, sources, chain of custody — the archive invoices, the physician's sealed directives, the dosages, the choreography of the presentations, the chamberlain's own word: *providence*. I named the offices involved. I named, carefully but completely, the fact that the Office had known since before my assignment that the heir lived, and had chosen display over disclosure, puppetry over policy. I wrote: *You did not recover the heir of Cindralle. You manufactured her absence and then sold tickets to her return.*

And then, because I am still, at the bottom of everything, the orphan from Thornfell who learned that the difference between survival and rot is a line you draw yourself, I added the part that ended my career:

*I entered the Empire's service because I believed we were the order that stood between people and the dark. I have spent eleven years discovering that we are frequently the dark, wearing order's coat. I will not certify this fraud. I will not arrest anyone for knowing about it. I am resigning my warrant effective immediately and going somewhere I can look in a mirror without an interpreter. Do not send anyone after me. You'll want to. Send them anyway, if it helps you sleep; I've read your files, and I know what helps you sleep.*

I signed it. I sealed it with my warrant — my actual warrant, cracked in half across the seal, because I am given to gestures and I've stopped apologizing for it — and I paid a disreputable courier I'd once arrested twice to deliver it to the Office by hand.

Then I sat in my lodgings with half a bottle of Thornfell rye that tasted like my whole childhood, and I waited to feel free.

I did not feel free. I felt precisely as frightened as I had ever felt, minus the structure. Fear without a chain of command turns out to be just weather. But underneath the fear was something new, small and stubborn, like the first green thing through ash: the knowledge that tomorrow, when the girl on the dais smiled her wallpaper smile, I would not be one of the reasons she wore it.

That's not heroism. I want that on the record, in my own hand, in my own voice: it is not heroism. It is the minimum. It is simply refusing, at long last, to be the ink in someone else's ledger.

* * *

By the ninth day, the Office's answer arrived — not by courier but by consequence, which is how the Empire prefers to correspond.

Two riders in plain clothes took a room at the inn across from my lodgings. They were bad at surveillance, which meant they weren't trying to hide; they were letting me see them, the way you leave a note on a table rather than slip it under the door. The message was legible: *We received your letter. We are deciding what you are now.* Asset, liability, or corpse — the three nouns available to ex-inquisitors.

I gave them until dusk, then packed one bag, left the second rider a note of my own (*She is a child. Whatever you decide I am, decide faster than she grows up.*), and went out the back into streets that had spent two hundred years pretending to be someone else's.

Which is when I allowed myself to think about Vesper Voss.

I'd been avoiding the thought for nine days, the way you avoid a stair in the dark that you know is broken. Because everything I'd seen in the Hall of Reconciliation pointed at him like a compass needle. He was coming here — I was certain of it, certain the way I'd never been certain about anything in a report. The map, the waypoints, the door. He was walking toward the girl on the dais with a stolen relic in his pack and a hole where his childhood used to be, and I knew — because I had read the same archives, because the map's design was older than the Empire and meaner than it — that what waited for him in the capital was not a rescue.

It was a final payment. And I did not think the map cared who made it.

I stood in the shadow of a doorway that had once belonged to a Cindrali family whose name was scrubbed from the tax rolls, and I understood that I had resigned from the Empire in time to arrive at the only post I'd ever truly wanted: the one between Vesper Voss and the worst mistake of both our lives.

The plain-clothes riders would come looking by morning. Vesper would come looking sooner. And somewhere above us both, in a gilded room smelling of laudanum and lilies, a sixteen-year-old queen was falling asleep with a smile she hadn't chosen, dreaming — if the drugs let her dream at all — of a door that remembered.

I pulled my hood up and started walking.
