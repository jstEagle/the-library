## 16. Crown of Borrowed Light

They drug the water.

I want to say that up front, because everything else I'm about to tell you only makes sense if you understand that I have spent eleven months pretending to be more asleep than I am, and that this requires a certain discipline. You would think it would be easy, playing at being a puppet. It is not. It is the hardest work I have ever done, and I once spent a full summer learning to curtsey without resenting it, which anyone who has met my grandmother's ghost will tell you is genuinely difficult.

The drug they use is called lullwater in the palace kitchens, though the guards just call it *her tea*. It arrives every morning in a porcelain cup painted with little blue birds — a joke, I suspect, on someone's part. Birds. In a cage. Very droll. The cup goes to a girl who is supposedly the last heir of Cindralle, returned from hiding to bless the Empire's occupation of her own country, and everyone involved in delivering it believes she drinks it down and drifts into a soft, pliable half-sleep suitable for public appearances.

I drink perhaps a third of it. The rest goes into the fern.

The fern is doing remarkably well, considering. It sits on the windowsill of my apartments in the Viceroy's Palace, three stories above the Avenue of Bells, and it has absorbed enough imperial sedative over the past year to fell a horse. Perhaps that is why its fronds have gone slightly purple. Or perhaps ferns simply turn purple when they are complicit in treason. I wouldn't know. Botany was not considered an essential skill for queens, according to my tutors, all four of whom are now dead or employed elsewhere.

My name is Elira Vayne-Solenne, and for most of my life I did not know that either.

But let me start where a story should start, which is not with ferns, but with a thief.

* * *

The first time I saw Vesper Voss, he was stealing something, which surprised no one, least of all me.

I had been watching visitors for months by then — that was the whole architecture of my survival, you understand. A girl in my position has two choices: she can be the doll they display, or she can be the ghost who watches. I chose ghost. Dolls get dressed up and eventually discarded; ghosts get ignored, and the ignored see everything.

So I watched. The viceroy came Tuesdays to rehearse my lines with me — *yes, Your Excellency, the people rejoice; yes, Your Excellency, Cindralle remembers its true friends* — and he always smelled of ink and bad conscience. The handlers came daily, adjusting the dose, checking my pupils against a little card of diagrams. Priests came monthly, to confirm publicly that the returned queen was pious and privately that she was docile. Courtiers came to be seen near me, the way one stands near a lit brazier in winter: not for warmth, exactly, but because proximity to power is its own kind of currency, even borrowed power. Even mine.

And then, on a grey afternoon in late autumn, a man came through my doors who moved like none of them.

He walked like a man counting exits. He stood like a man whose weight lived in his heels, ready to leave. Everyone else in that palace occupied space as if space belonged to them by imperial decree; this man occupied space the way a cat does — provisionally, watchfully, with a running tally of every object in the room that might be worth taking.

The jailers introduced him as a scholar. He was the worst-disguised scholar I have ever seen. Scholars fidget with their sleeves when nervous; he didn't fidget at all, which meant he wasn't nervous, which meant he was dangerous. His eyes went to the guards' keys, to the door hinges, to the window latch, to the fern — and then, briefly, to me, and something crossed his face that no one else who visited ever showed.

Recognition.

Not of me. Of what I was. Of the shape of a cage seen from inside one of your own.

I decided, in that moment, that he was the one.

It sounds mad when I put it plainly. But consider: everyone else who entered these rooms wanted something *from* me — my blessing, my face, my silence, my bloodline worn like a coat. This man looked at me the way I imagine a locksmith looks at a lock he didn't build: professionally, ruefully, with a kind of tired sympathy. And there was something else. Something about his hands. They were scarred across the knuckles in a pattern I recognized from the old stories my grandmother told, the ones smuggled into my childhood in fragments before even those fragments were taken from me — the marks of a memory-thief who works barehanded, because gloves blunt the gift.

A memory-thief. In my prison. Sent, I would later learn, by a map older than the Empire itself.

You must understand what the Empire took from me before you understand why I chose him. They took my grandmother's name off the histories. They took my mother's valley off the maps. They took my childhood — not metaphorically; the handlers dosed me young, to keep the claimant pliable, and whatever years I lived before the age of ten sit behind a fog so thick I cannot even mourn them properly, because mourning requires remembering what was lost. I am a queen of smoke, heir to a country that officially never existed, kept alive as proof of a mercy the Empire never extended.

So yes. When a thief walked into my cage, I did not think: *rescue*. I thought: *finally, someone who understands that some things should be stolen back, and other things should be broken beyond use.*

* * *

Getting messages to him through my jailers required patience, and a small talent for misdirection that I like to think would have made my grandmother proud, wherever her ghost is lodged these days.

Here is how you pass a message out of a gilded prison when every word you speak is catalogued: you don't speak it. You *arrange* it.

The guards rotate weekly, but their habits don't. Corporal Brennick takes snuff and always accepts a pinch from any tin offered, because palace snuff is finer than barracks snuff, and men are weak in predictable ways. Lieutenant Ossa is ambitious and reports everything I say, verbatim, in reports that go up the chain to a handler named Dreven who skims them — I know he skims them, because I once embedded a passage of pure nonsense in the middle of a Tuesday rehearsal and watched it travel unremarked all the way into the archive. Verbatim reporting, skimmed archives. That gap between the words and the reading is where I live.

Brennick's snuff tin travels. Ossa's reports travel. And I, the docile puppet, sit by my window embroidering — another skill deemed essential for queens, and the single most useful thing I was ever taught, because embroidery is a language and no one in this palace reads it.

Into the lining of a snuff tin I once tucked a scrap of linen worked with a pattern of thorned vines climbing a mountain, and beneath the vines, a small door with no handle. Into the margin of a prayer book I "accidentally" left behind after a priestly visit, I worked a crown drawn in black thread — black, not gold — resting on a bed of ash. Little poems in silk. Riddles fit for a thief: *the door remembers; the crown is ash; look closer at what you've been sent to steal.*

Whether they reached him, I couldn't know. Messages passed through enemy hands are letters thrown into rivers. You write them anyway. Hope is a discipline, like embroidery, like pretending to sleep. You do it daily whether or not it works, because the alternative is to stop, and stopping is how puppets stay puppets.

Then, six weeks after the scholar-that-wasn't first appeared, a new guard brought my evening tea and set the tray down slightly crooked, the way no trained servant would, and on the saucer, under the cup, was a pressed sprig of rowan.

Rowan doesn't grow within fifty miles of the capital. Rowan grows in the Thornfells. In the mountains where, according to the fog-shrouded scraps of my childhood, someone once carried me at night, humming a song about salt gates and winter roads, while the Empire's banners burned on the horizon behind us.

He had read the messages. He had understood them. And he was telling me, in the only language available to either of us, that he knew where I had come from — that he could trace the road home written in my own stitched riddles.

I held the rowan sprig until the handler came to collect the tray, and then I let him take it, unremarked, because a dead leaf means nothing to a man who skims.

That night I slept genuinely well, lullwater and all. The fern could spare it.

* * *

He came again today.

I want to record this properly, while it's still true, because I have learned that truth in this place is perishable — it spoils like milk, it fades like dye, and the Empire is very good at letting things fade.

They brought him at midday, formally this time, with the viceroy presiding and four guards and a clerk to record my responses for the archive that no one reads closely. The pretext was a petition — there is always a petition; the machinery of legitimacy requires constant oiling with paperwork — and the substance was this: a stranger had come asking after the queen's health, bearing tokens of Cindralle's restoration, and the court wished to know how Her Majesty received such news.

Which was, translated out of court-language: *we have caught the thief, we have taken his map, and we are going to make you smile about it.*

Except they hadn't caught anything. I could see it in Vesper's face, in the careful blankness he wore like the scholar's robe he'd traded for courtier's grey. A caught man looks defeated. This man looked like a man standing very still in tall grass. The map they thought they'd seized — the clerk displayed a wax-sealed roll with great ceremony — was, unless I missed my guess entirely, a forgery. I had sent instructions for exactly such a forgery in my fourth message, worked into a sampler of alphabet practice: *let them find a seal; seals can be false; the true door has no handle.*

He played his part beautifully. Humbled, awed, grateful for the queen's gracious attention. He knelt when instructed to kneel. And when the viceroy commanded me to give the visitor my blessing — the little theater they stage to remind the capital that Cindralle loves its conquerors — Vesper raised his eyes to mine, and I gave him my blessing, and under it, in the language of a lifetime spent watching faces, I gave him everything else.

Let me tell you what my blessing said.

It said: *I know what you carry. I know what it costs you — your hands shake when you think no one is looking, and you touch your temple when you think of home, the way people do when the home isn't there anymore. I know the map wants my memories to finish itself, because I have read the old accounts my grandmother hid in songs, and the door that remembers is not a door at all.* It said: *they will offer you my freedom as the price of my selfhood, and they will make it sound like mercy, and the map will make it sound like destiny, and both of them are lying.* It said: *I have been awake for every moment of my imprisonment, and I am choosing you, thief, of all the people who have ever walked through these doors, because you are the only one who has ever lost something to this empire and knows precisely how much a memory weighs.*

And then, aloud, in the voice of a docile puppet queen, I said: "May the Empire keep its faithful daughter safe," and smiled the smile they had drilled into me, and watched the clerk write it down verbatim for an archive no one reads.

Vesper's jaw tightened, just once. Good. He heard it. Or he heard enough, and the rest would arrive the way my messages always arrived — sideways, through the gaps.

Because here is what I need him to understand, and what I will spend whatever remains of my patience making him understand: the map cannot free me. That is its cruelest trick. It is keyed to the heir's memories, and the heir's memories *are* the heir. Feed it what it wants and what remains in this chair will be a body with a famous name and nothing behind the eyes — a vacant throne wearing my face, ready for whoever holds the crown to fill with whatever story suits them. My rescue and my erasure are, as far as the map is concerned, the same act performed with different music.

There is only one honest ending for that thing, and it involves breaking.

* * *

Tonight the palace is quiet in the way it gets before snow — the kind of hush that makes the Avenue of Bells below sound like it's holding its breath. The fern droops contentedly in its pot, drunk as a bishop. Somewhere below, a thief is deciding what to do with the truth I handed him, wrapped in a blessing and a dead leaf's worth of hope.

I sit at my window and I do the arithmetic I have done every night for eleven months.

If he frees me with the map, I walk out of here as a legend and wake up as nobody. The Empire wins anyway — they'll prop up whatever's left, or they'll lament the poor lost queen, or worse, some viceroy's cousin will wear my face's absence like a vacancy to be filled. Thousands of colonists keep their stolen homes built on my stolen country, and I become a story with the ending torn out.

If he destroys it — if he spends whatever he has left to overload that hungry thing and shatters the seal — then maybe the concealment breaks too. Maybe the valley becomes visible. Maybe the lie gets heavy enough to collapse under its own weight, with everyone watching. Maybe I lose the fog-bound scraps of childhood I have left, the song about salt gates, my grandmother's humming. Maybe I lose everything except the choosing.

I have thought about this a great deal, and here is what I have decided, here in my tower, with my drugged fern and my embroidered treasons:

I would rather be a person with no past than a queen with no present. I would rather choose wrongly, freely, than be chosen for, correctly, forever. The Empire has spent twenty years teaching me that I am a symbol. The map wants to teach me that I am a key. And I am done being objects, whichever way the sentence is arranged.

Tomorrow, if my messages land as they should, a man who trades in other people's memories is going to climb toward a room he thinks contains a sleeping girl. What he'll find instead is a ghost who has been awake the entire time, holding out her hand.

Take it, thief. And when you do —

When the doors below bang open, and boots come up the stairs faster than any rehearsal schedule allows, and the bells on the avenue begin to ring at an hour when bells should be silent — I will learn whether he understood the last riddle, the one I stitched into nothing at all, the one that said simply: *break it, not me.*

The first guard reaches my door. The handle turns.

Well. Let us see what kind of thief I chose.
