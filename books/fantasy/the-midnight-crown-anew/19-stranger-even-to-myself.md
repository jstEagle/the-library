## 19. Stranger Even to Myself

I woke to the smell of woodsmoke and barley porridge, and to a woman telling me my own name.

"Vesper," she said, for what I gathered was not the first time. "Vesper Voss. Can you hear me?"

The ceiling above me was rough-hewn timber, black with age and smoke, hung with drying herbs that swayed in some draft I couldn't find. My body felt like a house after a flood — everything still standing, but nothing where it belonged. I knew what hands were. I knew what cold was. I knew, distantly, the way you know the shape of a word in a language you no longer speak, that I had once known other things. Names. A road. A reason.

They were gone. All of them. I had fed them into something, I understood that much — there was a hollow in me shaped exactly like a map, and it ached the way a pulled tooth aches, and beyond that ache there was nothing but winter light through a shutter and this woman's face, weathered and careful, watching me like I might shatter.

"Vesper," she said again.

"That's me," I said. It came out as a rasp. "Apparently."

Something crossed her face — not quite relief, not quite grief. "You've been asleep two days. Fevered. We thought—" She stopped herself, pressed her lips together, started over. "You carried a girl down out of the high pass. Through the storm. Half-frozen yourself when you arrived. Do you remember?"

I searched myself honestly, the way you'd pat your pockets looking for coins. Nothing. No pass. No girl. No storm — only a general, secondhand knowledge of storms, like something read in a book.

"No," I said. And then, because she looked like she needed it more than I did: "But it sounds like something I'd do."

She laughed, or made a sound adjacent to laughing, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "You would," she said. "You did."

* * *

Her name was Odile Vay. She was Cindrali — one of the first families to come down into the valley after the night the mountains opened, after the ruins became visible to everyone, after whatever it was I'd done up there. She told me all of it in pieces, over the next day, while I sat wrapped in blankets learning to be a person from scratch.

"You broke the seal on an old thing," she said. "A map. Sera told us. The concealment died with it. The valley — the whole capital, what's left of it — everyone can see it now. The Empire can't pretend Cindralle never existed. Not anymore. Not with ten thousand colonists staring at its bones every morning."

"Sera," I said.

"The inquisitor." Odile's mouth went wry. "Ex-inquisitor. She's been standing at your door since they brought you in, and she only sleeps when we make her. You know her?"

"I don't know anyone," I said. "That's rather the point of me."

She didn't laugh at that one. Neither did I.

Here is what it is like, being empty: it is not darkness. Darkness would be honest. It is more like standing in a room where the furniture has been carried out — you can see the pale rectangles on the walls where pictures hung, the worn paths in the floorboards where feet once traveled, and you know, with total certainty, that someone lived here. That someone was you. But the rooms are bare, and echoing, and every so often you catch yourself reaching for a door handle that isn't there anymore.

I remembered how to speak three languages. I did not remember my mother's face — I'd sold that years ago anyway, Odile told me gently, before any of this. I remembered how to pick a lock with a straw and a prayer. I did not remember why I'd learned. I remembered Thornfell the way a cartographer remembers a coastline — accurate, useless, unloved.

And I remembered nothing at all about a girl named Elira, except that when Odile said her name, something behind my ribs leaned toward it like a plant toward a window.

"She asks after you too," Odile said, watching my face do whatever it was doing. "Every morning. *Is he awake yet. Is he himself.*"

"What do you tell her?"

"The truth. That we're not sure who 'himself' is yet."

* * *

On the third day, I walked.

The valley took me the way a tide takes a man — slowly, then all at once. Snow lay over the ruins of Cindralle's capital like a sheet over furniture in a closed house, and out of that whiteness rose walls, arches, the ribs of a palace stripped to its frame. People moved among the bones. Colonists — Aurelian faces, farm-coarse hands, the look of people who had been promised land and were now discovering the land had been promised to someone else first. Refugees — Cindrali faces, older than their years, carrying stones from the ruin to build new walls beside the old ones. They did not mix, exactly. But they shared fires. I saw an Aurelian child and a Cindrali child building something in the snow that was either a fort or a shrine, and both sets of parents pretending not to watch.

It should have moved me. Everyone kept saying it should move me. *You did this,* they said, with a reverence that sat on me like wet wool. *You gave us back our country.*

And I looked at the broken city and felt what a stranger feels at any beautiful grave: respect, distance, cold toes.

"There he is."

Sera Aldane was leaning against a fallen column with her arms crossed, and even without a single memory of her, I knew her instantly — the way you know a scar is yours. She was tall and gray-eyed and carried herself like a drawn blade pretending to be sheathed, and there were new lines around her mouth that hadn't come from smiling.

"You're supposed to be resting," she said.

"Am I?" I said. "Says who? I don't recall."

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip. Then, quieter: "Don't joke about it. Please."

We stood there in the snow, two people who had supposedly crossed a kingdom together, saved each other, traded truths on a frozen road — and I had none of it. She had all of it. I could see it sitting in her like ballast, heavy and precious, and I watched her decide, moment by moment, whether to spend it on me.

"He used to joke constantly," she said finally, to the ruin, to the air. "Terrible jokes. To keep from being afraid. He thought nobody noticed." Her jaw worked. "I'm going to tell you things, Voss. About you. About us. Whenever you ask. That's a promise. But I need you to understand — I'm not going to pretend it doesn't hurt that you don't remember. It hurts like hell. I just buried a career and a god and half my life for that map, and the man who broke it looks at me like I'm a stranger at a funeral."

"Whose funeral?"

"Ours," she said. "Both of ours. The people we were."

I thought about that for a long moment. Snow fell between us, patient as arithmetic.

"Then let's not mourn them standing up," I said. "Tell me about the man I was. Start anywhere."

So she did. She told me about two orphans in a Thornfell waystation, stealing bread and warmth in equal measure. She told me he'd sold his memories of her, years ago, to pay a debt — that she'd learned this on a riverbank and forgiven him on a bridge. She told me he was a thief of extraordinary skill and mediocre conscience who had spent his whole life keeping nothing, hoarding no past, because keeping things meant losing them.

"And now?" I asked.

"Now he has nothing," Sera said. "Which means—" and here she smiled, small and fierce and sad, "—now anything he keeps, he keeps on purpose."

* * *

Elira came on the fifth day.

I heard her before I saw her — a cane tapping stone, unhurried, sovereign in the way that has nothing to do with thrones. When she entered the long hall where I'd been convalescing, the room changed pressure. She was sixteen, thin as a winter branch, with her mother's crown-shaped shadow hanging over her that everyone politely ignored, and she looked at me across thirty feet of flagstone like she was reading a ledger and finding it balanced.

"So," she said. "You're the stranger."

"They tell me I used to be somebody."

"They tell me I used to be a queen." She lowered herself onto the bench opposite me, arranged the cane across her knees. Up close, her eyes were the color of old glass, and very, very awake. "I sent you messages, in the palace. Through the jailers. Did they reach you?"

"I wouldn't know," I said. "There's a gap where that should be."

Something flickered in her face — disappointment, quickly mastered. "Then I'll say it again, and this time you can't lose it. I chose you. Out of every thief and soldier and schemer who came near my cage, I chose the memory-thief, because a thief knows better than anyone that a stolen thing is never really yours. I asked you to break the map. You did. You paid more than I ever asked." She tilted her head. "Do you regret it?"

I reached inward, honestly, thoroughly. Patted all the pockets of my soul.

"I regret nothing," I said, "because regret needs a before, and I haven't got one. But—" and here I stopped, surprised by my own voice, "—when I think of the map breaking, something in me sits down by a fire. Like coming home. Only I don't have a home. So maybe that's the closest thing to an answer I own."

Elira studied me for a long time. Then she nodded, once, the way judges nod.

"Good," she said. "Because I'm going to refuse the crown. Publicly. And half these people are going to call me mad, and the other half are going to call me a traitor, and I will need at least one person in this valley who owes the old Cindralle nothing — who can't even remember it — to stand up and say the future isn't a restoration." She almost smiled. "You owe nothing to anyone, Vesper Voss. Do you understand what a gift that is? You're the only free man in this valley."

* * *

That evening, I found Sera on the wall above the eastern gate, watching the road where imperial banners would eventually come flying, because empires always come, eventually, to see what their maps got wrong.

"You shouldn't be up here alone," I said. "In case they send for the ex-inquisitor."

"They won't send for me," she said. "They'll send for the traitor Aldane. There's a difference, and the difference is rope." She glanced sideways at me. "Besides. Someone has to stand guard. It's the only job I know how to do."

"You could learn another."

"Could I?" For a moment the blade showed through, the old fear, the orphan's arithmetic: nothing held is nothing lost. Then she looked at me — at the pale rectangles on my walls, at all the doors with no handles — and something in her settled. "Maybe," she said. "Teach me a trade. Something honest."

"I don't know any honest trades," I said. "I know theft, survival, and apparently heroics."

"Then teach me the first one backwards."

I laughed — and it startled me, because the laugh fit. It sat in my chest like it had lived there always, which was impossible, which was exactly the point. The man I'd been had built that laugh out of stolen years and given it away piece by piece until there was nothing left. This laugh was new. Cheap. Mine.

Below us, the valley smoked with a hundred small fires, Cindrali and Aurelian, and somewhere a child shrieked with delight at the snow, and Elira was somewhere writing a speech that would end a dynasty, and the Empire was gathering itself in the dark like a fist deciding whether to close.

"Ask me tomorrow," I said. "Whether I regret it, I mean. Ask me every day if you want. I'll give you a different answer each time, probably. Because I'm not the man who broke the map — I'm the man who gets to decide what that was worth."

Sera was quiet a moment. Then she held out her hand, formal as a treaty.

"Stranger," she said. "Welcome to Cindralle. What's left of it. What comes next."

I shook it. Her grip was warm, and I memorized it — deliberately, greedily, the way a poor man pockets a coin — the first entry in an archive no one could steal.

Down the valley road, far off, torchlight. Riders. Too many riders, moving fast, flying no banner I knew — which meant they flew one I'd forgotten, or one that hadn't existed until now.

Sera saw them the same moment I did. Her hand dropped from mine to the sword she no longer had a right to carry.

"Well," she said. "So much for the honest trades."
