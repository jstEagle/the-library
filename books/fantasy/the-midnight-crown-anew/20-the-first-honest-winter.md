## 20. The First Honest Winter

### Vesper

I am told my name is Vesper Voss. I am told I was born somewhere in the Thornfells, in a mining town whose name even the people who remember it pronounce apologetically. I am told I was a thief of considerable skill and questionable ethics.

None of it lands. It's like being handed a coat that fits perfectly and belongs to someone else.

What I have instead is this: the smell of woodsmoke at dawn over the valley, which I am told is new to me but feels like the oldest thing in the world. The sound of forty-odd people arguing in two languages about where a well should go. And a small, stubborn certainty, sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone, that whatever else is true, this — the arguing, the smoke, the cold coming down off the peaks like an honest creditor — this is mine.

The refugees call the valley by its old name. The colonists call it by the surveyor's name on their imperial grants. Neither side will budge, so for now everyone just calls it *the valley*, said in a particular tone that means *we will absolutely be revisiting this*.

It has been eleven days since Elira melted nothing and broke everything, since the seal shattered and Cindralle's hiding place came apart like wet paper. Eleven days since I fed the map every scrap of home I had and watched it choke on them.

Eleven days, and I still don't know who I'm supposed to be grieving for.

This morning I'm hauling timber with a colonist named Brannic, a broad, sunburned man who lost his imperial land grant when the empire lost interest in pretending the grant meant anything. He talks while we work, the way men do when silence would force them to think.

"My grandfather cleared fields out there," he says, jerking his chin toward the terraces climbing the eastern slope — Cindrali terraces, cut centuries ago, abandoned when the city went under whatever spell erased it. "Three generations farming land that turned out to be someone's living room. You know what that does to a man?"

"No," I say honestly. "I don't know what anything does to anyone. I'm working from scratch."

He grunts. He's heard the story — everyone's heard the story, embroidered differently depending on who tells it — and I think what he hears in it isn't pity but recognition. A man without a past, building on land with too much of one. We are both squatters, in a way. Him in history. Me in my own skull.

"The way I see it," Brannic says, setting another beam into the frame of the meeting hall, "nobody made those terraces. Somebody did. Somebody with hands like mine. So either we use them or we're just two kinds of fool standing in a field refusing each other's help."

"That's almost wise."

"Don't tell my wife. She thinks I'm a rock with opinions."

By midday the hall's skeleton stands against the snowlight, and I stand back and look at it and feel — not memory, nothing so generous — but something adjacent. Like hearing a song you've never heard and knowing how the next line goes anyway.

Maren Quill finds me there. She arrived last week with the second wave of refugees, older now, one eye clouded, her ledger-hand still quick. She looks me up and down with the frank appraisal of a woman who once bought my mother's face for the price of a wagon ride, and I look back at her with the frank appraisal of a man who cannot remember why that transaction hurt.

"You don't hate me," she observes. "That's unsettling."

"Should I?"

"Probably. You hated beautifully." She tucks her hands into her sleeves against the cold. "You'd want to know — the map. There were copies. Rumors. Inquisitors kept duplicates; it was policy. If one surfaced—"

"It didn't," Sera says, arriving behind us the way she always arrives behind us, silent as a settled debt. "I burned the archive copies myself, before I left the capital. All but one." She glances at Maren. "And the one is accounted for."

Maren raises her eyebrows, files something away — some things don't change, apparently, regardless of who's doing the filing — and drifts off toward the cookfire.

Sera and I stand in the snow, two people who used to be children together in a town I can't picture, guarding a future neither of us was trained for.

"You could have led with 'the copies are destroyed,'" I say.

"You looked calm. I hate wasting a good entrance."

### Sera

There is a particular exhaustion that comes from winning. Nobody warns you about it. You spend years believing that if only the lie were exposed, if only the truth stood naked in the square, everything downstream would right itself — and then you tear the lie down and discover that truth is not a foundation. It's a flood. People drown in it. Then they get up, shivering, and start arguing about property lines.

I should know. I wrote the report that started the flood.

Now I stand guard over a valley full of people who have excellent reasons to hate me — I carried the warrant, I hunted the smuggler, I served the machine that erased a kingdom — and the strange thing, the thing I turn over at night like a stone looking for the soft underside, is that most of them don't. Not because they've forgiven me. Because they're too tired. Grief and hope burn through a person at roughly the same rate, and this valley has been burning through both since the day the mountains stopped hiding.

My duties, such as they are: I walk the perimeter at dawn. I settle disputes that turn sharp. I keep the one honest account of Cindralle's erasure locked in a wax-cloth wrapping against my ribs, because somebody has to remember what happened even if nobody wants to carry it.

Today's dispute involves a well.

The Cindrali elders want it dug near the old cistern works, which are sound engineering and sacred ground besides. The colonists want it near the eastern terraces, closer to the fields they intend to plant come thaw. Both positions are reasonable. Both factions have dug in like the war never ended, mostly because neither side has yet had a conversation that wasn't really an argument about who this valley belongs to.

The answer, which everyone knows and no one will say aloud, is: both. And neither. It belongs to whoever feeds it, and it will belong to their children, who will be neither purely one thing nor the other and will find all our categories quaint.

I let them shout for a while. There's a discipline to letting people exhaust themselves; I learned it in interrogation rooms and I'm still deciding whether it's a skill or a scar.

Then Elira walks in.

She's nineteen now, though the palace drugs aged her strangely and the free air is aging her back. She wears no crown — she melted it, or rather she watched it become plow-blades, which is a sentence I expect historians will mangle for centuries — and she carries herself the way water carries a current: gently, and with irresistible direction underneath.

"I'm going to say a word," she tells the assembly, "and I'd like everyone to stay calm. The word is *council*."

Someone laughs. Someone else throws a crust of bread at her, affectionately. She catches it and eats it, which is very much her.

"A queen," she continues, "is a door that only opens one way. I lived in a palace where I was a door, and I can tell you what it's like inside a door. Dark. Cold. Everyone knocks, nobody asks if you want to be opened." She lets that sit. "So no crown. No restoration. My ancestors' names go back on the stones, the old language gets taught to any child who wants it, colonist or Cindrali — and decisions get made by nine people. Three chosen by the refugee families. Three by the colonists. Three by lot, from everyone, because I've been *chosen* before and I'll tell you, being chosen is overrated."

An elder — Old Yeva, who remembers the hidden city from before the concealment frayed — speaks slowly. "And who leads this council?"

"Whoever's right," Elira says. "That's the whole trick. That's the experiment. Nine chairs, one fire, and we find out if people who were taught to erase each other can learn to count each other instead."

Silence. The long kind, the kind where a room decides something it doesn't yet have words for.

Brannic, the colonist, spits thoughtfully into the snow. "Nine's an awkward number for voting."

"So is starving," Yeva replies, and something that is almost laughter moves through the hall, and that — that ugly, tired, half-broken laugh shared across a chasm — is the first honest vote this valley has ever cast.

* * *

### Elira

They ask me afterward why I refused, and I give different answers depending on who's asking, and all of them are true.

To the elders I say: a restored Cindralle built on the bones of the colonists' homes would be my ancestors' crime performed again with my face on it. To the colonists I say: a throne is a debt your grandchildren pay. To Vesper I say nothing at all, because he asked with his eyes, the way he does — carefully, like a man handling someone else's belongings — and what my eyes answered was: *because I watched what wanting a crown did to the map, and the map learned it from people.*

Here is what nobody understands about being a puppet claimant. They think the horror was the drugs, the display, the gilded cage. It wasn't. The horror was that it *worked*. I sat on that throne and watched desperate people look at me and see salvation, and I understood, drugged half-blind as I was, that I could have asked them for anything. Their farms. Their names. Their children's futures. And they'd have paid, gladly, because hope makes excellent collateral.

Power isn't a temptation you resist. It's a hunger you notice, usually too late, wearing your own face.

So: nine chairs. One fire. And me, chairing it for now, because someone has to hand the thing its first breath and then get out of its way.

The first session convenes at dusk in the unfinished hall, snow sifting through gaps in the roof onto the ninth chair, which is empty by design — the lot-drawn seat, held open until spring. We argue about the well for three hours. We resolve nothing. But we resolve it *together*, which is a different animal entirely, and when we finally break for the cookfire, Old Yeva touches my sleeve.

"You did well today."

"I did nothing today. That was the point."

"Mm." Her old eyes glitter. "Wait until the second session. Doing nothing gets much harder."

I laugh, and outside the wind comes down off the peaks carrying the smell of snow and pine and woodsmoke, and none of it is mine alone anymore, and that is the entire point.

* * *

### Vesper

Evening. The cookfire. I sit where I always sit — at the edge of the light, close enough for warmth, far enough that no one expects conversation from me. Old habits of a man who can't remember having friends, apparently, except that Sera drops down beside me anyway, and Brannic on my other side, and Maren across the flames with her one good eye gleaming, and I begin to suspect the edge of the light has quietly become the middle of it without consulting me.

"First council session," Sera says. "History being made."

"History being *argued about*," Brannic corrects. "Same thing, slower."

I watch the fire. Somewhere behind my eyes, where twenty-some years of a life used to live, there's just clean empty space — and yet when Yeva says a word in the old tongue, *serrath*, meaning hearth or home or maybe something between the two that our language never bothered to make, something in me leans toward it. Not memory. Orientation. Like a compass needle in a country with no maps left.

"Do you regret it?" Sera asks quietly. "What you gave up. What it cost."

I consider the question honestly, which takes a while, because honesty is slow work when you have no past to consult.

"I don't miss anything," I say finally. "You can't miss what you can't find. But sometimes I think I *would* have missed it. Whoever I was — I think he'd have carried this moment like a jewel. First winter where nobody's lying to anybody. So I try to hold it the way he would have."

Sera is quiet a moment. "That's the saddest beautiful thing I've ever heard."

"I'm told I was good with words. I'll take your word for it."

Across the fire, Elira rises to speak — something about seed stores, about the thaw, about the first planting — and the crowd hushes for her not because she demands it but because she's earned it twice over, once as prisoner and once as refuser. Snow falls through the roof gap and dies in the firelight. Children chase each other between the tents, colonist and Cindrali mixed together and arguing about a game whose rules they're inventing as they go, which is to say: governing, in miniature.

And then Maren Quill, who misses nothing, goes still. Her good eye fixes on the dark tree line beyond the firelight, and her voice, when it comes, is pitched low enough that only our end of the circle hears it.

"We have a visitor."

Sera is on her feet before the sentence ends, hand finding the knife she swore she'd retired. At the edge of the dark, where the pines swallow the firelight, a figure stands swaying — snow-crusted, stumbling, one arm raised as if in greeting or surrender or simply to stay upright. As it lurches into the light I see the torn imperial courier's coat, the frostbitten face, and the leather document case chained shut around its wrist.

The figure collapses at the edge of the circle. Sera reaches it first, turns it over.

Its lips are moving. I lean close. Frost has cracked them, and what comes out is barely a whisper:

"They're coming for the valley. Inquisitor-General's own seal. They know the heir lives — and they know *who unbound the map*."

Every face at the fire turns toward me.

I look back at them, this stranger with my name, holding a moment I was only just learning to keep — and feel it begin, already, to slip.
