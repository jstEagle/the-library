## 2. What the Gulls Remember

The gulls had been wrong all week.

Dominic noticed it the way a musician notices a string gone sour — not at first, but as an accumulation of small offenses that finally could not be ignored. The gulls of Vellamare were creatures of habit so rigid they might have been clerks: they rose from the fish-market rooftops at the same hour each morning, wheeled over the harbor in the same lazy ellipses, and screamed their grievances at the same three fishermen who threw back the same unwanted catch. Dominic had listened to them for five years. He knew their moods the way other men knew weather.

And for six days now, they had been circling the drowned shrine at the harbor's western lip and refusing to land.

He told himself it meant nothing. He was very good at telling himself things meant nothing; he'd had practice. Five years ago, when the gods went silent and the Inquisition's fires lit the hills above Vellamare like a second, crueler dawn, he had learned to fold his gift up small and tuck it away beneath the trade of harmless charms — knotted red thread against headaches, salt in a sachet for sweet dreams, river-stones painted with eyes to watch over sleeping children. Nothing that worked. Nothing that *answered*. The Inquisitors could burn a man for speaking to beasts, but no law yet written forbade a charm-seller from being merely useless.

So on the seventh morning, when a herring gull dropped onto his stall's awning and said — in that flat, unlovely voice gulls used, all consonant and complaint — *"Not right, not right, the water talks,"* Dominic did what any sensible man would do.

He ignored it.

The gull stayed. It tilted its head with one yellow eye fixed on him, and repeated, patient as a debt collector: *"The water talks. The water answers. Come see."*

"Charm?" Dominic offered instead, holding up a knotted thread between two fingers. "For the throat. You sound hoarse."

*"Come see."*

"I don't go near the shrine."

*"Come see."*

Around them the night market was folding itself away — this was the hour Dominic loved best and trusted least, the gray seam between night and morning when the lanterns guttered and the honest people hadn't yet arrived and the market belonged to strays and sellers of small mercies. A rat crossed the cobbles behind his stall without hurrying, which rats only did when they wanted him to notice. It paused, sat up, and said in its dry whisper of a voice: *"They're all saying it. Down by the old lady's house. The water is talking back."*

*The old lady's house.* The drowned shrine. Every creature in Vellamare called it that, though none of them could say why — some memory older than the city itself, perhaps, left over from whatever the place had been before the sea took it. Dominic had avoided it for five years the way one avoids a grave one helped dig.

He looked at the gull. He looked at the rat. Both looked back with the terrible patience of animals who had never once needed to lie.

"I'm closed," he said, to no one, to everyone, and began packing his charms into their battered case with hands that were not quite steady.

* * *

He lasted until noon.

It wasn't courage that broke him. Courage would have been cleaner. It was the dog.

She came limping up the Salt Stair around midday while he sat in the doorway of his rented room eating bread that had gone hard at the crusts, and she was a mongrel of no particular beauty — ribs like a washboard, one ear folded, coat the color of wet sand — and she stopped three paces from him and sat down with the deliberate formality of a petitioner at a magistrate's door.

Dominic put down his bread. "No," he said.

*"You haven't heard what I want,"* she said. Her voice was low and rough, a voice that had been shouting across harbors for years.

"You want food. They always want food first."

*"Food after,"* she corrected. *"First you come to the water. First you hear it yourself. Then food, and I'll take bread, I'm not proud."*

"The water isn't talking."

*"The water is talking,"* she said, and there was something underneath her gruffness then, something that made the fur along his arms rise. *"It's talking to Maro the fisherman. Every morning he goes out and every evening he comes back and every day he asks the tide where his wife went, and today—"* She stopped. Dogs were honest creatures but even honesty needed breath. *"Today it answered him. Out loud. In her voice. And he fell down in the shallows and wept, and the crabs came out to look at him, and we are all frightened, charm-man. We are all so frightened. Because prayers aren't supposed to be answered anymore. That's the rule. That's been the rule since the sky went quiet. And rules that break once—"*

"—break twice," Dominic finished, barely above a whisper.

The dog's folded ear twitched. *"You know the rule."*

"Everyone knows the rule."

*"But you know why the rule exists."*

The bread had turned to stone in his hand. Below them, the harbor glittered with the innocent cruelty of beautiful things, blue and gold and utterly silent, as it had been silent for five years, as silence was supposed to be forever.

"I sell charms," Dominic said. "Thread and salt and painted stones. That's all I am."

The dog looked at him for a long moment with those amber, unhurried eyes, and then she stood, shook herself, and said: *"I'll wait for you at the Salt Stair until sundown. Bread after. Or not."* And she limped away down the steps toward the water, leaving him alone with the sound of his own heart, which had begun, treacherously, to hope.

* * *

He went at dusk, because of course he went. Hope was the one hunger he'd never learned to ration.

The path to the drowned shrine wound down through the warren of Netmender's Row, past houses leaning on each other like drunkards, past drying racks hung with fish that gleamed like coins in the last light. The people here were poor in the way that made them generous — they nodded to him as he passed, and one old woman pressed a dried fig into his hand and said, "For luck, charm-man," not knowing that luck was precisely the thing he feared.

The shrine revealed itself gradually, the way wrecks do. First the smell: brine and rot and something sweeter underneath, something floral and ancient, like flowers drowned long ago still trying to perfume the water. Then the stones — a semicircle of weathered pillars rising from the shallows, carved once with figures whose faces the sea had licked smooth. Then the water itself, pooled inside the broken ring, green-black and unnaturally still, holding the darkening sky the way a bowl holds wine.

The gulls would not cross over it. They circled high above, screaming their one flat note — *not right, not right, not right* — and would not come down.

Dominic stood at the edge of the stones and felt five years of careful silence pressing against the inside of his chest like a held breath.

Because he remembered this place. Not from stories. From *before*. He had been small — how small he couldn't say, the years before his twelfth birthday being a fog he'd stopped trying to part long ago — but he remembered hands, broad and warm, lifting him onto one of these pillars. He remembered a voice saying words in the old tongue, the beast-tongue, the language that lived below language. He remembered —

Nothing. There it ended, as it always ended, the memory shearing off clean as a cut rope. His childhood before the age of twelve was a door painted on a wall. Everyone assumed grief had closed it. No one — least of all Dominic — had ever dared ask what might be standing on the other side.

*"You came."*

The dog was waiting at the base of the stairs, true to her word, and beside her sat half of Netmender's Row's animal population: three cats arranged with feline disdain, a one-eyed crab making slow circles, a knot of rats who kept their distance but did not leave. All of them watching the water. None of them willing to touch it.

"Where's the fisherman?" Dominic asked.

*"Gone home. His neighbors carried him. He'll drink tonight and sleep like the dead and wake tomorrow believing he dreamed it."* The dog's ears flattened. *"Animals don't get to believe things. We heard it. We have to live knowing."*

"What did it say? Exactly. Word for word."

The cats exchanged a glance — cats could do that, a whole conversation in a slant of whiskers — and it was a scarred tabby who answered, in a voice like gravel poured slowly: *"The man knelt in the shallows and said, 'Liss, Liss, if there's anything left of you, tell me where they took your body.' And the water said—"*

The tabby stopped. Even reciting it, secondhand, seemed to cost the cat something.

*"The water said: 'You buried me under the fig tree, my love. You cried so hard you planted me too deep. Come dig me up and let me rest.'"*

The wind came off the harbor and smelled, faintly, impossibly, of figs.

Dominic's knees informed him, quietly, that they would prefer to stop working. He sat down on a pillar's fallen stump and stared at the black-green water and thought: *this is a trick. This is a remnant, a haunting, a thing wearing a dead woman's voice like a stolen coat.* And underneath that thought, weaker but persistent as a heartbeat: *but she knew about the fig tree. How could anything know about the fig tree unless—*

Unless the dead really did remember. Unless everything the gods had swallowed when they went silent — every prayer, every grief, every whispered name over every grave — had been kept somewhere. Stored. Like grain in a silo. Like coin in a vault.

Like food.

"Go home," he said aloud, to the animals, and his voice cracked in the middle of it. "All of you. Go home and don't come back here. If the water starts talking to *you*, don't answer. Promise me. Every word spoken to a beast is a promise — " He stopped himself, teeth clicking shut on the rest of the sentence, on the old teaching he had no right to know and no memory of learning.

The animals looked at him strangely. Even the crab paused in its circling.

*"Finish it,"* the tabby said softly.

"No."

*"Finish the rule, charm-man. You know it. I can smell that you know it."*

"Every word spoken to a beast is a promise," Dominic said, each word dragged out of him like a nail from wood, "*and every promise made in the old tongue binds the maker.* Now go. Please. Go."

They went — flowing away up the stairs and along the seawall in a silent exodus, all except the dog, who looked back once with an expression he refused to interpret, and then the shore was empty and the light was gone and it was only Dominic and the drowned shrine and the water that talked.

He should have left too. He wants it recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the universe keeps of foolish men: *he knew he should have left.*

Instead he knelt at the water's edge, the way the fisherman had knelt, and he did not pray — he had spent five years not praying and was good at it — but he listened. And the listening itself was the sin, was the crack in the dam, because listening was half of the old gift and the gift had never actually gone anywhere. It had only ever been starved.

The water breathed. In, out. In, out. The rhythm of something asleep, or pretending to be.

And then, so gently it might have been the lap of tide against stone:

*"Hello, little speaker."*

His whole body went cold. Not fear — or not only fear. It was the cold of a name being spoken that no living person knew. *Speaker.* Not charm-seller, not Almeida, not boy. Speaker. The word the beasts used, the word from the old tongue, the word that belonged to an order of men and women who had all been burned or scattered or silenced five years ago, an order he had never belonged to, could not have belonged to, because he was nobody, he sold thread and salt—

"We buried you," the water murmured, and the voice shifted as it spoke, sliding through registers — a woman, a man, a child, something vast and patient underneath them all like a reef beneath waves. "All of you. We kept every word. We kept the prayers you whispered into pillows and the curses you spat at the sea and the names you spoke over graves. We were hungry and you fed us for a thousand years without knowing. And now we are awake, little speaker, and we remember *everything* — "

"Stop." His voice came out small.

*"— everything except what we ate to wake. Do you want to know what we ate, little speaker? Shall I tell you whose memories opened our eyes? Shall I tell you about a woman with ink-stained fingers who sang to gulls, who taught her son the old tongue before he could write his own name, who walked into the sea one winter night and — "*

"*Stop!*"

The word tore out of him in the old tongue — he didn't decide to speak it, it simply came, rising from whatever locked room his childhood had been shut in — and the water flinched. Actually flinched, a ripple running backward from the shore, and for one instant the surface showed him a reflection that was not his own: a shape of drowned grandeur, crowned in weed and bone, many-eyed, *smiling.*

Then the tide slapped flat, ordinary, innocent, and the voice was gone, and Dominic was on his feet and running, up the Salt Stair two steps at a time, through Netmender's Row where lamps were being lit against the dark, past the fig tree — *the fig tree* — heavy with fruit in a courtyard where an old woman waved at him and he did not wave back, running, running, lungs burning, until the shrine was far behind him and he stood gasping in the maze of the upper city with no memory of choosing any of the turns.

That was when he heard the baying.

It came from the direction of the harbor wall — a deep, rhythmic cry, disciplined, nothing like a stray's lonely song. Dominic knew that sound. Everyone in Vellamare knew that sound. The Inquisition's hounds did not bark the way dogs barked; they *announced*, and what they announced was found.

He pressed himself into the shadow of a doorway as the sound drew closer, and told himself: *they hunt smugglers at night, it's routine, it has nothing to do with—*

The hound rounded the corner at the end of the lane and stopped.

It was a massive animal, black and tan, bred from lines that had once — Dominic understood this with sick, sudden clarity — been *speaker-hounds*, dogs trained in the old days to recognize the gift, to scent the old tongue on a person's breath. The church had kept breeding them after the burning, though the gift was supposed to be extinct. Though *he* was supposed to be impossible.

The hound turned its head. Its nostrils flared. And across forty feet of darkening street, it looked directly at Dominic — not at his shadow, not at his movement, but at *him*, at the place behind his eyes where five years of silence had just torn wide open — and it bayed again, one long note of absolute certainty, and from somewhere beyond the corner a human voice answered:

"Hold. What have you found?"

Boots on stone. Lantern light swinging. Dominic ran.

He ran the way he had run once before, five years ago, though he could not remember the first time — only that his body remembered, that his feet knew these rooftops and drainpipes and gaps between walls the way a river knows its bed. Behind him the baying swelled and multiplied; there were more hounds now, and voices, and the clang of the watch-bell at Harbor Gate. Ahead of him the night lay open and full of shadows, and every shadow seemed to whisper in a drowned woman's voice: *we remember everything.*

He did not stop running until he reached his room, barred the door, and slid down against it with his case of harmless charms clutched to his chest like a child's toy.

On the floor by the window, someone had left bread. A whole loaf, still warm, and beside it a single line drawn in the dust by what looked very much like a clawed paw:

*You owe me. And now they owe you. Sleep, charm-man. Tomorrow the water remembers more.*

Outside, far off, the hounds were still singing.
