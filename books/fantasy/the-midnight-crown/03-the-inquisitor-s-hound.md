## 3. The Inquisitor's Hound

Seraphine Vale had learned to read lies the way sailors read weather: not by looking at the sky but at the water beneath it.

The gift had come to her late and unwelcome, on the morning of her ordination, when the Inquisition's master of hounds pressed a leash into her hands and something in the animal's amber eyes looked back at her with a fluency no dog should possess. *Bound*, they called it — a sanctioned beast-speaker, one of perhaps three left in all Vellamare whose gift the Church had found useful enough to chain rather than burn. The hound's name was Orthrus, though he answered to nothing so kind. He was her instrument, her warrant, and her only companion, and through him she heard the world the way the old heretics once had: every creature a witness, every silence an accusation.

She did not like it. She had never liked it. But she was very good at it.

The charm seller sat across from her in the interview room beneath the Bastion of the Vigilant Flame, his wrists loose in iron rings bolted to the table, his posture suggesting a man who had been inconvenienced by weather. He was younger than she expected. Thin, dark-haired, with the calloused hands of someone who worked small things — wire, bone, thread — and eyes that were doing their best to be unremarkable and failing at it, because there was too much attention behind them. Unremarkable men did not watch the door. Unremarkable men did not count the steps between themselves and the window.

"Dominic Almeida," she said, reading from the docket. "Charm-seller, Lantern Row market. You were named by a fisherman named Aldous Perrin, who swears he saw you speaking with a gull on the seawall two nights past."

"I talk to birds all the time." His voice was mild. Almost bored. "Everyone does. You whistle, they come down, you give them bread. It's a market habit. Gulls follow anyone holding a sardine."

"That isn't what he said."

"No," Dominic agreed pleasantly. "It isn't."

Orthrus lay at Seraphine's feet, chin on his paws, radiating the studied indifference of a soldier off duty. But she felt him listening. She always felt it — a faint pressure behind her sternum, like a second heartbeat tuned to a different rhythm, and when the hound attended to something, the pressure sharpened into a wire drawn taut between them. Right now the wire hummed. The man interested him. That alone would have been enough for most inquisitors.

Most inquisitors did not have what she had.

Because here was the secret the Church buried with every bound speaker it retired: the gift ran both directions along the leash. When Seraphine questioned a suspect, she did not simply listen to their words. She listened to how the words landed in Orthrus — how they moved through the animal's hearing, which was older and stranger than hers, tuned to frequencies of fear and intent that human ears had traded away ten thousand years ago. A lie, spoken aloud in a room with a beast-speaker's hound, made a sound. Not in the air. In the *animal*. A wrong note, struck against a chord the creature already knew to be true.

She had sent eleven people to the pyre on that note. She remembered each of their faces. She told herself the remembering was penance. Some nights she suspected it was inventory.

"Tell me about your trade," she said. "Charms. What manner of charms?"

"Harmless ones." He turned his wrists slightly; the irons clicked. "Luck-knots for fishermen. Dried lavender against nightmares. Little woven birds — ornaments, nothing more. I'm a craftsman, Inquisitor, not a witch. If weaving a bird out of reeds were sorcery, half the children in Vellamare would be burning."

"And the luck-knots? Do they work?"

A pause. Small, precise, like a coin slipped into a pocket. "They work the way luck works. Sometimes."

*There.* The wire between her and Orthrus sang — not the full discord of a lie, but a vibration near it, a note held deliberately flat. He was telling her something true wrapped around something he refused to say. She had interrogated smugglers who hid contraband inside bolts of honest cloth. This was the same trick performed with words.

"You were seen again last night," she continued, unfolding a paper she had no intention of letting him see clearly. "Near the drowned shrine below the Widow's Stair. The tide pools there. A woman gathering kelp saw a man crouched at the waterline, very still, for the better part of an hour. She thought he might be dead. Then, she says, he stood up suddenly and ran as if the sea itself were chasing him."

"The tide comes in fast around those rocks," Dominic said. "Anyone would run."

"She also says you were talking. To the water."

"People mutter. Pray. Talk to dead relatives. Half this city talks to the sea; it's the only listener most of them can afford."

Orthrus's head came up an inch. The wire pulled tight.

And Seraphine understood, in that moment, that she was not going to get what she wanted by asking questions — because the man across from her was not lying to her. He was *withholding*, and withholding rang differently. A lie was a broken string. Withholding was a string never plucked at all, and no hound alive could sound a silence.

So she changed instruments.

* * *

"Take off your gloves," she said.

Dominic blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"Your gloves. You're wearing them indoors, in summer, in an interrogation cell. Either you have scars you'd rather I didn't catalogue, or you think they hide something. Take them off."

For the first time since the interview began, something moved behind his face — quick, quickly mastered, but she caught it. Fear, or its cousin. He worked the gloves off finger by finger, unhurried, and laid them on the table like a man surrendering weapons he knew were useless.

His hands were ordinary. Knuckles nicked from wire-work, a burn scar on the left thumb, ink stains in the creases. Nothing damning. She hadn't expected anything damning. That wasn't why she'd asked.

She asked because of what happened next, which was this: she reached down and unclipped Orthrus's lead.

"Inquisitor—" said Brother Cassian, her shadow-clerk, from the corner where he took notes in a hand like spider silk. Protocol required the hound leashed during interviews. Protocol existed because the last inquisitor to work an unleashed hound in a closed room had been a woman named Odile Ferran, and what happened in that room had been scrubbed from the records so thoroughly that even Seraphine knew only fragments: screaming, blood, a door broken outward, and Odile retired to a quiet house in the hill country where she was said to sit in her garden for hours, motionless, watching sparrows.

"Protocol is for suspects we intend to charge," Seraphine said. "I haven't decided yet."

Orthrus rose. He crossed the room without hurry, nails clicking on stone, and put himself in front of Dominic Almeida, and sat, and looked up at him.

The room went very quiet. Even Cassian's pen stopped.

This was the thing the Church did not advertise about bound speakers, the thing Seraphine had discovered in her first year and never reported, because reporting it would mean explaining how she knew, and how she knew could not be explained without confessing the direction the gift truly ran. A hound trained to detect heresy did not merely listen for the wrong note in a suspect's voice. When ordered to attend, it opened — the way a flower opens, the way a lock opens — and everything the suspect carried came pouring into the space between them. Guilt. Terror. Longing. And, if the suspect had the old blood in them, if somewhere back through the generations a grandmother had whispered to cats or a grandfather had argued with horses, then the hound *recognized* it. Recognition had a smell. Orthrus's hackles rose for it the way they rose for wolf-blood in a sheepdog's line.

He was smelling it now. She watched his nostrils flare. Watched the fine tremor start in his shoulders and travel down his spine, the tremor she had learned to read as surely as scripture: *kin. Old kin. Hidden kin.*

"Well?" she said softly, to the hound, to the man, to whatever passed between them. "Do you want to tell me what he is?"

"He's a charm seller," Dominic said. His voice had not changed at all. That was its own kind of answer. Men who were innocent got angry when a dog was set on them; men who were guilty bargained. Only men with something enormous to protect went perfectly, glacially calm, the way deep water goes calm over a drowning.

Orthrus whined. Once, low, a sound she had heard him make exactly twice before — once at a dying priest whose faith had been genuine, and once at a heretic child of six whom Seraphine had refused to send to the pyre and been reprimanded for refusing. It was the sound he made, she had come to believe, at things that hurt him to understand.

She clipped the lead back on. "Thank you," she said, to the hound, and meant it in a way she never meant her prayers.

* * *

She dismissed Cassian. He protested — protocol again, the record must be witnessed — and she looked at him until he gathered his papers and left, because there were conversations that could not survive being written down, and she had learned to know them by their taste in the mouth.

Then it was just the two of them, and the hound, and the lamp burning whale oil that smelled faintly of the sea it had been rendered from, and the silence that follows when both parties in a room admit, without saying so, that they are playing the same game.

"Here is what I know," Seraphine said, sitting down across from him properly now, folding her hands. "Not what I suspect. What I know. I know that five years ago the gods went silent — every oracle dry, every augury blank, every prayer falling upward into nothing. The Church called it the Quiet and preached that it was a test of faith. I wrote the homily myself, one of them. I believed it." Her hands stayed folded. Her voice stayed level. "I know that three weeks ago, the Quiet ended. Prayers are being answered again. And I know that the answers are coming back *wrong*."

Dominic's stillness cracked — barely, a hairline fracture around the eyes. "Wrong," he repeated.

"A widow in Saltrow prayed for her son's fever to break. It broke. She woke the next morning and could not remember his face. Not forgotten — *taken*, she said, like a tooth pulled clean. A chandler prayed for prosperity and received it, and lost the name of every street in the quarter where he'd lived forty years. The pattern holds, charm seller. Something is answering us. Something is taking payment. And the payment is always memory." She leaned forward. "Now. Two nights ago, a gull spoke to you on the seawall — don't bother denying it, Aldous Perrin heard it plain, and Perrin is a drunk but he is a drunk with excellent hearing. Last night you went to the drowned shrine and spoke to the tide, and whatever answered you frightened you badly enough to run. So before you decide what to tell me, consider: I am the only person in the Inquisition who believes the answers are real. My superiors think the Quiet never ended, that these reports are hysteria and fraud. I am the only one hunting what actually hunts this city. Which makes me, whether you like it or not, the only ally you have."

"That's quite a speech," Dominic said.

"It's quite a city," she replied. "It deserves one."

He looked at her for a long moment. Behind him, Orthrus had settled again, but the wire between them still hummed, and through it Seraphine felt something she could not name — a current moving from the man to the hound and back, faint as a pulse under bandages. He was talking to the dog. She was almost certain of it. Not in words, or not only in words. In the old way. The way the texts described, the way the burned books described, the way her own gift arrived to her backwards and upside-down through a leash and a muzzle: *speaker to beast, beast to speaker, the covenant running underneath everything like groundwater.*

If he confirmed it, she would have to arrest him. That was the law. The law was the only structure standing between Vellamare and the fire it had barely survived a century ago, when the beast-speakers' order fell and the pyres burned for a season and the smoke smelled, witnesses swore, of feathers.

If he denied it, she would have to let him go.

She had decided this before the interview began, and the decision sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"So," Dominic said at last. "What happens to me?"

"That depends entirely on what you say next."

"And if I say nothing?"

"Then I write in my report that the charm seller Dominic Almeida was questioned regarding allegations of unauthorized commerce with beasts, that no evidence of sorcery was found, and that he was released without charge." She kept her eyes on his. "Which will be true. Because you will look at me, and you will deny everything, and my hound will hear no lie in it — because you are very good, Mr. Almeida, better than any I've questioned, at saying true things that conceal false ones. 'I talk to birds all the time.' 'People mutter at the sea.' Every word you've given me today has been technically honest and completely empty, and Orthrus cannot ring a bell that was never struck. So. Deny it. Go home. Sleep in your own bed."

"Why would you do that?" His voice sharpened for the first time, suspicion cutting through the calm. "You've got enough to hold me. Suspicion alone gets a man a cell in this city. You're letting a confessed—"

"*Suspected*," she corrected, "—and because I don't want you in a cell." She stood, and the lamp threw her shadow long across the table, across his bound hands, across the gloves he still hadn't reclaimed. "I want you exactly where you are: free, frightened, and followed. You're going to go back to whoever taught you, or whatever speaks to you, or whatever door you think has opened — because something has opened, hasn't it, Mr. Almeida? Something old. And you're going to lead me to it, because you have no choice. You can't stop listening. I've watched your kind — the little I've been allowed to read of your kind — and I know that the gift is not a tool you pick up. It's a hunger. And right now, everything in this city that crawls and flies and swims is turning toward something hungry of its own, and you will be pulled along whether you wish it or not. All I have to do is wait, and walk quietly behind you, and let the sea bring us both to the same shore."

The room held its breath. Even the lamp flame seemed to lean.

"You're wrong about me," Dominic said quietly.

"Almost certainly," Seraphine agreed. "But I'm right about the sea."

She unlocked his irons herself. She watched him pull his gloves back on, finger by finger, unhurried even now, and she noticed — filed away, in the cold archive where she kept such things — that his hands shook only after the gloves were on, where no one could see.

At the door he stopped. "Your hound," he said. "Does he have a name?"

"Orthrus."

"Tell him—" He paused, and the pause had weight, and she felt the hair rise on her arms without knowing why. "Tell him I'm sorry. For the smell of fear. It wasn't for him."

Then he was gone, footsteps fading down the stone corridor, and Orthrus was looking up at her with an expression she had no doctrine for, and Brother Cassian was scratching his pen in the outer office, and somewhere above them Vellamare glittered in its evening haze like a city built on a sleeping thing.

Seraphine sat back down. She drew a fresh sheet of paper. She wrote: *Subject released. No evidence of sorcery found.* And beneath it, in the private shorthand she used for thoughts that could never be entered into any record: *He apologized to the hound. They always apologize to the hound. Grandmother spoke to cats. Follow him.*

Outside her window, on a rooftop across the yard, a single gull had landed, and it was looking directly at her window, and it did not leave for a very long time.
