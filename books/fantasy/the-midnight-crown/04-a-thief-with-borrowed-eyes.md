## 4. A Thief with Borrowed Eyes

Nix slept badly in the best of seasons, and this was not the best of seasons.

She slept in the loft of a rope-walk off Tanner's Lane, in a nest of stolen quilts that smelled of tallow and tar, beneath a roof patched with three different kinds of shingle and one flattened kettle. She slept alone because sleeping with people meant explaining things, and explaining things meant the workhouse or the pyre, depending on who was listening. And she slept — when sleep finally took her, dragging her down like a gull taking a fish — she slept in other bodies.

Not her own. Never her own.

That was the gift. That was the curse. The priests had a word for it — *theriomancy*, from the old tongue, *the sin of borrowed souls* — though Nix had learned the word only last winter, scratched into the margin of a burned book she'd fished out of a midden bin behind the Inquisitors' Hall. She'd read it by candlelight with her heart hammering and then fed the page to the flame, but the word stayed. Words always stayed.

When she dreamed, she was an animal. A cat crossing the rooftops of the Salt Quarter, feeling the warm breath of chimneys on her whiskers. A rat in the granary walls, hearing the heartbeat of the grain. A dog trotting beside a constable, smelling his fear under his confidence like rot under paint. She never chose which body. She woke exhausted, stiff in muscles she didn't have, sometimes tasting blood that belonged to something else's mouth. Once, when she was eleven, she'd spent a whole night as a pig being slaughtered, and she hadn't spoken for four days after, and no amount of anyone asking what was wrong could make her say.

So Nix did not sleep near people. Nix did not let herself be known. Nix stole, because stealing required no explanations, and she was very good at it — good enough to eat, good enough to keep the loft, good enough that the other thieves of Vellamare left her alone out of a superstitious respect they couldn't articulate. They said she had lucky hands. They were wrong about the luck, but not about the hands.

And on the night everything changed, Nix went to the night market on Gull Street with her sleeves full of razor-edged patience, looking for a soft mark, and found instead the charm seller with the quiet hands.

* * *

She'd seen him before. Everyone had. He worked the corner near the fishmongers' row where the lantern light went amber and forgiving, a young man with dark hair going gray at the temples far too early, selling charms off a tray that hung from his neck by a leather strap. Knotted cords for safe travel. Little clay dogs for loyalty. Feathers bound with red thread for luck at sea. Nothing dangerous, nothing illegal — the Inquisition tolerated hedge-trinkets the way it tolerated beggars, with contempt and occasional sweeps. He had a voice like low tide, unhurried, and he never pushed a sale. That was the first thing that had snagged her attention, weeks ago: a seller who didn't sell.

The second thing was that animals liked him. Not the way animals like anyone with food — genuinely, deliberately. Gulls landed near his stall and stood there like clients waiting their turn. Rats crossed the open cobbles at his feet in daylight hours and he didn't flinch, didn't stamp, just shifted his boot aside with the courtesy of a man making room at a table. Once she'd watched him murmur something to a moth battering itself against his lantern, and the moth had settled on his knuckle like it had remembered something important elsewhere to be.

Nix noticed things like that. It was how she stayed alive.

Tonight the market was thick with fog rolling off the harbor, the kind that turned lanterns into haloes and made everyone's face half a stranger's. Perfect weather. Her fingers were already loose and ready, her mind doing its cold arithmetic — the fat merchant by the oyster stall, purse on the left hip, drunk enough to sway; the pair of clerks arguing over eel prices, eyes down, coats gaping. Easy meat. She drifted toward them, weaving, becoming part of the crowd's rhythm the way she'd taught herself years ago: don't move through people, move *with* them, be a drop in the current, invisible because you belong to the water.

And then she passed the charm seller's stall, and the world tilted.

Later she would try to describe what happened, and fail, because language was built for people who kept their own soul in their own skull. But in the moment it was simple: she looked at him, and something looked back. Not with his eyes. With a pressure behind her own, like a hand laid flat against the inside of her forehead. Like standing at the edge of a deep pool and feeling something enormous rise from the bottom to meet your reflection.

He glanced up from his tray. Their eyes met across three feet of fog.

"Oh," he said softly. Not to her. To himself. Or — she would wonder later — to something over her shoulder.

Nix did the only thing her body knew how to do when the world went strange: she went to work. Her hand was moving before her fear caught up with it, sliding through the crowd's eddy, dipping into the pocket of his long coat where it hung open at the hip — sellers kept their takings low, everyone knew that — and lifting the little drawstring pouch there with a touch lighter than the fog itself. Then she turned, merged, flowed, gone. Three stalls down, around the eel cart, up the steps of the shuttered chandlery, over the wall, onto the roofs. Ten seconds of practiced flight. By the time her pulse slowed she was three streets away, sitting in the dark between two chimney stacks with her knees drawn up, the pouch warm in her palm.

It was heavier than a purse should be. And it was humming.

* * *

Not literally humming. She checked. She turned it upside down over her cupped hands and shook it, and what fell out was not coin.

A knucklebone, polished smooth as river glass. A bird's skull, tiny, its eye sockets filled with dried moss. A ring of keys so old the iron had gone orange and flaking. A child's wooden horse with one leg snapped. A scrap of sailcloth embroidered with a single letter — *M* — in thread that had been red once. Seven objects, each wrapped separately in cloth, each handled so many times the wrapping had worn soft as skin.

No money at all. No charms. Just — relics. The kind of things a man keeps when keeping them is the only way he can prove pieces of himself existed.

Nix sat in the dark with the loot in her lap and felt, for the first time in her adult life, like a burglar who had broken into a church. She told herself it was none of her business. She told herself a mark was a mark, that sentimentality was how thieves starved, that tomorrow she'd hawk whatever wasn't cursed to the rag-and-bone man on Cooper's Stair and buy herself a proper dinner and forget his face.

Then she fell asleep.

* * *

She was a cat. That was common enough — cats were frequent lodgings for her dreaming self, comfortable and quick, all whiskers and high places. She sat on a rooftop above the fishmongers' row, below her a stall hung with lanterns, and she understood immediately whose stall it was, because the charm seller was there, packing up his tray in the fog, and the cat-body she wore knew him the way a dog knows its street.

He spoke to the cat. To *her*.

"You again," he said, in that low-tide voice, crouching so their eyes were level. "You've been following me for weeks. I don't mind. But you should know I know." He reached out and scratched behind the cat's ears, and the sensation flooded up through the dream-body — the warmth of his fingers, the rough pad of his thumb, the purr that started without permission in the animal throat — and underneath it, threaded through it like wire through wax, came *him*.

Memories. His memories, pouring into her uninvited, unstoppable, the way her dreams always carried the knowledge of whatever creature she wore — except this was backwards, sideways, *wrong*. This was the animal's memory of the man, and the animal remembered everything.

She saw a boy kneeling in a barn, age seven maybe, whispering to a lame goose, and the goose rising on both legs. She saw a woman laughing in a kitchen, flour on her hands — his mother, some deep animal-certainty supplied the name even though the dream gave no words — and then she saw the woman's face go smooth and empty as an eggshell while men in gray robes led her away, and she saw the boy screaming and the boy's scream coming out as every dog in the village barking at once. She saw a door of black stone beneath green water, and something behind it turning over in its sleep like a whale. She saw five years of small kindnesses: a sparrow fed, a rat warned away from poison, a gull told *not today, friend, the harbor master's watching* — and under all of it, constant as tide, a loneliness so vast it had stopped feeling like loneliness and started feeling like architecture. A room he lived in. A room he'd built himself, stone by stone, out of silence.

And then the dream showed her the strangest thing of all: the animals' side of it. What it sounded like from outside. When Dominic Almeida spoke to a beast, the beast heard — Nix groped for the comparison and found it — bells. Bells underwater. A voice like the ones the old stories said the gods used to have, back when prayers were answered and nobody pretended otherwise.

The cat opened its mouth, and Nix, riding inside it, heard herself say — in the old tongue, the tongue from the burned book, syllables she didn't know and yet somehow understood —

*"You're one of the last. Why are you hiding? Who are we supposed to find now?"*

Dominic went very still. The fog moved between them. And in the dream, in the cat's sharp night-eyes, Nix watched him look at the animal speaking words no animal should speak, and watched him arrive — visibly, like a ship coming into harbor — at the truth.

"You're not a cat," he whispered.

She woke up on the rooftop with her heart slamming and the dawn gray at the edges of the sky, and the relic-pouch still clutched in her fist so hard her nails had left crescents in her palm.

* * *

She didn't pawn any of it. That was the decision, made somewhere in the cold hour before full light, before her rational mind could file its objections. She sat with the seven wrapped objects laid out in a row on the lead flashing and touched each one in turn, the way you'd touch the hand of someone sleeping, and she thought: *these aren't goods. These are a man's spine, taken apart.*

Because here was the thing Nix knew, the thing she'd never told another living soul: she understood what it was to have your insides live somewhere they shouldn't. Every morning she woke with sand in her teeth from a dog's dream of digging, or the vertigo of a bat's ceiling-world, or the salt ache of a herring's last mile, and every morning she lay there and thought *this is mine, this happened to me, and no one will ever know.* She had a whole life no witness could confirm. She was a country that issued no maps.

And Dominic Almeida walked through the fog every night selling knots and feathers to people who'd burn him if they knew, smiling his market smile, carrying his dead mother's embroidery in a pouch at his hip — and he was *alone* in exactly the way she was alone, except worse, because his solitude was a sentence handed down by the same church that would hand down hers.

She owed him. Not in coin. Deeper than coin.

Which was why, that evening, Nix did the second-stupidest thing of her life (the first, she would later learn, was getting involved with him at all): she went back to Gull Street and returned the theft.

* * *

He was there at his usual corner, lantern lit, tray arranged, and when she approached he saw her coming from twenty feet off — of course he did; the man had a flock of informants perched on every rigging in the harbor — and she watched him decide not to run. That interested her. Most marks either fled or postured. He simply watched her come, with the wary stillness of a man standing in a doorway when the storm arrives.

"I believe these are yours," Nix said, and held out the pouch.

He didn't take it at once. He looked at her — really looked, the way he'd looked at her across the fog the night before — and she felt it again, that pressure behind her eyes, that sense of deep water rising. Except this time she knew what it was. He was listening. Not to her voice. To what her voice *sat on top of*.

"Open it," she said. "Count them. Seven. I'm not shorting you."

"Why?" he asked quietly. "Thieves don't return."

"Thieves steal things worth keeping." She shoved the pouch at him until he took it. "There's nothing in there worth keeping. There's nothing worth anything. It's just—" She stopped. Careful. Careful. "It's junk. Sentimental junk."

"Mm." He weighed the pouch in his hand and did not open it. "My mother's needlework is in there. My brother's tooth. The key to a house that doesn't exist anymore." His voice didn't change register at all; it stayed level and mild, which was somehow more unnerving than anger. "Junk."

Heat crawled up Nix's neck. "Are you going to call the watch? Because if you are, do it quickly, the fog's lifting."

"No." He tucked the pouch away inside his coat, against his chest this time, not his hip. Then, after a moment: "You dreamed it. Didn't you. Last night. Through the cat."

The bottom dropped out of her stomach. Every instinct screamed *deny, run, vanish* — and her feet actually shifted, her weight already loading toward flight, but his eyes held hers and there was nothing in them but a terrible, gentle recognition, and her feet wouldn't obey.

"I don't know what you mean," she said, which fooled neither of them.

"The cat spoke to me." He said it plainly, factually, the way a man reports the weather. "In the old tongue. Cats don't speak the old tongue. People do — a rare few — and those people dream in fur and feathers, if the stories are true. I'd wondered, these past weeks, why the gulls kept pointing you out to me. Why the rats went silent whenever you passed. They weren't afraid of you." He almost smiled. It transformed his whole face and then vanished, like a fish surfacing. "They were *curious* about you. So am I."

"You're mistaken," Nix said, "and even if I weren't — which I am — you'd want to be very careful saying things like that out loud on Gull Street. The Inquisition pays children a copper a head for less."

"I know what they pay. I've buried friends who cost a copper." For a moment the mildness cracked and she saw down into the well of him: fury, old and banked and carefully rationed, the kind that keeps a person alive precisely by never being spent. Then it sealed over again. "I'm not reporting you. I'm thanking you. Those things in that pouch — most days I think I'm the only one left alive who knows what they're worth. You counted them. All seven." He paused. "Why?"

Because I watched your mother get taken, she didn't say. Because I've been inside your grief and it fits me like my own coat. Because when the cat spoke with your voice underneath it, for the first time since I was eleven years old and the pig died, I didn't feel like a monster wearing other creatures' skins. I felt like one half of something.

What she said was: "Maybe I'm just a bad thief."

"Maybe." He studied her a moment longer. Fog curled between them; somewhere down the row a fishwife was singing the counting song about the drowned bride, and a gull landed on the eel-cart awning and cocked one eye at the pair of them with unmistakable interest, like a chaperone.

"Dominic," he said finally, and extended his hand. "Dominic Almeida. Since we're apparently doing introductions with a confessed thief in a public market, which I'll likely regret."

"Nix." She didn't take the hand. Taking hands was a beginning, and beginnings got people burned. "Don't thank me again. Don't look for me. And Almeida—" she hesitated, hating herself, doing it anyway, "—that dream. The black door under the water. Is that real?"

Something moved behind his face like weather. "Why do you ask?"

"Because," Nix said slowly, "three nights ago I dreamed it too. Before I ever picked your pocket. I was a seal, down in the deep past the harbor bar, and there's a trench there that isn't on any chart, and something down in it is *awake* now. It wasn't awake before, Almeida. The fish have been avoiding that water for a week, and the crabs are moving inland in lines, like pilgrims. Whatever's behind that door of yours—" She swallowed. "It's not sleeping anymore. And it's hungry, and what it's hungry for, the fish won't say. Fish can't lie, you know. That's the horror of them. Whatever it wants, they can't even bring themselves to name it."

The lantern between them guttered in a wind that hadn't existed a moment before. Down the street, every gull on every ridgepole lifted off at once, all together, silent, wheeling once over the market like a drawn breath — and settled again, facing seaward, every single beak pointed toward the drowned shrine beyond the harbor wall.

Dominic Almeida had gone pale as bone.

"The shrine," he said. "The old one. Saint Maren's, the one the sea took." He wasn't talking to her anymore; she understood that; he was talking to the air, to the birds, to whoever was listening on the other side of the fog. "First the dog says the door is open. Now the seals. Something is answering prayers down there, and it isn't God."

Nix pulled her hood up. Everything in her said *leave, leave now, this man is a sinking ship and you are a good swimmer.* She had survived nineteen years in Vellamare by never boarding other people's wrecks.

"Last chance to walk away," Dominic said, and to her surprise he wasn't pleading. He was offering her the exit honestly, the way you hold a door. "Whatever you dreamed, forget it. Go back to stealing. Stay small. That's all any of us can do now."

Stay small. She'd been small her whole life. Small as a rat, small as a secret, small as a girl asleep in a borrowed body no one would ever miss.

"The fish are naming it," Nix said. "They just need someone who speaks their language. And I only know one of those, and he sells knots on Gull Street." She stuck out her hand this time — her choice, her terms — and his grip was warm and careful, like he was afraid she'd startle. "But understand me, Almeida. I'm not your ally. I'm your witness. There's a difference. Allies stay when it gets bad. Witnesses just write down what they saw."

"Then write fast," Dominic said, and turned to look at the harbor, where the fog was rolling back off the water like a curtain, revealing the drowned shrine's broken spire standing black above the low tide — and standing on the spire, impossibly, absurdly, a single white cat, looking directly at the two of them across all that distance and dark water, tail curled around its feet like a period at the end of a sentence.

"That's not one of ours," Nix said, her mouth going dry. "Almeida. That is *not one of ours.*"

The cat opened its mouth, and across the whole width of the harbor, thin as a wire, they both heard it begin to speak.
