## 5. The God That Answers Wrong

The fever came to Saltrow Street on a Tuesday, the way fevers always do — quietly, in the backs of throats and behind the eyes of children, arriving like a guest nobody remembered inviting.

Dominic heard about it before he saw it. The sparrows told him first, chattering from the eaves of the dye-house as he passed with his tray of charms: *hot nests, hot nests, the little ones burn.* Then a rat on the wharf-side confirmed it in its flat, practical way — rats were terrible gossips but excellent accountants of suffering — *nine dens sick, two cold already.* Two dead. Children, most likely.

By evening the whole street smelled of it: vinegar and boiled willow bark, tallow candles burned down to puddles, and under everything the sweetish smell of sickness that no amount of scrubbing ever lifted.

* * *

Mama Ilsa had kept the corner house at the end of Saltrow for forty years. She was not a witch — Vellamare had burned its last openly practicing witch before Dominic was born, and everyone agreed it had been a great shame and no one would ever speak of doing such a thing again while quietly making sure no one practiced anything worth burning for. Mama Ilsa was a *healer*, which was legal, because her art consisted of poultices and prayers, and prayers were only words until someone answered them.

For five years, no one had.

That was the arrangement of the world Dominic had grown up in: the gods had gone silent, magic had been outlawed, and the city had learned to live on charms and habit. His own gift — the whispering, the listening, the terrible fluency of beasts that lived in his blood like an inherited debt — he kept folded up small and hidden, shown only in bargains with gulls and gutter-rats who asked nothing worse than bread crusts and secrets.

But tonight, standing across the street from Mama Ilsa's lit windows, watching her carry another small body to her cot, Dominic felt the old silence pressing down like weather.

The stray dog sat beside him. It had followed him for three days now — a yellow-eyed, rag-eared thing that had come to him in the night market saying *the door is open*, and had not left since. Dominic still didn't know what door. He was beginning to be afraid he did.

"She'll pray again tonight," he said to the dog, low. "She prays every night. Five years of nothing."

The dog's ear twitched. *Maybe not nothing,* it said, in the wordless way dogs have, all scent and weight. *Maybe waiting.*

"That's what frightens me."

* * *

He should have gone home. He tells himself that later, in the years when he can still tell himself things. He should have taken his tray of tin-and-glass charms, his harmless lies, and walked back to his room above the chandler's shop, and let Saltrow Street suffer the ordinary way streets suffered, with medicine and mourning and time.

Instead he stayed, because the gulls came off the harbor at moonrise in a silence he had never heard from gulls before, wheeling over Mama Ilsa's roof without one cry, and the dog stood up, and every rat along the gutter-line went still at once, like notes held on a breath.

Inside, Mama Ilsa knelt by her cot with a boy's hand in hers — the ferryman's son, seven years old, burning at a heat that made the candle flames lean toward him as if drawn. And she prayed. Not the rote formulas of the temple, the polite memorized verses that were really just letters mailed to an empty house. She prayed the old way, the desperate way, the way people prayed before the silence: with her whole chest cracked open, offering whatever she had.

*"Whoever's listening," she said. "Anything. Anything at all. Take what you want. Just give him back."*

Dominic felt the words go out of her like heat out of an opened oven.

And something answered.

It didn't come as thunder or light. It came as a *leaning*, the whole night tilting toward the little house the way iron leans to a lodestone. The candle flames stood straight up, perfectly still, though the harbor wind was blowing hard enough to snap rigging three streets away. The gulls hung motionless in the air above the roof, dozens of them, wings spread, heads bowed.

And the boy breathed.

Dominic saw it happen — saw the fever break like a wave against a wall, saw sweat spring up cool on the child's forehead, saw color flood back into lips that had been gray as ash. Mama Ilsa made a sound he had never heard a human make, somewhere between a sob and a hymn, and pressed her face to the boy's hair.

Through the window, the night exhaled.

And then the street forgot.

* * *

It began gently, the way rot begins gently, inside the wood where no one looks.

The ferryman's wife came out onto her step to call her son home — and stopped, frowning at the empty air, because she could not remember his name. She knew she had one. She knew there was a boy. She stood there working her mouth like a woman chewing on a knot, and finally called out, *"You! Whichever of you is mine!"*

An old man who repaired nets sat down suddenly on his own doorstep and wept without knowing why, holding a wedding ring he could no longer attach to a face.

A woman in the doorway of the rope-walk turned to her neighbor and said, cheerfully, terribly, "Wasn't there a fire? I feel like there was a fire. Last year? Or —" and her brow creased, and smoothed, and the question simply fell out of her, dropped like something too heavy to carry.

Dominic stood in the middle of Saltrow Street and watched a year being eaten.

Not erased — he would understand the difference later, and wish he never had. Erased would have left clean edges, gaps, the honest absence of amnesia. This was *consumed*. The year was gone the way meat is gone from a bone: thoroughly, hungrily, licked smooth. People reached for the missing months and found their own minds rounded and polished where the memories used to be, so that they couldn't even feel the shape of the hole. They laughed at jokes whose setups had vanished. They greeted friends they'd quarreled with and couldn't say why the friendship had once been strained. A whole street full of people, subtly, permanently diminished, and not one of them knew enough to mourn.

Except the animals.

The animals knew. That was the worst of it. The rats poured out of the gutters and stood in the moonlight looking at the human doors with an expression Dominic had no human word for — grief, maybe, or the particular horror of creatures who live among giants and have just seen the giants shrink. A cat on a fencepost yowled at the sky, long and raw. And the gulls above Mama Ilsa's roof finally cried out, all together, one hoarse collective note, and broke apart into the dark.

The dog leaned against Dominic's leg. Its thought pressed up into him, heavy as a head resting on a knee:

*Paid.*

"Yes," Dominic said aloud, and his voice came out strange, flattened. "That was the price. She said take what you want." He swallowed. "It took a year. From everyone. Because —" and here the horror turned over, showed him its belly, "because one woman's grief wasn't enough. It needed more. It always needs more."

*Now you see,* the dog said. *Now you know why the door being open is not good news.*

* * *

He walked the street until dawn. He couldn't have said why; some instinct older than sense told him to witness it, to walk the length of Saltrow while it slept off its amputation, and look.

At the corner by the fish-press, a girl of maybe ten sat on a barrel, awake when every other child was asleep, crying silently. When Dominic stopped, she looked up with an expression far too old for her face.

"I lost my birthday," she said. "I know I had one. Everyone says I did. But I can't — it's like reaching into your pocket and finding the pocket's gone." She wiped her nose with her wrist. "The lady next door says the saint healed the ferryman's boy and we should all be grateful."

"What do you say?" Dominic asked.

She looked at him for a long moment. "I say saints don't take payment from children."

Out of the mouths of babes, his grandmother used to say — but his grandmother's face, when he reached for it, was oddly slippery, a fish in shallow water, and he stood very still in the dark and made himself not think about why.

Because that was the thing he had not yet let himself say, walking Saltrow's length, counting the emptied faces: five years ago, when the gods went silent, they hadn't gone anywhere. Something had happened on the other side of the door. Something had *happened to them*. And whatever had come back through the crack — answering Mama Ilsa, feeding on a street's remembered year — it wore the shape of answering, but it ate like a scavenger. The gods of Vellamare's old stories had demanded sacrifice, yes, obedience, devotion. But they had given something real in return. This thing gave a child's life and charged a hundred strangers for it, and called itself generous.

Power given is never free. Every charm-seller knows that; it's the first rule of the trade. You sell the pretty glass bead and you keep the string. But these — whatever these were — they sold the bead and took the string and the hand that held it and the memory of ever wanting a bead at all.

Dominic stood in the graying light at the end of Saltrow Street, his tray of harmless charms hanging dead and heavy from his hand, and admitted it to himself at last, in the plain speech he usually saved for gulls:

*They are eating us. Whatever answered her prayer, whatever is coming back through the open door — it eats memory the way we eat bread. And we are teaching it, prayer by prayer, that we will pay.*

The dog looked up at him. *What will you do?*

Dominic thought of his grandmother's face, just out of reach. Thought of the drowned shrine the gulls had led him to, weeks ago, where the tide had spoken in a dead woman's voice. Thought of the inquisitor's hound meeting his eyes across a market crowd, and the hound's handler, Seraphine Vale, letting him walk away — which was not mercy, whatever else it was.

"There was an order once," he said slowly. "Before the outlawry. Beast-speakers. My mother used to—" The sentence snagged, frayed, dissolved. He frowned at the place where it had been. "There were records. Archives. Someone would have written down what they knew about gods that answer wrong."

*Rats know where old paper sleeps,* the dog said. *Rats sleep everywhere.*

"Then tomorrow," Dominic said, "we bargain with rats."

Behind them, up the street, Mama Ilsa's window glowed warm and golden, and inside it a healed boy slept, and around the sleeping boy the whole street dreamed shallow, untroubled dreams, having paid without knowing, having forgotten even the bill.

Above the roofline, far out over the black water, something vast and patient turned its attention toward the city — and Dominic felt it pass over him like the shadow of a cloud, cold on his skin, and heard, faint as a tick of a clock, the sound of something licking its lips.

He tightened his grip on the dog's scruff, and went to find the last of the beast-speakers' books before the hunger found them first.
