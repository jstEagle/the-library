## 6. The Last Bestiary

### Dominic

The gull had led them north along the cliffs for three days before Nix finally asked the question I'd been dreading.

"So when you talk to it," she said, walking backward on the seawall with her hands jammed in her pockets like a girl who'd never learned to be afraid of edges, "what does it sound like? In your head. Is it all screechy? Does it have an accent?"

"It sounds like itself," I said. "Which is worse."

She laughed — the quick, startled laugh she gave away like a pickpocket giving back coins, only what she didn't want you to keep. We'd been walking together for two days since she'd returned my purse and my secrets in the same breathless hour, and I still didn't know which of us was more unnerved by the arrangement.

The gull — she called herself Skaal, and she was old, and her voice tasted of salt and iron the way old voices do — wheeled above us and screamed down: *There. Where the land remembers being holy and pretends it doesn't.*

I stopped. Below us, past a slope of gorse and broken slate, the cliff jutted out over the sea in a shelf of black rock, and on that shelf sat ruins. Not dramatic ones. No towers, no arches. Just low walls grown over with thrift and sea-pink, so humble they looked less like something built than like something the land had grown and then regretted.

"That's it?" Nix said, following my gaze. "That's your grand order of beast-speakers? Looks like a sheep shed."

"Most holiness does," I said, "from the outside."

*Most holiness does,* Skaal repeated from above, because gulls are thieves even of sentences, and I wondered how many generations of my order had heard their own wisdom mocked back at them by birds.

We climbed down. The wind came off the water hard enough to lean on. And as I crossed the first threshold — a gap in a wall worn smooth as a river stone, smooth by hands, hundreds of years of hands — I felt something I hadn't felt in five years, not since the gods went silent and the world went deaf to me except for its animals.

I felt listened to.

The stones themselves seemed to lean in. Not speaking — not like the beasts spoke — but *attending*. The way a room attends when you say the name of someone dead.

"Dominic." Nix had gone still beside me, one hand pressed flat against a wall. Her eyes had changed — gone wide and dark and somewhere else. "There's something under here. Under the ground. It's like... sleeping animals. Lots of them. But they're not animals."

"The archive," I said, though I don't know how I knew. Maybe the stones told me. Maybe inheritance works that way — knowledge waiting in the blood until you stand in the right place, like a lock waiting for a key it has never seen but shaped itself around anyway.

"Underground," she repeated. "Sleeping."

"Then let's not wake anything we can't put back down."

But of course, we woke everything. That is what my family line has always been best at.

* * *

### Nix

Here's what nobody tells you about seeing through other eyes: you stop trusting your own.

I'd been doing it since I was small — falling asleep in some rookery or stable or fish-cellar and waking up knowing things no girl should know. Which warehouses had rats in the walls (all of them) and which had cats worth bribing. Where the harbor watch drank after shift. What the dogs thought of the men who fed them, which was usually more honest than what the men thought of themselves.

It made me good at my trade. It also meant I never slept properly alone, because every night I went to bed a girl and woke up a whole city's worth of strays, and some mornings I couldn't remember which set of teeth were mine.

Dominic's memories had been different. When I'd robbed him and dreamed his dreams without asking, I hadn't seen through an animal's eyes. I'd seen through his. And now, standing in this drowned ruin with salt wind trying to peel my hair off my skull, I saw the place the way he would see it — reverent, grieving, hungry — and I hated that I could.

"There," he said. He'd found a slab in the courtyard floor, a flat stone maybe four feet across, carved all over with spirals and paw-prints and bird-tracks and — I crouched closer — claw-marks that weren't decorative. Real ones. Something big had stood on this stone once and left its opinion of it.

"It's a door," he said. His voice had gone strange. Quiet and certain at once, like a man reading his own handwriting from long ago. "You need a speaker's hand. Blood, probably, knowing my lot."

"Your lot," I said. "You say that like you remember them."

"I don't remember them. I remember *around* them." His jaw worked. "My mother sang to the gulls. I know that the way you know a smell. I couldn't tell you her face anymore, but I know she sang, and I know the gulls went quiet for her, and I know that whatever our family did here, they did it together, and they did it last, and then everyone agreed to forget us."

He cut his palm on a knife he kept for exactly this kind of occasion, which told me something about how he'd spent the last five years, and pressed it to the spiral at the center of the stone.

Nothing happened for ten heartbeats. Then the ground exhaled.

I've been in collapsing buildings; I know what it sounds like when a structure gives up. This was the opposite. The whole ruin *drew breath*. Sand sifted downward instead of up. Gorse bent toward the center of the courtyard like flowers toward sun. And beneath our feet, those sleeping not-animal shapes I'd felt before all turned over at once, like dogs hearing their names, and a seam opened in the stone floor with a groan that went on so long it became almost musical.

Stairs. Going down into the dark, breathing out cold air that smelled of vellum and tallow and very old sea.

"After you," I said, because I'm a thief and thieves go second. Second can run.

* * *

### Dominic

The archive was a long hall cut into the cliff's root, and it had been ransacked — but carelessly, long ago, by people looking for treasure and finding only books. Shelves of black oak ran back into shadows beyond lamplight, and most of them were empty, tipped, shattered. But the deep shelves, the ones behind collapsed masonry, had been overlooked, and on those shelves waited the survivors.

Nix lit the lamps she'd stolen from me — I'd bought them for this trip without admitting why — and the light found spines. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Leather and vellum and bark-paper and one entire shelf of clay tablets wrapped in oilcloth like mummies.

"What is all this?"

"A bestiary," I said. My voice cracked on the word. "Not one book. A library of them. Every beast-speaker who ever lived wrote down what they learned — every animal's tongue, every covenant, every—" I pulled a volume free and opened it, and my hands were shaking badly enough that Nix noticed and pretended not to. The pages were covered in script I shouldn't have been able to read.

I read it.

*"Of the wren: her true-name has seven notes and the seventh is grief. She will trade the seventh for safe passage in winter, and she will keep the bargain, and do not ask this of her twice, for grief spent twice is grief doubled."*

"I can read it," I whispered.

"Yeah," Nix said softly. "Your face says you can." She was watching me the way she watched locks — assessing, deciding whether to pick or walk away. "Dominic. What are we actually looking for?"

And there it was. The thing I'd been circling for three days of cliff-path and two nights of bad sleep and one conversation with Skaal that had rearranged my understanding of the world. The gulls remembered the shrine. The dog had begged me about an open door. The healer's street had lost a year of itself to something that answered prayers. Somewhere under the sea-shrine where a dead woman spoke through the tide, there was a gate, and gates open both ways, and if the old gods had come back wrong — hungry, memory-eating, wearing divinity like a stolen coat — then someone had to close what had been opened.

"You remember what the rat-king told me," I said. "Before the market. Before any of this started."

"'The door is open,'" Nix quoted. "From the dog, too."

"The dog said the door was open. The rat-kings said the door had *keepers*, once. An order whose whole purpose was standing between the worlds and holding the hinge shut." I looked down the long dark hall, at all those surviving books. "I think this is where they kept the instructions."

We worked until the lamps burned low. Nix was faster than me — she read the way she picked pockets, quick and sideways, grabbing the useful thing — and I was slower and deeper, and between us we reconstructed it piece by piece from a dozen fragmented volumes: the covenant, the gate beneath the sea-shrine, the sealing.

The covenant was a promise, sworn in the time when gods still answered honestly. Humanity's speakers — beast-speakers, dream-speakers, tide-speakers, all the thousand gifts — had bound themselves as living hinges between the divine realm and the mortal one. The gods could pass only where a speaker permitted. The speakers' payment was service, vigilance, memory. Everything the gods gave, the covenant recorded; everything the covenant recorded, someone had to carry.

And the sealing — the closing of the gate, should it ever be forced open —

Nix read that passage aloud. She read it twice. The second time, her voice wasn't steady.

"*'Let it be known to all heirs of the hinge: the gate answers not to force nor faith but to payment freely given. To seal the way, a speaker must surrender to the threshold that which anchors them to this side. Not blood. Not life. Memory — the sum and substance of the self, held in trust by the gate until the worlds are one again or the world is done.'*" She lowered the page. "Held in trust. That means—"

"It means whoever seals it loses everything they are," I said. "Everything they remember being. Permanently. Or until such a day as the gate opens again, which is rather the opposite of the point."

Silence, in that underground hall, has texture. This silence had weight and dust in it.

"That's not hope," Nix said finally. "That's a price tag."

"Hope always has one. Most just hide it better."

She threw a scroll-end at my head. It missed, mostly.

* * *

### Nix

He took it too calmly. That's what scared me.

I watched him copy passages by lamplight — careful, patient, copying the old script into plain Vellamarese in a little ledger he'd brought for the purpose — and he copied the sealing rite the same way he copied the recipe for a luck-charm. Same neat hand. Same level voice. As if the words weren't saying *you will forget your mother's face forever* in grammar older than the city.

"You're planning to do it yourself," I said.

"I'm planning to understand it."

"That's not what your handwriting says."

He didn't look up. "What does my handwriting say, then?"

I crossed the hall, took the ledger out of his hands — he let me, which was either trust or exhaustion — and read his own marginal note back to him. He'd written it small, tucked against the margin like a secret:

*If it must be paid, I am the heir. The debt comes to me.*

"Dominic."

"It's arithmetic, Nix."

"It's suicide with extra steps." I heard my own voice crack and hated it. "You'd forget everything. Your mother. Your name. The market. Me, and we've known each other a week, so fine, cheap loss, but the others — Crow, when you find him — Seraphine Vale, who is hunting you and will probably end up saving you because that's how these stories go—"

"You've been dreaming my dreams again," he said, and there was no anger in it, which somehow made it worse.

"No. I've been paying attention. There's a difference, and the difference is consent." I shoved the ledger back at him. "Look. Whatever's under that shrine ate a whole street's year out of their heads. Whole year. Gone, like it never happened. You want to volunteer for that voluntarily? Forever?"

"Yes," he said simply. "If it stops them taking it involuntarily. Someone already paid, Nix. Everyone in that street paid, and got nothing for it, and don't even know what they lost. At least I'd get to choose."

I wanted to hit him. I wanted to shake him. Instead I did the thing I'm best at: I watched, and I waited, and I stole.

Because while he bent back over his copying, I drifted down the aisle to the shelf where he'd found the sealing text, and I looked at what lay behind it. One more volume. Small, thin, bound in gray leather gone soft as cloth. No title on the spine. When I opened it, the first page said:

*Bestiary of the Almeida Line.*

His name. His family. Entries in different hands going back centuries — a child's wobbly letters recording the speech of moths, a firm adult hand cataloguing whale-song, and then, near the end, a woman's graceful script that stopped mid-entry, unfinished, the ink browned with age:

*Of the nightjar: he asks nothing and gives nothing, but he listens better than any creature I have known, and when the silence fell he was the only one who stayed. If anyone finds this book —*

It stopped. Just stopped. No period, no flourish. A sentence interrupted the way a life gets interrupted.

I closed the book and held it against my chest and thought about a boy whose mother sang to gulls, and how he'd said *I couldn't tell you her face anymore,* and how he'd said it like a man describing weather, like it was ordinary, like losing her hadn't hollowed him out and filled the hollow with charm-sellers' patter.

Above us, faint through the cliff's bones, a gull cried. Skaal. Calling us, or warning us, or both.

"Nix." Dominic's head had come up. He was staring down the dark end of the hall, where the lamplight didn't reach, and his face had gone pale in a way I'd only seen once before — the night the healer's street forgot its year, and he'd understood what fed on us now. "Did you feel that?"

"Feel what?"

"The books." He rose slowly. "They just all... turned their pages at once. Like the building inhaling." His eyes found mine, and I saw the moment he spotted the gray volume clutched to my chest, and the moment he recognized the name on it, because his whole body went still as prey. "Where did you get that."

"It fell open," I lied, badly, and then, because lying to him felt like kicking a dog: "It's hers, isn't it. Your mother's."

He reached for it and I handed it over — I'm a thief, not a monster — and he held it the way you hold something that might be alive. And in the silence, from the far dark end of the archive where no lamp burned, something answered the gull's cry.

Not a gull. Not any animal I knew.

A voice, dry as old vellum, amused as a knife: *"Well. The Almeida whelp finally came home. And he brought a thief. How wonderfully traditional."*

Footsteps. Slow, uneven, human-shaped, coming out of the dark.

Dominic's mother's bestiary slipped from his hands, and neither of us moved to catch it.
