## 7. Brother Crow

The crow had been watching Dominic Almeida for eleven days before he decided the boy — no, not a boy, a man grown soft at the edges, thirty years and more, with salt already in his dark hair — was worth the trouble of hating properly.

Kestrel-that-was-Crow-now sat on the gutter of the tanner's roof where the smell kept honest folk away and the dishonest ones hurried, and he watched Dominic open his stall of harmless charms in the gray hour before the night market woke. The man talked to his wares. That was nothing; half the sellers in Vellamare muttered to their goods like they were children or creditors. But Kestrel heard what the others could not, because Kestrel had been made to listen for sixty years, first by an order that no longer existed and then by its absence, which shouted louder.

Dominic was speaking *under* his words. A second voice beneath the merchant patter, pitched in the old tongue, the tongue that had been beaten out of three generations and buried under the Inquisition's white stone. *Good morning, small ones. Nothing today but patience.* He was talking to the rats in the wall.

Kestrel's feathers rose along his neck, though crows do not have manes and cannot bristle like dogs, and so it only looked like discomfort, which was accurate.

*He's one of them,* said the starlings roosting under the eaves, who knew everything and valued none of it. *He's one of them and he doesn't know how much he is.*

*I know,* Kestrel told them, and hated how human his thought-shapes had gone after all these years. Exile did that. You spent enough decades thinking in the shapes of the people who threw you out and you began to dream in their grammar.

He should have flown away. He had flown away from every reminder of the order for twenty-two years — from the burned archive at Saltmere, from the drowned shrine, from the mountains where the last grandmaster had died alone and unshriven, calling crows with her final breath because there was no one else left to call. Kestrel had heard her die. He had been three miles away and he had heard her anyway, the way you hear a door close in a house you've left.

But the animals were all saying the same thing now, in a hundred voices across the city, and the thing they were saying was *the door is open*, and Kestrel was too old to pretend he hadn't heard it.

So he watched the charm seller. Eleven days. And on the twelfth, when Dominic closed his stall and walked home along the seawall with his collar up against the spray, Kestrel dropped off the gutter and landed on the railing two feet from the man's hand, and spoke to him in the old tongue, plainly, the way no animal had spoken to a beast-speaker in Vellamare in living memory.

*You promise carelessly.*

Dominic stopped walking. The sea went on without him, gray and patient. His face did the thing faces did — the flicker, the fear, the terrible hope, all of it moving under the skin like fish under ice. "I'm sorry," he said aloud, which was wrong, which was the first failure, answering a crow in the trade-tongue as if the crow were a madman and he a polite one.

*Wrong,* Kestrel snapped. *Again.*

"I—" Dominic glanced up and down the seawall. An old woman with a net passed them; she saw only a man standing very still beside a crow, which was common enough in a port city. When she was past he tried again, and this time his voice came out doubled, the way it did when he forgot himself: "*Forgive me. I don't— I learned from listening. No one taught me the manners.*"

*No,* Kestrel agreed. *No one did. That is the whole of your problem and the world's.*

He flew then, deliberately, a short insolent hop along the rail, making the man follow. Making him want to follow. This was the first test and it was cruel in the simplest way: Kestrel simply left, and let Dominic's hunger do the work.

The man followed. Of course he followed. They always followed. It was the following that had gotten the order killed, once upon a time — speakers chasing the sound of their own gift into white rooms with iron chairs. Kestrel led him anyway, through the fish-reeking lanes, up the cliff stair where the gulls screamed insults, to the ruined watchtower above the harbor that the city had forgotten twice over: once as a fort, once as anything at all.

Inside, among the pigeon droppings and the wind, Kestrel settled on a fallen beam and looked at the man with one flat black eye, and began to hurt him.

*Say your name in the old tongue.*

"I don't know my name in the old tongue."

*Then say something true. Anything. Say it to the sparrows in the rafters.* There were four of them up there, winter-thin, watching with the frank greed of their kind. *And mean it.*

Dominic looked at the sparrows a long moment. Then he said, softly, in the doubled voice, "*There is bread in my pocket. I was saving it, but you may have half.*"

Kestrel struck like a whip. *Liar. You have no bread. You bought no bread today. You gave your supper coins to the dog on the seawall Tuesday and have been eating nothing since but the kindness of the soup-wife on Tanner's Row. You promised bread you do not have, to birds who will now wait for it. Every word spoken to an animal is a promise. Do you understand? Not a wish. Not a figure of speech. A PROMISE. And you have just taught four starving sparrows that the man with the doubled voice breaks his word before he's even finished making it.*

The sparrows came down anyway, hopeful, and found nothing, and left one by one, and each departure was a small cold accusation. Dominic watched them go and Kestrel watched his face come apart — not dramatically, not weeping, but the slow structural failure of a man discovering the shape of a sin he'd been committing daily for years without knowing it had a name.

"How many promises have I broken?" Dominic asked quietly.

*Thousands,* Kestrel said. *Every day of your life. Every 'good morning' to the rats that meant nothing. Every comfort you gave some creature to make yourself feel less alone, that you never intended to keep. You have been spending trust you didn't know you were borrowing, from creditors who remember everything.* He let that sit. Crows were good at letting things sit. *That is why the gulls tell you secrets and forget them by morning. That is why the rat-kings bargain with you and laugh behind their whiskers. You are known in this city, beast-speaker, as a pleasant liar.*

"That's not—" Dominic's hands curled. "I never lied on purpose."

*No one does, at first.* And here the grief arrived, right on schedule, right on cue, the old grief that lived in Kestrel's crop like a stone he'd swallowed decades ago and never managed to pass. *My brother-speakers said the same. We had a word for it. We had lessons. We had a whole order built around the simple truth that a gift like ours is not a gift at all — it is a debt structure. You speak; you owe. And when the Inquisition came, they didn't need to torture us for confessions. Our own broken promises confessed for us. Every sparrow we'd charmed and forgotten. Every dying horse we'd soothed with 'you'll be well' when we knew it was a lie. The bound speakers in the white rooms sang our sins back to us in the voices of everything we'd ever failed.*

Dominic sat down on the wet stone. Just sat, abruptly, like a puppet with cut strings. The wind came through the empty windows and moved his hair.

"The archive," he said. "Nix and I found the archive. Under the salt-stairs. It said the covenant needs a speaker willing to pay in memory. It said—" He stopped. Started again. "It said there was a seal, and a price, and that the order kept bestiaries of everything that owed and was owed, and that the last of them—" He looked up at Kestrel, and there it was, the naked hope, the thing that made Kestrel want to peck his eyes out and also, shamefully, want to shelter under his wing, if crows still had wings in any sense that mattered. "You were one of them. You're the last one. Aren't you?"

*The last,* Kestrel agreed. *Which makes me either a treasure or a relic, depending on the century.*

"Teach me."

*No.*

"Please—"

*No.* He said it in the old tongue and in the trade-tongue both, so there could be no doubling, no ambiguity, no pleasant lie. *I will not teach you. Teaching you is how the last three of us died — believing that love and talent were the same as discipline. My grandmaster died alone on a mountain calling for crows. I am the crows. Do you understand what that means? I am what answered her. And I was not enough, and neither will you be, when the door opens all the way and the things on the other side start wearing faces you love.*

"The things are already here," Dominic said. "A god ate a street's memories. A year of them, whole. The healer prayed and the whole lane paid. You've heard the gulls—"

*I have heard the gulls. I have heard the rats, who are frightened in a way rats are never frightened — not of traps, not of owls, but of being *unremembered*. Something in the deep water is drinking the city, Almeida, sip by sip, prayer by prayer, and it has your face on its list of doors.* He hopped closer, deliberately menacing, deliberately close enough that the man could see the scar through his left eye, the one the Inquisitor's falcon had given him the day the order fell. *So here is what I will do instead of teaching. I will test you. Until you break or until you learn, whichever comes first. And if you break, you crawl home to your charms and your loneliness and you never speak the old tongue again. If you learn — then perhaps, perhaps, we talk about the gate.*

"What kind of tests?"

*Cruel ones,* Kestrel said, and found, to his own disgust, that some dried-out husk of him almost enjoyed saying it. *The kind that teach. Come back tomorrow at the gray hour. Bring nothing. Promise nothing. And Almeida — the dog that follows you now. The yellow one from the seawall. You told it you would keep it safe.*

Dominic went still. "Yes."

*Did you mean it?*

"Yes," he said, and his voice did not double this time. It was single and plain and absolutely certain, and it was the first true note Kestrel had heard from him in twelve days.

*Then that is your first lesson already passed, and you didn't even know you were sitting it. Keep that dog alive through what's coming, and you'll have learned more than my grandmaster's whole last class.* He spread his wings, black against the gray light. *Tomorrow. Gray hour. And bring bread — real bread — for the sparrows. Debts first. Always debts first.*

* * *

The tests were cruel. Kestrel had promised cruelty and he delivered it with the thoroughness of a man settling accounts.

He made Dominic sit motionless in the rain while a starving cat circled him crying, forbidden to speak a word of comfort until Kestrel gave leave — and the leave came only after the man had learned to give comfort without words, with warmth and stillness and shared silence, which was the older language, the one under the tongue. He made him bargain with the rat-kings of the sewer-cathedral and hold to terms that cost him — three secrets surrendered, one of them about Nix, given only with her prior leave, wrung from Dominic's pride like water from cloth. He made him stand at the tide-line and speak to the sea itself, which does not listen, which has never listened, until Dominic wept with the futility of it, and then Kestrel showed him the barnacles, the small listeners, the thousand tiny lives in the rock that had heard every word, and made him reckon with the fact that nothing he said ever fell on no ears at all.

*Everything hears,* Kestrel told him, evening after evening, in the ruined tower while the harbor lamps came on below like a spilled jewelry box. *That is the terror and the discipline of it. You are never unobserved. You are never unheard. The order fell because its speakers thought the gift was power. It was never power. It was witness. You are a witness, Almeida, sworn by every syllable, and the gods coming back through that door — they know it. They will offer you witnesses' comforts. Restored childhoods. Your mother's voice. And every comfort will be a hook, because they are hungry the way debt is hungry, and memory is the only coin they take.*

On the ninth evening of testing, Dominic brought the bread. Real bread, dark rye, broken carefully into the rafters for the four sparrows, who came down one by one and ate within reach of his hands, which sparrows do not do, which meant the ledger between them was mending. Kestrel watched from the beam and felt something in his ancient chest turn over, slow and painful, like a ship changing tack.

"You lost someone," Dominic said that night, not looking at him, feeding the sparrows. "Someone specific. That's why you test like this. Grief teaching discipline — you're not teaching me the old tongue. You're teaching me the shape of whatever mistake killed them."

The wind filled the tower. Below, the city murmured. Somewhere out past the harbor bar, in the drowned dark where the old gate slept, something vast turned over in its sleep and dreamed of names.

*Her name was Maren,* Kestrel said at last, because a debt acknowledged is a debt halved, and he had promised nothing carelessly for twenty-two years and would not begin tonight. *She was my speaker. She broke one promise. One, in forty years — she told a drowning sailor's wife that the sea would give him back, and the sea did, three days later, folded and wrong, and Maren never forgave herself, and when the Inquisition came for her she walked into the white room without running, because she believed she owed a death.* His voice, if a crow can be said to have a voice, went thin as wire. *I was outside the window. I heard everything. And I have spent two decades making sure no speaker of mine — no speaker at all — ever makes a promise they cannot carry.*

Dominic's hand stopped mid-offering. A sparrow took the crumb anyway, bold as tax.

"So teach me to carry them," Dominic said. "All the way to the gate."

Kestrel looked at him for a long time — the flat black eye, the scarred one, both of them, the whole corvid weight of sixty years pressed into a single regard. Then, far below, across the sleeping city, a scream went up from the direction of the night market. Firelight bloomed orange against the low clouds. Voices, many voices, and among them the particular disciplined barking of Inquisition hounds on a scent.

They had found the charm seller's stall.

*Well,* Kestrel said, rising off the beam with a crack of wings, *it seems the lesson schedule has accelerated. Run, Almeida. And mind what you promised the dog.*
