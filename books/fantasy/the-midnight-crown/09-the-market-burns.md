## 9. The Market Burns

The night market of Vellamare woke the way it always did — by smell before sound. Charcoal and cardamom first, then brine off the harbor, then the sweet rot of overripe figs that old Maretta refused to throw away because, she claimed, the flies deserved a festival too. Dominic moved through it with his tray of charms bumping his hip, counting stalls the way other men counted coins: forty-one sellers tonight, six lanterns unlit, one gull circling too low.

That last one bothered him.

*Too low,* he murmured under the noise of the crowd, a thread of sound only the bird could catch. *Why so low, white-wing?*

The gull banked, screamed once — not its usual greedy holler but something shorter, sharper, a syllable in the old tongue that Crow had spent three bitter months hammering into him — and wheeled away toward the harbor.

*Trouble,* the scream said. *Trouble from the water.*

Dominic stopped at his usual corner between the candle-seller and the man who fried sardines until the whole lane smelled like salt and smoke. He arranged his charms without looking at them: knotted red thread for luck, river-stones drilled and strung for safe passage, little clay whistles shaped like wrens that made mothers laugh and children shriek. Harmless things. Honest things, almost. No one buying a luck-knot from Dominic Almeida ever guessed that the same hands could ask a rat what the watchman ate for supper, or trade gossip with a cat for the price of a dried fish.

Five years of silence since the gods went quiet, and the city had learned to live without miracles. But the animals hadn't gone quiet. The animals never went quiet. And lately they'd been saying things that kept him awake past dawn — *the door is open*, the stray dog had told him, weeks ago now, eyes rolling white with fear. *The door is open and something is walking through.*

He was pretending to straighten his display when Nix dropped onto the edge of his stall as silently as a cat, which was fitting, because she'd been talking to cats again.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Thank you. You're radiant as ever."

She grinned. She was all sharp angles and quicker smiles, hair chopped ragged at her jaw, a smear of soot on her cheekbone she didn't know about. Seventeen years old and already the best thief in the Tidewalk district, though she'd swear on her mother's grave she'd retired from lifting purses and only lifted them recreationally now.

"The gulls are wrong," she said, quieter. "Or right. One of those." Her fingers drummed the stall's edge. "I dreamed through a dog's eyes this afternoon. Down by the shrine quarter. There were men in grey coming off a ship, and the dogs could smell iron and incense and—" her throat worked, "—burning feathers."

Inquisitors. Dominic's hands went still over his charms.

"They don't come to the market," he said. "They've never come to the market. It's beneath their dignity."

"Maybe you've offended their dignity." Nix's grin faded into something more careful. "Dom. If they're hunting what I think they're hunting—"

"Then I should have left Vellamare in the spring, and you should stop saying my name in the open air like an idiot."

"I say lots of things like an idiot. It's part of my charm."

But her hand found his wrist under the stall, brief and hard, and squeezed once. That was the whole language of it, with Nix. She'd robbed him half-blind eleven months ago, then dumped everything back in his lap shaking, babbling about dreaming his memories through the eyes of pigeons — his mother's kitchen, a door painted blue, a voice singing in a tongue that made animals sit down and listen. She hadn't stolen from him since. She'd stayed instead, and neither of them had ever said out loud why.

He was reaching for her hand again when the first lantern went dark.

Not blown out. *Snuffed* — a wet black absence where flame had been, and then the next one, and the next, a ripple of darkness running up the market street like a wave pulling back from shore. The crowd noise thinned. Somewhere a child started crying before anyone knew why, because children always knew first.

And then the drums. Inquisitorial march-drums, deep as a heartbeat heard through a wall, and torchlight pouring into both ends of the lane at once.

"Greycoats," Nix breathed. "In the market. Dom—"

"*Go.*"

"No." She was already moving, low along the stall fronts, quick as spilled water. "You go. I know every drain and cellar in Tidewalk. You'd get lost in your own pocket." She flashed him a look over her shoulder, fierce and bright-eyed. "Corner stall, behind Maretta's awning. Count two hundred. If I'm not there—"

"If you're not there I'm coming to find you."

"That," she said, "is why you need me to do the counting for both of us."

Then she was gone into the crowd, and Dominic did the hardest thing he'd done in five years of hiding: nothing. He stood at his stall among his harmless charms and let the tide of grey robes flow around him, and he counted.

*

The inquisitors did not search like men searching. They searched like weather. They came in a wall, torches high, and where the wall passed, the market simply ceased — stalls overturned, awnings slashed down, braziers kicked scattering coals into dry canvas. An old woman selling oranges stood with her arms full of her own scattered fruit while a grey-robed man ground it under his heel without looking at her.

At their center walked a figure Dominic knew even before his stomach clenched around the recognition. Inquisitor Seraphine Vale. Tall, silver-haired, her face carved from the same stone as the cathedral saints, her grey cloak marked at the shoulder with the sun-and-chain of the Holy Office. And beside her, moving at her knee like a shadow given teeth, her hound — a huge black beast with pale eyes that swept the crowd not the way a dog looks but the way a hunter reads tracks.

The hound's head turned. Found him. Held.

Every animal instinct Dominic owned screamed at once. He looked away first, heart slamming, and pretended to count coins. Behind him, he heard Vale's voice — level, unhurried, carrying the way a blade carries light.

"A beast-devil walks your market," she announced to the whole street. "It speaks with animals. It has spoken with them for years. Anyone who shelters it shares its crime. Anyone who names it shares our mercy."

*Mercy.* The word landed in the silence like a coin in an empty bucket.

A fishmonger — Paulo, who gave Dominic sardine tails for the gulls every single night — stepped forward, face grey in the torchlight. "There's no devil here, lady. Just poor folk selling what they've got."

"There is always a devil here," Vale said. "That is what markets are for." She raised one gloved hand, and the hound surged forward, nose to the cobbles, dragging its handler after it in a long arc through the crowd — toward the candle stall, the sardine fire, the corner where Dominic's own tray sat with its little clay wrens.

They would find him. Of course they would find him; the hound had already found him; it was playing with him now, or waiting for its mistress's signal—

Which was when the fire started.

It began small, as fires do: a coil of flame licking up the sardine-man's abandoned oil vat, quick and hungry, and then the wind shifted — no, the wind was *shifted*, Dominic realized, because Nix had been sleeping in stables and dove-cotes for a month learning which way the birds turned a storm — and the flame jumped the lane like a thought. Canvas caught. Rope caught. The night market, built of cloth and timber and twenty years of hoarded junk, lit up gold from end to end.

The crowd broke like a dam failing. Screaming, shoving, oranges rolling everywhere underfoot, and in the chaos Dominic saw her — saw Nix — standing on Maretta's overturned cart with a torch in each hand, feeding the fire deliberately, grinning like sin itself, buying him exactly the chaos she'd promised.

*Two hundred,* she mouthed. *You only got to ninety.*

Then a grey-robed hand closed around her ankle and dragged her off the cart into the torchlight, and the grin was gone, replaced by something Dominic had never seen on her face: fear, quickly buried, quickly mastered, but real.

"Nix!" His voice tore out of him before any caution could catch it.

Her head snapped toward him. For half a heartbeat their eyes met across the burning market — and she shook her head. Once. Sharp. *Don't.*

Then they bound her wrists and the crowd closed and she was gone, and the world narrowed to a roar of flame and blood in his ears.

*

He did not remember deciding to move. Later he would piece it together: that he had vaulted his own stall, that he had run three steps toward the greycoats, that some animal cunning older than his name had yanked him sideways into Maretta's cellar hatch before his mind caught up with his body. He crouched in the dark among pickled eels and dust, listening to the market burn above him, and the animals spoke to him whether he wanted them or not.

*Rat under the floorboards:* *Grey-men took the small fierce one east. East and down. Iron doors.*

*A pigeon rattling loose through the smoke:* *They hold her at the water-gate first. Chains. They wait for the tall one, the silver one, before they take her below.*

*Even the burning cats, streaming past him toward the harbor, paused to press their flanks against his ankles —* *run, speaker, run* — *and he could not.*

Nix, who had dreamed his memories without permission and given them back. Nix, who slept in hay with the horses because she trusted them more than landlords. Nix, seventeen years old, in chains at the water-gate, because she had set a fire with her own two hands to save a man who had done nothing for her except fail to leave the city when he should have.

Guilt is a strange fire. It burns colder than rage but longer. Dominic knelt in the dark under the ruined market with soot sifting down through the boards like black snow, and he felt it settle into his bones and make itself at home.

Above him, muffled by earth and timber, he heard Seraphine Vale give an order in a voice like a closing door: "Take her to the cells. Question nothing tonight — the Father Superior will want the beast-devil first. She is bait, not prey. Keep her breathing."

*Bait.* Of course. They hadn't caught Nix. They'd caught the thing they believed would bring him running.

The worst of it — the part that would keep him company for years — was that they were right.

*

The market burned until near dawn. Dominic watched it from the roof of the tannery, far enough away to be invisible, close enough to feel the heat push against his face like a living thing. Around him, the displaced animals gathered — cats with singed whiskers, dogs with ash-grey paws, rats in a chittering ribbon along the rooftop ridge, gulls wheeling overhead screaming their anger into the smoke. They came to him the way mourners come to a graveside, because he was the only one in the city who could hear what they'd lost.

Maretta's stall. Paulo's barrow. The candle-seller's stock of thirty years. All of it ash, and all of it ash because a girl with borrowed eyes had struck a match for him.

"We'll get her back," he said to them, to himself, to the smoke. His voice cracked in the middle like green wood. "Do you hear me? Whatever it costs. Whatever I have to pay. We'll get her back."

An old tomcat with one ear chewed to lace pressed against his leg and answered in the flat practical grammar of cats: *The grey-women's den has many mouths and few windows. You will need the small ones. The ones in the walls.*

The rats. He'd need the rats — the rat-kings of Vellamare, who remembered every tunnel dug beneath the city since before the gods went quiet, who traded in secrets the way merchants traded in spice, and whose prices were never paid in coin.

Behind him, wings beat the smoky air. A crow — not just any crow; *the* crow, black as a hole cut out of the night — landed on the ridgepole beside him and regarded the burning market with one cold eye.

"You taught me that every word to an animal is a promise," Dominic said, not looking at him. His fists were trembling. "I promised her I'd come find her if she didn't come back."

Brother Crow was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was gravel dragged over grief. "Then you had better learn what the rat-kings charge, heir. Because the Inquisition's cells are beneath a mountain of stone, and stone does not love our kind."

Far below, the last timbers of the market folded inward with a sigh like a dying breath, and sparks rose — hundreds of them, thousands, climbing the smoke column toward the indifferent stars.

Somewhere east and down, in the dark beneath the Inquisition, Nix was alive. That was the only prayer Dominic had left, and he wasn't sure anymore who was listening.

"East and down," he said to the crow, and the crow spread its wings against the firelight like a page being opened.
