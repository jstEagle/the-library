## 10. Bargaining with Rats

The rain had stopped an hour past midnight, but Vellamare still dripped from every eave and gutter like a city wrung out and left to dry. Dominic moved through the flooded alleys of the Salt Quarter with his collar up and his boots soaking through, and at his shoulder walked a crow the size of a small cat, who had not stopped complaining since they left the burned market.

"You smell of smoke and guilt," Crow said, his voice a dry rasp that only Dominic could hear — or rather, that only Dominic could *understand*, which was a different thing entirely. "Both are useful, in their way. Guilt especially. Guilt makes a man generous."

"I'm not in the mood for your philosophy."

"You're never in the mood. That's why I provide it free of charge." Crow hopped from a barrel to a windowsill, feathers slicked black as spilled ink. "The rats are already watching us, by the way. Have been since the tannery. Three of them. Bad ones — the kind that report to the Undercellar kings. Smile."

Dominic did not smile. He stopped in the middle of the alley, rainwater seeping cold around his ankles, and said, to the darkness beneath a collapsed drainpipe, "I know you're there. Tell your masters the charm-seller comes to trade, not to beg."

A long silence. Then a rat emerged — a big one, gray as wet slate, with a torn ear and eyes like two drops of tar. It sat up on its haunches and regarded him with the flat patience of a customs official.

*The charm-seller,* it said, in the chittering tongue that unfolded itself in Dominic's mind like a letter written in a hand he'd almost forgotten. *The charm-seller who let the girl be taken.*

The words went through him like a fishhook. He kept his face still. Five years of selling charms in the night market had taught him that much: the face is a shopfront, and the shopfront must never show the ledger.

"I didn't let anything happen," he said. "She pushed me out of the way."

*And you ran.* The rat's whiskers twitched. *The gulls saw. The gulls tell everyone.*

"The gulls are gossips."

"The gulls," Crow said dryly from the windowsill, "are *correct*, which is worse."

*The kings will hear your trade,* the rat said. *Bring your secrets to the Undercellar. Bring them before the third bell. After the third bell, the kings sleep, and sleeping kings bite first and listen never.* It turned, paused, and looked back over its shoulder with what Dominic could have sworn was contempt. *Bring something good, charm-seller. The kings are old, and old things are bored. Bored things are cruel.*

It vanished into the dark of the drain, and Dominic stood a moment in the dripping alley, feeling the hook turn in his chest. *The girl.* Nix. Two days in an Inquisition cell now, maybe three — time enough for the questions to become the other kind of questions. Time enough for a girl who saw through borrowed eyes to learn what inquisitors did with people who saw too much.

"You're doing the thing," Crow observed.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face goes to stone and your hands go to fists and you rehearse every way you failed her. I've seen it on better men. Well. On men, anyway." He ruffled his feathers, shedding a bead of water. "Save it for the kings. They buy grief at a fair price. It's the only honest currency left in this city."

* * *

The Undercellar lived beneath the old customs house, in a warren of flooded vaults that predated the fall of the gods — or so the rats claimed, and the rats were the only historians who had never once been paid to lie. Dominic went down the broken stair with a lantern he didn't light, because the rats saw well enough in the dark and they considered fire a vulgar habit of the surface world.

Crow rode his shoulder. He had insisted. "If they eat you," he'd said, "I want to see it coming."

The vault opened around them like the inside of a drowned bell. Water stood knee-deep on the floor, black and perfectly still, and above it the arched ceiling was hung with the roots of the city itself — foundations, pipes, the buried bones of Vellamare. And on every surface that rose above the waterline, the rats were waiting.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. They lined the arches like a congregation, they sat on submerged crates with only their heads above water, they hung from rusted chains in clusters that shifted and breathed. And in the center of the flooded floor, on a throne built from a broken statue — some merchant god whose face had been chiseled away in the purges, so that only the robes remained, stone drapery pooling around a headless torso — sat the kings.

There were three of them. Dominic had dealt with rat-kings before, in small ways, over small things: which warehouses had soft grain, which gutters ran to the sea. But those had been mayors. These were monarchs.

The first was vast, an old male gone silver at the muzzle, one eye clouded white, and he sat atop the statue's shoulders like a gargoyle come to judgment. The second was lean and quick-eyed, a female with a scarred spine, perched on the statue's severed neck. The third was the one that made Dominic's skin crawl: a rat no bigger than the others, but still as carved wood, with eyes so dark and deep that looking into them was like looking down a well and hearing, faintly, your own name called from the bottom.

*Charm-seller,* said the old king, and his voice filled the vault, or perhaps a thousand voices spoke at once and he was only the mouth of them. *You come to the Undercellar for the first time in five years. Five years of silence. Five years of hiding your tongue behind trinkets and dried lavender.* A ripple moved through the congregation — amusement, or hunger; with rats the two were often indistinguishable. *Now you want something. Now the surface world has finally frightened you.*

"I want to know where the Inquisition is keeping a prisoner," Dominic said. "A girl. Dark hair, quick hands, goes by Nix. She was taken two nights ago in the raid on the Lantern Market."

*We know the girl.* The lean queen's voice was sharper, quicker than the king's. *The pigeons saw her carried out in a wagon. The pigeons see everything and understand nothing, but we make do with what they give us.* She descended the statue in a fluid ripple, stopping on a crate an arm's length from Dominic, close enough that he could see the fine stitching of her old scars. *We know where the Inquisition keeps its soft prisoners. We know which cell, which corridor, which guard sleeps at which bell. We know it the way you know the back of your own hand.*

"Then name your price."

*Ah.* All three kings went still, and the stillness spread outward through the vault like frost. *He says it so easily,* the old king murmured. *Surface-folk always do. They think we want gold. Gold rots in the dark, charm-seller. Gold means nothing to a people who can chew through it.* The clouded eye fixed on him. *We want what you have been hoarding. We want your secrets.*

"Secrets are cheap."

*Yours are not.* The third king spoke for the first time, and its voice was quiet as dust settling. *You are the last of the speakers. You have walked the drowned shrine. You have heard the tide answer prayers. You have met the thing that wears a god's face and you have run from it and lived. You have a crow on your shoulder who was cast out of the order that built the gate beneath the sea. You have a plan — a half-plan, a mad half-plan — to reach that gate.* The dark eyes did not blink. *These are not cheap secrets. These are the most expensive secrets in Vellamare. And we are a patient people. We have waited out empires. We can wait out you.*

Dominic's mouth had gone dry. He was aware, distantly, of Crow's claws tightening on his shoulder — a warning, or a reassurance; with Crow it was always both.

"You want me to tell you everything."

*We want a trade,* the old king said, almost gently. *A secret for a secret. That is the old law, older than your gods, older than your gate. The law of small mouths. You give us what you know, truly given, and we give you the way to your girl. Refuse, and you leave with nothing, and the girl learns what the Inquisition does to thieves with strange eyes.*

*Or,* said the queen, with a flash of teeth that was almost a smile, *you can lie to us. Speakers always think they can. But we are rats, charm-seller. We have smelled lies for ten thousand years. We were chewing through the walls of the first liar's house.*

Dominic looked out over the congregation — thousands of small dark eyes, every one of them fixed on him, patient as the tide. And he thought about Nix, who had picked his pocket and then given everything back because his dreams had gotten into her head without her permission. Who had stood between him and the inquisitors' nets and shouted *run, you idiot* with a grin on her face. Two nights. Maybe three.

"Ask," he said.

Crow made a sound low in his throat that was not quite a word.

*Everything,* said the third king, softly. *Begin with the shrine.*

* * *

He talked until his voice went raw.

He told them about the drowned shrine, and the fisherman's wife who had spoken through the tide with her dead mouth, and the hound that had caught his scent on the wind. He told them about Seraphine Vale — her bound gift, her wrong-note lie-sense, her mercy that was a trap with the trapdoor still open. He told them about the healer's miracle on Candle Street, and how the whole block had lost a year of itself, grandmothers staring at grandchildren like strangers, and how he had understood, standing in that hollowed-out street, what the returned gods were eating and why the menu had grown so generous of late.

The rats listened the way only rats listen: absolutely, motionlessly, every whisker a needle pointed at the truth. Once, when he faltered over the memory of the market burning — the nets, the smoke, Nix turning back to shove him clear and the door of the wagon closing on her grin — the whole congregation seemed to lean in, drinking it, and Dominic understood with a lurch that his grief was not incidental to the trade. It *was* the trade. Secrets were only the wrapping. What the kings wanted was the taste of the surface world, carried down into the dark in his voice.

"Careful," Crow murmured against his ear, barely a breath. "They're not just listening. They're *keeping*. Everything you say here becomes theirs. Rats don't forget, boy. It's the one thing they have over gods — gods have to steal their memories. Rats just hold on."

"Then they'll hold this too," Dominic said, too low for the congregation to hear — though he suspected they heard everything. "She's worth it."

"Sentiment," Crow said. "Disgusting. Keep going."

When he reached the Last Bestiary — the ruined archive, the covenant, the seal that could only be cast by a speaker willing to pay in memory — the vault went so quiet that Dominic could hear the water dripping somewhere far above, counting out the seconds like a patient clerk.

*The gate,* said the old king at last. *You mean to open the drowned gate.*

"I mean to *close* it."

*Close. Open. To a gate, these are the same door.* The silver muzzle lowered, and the one good eye was very close now, very old, very bright. *We remember the gate, charm-speaker. We were there when it was made. Our grandmothers' grandmothers carried the mortar-stones in their teeth. We remember what the world was like before the seal — when the gods walked openly and everything that lived was either a servant or a meal.* A pause, and the whole vault seemed to draw breath. *We remember the last beast-speaker who came down here, asking for our help. Do you know what we traded him?*

Dominic's throat tightened. "What?"

*Nothing. We refused him. We told him the price was too high, that no one should pay in memory, that a man who forgets is a man unmade. And he went anyway, alone, and he paid, and the gate closed, and the gods went silent, and the world got five years of quiet and a hundred years of fear.* The old king's whiskers trembled. *He was your grandfather's teacher, or his teacher's teacher. The line runs thin, but it runs. And now you sit where he sat, and you are about to make the same bargain, and we are about to make the same mistake.*

"Then don't make it," Dominic said. "Refuse me. I'll find another way."

The dark-eyed king laughed. It was not a comforting sound. *No,* it said. *We have grown bored of being wise. Wisdom is cold company in a wet cellar. Besides —* the dark eyes glittered — *you have given us better secrets than he ever did. He came with fear. You came with hope. Hope spoils faster. We find we want to see what it rots into.*

The three kings conferred then, in a chittering so fast and dense that Dominic caught only fragments — *wagon, third bell, the soft cells, the one with the hound* — and then the old king raised his head.

*Here is your price, already paid. Listen well, because we will not say it twice, and the walls of the Inquisition drink secrets like wine.*

*The girl is held in the Undercroft of the Bastion of the Pure Word, not the main cells — they keep her apart because her gift frightens them, and frightened inquisitors are careless inquisitors. The Undercroft is old, older than the Bastion, and it has a drainage system, and the drainage system is *ours*, as all drainage in this city is ours, has been since before the Bastion had a name. There is a grate behind the coal store on the north side, third stone from the corner is loose, and the tunnel runs under the exercise yard and up through the cistern room.*

*You will go at the change of the night watch, when the hounds are fed and slow. You will avoid the fourth corridor, because the fourth corridor is watched by a woman named Vale, who is no longer an inquisitor in anything but her paperwork, and who has been asking strange questions about a crow, a charm-seller, and a drowned shrine. She has been asking them in a small voice, to herself, in an empty chapel. The rats in the chapel walls have heard. The rats tell us everything.*

Dominic went cold. "She's — what do you mean, asking? Asking what?"

*Asking whether a god that eats memories can be forgiven,* said the dark-eyed king, *and whether a church that feeds people to it can be survived. Asking, in short, the questions of a woman whose faith has begun to smell wrong to her. We know the smell. We have lived under churches for a very long time.* The dark eyes gleamed. *We tell you this for free, charm-speaker, because it is not a secret — it is a warning. The woman Vale is a door that has come off its hinges. Doors like that swing. Mind your fingers.*

Dominic stood in the flooded vault, knee-deep in black water, with a map of the Bastion unfolding in his mind and a name — *Vale* — sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone. The trade was done. He had paid more than he'd planned and gotten more than he'd dared ask, and both facts felt like the two jaws of the same trap.

"Thank you," he said, and the words came out rough.

*Do not thank us,* said the old king, settling back onto the headless statue like a shadow returning to its shape. *Thank us when the girl breathes free air. And charm-speaker —* the clouded eye caught the last of the lantern-light he'd never lit — *when you go down into the Bastion, remember that we told you about the fourth corridor. Remember that we told you about the woman. And remember, above all, what we said about the last speaker who stood where you stand.*

*He also thought he could save everyone,* said the dark-eyed king, from the dark, softly, *and the rats who warned him are still down here, still waiting, still keeping his name. It was Almeida too.*

Dominic's blood stopped.

*Go,* said the queen, almost kindly. *The third bell is ringing. And boy — the small voices are watching. We are always watching. It is the one prayer we never stopped saying, even when your gods went quiet.*

* * *

They climbed out of the Undercellar into the grey hour before dawn, when Vellamare is neither night nor day but only wet. Dominic walked without speaking for three streets, and Crow let him, which for Crow was a form of mercy.

Finally, at the mouth of the Lantern Market — burned stalls still standing like black teeth against the lightening sky — Dominic said, "My grandfather's teacher. The one who sealed the gate. He was an Almeida."

"I heard."

"You knew."

"I suspected. The order kept its lines thin on purpose — one name, passed down like a candle through the dark, so no one could snuff them all at once." Crow's feathers lifted in something between a shrug and a shudder. "I told you I was cast out. I never told you what I was cast out *for*. It was for saying, in front of the whole convocation, that the last sealing was a crime and the man who did it should have been stopped." He was quiet a moment. "I was young. I thought forgetting was the worst thing that could happen to a person. I've since been exiled, starved, shot at, and lectured by rats, and I've revised my opinion of several things, though not that one."

Dominic looked at the burned market. Somewhere under the Bastion's stones, Nix was sitting in a cell, and she didn't even know his family name was a debt the world had been collecting for a hundred years.

"The fourth corridor," he said. "Vale. The rats say she's coming apart."

"The rats say a great many things. Half of it is true and the other half is true in a way you'll regret." Crow hopped down onto a burned stall's charred beam, and the dawn caught his eye, black and gold. "So. The Bastion of the Pure Word, at the change of the watch, through a coal store and a cistern, past a corridor we've been explicitly warned to avoid, to steal a thief out of an inquisition's cellar." He clicked his beak. "I've had worse plans. I've had most of them, in fact. When do we go?"

"Tomorrow night," Dominic said. "Tonight, we prepare. And Crow—" He stopped. The question had been sitting in him since the vault, cold and small, and it came out before he could stop it. "The last speaker. The one the rats refused. Do you think he regretted it? Paying what he paid?"

Crow was silent long enough that the gulls began to stir on the rooftops, crying the city awake.

"I think," he said at last, "that he sealed the gate to save the world, and the world never learned his name, and the only creatures who kept it were rats in a flooded cellar. And I think tomorrow night you're going to walk into the one place in Vellamare where a woman with a bound beast-speaker's gift is asking the walls whether her church deserves to survive." He spread his wings, black against the pale sky. "So sleep, Almeida. Tomorrow we find out whether the rats sold you a way in — or sold you to her."

Above the burned market, the third bell finished ringing, and the gulls rose all at once, thousands of them, wheeling east — toward the Bastion, toward the sea, toward the drowned gate — like a city's worth of small voices, pointing the way.
