## 11. The Cells Below

The Bastion did not look like a prison from the sea side. It looked like a cathedral that had grown tired of prayer and decided to keep watch instead — buttresses like folded hands, windows narrow as knife wounds, and beneath it all, at the tide line, a mouth of black rock where the water went in and never came back out.

Dominic watched it from the roof of a salt-house across the harbor, his collar up against a wind that smelled of brine and old stone and, faintly, of Nix. Not her perfume — she didn't own any — but the particular ghost-smell she left behind: tallow, copper coins, the inside of other people's pockets. He had carried her satchel for three days now, because carrying it was easier than admitting he might be carrying it to her funeral.

*She's alive,* said the gull on the ridge beside him. It had followed him from the market ruins without being asked, which gulls never did unless they wanted something or knew something. *Warm hands below. The woman with the iron smell feeds her.*

*Feeds her,* Dominic repeated silently, testing the words like a man testing ice.

*Soup. Bread with weevils. Alive is alive.* The gull preened, unimpressed by his gratitude. *The dog is there too. The iron-woman's dog. It does not eat what she gives it. It gives half to the thief-girl through the bars.*

Dominic closed his eyes. Three days since the raid. Three days since Nix had shoved him down an alley mouth and turned to face the inquisitors with a stolen constable's badge held up like a torch and a grin that said *go, you idiot, go* — and he had gone, because going was the only thing that would make her sacrifice mean anything, and hating himself for it every step since.

*And the cells?* he asked the gull. *Where do they keep them?*

*Below the chapel. Where the sea can hear.*

Crow had told him once, in one of his gruffer moods, that the old beast-speakers built nothing by accident. That when the order chose sites for their shrines, they listened first — put their ears to stone and soil and asked what the ground remembered. The Bastion had been built on the drowned shrine's outer wall, before the Inquisition, before the silencing. Which meant the cells below were carved into stone that still, faintly, remembered being holy.

Which meant, if Crow was right about anything — and he was right about most things, loudly and often — that the stone could still be spoken to.

"You're going to get us both killed," Crow had said that morning, not looking up from the crow's skull he was carving sigils into with a fishbone awl. "You understand that. You're a charm-seller. You sell luck to sailors who should know better. You are not a rescuer."

"I'm the last heir of the covenant," Dominic said.

"You're a man with a rat problem and good intentions." Crow blew shavings off the skull. "In my experience those die more often than cowards."

"Then help me be efficient."

That had been the whole argument. Crow came anyway, because grief teaches discipline, and Crow's discipline looked exactly like following the people you couldn't bear to lose into the dark.

* * *

They went in where the water went in.

The tide-mouth swallowed them to the chest, cold enough that Dominic's breath came in white rags, and then the passage rose and the dark became total in a different way — the dry, listening dark of deep places. He spoke to the stone as he walked, the way Crow had taught him: not with his mouth, mostly, but with intention shaped into the old grammar. *Stone, I am small. Stone, I pass. Stone, remember me kindly.*

The stone answered the way stone always answers — slowly, in images rather than words. A sense of weight. A memory of singing, very old, worn smooth as a pebble. And underneath it, threaded through everything like rot through timber, something hungry that had been feeding here a long time.

"Don't listen too long," Crow murmured behind him. His voice echoed wrong in the passage, doubled and flattened. "The walls here have opinions. Some of them aren't ours."

They climbed. The passage narrowed, widened, forked; the rats guided them at the forks — three of them waiting on ledges like little gray priests, whiskers twitching, trading directions for the secrets Dominic paid out of his own head. That was the price of rat-bargaining: they didn't want coin. They wanted memories, small ones, the way men wanted small coin. He gave them the taste of oranges from his seventh summer. He gave them the name of the girl he'd kissed behind the chandlery at fifteen and immediately regretted giving, because it went out of him like a pulled tooth, leaving a smooth socket where the sweetness had been.

*Worth it?* Crow asked, watching him sway against the wall.

"Ask me when she's out," Dominic whispered.

*That,* said Crow, *is the correct answer. For whatever it will be worth later.*

The cells began as a change in the air — the wet salt-dark giving way to lamp-oil and lye and the particular animal misery of confined things. Dominic felt them before he saw them, a chorus of small suffering pressing against the stone: dogs, cats, a hawk somewhere above, all of them bound the way inquisitors bound their beasts, with iron and liturgy and pain dressed up as obedience.

And under all of it, fainter, human: Nix. He couldn't hear her thoughts — no one could, she wasn't a speaker — but he could feel the shape of her presence the way you feel someone standing behind you in a crowd. Close. Two levels down. Behind iron.

*Guards?* he asked the nearest rat.

*Four at the stair. One asleep. One afraid of the dark and won't admit it. Two cruel, and cruelty is heavy — you'll hear them coming.*

He heard them coming. Everyone did; their boots rang on the steps like bells announcing their own damnation. Dominic pressed into a cell alcove with Crow, heart slamming, and the two guards passed close enough that he could see the keys at the tall one's belt and the brand on the short one's jaw — the Inquisition's mark, a closed eye.

When they'd gone, Crow exhaled through his nose. "Keys," he said. "There are always keys."

"There are always four more guards past the keys."

"That's why you brought me." Crow produced, from some impossible fold of his coat, a vial of something that smelled like burnt feathers and bad decisions. "Sleep-rot. Rats carry it into grain. Two drops in their wine and they dream until the second bell. I need ninety seconds alone with that wine cask."

"And I need—"

"To go get yourself cornered, yes. I've read the rest of this story." Crow's eyes caught the lamplight, black and glittering. "Ninety seconds, Almeida. If you're late, I'm telling your rats embarrassing stories about you forever."

* * *

It went wrong the way things go wrong in the Bastion: quietly, then all at once.

The wine worked. The guards slumped over their table mid-card-game, faces slack, cards sliding from their fingers like dying leaves. Dominic took the ring of keys with hands that shook only a little, found the stair down, found the second level, found the door with fresh scratches around its lock — Nix's work, he'd have known those marks anywhere, the impatient gouge of a pick made from a bent nail because they'd taken her real picks.

"Nix," he breathed through the bars. "Hey. Thief. Wake up."

She woke the way she did everything — instantly, silently, and already reaching for a weapon. Then her eyes found him and something in her face cracked open, relief and fury fighting for the same ground.

"You absolute—" she started, loud, then strangled it to a whisper. "Three days. THREE DAYS. I covered for you. I let them put *manacles* on me, do you know how humiliating manacles are for a professional—"

"I brought your satchel."

She stopped. Her eyes went bright and vicious with unshed tears. "Of course you did," she whispered. "Give it here before I forgive you."

The lock was old iron and older prayers; the key turned with a groan that sounded, to Dominic's strained nerves, like a trumpet. She slipped out, rolled her shoulders, flexed her fingers, and was already scanning the corridor with those quick thief's eyes that counted exits the way other people counted money.

"Up the stair, through the wine room, two guards dreaming," she murmured. "Then?"

"Then the tide-passage and the sea and never seeing this place again."

"Romantic." But her hand found his sleeve — just briefly, just to confirm he was solid — and then the world ended.

Because the lamps above went out. All of them, at once, snuffed like candles pinched between god-fingers, and the dark that came down the stairwell wasn't ordinary dark. It moved. It had texture. And walking inside it, unhurried, came the sound of boots and chain mail and a voice Dominic had heard only once before, in an interrogation room five years' worth of nightmares ago:

"Beast-speaker."

Seraphine Vale.

* * *

She had changed since that room. He could see it even in the near-black — or perhaps he could see it because of the near-black, because Seraphine Vale had always seemed to him like a person assembled entirely from light and doctrine, and now pieces were missing. There were shadows under her eyes that doctrine hadn't put there. Her hand rested not on her sword but on her hound's head, and the hound —

The hound was trembling.

Dominic felt it through the stone the way he felt everything now, Crow's lessons having pried his senses permanently open: the animal's fear, vast and wordless, layered under obedience like water under ice. It knew its mistress was frightened. It knew the thing in the chapel above — the thing the church had been feeding prayers to, the thing that ate a street's worth of names — was frightened too, in its vast hollow way, and that frightened it worse. Animals knew hunger when they smelled it. Even holy hunger. *Especially* holy hunger.

"You came for the thief," Seraphine said. Her voice echoed off the cells. "Sentimental. I expected better strategy from a man who survived my questioning."

"You let me survive," Dominic said. "We both know that was a trap. Well. Here I am. Caught in it."

"Here you are." Something moved in her voice that might have been tiredness. "Do you know what I found above, Almeida? In the chapel. Do you know what answered when the congregation prayed?"

"The god."

"*A* god. Wearing the Quiet Mother's face, wearing it badly, like a mask cut by someone who'd only had the face described to them." Her grip tightened on the hound's scruff. "It asked for their names. It said names were the proper offering. And forty-two people gave theirs gladly, and forty-two people walked out of that chapel unable to remember their children's birthdays, and the Grand Inquisitor called it a *miracle*, and I stood there and said amen because that is what I was made to say."

The hound whined, low.

"Stand fast, Bracken," Seraphine murmured, and the command went through the animal like a nail through a paw.

And Dominic — exhausted, cornered, lampless, three days without sleep and running on rat-traded memories and fury — heard himself say, in the old tongue, softly:

*Bracken. No one has ever asked if you wanted to stand fast.*

Everything stopped.

The dark seemed to lean closer. Nix's hand tightened on his sleeve. Seraphine went absolutely still, the stillness of a woman hearing a language she'd been told was extinct, a language she herself half-spoke, a language the church had bound out of her gift with iron and liturgy years ago — and the hound turned its great scarred head away from her hand and looked at Dominic.

Looking isn't the right word. Beasts don't look at speakers; they *arrive* at them, attention pouring across the distance like heat off coals. Bracken arrived. And in that contact Dominic felt the whole architecture of the binding at last, laid bare the way Crow had promised it would be someday: chains of iron yes, but also chains of *words*, vows the hound had been forced to swear in a grammar it had never consented to, layer upon layer of *obey, obey, obey* wound so tight around the poor creature's soul that it had forgotten there had ever been anything underneath.

Every word spoken to an animal is a promise, Crow had taught him. Which meant every word spoken *at* an animal, without asking, was theft.

*I can't unbind you,* Dominic told the hound honestly. *Those vows aren't mine to break. But you swore them to the church. To the office. And I'm asking you — not commanding, asking —* whose servant are you, truly?

The silence stretched. Somewhere above, the false god hummed in its chapel like a wasp in a bottle.

Bracken crossed the corridor.

Not to Dominic. Past him — past Nix, who flattened against the wall — and sat down heavily at Seraphine Vale's feet, leaned his whole weight against her legs, and looked up at her with eyes that said, in the only grammar animals have ever needed: *not them. You. It was always going to be you. Choose better than they taught you.*

"Oh," said Seraphine Vale, in a very small voice, and the sound of the Inquisition's finest interrogator saying *oh* like a child was the strangest, most terrible sound Dominic had ever heard.

"They choose," he said aloud, hoarse. "That's the whole secret, Inquisitor. That's what the church forgot. Loyalty can't be commanded. It can only be given. And yours just gave itself to *you* — not your office. You. Whatever that means to you now, it means you're standing in the wrong building holding the wrong leash."

For a long moment she didn't move. Then her hand came down and settled on Bracken's head, gently, differently than before — not a claim but a greeting — and when she raised her eyes to Dominic's they were wet and furious and utterly, irreversibly awake.

"The cells have a back stair," she said. "Past the reliquary. I'll walk ahead of you and if anyone asks, I'm transporting prisoners." She unclipped something from her belt — her inquisitor's seal, the closed eye — and dropped it on the flagstones. The sound it made was very final. "Move. Before I remember how much trouble I'm in and change my mind out of sheer habit."

They moved. Up the back stair, through the reliquary smelling of dust and silver, past a guard who saluted Seraphine and got a nod in return and never knew he'd waved a heresy through the door. At the tide-mouth, cold air and open sky, Nix paused with one foot in the water and looked back at the defrocked woman silhouetted in the passage, her hound at her heel like a shadow that had finally chosen its shape.

"This doesn't make us friends," Nix said.

"No," Seraphine agreed. "But it makes us allies, and I have information that will curdle your blood, and nowhere left to bring it. So you're stuck with me."

Above the Bastion, the third bell began to ring — and halfway through its first stroke, the bell faltered, the note bending wrong, and every dog in Vellamare began to howl at once, and Bracken lifted his great head and answered them, and Dominic understood with sick certainty that the gods had noticed the empty cells.

They would come asking questions soon. But first — Seraphine said, pulling her hood up as the tide swallowed their footsteps — first they needed to talk about what the church had buried under the drowned gate.

And why it was still screaming.
