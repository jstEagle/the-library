## 12. An Inquisitor Defrocked

The brand was still cooling when they took her robes.

Seraphine Vale stood in the Chapterhouse yard with her arms bared to the drizzle — the law required it, required that the flesh be shown to the assembled faithful so that the mark of heresy could not hide — and she watched a junior clerk fold her inquisitor's coat into an evidence box the way one folds a shroud. The wool had held the smell of ten years: incense and dog and rain on stone. It did not hold it well enough. When the lid closed, the smell escaped anyway, thin as a ghost, and she thought: *that is what I am now. A scent escaping a closed box.*

The hound was not permitted at the branding.

They had named him Cassiel, once, when he belonged to the Order — *belonged*, as though loyalty were a leash — but everyone in Vellamare knew whose hound he was. He had sat outside the Chapterhouse gates for three days while they tried her, refusing food, refusing the handlers who came with chains and sweet-voiced commands, and on the third day he had dug under the wall like something out of a beggar's story and simply appeared beside her in the dock, mud to the shoulder, eyes level with the tribunal.

*You cannot command me,* he had said, in that voice only speakers heard, gravel poured slow over honey. *You never could. You only ever asked. I am asking now to stay.*

They had branded her for many things. Collusion with the beast-devil. Unbinding a consecrated animal. Heresy of the old covenant. But the clerk recording charges had paused, quiver in his hand, when the Provost dictated the final count: *for permitting a hound of the Order to exercise unlicensed will.*

Permitting. As if Cassiel had needed her leave.

Now she walked out through the postern gate in a plain gray shift, hair unbound, the brand on her forearm wrapped in linen that already stank of rendered fat, and Cassiel fell into step beside her without a word between them, because there was no longer any need for words. Ten years of partnership. She knew the rhythm of his gait the way she knew her own pulse.

"Where?" he asked, when the Chapterhouse towers had sunk behind the rooftops.

She looked down at the bundle under her arm. Three nights before her trial, when she understood how it would end, she had gone back into the sealed archive beneath her own chapel — the chapel she had kept clean for a decade, sweeping ash from the gutters, polishing a god that never answered — and she had taken everything.

"The night market," she said. "The charm-seller's stall."

Cassiel made a sound low in his chest that might have been a laugh. "They'll kill you there."

"They'll try," she said. "Everyone tries. I'm getting used to it."

* * *

The market had burned and been rebuilt in the space of two weeks, which told Seraphine everything about Vellamare that a decade of inquisitions never had. The stalls stood crooked on their new timbers, tar still bleeding dark from the joints, and the vendors had hung lanterns of colored glass — salvaged, some of them, from the wreckage — so that the whole lane glowed like a wound lit from within. People moved through it quickly, heads down, the way people moved anywhere the Inquisition had recently expressed an interest. But they moved. That was the thing. They had not stopped coming.

She felt their eyes on her the moment she passed the first stall. A fishwife stopped mid-haggle. A boy selling roasted chestnuts pulled his tray close to his chest like a shield. Word traveled faster than fire here; they all knew her face. Ten years of raids, of ledgers, of neighbors denouncing neighbors while she wrote it all down in her neat, terrible hand. She had been the face of the order that hunted them.

She deserved every look she got. She kept walking.

The charm-seller's stall was where Crow had said it would be — because of course the corvid-speaker had known, of course he had sent word to her through a rat or a starling or some other small spy she'd never sanctioned, telling her exactly where to find them, exactly what price she'd pay in suspicion for coming. *If you mean it, come unarmed and come marked,* the bird had rasped at her window two nights ago, and then had stolen the crust she offered and left the message like a debt unpaid.

Dominic Almeida was not at the stall. A girl was.

Nix. The thief with borrowed eyes. Seraphine had read her file a hundred times — orphaned, unregistered gift, seventeen recorded thefts, zero convictions, because you cannot convict what you cannot catch — and none of the files had mentioned that the girl was so young, or that she looked at strangers the way cats look at dogs: measuring the distance to your throat.

The girl saw the brand before she saw the face. Her hand went under the counter.

"Don't," Seraphine said quietly. "Whatever you keep under there. Don't."

"You've got nerve." Nix's voice was flat as river ice. "Walking in here wearing that."

"Wearing this is the point." Seraphine set her bundle on the counter between them and unwrapped it slowly, deliberately, hands visible the whole time — an old interrogator's trick, showing empty palms, except her palms weren't empty. What spilled out onto the stained wood was a decade of the Inquisition's worst secret.

Ledgers. Dozens of them, their spines cracked, their pages dense with her own handwriting. And among them, loose leaves, transcripts, a reliquary box that hummed faintly when Nix leaned toward it, her nostrils flaring like she smelled meat.

"What is that," the girl said. Not a question.

"That is a fragment of the thing your healer prayed to on Candlemas Street." Seraphine's voice stayed level. Inside, she was surprised at how steady it came out. "The miracle that took a year from four hundred people. The Provost has six more fragments in the crypt below the Grand Chapel. He feeds them. Names, mostly — confession records, baptismal rolls. Whole congregations' worth of remembering, fed into those boxes a mouthful at a time. I watched it eat a parish last month. Forty souls. They stood up after and couldn't tell me their mothers' names."

Nix had gone very still. Behind her, deeper in the market, a dog began to bark — sharp, rhythmic, deliberate — and Seraphine realized with a cold drop in her stomach that it wasn't barking at her. It was signaling.

"They're coming," Cassiel rumbled at her side. "Three. Armed. The tall one walks heavy on his left heel — the sergeant with the scarred knuckles."

"How do you—"

"I can hear a boot across a courtyard," the hound said. "Move, or don't. But decide."

* * *

They decided fast. Nix swept the evidence off the counter into a flour sack with the speed of long practice, kicked open a trapdoor Seraphine hadn't even seen, and jerked her head downward. "In. Now. If they take me, I never saw you. If they take you—" a thin, sharp smile, entirely without warmth, "—well. You were always good at cells."

The passage beneath the market was older than the city above it — Seraphine catalogued that automatically, the way she catalogued everything, the salt-eaten brick, the smell of drowned things, the way the walls curved inward like the inside of a throat — and by the time the boots thundered over the trapdoor above, they were forty paces into the dark, Cassiel leading with his nose though his eyes worked fine.

"He'll search the stall," Seraphine whispered. "Your sergeant."

"Mm." Nix's voice floated back, unconcerned. "He'll find a stall full of love-charms and rat-bones and nothing else. Dominic wards it. Has since the raid." A pause, and then, grudgingly, almost against her will: "He wards it so nobody else gets caught. Not himself. He doesn't much care what happens to him, which is a problem I'm working on."

They emerged through a broken seawall into a net-mender's yard strung with drying cod, and from there through lanes Seraphine didn't know, climbing always, until the city fell away below them and they stood before a door set into the cliff itself, half-hidden by ivy gone feral. Nix knocked a pattern — three, pause, two — and the door opened on darkness and the smell of lamp oil and old paper.

Crow stood in the doorway. Up close, without the distance of surveillance reports, he was smaller than she'd imagined and more frightening: a lean man with a face like weathered rope, a crow perched on his shoulder with its head cocked at her at an angle no honest bird would use.

"So," he said. "The Inquisitor."

"Inquisitor no longer." She touched the linen on her forearm. "Defrocked. Branded. The full litany."

"Cassiel," the crow said, in a voice like a hinge, and the hound dipped his great head. Old acquaintances, apparently. Old grievances too, judging by the way Crow's eyes narrowed. "You kept her leashed well enough, these ten years."

"No one kept me leashed," Cassiel said mildly. "Least of all her."

"Inside," Crow said, stepping aside. "Before someone sees the traitor and the heretic sharing a doorstep. Bad for both our reputations."

* * *

The room inside the cliff was a cave that men had civilized: shelves hammered into the rock, lamps burning whale oil, maps of Vellamare pinned up in layers like the sediment of obsession. And there, rising from a chair by the lamp with a wariness that hurt more than hatred would have, was Dominic Almeida.

He looked worse than he had at his interrogation. Thinner. There was a healing cut along his jaw, and his hands — the hands of a craftsman, clever and careful — were bandaged at the fingertips. But it was his eyes that struck her. At their interrogation he had lied to her magnificently, layering falsehoods like lacquer, and she had hated him for it. Now there was no lacquer. Just exhaustion, and grief, and underneath both, a watchfulness that recognized hers.

"Inquisitor Vale," he said.

"Not anymore."

"That's what worries me." He didn't sit back down. Neither did she. Two people standing across a lamplit room, each waiting for the other to show a throat. "The last time we spoke, you were hunting me. Now you've been branded for failing to. Forgive me if I need a moment to work out which version of you wants me dead."

"All of them, once," Seraphine said. "I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. I ran raids on this district for ten years. I broke the Coriander Street ring. I deported the Ferro sisters to the salt mines, and Marta Ferro died there, and I signed the paper with my own name because that was the job." She let it stand in the air between them, ugly and complete. "I'm not asking you to forgive any of it. I'm asking you to look at what I brought, and then decide whether you want to hate me efficiently or usefully."

Nix dumped the flour sack onto the table. Ledgers scattered. The reliquary box rolled free and came to rest against a candlestick, humming its faint, hungry note, and every animal in the room reacted at once — Cassiel's hackles lifting, the crow spreading its wings half-open with a dry whisper, and somewhere outside the door, the scrabble of rats going silent.

Dominic looked at the box the way a man looks at a snake he recognizes.

"That's it," he said. "That's what took the year from Candlemas Street."

"A fragment of it. The Provost keeps the rest beneath the Grand Chapel, in the crypt they told novices was flooded." Seraphine opened the largest ledger to a page near the middle and turned it toward him. "These are requisition records. Confession transcripts, marked for 'archival combustion' — which is what we call destruction. Except they aren't burned. They're carried down in locked cases, twice weekly, by the Under-Chaplain. I followed him once. I watched him feed the pages into a grate, and I listened to what was behind the grate." Her voice caught, just slightly, and she hated it, and kept going anyway. "It recited names back to him. All the names on the pages. And then it asked for more. It said—" she made herself repeat it, made herself hear it again in her own mouth, "—it said, *the faithful give freely. Why does the church hoard?*"

Silence. The lamp flame bent in a draft from the sea-crack in the rock.

"It talks to him," Dominic said softly.

"It talks to anyone who brings it memory. That's how it feeds. That's how all of them feed — the ones answering prayers, the ones wearing the faces of saints. Memory is the coin. Every miracle since the silence broke has been bought with someone's past, and the church has been acting as their moneylender." She spread the loose transcripts across the table with both hands, laying out the pattern like a general laying out a campaign. "Here. Candlemas Street — four hundred people, one year each. Here, the Harbormen's Guild hall — the entire guild lost the year of the great storm. Here—" her finger stopped, pressed flat on a page, "—here is the requisition for Saint Elarin's Foundling Home. Sixty children's memories of their lives before the foundling home. The Provost approved it personally. He called it *mercy*, in the margin. He wrote that the children were better off unburdened."

Nix made a sound. Small, bitten-off. Seraphine glanced at her and saw the girl had gone white around the mouth, one hand pressed flat against her own sternum as if holding a door shut.

"The gate," Dominic said. His voice had changed — lower, rougher, the lacquer fully stripped now. "Under the sea-shrine. That's what they're feeding. The remnants are trying to rebuild themselves strong enough to force it open from this side. The memories aren't food, they're *material*. They're rebuilding themselves out of us."

"Yes."

"And the church knows."

"The church believes it is serving returning gods." Seraphine met his eyes. "Which is worse, in its way. Faith makes better slaves than fear. Fear runs out. Faith refills itself."

Crow had drifted to the table and was reading the ledger upside down with startling speed, the crow on his shoulder reading right-side up, which should have been comical and was instead deeply unsettling. "The crypt under the Grand Chapel," he said. "How deep?"

"Below the old cisterns. There's a stair behind the reliquary of Saint Anselm — the saint whose feast day the church stopped celebrating eighty years ago, and no one remembers why." She almost smiled. "I remember why now. Someone remembered too hard."

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. He picked up one of the transcripts — the foundling home record — and read it twice, his thumb moving over the Provost's marginal note. Then he set it down very carefully, the way one sets down something that might detonate.

"Why," he said. "Ten years. You were good at it, Vale. I know how good — I spent five years staying ahead of you. You could have stayed good at it forever if you'd never opened that archive. So why did you look?"

It was the question she had been turning over during every sleepless hour since, and she still didn't have a polished answer, so she gave her only honest one.

"My hound asked me a question I couldn't answer." She put her hand on Cassiel's head, and he leaned into it, ninety pounds of loyalty pressing warm against her palm. "After the unbinding. After he chose me over the church. He asked what I would have done if the church had ordered him destroyed — not commanded it, *ordered* it, the way orders come, on letterhead, in triplicate. And I started to say I'd have refused, and then I realized I'd have written the refusal in the same neat hand I wrote everything else, filed it properly, and then obeyed. Because that's what the job was. The job was obedience wearing the costume of righteousness." She looked down at the branded forearm. "I spent ten years hunting people for the crime of being what I now understand myself to be. My grandmother spoke to birds, you know. Family story. We thought it was a story." Her breath went out of her slowly. "It was a warning."

Dominic searched her face for a long time. She let him. She had been interrogated by professionals; she knew the technique of silence, knew how to endure being read. But this was different, and the difference undid her a little: he wasn't looking for the lie. He was looking for the person.

"Nix," he said finally. "What do you think?"

The girl had picked up one of the transcripts — the foundling home one, Seraphine noted, and filed that away — and she set it down with exaggerated care, like it was hot.

"I think," Nix said, "that she slept in cells she built. I think she can walk us into the crypt nobody can walk into. And I think if she betrays us, I'll know, because I dream what animals see, and there isn't a rat in this city that wouldn't sell her out for a rind of cheese." She shrugged, all sharp edges. "So yeah. Let her in. On a short rope."

"There is no rope," Cassiel said gravely, "long enough."

"Then we improvise." Dominic looked at Seraphine again, and something in his expression shifted — not trust, nothing so generous, but a decision. "You'll come with us to the shrine. You'll show us the crypt route, the stair behind Saint Anselm, whatever else is in those ledgers that helps. And when it's done — whatever happens at that gate — you don't get to disappear back into your old life. There's no old life. There's only forward."

"I know," Seraphine said. "That's rather the point of a brand."

Crow snorted. The crow on his shoulder made a sound like tearing silk, which Seraphine suspected was laughter.

Then the lamp guttered — no draft, she was certain, no draft at all — and every animal in the room turned its head toward the seaward wall of the cave at the same instant, the way a congregation turns toward a door. Cassiel growled, low and continuous. Outside, far below, the gulls had gone silent, all of them, the whole screaming chorus of the harbor cliffs stopping at once as if a hand had closed over the sea's mouth.

And from beyond the rock, faint but unmistakable, came a voice. Many voices braided into one, patient as tide, familiar as prayer:

*Little speaker. Little thief. Little bird. Bring us the heir, and the church will forgive the heretic everything.*

The reliquary box on the table began, softly, to sing.

Dominic looked at Seraphine. "Tell me that's not your god."

"It was never mine," she said, reaching for the ledger with the map of the crypt. "But I know the way it sings. And I know the door it sings through."
