## 14. The God of Small Hungers

The sea-shrine had a second heart.

Dominic felt it before he saw it — a pull beneath his ribs like a fishhook set in meat, gentle and insistent, drawing him down the flooded stair that spiraled away from the ruined chapel floor. The tide had been held back for them; he did not ask how, only noticed that the water stood in walls on either side of the descending steps, black glass in the moonlight, trembling with the shapes of fish that watched him pass with old, patient eyes. Every one of them turned as he went by. Every one of them bowed its head.

*They remember you,* said a voice that was not quite a voice, coming up through the stone, through the water, through the soles of his feet. *Little heir. Little last one. They remember what you have forgotten.*

He kept walking. Behind him, Crow's talons clicked on wet marble; ahead, Nix carried the lantern low, and the light made her shadow enormous against the curved wall, a giantess with a thief's quick hands. Seraphine came last, unarmored, her defrocked hands bare, and Dominic could hear her breathing — steady, deliberate, the breath of a woman praying without permission to pray.

The stair ended in a chamber like the inside of a bell.

It was round, vast, ribbed with pillars of white coral grown over older stone, and the ceiling arched far above them, lost in dark water where pale things drifted like snow falling upward. The floor was dry — a perfect circle of it, an island of ancient tile — and across every tile ran lines of script that glowed faintly when Dominic's shadow touched them, letters in the old tongue of beasts, the tongue Crow had spent three bitter weeks beating into him. He read them slowly, haltingly, aloud.

*"We who speak, seal. We who seal, pay."*

"That's it," Nix whispered. "That's the gate."

It didn't look like a gate. It looked like a door lying flat, buried in the floor — a slab of grey stone at the chamber's center, ringed with chains of bronze gone green as sea-glass, each chain anchored to a pillar, each anchor carved with the shape of an animal: wolf, whale, wren, rat, crow. And in the center of the slab, worn nearly smooth by centuries of salt, was a crown.

A crown of small carved beasts, circling.

"The Midnight Crown," Seraphine said quietly. "I thought it was a story the church told to frighten children."

"Most true things are," said Crow.

Dominic knelt beside the slab. The moment his knee touched the tile, the glow spread up through the script like fire catching along a fuse, and the whole chamber filled with light the color of dawn under water, and every animal-shaped anchor seemed to turn its head toward him.

And something answered from below.

* * *

It did not come roaring. That was the first thing he would remember afterward, when he tried to explain it to himself: it did not come roaring, or howling, or in any shape his nightmares had prepared him for. It came the way hunger comes — gradually, everywhere, a change in the temperature of the room.

The light dimmed. The fish fled upward. The water walls shivered.

And then, standing on the far side of the sealed door, where a moment before there had been nothing but tile and shadow, there was a woman.

She was small. Grey-stoled, grey-haired, with ink stains on her fingers and flour under her nails and a smell about her of bread and lamp-oil and the particular soap she had used all his childhood — the kind sold loose from the chandler's shop on Tern Street, wrapped in brown paper. She looked tired in the way mothers look tired, fondly tired, end-of-a-long-day tired, and she was smiling at him.

"Dominic," she said. "Look how tall you've got."

His body knew her before his mind did. His body lunged forward an entire step before Crow's wing slammed across his chest like a bar.

"Don't," Crow breathed. "Don't look at its face."

But he was already looking. He couldn't stop looking. Because the face was right — the strong nose, the laugh-line deeper on the left, the small scar above the eyebrow from the time she'd walked into the pantry shelf laughing — and the voice was right, warm and roughened, and the way she tilted her head when she waited for an answer was so exactly right that five years of grief tore open in his chest like a wound that had never once, not for a single day, actually closed.

"You died," he said. His voice came out very young. "I was nine. You went out for mending thread and the fever took the street and they burned the bodies because of the quarantine law, and I never—" His throat closed. "I never got to say goodbye."

"I know, love." She stepped closer. The chains on the door did not rattle. Nothing stopped her. "I know. I've missed your whole life. Isn't that the cruelest thing? I've missed every minute of it, and I've been *right here*, waiting, the whole time. Watching you grow. Watching you sell your little charms and talk to your little rats and pretend you're nobody." Her smile deepened. "You were never nobody, Dominic. You were my whole world."

"It isn't her," Seraphine said sharply, from behind him. "Almeida. It wears faces it has eaten. I've seen it do this. It takes the dead out of the living's memories and puts them on like coats—"

"Hush now," the woman said, gently, kindly, without even turning her head, and Seraphine's voice simply — stopped. Not silenced. Stopped, the way a conversation stops when a mother enters the room.

"She's right, though," the woman admitted, and laughed, and the laugh broke something loose behind Dominic's ribs. "I'm not entirely her. But I'm not entirely *not* her either, sweetheart. That's what no one understands. When we eat a memory, we become it. Your mother is in me now — everything of her that anyone remembers, everything the tide carried down here, everything the gods drank before they went quiet. I am wearing her the way you wear your coat, and underneath—" the smile turned, just slightly, just enough, "—underneath I am very, very old, and very, very hungry, and I am offering you a bargain no one else alive can offer you."

"Walk away," Crow rasped. "Boy. *Heir.* Walk away."

"In a moment," Dominic heard himself say. His feet had roots in them.

"What bargain?" he asked.

The woman crouched, bringing her face level with his, and up close he could see the details that were wrong — the pupils a fraction too wide, the skin with no pores at all, smooth as painted porcelain beneath its warmth. But the eyes were hers. The gods had drunk her out of him, out of the neighbors, out of everyone who had ever loved her, and poured her back, and the pouring was perfect, because stolen things are always handled more carefully than owned ones.

"When the covenant broke," she said, "when the speakers scattered and the seal cracked, we starved. Do you understand? We are remnants, we fragments, we leftovers of divinity, and worship is food and prayer is bread and for five hundred years there was nothing. So we learned to eat the only meal left." She spread her ink-stained hands. "Memory. A year from a street. Names from a congregation. Small hungers, small meals. But a seal-breaker's memories — an heir's memories — those are a feast. Those are the whole herd given up at once. And yours—" her voice went soft, reverent, greedy, "—yours are still *whole*. Untouched. Locked behind the door of your own skull, waiting. No one has drunk from you yet, little king. You are the richest man in Vellamare and you don't even know what you own."

"I don't want anything from you."

"Yes you do." Gentle. Terrible. "You want your ninth year. You want the name of your father that no one living can tell you. You want the sound of her voice saying your name instead of mine wearing it. You want the lullaby whose tune you've been humming wrong for twenty years and it *hurts*, doesn't it? Humming it wrong? Knowing it's wrong and never being able to fix it?"

He hadn't known anyone could see that. He hadn't known it showed.

"I can give it all back," she said. "Every erased hour. The fever year. The quarantine. Her face, which you've lost — don't lie to yourself, boy, you lost it years ago, you keep her alive now out of duty and description, but her *face*, the real one, moving and laughing — I have it. I *am* holding it." She reached out, and though her hand never crossed the distance between them, he felt fingertips brush his cheek anyway, cold and soft as seafoam. "Open the door fully. Unchain it, speak it awake, let us come through into the world again — all of us, hungry, yes, hungry, but *alive*, Dominic, worshipped again, fed properly, fed on freely-given memory instead of theft — and I will pour her back into you. All of her. Every year. Every morning. You will go to sleep an orphan and wake up a son."

The chamber was silent except for the water, standing in its walls, listening.

"You're lying," Seraphine managed. Her voice shook. "That's what they do. They take and take and call it giving—"

"I might be," the god agreed pleasantly, in his mother's mouth. "Or I might not be. That's the beautiful part of the wager, isn't it? You'll never know unless you open the door. Faith, little inquisitor. I'm only asking for faith."

*Every word spoken to an animal is a promise,* Crow had taught him, in the reek of the rookery, with grief still hardening in his voice like mortar. *So learn to mean what you say, boy, because the beasts always do.*

And he thought: *and every promise a god makes is a debt written in someone else's blood.*

But his mother was smiling at him. His mother — or the exact weight and shape of her absence, offered back — was smiling at him, and saying, "You used to fall asleep sitting up, you know. In the chair by the window. I'd carry you to bed and you'd weigh nothing, nothing at all, my little bird-boned boy, and you'd murmur in your sleep in the old tongue and I never knew what you were promising the mice in the walls." Her eyes glistened. Porcelain eyes. "Let me tell you what it was. Let me tell you everything you've lost. Say yes."

His hand was moving. He watched it move, from somewhere outside himself, reaching for the nearest chain — green bronze, whale-carved, heavy with five centuries of salt — and the script on the floor blazed brighter at his approach, eager, welcoming, and somewhere very far away he heard Nix say his name, and then say it louder—

"Nix." He stopped. His fingers hovered over the chain. "Nix, don't touch me. Don't touch any of it. Whatever happens — whatever I do in the next minute — if I reach for that chain, you run. Promise me."

"No," said Nix.

"*Promise me.*"

"No," she said again, flat and final, and stepped up beside him, close enough that he could feel the tremor she was hiding in her small thief's hands. "I don't make promises I plan to break, charm-seller. Crow taught me that much."

The god's smile widened. It had been watching them both, he realized. It had been watching them both the entire time, the way a diner watches a table laid with two plates.

"And you," it said to Nix, in his mother's voice, tilting its head with her tilt, wearing her warmth like a mask worn slightly askew. "Come closer, little borrowed-eyes. Come and see what I've got under my coat for *you*."
