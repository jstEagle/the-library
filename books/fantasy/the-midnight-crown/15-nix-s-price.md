## 15. Nix's Price

The bestiary's last page had been copied three times, Crow said, because the first two had been burned by men who feared what was written there, and the third hidden inside a hymnal where no inquisitor would ever look. Nix found that fitting. She'd spent her whole life hiding true things inside worthless ones — a stolen ring sewn into a beggar's coat hem, a real name buried under three false ones, a girl's whole heart tucked behind a grin sharp enough to cut.

So she read it the way she read everything: quickly, sideways, like a mark watching for the watchman.

*The seal requires a speaker willing to pay. The gate does not take blood or breath or bone. It takes remembrance. All that the caster has been, all that the caster has loved, poured out into the stone until nothing remains but the sealing itself.*

She read it twice more before the words stopped swimming.

Then she folded the page very carefully along its old creases, put it back inside the hymnal, and went to find somewhere no one would ask her why her hands were shaking.

* * *

She ended up on the roof of the chandlery, which was as close to church as Nix ever came. Vellamare spread below her in its evening colors — lamp-oil gold and harbor-smoke blue, the night market kindling stall by stall like someone striking matches against the dark. Somewhere down there a fishwife was arguing about the price of eels, and a child was chasing a cat who was almost certainly reporting the chase to Dominic later, and the world was going on being ordinary, which suddenly felt like the most precious and most perishable thing Nix had ever held.

She knew how prices worked. That was the one education the streets had given her complete, with honors.

Everything cost something. A meal cost a pocket picked. A night's sleep cost a wall you could trust at your back. Love — and she was only recently learning to admit she knew anything about love — love cost the terrible risk of being known. And magic, real magic, the kind that moved tides and unmade gates, cost exactly what the page said it cost: everything you remembered being yourself.

Dominic would pay it. She'd known him long enough now — through the market fire, through the cells below the Inquisition, through the long cold walk to this drowned archive — to know it the way she knew the weight of a purse by its shape. He was built for sacrifice the way other men were built for carpentry or soldiering. Give him a choice between himself and everyone else and he wouldn't even hesitate, and he'd smile while he did it, that quiet apologetic smile, like he was sorry to inconvenience you by dying.

And the worst part, the part that made her want to scream at the gulls wheeling overhead, was that his memories were worth infinitely more than hers.

His were full of a mother's face, an order of speakers, a childhood spent learning the old tongue before the silence came. His were the heir's memories, the covenant's memories, the ones the rite itself might need like kindling needs a spark. Hers were — what? A dead mother she couldn't clearly picture anymore anyway. A father who'd sold her coat off her back one winter and vanished by spring. Alleys, hunger, a dozen names, none of them true. Cold nights and colder mornings and the particular gray taste of fear that lived at the back of the throat when you slept in doorways.

Take hers. Please. They weren't doing anything.

The thought arrived whole and calm, the way her best plans always did, and Nix sat with it on the rooftop until the lamps below burned steady, and by the time she climbed down she wasn't shaking anymore.

That was the trouble with resolutions made on rooftops. They always felt like mercy from up high.

* * *

She waited for the right moment, because even a gift given free deserved good timing. Not in front of Crow, who'd argue like a knife-fight and win. Not in front of Seraphine, whose new-defrocked presence still made Nix's fingers itch toward things that weren't hers. Just the two of them, then, on the last morning before they walked down to the gate, in the shrine's ruined antechamber where the tide came in through broken arches and filled the room with a sound like slow breathing.

Dominic was talking to a crab.

This was such an ordinary sight now that it took Nix a moment to remember it had ever been strange — Dominic crouched at the waterline, sleeves rolled, murmuring in that low clicking dialect the crustaceans favored, listening with his whole body tilted like a man leaning into wind. The crab, for its part, waved one claw in what looked suspiciously like emphasis.

"—understood," Dominic was saying. "West channel, past the third pillar. You've been very helpful." He reached into his pocket and produced — Nix squinted — half a biscuit, crumbling it into the shallows. "Payment as agreed."

"You bribe crabs now," Nix said, dropping onto a fallen pillar.

"I honor contracts," Dominic corrected, standing. "There's a difference. Also he found us a dry route to the gate chamber, so frankly I'd hire him over half the people in this city."

The laugh came easy, the way it had started coming easy these last months, and that was exactly the problem. Every easy moment between them was just another thing the gate would eat.

"Dominic." Her voice came out flatter than she meant. "About tomorrow. About the seal."

He turned, and she watched the lightness drain out of him by degrees, the way water leaves a lock. He'd known. Of course he'd known; she could see it in his face, the way you saw a pickpocket's knowledge of your own pocket. Everyone had known this conversation was coming.

"The seal takes memories," Nix said. "All of them. Everything the caster is."

"Yes."

"So it should be me."

The tide filled the pause. In-out. Slow breathing.

"Nix—"

"Think about it. Actually think, instead of going noble at me." She was up off the pillar now, pacing, hands moving because her hands always moved when the truth got too big for her mouth. "You're the last heir. Your memory is the whole inheritance — your mother, the order, the old tongue, all of it. If the gate takes yours, we lose the covenant's history forever. But mine?" She shrugged, and hated how casual she made it sound, and made it sound casual anyway because that was the trick, that had always been the trick, make the worthless thing look worthless so nobody fights you for it. "Mine's alleys and hunger and a bunch of names I invented. There's nothing in my head the world needs. It's the cheapest payment anyone's ever offered a god."

For a moment Dominic just looked at her, and Nix had spent her life reading faces, reading marks, reading danger, and she read his now and saw something that frightened her worse than anger.

He looked heartbroken. Not surprised. Heartbroken, like she'd confirmed something he'd suspected and hoped against.

"Nix," he said quietly. "That's not true."

"It's completely true—"

"Every word of it is a lie you tell yourself so well you've forgotten it's a lie." He stepped closer. Tide-light moved on his face. "Your memories are the only ones in this company nobody else can carry. I know my mother's face because Seraphine's heard me describe it. Crow knows the old tongue better than I do. But who else remembers what it was like, being nine years old and invisible in a city of ten thousand people? Who else knows every bolt-hole in the Saffron Quarter, every honest fence and every crooked constable? Who else—" his voice caught, and he pushed through it, "—who else taught me that family isn't something you're born into, it's something you steal, one person at a time, and refuse to give back?"

"Don't quote me at me," Nix snapped. "That's cheating."

"Then here's no quotation." He took her hands — both of them, stilled at last, trapped gentle in his own. "If the gate takes my memories, I lose a mother I mourned years ago. A childhood that hurts more than it heals. An order that failed the world. Grief, Nix. Mostly grief, and some wonder, and grief again. If it takes yours, we lose *you*. The person who laughed at Crow until he smiled and will deny it to his grave. The person who went back for me in the cells when every sane instinct must have screamed run. The person who—" he stopped, and his thumb moved once across her knuckles, and Nix realized with horror that her eyes were burning, "—the person I—"

"Don't," she whispered. "Don't say it tomorrow you won't remember saying."

"That's why I'm saying it today."

The tide breathed. Somewhere above them, gulls argued over something small and dead, and Nix thought: *that's us, that's exactly us, fighting over scraps of time.*

"No," she said, and pulled her hands free, and the pulling hurt worse than any cut she'd ever taken. "No, you don't get to make this noble. You don't get to decide your memories are trash so the rest of us feel better about feeding them to a rock." Her voice was climbing and she couldn't stop it. "I've watched my whole life, Dominic. Watched rich men throw coins at beggars so they didn't have to see them. Watched my mother die of a fever ten coppers' worth of physic would have cured, because her life was priced cheap by everyone including her. I swore I'd never be cheap. And now you're standing there pricing yourself at *nothing*, and you expect me to stand here and let you?"

"I expect you to let me choose my own price!"

"Well, I'm choosing mine first!" She was shouting now, and the gulls rose shrieking off the ruined roof, and the tide seemed to hold its breath. "I volunteered. First. That's the rule of the streets — first claim holds. You taught me promises, well, consider this one made: I'm casting the seal, and you can't stop me, and if you try I'll pick every pocket in your miserable saintly wardrobe and sell the lot."

"You wouldn't."

"I absolutely would."

They stood there, chests heaving, two feet of salt water and five months of found family between them, and Nix saw the exact moment Dominic understood she wasn't bluffing. Saw him arrive, reluctantly, painfully, at the same country she'd been living in since the rooftop.

"You really believe yours are worth less," he said. Not a question. An autopsy.

"I believe mine are worth less *to lose*."

"And I believe," Dominic said, very softly, in the voice he used on frightened animals, the voice that slipped under skin like a splinter, "that somewhere deep down you know that's the hungriest lie you've ever told. You know what you're actually buying with this trade. You're buying the belief that losing you costs nothing. And I am telling you, as the man who will wake up tomorrow without knowing your name—" his voice finally broke, all the way through, no repairing it, "—it will cost *everything*. Do you hear me? It will cost me everything, and I won't even know what I paid."

Nix turned away because he was crying and if she watched him cry she would break, and she had never once broken in front of anyone, and she was not going to start the day before the end of the world.

"The vote's tomorrow," she said to the broken arches. "Crow votes, Seraphine votes, you vote, I vote. Majority rules. That's the accord we wrote ourselves, and you don't get to break it just because you're the heir."

"That accord was for route decisions and ration shares."

"It's for whatever we say it's for."

"*Nix.*"

But she was already walking, fast, up the shingle and through the arches, and behind her the tide kept its slow patient breathing, in and out, like something enormous waiting to be fed.

* * *

She didn't go back that night. She told herself it was tactics — stake out the gate chamber alone, learn the ground, be ready — but the truth was uglier and she made herself look at it, because lying to herself was the one theft she'd never allowed.

She couldn't bear to watch him sleep. Because tomorrow one of them would forget the other, and she'd decided it would be him, and loving someone you were about to rob felt too much like every other crime she'd ever committed, except this time the stolen goods were a soul and the victim had held her hands while she planned it.

So she sat in the dark above the drowned gate, knees pulled up, and around her the sea creatures gathered without her asking — small silver fish in trembling schools, a blind white crab, an eel like a ribbon of ink. They couldn't speak to her. Her gift only ran the other way: she saw through their eyes when she dreamed, borrowed their seeing, never their words. But they gathered anyway, the way animals had been gathering near her all year, as if proximity to Dominic's people had taught them something about refuge.

*I'm doing the right thing,* she told them silently. *Tell him. One of you. Tell him I'm sorry.*

The eel uncoiled from its crack in the stone and flowed toward the camp below, toward the glow of Dominic's small fire, and Nix closed her eyes and slipped into its seeing — the dark water blooming into strange silver senses, pressure and electricity, the heat of a human body ahead.

Through the eel's eyes she watched him sitting awake at the fire, alone, turning something over in his hands. Her own charm, she realized. The cheap tin sparrow she'd sold him the first night they met, back when he was just the charm-seller and she was just the thief who'd robbed him and given everything back. He was holding it the way other men held relics.

And through the eel's skin she felt it then, faint but unmistakable, rising through the water from the direction of the drowned gate: a pull. Gentle, patient, hungry. Like a hook set soft in the current.

The god below already knew they were coming. And it already knew — she felt this too, a cold attention sliding over her borrowed senses, considering her, weighing her — it already knew what she intended to offer.

It approved.

Nix opened her eyes in the dark, heart slamming, and found the firelight below flickering — Dominic on his feet, staring out at the black water, and every creature around the camp gone suddenly, perfectly silent.

Above the drowned gate, far down in the green dark, something ancient began, very softly, to sing.
