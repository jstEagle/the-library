## 16. The Choir of Beasts

*Seraphine*

The bells of the Basilica of the Quiet Word had not rung in five years. Seraphine Vale knew this the way she knew her own heartbeat, because she had been the one to order them silenced — a young inquisitor, flush with her first promotion, standing before the archdeacon and swearing that silence was the truest form of worship now that the gods had stopped listening.

At dusk on the third day after her defrocking, the bells began to ring themselves.

She stood in the doorway of Dominic Almeida's safehouse — a candlemaker's storeroom above the Salt Lane, thick with tallow-smell and the nervous musk of too many animals in too small a space — and watched through the window as the sound rolled over Vellamare like a tide coming in wrong. The bronze throats of the bells were empty. She could see the towers from here, black against a sky the color of an old bruise, and there was no rope moving, no ringer's silhouette, nothing but the bells swinging against their own stillness, crying out a note that was almost joyful and entirely hungry.

"They're here," Crow said behind her. His voice was gravel dragged over grief. He sat on a crate of candle stubs with a raven hunched at his shoulder, its feathers slicked flat with dread. "All of them. What's left of all of them."

Seraphine turned. In the lamplight, Dominic Almeida looked smaller than he had any right to look. This was the man the Inquisition had hunted for months — the beast-devil, the heretic heir, the last speaker of the covenant — and he stood there with sawdust on his sleeves and a dog pressed trembling against his leg, and he looked like what he had probably always been underneath everything: a tired man who sold charms because it was kinder than selling truth.

"How long do we have?" he asked.

"Until the bells stop," Crow said. "When they stop ringing, they'll have finished drinking the city's edges. The outskirts first. The fishermen who pray without thinking. The old women who talk to their cats. Then they'll come inward, toward the gate." He looked up, and his eyes were terrible with certainty. "Toward you."

The dog against Dominic's leg made a sound Seraphine had never heard an animal make — low, deliberate, shaped almost like language. Dominic closed his eyes as though listening to something very far away.

"He says," Dominic translated quietly, "that they remember when the gods were real. And that if I'm going to do this thing, I should do it soon."

Outside, the bells rang on, and somewhere beyond them, out past the harbor bar, something vast turned over beneath the water and began, slowly, to sing back.

* * *

*Nix*

Nix had stolen from nobles and beggars, from priests and murderers, once — her proudest hour — from the Inquisition's own evidence vaults. She had thought herself immune to fear the way a pickpocket must be: fear slowed the hands, and slow hands lost fingers.

But nothing had prepared her for the birds.

She was on the rooftops doing what she always did when her chest got too tight — running the ridgelines of Vellamare like a thief runs a mark, reading the city below — when the sparrows came. Not flying past her. Not scattering. Coming *toward* her, hundreds of them, pouring along the gutters and eaves until the roofline around her seethed brown and gray, and every small dark eye fixed on her with an attention that felt like being read aloud.

"They want you to carry a message," said a voice at her elbow, and she nearly went off the roof.

Crow crouched beside the chimney stack, impossibly, having climbed six stories of sheer brick at an age when most men complained about stairs. The raven rode his shoulder like a second skull.

"I don't take messages," Nix snapped. Her heart was hammering against the cage of her ribs. "I *take* things. It's different."

"The message isn't for you." Crow nodded at the sparrows. At his nod, they did something Nix had never seen birds do in all her years of watching the city from above: they fell silent. All of them, all at once, a silence so complete she could hear the bells across the water and the wet slap of the harbor swell. "It's for him. They're offering. Every flock in Vellamare, every rat in the sewers, every fish under the pier. When the time comes, they'll lend him their voices for the rite. A speaker can't seal the gate alone — the covenant needs a chorus. But a chorus is a debt, girl, and debts get called."

Nix looked down at the sparrows again. They were so small. Their hearts would fit inside a thimble. And yet they had chosen — she understood it suddenly, the way she understood locks — they had *chosen*, each ridiculous feathered scrap of them, to stand on the losing side of a war against gods.

"Why?" she asked. Not about the message. About the choosing.

Crow was quiet a long moment. Below them, a god wearing the face of a drowned saint walked the length of the harbor mole, and where its bare feet touched the stone, gulls lifted into the air screaming and would not land again.

"Because," Crow said finally, "when the old covenant held, no beast was ever only meat. Every animal had a voice at the table of the world. The church took that from us, and then the gods went silent, and my order burned, and the beasts learned to be small and afraid." He rose, joints cracking. "They remember being large. That's why. Now go home, thief. Tell them the choir is gathering. And tell Dominic—" his voice caught on the name, roughened— "tell him the ravens will sing too. Even for me."

* * *

*Dominic*

He did not sleep that night. Sleep, he suspected, had left the city entirely; lights burned in every window along Salt Lane, and behind the shutters he could hear people talking to their animals — really talking, or trying to, the words clumsy and pleading. *Do you know what's happening? Are you frightened? Will you stay with me?*

At midnight, the rats came to bargain.

They poured up through the floorboards in a silver-brown river, and where other men would have screamed, Dominic knelt among them and opened his gift like a door. The rat-king of the eastern sewers — an enormous scarred female with half an ear gone — climbed onto his knee and spoke in the old tongue, the tongue Crow had beaten into him syllable by syllable over weeks of cold mornings.

*Speaker. The many-mouthed ones are moving. Three of the great remnants walk the streets already, wearing faces. One sits in the Basilica wearing the archdeacon. We have eaten well tonight — the dead they leave are full of memories, spilled like grain.*

"You've been eating what they spill?" Dominic asked, horrified.

*We are rats,* the rat-king said, with the terrible simplicity of her kind. *Waste is our sacrament. But hear the bargain, speaker. When you go down to the gate, the way is flooded. You cannot swim it carrying your own air. Our children know the drowned tunnels. Let them guide you, and when the singing starts, count us among the choir.*

"And your price?"

The rat-king's whiskers twitched. *A promise. When the new covenant comes — if it comes — that no trap be set for us again. No poison in the granaries. No bounty on our tails. Let the small ones have a seat at the table, or let the table burn.*

Dominic thought of his mother's hands, which he could barely picture anymore. He thought of Nix arguing with him about the price of the seal until three in the morning. He thought of Seraphine scrubbing Inquisition ink from her forearms in the washbasin, staring at her bare skin like a stranger's.

"All right," he said. "No poison. No bounty. A seat at the table." He extended his hand, palm down, and the rat-king placed her scarred paw upon it, and the bargain sealed itself somewhere in the marrow of the world.

The dog — Brindle, the stray from the night market, the one who had started everything with two words, *the door is open* — leaned against his side and spoke in the low voice only Dominic heard:

*"You're giving away promises like bread. What will be left of you?"*

Dominic scratched the grizzled ruff behind the dog's ears. Outside, the bells were slowing. Each peal now came further apart, deeper, as though some vast throat were swallowing between notes.

"What's left of any of us," he said, "is what other people remember. So I'm spending it while I'm still me."

Brindle whined, laid his heavy head on Dominic's boot, and kept watch.

* * *

*Seraphine*

In the hour before dawn, Seraphine walked out to meet a god.

She told herself it was reconnaissance. She was still an inquisitor in her bones, whatever they'd branded into her skin; you gathered intelligence on the enemy, you catalogued its habits, its weaknesses, the pattern of its predations. She told herself this as she crossed the Fishmarket square, empty except for the stalls abandoned mid-sale, a fish still gasping on a cutting board, coins scattered where buyers had dropped them and run.

The remnant wore the face of Saint Anselm of the Tides, patron of safe passage. Seraphine had prayed to that face every morning of her childhood. It stood at the edge of the square where the fountain used to be — the fountain was gone, simply gone, stone and water and the memory of both, replaced by a shallow bowl of something that gleamed like mercury and moved like breath — and it turned to regard her with eyes that were the exact blue of the icons, and utterly empty behind.

"Inquisitor," it said. Its voice was her childhood prayers recited backward. "You were ours. The brand says so. The brand is a door."

"I'm defrocked," she said. Her hand found the knife at her belt anyway. Old habits. "Whatever I was, I unbound myself. Ask your records."

"We keep no records." The god smiled, and the smile was worse than anything, because it was *kind*. "We keep memories. Yours are rich, little hunter. Your mother teaching you the catechism in a kitchen that smelled of cloves. The first heretic you burned and how part of you sang. The hound, when he chose you, and how it broke something open that you'd spent thirty years welding shut." It drifted closer; the mercury bowl rippled. "Come back to us and we will give you all of it, polished, eternal. Or stand against us, and when the gate opens, we will take it anyway — slowly, while you watch yourself forget."

Somewhere above, a crow screamed — Crow's raven, wheeling over the rooftops — and the sound cracked the moment like ice cracking.

Seraphine did not run. She wanted to; every nerve shrieked for it. Instead she looked the god full in its borrowed face and said the truest thing she had said in years:

"You aren't them. Whoever they were, the ones people actually prayed to — you aren't them. You're what's left when something holy rots. And I have spent my whole life serving rot and calling it faith." She drew the knife, not to fight but to cut her own palm, and held the bleeding fist up like a lantern. "I choose the other side. Write that in your memory-eating heart."

The god regarded her for a long moment. Then it laughed — a sound like a congregation sighing — and dissolved into the mercury bowl, and the bowl sank into the stones, and the stones forgot it had been there.

But Seraphine remembered. That was the point. That was all the point there was.

* * *

*Dominic*

Dawn came the color of an open wound, and with it came the choir.

He was standing in the candlemaker's yard with Nix on one side and Seraphine on the other and Crow chanting the opening measures of the rite in a voice like dry leaves, when the animals of Vellamare turned to look at him.

There was no other word for it. He felt it happen — felt it as a pressure, a turning, ten thousand small attentions pivoting toward him at once. Sparrows on a thousand roofs. Cats in windowsills, going still mid-wash. Oxen in their yokes raising their heads. The horses of the Inquisition stables stamping and straining toward the south. Rats lining the gutter-tops in ranks like soldiers. Gulls rising off the harbor in spiraling columns, and below them, beneath the grey water, the pale flicker of fish turning in uncountable schools, all of them facing shore, all of them facing *him*.

"Oh," Nix breathed beside him. Her pickpocket's hands hung open at her sides, empty, holding nothing. "Oh, Dom, look at them."

*I see them,* he started to say, and then the voices began.

Not aloud. Not in words, mostly. It came up through his gift like floodwater through a cellar grate: every beast in the city speaking at once, and the old tongue catching all of it, braiding it, making music of it. The mice offered their quick high tremble of a song. The oxen offered a note deep enough to feel in the teeth. The cats offered something sly and ancient that predated the city, maybe predated the gods. The dogs offered loyalty, plain and blazing. The rats offered cunning, and the crows offered memory, and the gulls offered the long loneliness of the open sea, and the fish offered the silence at the bottom of the world.

And beneath it, threading through it, the smallest voice of all — Brindle, pressed warm against his knee — saying over and over, like a heartbeat under the symphony: *we chose you, we chose you, we chose you.*

Tears ran down Dominic's face and he did not wipe them away. Terror and awe had become the same sensation in his chest, indistinguishable, a bell and a wound struck together. These creatures owed him nothing. Half of them he had never spoken to. And they were laying their voices at his feet like coins, knowing — because Crow had warned them, because the rats had spread the word through the walls of the world — that the rite might unmake the one who cast it.

"The gate is waking," Crow said. His chant had stopped; the raven on his shoulder had spread both wings wide, trembling. Far off across the water, the sea was bulging upward in a slow dome, and light was leaking from beneath it, gold and wrong. "Now, boy. Now, before they finish feeding. Take the choir and go down to the drowned shrine, or everything these small ones offered dies with the city."

Dominic wiped his face with the back of his hand. He looked at Nix, who was already checking her knives. At Seraphine, rolling her shoulders like a woman stepping back into a burning building she'd sworn never to enter again. At Crow, whose eyes said *forgive me for what the lessons cost you* in the old tongue of mentors everywhere.

"The door is open," Dominic said, and it wasn't the god's phrase anymore; it belonged to a stray dog and a charm-seller on a night five years of silence ago. "Then let's go close it."

They went down through the waking streets together, and the choir of beasts sang above and below and around them, every small voice in Vellamare choosing sides, following the last beast-speaker toward the water — where the dome of light was splitting open like an eye, and something older than the city was rising to meet them, wearing the faces of everyone they had ever loved and lost.
