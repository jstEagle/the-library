## 17. Covenant, Broken and Remade

The sea held its breath.

Dominic stood on the threshold of the old gate — a ring of black stone half-buried in silt beneath the drowned shrine, wide as a cathedral door, carved over every inch with the shapes of beasts that no longer walked the world. The water here was strange: not cold, not warm, but *attentive*, pressing against his skin like a crowd leaning in to listen. Above him, filtered through forty feet of green-dark sea, the surface shimmered like a ceiling of hammered silver, and beyond it he could just make out the hulls of the fishing boats Seraphine had borrowed, and beyond those, far above, the lights of Vellamare burning against the coming storm.

The choir had come down with him.

That was the only word for what waited in the flooded shrine around the gate. Gulls hung suspended in the water as though it were air, wings spread, unbothered by drowning. A school of silver fish turned in slow constellations. Rats — dozens of them, the rat-kings of the undercity and their whole swarming courts — clung to the broken columns with their whiskers trembling. Eels threaded through the doorway of the ruined sacristy. Somewhere in the dark beyond the lamplight, something enormous breathed, slow and patient, and Crow said it was an old whale who remembered when the gate was built, or else the current itself grown old enough to have opinions.

Every animal in Vellamare had turned toward Dominic at moonrise. He had felt it happen — felt ten thousand small minds swing toward him like compass needles finding north — and then they had come, flowing through the streets in rivers, pouring down into the sea after him without hesitation, without fear, offering their voices for whatever came next.

He did not know if he deserved that. He knew it did not matter whether he deserved it.

"You're stalling," Nix said.

She floated beside him, her stolen-lantern light cupped in one hand, her hair drifting around her face like ink spilled upward. Her eyes were red-rimmed. They had fought on the boat ride out — fought hard, the worst words either of them owned flung like knives — and neither of them had apologized, because apologizing would mean admitting the other might be right. She still believed her memories were worth least. He still refused to let her believe it.

"I'm not stalling," Dominic said. "I'm memorizing."

The lie of it sat strangely in his chest. Because it wasn't entirely a lie. He was looking at their faces — Nix's sharp, defiant face; Crow's weathered scowl; Seraphine's jaw set like a woman walking into her own execution and daring the headsman to hurry; even the hound, Bristle, treading water beside her mistress, ears pricked. If the rite worked, he would pay for the sealing with everything he was. Every memory. Every name. And some stubborn, superstitious corner of him wanted to look at them now, while he still knew why they mattered.

Crow drifted closer, his grey braid floating behind him like a rope trailing from a moored boat. The old corvid-speaker had a crow perched on each shoulder — his exiles, returned to him on the pilgrimage road, and he had wept when they came, though he'd deny it until the sea dried up.

"Last chance to turn back," Crow said. "Not that there's anywhere to turn back *to*. But I'm told the ritual requires the offer be freely made."

"It's freely made."

"Say it properly." Crow's voice was gravel and salt. "In the old tongue, boy. Words are promises. You know this."

Dominic closed his eyes. He had learned the old tongue of beasts across a winter of cruel lessons — lessons Crow had designed to break him, because grief teaches discipline or nothing does. He let the language rise in him now, that speech that was less sound than meaning, that lived somewhere below the throat:

*I am Dominic Almeida, son of Vellamare. Heir of the beast-speakers. I offer my memory entire as payment for the seal. Freely given. Fully paid.*

The gate answered.

It did not open. It *woke*. The black stone began to glow along its carvings — faint gold light rising in the grooves where ancient chisels had traced wolf and whale and wren — and the water around it shivered, and every beast in the shrine went silent at once, a silence so total Dominic could hear his own heart stumbling.

And then the gods arrived.

* * *

They came the way weather comes.

First the pressure changed — a vast attention settling over the drowned shrine like a hand closing over a candle flame. Then the water darkened at the edges of the lamplight, and shapes assembled themselves out of the gloom: figures too tall for the space they occupied, folded impossibly into the ruin like giants kneeling in a child's bedroom. They wore faces. That was the horror of them. Faces lifted from legend, from the temple friezes, from the prayers Seraphine had once taught congregations to sing — but wrong, all of them wrong, stretched or hollowed or smiling a few degrees too wide, the way a mask is a face the way a corpse is a sleeper.

The God of Small Hungers stood foremost, and it had kept the shape it had shown Dominic on the night it first spoke to him: a kindly figure with a healer's hands and eyes like wells. It had offered him back his childhood once before. It was here to collect on its own generosity.

*Little speaker,* it said, and its voice did not come through the water so much as through the bones of the water, through the stone, through the marrow in Dominic's teeth. *You called us. We answer. That is what gods do.*

"I didn't call you," Dominic said aloud, and the beasts carried his voice for him — gulls crying it, fish mouthing it, the great thing in the deep rumbling it — so that it rang from every direction at once. "I called the covenant. You're what's left when the covenant starves. You're the hunger wearing the name."

*Names,* said another of them, and this voice was sweeter, a woman's voice, a mother's cadence, and Dominic's whole body flinched. *Are just memories worn like clothes. And we have gathered so many clothes, little heir. So many years. Would you like to see what your city has been feeding us?*

"No," Seraphine breathed beside him. She had her blade drawn, useless as a toothpick against the deep, and she held it anyway. Former inquisitor, defrocked heretic, and still her first instinct was to stand between the faithful and the thing that preyed on them. "*Don't listen—*"

The gods opened their hands.

Or — there were no hands. There was a gesture, an unfolding, a door swinging inward, and then the memories came.

They came like floodwater.

There was no other word for it. One moment the shrine held only seawater and lamplight; the next it held *everything* — the stolen year of Salt Row, the congregation's names, the fisherman's dead wife, the childhoods of strangers, decades and centuries, a tide of other people's lives crashing down onto the gate and through it and into the rite, and Dominic, standing at the center of the seal, took it all.

He heard himself scream. It sounded very far away.

Memory poured into him the way the sea pours into a cracked hull. Not images, not yet — first it was pure weight, pressure, the sheer tonnage of ten thousand lives, and he understood with a clarity that cut like ice why the old speakers had needed a whole order for this, a hundred trained minds sharing the burden, because no single soul was built to hold a city's remembering.

But the order was gone. There was only him. Him, and the voices.

The beasts sang.

It began as a hum in his blood and rose to fill the drowned shrine — gull-song and rat-chitter and the long bell-note of the whale in the dark, the eels' whisper and the fish-school's shimmer, thousands of throats joining the rite, taking pieces of the flood and carrying them, distributing the weight the way the old covenant had always been meant to work. Not one vessel. A thousand. The animals had not come to watch him. They had come to hold him up.

*Steady,* Crow's voice said, inside the roar, inside his skull, carried on a crow's wing-beat. *Breathe, boy. You don't drink the sea. You give it a channel.*

Dominic gave it a channel.

He stopped fighting the flood and let it pass through him, through the gate, and as it passed he began to *see* — because the memories could not help showing themselves, could not help being what they were. He saw a baker kneading dough in a kitchen that no longer existed, humming a song whose words had been eaten from everyone who ever loved it. He saw a wedding on the Salt Row steps, the bride's face bright, her name dissolving even as the guests toasted it. He saw a boy chasing a hoop down an alley, and the alley, and the hoop, and eventually the boy himself, all of them worn away to smooth nothing by the patient appetite of things that answered prayers.

Grief came with it. Grief came like the cold of the deep, everywhere at once, and he let it come, because the rite required truth and the truth was that this was unbearable and he was bearing it.

Then the flood changed.

*Ah,* said the God of Small Hungers, and its well-deep eyes fixed on him with something like tenderness, which was the cruelest thing it could have done. *You've found the deepest vein. Shall we show you what you paid us five years ago?*

And his mother's face came home.

* * *

It arrived mid-casting, in the middle of the roar, between one carried memory and the next, and it nearly destroyed him.

He had forgotten her. That was the price he'd already paid, at fifteen, when the last of the beast-speakers had sealed the small gates and taken from him everything that made the gift dangerous — his childhood, his mother's voice, the reason he flinched at the smell of bread baking, the reason he woke some mornings with wet eyes and no knowledge of why. Five years of a hole shaped like a woman, and he had learned to walk around it the way you learn to favor an injured leg.

Now she filled it.

She was standing in a kitchen with steam on the windows, laughing at something — at *him*, he realized, at something small-Dominic had said, some joke lost even now in the flood — and she had flour on her cheekbone and her sleeves were rolled up and she pushed hair out of her face with the back of her wrist the way he pushed hair out of his own face, the gesture identical, inherited, and Dominic made a sound underwater that was not a word in any tongue, human or beast.

*Mama.*

The memory did not stop. It unfolded — the kitchen became a street, the street became a market, the market became a harbor morning, she was waving from a boat, she was teaching him the name of a gull, she was —

*Dominic.* His own name, in her voice. He hadn't known how it was supposed to sound. Now he would never unknow it.

*You can keep this,* the god said gently, folding itself down closer, its kind hands opening, and around them the rite roared and the beasts strained against the weight of the flood and Nix was shouting something — his name, his name, *Dominic* — and Seraphine's blade-arm trembled with the effort of not charging a god. *This is yours. We only kept it safe. Stay your hand. Let the gate stay open. Let the world remember again — every prayer answered, every dead wife restored to every fisherman, every lost year returned — and you will have her back. All of her. Breakfast and bedtime and the flour on her cheek. We ask so little in return. Only the door. Only the way between.*

The memory washed through him again, and again, wave after wave, his mother laughing, his mother singing, his mother saying his name, and it would have been so easy.

That was the thought that saved him — arriving cold and clear through the drowning sweetness: *it is easy, and everything easy about it is a lie.* He saw the seams now, looking with the old tongue's sight, the sight Crow had beaten into him: the memory was real, but the promise was rot. The gods could not restore anything. They could only spend what they had stolen, and spending was eating, and every gift they gave dug the gate wider and widened the hunger with it. His mother's laughter, returned, would cost another street its year. Another thousand names. The flood would not stop until Vellamare stood empty-eyed on its hills, smiling at a kindness that had eaten it alive.

His mother's face was not an offering. It was bait on a hook the size of a world.

"I know what you are," Dominic said, and the beasts said it with him, ten thousand voices, and the water shook.

He looked at the memory of his mother one last time — flour on her cheekbone, his name in her mouth — and he began, deliberately, deliberately, to let it go.

It tore. Nothing in the rite had hurt like that. Not the flood's weight, not the gods' pressure, not the cold of the deep. He let her go the way you let go of a hand at a station window, the way you let go of anything you love when love requires it: not because the grip loosens, but because you pry your own fingers open one by one.

The god's smile finally faltered.

*Foolish little heir. You could have—*

"Heir," Dominic said, "and speaker, and seal."

He reached for the gate.

* * *

The black stone met his palms and knew him. Light climbed his arms — gold, then white, then a color he had no name for, a color like the moment before dawn when every bird in the world goes quiet — and the carvings blazed, wolf and whale and wren, and the covenant's old architecture woke in his blood like a language he'd spoken before he was born.

The words rose in him. Not learned. Remembered — or rather, waiting, planted in him by every inheritance he'd never asked for, and he understood suddenly that this was why he had been born the last heir: not to be special, but simply to be present, a key that happened to be lying near the lock.

He began to speak the sealing.

Each word was a promise, and each promise had a price, and the price was collected instantly. He spoke the word for *closed*, and forgot the smell of the charm-seller's stall where he'd spent five years hiding in plain sight. He spoke the word for *fast*, and forgot the name of the first rat-king he'd ever bargained with. He spoke the word for *whole*, and watched, from a strange calm distance, entire rooms of his life go dark — a schoolroom, a rooftop, a summer, a friendship, a funeral — extinguished like lamps down a corridor, each one going out with a small soft sound only he could hear.

Nix was screaming at him. He could see her mouth moving. He could no longer make out the words.

*Hold the line,* Crow sent, grim and steady, and the crows on his shoulders were screaming too, screaming in the old tongue, holding the channel open as the flood tried to drown the rite in raw memory — his mother's face one final time, offered desperately, furiously, the god spending everything it had—

And the beasts rose.

The whole choir surged forward — gulls and rats and eels and the immense patience of the whale — and placed themselves between Dominic and the gods, a wall of small bodies and older loyalties, absorbing the flood, buying him seconds, seconds, seconds.

Seraphine drove her blade into the stone beside the gate and gripped it like a pilgrim. Bristle pressed her flank against Seraphine's legs and growled at heaven.

Dominic spoke the last three words.

The first two he spent gladly. The third — the third contained everything remaining, every memory the rite had not yet claimed, and as it left him he saw, in one final flash, the faces arrayed around the gate: Nix with her lantern and her fury; Crow with his crows; Seraphine with her broken faith held together by will alone; Bristle's dark eyes; and behind them all, patient, mud-brown, impossible, the stray dog from the night market who had started everything, watching him with an expression that looked, absurdly, like forgiveness in advance.

*Who are they?* was the last coherent thought he had, looking at those weeping strangers. *Why are they crying?*

The gate closed.

Light went out like a snuffed candle. The gods shrieked — a sound that was not sound, a tearing in the fabric of the water — and unraveled, their stolen centuries ripped away from them, their masks collapsing into hungry, formless dark that drained backward through the seam of the world even as the seam sealed, and sealed, and was stone again, ordinary black stone, dumb and cold and forever shut.

The sea rushed in, plain and salt and indifferent.

Dominic Almeida opened his eyes in green darkness, surrounded by strangers who were reaching for him, and did not know a single one of their faces.

Above him, far above, through forty feet of ocean, the lights of Vellamare burned on — and he could not remember why that should matter to him, or why his chest ached like a house with every lamp lit and no one home.

A brown dog swam to him through the dark and pressed its head against his hand.

He let it. It seemed friendly enough.

He just wished someone would tell him who he was.
