## 19. A Stranger in His Own Life

The first thing Dominic knew was salt.

It lay on his tongue like a coin left there overnight, and beneath it the sweetness of beeswax and old smoke — a room he did not know, breathing around him. He opened his eyes to whitewashed beams and a window hung with fishing nets gone soft with age, light coming through them in diamonds. The sea spoke somewhere below the floorboards, patient as a heartbeat.

He lay still and took inventory of himself the way one takes inventory after a theft.

His body was his own. That much he could confirm: hands callused in familiar places, the ache in his left knee that came from crouching over a market stall for years, a scar on his thumb from a carving knife. His body remembered him even if nothing else did. But when he reached backward through time for himself — for the boy who had become this man — his fingers closed on water. There had been a mother. He knew that the way you know a room has a window without turning your head; he knew it by its absence, by the cold draft where her face should have been. He knew he had loved someone once, or several someones, because grief sat in his chest like furniture too heavy to move, and no grief comes from nowhere.

But the names were gone. All of them. Every name but his own, and even that felt like a coat handed back to him by a stranger — *Dominic*, yes, that fit across the shoulders, but who had given it to him? What had it meant?

"You're awake."

The voice came from the doorway, and he turned his head and saw three people looking at him the way people look at a shipwreck survivor washed up on their shore — with relief and terror tangled together, joy wearing mourning clothes.

A woman stood closest, tall and straight-backed despite the exhaustion carved into her face. Her hair was iron-grey at the temples though she could not have been past forty, and she wore the plain dark traveling clothes of someone who had recently stopped wearing something else. An Inquisitor's coat, maybe. He didn't know how he knew that. He didn't know most things. But her eyes were red-rimmed, and they fixed on him with an intensity that made his skin prickle — the look of someone seeing a ghost, or being seen by one.

Behind her, a slight figure perched on the windowsill like the room belonged to her more than to anyone, which perhaps it did. A young woman, sharp-faced, quick-eyed, with a thief's economy of motion and feathers braided into her hair — gull feathers, tern, one long black one that might have come from a crow. She wasn't crying. She was gripping the sill hard enough that her knuckles had gone white, and her jaw was set in the particular way of someone who has decided, fiercely and in advance, not to cry.

And standing just inside the door, half in shadow, an old man with a crow perched on his shoulder like a second, darker self. His eyes were the color of storm water and about as warm. When he looked at Dominic, he looked at him *hard*, the way a teacher looks at a student returned from somewhere no one returns from.

"Hello," said the tall woman, and her voice cracked on the single syllable. She cleared her throat. "I'm Seraphine. This is Nix. And Crow. We—" She stopped. Started again. "You probably don't know us."

"No," Dominic said. His voice was rust. "I don't know anything. I know my name is Dominic Almeida. I know I sold charms in Vellamare's night market." He paused. "I know I've forgotten something enormous. It has a shape. I can feel it pressing against the inside of my skull."

The one called Nix made a sound — small, bitten off, like a laugh and a sob fighting to a draw.

"Then we'll tell you," said the old man, Crow, and his crow said something in reply, low and liquid, in a language Dominic almost understood. Almost. The words slid past him like fish under ice, and the loss of them hurt worse than the rest somehow — like reaching for a doorknob and finding the door had been bricked over while he slept.

* * *

They told it slowly, that first day, sitting around him in a circle like physicians taking turns at a bedside.

Seraphine told the parts that were hers to tell: the Inquisition, the hunt, the hound. "I was sent to find you," she said, and her hands were folded in her lap like a penitent's. "I cornered you beneath the cells and you unbound my hound rather than let me take you. And Balthasar — the hound — he chose me over the church anyway. Loyalty can't be commanded. You taught me that before you ever trusted me." She smiled, and it was a broken, beautiful thing. "I branded you a devil in my reports and a friend in everything else. I lost my faith in a chapel full of stolen names, and I found something sturdier to hold onto instead. It looks a great deal like you."

Nix told hers differently — fast, sideways, with her feet drawn up on the chair and her chin on her knees. "I robbed you," she announced, with the pride of a confession. "First thing I ever did to you. Took your purse right out of your coat at the night market, and then I dreamed your memories without asking, because that's what I do, I see through animals' eyes when I sleep, and yours were —" she faltered, the first crack in the bravado, "— yours were loud. Full of loneliness. So I gave it all back. Every copper. And then I wouldn't leave." She shrugged, elaborate and defensive. "You fed me. Nobody fed me. You taught me that gifts aren't traps." Her voice dropped. "You called me family first. Before I'd earned it. Before I believed it. You just — decided. Like it was obvious."

Crow told his part last, and shortest, and hardest. "I tested you cruelly because cruelty is the only teacher I trust," he said. "I taught you the old tongue. First lesson: every word spoken to an animal is a promise." The crow on his shoulder ruffled its feathers and croaked, and Crow translated, or perhaps didn't: "*He says you kept every promise. Even the last one. Especially the last one.*"

"And what was the last one?" Dominic asked.

The room went quiet. Out the window, gulls wheeled over the harbor, and every one of their cries sounded, to his ruined ears, like a word he used to know.

"The gate," Seraphine said gently. "We'll get to the gate. Not today. Today you eat something, and sleep, and learn the shape of our faces. Memory isn't rebuilt in a day, Dominic. It's rebuilt the way a wall is rebuilt — stone by stone, by hands that remember what the wall looked like."

"I'm the wall," he said.

"You're the wall," Nix agreed, and grinned at him through wet lashes, fierce as a knife. "And we're the masons. Lucky for you, we're very good with our hands."

* * *

The days took on a rhythm, and the rhythm was a kind of rope thrown into dark water.

Each morning Seraphine brought tea and began where they had left off, patient as tide. Each afternoon Nix dragged him out into the world — down to the harbor, through the streets, pointing at buildings like a tour guide of his own life. *"That's where you bought the honey for the cough-charms. That's the alley where the rat-kings held court — yes, actual rats, actual kings, stop laughing, it worked."* Each evening Crow drilled him in the beast-tongue, and each evening Dominic failed, and each evening Crow said only, *"Again,"* until the word stopped sounding like judgment and started sounding like faith.

The animals confirmed everything.

That was the strangest mercy of it. When Nix told a story — the night the market burned, how she'd been captured covering his escape, how he'd traded secrets with rat-kings to find her — the sparrows in the eaves would chirp agreement, and a dog would thump its tail, and once a whole flock of pigeons lifted off a rooftop in what Crow swore was applause. The city itself vouched for him. He walked through Vellamare like a man walking through a biography written by strangers, and everywhere he went, small creatures turned to watch him pass with an attention that was nearly reverence.

"They remember," Crow said, watching a cat sit solemnly outside Dominic's window like a sentry. "Every promise you ever made them, every bargain, every kindness. Beasts keep accounts older than any church. You are written into this city's smallest ledgers, boy. The forgetting lives only in you."

"It doesn't feel like forgetting," Dominic said. "Forgetting is empty. This is — furnished. Full of shapes with the names rubbed off." He pressed his hand flat against his sternum. "There was a dog. Early on. Before any of you. He said the door was open, and I followed him, and—" The shape slipped away from him again, the way a dream slips away in the telling. He closed his eyes. "Tell me about him."

So Nix told him about the stray dog from the night market — how he'd begged Dominic for help in words, the first animal speech the city had heard in five silent years, how he'd stayed through everything afterward, through fire and cells and the long road to the drowned gate. How he was downstairs right now, actually, refusing to be anywhere else, lying against the door like a paperweight holding down a page.

Dominic went down and knelt in front of him. The dog — grey-muzzled, scarred, patient beyond all reason — lifted his head and looked at Dominic with eyes that held no disappointment whatsoever, which broke Dominic open worse than reproach ever could.

"I'm sorry," Dominic whispered. "I don't know you."

The dog leaned forward and put his head, briefly and gravely, against Dominic's chest — the way one greets an old friend, or forgives one — and then lay back down, settling in for the long wait, because dogs understand better than people that love does not require recognition. It only requires staying.

* * *

On the ninth day, the memories started coming back wrong.

Not his own — those stayed sealed behind whatever price he had paid. These arrived like letters addressed to other people delivered to his house. He woke gasping from a dream of a drowned shrine, of a prayer answered aloud through the tide, and it took Seraphine an hour to convince him the vision belonged to a fisherman whose dead wife had spoken back to him, a story Seraphine herself had witnessed, a story that had been *told to him*, and his sleeping mind had dressed it in his own flesh. Other times he'd reach for a memory of Nix laughing and find only the version of that moment she had described aloud — secondhand, hearsay, a copy of a copy. His own past was a locked room, but the hallway outside it filled daily with everyone else's accounts, and sometimes he couldn't tell which was which.

"I'm becoming a story," he said to Crow that night, staring into the hearth. "Is that what I am now? A tale you're all agreeing on?"

Crow considered this longer than a kind man would have, which was why Dominic trusted him.

"When I exiled myself from the order," Crow said finally, "I burned my bestiary. Every page. Forty years of work. I thought I was destroying it." He turned his hands palm-up, scarred and spotted with age. "My students had memorized it. All of it. They recited it back to me over twenty years, correcting each other, arguing over passages. The book exists now only because they hold it between them. It's better than it was. More alive. A book in one mind is a corpse in a box, boy. A book in many minds is a living thing." He looked at Dominic with those storm-water eyes. "You are not becoming a story. You are becoming a *shared* story. There is a difference, and the difference is everything."

"But I'll never be whole."

"No." Crow said it plainly, without pity. "You will be something else. Wholeness is a myth told by people who haven't lost enough yet. What you'll be is *held*. Held by everyone who carries a piece of you. That's heavier work than wholeness. It's also worth more." The crow shifted on his shoulder and croaked, and this time Dominic caught a fragment of it — two words of the old tongue, *promise-kept*, *promise-kept* — before the meaning sank back under the ice.

* * *

On the twelfth night, Nix climbed through his window instead of using the door, because some habits are load-bearing.

She sat on the sill with her knees up and didn't speak for a long time, and Dominic had learned enough by now to let silence do its work. Below them the harbor glittered. Somewhere in the dark, the night market was opening — brighter now, lawful now, magic spoken openly between stalls under the new accord, though that story hadn't been told to him yet in full. He could hear it faintly: music, laughter, a vendor calling wares, and underneath it all, so faint it might have been imagination, a thousand small voices chattering in the old tongue, the whole hidden city of beasts talking among themselves now that talking was allowed.

"Do you want to know the worst part?" Nix said at last.

"Probably."

"You forgot me on purpose. You chose it. You stood at the gate with your whole life offered back to you — your mother's face, your childhood, everything they'd eaten out of you — and you spent it instead. On us. On closing the door." Her voice was steady, which is how he knew it cost her. "I volunteered first, you know. For the seal. I thought my memories were worth least." She laughed, short and bitter. "You fought me. Actually fought me, in the mud, like children. Said nobody gets to decide they're worth less. And then you paid yourself."

"Nix." He reached for something to say and found only honesty. "I don't remember doing it. I don't remember you. I can't feel whatever I felt when I chose."

"I know." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, angry at them. "That's the worst part, like I said. But here's the thing." She turned on the sill to face him fully, and in the lamplight her eyes were bright and absolutely certain. "I remember it for both of us. And I'm going to tell it to you every day if I have to. And someday you won't remember it — but you'll *know* it, the way you know a song you can't place, the way your body knows the market stall and the bad knee and the way to talk to rats. Because love isn't memory, Dominic. Love is what's left when memory goes. You taught me that too. You just don't remember teaching me."

"That's very unfair," Dominic said, and found he was smiling. "Using all my own lessons against me."

"I learned from the best," Nix said. "Whoever he was."

* * *

Later — deep in the blue hour before dawn, when the house breathed slow around him — Dominic went downstairs alone.

The dog raised his head. Dominic settled beside him on the cold floor, shoulder to shoulder, and looked out the window at the harbor, at the drowned shrine's distant rocks, at the sea that held the sealed gate and everything he had bought its closing with.

He tried, as he did every night, to reach backward. To find the seam. And as always, he found the locked room, the hollow years, the grief-shaped furniture with no names attached.

But tonight — for the first time — the hollow did not feel like absence.

It felt like a cup. Emptied, yes. But shaped exactly to be filled again, by voices, by stories, by three people upstairs who had already begun telling him who he was, and by a city of small creatures who kept his promises in their oldest ledgers, and by a dog who pressed his warm flank against Dominic's side and asked for nothing except morning.

Somewhere out over the water, a gull cried once — and in the old tongue, in words he almost understood, it sounded less like a bird and more like a bell.

Tomorrow, he thought, they would tell him about the gate. About the accord. About the crown of woven feathers and bone charms he'd heard whispers of, waiting somewhere for reasons no one would explain.

Tomorrow the telling would begin again. Stone by stone. Hands that remembered.

He leaned his head against the dog's shoulder, and waited for morning like a page waits for ink.
