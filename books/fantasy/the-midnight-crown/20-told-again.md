## 20. Told Again

*Nix*

The dog came back on the third morning.

Nix saw it first from the window of the salt-stained house they'd rented above the chandler's shop — a lean brown thing with one torn ear, sitting in the street below with the patience of a creature that had decided where it belonged and would not be argued out of it. It looked up at her window. Not at the door. At her, specifically, as if to say: *I know why you're all here. Let me up.*

"Dom," she called over her shoulder, and then stopped, because that was still a strange word to say to him. He turned from the hearth where he was burning porridge for the fourth day running, and he looked at her the way he always did now — open, attentive, and utterly unburdened by recognition. A stranger's face wearing a face she knew.

"The dog's back," she said.

His whole body changed. She'd learned to watch for it — the way his shoulders dropped half an inch, the way something behind his eyes went quiet and glad. He didn't remember the animal. But his body remembered, the way his hands remembered knots he couldn't name and his feet remembered the route to the drowned shrine without anyone pointing the way.

"That's good," he said. "That's — I dreamed about a dog last night. Brown. One ear folded wrong."

"You dream about it every night," Nix said. "Crow says dreams are just memories looking for a loose board."

She went down and opened the door, and the dog walked past her like an invited guest, crossed the room, and laid its head on Dominic's knee as if it were resuming a conversation interrupted years ago instead of weeks. Dominic's hand found its ears. His eyes went bright and bewildered.

"I know you," he whispered. "Don't I? I know you."

The dog made a sound low in its chest, almost speech, and Nix felt her gift stir — that old borrowed-eyes feeling, the edges of the animal's mind brushing hers. What came through wasn't words. It was simpler and worse: *yes*, and *stay*, and a memory-shaped warmth of a night market and a boy crouched in an alley sharing bread.

"He says yes," she said, before she could stop herself. "He says stay."

Dominic looked up at her, and there it was — the question underneath all his questions, the one none of them could answer honestly without breaking something: *Then why don't I remember you?*

"We're going to fix that," Nix said. She sat down across from him, cross-legged, thief-style, ready to work. "Starting today. We're going to tell you everything. And when we run out of things to tell you—" she jerked her chin at the dog, at the gulls wheeling past the window, at the rats she could feel moving in the walls like small gray clerks, "—they'll fill in what we miss. They remember too. Turns out you're famous, Almeida. The animals kept receipts."

* * *

*Seraphine*

Seraphine Vale had interrogated hundreds of souls in her years beneath the Inquisition, and she knew precisely how much truth a person could absorb before it curdled into horror. So she set rules for herself, rules she wrote in her own hand and pinned above the little desk she'd claimed in the corner of the chandler's house:

*One memory per telling. No more. Let him ask for the next.*

*Never describe his mother's face. Let the animals do it, if it must be done.*

*Stop when his hands shake.*

She was not good at following them. She had never been good at mercy; she'd been trained in certainty instead, and certainty was useless now. Every morning she climbed the stairs with her ledger of their shared history — she was the only one among them who'd thought to write things down, in the old inquisitor habit, dates and names and testimonies — and every morning Dominic received her like a guest whose name he kept forgetting, courteous and hungry.

Today she began where the record began. Or near enough.

"Five years ago," she said, "you sold charms in the night market on Tallow Row. Little brass fish. Knotted cords. Nothing that worked, officially." She watched his face. "Unofficially, you bargained with rats. You paid them in bread crusts and secrets, and they told you which merchants watered their wine and which watchmen took bribes and where the stray dogs were denning that winter. You thought it was a small gift. A harmless one."

"A rat king," Dominic said slowly. His brow furrowed. "I dreamed — there were three of them, fat ones, sitting on a stair like judges—"

"The Triune of the Granary Cellars," Seraphine confirmed. Her ledger trembled slightly in her hands, and she stilled it. "You traded with them the night before your arrest. That's how I found you. That's how I always meant to find you — I let you go first, you understand. From the interrogation. I wanted you to lead me somewhere."

His eyes lifted. There was no accusation in them, which was somehow worse than accusation. "And did I?"

"You led all of us," she said. "To places I will tell you about when you're stronger. Today is only Tallow Row. Today is only the beginning."

A gull landed on the sill outside, white-headed and insolent, and croaked twice. Dominic tilted his head — that listening tilt, the one that came back to his body faster than any fact came back to his mind — and then he laughed, short and startled.

"It says you're lying about one thing," he told her. "It says you weren't hunting me at all, near the end. It says—" he stopped, and color rose in his face. "It says something unkind about the church and something kind about you, and I don't think I should repeat either."

Seraphine closed the ledger. Under the desk, her hand pressed flat against her sternum, over the brand scar where her office had been burned away. *Kind.* The gulls remembered kindness in her. She herself had to take the animals' word for it.

"Tell me about Tallow Row," she said, steadying. "Tell me what you dreamed. Dreams count, Dom. Crow says so, and Crow is insufferable but rarely wrong."

* * *

*Nix*

Week three, and Nix taught him pickpocketing.

Not because he needed it — his fingers still did their old work without instruction, lifting a coin off her sleeve so smoothly she didn't notice until she heard it singing in his palm — but because he'd asked. *Tell me who I was,* he'd said, *not just what happened to me.* And who he was, was a man who'd once stolen his own charm stock back from a fence and left in its place a polite note and a dead herring.

So she ran him through drills on the roof, in the wind, while the city spread below them like spilled jewelry. And between drills she told him pieces. That was their arrangement: she gave him a story, he gave her back a trick. Trade, fair trade. Everything with Nix was fair trade or it was nothing.

"You robbed me," she said, watching him palm a button off her collar without looking. "First time we met. Well. I robbed you first, technically, but then I robbed you *again*, in your sleep, sort of—"

"I dreamed that," he said eagerly. "I dreamed someone going through my pockets who wasn't really there. It was warm, though? That part doesn't make sense. Being robbed shouldn't feel warm."

"Because you weren't being robbed." Nix sat down on the roof ridge, legs dangling over the drop, and made herself look at him straight. This part cost her. She told it anyway. "I see through animals' eyes when I sleep. Always have. And that night my gift went sideways — it slipped into *your* head, into your memories, without asking. I saw your childhood, Dom. Your mother teaching you the beast-tongue in a kitchen that smelled of bay leaves. I saw her face before—" Before the gods ate it. Before he spent it sealing a gate. She swallowed. "I saw things no thief has a right to see. So I brought everything back. Every coin. And I stayed, after, because I'd already seen the best of you and I figured I might as well meet the rest."

He was quiet a long moment. Below them a cat picked its delicate way along a gutter, and Nix felt it glance up at them both, incurious, unbothered.

"My mother," Dominic said. "In the kitchen. Bay leaves." His voice cracked down the middle. "I can almost — there's a shape where she should be. Like a coat still hanging on a hook. I know someone wore it. I don't know who."

"She wore it," Nix said fiercely. "And I'm going to hang it back on you, piece by piece, until you recognize the fit. That's a promise, and Crow says promises are the only real words, so."

He smiled — the first full smile of the day — and stole the other button off her sleeve.

* * *

*Seraphine*

By the fifth week, the retellings had a rhythm, and the animals had appointed themselves witnesses.

It happened without anyone arranging it. Seraphine would testify to some event — the raid on the market, the cells below the Inquisition, the terrible blue hour when Dominic unbound her hound — and some creature would arrive to corroborate. Sparrows on the sill during the account of the burning market, chirping in what Crow insisted was agreement. A tide-pool crab carried up from the shore in Nix's cupped hands to confirm, by furious clacking, the details of the drowned shrine. Even rats, once — three fat ones seated on the stair like judges, and Seraphine, former terror of the Inquisition, had given testimony to rats and waited respectfully for their verdict.

They never contradicted her. That was the miracle she hadn't expected. In her old life, every testimony she'd ever taken had been bent somewhere — shaped by fear, by hope, by the weight of the questions themselves. But the animals remembered flat and true, and their truths matched hers, and slowly she began to trust not only her own history but her own heart. If the gulls agreed she had chosen rightly at the gate, standing beside a man she barely understood against gods she'd worshipped wrongly — then perhaps she had.

Today the subject was the hound.

"His name was Bramble," she said. Dominic sat across from her, the brown dog's chin heavy on his knee — the two animals regarding each other with the wary courtesy of veterans of different wars. "The church bound him to me when he was a pup. Bound his voice, bound his will. I was told it was an honor. I was told he felt nothing but purpose." Her throat tightened around the old lie. "For eleven years I believed I served the gods, and the leash I held was holy. And then a stranger in chains looked at my hound and spoke to him — not commanded, *spoke* — and Bramble turned around and looked at me like he was seeing me for the first time. And I understood that everything I'd been certain of was a cage, and I had been holding the latch."

Dominic's hand moved toward her across the table — hesitated — and settled, gently, over her wrist. The touch of a stranger, offered like a friend's.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I don't remember doing it. But it sounds like something I'd want to have done."

Bramble — the brown dog, whom they had all quietly begun calling Bramble, because names should be returned along with everything else — made his low chest-sound, the almost-word. And outside the window, a dozen sparrows erupted into brief song, like a jury murmuring assent.

Seraphine let herself weep, briefly, without shame. She'd earned the right, she supposed. And then she opened the ledger to the next page.

"Now," she said. "Let me tell you about the night the market burned."

* * *

*Nix*

On the seventh week, he started telling stories back.

They weren't his — he couldn't produce his own yet, the hollow years still stood in him like flooded rooms — but he'd begun gathering theirs. Nix would come down the stairs and find him listening to the chimney swifts, or deep in conference with the rat Triune, who had apparently decided he was worth re-courting now that the covenant was remade, and by evening he'd have assembled what he'd heard into whole cloth: the story of the night Nix got caught covering his escape, told back to her with every detail intact except his own face in it.

"You were magnificent," he said, pacing the kitchen while she shelled peas. "You saw the net coming and you threw yourself sideways into the inquisitors' path so they'd take you instead. And you were laughing. The rat who saw it says you were laughing."

"I was scared out of my mind," Nix said.

"I know," he said. "That's what makes it magnificent." He stopped pacing. "Why did you do it?"

She considered lying, considered joking, considered throwing a pea at him. Instead she set down the bowl. "Because you're the seal, Dom. Because the covenant needed you alive more than it needed me free. Because—" and here was the hard true thing, the one she'd only ever said to the dark, "—because I'd seen your memories, and I knew what it would cost you to close that gate, and I couldn't stand the idea of the world losing you before you even got to choose. Somebody had to make sure you got to the choosing. That somebody was always going to be me. Or Seraphine. Or Crow. Or every rat in this city. You were never going to reach that gate alone, and you were never supposed to."

The dog, sprawled by the hearth, thumped his tail twice against the floorboards. *Yes,* the thump said. *Stay. All of you.*

Dominic sat down across from her, slowly, like a man lowering himself into deep water. "When I sealed it," he said, "when I forgot — did it hurt you? My forgetting?"

"Every day," Nix said. "But we're fixing that. One story at a time." She pushed the pea bowl toward him. "Now shell. And I'll tell you about the fight we had when you wouldn't let me pay your price. You were insufferable. You were right, too, but mostly insufferable."

* * *

*Seraphine*

On the ninth week, they reached the chapter Seraphine had been dreading since she first pinned her rules above the desk: the rite itself. The flood of stolen memories. His mother's face, returned mid-casting like a knife made of light.

She told it plainly, because he deserved plainness. How the remnant gods had poured everything they'd eaten back at him to break his concentration — a lifetime of the taken, a city's worth of grief, and then, cruelest and most merciful, the woman with bay-leaf hands, saying his name. How he had stood in that flood with tears cutting channels through the grime on his face, and spoken the sealing words anyway. How the price had been exacted not in blood but in *self*: his childhood, his name's meaning, and finally — Seraphine's voice failed here, and the ledger's ink had gone blotchy at this passage weeks ago and gone blotchy again now — the people beside him. Nix's laugh. Crow's gravel lessons. Her own hand on his shoulder as the gate closed.

"You chose the world," she finished, "over yourself. Over us."

Dominic was silent for a long time. On his knee, the dog pressed closer, a warm brown weight of corroboration.

"I want you to tell me her again," he said at last. "My mother. Not the face — I'm not ready, the animals keep trying to show me and it's like drinking the sea. Just... tell me what she was *like*. You never met her. Tell me what I must have gotten from her."

Seraphine set down the pen. "Stubbornness," she said. "You argue with doors, Dom. With locks. With the concept of closed. When the archive was buried, you dug with your bare hands. When the god offered you your childhood back, you nearly said yes — and then you didn't, because you'd promised Nix you wouldn't spend her life to buy yours. Whoever raised you built a man who keeps promises like load-bearing walls." She smiled, wet-eyed. "She must have been formidable."

The sparrows sang from the sill. The dog's tail swept the floor. And Dominic Almeida — heir of nothing, keeper of an empty ledger where his life should be — nodded slowly, filing it away, adding the plank to the bridge being built across his hollow years, story by corroborated story.

"Tomorrow," he said, "tell me about Crow. Start with the cruel test. Nix says I deserved it. Nix also says he cheated, so I'll want both versions."

"You'll get three," Seraphine said. "The animals were there too."

Outside, the evening filled with wings and small feet, all of Vellamare's creatures converging gently on one salt-stained house above the chandler's shop, carrying their piece of a man back to him — and far out past the harbor, beneath the green water, something at the drowned gate pulsed once, softly, like a heart remembering its rhythm.
