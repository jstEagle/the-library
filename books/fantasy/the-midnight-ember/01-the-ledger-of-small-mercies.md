## 1. The Ledger of Small Mercies

The candle had been burning for six hours when Zephyrine noticed it had stopped flickering.

That was the trouble with working past midnight in a house full of impossible things: you learned to distrust stillness. A flame that held itself perfectly steady was either a miracle or a warning, and in Zephyrine's experience the two were often the same animal wearing different collars.

They set down their pen and watched the flame stand there like a soldier at attention, and they felt the familiar prickle rise along their forearms—the static, the hum, the storm that lived under their skin and had lived there since childhood, coiled and patient as a cat pretending to sleep. Seven years of practice had taught Zephyrine to ignore it the way one ignores an old injury on a cold night. You acknowledged it. You did not answer it.

"No," they told the candle quietly, though what they were really telling was themselves.

The flame remembered how to flicker. Zephyrine returned to the ledger.

It was a handsome ledger, bound in oxblood leather gone soft at the corners, its spine stitched twice because once had not been enough for what it carried. The Guild of Impossible Things kept three ledgers—one for coin, one for goods, and this one, the third, which Corvane called the Ledger of Small Mercies and which was neither small nor, strictly speaking, merciful. It recorded every transaction the guild conducted that could not be paid in ordinary currency. Bottled thunderclaps. Borrowed years. Last words purchased from dying mouths and resold to grieving ears. The names of unborn children, traded by mothers too poor to feed them, bought by women too old to bear them. A jar of someone's courage, decanted in 411, still unsold, gathering dust on shelf nine because courage, it turned out, was not in demand among those who needed it most.

Zephyrine's hand moved across the page in small, precise strokes. They had been keeper of accounts for seven years now, ever since Corvane had found them half-frozen at the guildhouse door with frost in their hair out of season and no explanation they were willing to give. *You balance books?* Corvane had asked. *I balance anything,* Zephyrine had said, which was true, and also a lie so large it had become load-bearing.

Because here was the secret Zephyrine kept even from the ledger: numbers were the only prayer they trusted anymore. Every line item was a small act of order imposed on a world that crackled and sparked at the edges. When Zephyrine wrote *one bottled thunderclap, condition: restless, stored away from iron,* the storm in their blood settled, just slightly, as if somewhere a cousin had been accounted for. When the columns balanced—when debits equaled credits, when nothing was owed that could not be named—they felt, for a few hours, like a person rather than a weather waiting to happen.

The guildhouse breathed around them. Above, floorboards creaked where Bramble's smuggled memories sat in their crates, dreaming other people's dreams; Osk had warded the ceiling against eavesdropping, but memory has a way of leaking through any lock. Somewhere below, Nell slept in the coal cellar again, curled among the ashes she insisted were warmer than blankets. The building itself was old enough to have opinions, and tonight it seemed to be holding its breath along with the candle.

Zephyrine turned to the quarterly audit.

Routine. That was the word they chose for it, the way one chooses a coat: *routine audit, nothing to fear, only the guild's ordinary strangeness passing under my pen.* They worked through the autumn entries. Two borrowed years, returned slightly used to a farmer who claimed they didn't fit right anymore—Zephyrine noted the complaint and flagged the vendor. Half a moonlight, sold to a poet. One grief, heavy grade, purchased from a widow in Vellsmark and—

Vellsmark.

Zephyrine's pen stopped.

There was nothing alarming about Vellsmark in the ledger. It appeared forty times a year, a river town two days east, a reliable supplier of small sorrows and occasional wonders. But something about seeing the name made Zephyrine look up at the window, where beyond the glass the autumn sky sat low and brownish, the color of a bruise deciding whether to become something worse. The wind had been carrying ash for three days now. Corvane said it was kilns. Corvane said a great many things in a voice designed to end conversations.

Zephyrine looked back down and kept reading, and that was when they found the entry that did not belong.

*Received: one payment in kind. Contents: memories, assorted, eleven (11). From: broker unknown. Condition: fresh. Provenance:* and then a smudge, as if the original clerk had written something and thought better of it, and pressed the page with his thumb while the ink was wet.

Eleven memories. Sold to the guild by an unnamed broker, received four months ago, logged by Osk in her slanted, impatient hand—and then, oddly, never entered into inventory. No shelf number. No resale price. Eleven memories sitting unaccounted for like coins dropped behind a counter.

An imbalance. Small. Almost nothing. The kind of thing a lesser accountant would wave through with a shrug.

Zephyrine stared at it for a long moment, and the static under their skin rose up their arms like water finding its level, prickling at the wrists, humming faintly in their teeth. They pressed their palms flat against the desk until the oak creaked.

"Routine," they said aloud, to the empty room. "This is routine."

The guildhouse, which knew better, said nothing.

* * *

Finding Osk at this hour was easy, because Osk was always exactly where she said she wasn't. Zephyrine descended two flights and crossed the hall of doors—which is to say, a corridor lined with thirty-one doors, twenty-nine of which opened onto walls, one onto the coal cellar, and one, on Thursdays, onto a beach that may or may not have existed—and found her sitting cross-legged before the thirtieth door, picking its lock with a hairpin and a word she'd invented herself.

"You're supposed to be asleep," Osk said without turning around. She was a compact woman built like a well-kept tool, gray-streaked braid, rings on eight fingers and none on the two that mattered. "People who audit ledgers at midnight are people looking for trouble."

"People who lock doors at midnight are people hiding it."

"Mm." The lock clicked—a sound like a small bone setting—and the thirtieth door swung open onto a narrow storeroom that was, tonight, a narrow storeroom. Osk stood, brushed off her knees. "Well? What's missing?"

"Eleven memories. Bought in high summer from a broker nobody logged. Paid in kind." Zephyrine held out the ledger, open to the offending page. "Paid in *memories*, Osk. Which means somebody gave up pieces of their own head to buy these. And then they were never shelved. Never priced. Where are they?"

Osk took the ledger and read the entry twice. Zephyrine watched her face do something complicated and quick, there and gone, like a fish turning under ice.

"I don't remember," Osk said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I have." She handed the book back. "I logged the purchase because logging is your religion, not mine, and I wanted you asleep when you found the gap. But I don't remember receiving them. There's a hole where that week should be, roughly the shape of a week, and before you ask—no, I didn't sell it. I've checked my own ledger. Someone took it."

The static climbed to Zephyrine's shoulders. Outside, far off, thunder muttered—not a real thunderstorm; the sky was dry as paper—but the particular low grumble that meant *somewhere, something is restless*, and Zephyrine had learned over seven years that when the air grumbled without clouds, it was usually grumbling about them.

"Memories that erase themselves from the minds of a lock-witch," Zephyrine said slowly. "Bought by a broker with no name. Kept out of inventory." They looked at Osk. "What kind of payment is worth doing all that to hide?"

"The expensive kind." Osk rubbed her rings together, a nervous habit she'd deny to her grave. "Bramble decants everything that comes through here. If anyone knows what's in those eleven, it's her. Assuming she remembers."

*Assuming.* The word hung in the stairwell air like smoke.

* * *

Bramble's workshop occupied the top floor and smelled of lavender, hot copper, and the faint green scent of things growing in places where nothing should grow. She worked at night the way other people prayed—with total absorption and occasional muttering. When Zephyrine and Osk came up the stairs, she was bent over a decanting rack, coaxing a ribbon of silver light from a phial into a shallow dish of water, where it swam in slow circles like a fish remembering being young.

"Don't touch the moonlight, it bites," she said, by way of greeting. Then she saw their faces and straightened. "Or you can touch whatever you like, apparently, if you're both wearing that expression. What's happened?"

Zephyrine set the ledger on her workbench and turned it toward her. "Eleven memories. Unshelved. Osk doesn't remember taking delivery. I want to know what we own."

Bramble read the entry. Her hands—broad, scarred, gentle hands that handled other people's pasts with more care than most people handled their own—went still above the open page.

"I know this broker," she said at last. "Came at midsummer, hood up, paid too much, asked too little. I remember thinking the deal was wrong-shaped and taking it anyway, because the memories were good stock. Fresh. Vivid. I remember—" She frowned, and Zephyrine watched the frown deepen into something genuinely frightened. "I remember eleven crates. I don't remember what was in ten of them."

"And the eleventh?"

Bramble didn't answer. She crossed to the back of her workshop, to the shelf where misfit inventory went to be forgotten—the cracked phials, the spoiled lots, the things too strange or too sad to sell—and pulled out a single vial of dark glass, stoppered with wax the color of dried blood. No label. But scratched into the wax with a pin, in handwriting Zephyrine did not recognize, were four words:

*DO NOT SHOW HER.*

"Him," Bramble said. "I assumed it meant Corvane." She turned the vial in the lamplight. "But I've been keeping it down here for four months without knowing why, and I've just realized I've been keeping it *from myself*. Whatever's in this, someone paid a great deal to make sure certain people never see it—and arranged for me to forget I was one of those people."

"Then don't show us," Osk said quickly. "Log it, seal it, bury it in the garden. Some debts aren't ours to collect."

But Zephyrine was already reaching for the vial, and the terrible thing—the thing they would turn over and over in the months to come—was how calm their hand was. Steady as a clerk's. The static sang under their skin, loud as bees, and their hand did not shake at all.

"It's guild property," they heard themselves say. "Unaccounted for. I'm the keeper of accounts."

"That's not why you're opening it," Bramble said quietly.

"No," Zephyrine agreed. "But it will do."

* * *

Decanting a memory was a simple ritual, older than the guild and probably older than sense. Bramble warmed the vial between her palms until the wax softened, murmured the unwinding words—words with no magic in them, she always insisted, only permission—and tipped the contents into the viewing dish, where memory pooled like oil, dark and slow-turning, throwing off colors that had no business existing outside a mind.

"Bramble," Zephyrine said. "Whose memory is this?"

"That's the thing about stolen goods." Bramble's voice had gone very soft. "You never know until you look."

She touched two fingers to the surface of the pool, and the memory opened like an eye.

Zephyrine had seen memories viewed a hundred times—a spectator's distance, a ghost's dimness, the viewer floating at the edge of someone else's moment. So the first wrongness was that this memory did not push them away. It pulled. The viewing room, the lamp, Bramble and Osk—all of it folded up small and vanished, and Zephyrine was standing somewhere else entirely, in a body that was and was not theirs.

Marble underfoot, cold through thin shoes. Gold everywhere, gold on every wall, and banners hanging from a vaulted ceiling: the stag-and-sun of House Aldric, bright as the day they'd been sewn. Courtiers in a half-circle, faces blurred the way faces blur in dreams, all of them turned toward the dais at the far end of the room. Toward the throne.

Toward the king.

King Aldric sat slumped in state, and even blurred, even dreamed, he was unmistakably dying. Zephyrine—who had never seen the man, who had grown up three kingdoms' width from the capital—knew him anyway, the way you know a face from coins. His skin was gray as candle tallow. His crown sat crooked. His mouth was open on a breath that would not come back.

And Zephyrine—this younger self, this stranger with Zephyrine's own cheekbones and a jaw not yet hardened by seven hard years—stood alone in the middle of the throne room floor, too close, much too close, close enough to see what crawled out of the hearthfire behind the throne.

It came out the way smoke comes out, if smoke could want things. It poured itself up the side of the dais, glowing at its seams like banked coals, and it reached the king's body and hesitated—hesitated, the way a thief hesitates at an unlocked door, savoring—and then it began to put him on. Not eat him. *Wear* him. Zephyrine's younger self watched it pour into the slack mouth, watched the gray skin flush suddenly warm and ruddy, watched King Aldric sit up straight, smile, and adjust his crown with a hand that glowed faintly, at the knuckles, like a poker left too long in the fire.

The courtiers applauded. Of course they applauded. They had seen their king recover before their eyes, hale at fifty after an hour's illness, and what was a little glow in the knuckles, a little too much warmth in the smile?

Only the young figure in the center of the floor had seen the hearthfire move. Only they were staring, stricken, at a king whose eyes were the color of coals.

And the Ember—wearing Aldric's face, smiling Aldric's smile—turned its head and looked directly out of the memory, directly at Zephyrine across seven years and one stolen vial, and its gaze was a hand closing around a candleflame, and the last thing Zephyrine felt before the memory snapped shut like a book was a voice that was warm as a hearth and vast as a winter saying, with perfect kindness:

*Found you.*

* * *

Zephyrine came back to themselves on the floor of Bramble's workshop with Osk's rings digging into their shoulder and Bramble pressing a cup of something bitter against their lips. The lamp had gone out. The moonlight in its dish had scattered. Their whole body was humming, the static risen fully to the surface for the first time in seven years, tiny blue sparks jumping between their fingers when they moved, and distantly, absurdly, the accountant in them noted: *uncontrolled discharge, cause unknown, recommend immediate containment.*

"What did you see?" Bramble asked. Her face was wet. Zephyrine realized with a jolt that Bramble had seen it too—that she'd been pulled in alongside them, that the memory had taken them both.

"A throne room," Zephyrine said. Their voice came out scraped. "I saw—I was *in*—" They stopped. Started again. "Bramble. That memory was mine. That was my face. I was standing in the throne room the night King Aldric died, and I watched something climb out of the fire and put him on like a coat, and I have never in my life been to the capital."

Osk had gone very still by the door. "Then whose head did that broker cut it out of?"

"Mine," Zephyrine said. "Someone sold the guild a piece of my own past. Someone walked into my life, took the throne room out of it, sealed the wound, and left me standing at Corvane's door in the snow with no reason to be there." They looked down at their sparking hands. "Seven years ago. Right before my exile. Right before—"

The sentence wouldn't finish, because finishing it required a shape they didn't yet have. But somewhere below them, in the hall of doors, the thirtieth door swung gently open onto the coal cellar, and Nell's voice floated up the stairwell, sleepy and puzzled: "Why's all the ash falling upward?"

Outside the window, over the brown autumn sky, thunder rolled again from a cloudless horizon—closer this time, and this time, unmistakably, in no hurry to pass over. It circled the guildhouse like something reading an address.

Zephyrine closed the ledger of small mercies and found, without surprise, that their hands were shaking now. All accounts, sooner or later, came due.
