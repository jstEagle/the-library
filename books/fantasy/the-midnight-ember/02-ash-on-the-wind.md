## 2. Ash on the Wind

The ash came before the news did.

Zephyrine noticed it first on the windowsill of the ledger room, a fine grey grit that had not been there at dawn—too coarse to be soot from their own chimney, too pale to be road dust. They rubbed it between finger and thumb and it whispered, faintly, the way snow whispers when it lands on something that is still warm. Their skin prickled in answer. Under their sleeves, beneath seven years of careful stillness, the static stirred like a dog hearing its name spoken in another room.

*No*, they told it, as they told it every day. *Lie down.*

They opened the window and looked east, toward Vellsmark, and saw the sky there wearing a bruise of smoke the color of an old bruise gone yellow at the edges. The wind was wrong for chimney smoke. The wind was wrong for anything but a city burning slowly, thoroughly, with the patience of official work.

Below, in the courtyard, Bramble stood in the middle of the morning delivery, holding a crate she had not yet unpacked, her face turned east, her lips moving as though reading something written on the air.

Zephyrine closed the window. The ash stayed on their fingertips all through the morning's accounting, and every time they turned a page, a little grey ghost of it transferred to the paper, so that by noon the Ledger of Small Mercies looked as though it were already being mourned.

* * *

The courier arrived at half past three, riding a horse that had been ridden past the point of kindness, foam dried to dirty lace along its neck. He would not come inside. He stood in the courtyard with his hat in his hands and his eyes everywhere except on faces, and he read the proclamation aloud in the flat voice of a man who has said terrible words so many times they have worn smooth as river stones.

"By decree of His Majesty King Aldric, blessed and long-reigning: let it be known throughout the realm that the practice of unlicensed gifts—of storm-calling, memory-working, door-witching, hearth-speaking, blood-reading, and all other arts of the hidden kind—is hereby declared treason against the crown's peace. Let all persons thus gifted present themselves at any royal garrison within forty days to be entered upon the Register of Loyal Talents, there to be examined, licensed, or—" here the courier's voice caught, not from feeling but from fatigue, "—corrected. Any who harbor, conceal, trade with, or fail to report such persons shall share their correction. This is called the Long Hunt. It will not end until the kingdom is clean. So says the Ember Throne."

The last two words he said quickly, the way one steps over a grave.

Nobody in the courtyard moved. Zephyrine counted them without meaning to—an accountant's tic, counting things when counting was the only prayer available. Bramble by the crates, her knuckles white on a bale of wrapped cloth that was certainly only cloth. Osk at the stable door with a currycomb forgotten in her hand, her clever face arranged into perfect, insulted boredom, the best mask in the house. Nell up in the loft of the cart-shed where she was absolutely not supposed to be, her small body gone very still, the way animals go still when they understand more than they should. Corvane at his study window above, behind glass gone grey with reflected sky, a shape rather than a face.

And the courier, waiting for acknowledgment, his own gift—or lack of one—irrelevant now, because in the new arithmetic even the ungifted could burn for knowing one.

"Forty days," Corvane said from the window. His voice carried the way it always did, dry and unhurried, the voice of a man discussing freight rates. "You'll want water for your horse."

"Water won't save him," the courier said. "Nothing will. It's Vellsmark, you understand. They registered. Half of them registered." He put his hat back on as if it were armor. "Registered folk went first anyway. The lists tell you where everyone lives."

He rode out the way he'd come, and the gate closed behind him, and the sound of it was very small and very final, a book shutting.

* * *

That evening the guildhouse held its emergency council in the ledger room, which was the only room with a lock Osk trusted, and even then she checked it twice, running her thumb along the keyhole and murmuring something that made the iron taste itself and confirm its own honesty. Candles burned low. Nobody suggested lighting more.

"Vellsmark," Corvane began, standing, because he never sat for bad news; sitting, he said once, was how you agreed with it. "Population nine thousand. Guild presence: a chapter house of eleven, plus freelancers. As of this morning, the chapter house is gone. Not arrested. Gone. And the city with it."

"Burned," Osk said. It wasn't a question, but she wanted it said aloud, plainly, without decoration.

"Burned under writ. The proclamation calls it *cleansing*. Eyewitness accounts—" Corvane stopped himself, and Zephyrine watched him stop himself, and understood that whatever accounts existed, Corvane had decided the room couldn't hold them. "The commander of the operation is one Serah Vail. Formerly of the Border Reaches. She arrives in each district ahead of the soldiers, alone, and inspects ledgers, registries, correspondence. Then the soldiers come. There is no pattern to what survives. That worries me more than fire would."

"Then we run," Bramble said. She was turning her ring around and around her finger—a band of dull silver set with something dark that was not a stone; Zephyrine had never asked and had always known. "We've got wagons, we've got routes, I've moved cargo through three wars and none of them ever thought to check a coffin. We pack tonight—"

"We bury deeper," Corvane said.

The words dropped into the room like stones into a well. Bramble stared at him. Osk's eyebrows climbed with elegant slowness.

"Master," Osk said, "the crown just declared us kindling. Burying deeper is how you make yourself easy to find and slow to escape. A hole is a trap with good manners."

"A hole is also invisible." Corvane's hands were folded behind his back, and Zephyrine noticed—for they noticed everything, it was the curse of the trade—that his knuckles were bloodless. "No deliveries for six weeks. No couriers. No trades. We eat what's in the cellar and we let every contract lapse and we become, officially, a shuttered counting-house whose master took ill. Vail inspects ledgers. Very well. She will inspect ledgers that are boring beyond endurance, kept by a house that does nothing worth hiding, because for six weeks it genuinely does nothing at all. Neutrality is our charter. Neutrality is our armor. It has kept this guild alive through four reigns and it will keep us alive through this."

"And the people who can't bury?" Bramble asked quietly. "The hedge-witches in the villages. The boy in Millbrook who sings rain onto his mother's crops. Do they have charters?"

Corvane was silent a moment. "The charter protects the guild," he said. "It was never meant to protect the world."

"No," Zephyrine said.

Everyone turned. Zephyrine heard their own voice as if from a slight distance, calm, level, the ledger-voice, and underneath it the static rising like floodwater against a door.

"I said no." They rose. "I've kept the books for seven years, Master Corvane. I know precisely what neutrality buys. It buys us watching the register lists come through and copying out the names of everyone we've ever traded with, so we can stop trading with them before Vail finds the connection. It buys us letting the Millbrook boy burn because reporting him would be *involvement*. You're not proposing armor. You're proposing we dig our own graves deep enough that we can't hear the knocking."

"You forget yourself," Corvane said, and there was something strange in it—not anger, Zephyrine thought. Something closer to fear wearing anger's coat.

"I never forget myself," Zephyrine said. "Forgetting myself is the one thing I have never been permitted to do."

The silence after that had edges. Nell, who had wedged herself into the corner with the account-books she pretended to study, was looking at Zephyrine with her wide, feral attention, the look of someone memorizing.

"Six weeks," Corvane said at last, to the room, to no one, to Zephyrine. "Bury deeper. That is my order, and it is final. Meeting's done."

* * *

Zephyrine could not sleep.

They sat in the ledger room with one candle and the stolen memory-decant from the audit, the little glass vial of grey-silver light that had no business existing in their inventory, and they did not open it. Opening it was Bramble's craft; Zephyrine only knew that since the night they'd found their own younger face inside its catalog entry, they had not been able to walk past the strongroom without their heart stumbling like a man on stairs he thought he knew.

Instead they sat with the old ledger open to the page they had copied from it—the payment record, the memory-trade, and beside it, in the broker's hand, a note of provenance that Zephyrine had transcribed without understanding:

*Item: one (1) intact episodic memory. Subject: court functionary, retired. Contents: throne room, winter court, year of the comet. Note unusual clarity; seller claims the scene would not leave them. Provenance chain begins [illegible].*

*Year of the comet.*

Seven years ago. The year Zephyrine Dunmore had been exiled from the capital in disgrace, the year the storm had left their hands and refused to return, the year they'd walked north with one bag and a contract they had been forbidden to read.

They had told themselves for seven years that exile had taken their memory of why. Shock, grief, the mind protecting itself—all the soft explanations. But sitting here with ash still ghosting the windowsill, Zephyrine felt the truth move underneath those explanations like a fish under ice: there was no forgetting. There was a hole, cut cleanly, with a ledger's precision, by someone who knew exactly what to remove.

And now a fragment of it had surfaced in someone else's sold memory, like a coin worked loose from a buried hoard.

A throne room. A winter court. And—Zephyrine pressed their palms flat against the table until the static in their arms sang against the wood—a figure in the corner of that fragment, tall and still, in a commander's cloak, watching the young storm-caller with an expression that even across the blur of borrowed recollection had read unmistakably as *recognition*. As *there you are*. As a hunter confirming quarry.

The knock came softly, two taps, and Zephyrine knew before answering that it would be Nell, because Nell knocked like someone asking permission to exist, and it was past midnight, and only Nell was awake.

"They're saying things in the kitchen," Nell announced, slipping in and closing the door behind her with exaggerated care, as though the door might gossip. "Bramble and Osk. They think I'm asleep. They're saying Master Corvane got a letter three days ago. Before the courier. Before the proclamation." She climbed onto the table, cross-legged, uninvited, her frost-bitten-chapped fingers laced together. "Before, Zeph. He knew."

"Did they say what the letter said?"

Nell shook her head. "But Bramble said a word I didn't know and Osk told her to hush, and then Bramble said, *he bought that girl fair and square and he knows what the contract forbids him to say.*" Nell's eyes were enormous in the candlelight. "What contract? What's he forbidden to say?"

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft. Zephyrine became aware, distantly, that the shadows in the corners of the ledger room had gone sharp and trembling, that the ink in the well was shivering into fine concentric rings, that somewhere overhead the weather was gathering itself around the guildhouse like a wolf circling a lantern. Seven years of holding it down, and it rose now not because they willed it but because grief and fear had pried the lid off a corner of it, and pressure seeks any opening.

"Nell," Zephyrine said carefully, "go get Bramble. Tell her I need the decant opened. Tonight. The one from the audit."

"The forbidden one?"

"Especially the forbidden one."

Nell slid off the table and paused at the door, and looked back with an expression far too old for her face. "You're going to do something," she said. "I can tell. The lights do the thing when you're going to do something."

"Go."

She went. And Zephyrine stood alone in the shivering room with the ash of Vellsmark on the sill and the fragment of their own stolen life locked in glass downstairs, and looked east through the dark toward the ruined city, and understood at last that invisibility was not ending.

It had ended seven years ago, in a throne room, in front of a witness in a commander's cloak.

It had simply taken the rest of the world this long to catch up.

Down in the strongroom, behind three locks, a small glass vial of grey-silver light waited to give them back their name—and Zephyrine, keeper of impossible accounts, found for the first time in their careful life that they were afraid to know the sum.
