## 3. The Guild of Impossible Things

### Bramble

Bramble kept her most dangerous inventory in jam jars.

Not the memories themselves—those lived in vials of smoked glass, stoppered with wax and labeled in her own cramped hand, shelved in the cold cellar where nothing warm could reach them. The jam jars held other things: a widow's last laugh, purchased for three silver; a soldier's courage, sold back to him twice at a markup; the smell of bread from a bakery that had burned down eleven years ago. Small stock. Filler stock. Things she could show a crown inspector without flinching, if it ever came to that.

She was decanting a jar of summer rain when Corvane's bell rang through the guildhouse—three strikes, then two, then one. The assembly pattern. Her hands went still over the shelf.

Three-two-one meant everyone. Not just the traders, not just the licensed oddities who paid their guild dues and pretended to be bookbinders and apothecaries and piano tuners. Everyone. Even the ones whose names were written in no ledger at all.

Bramble capped the rain, tucked it between a jar of plum preserves and a bottle of somebody's grandmother, and climbed the cellar stairs with her heart tapping out a rhythm against her ribs that had nothing to do with the climb.

The common room smelled of tallow and toasted barley, of wet wool drying by the hearth, of the particular staleness that gathered in a house where too many people were trying very hard to be unremarkable. They came up from every corner: the apprentices from the copying room with ink still on their knuckles, the night-shift smugglers blinking in the lamplight, old Marta from the forgery desk who had not left her chair in four years and did so now, slowly, like a ship raising anchor.

And Zephyrine Dunmore, standing apart as always, near the window where the light was worst. Bramble had watched them for seven years across ledgers and candle smoke—the storm-caller who filed reports in perfect copperplate and never once let their hand tremble, even on the nights the lamps flickered without wind. She had smuggled enough memories to know what suppressed things looked like from the outside. Zephyrine looked like a river frozen mid-rapid.

Corvane stood at the head of the long table. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a beard he'd stopped dyeing the year Vellsmark's trade route opened and gray hair now going white at the temples, and he had the look of someone who had rehearsed his speech all morning and hated every word of it.

"You've heard," he said. No preamble. That alone frightened Bramble more than anything else he might have said.

Nobody answered. The word *Vellsmark* hung in the room anyway, unsaid, the way a burned house keeps its shape in ash.

"The crown has declared the Long Hunt." Corvane's voice was level, the voice he used for negotiating with people who wanted to hurt him. "Registration for the gifted. Those who decline will be—" He paused. Bramble watched him choose the word, watched him reject *burned* the way a merchant rejects a bad coin, and land on: "*processed.* The edict uses the word processed."

A sound went through the room, low and involuntary, like wind through wheat.

"We are the Guild of Impossible Things," Corvane continued. "We have survived four kings, two inquisitions, and the entire reign of Aldric's grandfather, who hanged a lock-witch from the guildhouse gate. Do you know how we survived?"

"Neutrality," said Marta, from her chair.

"Neutrality." Corvane nodded. "We trade with the crown. We trade with the hunted. We ask no one what they carry through our doors, and we tell no one what leaves them. That code has kept every soul under this roof alive through worse than this. It will keep us alive through this. We register no one. We report no one. And no one—" his eyes moved around the table, slow, deliberate, "—no one does anything brave. Is that understood?"

Bramble looked at Zephyrine.

It was a small thing, what she saw. A fractional turn of the head, away from the table, toward the dark window where the autumn wind worried the glass. The flicker of a lamp flame bending toward them though the windows were shut. The particular stillness of a person holding something down.

Zephyrine knew something. Had known it since the audit three days ago, since the memory-vial with the impossible entry had crossed their desk—and Bramble would know, because Bramble had been the one who logged it into the vault, and she had seen Zephyrine's face in the ledger's margin notes, younger, standing on marble, wearing court colors.

*No one does anything brave,* Corvane said.

Bramble thought about the vial sleeping in her cellar. Thought about how many nights she'd carried it up the stairs and stopped at the top, and turned around, and gone back down. Loyalty was a heavy thing to hold in both hands. Truth was heavier, and it cut.

She thought: *not tonight.* Tonight there would be barley bread and the family gathered close and the lie of ordinary evening. Tomorrow she would climb those stairs again, and maybe this time her feet would carry her all the way.

Outside, far off in the direction of the capital, the sky grumbled without clouds. Everyone pretended not to notice. Everyone except Zephyrine, whose shoulders eased by a degree, the way a dog's do when it hears its name called from very far away.

### Osk

There were forty-one doors in the guildhouse. Osk had locked thirty-nine of them.

The remaining two were locked by other people—the front door, which Corvane barred himself each night with an iron bolt older than the kingdom, and the cellar door, which only opened for Bramble because it had learned her step the way a dog learns its master. Everything else belonged to Osk. Doors that weren't doors: the pantry's false back, the seam in the library wall, the wardrobe that opened onto the chimney flue, the little door behind the third-floor wainscoting that nobody remembered installing and everybody used. Osk hadn't built any of them. Locks existed before locks were made; they slept in the joints of things, in hinges and seams and the places where wood met wood, and a lock-witch was simply someone who could hear them snoring and wake them politely.

Or impolitely. Depending.

Tonight, after Corvane's assembly, Osk walked the house the way they always did—barefoot, so the floorboards would speak honestly, candle held high, listening. The house talked. Old buildings always talk; you just have to know which silences are words.

The front hall said: *cold coming, colder than the season owes us.*

The stairwell said: *someone is awake on the third floor, sitting very still.*

Osk climbed. Of course they climbed. Curiosity wasn't a gift; it was a condition, and Osk had it worse than anyone.

The third-floor landing smelled of ink and ozone—that faint metallic sharpness that had clung to Zephyrine Dunmore for seven years like a second skin, so familiar now that Osk barely registered it, the way you stop smelling your own home. But tonight the ozone was stronger. Tonight it sat on the air like the first breath before lightning.

Zephyrine sat in the window seat of the disused map room, a ledger open on their knees, not reading it. Looking out at a sky that was too clear and too loud at once.

"You're supposed to be asleep," Osk said, leaning in the doorway. "House rules. I lock the doors at midnight. People included, technically, but I make exceptions for insomniacs and ghosts."

Zephyrine didn't startle. They never startled; Osk had tested this early on, dropping a tray behind them once, and Zephyrine had caught the falling teapot without looking up and set it aside and kept writing, and Osk had decided in that moment that whatever the storm-caller was afraid of, it was nothing that moved fast.

"I'm balancing accounts," Zephyrine said.

"At midnight."

"Some numbers won't wait."

Osk came further into the room. The map room hadn't been used in years—the maps in it predated three border changes and showed a world that no longer existed, kingdoms colored in faded rose and lapis that had since been renamed or erased. Zephyrine sat among all that beautiful wrongness like a coin from a dead mint.

"Vellsmark," Osk said quietly. "You knew someone there."

Everyone knew someone in Vellsmark. It was a trading city; half the guild's routes ran through it. But Zephyrine's silence had a specific weight tonight, and Osk, who read silences the way other people read letters, felt its shape.

"I knew the city," Zephyrine said at last. "I passed through it. Years ago." A pause like a held breath. "I remember it being warm."

"And now it's ash."

"And now it's ash."

Osk settled onto the windowsill opposite, bare feet against cold stone, and looked out at the same sky. Somewhere south, a smudge on the horizon that might be cloud and might be smoke. The wind carried a taste of char even at this distance, faint as a memory of a memory.

"They'll come here eventually," Osk said. "Hunters. Inspectors. Men with lists." They said it the way they reported weather, because someone had to say it plainly and Corvane was busy saying comforting things downstairs. "When they come, they'll want the ledgers. Real ones. And this house has forty-one doors and I can lock thirty-nine of them, but I can't lock a ledger."

"No," Zephyrine agreed. "But you can buy time."

"How much time do you need?"

Zephyrine finally looked up. In the dark their eyes had that quality Osk had never quite gotten used to—a glint, like distant weather seen through glass. Something was moving behind them. Something vast, held very still.

"I don't know yet," Zephyrine said. "That's the honest answer. Ask me again tomorrow."

Osk nodded, and got up, and paused at the door with their hand on the frame—an ordinary door, unlocked, one of the few—and said, because it needed saying and no one else would say it:

"Whatever number won't wait—I've counted the entries in your hand for seven years, friend. You round everything down. You take the losses yourself and hide them in margins. Whatever it is you're carrying, the house sees it. The house remembers." They tapped the doorframe gently, the way you'd tap a sleeping thing. "So does the door."

They left Zephyrine among the dead maps, and went down to walk the halls again, waking locks as they passed, setting the whole guildhouse humming softly to itself like a struck bell fading. Outside, thunderless, the pressure dropped. The candles all leaned east at once, toward the capital, as if pointing.

### Nell

Nell slept in the rafters because beds were traps.

She had told the guild this when she arrived at eleven, feral and flea-bitten and speaking mostly in gestures, and Corvane had looked at her a long moment and said, "Mind the beams over the kitchen, they're soft," and that was that. Seven years later she still slept in the rafters, though the fleas were gone and her vocabulary had grown to fill whole sentences, and sometimes, when the nightmares got loud, she climbed down and slept on the floor beside Bramble's cot instead, and Bramble pretended to be asleep and let her.

Tonight the nightmares stayed quiet, but the house was loud. Nell lay along the main beam above the common room, chin on her folded arms, watching through the gap in the boards as the family sat below pretending the world hadn't changed.

Pretending was the guild's chief industry, Nell had decided long ago. More profitable than memories, more reliable than locks. Everybody in this house was pretending something, all the time, and the trick—the real magic—was that they pretended *together*, and somehow that made it almost true. Marta pretended her legs worked fine. Corvane pretended he wasn't scared. Osk pretended to be merely clever. Bramble pretended her cellar was full of jam. Zephyrine pretended—

Nell frowned, watching them below, seated at the end of the table with their supper untouched.

Zephyrine pretended hardest of anybody. Nell didn't know what the pretense was. But she could smell storms the way other people smelled rain coming, had been able to since she was small—it was why she'd run from the village that gave her up, why she'd hidden in hedgerows whenever the sky went green, why the hunters' dogs had never once tracked her on a windy day. Storms liked her. Or recognized her. Something. And Zephyrine Dunmore, keeper of ledgers, quietest person in the guildhouse, smelled like the inside of a thundercloud on the day it decides.

Below, Corvane raised a cup and said something that made the table laugh—not a real laugh, one of those laughs people build out of planks to shelter under. Nell watched Zephyrine not laugh. Watched them look around the table, face by face, slow, like someone memorizing. Like someone counting.

Nell knew counting. Counting was what you did when you were afraid you'd lose something.

She slid along the beam to the trapdoor above Zephyrine's end of the table, lifted the board an inch, and dropped a crust of barley bread through the gap. It landed on Zephyrine's plate with a small, precise *tap*.

Zephyrine looked up. Found the gap. Found, inevitably, her eye peering through it.

"You should eat," Nell said through the hole. "Storms need feeding. Everybody knows that."

"I'm not a storm," Zephyrine said.

"Right." Nell nodded sagely. "And I'm not sleeping in the ceiling."

For a moment—one heartbeat, maybe two—something crossed Zephyrine's face that wasn't the ledger-keeper's calm. Something young and cracked open and grieving, there and gone like heat-lightning. Then the calm reassembled itself, piece by piece, and Zephyrine picked up the crust and ate it, deliberately, holding her gaze through the gap until she giggled and let the board drop.

Later, much later, when the lamps were out and the house had settled into its creaking sleep, Nell lay in the dark listening to the building talk in its wooden language. Wind outside. Wind that shouldn't be there—autumn wind with winter's teeth already in it, moaning at the eaves like something hungry pacing a fence.

And underneath the wind, so deep she felt it in her breastbone more than heard it, a pulse. Slow. Rhythmic. Coming from everywhere and nowhere, from the ground beneath the foundations and the sky above the chimneys alike.

Like a heart. A huge one, beating somewhere south, keeping time with nothing.

Nell pulled Bramble's spare blanket up over her ears and told herself it was the house settling. Houses settle. Houses breathe. That was all.

But she stayed awake a long while anyway, watching the frost creep across the underside of the roof tiles—in October, weeks too early, inching inward from every edge of the guildhouse at once, as if the cold outside had found a scent it liked and was following it home.

### Corvane

Corvane stood alone in the ledger room after midnight, holding the guild's oldest contract, and allowed himself—for exactly the length of one guttering candle—to feel what he actually felt.

Fear. Old, patient, specific fear.

Seven years ago, a hooded broker had come to the guildhouse in a snowstorm, carrying a document sealed in black wax, and had offered Corvane a sum beyond reason for a single service: sanctuary. One exile, storm-caller, no questions permitted. The contract's terms were elegant and terrible. Corvane could house the exile, feed them, employ them—but he could never permit them to learn why they had been exiled. Never answer questions about it. Never, the clause read in its dry legal script, *allow the subject access to information bearing upon the circumstances of their banishment,* on pain of forfeiting the guild charter entire.

He had signed. The guild had been three months from bankruptcy and a winter from starvation, and he had signed, and he had told himself ever since that he'd saved forty lives with one small cruelty.

He had never asked the broker who the hood was. He had never asked what Zephyrine saw. Some part of him had understood, even then, that asking would be the beginning of the end of everything—and some meaner part of him had preferred not knowing, because not knowing required nothing of him.

Now Vellsmark was ash. Now the Long Hunt was declared. Now the crown that had once bought his neutrality was burning cities, and the contract in his hands suddenly weighed what it had always weighed.

He read the clause again. *Never allow the subject access to information bearing upon the circumstances of their banishment.*

And thought, with a cold that had nothing to do with the frost creeping up the windowpanes, of Bramble's cellar. Of the memory-vials. Of the audit entry logged three days ago, the one with Zephyrine's own face in the margin notes—the one that bore, unmistakably, on the circumstances of their banishment.

The contract did not forbid Zephyrine from learning the truth.

It forbade *him* from letting them.

And somewhere below his feet, in a cold cellar full of jam jars and smoked glass, the truth sat waiting for someone brave enough to carry it upstairs.

Corvane blew out the candle, and in the dark he whispered the only prayer he knew: *let me be a shield and not a cage.* But prayers are contracts too, and he had learned the hard way what came of signing things he hadn't read to the end.

In the morning, he would check the cellar.

By then, he suspected, it would already be too late.
