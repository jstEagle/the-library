## 4. What the Memories Cost

Bramble had smuggled a great many things through the world's narrow places, and she liked to think she knew them all by weight. A stolen year felt like a plum—dense, sweet, faintly bruised at one end. A bottled thunderclap rattled like dice in a gambler's fist. Grief, when it came through her hands, was always cold on one side and warm on the other, like a stone that had lain half in sun.

The vial she held now weighed nothing at all.

That was the first wrong thing. She'd carried it up three flights of stairs from the guild's deep cellar, past the shelves of impossible inventory—the jarred last words, the caged lullabies, the single snowflake that refused to melt because it belonged to someone who could not let go of the winter it fell in—and the whole way it had sat in her palm like a coin of clear glass, lighter than breath. Seven years old, this memory. Bought from a broker who dealt only in things the crown wanted forgotten, paid for with two of Bramble's own childhood summers, which she would never miss until some June evening found her reaching for them and finding the shelf bare.

She set it on the decanter's velvet cradle and did not touch it for a long moment.

The workroom smelled of beeswax and cold iron, of lamp oil and the particular dry scent of old paper that Bramble loved better than any perfume. Rain worked at the shutters in a patient, businesslike way, as though it intended to be paid for its trouble. Somewhere below, Osk was arguing with a lock that didn't want to be argued with, and Nell's laughter came up through the floorboards like bubbles through dark water.

And somewhere below, too, Zephyrine Dunmore was doing the ledgers, pen scratching away in their careful hand, balancing accounts of small mercies, hiding whatever it was that crackled under their skin the way other people hid a limp.

*Seven years,* Bramble thought. *Seven years they've been ours.*

She turned the vial in the lamplight. The memory inside moved like smoke that had been taught manners—coiling, settling, coiling again—and every so often a shape suggested itself within it: a doorway, a bed, a crown.

Bramble was the guild's memory-smuggler, which meant she was also its memory-decanter, which meant that when a purchased recollection needed to be viewed—verified for authenticity, catalogued for resale—it fell to her to pour it out and walk into it. It was delicate work. You couldn't just drink a stranger's remembering; you'd come out the far side wearing their regrets like a coat. Instead you decanted a thread of it, held the thread, and let the images pass across your mind like reflections in moving water. You watched without wading. You kept your boots dry.

That was the craft. That was what she told apprentices.

The truth was that no one kept their boots entirely dry. Every memory left silt behind.

She unstoppered the vial.

The sound it made was very small—a sigh, almost, the sound of something that had been holding itself still for seven years finally being permitted to move. The memory poured upward against all sense, threading through the air into the decanter's basin, where it pooled like moonlight in a dish of oil. Bramble pressed two fingers to the surface and spoke the opening word of her trade, the one that meant *show me, gently*, and the world folded over her like a page being turned.

* * *

She stood in a throne room she had never entered, at an hour that did not exist anymore.

Candles everywhere—or what passed for candles, their flames burning a color flames should not burn, a deep hearth-orange shot through with veins of black like rot in fruit. The room was warm. That was the thing she noticed first, before the throne, before the sleeping figure in it: the warmth. It lay over everything like a pelt, thick and greedy, and beneath it the stone walls sweated.

King Aldric slept in his throne. Not properly—not in a bed, not with the dignity of a man laid out—but slumped in the carved seat with his crown askew, his mouth open, his hands loose in his lap like the hands of a marionette whose puppeteer had stepped out for wine. He was younger than his portraits. There was ink on the fingers of his right hand; he had been signing something, or trying to, and sleep had taken him mid-word.

Bramble knew whose eyes she looked through. She had decanted enough memories of Zephyrine to know the angle of their shoulders even in a body seven years younger, even seen from the inside, where the self is just a pressure behind the breastbone and a taste in the mouth. And the taste in this mouth was copper and storm.

Young Zephyrine stood at the edge of the shadow near the doors, a ledger-clerk's gray coat buttoned wrong, holding a sealed writ of delivery they were not supposed to have read. A storm-caller hidden among clerks. A witness who should have been anywhere else in the palace, or the city, or the world.

The king stirred. His lips moved. From this close, inside the memory, Bramble could hear him—not words, exactly. A name. He was saying a name over and over, the way a man says a name when he is trying to hold onto himself by it, an anchor-rope thrown back toward shore. She couldn't catch it. The memory blurred there deliberately, the way memories blur the things they cannot bear to keep sharp.

Then the hearthfire moved.

Not flickered. *Moved*—leaned out of its grate like a cat stepping off a windowsill, all its orange going black as it crossed the stones, leaving no scorch, no ash, only a kind of hunger-shaped absence where the warmth had been. It crossed the throne room floor the way water finds a low place. It reached the throne.

It climbed the king like ivy claims a fence.

Aldric did not wake. That was the worst of it, Bramble thought distantly, her smuggler's calm fraying at the edges—that he did not wake, that his lips kept shaping the name he could not say aloud, that the fire slid into his open mouth like a ribbon into a spool and his chest rose once, deeply, the way a man breathes after drinking something hot, and then—

Then it sat up straighter than he ever had.

The thing opened the king's eyes. They were Aldric's eyes, brown and mild, and they were completely empty, and behind the emptiness something looked out with the patience of a banked coal. It reached up with the king's own hands and straightened the crown upon the king's own head, tilting it just so, adjusting itself the way a person adjusts a mask.

And it smiled. The smile was perfect. That was how Bramble knew, watching from inside another woman's terror, that it had practiced.

Young Zephyrine made a sound. Small—a caught breath, nothing more—but the thing's head turned toward the shadows by the door with the smooth, oiled precision of a weathervane finding wind. For a moment, an eternity measured in heartbeats, the empty eyes looked directly at where Zephyrine stood. At where Bramble stood, wearing Zephyrine's fear like a borrowed coat that fit too well.

The smile widened by one tooth.

Then the doors at the far end opened, letting in ordinary torchlight and an ordinary guard captain announcing something about the morning council, and when Zephyrine blinked, the king on the throne was simply a king, awake, gracious, complaining mildly about the weather. The hearthfire burned in its grate, orange and innocent. Only the warmth remained wrong—thick, greedy, sweating the walls—and only Zephyrine knew.

The memory lurched forward the way dreams do when they change scenes, days collapsing like wet paper.

* * *

An office. Smaller. Colder, though autumn had barely begun outside its window. Young Zephyrine stood before a desk, and across the desk sat a figure in a hooded cloak, face lost in shadow that had no business existing indoors, in daylight, with lamps lit. The hooded one's voice was pleasant. That was the horror of it—the voice of a helpful clerk, a friendly neighbor, someone you might buy bread from.

"—of course, the Guild of Impossible Things has openings," the hooded one was saying, sliding a contract across the desk, and Bramble's blood went cold around the words, because she knew that guild, she *lived* in that guild, she had signed its charter with her own hand. "Exiled persons of talent are our specialty. They'll take you in. They're good people." A pause, and the shadow where the face should be tilted, considering. "They'll never ask why you were exiled. That's in the terms. Very protective, our arrangements."

Zephyrine's young voice, shaking: "I saw—what I saw in the throne room—"

"Was nothing," the hooded one said kindly. "You saw nothing. You will continue to see nothing. The exile ensures it. Distance, forgetfulness, a life among strangers—these are such healing things. Sign here."

And Zephyrine—confused, grieving, half-convinced already by seven days of whispered doubts planted in colleagues' mouths, of documents altered, of their own memory made to seem unreliable—reached for the pen.

The memory ended there. Of course it ended there. The broker had sold what the crown had bought, and the crown had bought the erasure of everything after.

Bramble came back to herself kneeling on the workroom floor, both hands flat against the boards as if the room might pitch, sweat cold between her shoulder blades, Nell's laughter still bubbling up through the floorboards, oblivious, warm, alive.

*They'll never ask why you were exiled. That's in the terms.*

Corvane had those terms. Corvane had shown her the exile contract once, years ago, proud of the bargain he'd struck—fair price, clean papers, standard clauses. And one clause, odd but not unheard-of: the sanctuary-giver bound never to reveal the grounds of exile. Standard, he'd said. Protective.

Protective of whom?

* * *

Bramble sat for a long time with the empty vial in her hands.

Rain kept working at the shutters. Below, the guildhouse went on being the guildhouse—Osk's muffled triumph as the lock finally surrendered, Nell demanding to know if she could eat the celebration biscuit, the kettle starting its slow climb toward song. All of it resting, Bramble understood now, on top of a lie like a house built on a frozen lake, everyone walking around warm and certain while underneath, seven years down, something waited.

Because here was the arithmetic, and Bramble was a smuggler before anything else, and smugglers know cargo:

Zephyrine had seen the king die—or worse than die, *be replaced*—and had been exiled for it by arrangement, by contract, by design. Someone in a hood had engineered the whole of Zephyrine's last seven years. Had placed them, precisely and deliberately, in the one guild in the realm that traded in secrets, where a broker could later sell a copy of the erased memory back into circulation. Where it could sit on a shelf waiting for Zephyrine to find their own younger face staring out of it during a routine audit.

Or maybe that was paranoia. Maybe the hooded broker was only a broker, the exile only an exile, and the memory had come to her shelf by chance, the way all cargo comes.

But Bramble had handled ten thousand memories, and she knew forgery the way a jeweler knows paste. This one was true. Every grain of it. Which meant the thing on the throne was true, which meant the long winter pressing at the kingdom's edges like a thumb testing fruit was not weather at all, but appetite.

And Zephyrine did not remember. The exile had done its work; the memory-vial held what Zephyrine's own mind had been made to release. They walked around downstairs balancing their ledgers, keeping their head down, mourning a gift they thought they'd merely misused, believing their exile was justice, or accident, or fate—and none of it was. They had been *filed away*. Shelved. Like contraband.

So: tell them, or protect them?

Bramble turned the question over the way she turned contraband, looking for seams. If she told, Zephyrine would act. Zephyrine would march into Corvane's study with the truth in their teeth, and Corvane—bound by the contract, forbidden even to say *why* he couldn't say—would stonewall, or panic, or do something desperate to protect the guild's neutrality, and the family would fracture at exactly the moment the crown had declared a Long Hunt and every gifted soul needed every other gifted soul. And if Zephyrine acted alone—if they went at the throne with seven buried years of fury—they'd burn. Storm-caller or not, one witness against a kingdom wearing a mask.

If she protected them—kept the vial, logged it as unverified stock, buried it in the cellar among the other impossible things—then Zephyrine stayed safe. Stayed blind. Stayed *hers*, in the small selfish corner of her heart where she kept the people she loved and the reasons she'd stop at nothing to keep them. Safe and blind and slowly freezing along with the rest of the realm, because the Ember drank feeling first and Zephyrine had already had seven years of feeling drunk from them, hadn't they, wasn't that exactly what an exile was—

No. She set the thought down. That way lay the hooded broker's logic: distance, forgetfulness, a life among strangers. Such healing things.

Bramble had built her whole trade on a single article of faith: that nothing anyone needed to survive should ever be sold, and nothing true should ever be locked away, not even—*especially* not even—from the person it belonged to. The memory in the basin had belonged to Zephyrine since the moment a younger Zephyrine stood in a throne room and refused to look away. She was only its courier. Couriers deliver.

But oh, the cost. She could already see it: the vertigo in Zephyrine's eyes when the years came back, the grief for the self they'd lost, the terrible arithmetic of realizing that their found family's sanctuary had been the instrument of their silencing. You can't unknow a thing like that. You can't pour it back in the vial. Whatever Zephyrine had been before tonight—clerk, keeper, quiet survivor of their own history—they would not be that person after Bramble opened her mouth.

Loyalty said: protect them. Truth said: they are not yours to protect. Loyalty said: they are happy, or near enough. Truth said: their happiness is a room the Ember built, furnished with their stolen years.

Bramble closed her fist around the empty vial until the glass bit.

Then she rose, tucked the vial into her coat where the most dangerous cargo always rode, and went down the stairs on legs that felt borrowed, through the warm smell of bread and lamp oil, into the common room where Nell was teaching Osk a song and the kettle sang along badly and Zephyrine sat in the corner with the ledgers, pen scratching, static whispering under their skin, utterly unaware that the ground floor of their life had just been pulled out from under them.

Zephyrine looked up. Saw Bramble's face. And Bramble watched, in real time, as seven years of trained observation did their work—the pen slowing, stopping, the gray eyes narrowing with the particular attention Zephyrine gave to accounts that wouldn't balance.

"You've been decanting," Zephyrine said quietly. Not a question. "What came through?"

Bramble opened her mouth. Closed it. Thought of the hooded voice, pleasant as bread: *they'll never ask why.*

"Sit down," she said, and heard her own voice come out strange, "and pour us both something strong. Because I bought a memory today, Zeph, and I need you to tell me—" her throat tightened around it, the loyalty and the truth strangling each other on the way out, "—I need you to tell me if you recognize the face in it. Because it's yours."

Across the common room, unnoticed, the fire in the guildhouse hearth flickered—just once—black at its edges, like a page curling.
