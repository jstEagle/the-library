## 6. Hunters at the Door

Osk felt them coming before she heard them—felt it in the locks.

She had forty-one locks in the guildhouse of the Impossible Things, and only nine of them lived on actual doors. The rest were stranger stock: a lock on Tuesday itself, so that no one could enter the house before noon without her leave; a lock on the cellar's memory, which forgot its own contents unless spoken to gently; a small brass thing on the gap between one heartbeat and the next, which had cost her three fingers' worth of sensation and was worth every shivering hour. Locks were not, strictly speaking, her gift. Her gift was knowing where things could be shut. Doors were merely the most honest example.

That morning, all forty-one of them woke at once.

It came as a taste first—iron filings and cold candle smoke—and then as a vibration in the wards, each lock humming a half-tone sharp, like instruments tuning for a piece none of them wanted to play. Osk set down her tea. She was in the front parlor, ostensibly mending the hinge on a cabinet that held nothing but a jar of borrowed silence, and she watched the hinge's shadow shiver against the wall.

"Hunters," she said to the empty room, conversationally, because talking to rooms was another habit of her trade. "And not the polite sort."

The polite sort knocked. These would arrive with writs and torch-staves and boots polished for the burning. Vellsmark was four days gone into ash by now—four days since the wind had carried the smell of it over the hills, sweetish and wrong, like a bakery fire full of hair—and Osk had spent those four days doing what she did best. She had locked things.

She went through the house room by room, touching wood and stone and air. The ledger room: sealed, its door now remembering being a wall. The stair to Bramble's decanting loft: locked behind a lock made of miscounted steps, so that anyone climbing it would always find one more than they'd climbed and grow quietly, uselessly uneasy. Nell's cubby under the eaves: the girl herself was out scavenging, thank whatever gods still took calls, but her blanket-smell of foxes and chalk was everywhere, and Osk folded it away behind a lock of unremarkable dust. Corvane's study she left alone; the guild master kept his own wards, older and meaner than hers, and he did not like her touching his work.

By the time the hooves sounded on the cobbles of Cindermarket Row, Osk stood in the guildhouse doorway wearing her second-best smile and an apron that said *ordinary* in every stitch.

Six riders dismounted beneath the sign of the Impossible Things—a cracked bell hanging from an iron bracket, which rang, when the wind caught it right, with no sound at all. Their armor was white enameled steel gone grey at the joints, and their livery bore the new device: a flame inside a closed eye. The Long Hunt's mark. It had been stitched onto the kingdom's imagination only that season, and already it looked ancient, the way fear makes things old.

Osk counted the way other people counted coins. Six hunters. Five were furniture—big men with the flat eyes of those paid to hold a line. The sixth was the commander, and Osk knew it before the woman removed her helm because her locks told her so. Every ward on the building leaned toward her like grass toward weather.

"Morning," Osk said, cheerful as bread. "If you've come about the rent, we're paid through the quarter."

"We've come about ledgers." The commander's voice was low and even, a voice that had been sanded down until nothing sharp remained. She pulled off her gauntlets finger by finger, unhurried. "Every guildhouse trading in unregistered goods keeps accounts. We are here to read yours."

"Accounts, certainly." Osk spread her hands, ink-stained, calloused, harmless. "Though I warn you, madam, ours are dull enough to constitute a weapon. We once put a tax assessor to sleep mid-column. He apologized on waking."

Something moved at the corner of the commander's mouth—not quite a smile, more the place a smile had been excavated from. "Serah Vail," she said, producing a writ sealed in black wax. "Commander of the Second Vigil. You'll have heard of Vellsmark."

"Heard of the ash," Osk said. "Wind carried it right past our windows. Terrible waste of a good city."

"Waste." Vail looked up at the crooked bell, at the shuttered upper windows, at the sign whose lettering had been painted over so many times it read differently depending on the light. "Is that what you'd call it?"

"I'd call most burning a waste. Fire's never been much of a reader."

Vail handed her the writ instead of answering, which Osk understood to be a courtesy of exactly the kind that preceded unpleasantness. The seal crumbled under her thumb—black wax, and inside the wax, if you knew how to feel, a thread of something that made her back teeth ache. Ember-wax. The crown's own fire, grown strange. She filed that away in the drawer of her mind reserved for things to be terrified of later, and stepped aside.

"Do come in," she said. "Mind the third stair. It lies."

* * *

They went through the ground floor like a slow tide. Two hunters turned shelves; one ran a gloved hand along the wainscoting, tapping for hollow places; the last stood in the doorway facing outward, watching the street, watching for the neighbors who had suddenly found urgent business elsewhere. Cindermarket Row had learned fast. Ash travels quicker than news.

Vail herself walked the rooms the way Osk walked locks—with attention. That was what unsettled Osk most, more than the writ or the wax or the smell of cold iron. Most hunters swept. Vail *read*. She paused at the parlor hearth where Zephyrine's storm-static had scorched a faint fern-pattern into the brick seven years ago, though any ordinary eye would see only soot. She lingered at the window where the light came through at an angle the geometry of the street didn't permit, because Osk had locked the morning sun into that pane years back and never gotten round to releasing it. At each oddity she stopped, considered, and moved on without comment, and each time she moved on, Osk's stomach dropped a little further, because a hunter who misses nothing and says nothing is keeping score.

"You keep an unusual house, Mistress...?"

"Just Osk. Like the sound a coin makes dropping."

"Like the sound," Vail repeated softly, "a coin makes dropping." She turned a small glass bottle on the mantel—the thunderclap, bottled by Zephyrine's predecessor, still muttering in its sleep—and set it down exactly where it had stood, to the finger's width. "Your guild trades in impossible things. That's legal, technically. The statutes haven't caught up to impossibility." Her eyes were grey, the grey of a blade kept clean. "But the Long Hunt isn't about legality. It's about inventory. Everything gifted gets registered. Everything registered gets used. And everything that refuses—" she nodded, once, toward the eastern sky, where Vellsmark wasn't anymore, "—gets counted differently."

"And which column am I in, Commander? I'm only a locksmith."

"Mm." Vail picked up a poker, weighed it, set it down. "You know what I noticed at your threshold, Mistress Osk? Your door has no draft. Every door in this city breathes a little. Yours holds its breath." She said it mildly, almost idly, the way one remarks on weather. "Doors that hold their breath are listening."

Osk kept the smile nailed in place while everything behind it rearranged itself. "Old joinery. Good oak doesn't gossip."

"No," Vail agreed. "It doesn't. People do. People who ought to be careful." And then, so quietly that the two hunters in the hall couldn't possibly have caught it, she added: "Where do you keep the ledgers?"

"The ledger room's through there—" Osk began, gesturing grandly at a closet containing mops.

"Not the ones you want me to see."

The clock in the hall chose that moment to strike ten, which was fortunate, because Osk had locked it weeks ago so that it struck only when someone lied in its hearing, and she needed the beat of cover. She laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and saw Vail register the laugh, and file it, the way she filed everything.

"Mistress Osk." Vail drew closer, close enough that Osk could smell her: leather, snow, and underneath both a faint burnt-sugar sweetness that Osk's whole body flinched from before her mind named it. Gift-signature. Faint, banked, disciplined—the scent of a talent kept in a drawer and sat upon. "I inspected the chandler's guild yesterday. Their ledgers were immaculate. Do you know why? Because they burned the true ones the night the proclamation went up, and the man who burned them wept the whole time, and I let him think I didn't notice." Her voice stayed level as a frozen pond. "I notice everything. It is my single flaw as a servant of the crown. So understand me when I say: I am going to find what this house is hiding, whether it takes today or takes a month. The only question your guild gets to answer is whether finding it costs anyone anything."

* * *

In the end, it was the ledgers themselves that saved them—or rather, the ledgers Osk let her find.

This was Corvane's instruction, delivered that dawn by a note slipped under her door in his crabbed, beautiful hand: *Give them true books. Truth is the best lock; nobody believes it.* So Osk unlocked the real ledger room—she could feel the false wall sigh with relief as she did—and fetched down the guild's actual accounts, and Vail sat at the long table for two hours reading them while the hunters prowled and the house held its breath in forty-one places.

And the terrible, wonderful joke of it was that the ledgers were damning. Anyone who truly read them would see it. *One bottled thunderclap, sold to a miller in Harrowfen, payment: a memory of his father singing.* *One borrowed year, lent to a dying woman, repayment deferred indefinitely.* *One grief, lifted from a widower at cost, stored in the east cellar, jar unnumbered.* The Guild of Impossible Things wrote its sins in a fair hand with generous margins, because Corvane had never believed in hiding what the guild was—only in keeping it small enough to survive.

Vail read every word. Osk watched her do it, poured tea she didn't drink, made jokes that landed nowhere, and waited for the world to end.

Near noon, Vail closed the final volume and sat very still for a long moment.

"These entries," she said at last. "The memories purchased. Where do they come from?"

"Sellers come to us. Grief-heavy folk, mostly. Folk who'd trade a bad year for bread." Osk shrugged elaborately. "We're not thieves, Commander. We're pawnbrokers. There's a difference, though I grant it's thin."

"There was an entry dated seven years ago. A purchase made in memories—an unusually large lot. No seller listed. Only a broker's mark." Vail's finger rested on the page without touching it. "A hooded figure. Payment rendered in a single memory, described as *one witness, throne room, value beyond accounting*."

Osk's heart performed a maneuver she had not authorized. "I wouldn't know about that. Before my time."

"Before your time," Vail echoed. She looked up, and for a moment—for just a moment—the blade-grey went out of her eyes and something else looked through, tired and young and drowning. "Everything is before everyone's time. That's how it works. That's how it's *meant* to work." Then the grey returned, and she rose, and buckled her gauntlets, and became again the commander of the Second Vigil.

At the door she paused. The five hunters mounted up in the street, and the horses stamped, and the ash-smelling wind came down off the hills and stirred the cracked bell into its soundless ringing.

"Your accounts are in order," Vail said loudly, for the record, for the street, for the report she would file. "The Guild of Impossible Things presents no irregularities."

Then she leaned close, as if adjusting her stirrup, and spoke with her lips barely moving, words meant for Osk alone and pitched lower than shame:

"They're coming back within the fortnight, and it won't be me. Vellsmark wasn't a hunt, it was a harvest, and the harvest continues. Whatever your guild is hiding—your storm-caller, your smuggler, your girl with fox-smell in her hair—move it or bury it deep, because the next inspection won't read ledgers. It will read people." A pause, one heartbeat wide. "And tell whoever keeps your doors: the lock on the gap between heartbeats is elegant. I nearly missed it. I never miss twice."

She straightened, remounted, and rode, and the six of them dwindled down Cindermarket Row into the grey afternoon.

Osk stood in the doorway a long while. Then she looked at the doorframe, at the old oak beside her shoulder, and saw the letters cut there—fresh, shallow, done with a spur or a knife-tip while she'd been pouring tea, done by hands steady as her own locks:

**THE HEARTH IS HUNGRY. ASK WHO TENDS IT.**

She touched the grooves. They were warm.

Inside, faintly, from the direction of Bramble's loft, came the sound of a memory being decanted—the soft, wet chime of a truth leaving its bottle—and Osk, who locked things for a living, stood before the open door and realized with cold, perfect clarity that some things had already escaped, and that the woman who had just ridden away knew it too, and had, for reasons Osk could not yet count, chosen to leave the key.
