## 7. Corvane's Bargain

Seven years ago, on a night so cold the river forgot how to move, Corvane met the hooded broker at the drowned chapel beneath Tanner's Row.

He had been to a hundred such meetings in his long, careful life as guild master — trades conducted in whispers over candle stubs, contracts signed with blood and ash and other people's names. He knew the grammar of illicit commerce the way priests know liturgy. And yet nothing in that grammar had prepared him for the document the broker unrolled across the altar stone: an exile contract, written not in ink but in something darker, something that seemed to drink the candlelight rather than reflect it.

"You understand what you're buying," the broker said. The voice was neither man's nor woman's, but the sound of a fire burning down — warm once, perhaps, and hollow now.

"I understand it's a person," Corvane said.

The broker laughed, or performed the shape of laughter. "It's a storm wearing a person. Zephyrine Dunmore. Exiled by decree of the crown. No trial, no record, no grave for the name they buried. You are purchasing a ghost, Master Corvane, and ghosts are cheap."

"Everything is cheap when no one else will touch it." Corvane counted out the payment himself, coin by coin onto the wet stone: silver mostly, a few gold, and one item wrapped in oilcloth that made the broker go very still — a bottled thunderclap, harvested from a summer sky thirty years dead, worth more than the chapel they stood in. He had traded three impossible things from the guild's own vaults for this. Bramble would have wept. Osk would have called him a fool, and she would not have been wrong.

But he had seen the ledgers. That was the trouble with keeping accounts of everything — you saw patterns. A storm-caller erased without trial. A city's records burned in a single night. A king who had stopped appearing in public two years before his face started appearing everywhere again, smiling a little too evenly in every portrait. Corvane did not deal in truth, but he dealt in weight, and something about these entries weighed wrong.

"The contract has conditions," the broker said, sliding it across the stone.

Corvane read them twice. The first condition was simple enough: shelter, food, work, silence. The guild would take in the exile and ask no questions. Fine. He had never asked questions anyway; questions were bad for business.

The second condition stopped his breath.

*The keeper shall not, under any circumstance, reveal to the exile the cause or circumstance of their banishment. Should the exile learn why they were cast out, the contract is void, and the debt returns to the holder.*

"Debt," Corvane said slowly. "What debt?"

The broker gathered the coins with fingers too long for their sleeves. "You needn't worry about that, if you keep your side of it. Keep the storm-caller safe. Keep the storm-caller ignorant. Do those two small things, and the debt stays where debts belong — far away from you." The hood tilted, and though Corvane could see nothing within it, he felt observed the way a ledger feels observed: measured, weighed, found wanting. "You're a careful man. Careful men make excellent keepers."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I sell the contract to someone less gentle. There is a list, Master Corvane. It is shorter than you'd hope, and none of the others offer bread and a bed." The broker rose, and the candle flames all leaned toward them like courtiers. "Consider it charity, if it helps you sleep. The world is full of people who need hiding, and full of people willing to pay for the hiding. You simply happen to be paid in advance."

Corvane should have walked away. He told himself, for seven years afterward, that there had been no walking away — that refusing meant handing Zephyrine Dunmore to whatever waited at the bottom of that list. This was true. It was also true that he wanted the trade, wanted it the way misers want gold: the guild's greatest liability converted overnight into its greatest asset, a storm-caller behind his counters, bound to him by a contract he controlled. He was not a kind man pretending to be cruel. He was a greedy man pretending to be kind, and he had been doing it so long that even he sometimes lost track of which was the costume.

He signed.

His blood sank into the parchment and vanished, and somewhere far away — he would swear to this later, to Osk, to anyone who would listen, though no one ever quite believed him — something exhaled.

* * *

Zephyrine Dunmore arrived at the guildhouse four nights later, half-frozen, carrying one bag and a look on their face like a house whose owner had died inside it.

Corvane met them in the entry hall, beneath the sign of the impossible thing — a key that opened nothing, hanging from a chain of knotted string — and said the words he had rehearsed for four days: "You'll be safe here. We don't ask where people come from. We don't ask what they left behind."

He watched the relief move through them like weather. He watched the tension leave their shoulders one muscle at a time, watched them set down their bag as if setting down seven years of running, and he felt — God help him, he felt — proud. Look what I've done, he thought. Look at this ruined creature I've rescued.

And beneath the pride, quiet as frost forming, the second condition sat in his chest like swallowed glass: *never let them learn why.*

"Welcome to the Guild of Impossible Things," he said, and shook their hand, and felt static jump between their skin and his — a tiny, startled spark, the gift flinching awake at contact. Zephyrine snatched their hand back, mortified, already apologizing.

"It's fine," Corvane said. "We see stranger things than sparks around here." He smiled. It was, he would think much later, the first lie of ten thousand.

* * *

For seven years, Corvane kept the bargain the way he kept every ledger: meticulously, and with growing dread.

He learned the shape of Zephyrine's exile by learning its edges — all the places he must never step. When Zephyrine asked, in the early days, whether Corvane knew anything about the decree that had named them, he said no, and it was almost true; he knew only the weight of it, not the words. When Zephyrine wondered aloud, some nights over cheap wine, *why me? Why erase a bookkeeper? What did I possibly see?*, Corvane refilled their cup and changed the subject to tariffs. He became fluent in deflection. He could steer a conversation away from Zephyrine's past the way Osk picked locks — quickly, quietly, without leaving marks.

He told himself it was mercy. A wound doesn't heal faster for being looked at. Let them build a new life on top of the old one; the foundation didn't matter if the house stood.

But the glass in his chest grew.

Year by year, the guild knit itself around Zephyrine the way a tree grows around a fence post. Bramble taught them the memory-trade, laughing, spilling decanters, calling them "ledger-lark" until the nickname stuck. Osk grudgingly admitted that Zephyrine's arithmetic was better than hers and never forgave them for it. Nell arrived feral and nine years old and attached herself to Zephyrine like a burr to a coat. Corvane watched his collection of broken, gifted, glorious misfits become a family with Zephyrine at the center of it — the still point, the steady hand, the one who balanced what everyone else broke —

— and every time he saw them laugh, the contract's second clause tightened another notch.

Because he understood, eventually. He was slow, but he was thorough. A person is not exiled without trial for being dangerous. Dangerous people get trials, because trials are how you justify what happens next. A person is erased without trial for knowing something. And the broker's terms made the rest brutally plain: the crown — or whatever wore the crown — did not merely want Zephyrine gone. It wanted them *ignorant*. Forever. Someone had decided that Zephyrine Dunmore must never remember what they knew, and had built an entire architecture of exile to ensure it, and had hired Corvane — careful, greedy, comfortable Corvane — as the lock on the door.

He wasn't a rescuer. He was a jailer who fed his prisoner well.

The knowledge came to him on an ordinary evening, third bell, in his office above the counting room. He had been reviewing the quarterly audit — Zephyrine's neat columns, their cramped, beautiful handwriting, the little marginal notes they made about clients' moods (*paid in memories again; seemed lighter afterward; worried about her*) — and he had looked at that handwriting for a long time and thought: *I know what this is. This is the handwriting of someone who trusts me.* And then, with the clarity of ice cracking: *and I am the reason they cannot trust themselves.*

He poured a drink. He did not drink it. He sat in the dark with the untouched cup until the candles burned to pools, and somewhere below him Zephyrine laughed at something Nell said, bright and brief through the floorboards, and Corvane put his head in his hands and understood that he had committed the only sin he had ever truly feared: not theft, not fraud, not any crime of the ledger, but the crime of standing between a person and their own life while calling it protection.

* * *

Now, in the present, with Vellsmark's ash still sifting down on the town like grey snow, Corvane stood at his window and watched Zephyrine in the courtyard below, teaching Nell to double-entry a shipment of bottled fog.

They were laughing. Nell had gotten the columns backwards — deliberately, probably, for the pleasure of the correction — and Zephyrine was showing exaggerated despair, both hands thrown up, the apprentice shrieking with delight. Ordinary morning. Ordinary light. Seven years of ordinary mornings, each one bought with a silence.

Bramble had come to him last night, white-faced, holding a decanter like it was a live coal. She hadn't said what was in the memory she'd decanted. She'd only asked, in a voice he'd never heard from her, "Corvane. What do you know about why Zephyrine was exiled?" And he had looked at her — honest Bramble, who lied beautifully to strangers and never once to family — and he had said, "Nothing. I never asked."

The lie had cost him more than any payment he'd ever made.

He watched Zephyrine in the courtyard and felt the contract's clause pressing against the back of his teeth. Because he knew now — had known since Vellsmark burned, since the hunt's commander appeared in the ledgers with a face that made his blood run cold — that the silence was no longer sustainable. The Ember had woken. The hunt had begun. And a contract built on ignorance was a dam built on sand, and the water was rising, and Corvane had exactly two choices left: break his word and tell Zephyrine the truth himself, or stand aside while the truth broke over all of them like the storm he'd spent seven years pretending didn't live in his bookkeeper's skin.

There was a third option, of course. There was always a third option for men like him. He could sell the problem upward, as he had sold everything upward his whole life. He had already received the inquiry — a discreet note, delivered yesterday by a courier whose boots were too good for his clothes: *The crown is aware the exile resides with you. Terms available for cooperation. Inquire at the usual channels.*

Terms available. His guild's survival, priced against one person's freedom. The same arithmetic the broker had offered him seven years ago, dressed in new clothes.

Corvane took out the exile contract — he kept it locked in the iron box beneath his desk, and he hated that he had never destroyed it, hated that some cautious, cowardly corner of him had always kept the receipt — and read the clauses one final time by daylight.

Then he heard the hooves.

Not one rider. Many. The particular disciplined rhythm of crown cavalry coming up the hill road toward the guildhouse, unhurried, confident, the way men ride when they intend to arrive. Below him, Nell's laughter cut off mid-note. Zephyrine looked up at the gate, and Corvane saw their whole body change — the static rising in their skin, visible even from the window, a faint blue shimmer along their forearms like a storm deciding.

They know, Corvane thought. They're finally going to learn. And every word of it will be true, and every word will be my fault.

He put the contract in his pocket, went down the stairs two at a time, and went to open the door before the knocking started — the last act of protection he had left to give, and the first honest one in seven years.
