## 8. Running on Thunder

The storm had been sitting on the city for three days like a creditor who refused to leave.

Zephyrine felt it the way a man with a splinter feels every doorframe he passes—each pressure change dragging along nerves they had spent seven years teaching to be quiet. Rain came down in gray ropes. The gutters of Copperlane ran loud enough to drown thought. And beneath it all, under their skin, in the marrow-deep place where the gift coiled and pretended to sleep, something turned over.

*Not yet,* Zephyrine told it, as they told it every day. *Not yet. Not ever, if I'm lucky.*

But luck was a currency the guild no longer traded in.

They were in the counting room past midnight, reconciling October's impossible accounts—a bottled thunderclap sold twice by accident, a borrowed year returned at half its length with an apology note attached—when Nell came skidding through the doorway in her socks, hair wild, breath coming in white rags.

"They're coming," she said. "Osk says—they're *coming*, Zeph."

Zephyrine set down the pen with exaggerated care, because if they did not do things with exaggerated care tonight, they would do them with shaking hands. "How many?"

"Twelve." Nell's voice cracked around the number. "Maybe more. Serah Vail's own company this time. No ledger inspection. They brought chains, Zeph. Iron ones. The kind Osk can't argue with."

From somewhere below came three sharp knocks, then two, then one—the guild's oldest signal, older than Corvane's mastership, passed from smuggler to smuggler like a folk song: *hunters at the door.*

Zephyrine stood. And the static under their skin stood with them.

* * *

The guildhouse had always been built for hiding. Its corridors bent where corridors shouldn't; its staircases counted differently going up than going down; Osk had locked so many doors that weren't doors over the years that the building had developed a sort of architectural shyness. But hiding was a language, and the Long Hunt spoke it fluently now. Boots in the courtyard. Torches hissing in the rain. The flat, official voice of a sergeant reading charges off wet paper.

"—by order of His Majesty, the storm-caller known as Dunmore is to surrender for registration and judgment—"

*His Majesty.* The words landed wrong in Zephyrine's chest, the way they had landed wrong since Bramble had decanted that memory into the blue glass vial. A thing wearing Aldric's face wanted them in irons. A thing that crawled out of a hearthfire seven years ago and put on a crown had sent twelve armed men through the rain to collect the one witness who had seen it happen.

Corvane met them at the top of the cellar stair, candle in hand, his long face lit from below so he looked like a man being shown his own grave. Behind him, Bramble pressed a satchel of memory-vials against her chest like a mother gathering children. Osk stood at the courtyard window with her palms flat against the glass, reading the locks of the night itself.

"There are too many," Corvane said quietly. "And they have the writ signed in the king's name. If we fight them here, we're not fugitives anymore, Zephyrine. We're rebels. Everything ends tonight, one way or the other."

"Everything ended when Vellsmark burned," Zephyrine said. "We've just been pretending otherwise. Very well organized pretending. You'd be proud. It's in the ledgers and everything."

Something almost like a smile crossed the old man's mouth and died there. "I bought you safety once."

"I know." Zephyrine looked at him—at the guilt he thought he'd buried deep enough that even a keeper of ledgers couldn't audit it—and chose, for now, not to say what they knew about hooded brokers and exile contracts. "It kept. Seven years. That's better interest than most investments earn."

Below, the front door boomed. Once. Twice. Osk's locks would hold—but Osk had gone pale as paper, and Osk never went pale.

"The iron," she said, without turning. "It's not just iron. Someone taught them how to make it hungry. It's eating my locks, Zeph. It's eating them like they're *food*."

The second boom came, and the third, and then a sound none of them liked: wood giving up.

* * *

Afterward, Zephyrine would try to remember which came first—the door breaking or the storm breaking—and never quite manage it. Memory, like everything else in the world, insisted on keeping accounts, and some entries refused to balance.

The hunters poured into the entrance hall with torches and wet steel, six of them, then eight, boots grinding guildhouse grit into the floorboards that Nell had scrubbed every morning for three years. Serah Vail stood at their head, rain streaming off her gray cloak, her sword still sheathed. Her eyes found Zephyrine across the hall with the terrible precision of someone who had already read every page of the book she was holding.

"Dunmore." Even her voice was dry, somehow, above the rain. "You'll come quietly. I'd prefer it. So would you."

Zephyrine opened their mouth to answer—to bargain, to lie, to buy the family time with whatever coin a cornered accountant had left—and felt the static rise instead.

Seven years of holding it down. Seven years of willow-bark tea and breathing exercises and counting backward from a thousand whenever thunder rolled, of pressing their palms flat to table-tops during storms and reciting shipping manifests until the lightning in their blood forgot its own name. Seven years of a life built on top of a sealed well.

The seal did not crack. It *shattered*.

It began as light under the skin—Zephyrine watched their own forearms go luminous, veins filling with white fire like rivers seen from very high up—and then the smell hit the room, ozone sharp enough to taste, and every candle guttered sideways though no wind moved, and the hunters' torches bent toward Zephyrine like flowers toward an ugly sun.

"No," Zephyrine whispered, to the gift, to the room, to themselves. "*No—not here—*"

Here was exactly where it went.

Lightning came down through the roof.

Not from outside—from *inside*, from Zephyrine, out through the crown of their skull and up through the rafters, splitting the ancient beam overhead with a sound like the sky tearing linen, and the whole hall went white. Zephyrine heard themselves scream and could not tell whether it was pain or joy and suspected, with a vertigo that would stay with them forever, that it was both. Seven years of held breath leaving all at once. The storm had been sitting on the city for three days, and it recognized its own, and it *answered*.

When sight returned, the entrance hall was a cathedral of falling sparks. Half the hunters lay on the floor, twitching, alive—Zephyrine would count the heartbeats later, obsessively, gratefully—all their weapons fused to screeching slag. The great door hung off one hinge. Rain blew in sideways, hissing on scorched stone.

And Serah Vail stood untouched in the middle of it, cloak smoking faintly, staring at Zephyrine with an expression that was not fear.

It was recognition.

"*There* you are," she said softly. Almost kindly. Then, louder, to the hunters struggling up behind her: "Iron! Now!"

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters and mercies always do—too quickly for accounting, too completely for forgetting.

The hunters rushed. Zephyrine's gift, drunk on seven years of famine, lashed out again—a whip-crack of arc-light across the hall—and one hunter went down screaming and another dove clear, but there were too many, there had always been too many, and the iron manacles were in Serah's hands now, open, reaching, and Zephyrine felt the hunger in them, the cold pull of metal made to drink magic, and knew with perfect clarity: *one touch and it's over, they take the storm out of me like a tooth.*

Then Nell was there.

She came out of nowhere the way she always did, small and quick and utterly without sense, and she threw herself between the manacles and Zephyrine's wrists with her arms spread wide, a sixteen-year-old girl standing in the path of the crown's iron like she was standing in a doorway against weather.

"Nell—" Zephyrine reached for her. Too slow. Too slow by half a heartbeat, by the width of a falling spark.

The manacles closed on her forearm.

Nell made a sound Zephyrine had never heard a person make—a thin, folded-up sound, the sound of something inside her going out—and dropped. And the static in Zephyrine's blood, the gift that had slept and starved and begged for seven years, looked at the girl crumpled on the stones with frost-white lines crawling up her arm from the iron's bite, and stopped being weather at all.

It became a verdict.

Zephyrine did not decide anything. Deciding was for people whose blood wasn't singing. The storm came out of them in a ring—a full circle, a halo laid flat, expanding outward through the hall and up through the broken roof and out into the courtyard beyond, and every torch in the guildhouse died, and every lock Osk had ever placed sang one clear harmonic note, and the iron manacles burst apart in Nell's slack hand like a nut cracked by winter.

Silence. Rain. The ringing in Zephyrine's ears shaped almost like bells.

Bramble got to Nell first. Osk got there a breath behind, hands flying over the girl's arm, muttering in the language she used for locks that fought back. Nell's eyes fluttered. Focused on Zephyrine. And she smiled—smiled, the absolute wretch—with lips gone gray as slate.

"Told you," she slurred. "Told you I'd be useful."

"You absolute *fool*—" Zephyrine dropped to their knees beside her, and the storm under their skin, still roaring, still ravenous, did the last thing Zephyrine expected: it gentled. It poured down their arms and out through their fingertips in threads too fine to see, wrapping the blackened veins of Nell's arm, holding her heart to its rhythm the way you cup a matchflame in wind. Not healing. Zephyrine couldn't heal. But *keeping*. Keeping her here, keeping her warm, keeping her theirs.

"She'll live," Osk said, and her voice shook only a little. "The iron took something. I don't know what yet. But she'll live—if we move *now*, while every hunter in that courtyard is lying in a puddle reconsidering his career."

Serah Vail was gone. Zephyrine noticed it with a jolt—the commander had simply vanished between one lightning stroke and the next, and the remaining hunters were stumbling upright in the rain, leaderless, dazed, making no move to press the attack. Through the shattered doors, in the courtyard, torches were regrouping at a distance. Voices argued. Nobody charged.

They were afraid. For seven years Zephyrine had been a clerk, a ghost, a rumor in a ledger. In ninety seconds they had become the thing witch-hunters told stories about.

Corvane appeared in the hall's ruined doorway, and Zephyrine braced for the order—*stay, hide, bury deeper, we can still fix this*—but the old man looked at the burning hole in his roof, at Nell's gray face, at the fused slag that had been the guild's beautiful front door, and he closed his eyes like a man closing a ledger he can no longer bear to audit.

"Get her wrapped," he said hoarsely. "Get everything that matters. We're done pretending this house can hold any of us."

"Corvane—" Bramble started.

"They'll come back with a hundred, and next time they'll burn it with us inside, and they'll call it justice in the morning papers." He opened his eyes, and they were wet, and furious, and free in a terrible way. "The Guild of Impossible Things is neutral. The Guild of Impossible Things has always been neutral. So we'd best become something else before dawn, hadn't we? Something with no charter to violate." He looked at Zephyrine, and there was seven years of guilt in the look, and something underneath the guilt that might, finally, have been love. "Go pack. All of you. Quickly."

* * *

They left through the cellar, because of course they did; the guild had always kept a door for exactly this night, and Osk had been oiling its hinges in secret for months, saying nothing, hoping nothing, preparing for everything.

The tunnel came up in the tanner's yard on Midden Row, where the rain fell soft and ordinary, as if the world hadn't split open an hour ago. Nell rode on Bramble's back, wrapped in two cloaks, her bandaged arm tucked against the memory-smuggler's shoulder blades, drifting in and out of a fever-sleep. Osc walked rear guard with her crossbow loaded and her jaw set. Corvane carried the guild's charter itself, rolled and waxed, though God knew what he intended to do with a document that had just died.

At the mouth of the alley, Zephyrine stopped and looked back.

The guildhouse stood against the storm-dark with its new wound glowing dull orange at the crown—roofbeams exposed like ribs, rain falling into the counting room where the impossible accounts lay abandoned, half-reconciled. Lightning flickered far off over the hills, and Zephyrine felt it the way they used to feel everything: a tug, a kinship, a call.

But different now. Not caged anymore. *Companioned.*

Their hands still smoked faintly. Their whole body hummed like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing. It should have been terrifying—it *was* terrifying, terror sat in their stomach like swallowed coal—but underneath the terror ran something they had forgotten the shape of, something from before the exile, from before the throne room, from the girl-or-boy-they'd-been who had once called storms down over the summer fields just to watch the wheat dance.

Joy. Raw, guilty, incandescent joy.

"We can't ever go back, can we," Bramble said quietly beside them. Not a question. She shifted Nell's weight. "Whatever happens next. Whatever we find at the end of this road. That person who hid in the counting room—that life's ash."

Zephyrine watched the lightning walk the horizon, counting the seconds between flash and thunder the way they'd counted everything for seven years. One. Two. Three. Closer than yesterday. Everything closer than yesterday.

"No," they said. "But look what we're taking with us."

Ahead, Nell stirred against Bramble's shoulder and murmured something in her fever—two words, repeated, like a refrain, like a name she was learning:

"...the capital... the capital..."

Zephyrine went cold in a way the storm couldn't explain. Because the capital was where the Ember sat wearing a dead man's smile. Because the road there ran through a winter that had no business existing in October. Because Nell was delirious and frostbit and half-dead, and even so, some part of her—some stubborn, unreasonable, utterly certain part—was already pointing the way.

Osk unhooded a small lantern. Its light fell on five faces, rain-wet and exhausted and, impossibly, grinning.

"Well?" she said. "Which way, storm-caller?"

Zephyrine looked down the dark street, north, toward the throne and the thing upon it, and felt the thunder in their chest answer before their mouth could.

"North," they said. "We're going to give the king back his name."

The storm, rolling in off the hills, seemed to agree.
