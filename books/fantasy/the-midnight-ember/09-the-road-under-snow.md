## 9. The Road Under Snow

Nell had never seen snow before. She'd heard of it, of course—Bramble kept a jar of it in the guildhouse cellar, bought from a northern trader three winters back, and Osk used to shake it over the windowsill at midwinter to make the apprentices shriek with delight. But hearing of a thing and walking through it were different animals entirely.

The first flakes came down on the second day out from Ashford Hollow, drifting sideways through a sky the color of old pewter, and Nell caught one on her tongue like she'd been told children in the north did.

"It tastes like nothing," she said, disappointed.

"Everything does now," Zephyrine said quietly, and would not say more.

By the third day the snow was knee-deep and still falling, and the hedgerows wore white caps as though the whole world had dressed itself for a funeral. And this was wrong, wrong in the marrow of it, because it was only the last week of Harvestmonth and the apples should still be hanging heavy on the boughs. Nell knew this because she knew every month by its food, the way other people knew them by their saints' days. There should be cider. There should be roasting chestnuts and late honey and the smell of woodsmoke mixed with ripe plums going soft in the orchards.

There was none of that. There was only the white hush, deepening daily, and the five of them trudging through it toward a capital that had forgotten how to be warm.

* * *

They walked in a loose line: Corvane at the front because he could not bear not to be, Zephyrine just behind him with their hood up and their hands buried in their sleeves, then Bramble with her pack of bottled memories clinking softly at her hip, then Osk, who never walked where anyone expected her to be, and Nell at the rear, which suited her fine. From the rear you could watch everyone at once. From the rear you could see how tired they all were, and how frightened, though only Bramble admitted it aloud.

Nell did not mind being afraid. Fear was just another feeling, and feelings were the only currency she'd ever been rich in. What frightened her—not scared her, but *frightened* her, in the way of a thing too large to look at straight—was how quiet the villages were.

The first one they passed through was called Merrow's End, according to a signpost half-swallowed by snow. It should have held forty souls, maybe fifty. Nell counted the chimneys. Smoke rose from most of them, thin and gray, so the people were still there. The people were there, but the village was empty anyway.

A woman stood in a doorway watching them pass. She was shelling peas—Nell saw the bowl, the green little piles—and she watched them with eyes like two coins left out in the rain. When Nell smiled at her, the woman's mouth moved into something shaped like a smile, the way a glove holds the shape of the hand that once filled it.

"Good harvest to you," Nell said, because it was Harvestmonth and you said it, everyone said it, it was the smallest prayer there was.

The woman looked down at her bowl of peas. "I don't remember why I'm doing this," she said, not unkindly. Just stating it, the way you'd state the weather. "I don't remember what they're for." And she went on shelling, pea after pea, into a bowl that no one would ever cook from.

Nell walked faster after that. She didn't look back.

* * *

That night they sheltered in a hay barn whose farmer let them stay without asking a single question, which was itself an answer. He brought them barley broth and bread without salt, set it down among them like a man feeding animals he couldn't afford to keep, and when Bramble tried to pay him he waved the coins away and said, "What would I buy?"

He meant it literally. That was the worst part. He stood there genuinely wondering what money was *for*, and then he went back to his house where his wife sat staring at the fire, and Nell heard him say to her, gentle as anything, "Do you want to eat tonight? I can't recall if you'll want to."

Osk waited until his footsteps faded, then said what they were all thinking. "It drinks the feeling first. Before it takes anything else."

"The Ember," Zephyrine said. They said the name the way you'd spit out a bad tooth.

"It takes joy first," Bramble murmured. She had her memory-jar open in her lap, letting a little golden warmth leak between her palms, and even that much made the barn feel less like a tomb. "I've smuggled enough of it to know the shape of theft. Joy goes first because it's easiest to carry. Then hope. Then grief—grief takes longer, it's heavier, but it goes too, eventually. What's left is—" she gestured vaguely at the farmhouse, the peas, the whole silent white country, "—this. Bodies that eat and sleep and work. A kingdom of people keeping themselves alive out of habit."

"Then we're carrying the most valuable cargo in the realm," Nell said.

Everyone looked at her.

"Well, we are." She shrugged, feeling her ears go hot. "You're all full of feelings. Real ones, loud ones. If the Ember eats joy, then every laugh we manage is worth more than gold right now. So we ought to spend it freely. That's what my gran always said about anything you can't keep—spend it while it spends."

Corvane made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "Your grandmother sounds like she'd have made a fine guildmaster."

"She'd have made a terrible one. She was honest." Nell grinned until, reluctantly, Corvane's mouth twitched, and Osk snorted, and even Zephyrine—hood back, face pale as the snow outside—let out something that was almost, almost a breath of laughter.

The barn felt warmer after that. Not actually warmer; Nell checked, pressing her palm to the boards. But some other kind of warmer, and she decided that counted.

* * *

On the fifth day they found the wedding.

Or rather, they found what was left of it—a village chapel with its doors thrown open, garlands of dried rowan berries sagging over the lintel, tables set out in the yard under a foot of snow with the feast still laid upon them. Bread gone hard as brick. A honey cake wearing a white cap of frost. Jugs of small beer frozen and burst, their contents spilled in amber sheets that had frozen mid-pour, gleaming like something precious.

Nobody had come back for any of it.

"They left mid-feast," Osk said softly, circling the tables. "Look—the benches pushed back all at once. No coats on the pegs. They just... stopped."

"No," Bramble said. She had gone very still, one hand pressed flat against a table's edge, and her voice came out strange and thin. "No, they didn't stop. Look closer. The cups are drained. The plates are cleared. They finished the feast. They sang—I can hear the shape of it in the walls, they sang the long version, the one with all eleven verses—they danced until the candles drowned in their own wax. And then they went inside and sat down, and nobody ever picked the cups up again, because picking up the cups would mean the day was over, and the day being over would mean..." She trailed off. Her fingers curled against the wood. "The walls don't remember anyone leaving. Only stopping."

Nell touched the honey cake. It was beautiful even spoiled, iced with frost instead of sugar, and she thought about the hands that had made it, hands that had beaten the eggs and licked the spoon and been happy, and how those hands were probably somewhere inside one of the shuttered houses right now, folded in a lap, doing nothing, wanting nothing.

She cried a little. She didn't hide it, because hiding things was exhausting and she'd never learned the knack. Corvane put a hand on her shoulder, briefly, heavily, and said nothing, which from Corvane was practically a speech.

Zephyrine stood apart, looking east toward the capital, and the wind pulled at their coat, and Nell thought: *they know. They know what's doing this.* Whatever Zephyrine had seen in that stolen memory—and Nell had watched them grow quieter and quieter since Bramble had shown them whatever it was—it lived behind their eyes like a lamp behind a shuttered window.

Nell made a private vow, standing in that ruined wedding yard. She wasn't clever like Osk or brave in the fighting way or wise in the ledger way. But she could carry things. She'd carried water, coal, firewood, her gran's shopping, other people's secrets. She could carry this too—all of them, if it came to it. Somebody had to be the heart, and hearts mostly just had to keep beating, and that she knew how to do.

* * *

The cold got into everything after that.

Not the ordinary cold, either. Nell had known ordinary cold—drafty lofts, winter washing, the bite of a pump handle in Frostmonth. This was different. This cold seemed to have intentions. It found the gaps in your resolve the way water finds cracks in stone. By the eighth day Osk's jokes came slower. By the ninth, Bramble stopped singing under her breath while she walked, and the silence where her humming had been was louder than any noise.

And Nell's fingers—

It happened stupidly, the way real things happen. She'd slipped crossing a frozen ford, gone in to her wrists, laughed it off because everyone was already frightened and laughter was her job. She hadn't noticed until evening that two fingers on her left hand—the ring finger and the one beside it—had gone a smooth, waxy white, and when she pinched them they didn't hurt, and that was the frightening part, that they didn't hurt.

Osk saw her looking. Osk saw everything.

"Inside the glove. Now." Osk took Nell's hand in both of hers, and her own hands were shaking, though whether from fear or the cold Nell couldn't tell. "Bramble. Snow, packed snow, not ice—we have to warm them slow or she loses them."

"Loses them?" Nell asked, interested despite herself. Fingers seemed very far away when you were watching someone hold your hand like it might break.

"Fingers are negotiable," Osk said, with terrible gentleness. "Hands aren't. Hold still."

They made camp early, in a shepherd's bothy with a roof half caved in, and Bramble built a fire while Corvane packed snow around Nell's fingers like a man setting a bone, his jaw tight, saying nothing except once, very low, "You should have said. You should always say."

"But everyone was tired—"

"I don't care." His voice cracked like river ice. "Nell. Listen to me. You are seventeen years old and you have been walking at the back of this line for nine days making sure none of us fell behind, and you did not say your fingers were dying, because you thought it would cost us too much to hear it. Do you know what it costs me to hear it *now*?" He didn't wait for an answer. He didn't seem able to finish the thought himself. He just packed the snow tighter and bowed his head over her hand like a man praying.

Nell felt tears start and let them. Warm tears on a cold face, the best kind. "I'm sorry," she whispered.

"Don't be sorry. Be *loud*. You're the loud one. That's your whole job." And here Corvane, guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things, keeper of neutral accounts, looked up at her with wet eyes and said, "If you go quiet, girl, we all freeze. Do you understand? You're the fire we're walking toward. Don't you dare bank yourself."

* * *

The pain came back with the thawing, that night, and Nell discovered that pain was its own strange mercy. She lay wrapped in every blanket they owned, her throbbing hand bound in wool, listening to the others breathe around the dying fire.

Zephyrine sat awake across the embers, arms around their knees, watching the coals with an expression Nell recognized now—she'd seen it on the woman in Merrow's End, almost, except reversed. Where the villagers had faces with the feeling scooped out, Zephyrine had a face with too much feeling crammed in, held behind clenched teeth.

Nell shuffled over and sat beside them. She didn't ask. Asking was Osk's gift. Nell's gift was simply arriving, and staying.

After a long time, Zephyrine said, "Does it hurt badly?"

"Like bees live in my fingers."

"Good. Pain means they're yours still." A pause. The coals ticked and settled. "I had a thing taken from me once. Seven years ago. I only recently remembered having it." Their voice was very level, the levelness of a person holding a door shut against a gale. "Do you know what I miss most? Not the power. Everyone assumes you'd miss the power. I miss the *hum*. It was like a song under my skin all the time. Even asleep. Even afraid. I was never alone in my own body, before. Now the quiet in me is enormous."

Nell considered this with great seriousness, because Zephyrine deserved serious consideration. "Well," she said finally. "When we get your hum back—or your whatever-back—you can lend me some. For the nights the bees get loud."

That surprised a laugh out of them, small and rusty, and Nell counted it as payment rendered in full.

"You're not afraid," Zephyrine said. It wasn't quite a question. "We're walking into the seat of a thing that eats kingdoms, and you lost two fingers today, and you're not afraid."

"Oh, I'm terrified." Nell wiggled her bandaged hand cheerfully. "But being scared is just loving something that might get hurt. And I love all of you ridiculous people an awful lot. So." She shrugged. "Lots of scared. Comes with the territory."

Zephyrine looked at her for a long moment, firelight moving in their eyes, and then reached over and—carefully, so carefully, avoiding the bandaged fingers—squeezed her shoulder. "The Ember should be afraid of you," they said. "I mean that. It drinks joy, and you make it everywhere you go. You're contraband, Nell. Walking, talking contraband."

Nell glowed about that for the rest of the night, bees and all.

* * *

On the eleventh day the road began to climb through bare hills toward the river valley, and the capital's distant smoke-haze showed itself on the horizon like a bruise on the sky. And on the twelfth day, they saw the riders.

One rider. Alone, coming up the road behind them at an easy canter, black cloak, the crown's colors muted under road dust. Corvane had them off the road and into the rocks before Nell had even finished squinting at it, and they crouched in the shadow of a leaning stone while the hoofbeats came on and on—

And stopped.

"Guildmaster Corvane," said a woman's voice, dry as paper. "Four of your misfits and the apprentice. I counted your fires. I've been counting them for six days." A pause, and then, in a lower register, almost weary: "Come out. I'm not here for arrests. I'm here alone, and off the record, and I have something to show you that will cost me my life if the Ember learns of it. My name is Serah Vail. And I need to talk to the storm-caller about my sister's soul."

Nell, crouched in the snow with her bandaged hand pressed to her heart, felt the whole group go still as the frozen fields—and felt, beneath the fear, a small stubborn spark of something that the cold had not yet managed to touch.

Hope. Still burning. Contraband, all of it.
