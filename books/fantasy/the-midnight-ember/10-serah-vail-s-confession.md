## 10. Serah Vail's Confession

The road under the pines had gone quiet in the way Serah Vail had learned to distrust most.

Not the quiet of empty land—empty land breathed, creaked, settled. This was a held breath, a silence with people inside it. She counted them without looking: six heartbeats scattered through the campsite ahead, one of them small and fast as a wren's, one of them old and steady as a millstone. The storm-caller's was the hardest to pick out—a low hum underneath everything, like a wire singing before rain.

Seven years of hunting the gifted had taught her to hear what others couldn't. That was its own kind of gift, though she'd never once called it that aloud.

She left her horse tied two hundred paces back and walked the rest alone, no lantern, sword unbuckled and left across the saddle. If this went wrong, she wanted it wrong in only one direction—hers.

* * *

The camp was a poor one, she noted professionally. Fire built too high for fugitives, tucked into a hollow where smoke would pool like spilled milk. Nell—the half-feral apprentice, if the guildhouse records were worth anything—was asleep in a heap of Bramble's spare cloak, frost silvering her lashes. Two of the girl's fingers were wrapped in stained linen. Frostbite. They hadn't even had sense to camp near running water.

Osk sat awake at the hollow's rim, whittling something small, her eyes catching the firelight like a cat's. She saw Serah before Serah meant her to. Of course she did. Lock-witches always knew when something was about to be opened.

"Don't," Osk said quietly. "Whatever you've come to do—don't wake the girl."

"I came alone." Serah spread her hands, empty, and let the fire show it. "No escort. No writ. No record."

"There's always a record." Osk didn't move from her seat, but Serah felt the ground shift faintly under her boots—not physically, but in that locked-door way, the sense of hinges turning somewhere behind the world. "You wrote it yourself, Commander. *All gifted persons to be registered or burned.* Nice handwriting."

"Vellsmark wasn't my handwriting." The words came out of her rougher than she'd planned. She crouched by the fire's edge, close enough for warmth, far enough to be struck at. "I want you to wake the storm-caller. I have something to say that they need to hear, and I will only say it once."

"And if we decide you'll say nothing ever again?"

"Then your witch-friend dies knowing she was right not to trust me." Serah held the lock-witch's gaze. "But you won't. Because you've been watching the roads for three days, waiting for my company to catch up, and you know exactly how much time you bought with those false trails through the marsh. You're out of cleverness, Osk of the Nine Locks. All you have left is the choice of who walks into your camp. So choose me."

For a long moment there was only the fire cracking and the small wet sound of Nell breathing. Then Osk stood, unhurried, and nudged Zephyrine Dunmore's shoulder with the toe of her boot.

* * *

Zephyrine woke the way Serah imagined a storm would wake—if a storm could think, could be afraid. One instant stillness; the next, a pressure in the air that made her back teeth ache and the hairs on her arms lift. The static under their skin. Seven years buried and it came up through the snow like spring water finding a crack.

They looked at her across the fire, and she watched them take inventory: her missing sword, her missing escort, her missing writ. A ledger-keeper's eyes. Good. Ledgers were honest, or they were worthless.

"Serah Vail," Zephyrine said. "Commander of the Long Hunt."

"At present."

"You burned Vellsmark."

"No." She said it flatly, because anything softer would have been a lie dressed up. "I arrived after. I read the reports. I signed the confirmations. I did not light it—but I have stopped pretending there is a difference that matters. You may hate me for the whole of it. It's simpler."

Bramble was awake now, sitting up with a memory-vial cupped in her palm like a knife she'd decided not to draw. Corvane wasn't in camp—he'd gone on ahead toward the capital, buying passage, buying silence—and Serah noted his absence with a cold little twist of relief. Easier to confess to strangers than to a man whose guilt she could smell the way dogs smell fear.

"Why are you here?" Zephyrine asked. Not *what do you want*. Why. As if the why were the load-bearing wall.

Serah looked at the fire for a while. Then she reached into her coat and drew out the thing she had carried against her heart for seven years, wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it with fingers that refused, entirely, to shake.

A locket. Small, tarnished silver, shaped like an open hand.

Inside it, where a portrait should have been, there was nothing. And yet the space was not empty—it *shimmered*, faintly, the way air shimmers over a forge. Something was folded in there. Something alive.

"My sister's soul," Serah said. "Or the piece of it the Ember keeps. Her name was Wren. *Is* Wren. She was a song-healer in the capital, before music became sedition. When the Ember took the throne properly—seven years ago, the week the real winter began—it collected her. It collects the bright ones first. The ones whose gifts feed on feeling." She closed her fist around the locket until the edges bit her palm. "It told me, the day I took the commander's oath, that so long as I hunted its enemies well, Wren would stay warm. Folded small, but warm. Like bread in an oven. Like a sleeper, almost."

"And you believed it," Bramble said softly. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse—an understanding.

"I *knew* it," Serah said. "Belief is for things you can't verify. I verified. Every year, on the solstice, I'm permitted one hour in the vaults beneath the palace, to look at her. To make sure she's still—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and let it. "She's still there. Faint. But there. The Ember is careful with its hostages. It wants me obedient, not broken. A broken hound doesn't hunt."

Nell stirred in her sleep and murmured something, and every adult in the camp went silent until the child settled again. In that shared silence, Serah found the rest of it easier.

"I'm not asking you to forgive the Hunt. I'm not asking you to believe I'm good. I am neither of those things and I've stopped auditioning for either role." She turned the locket over in her fingers, once, twice. "I'm offering you the servant ways into the palace. The routes beneath it—the old kitchens, the ash-chutes, the corridors the Ember doesn't watch because it doesn't remember they exist. It erased so much of the castle's own history that it forgot the walls themselves. There are ways in that exist only in the memory of the stones, and I have walked every one of them."

Zephyrine had gone very still. "And the price?"

"One promise." Serah met their eyes. "When you unbind the king—when you free Aldric from whatever frozen fire it keeps him in—you find Wren Vail. You free her too. Not after. With. The same breath, if you can manage it. I don't care what it costs your storm, your ledger, your life. She goes out of that vault the same night he does, or I will spend everything I have making sure none of you leave the palace at all."

The fire popped. Snow ticked down through the pine branches, soft as ash.

"That's it?" Bramble said. "That's the whole bargain? Help us commit treason, betray your own command, and all you ask is your sister?"

"All I ask?" Serah laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "Memory-smuggler, I am asking you to help me do the one thing I've been too cowardly to do for seven years. Do you understand what I am? I trained the hunters who burned Vellsmark. I taught them how to find gifted children by teaching their parents to inform on them. I built the machine that hunts you, bolt by bolt, because every bolt kept Wren warm another winter." She looked down at her hands—clean hands, officer's hands, hands that had never personally held a torch and had personally ordered a hundred. "The Ember thinks I'm its best weapon. Let it keep thinking so, right up until the night I turn the weapon around. That's my price. That's my confession. Take it or leave it."

* * *

They talked without her, off beyond the firelight—she heard the murmur of it, Osk's dry caution, Bramble's fierce whispering, Zephyrine's voice rising and falling like weather. Serah sat with her back to a pine trunk and let the cold seep through her uniform and thought about how strange it was that this—sitting unarmed among fugitives, confessing treason to a fire—was the most honest night she'd had in seven years.

Nell woke at some point and padded over to her, barefoot in the snow, and simply sat down beside her and said nothing, which was more disarming than any interrogation. After a while the child offered her a heel of bread.

"You're sad," Nell said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Because of your sister."

"Yes."

"She's in the little metal thing?"

"In the locket, yes. Well—in the palace. The locket is just... where I keep the promise."

Nell considered this with the terrible seriousness of children. "My ma used to say you can't keep somebody safe by keeping them small. Even if you love them. Especially then." She bit her bread. "Maybe that's what the Ember does to everyone. Keeps everybody small so it can love being big."

Serah stared at her. Somewhere behind the firelight, she heard Bramble stifle a sound that might have been a laugh or a sob.

From the mouth of babes, as her mother had never said, having died before the Ember came.

* * *

Near dawn, Zephyrine came and sat across from her, and for a long time neither spoke. The sky through the pines was going the color of old pewter, and the snow had stopped, and somewhere far off a crow said one dry word and thought better of it.

"The vaults," Zephyrine said at last. "You've seen the king."

"I've seen the shape of the binding. A locket of frozen fire, big as a coffin. I've heard—" She hesitated. This next part she had told no living soul. "I've heard it beat. Once a year, when they let me down there for Wren. There's a second heartbeat behind the throne room wall. Slow. Patient. Alive. I thought I was mad for years."

"You weren't." Zephyrine's voice was quiet and certain. "We've confirmed it another way. He's alive, Serah. Aldric is alive in there."

Something moved through her then that she had no name for—grief and hope tangled so tightly she couldn't tell which end to pull. Seven years she had served the thing on the throne believing the man was dead, that her sin had a clean shape: one hostage, one sister, one soul. Now the ledger had a second entry, and the debt doubled, and some traitorous part of her sang *good*, *good*, because a bigger debt meant a bigger reason, and reasons were all she had left.

"Then hear my terms again, ledger-keeper, and hear them exact." She leaned forward. "Wren comes out the same night. Not as an afterthought. Not as gratitude. As a term of contract. I know your guild trades in promises—well, here is mine, witnessed by this fire and this child and whatever gods haven't been burned yet: I will give you the servant ways, the guard rotations, the Ember's habits, its hungers, the hours it goes dimmest. I will stand between you and my own hunters and I will call it discipline. And if you break faith with me—" she smiled, and it was the smile her hunters feared, "—I am very good at breaking things back."

Zephyrine studied her the way they must have studied ledgers for seven years—looking for the hidden column, the falsified line. Whatever they saw, they apparently decided it balanced.

"Witnessed," Zephyrine said. "By the fire and the child. Freeing the king means freeing everyone the Ember holds. We'd never have left her behind anyway. But I'm glad you made us say it."

"It matters that it's said." Serah rose, stiff from the cold, and buckled her sword back on—no longer a gesture of parting enmity, somehow, but of shared work. "I ride for the capital within the hour. My company follows in two days. You'll come the servant ways behind me. Look for chalk marks shaped like a wren's foot on the third door past the ash-chute."

She paused at the edge of the firelight, one last silhouette in grey and steel.

"Ledger-keeper. When you see the Ember wear his face—it smiles at you with the voices of everyone you love. Don't listen to what it says. Listen for what it *doesn't* say. That's where it's thin. That's where it tears."

Then she was gone between the pines, hoofbeats fading north, and the camp sat in the pewter dawn with a new weight in its keeping.

Nell finished her bread, brushed the crumbs from her knees, and tugged Zephyrine's sleeve.

"I liked her," the child announced. "She smells like storms too. Just quieter ones."

Zephyrine looked north along the empty road—toward the capital, toward the walls that remembered everything, toward the second heartbeat patient as a clock—and said, very softly, as if entering it into a book:

"Two days. Then we go to war with winter itself."
