## 11. The City That Forgot How to Sing

Bramble had smuggled a great many things through a great many gates: bottled thunderclaps wrapped in wool and declared as pickles, borrowed years sealed in wax, a saint's last breath sold three times to three different collectors. She knew the grammar of borders, the small lies that customs men wanted to hear. But nothing in her long apprenticeship to dishonesty had prepared her for the capital of Vael Arden, because the capital did not check her papers at all.

It did not need to. Nothing in the city was trying to get out.

They came down off the northern ridge at dusk, five travelers under one threadbare cloak of a plan, and Bramble stopped on the last stretch of road with the city spread below them and felt something in her chest go wrong—a key turning the wrong way in a lock she hadn't known she carried.

"Look at the windows," she said.

Zephyrine looked. Nell looked. Osk looked with the particular narrowed attention of someone who locked things for a living and could tell when a thing was already locked from the inside.

The windows were lit. That was the trouble. Hundreds of them, thousands, honey-colored against the early dark, and every single one burning at the same even pitch, like candles cut to identical lengths by an obsessive hand. No flicker where a draft came through. No bright room where a family gathered close. No dim room where they hoarded their candle-ends. Just light, uniform as a printed page.

"It's warm," Nell said slowly. "You can see it's warm in there. Why does it look cold?"

Because it is, Bramble thought, and did not say. She had spent seven years handling other people's memories, decanting them, weighing them, learning their textures the way a cloth merchant learns wool, and what she saw in those lit windows was the visual equivalent of a memory that had been drained—its warmth siphoned off, its color kept but its heat gone, a hearth-fire painted on glass.

"Smile," Osk murmured, as the first checkpoint loomed. "Everyone. We're happy pilgrims come to see the king."

Nell smiled instantly and beautifully. Zephyrine produced something gray and dutiful. Bramble smiled too, and it cost her more than either of them would ever know, because smiling in this city felt less like lying and more like surrender.

* * *

The gate guards barely glanced at them. That was the second wrongness. Bramble had crossed enough borders to know that bored guards were either corrupt or terrified, and these were neither. They were *empty*—not stupid-empty, not tired-empty, but emptied the way a cup is emptied. They waved the travelers through with hands that moved efficiently and eyes that moved not at all.

Inside the walls, the city smelled of bread baking and woodsmoke and clean snow, all the right smells, laid over each other like fresh paint over rot. The market stalls were open at dusk, which markets should be, and the sellers stood behind their goods, which sellers should do, and no one was haggling.

"Do you hear that?" Zephyrine whispered.

Bramble heard it. Or rather heard the absence shaped exactly like it. A market at dusk should roar. It should brim with argument—over prices, over quality, over whose turn it was to watch the stall—and under the argument it should hum with the low music of commerce, the shuffling, clinking, calling song that every city Bramble had ever robbed or saved sang without knowing it sang.

This market made no sound above a murmur. Coins changed hands without counting. A woman bought a loaf and thanked the baker in a voice like a bell with its clapper removed, and the baker bowed, and neither of them smiled, though both of their mouths curved upward.

"They're smiling," Nell said, and her voice cracked on it. "Why are they all smiling if nobody's happy? I can tell. I'm good at telling. Nobody here is happy."

"The Ember drinks feeling first," Zephyrine said. The storm-caller's voice had gone quiet in the way Bramble had learned meant danger—the stillness before lightning, except Zephyrine hadn't been able to call lightning since the courtyard, since the guildhouse, since everything. "Serah told us. It doesn't take joy all at once. It takes the wanting of it. You keep the shape of happiness so you don't notice it's gone."

A fiddler sat in the mouth of an alley with his case open and empty and his instrument silent in his lap. Not broken. Not confiscated—though there was a notice pasted to the wall beside him, official script on crown paper: MUSICK & OTHER SEDITIOUS NOISES PROHIBITED BY ORDER OF HIS MAJESTY. The fiddler just sat there, holding the thing he was forbidden to play, looking at it the way you'd look at a grave.

Nell stopped beside him. Bramble watched her do it, watched her dig in their shared purse and drop a coin in the case, and the fiddler looked up at her with those hollowed eyes and said, "Thank you," in the tone of a man reading someone else's letter aloud, and Nell walked on with two tears freezing on her cheeks and her remaining eight fingers curled into fists.

"He can't even mourn properly," she whispered. "That's the worst part. He's sad and he can't even *feel* it all the way."

Bramble put an arm around her and steered her on, and behind her own ribs, where she kept the inventory of everything she'd ever stolen and everyone she'd ever failed to save, she added another entry, and then another, and understood that in this city the list would fill a book before morning.

* * *

They lodged that night in a smugglers' safehouse above a chandler's shop, a place Bramble knew from her old routes—a low room smelling of tallow and secrets, with a false floor she trusted the way other people trusted scripture. Corvane had taught her these routes back when the guild traded honestly in dishonest goods, and she had never been more grateful for her criminal education.

She sat up while the others slept—Osk curled like a cat in the corner, Nell sprawled and snoring with her bandaged hand tucked against her chest, Serah awake too, always, watching the door with a soldier's patience—and she took out the memory-vials.

She carried thirty-one of them now, salvaged from the guild's strongroom when they fled. Purchased memories. Traded years. The Ember had erased so much from this city, but erased things went somewhere; that was the whole dirty secret of memory-smuggling. Nothing truly vanished. It was only displaced, decanted, sold. And somewhere in her cases was proof of what the throne was eating, if she could only find the vial.

She worked by candle stub, holding each little glass prison to the flame. Memories read like weather. Grief was heavy and blue-gray. Love ran gold and stuck to the glass. Fear was thin, quicksilver, always trying to escape. She sorted through childhoods and weddings and one remarkably detailed memory of a pie, and near the bottom of the third case she found what she was looking for—or rather, it found her.

The moment her fingers closed around it, the vial went *warm*. Not candle-warm. Hearth-warm. Blood-warm. It pulsed faintly against her palm like a small trapped animal, and Bramble, who had handled memories since before Nell was born, felt the hair rise on her arms.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor thing. Where have you been?"

She didn't decant it. Decanting meant pouring a memory into a living mind, usually her own, and some instincts you honored. Instead she held it close and let it leak, the way damaged vials leaked, just a thread of it against her skin.

She saw firelight. A throne room, vast and gold. And a voice—she couldn't tell whose, it seemed to come from everywhere—saying: *the walls remember. Everything taken must be stored. Take it to the vaults beneath.*

Then the vision guttered out, leaving her heart slamming and her hand shaking.

Memories did not speak. Memories were not addressed to anyone. This one had been *sent*—delivered, filed, like correspondence. Which meant somewhere in this city there was a place where stolen feeling was stored, deliberately, systematically, the way grain was stored against famine.

Except the famine never ended here. The granary only filled.

"Bramble." Serah's voice, low, from the doorway. The hunt-commander had shed her armor days ago but not her posture. "You look like you've seen a ghost."

"Worse. I've seen a filing system." Bramble told her, briefly, in the flat professional language of smugglers. Serah listened with a face that grew grayer at each sentence.

"There are vaults under the palace," Serah said at last. "Old servant ways. My grandmother was a scullery maid there; she used to talk about tunnels that predated the crown itself, older than Aldric's line, older than anything. Everyone assumed she was senile." A pause, and something bitter moved through her expression. "Everyone assumes a lot of things about women who remember too much."

"Can you get us in?"

"I can get us to the door." Serah's jaw tightened. "What's behind it is yours to manage."

* * *

They went down at the dead hour, when even the Ember's city slept its shallow, unrefreshing sleep. Bramble led them through the back half of the capital the way water finds cracks—chandler's alley, the tanner's run, the covered drain that smelled of nothing worse than old rain. Her routes held. Routes always held; it was people who broke.

Above them, the palace rose like a mountain of lit windows, and Bramble finally understood what had been bothering her about the light since the ridge. In any living city, light pooled unevenly—the rich blazed, the poor scraped by on rushlights, and between them the darkness told the truth about the place. Here the darkness had been abolished everywhere except where it mattered most. Every window burned evenly, obediently.

Every window except the ones near the throne.

There, the light was *different*. Warmer. Richer. Almost—almost—alive. All the warmth the Ember drank from ten thousand hearths, Bramble thought, and it pools around the one chair that never needed warming.

"Here." Serah stopped at a wall that appeared to be a wall, in a passage that appeared to be nothing, behind a tannery that had gone out of business so recently its hides still hung stiff in the cold. She pressed her palm flat against the stones and waited, and Bramble realized the commander didn't know how to open it—she only knew that it existed, a story from a senile grandmother, a door half-believed.

It was Osk who found it, of course. Osk knelt, pressed her ear to the stone the way a physician listens to a chest, and after a long moment said, "There's a lock in there that isn't a lock. It's a *knot*. Someone tied this door shut." She flexed her fingers. "Someone very long ago. Stand back."

What followed took eleven minutes and three of Osk's favorite picks, and Bramble passed the time cataloguing exits and memorizing the smell of the air—which was wrong, she noted, wrong in a specific way. Under the tallow and the tannery stink, the air here smelled of *storage*. Of attics and cellars and sealed rooms. Of things kept.

The knot surrendered with a sound like a sigh. The wall swung inward on darkness and cold, and stairs went down, and down, and down.

"Grandmother," Serah murmured, almost reverent, "you magnificent old liar."

* * *

The tunnels beneath the palace were older than the palace. Bramble felt it immediately in the masonry—these lower courses were fitted without mortar, stones worn round at the edges by hands centuries dead, and the arches overhead weren't the pointed kind the current kingdom built but something older, rounder, humbler. A foundation that kings had forgotten they inherited.

And the walls—

Bramble stopped so suddenly that Nell walked into her back.

The walls remembered.

She had known it intellectually; the leaked memory had told her. Knowing and standing inside it were different countries. The stones around her were saturated, humming, *full*, and her gift—not a grand gift, never a grand gift, just the smuggler's knack for reading what memory weighed and tasted—opened like a floodgate she had never once been able to shut entirely.

Grief, layered in strata. Joy, older than grief, pressed down deep like flowers in a book. Songs—actual songs, dozens of them, hundreds, melodies soaked into the rock from centuries of people singing in these passages on their way to work and worship and war. Lullabies. Drinking songs. A love duet someone had hummed here two hundred years ago that still hung in the stones like perfume in a coat.

All of it *taken*. All of it stored. The Ember hadn't destroyed what it drank—it had poured it into the foundations of its own house, and the house was so full it was beginning to sing back.

"Bramble?" Zephyrine's hand on her shoulder. "You've gone white. What is it?"

"It's a library," Bramble whispered. "No—it's a *granary*. Everything it's eaten, everything it's taken out of them—" she gestured vaguely upward, toward the smiling dead-eyed city above, "—it's all here. Every song. Every wedding. Every summer afternoon anyone ever loved. It's not feeding on the people, Zeph. It's feeding on what the people *were*, and keeping the receipts."

Nell reached out and touched the wall, gently, the way she touched everything—as if the world were a wounded animal that might startle—and gasped. "I can hear it. There's a woman singing. She sounds like my mother." Her voice wobbled. "Is my mother in the walls?"

"No, love." Bramble pulled her back, gently. "Your mother's memory is safe in your head where it belongs. These are strangers' songs. Strangers' mothers." Though even as she said it, she wondered. How far did the Ember's reach extend? Whose lullaby had Nell just heard?

They went deeper. Serah led now, following servant-corridor logic—kitchens above, coal stores above, the geometry of service—and the warmth began. Not the warmth of the walls' stored feeling, which was diffuse, ambient, a sea. This was directional. Focused. Rising from ahead like heat off a forge.

"The throne room should be directly above us," Serah said quietly. "We're underneath it."

"And the warmth comes from below the throne," Zephyrine said. The storm-caller had gone very still, head cocked, wearing the listening expression Bramble had seen once before—in the guildhouse, the night the static first woke under their skin. "Do none of you feel that? Under the heat. Underneath."

Bramble felt it then, or felt the absence of it, which registered on her smuggler's senses as a presence all its own: a rhythm. Slow. Deep. Patient. Coming up through the soles of her boots and the stones of the ancient floor.

Not machinery. Machines ticked. This *beat*.

"A heartbeat," Nell breathed. Her face had gone luminous with wonder and horror in equal parts, the way only Nell's face could. "There's a heart down there."

"No," Bramble said, and her voice came out strange, thin, the voice of a woman reading a ledger entry she has spent her whole career hoping never to audit. Because she could feel the walls around them agreeing, resonating, every stolen song and swallowed summer vibrating faintly in time with it, the whole granary pulsing like one enormous throat swallowing. "No, Nell. Listen again. Count."

Nell counted. Her eyes widened.

"Two," she whispered. "There's two. One big one and—" she pressed her palm to the floor, and her bandaged fingers curled, "—and a little one. A little heartbeat. Behind the throne room. Right behind it. It's *small*, Bramble, it's folded up small, like—like something hiding."

Or something kept, Bramble thought. Something stored. The granary held songs and summers and the warmth of ten thousand homes, filed away against a hunger that never ended—but the Ember had never struck her as a creature that wasted anything.

It kept receipts. It kept everything.

Even, perhaps, the thing it had replaced.

The great heartbeat slowed. The little one stuttered—once, twice—and Bramble would swear until her dying day that in the pause between those beats, from the stones, from the stored and stolen voices of a hundred years of the forgotten dead-and-drained, she heard something that was almost a word.

Almost a name.

Zephyrine had gone rigid, staring at the floor as if they could see through it, and when they spoke their voice was the voice of a person reading their own death warrant and finding their own handwriting on it.

"That's a king's name," they said. "Somebody down there knows a king's name."
