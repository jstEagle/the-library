## 12. The Second Heartbeat

Osk had opened a thousand doors that weren't doors, and she had never once been afraid of what waited on the other side.

Doors, in her experience, were honest. A door wanted to be opened. Even the cruel ones—the vaults of moneylenders, the sealed rooms where families hid their gifted children from the registrars—even those were honest in their wanting. A lock was just a question asked in iron, and Osk had spent forty years learning to answer in kind. She could hear the grammar of a mechanism the way other people heard music: the tumblers' consonants, the wards' long vowels, the little sigh a well-made lock made when it finally gave up its argument.

But the way beneath the palace was not a door. It was a throat, and they were walking down it, and every instinct Osk possessed told her that throats did not want to be opened from the inside.

"Bramble," she murmured, "how much farther?"

The memory-smuggler held her lantern low so its light wouldn't climb the walls. It didn't matter. The walls were already lit—or remembering light, which was worse. The stones around them glimmered faintly with images that came and went like fish under ice: a serving girl laughing, a guard's yawn, a candle guttering. Everything these walls had ever witnessed, leaking back out of the stone now that the Ember's attention was turned upward, toward the throne room, toward the warmth of the city above.

"Past the old cistern," Bramble said. "Then down. The heartbeat gets louder."

It did. Osk had felt it first an hour ago—a pulse beneath her boots so slow and deep she'd mistaken it for her own heart misbehaving. *Thoom.* A silence as long as a held breath. *Thoom.* Nell had pressed both hands flat to the floor the first time she'd felt it and said, wonderingly, "Someone's asleep down here." Then she'd said, less wonderingly, "No. Someone's *awake* down here. That's different."

Zephyrine walked at the front, close to Corvane, who had not left their side since his confession on the road—since he'd knelt in the snow outside Vellsmark's ashes and offered them his life and been refused with a look that Osk thought must have hurt more than any blade. The storm-caller's face was pale in the lantern light. Static lifted the fine hairs at their temples, and the air around them tasted faintly of copper and rain. Since the courtyard, since the lightning had split the guildhouse sky open like a struck bell, Zephyrine's gift had not gone quiet again. It hummed against Osk's lock-sense like a badly tuned ward, patient and enormous, waiting to be asked for something.

"Osk." Zephyrine's voice, low. "You feel it too."

Not a question. Lock-witches and storm-callers, it turned out, listened with the same organ.

"I've felt it since the cistern," Osk admitted. "It's not a heart, whatever it is. Hearts don't keep time like that. That's—" She searched for the word and hated the one that arrived. "That's a metronome. Something wound up. Something keeping count."

"Seven years of counting," Bramble said softly, and would say no more.

* * *

The cistern had been dry for a century; the smuggler routes ran through its bones like veins through marble. They crossed it single file over a causeway no wider than a coffin, and below them the dark went down and down, and the walls remembered water—Osk could see it, ghost-blue, lapping at stone that hadn't been wet since Aldric's grandfather ruled. The Ember erased things, Bramble had told them. But erasure took effort, and effort left seams, and underneath the palace the seams were everywhere. Here, in the dark where the Ember never bothered to look, the city's stolen past pooled like groundwater.

Nell walked beside her, and Osk kept one hand on the girl's shoulder—not because Nell needed guiding, but because Osk needed the contact. The half-feral apprentice had lost two fingers to frostbite on the road, the middle and ring of her left hand, and she carried the bandaged stump tucked against her chest like a bird with a broken wing. She had refused, absolutely refused, to be left behind at Serah Vail's safehouse.

"You're the one who opens things," Nell had said. "And I'm the one who finds things. We go together or you'll miss it."

Miss what, she hadn't said. But Nell found things. Rats in walls, coins in gutters, the exact loose stone that hid a key. Her gift had no name and no shape; Corvane had once called it *noticing*, dismissively, and Nell had smiled her crooked smile and said nothing, because being underestimated was a form of armor too.

Now she stopped dead on the far side of the causeway, and Osk nearly walked into her.

"There," Nell whispered.

Beyond the cistern the passage narrowed, descended, and changed character entirely. The smuggler routes were rough-hewn, practical, forgettable. This corridor was not rough anything. The walls were fitted stone without mortar, seamless, and carved—Osk ran her fingers along them and found the carvings by touch before the lantern showed them: spirals, thousands of them, nested inside each other, all winding inward toward some center ahead. Old work. Older than the palace. Whatever room lay at the end of this throat, the palace had been built *around* it, the way a pearl forms around a grain of grit.

Or around a seed, Osk thought, and did not care for the image.

"It's warm," Zephyrine said, and stopped.

They all stopped. Because Zephyrine was right. The cold that had pursued them across half the kingdom—the autumn that behaved like deep winter, the villages emptied of joy, the breath-fog and the frostbitten fingers—that cold ended here, at a line across the corridor as clean as if drawn with a ruler. Beyond it, the air was blood-warm. Faintly sweet. It smelled, Osk realized with a lurch of recognition, like the guildhouse hearth on a winter evening, like cedar smoke and beeswax, like *safety*. It smelled engineered. It smelled like a lure dressed in the scent of home.

"Don't breathe it in like it means it," Bramble muttered, pulling her scarf up over her nose. "Nothing down here is offering warmth for free."

Corvane drew himself up. The old man looked, in the lantern light, suddenly every year of his age—and then something else, something harder. "I bought the contract that put Zephyrine in my guildhouse," he said, to all of them, though his eyes were on the warm dark ahead. "I have been a jailer and I didn't know it. Whatever's down there, whatever part I played in keeping it fed—" His jaw worked. "I'm through being useful to it. Open your doors, Osk. All of them. We finish this."

Osk nodded and stepped past the warmth-line, and the moment her boot crossed it, she heard the locks.

There were three. She felt them the way a musician feels a chord: the first was iron and honest, a vault door of ordinary make installed maybe fifty years ago; the second was a warding, priest-work, the kind the crown's own consecrators laid down to keep *things* contained; but the third—

The third made her laugh out loud, a short, disbelieving bark that echoed down the spiral corridor.

"What?" Nell whispered.

"The third lock is me," Osk said. "Or rather—it's *asking* for someone like me. Listen." She pressed her palm to the air where the third ward hung, invisible, dense as honey. "This isn't built to keep people out. It's built so that only a person who can open the impossible can reach what's inside. It wants a lock-witch. It's been *waiting* for one." She paused. "For seven years, I'd guess."

"That's not a defense," Zephyrine said slowly. "That's an invitation."

"Everything down here is an invitation," Osk said grimly. "The question is whether we RSVP."

She set her shoulder against the first lock—the honest iron one—and listened to its grammar. Old-fashioned. Proud of itself. She answered it in kind, a quarter-turn here, a whispered pressure there, and felt it surrender with the sigh of a thing that had been locked so long it had forgotten it was ever meant to move. The vault door swung inward with a groan of disuse, exhaling seven years of stillness.

The priest-ward fought harder. It recognized what she was—it flared against her senses with cold disapproval, *unclean, unclean*—and Osk, who had been called unclean by consecrators since she was nine years old, smiled without warmth and unpicked its certainties stitch by stitch while it screamed silently. Forty years of practice. It took her four minutes. She counted them by the great slow pulse behind the wall. *Thoom. Thoom. Thoom.*

Then only the third remained, the invitation, the lock that wanted opening.

She stood before it with her hand raised and did not move.

"Osk?" Nell's voice, small.

"It's going to know me," Osk said. "The moment I open it, it will know everything about how I open things. What I am. Maybe what we all are." She looked back at them—at Bramble with her lantern and her ledger of stolen memories, at Corvane with his guilt worn openly now like a scar, at Zephyrine with the storm barely leashed beneath their skin, at Nell with her bandaged hand pressed to the wall, feeling, always feeling.

"And if we turn back," Zephyrine said quietly, "the winter eats another village by morning. And another by the morning after that." Their eyes were steady. Under their skin, faintly, blue light moved like weather seen through a window. "Open the door, Osk. Let it know us. We came here to be known."

So Osk unlocked the invitation.

It opened the way water parts—not swinging, not dissolving, simply *rearranging* itself around her hand, around her, around all of them as they stepped through, and in the instant of passing Osk felt it read her like a ledger entry: *lock-witch, forty-one years old, afraid of drowning, loves an apprentice with a crooked smile, has never once regretted her crimes, regrets daily the things she did to survive them.* It filed her away somewhere, gently, and let her pass, and she carried the strange intimacy of it with her into the room beyond.

* * *

The room was round. Of course it was round; the spirals had promised it. And it was old beyond reckoning, older than the palace, older perhaps than the crown, its dome ribbed like the inside of a shell. No torches burned. None were needed. Light came from the center of the floor—from a raised dais of black stone, and on the dais, a locket.

It was the size of a shield, wrought of silver gone grey with age, hanging suspended in the air by nothing at all. And within it—

Within it was fire. Frozen fire. A flame caught mid-flicker, every tongue of it crystalline, unmoving, beautiful the way glaciers are beautiful, the way winter stars are beautiful: beauty with death in it. The frozen flame beat. Slowly. Enormously. *Thoom.* Each pulse sent a shiver of heat rolling outward through the chamber, and each pulse was followed by a silence as long as grief.

And inside the fire—folded, curled, small as a child in the womb, impossibly, terribly *alive*—was a man.

"Oh," said Nell, and began to cry without any noise at all. "Oh, he's in there. He's *in there*."

King Aldric. Osk knew the face from the coins, from the portraits that still hung in taverns, from the proclamation broadsheets that had declared him dead of a fever seven years ago. He was thinner than his coin-face, greyer, his beard grown wild and long, his fine clothes rotted to rags—but alive. Alive and aware. As they watched, his eyelids fluttered. His lips moved. No sound crossed the frozen fire, but Osk had spent a lifetime reading mechanisms, and mouths were mechanisms too, and she read his easily enough:

*Help me. Help me. Help me.*

"He can see us," Bramble breathed. Her voice shook. "After seven years in there, he can still—"

"He's not a prisoner," Osk said, and her own voice came out wrong, flattened with horror. "Look again. Look at the fire. Where does it draw?"

Bramble looked. So did Zephyrine, and Osk watched the storm-caller's face do something terrible—watch understanding arrive like a blow.

"It draws from him," Zephyrine said. "The binding doesn't imprison him. It *harvests* him. Every warmth the Ember steals—the joy in the villages, the music, the summer, the love—it burns it through him. Through his life. He's not kept alive despite the Ember. He's kept alive *because of* it." Their hands had closed into fists, and lightning crawled faintly between the knuckles, blue-white, hungry. "A battery. Bramble, you said it. A battery. Seven years as a battery."

"That's why the winter needs him," Corvane said hoarsely. "If he dies, the engine dies. The Ember can't wear his face unless he's wearing—unless he's *here*—" The old man put a hand over his mouth. "Gods. It didn't kill him because killing him would be mercy."

In the fire, the king's lips moved. Osk read them again, and this time the shape was different, and it undid her more than the pleading had.

*I know you,* the mouth said. *Storm-caller. I saw you. I remember.*

"Zephyrine." Osk turned. "Tell me you understand the binding. Tell me you can read it."

Zephyrine had already stepped forward, up onto the dais, close enough that the frozen fire's light played across their face in shifting auroras. Their eyes were half-closed, moving, reading—not the metal, not the flame, but the structure beneath, the grammar of the thing. When they spoke, it was in the tone of an accountant confirming arithmetic they wish were wrong.

"It's balanced," they said softly. "Of course it's balanced. Everything the Ember builds is balanced. The binding holds because something of equal weight holds the other side. His name. His true name, spoken into the binding at the moment it was made, anchoring it. To break the lock, the anchor has to be spoken *out*—the name called through the binding, by a voice with enough storm in it to carry truth." They opened their eyes. "By a storm-caller."

"Then we do it," Nell said instantly, wiping her face with her bandaged hand. "We do it now, right now, before—"

"We can't," Bramble said. "Not yet. Think. The moment that binding breaks, the Ember knows. Every warmth it's stolen, every trick it's built—it all runs through this room. Break the lock and it feels the door open. We get one chance, and it has to be the last chance. We need everyone in place. We need Serah turning her hunters. We need Corvane's roster holding the ways out. We need—" She stopped, because Zephyrine had raised one hand, very gently, and touched the frozen fire.

The static around the storm-caller's skin leapt toward the crystal flame like iron filings to a lodestone, and for one heartbeat—for one impossible, harmonizing heartbeat—the frozen fire flickered, actually flickered, and the whole chamber filled with a sound like a struck bell underwater.

And King Aldric opened his eyes fully, and looked at Zephyrine Dunmore, and mouthed three words that Osk would carry to her grave:

*You came back.*

Zephyrine stepped down off the dais. Their face was wet. Their voice, when it came, was steady as a level.

"Tomorrow," they said. "We come back tomorrow, with everything we have, and I call his name through the binding, and we take our king out of the fire." They looked around the circle of their family—Osk, Nell, Bramble, Corvane—and Osk saw that the fear in their face had finished hardening into something else. "And then we go introduce him to the thing wearing his smile."

Behind them, unheard until that moment, far above through seven floors of stone, a bell began to ring—the palace alarm, urgent and wrong, and under it, rising like smoke through the walls, a voice made of every voice they loved, speaking their names one by one.

It already knew they were here.
