## 13. What Loyalty Is Worth

Corvane had always believed that a man's sins arrived like debt collectors: politely at first, then all at once, and never alone.

He stood in the snow outside the capital's southern gate with a signed confession folded against his heart and the entire roster of the Guild of Impossible Things arrayed behind him like a bad joke told in the dark—thirty-one souls, most of them half-frozen, several of them wanted by the crown, one of them a lock-witch currently arguing with a gatehouse guard about whether a door could be persuaded to open itself.

The paper against his chest was damp with his sweat. He had written it three times. The first draft had been lies dressed as apology; the second, apologies dressed as strategy. Only the third said what needed saying, in words so plain they embarrassed him:

*I sold them. I sold the road they walked. And I am here to un-sell it if it kills me, which it may.*

The gate guards let them pass because Serah Vail's seal was on the writ Corvane carried—a writ he had bought from her second-in-command for more coin than the guild made in a year—and because thirty-one travelers moving with purpose are far less suspicious than thirty-one travelers trying not to be noticed. The city swallowed them the way the city swallowed everything now: without comment, without appetite, without song.

Corvane had not been to the capital in nine years. He remembered it loud. He remembered fishmongers shouting over gulls, remembered fiddlers on the Fountainbridge steps, remembered the particular argumentative roar of a thousand people who believed their voices mattered. Now the streets moved like water under ice. People walked where they were meant to walk. They smiled when they were meant to smile—the smiles arriving a half-second late, like echoes of something that had already died. A baker stood in his doorway handing out rolls to children, and the children took them and thanked him and did not run off laughing. They simply stood there, chewing, watching nothing.

"Gods below," murmured Wren the cartographer, at Corvane's shoulder. "It's like the whole city has a fever breaking backward."

"It's worse than fever." Corvane pulled his collar up. "Fever burns hot. This is being *eaten*."

They moved through the cold streets in loose file, following routes Osk had scratched onto a scrap of vellum before they'd left the guildhouse—servants' ways, smugglers' cuts, the veins of the old palace that ran beneath the new one like roots beneath a tree. Somewhere down there, if Bramble's reading of the walls was true, a king breathed inside a locket of frozen fire. Somewhere up there, a thing wearing that king's face sat on a warm throne in a cold room and called itself Aldric.

And somewhere between those two points, Corvane intended to kneel before Zephyrine Dunmore and offer them his throat.

He found them in the undercroft of the Lamplighters' Chapel—a deconsecrated vault that smelled of tallow and old stone, where the fugitives had holed up behind a door only Osk could open. He knocked the way Osk had taught him: twice, pause, once, pause, twice, and felt the lock taste him through the wood like a horse testing an apple.

The door opened on Nell, frostbitten fingers bandaged, eyes going wide as moons.

"You came back," she said. "You came back *here*. Nobody comes here."

"People come where they're ashamed," Corvane said. "It's the only place some of us can stand."

Zephyrine rose from a camp bed near the far wall. Seven years of ledgers had inked themselves into their hands; seven years of hiding had thinned them to wire and watchfulness. But their eyes—those grey storm-colored eyes that Corvane had spent seven years avoiding across ledger-desks and supper tables—those eyes had not thinned at all. They took him in the way weather takes in a landscape.

"You're supposed to be in Vellsmark," Zephyrine said quietly. "Or on the coast road. You're supposed to be anywhere but standing in front of me."

"Yes," Corvane said. "I know what I'm supposed to do. That's rather the problem."

Behind Zephyrine, Bramble unfolded from a shadow with a memory-vial already palmed, her thumb on its stopper like a knife-hand. Osk leaned in the doorway, arms crossed, and Corvane saw that she had locked every exit in the chapel except the one behind him. She was giving him nowhere to flee. Good. He hadn't planned to.

"I need to tell you all something," he said, "and then you'll decide what to do with me. I won't argue with the verdict."

"Sit down, Corvane," Zephyrine said.

"No," he said. "I think this should be done kneeling."

And he knelt. On the cold flagstone, in the tallow-smelling dark, the master of the Guild of Impossible Things put his knees on the floor in front of the clerk he had caged for seven years, and began to confess.

"Seven years ago," he said, "a broker came to my office. Hooded. Paid in advance. The contract was simple: sanctuary for Zephyrine Dunmore, exile contract transferred to my keeping, in perpetuity. Rent-free rooms, honest work, no questions asked by anyone—including me. There were clauses. There is always a clause. Clause four forbade me from ever letting you learn why you were exiled. Not from telling you myself—there was a separate penalty for that—but from letting you find out through any means I controlled or could have controlled. Your ledgers. Your mail. Your memories, if any ever crossed my threshold."

"The payment we audited," Zephyrine said. "The memory of my own face in the throne room. That came through your books."

"It did. And when I saw it listed, I burned the entry and lied to you about the audit. That was six days ago. It was the hundred-and-nineteenth lie I have told you since you arrived at my door." He said it evenly, because he had counted, and the counting was the penance. "The broker paid me well. Not coin—I didn't need coin. He paid me in guarantees. The guild would be left alone. The Long Hunt, when it came—and he told me it *would* come—would pass our door. All I had to do was keep one witness small and blind and grateful."

Nell made a small sound, like a kicked dog hearing a kind voice.

"And I told myself," Corvane went on, "that it was mercy. That's the filthiest word in any language, *mercy*, when it's spoken by someone protecting himself. I told myself a safe cage is better than a cold road. I watched you balance impossible accounts for seven years and I thought, *at least they're alive, at least they're warm,* and I never once asked what it costs a storm to live indoors."

Bramble spoke from the shadows, her voice flat as a blade laid flat on a table. "Three nights ago you sold us again. Our route. Our numbers. To the Ember's people."

Corvane bowed his head. "Yes."

"Say the rest," Zephyrine said. Their voice had gone very quiet, very level, the voice of a person reading a final column of figures before closing a book forever.

"I met with the hunt's quartermaster in a tollhouse on the Ash Road. I gave them the Lamplighters' Chapel. I gave them the route through Fountainbridge. I gave them your faces and your number and the fact that Nell is injured and slowing you down. In exchange—" his jaw worked— "in exchange they renewed the guarantee. The guild survives. Every soul under my roof. Thirty-one lives for five."

"And then?"

"And then I walked out of that tollhouse," Corvane said, "and sat by the roadside until dawn, and I understood that I had just done the same thing I had done seven years ago, and that I would do it every year for the rest of my life, because a man who trades other people once will trade them forever unless something breaks in him. So I broke something." He looked up. "I broke the deal."

Osk shifted in the doorway. "The tollhouse," she said slowly. "That was two nights ago. The hunters who hit Fountainbridge—"

"Were expecting five fugitives on a specific road at a specific hour and found instead thirty-one guild members who had marched through the night to get ahead of the information I'd sold. We ambushed the ambush. Took their horses, their writs, and their commander's spare seal." He touched his coat, where Serah Vail's borrowed authority rode against his confession. "No one died. I made sure of that much. I'm done spending blood I borrowed from other people."

Silence filled the undercroft like water filling a hull.

It was Zephyrine who finally moved. They crossed the space between the camp bed and the kneeling man slowly—not like someone approaching an enemy, but like an auditor approaching a discrepancy that had been warping every column for years. They stopped an arm's length away.

"Look at me."

He looked.

"Why?" Zephyrine asked. "Not the redemption answer. Not the guilt answer. Why did you take that contract in the first place? The truth, Corvane. You've fed us enough lies dressed as protection."

And Corvane—who had rehearsed noble answers all the way down the Ash Road, who had composed speeches about duty and conscience while his horse picked its way through frozen mud—found that every rehearsed word had fallen out of him somewhere on the road, the way heat falls out of a house with a broken window.

"Because I was afraid," he said. "Because seven years ago the Ember's broker showed me what happened to the last guildmaster who refused a deal, and I looked at my little empire of misfits and smuggled thunderclaps and I loved it more than I loved what was right. Because neutrality was never a philosophy, Zephyrine. It was a bribe I paid myself to sleep." His voice cracked, and he let it crack; hiding the crack would have been one more lie. "You asked me once, your first week in the guildhouse, why the ledger had no column for debts of honor. Do you remember? You asked why we recorded everything except the things that actually bind us. And I told you the guild dealt in the possible."

"I remember," Zephyrine said.

"I've thought about that question every day for seven years. Because you were right, and I knew you were right, and the contract in my strongbox was the proof that I had sold the impossible things—the honorable ones—just to keep trading in the merely strange."

Zephyrine crouched then, bringing their eyes level with his. Close up, Corvane saw what the storm had left in them: a stillness like the center of a turning wind, calm and enormous and not remotely gentle anymore.

"I ought to hate you," Zephyrine said. "I've been trying to, since you knelt. It isn't working. You built the cage, but you also kept the fire lit and taught Nell her letters and paid for Bramble's mother's medicine and never once asked Osk what she keeps in the third drawer. You were a jailer who also kept the prisoners alive, and both of those things are true, and I am so tired of the world pretending only one thing can be true at a time." They straightened. "So here is your sentence, guildmaster. Not your death. Your testimony."

"My—"

"When we face the Ember—and we will face it, tonight or tomorrow or however long it takes Osk to crack the servants' ways—it will speak in the voices of everyone I love. It did it once already. It will wear your voice, Corvane. It will say the things you'd say, the kind ones, the ones you used to keep us small. And when it does, I need to know the difference. So you're going to stand next to me in that throne room and say the truth out loud, in your own voice, where I can hear it: what you did, what you sold, what it cost. You're going to be my proof that the real Corvane tells the truth even when it ruins him. Can you do that?"

Corvane stayed on his knees a moment longer. He had offered his life, and been refused, and discovered that refusal hurt far more than a blade would have. A death would have ended the account. Testimony would keep it open forever, in front of witnesses, in his own voice, every day for whatever days remained.

It was, he understood dimly, exactly what he deserved.

"Yes," he said. He stood. His knees ached. He was absurdly glad of the ache. "And Zephyrine—one thing more. The contract. I brought it." He drew from his coat not only the confession but the original brokered document, seven years yellowed, clause four still legible in its elegant cruel hand. He held it out. "I couldn't destroy it. I tried, the night I decided to come. My hands wouldn't strike the match. Some part of me thought you should see the shape of the cage before it burned."

Zephyrine took it. Read it. Their face did something complicated—not rage, not grief, but the terrible clarity of a person seeing the exact dimensions of a wound for the first time.

"*Engineered,*" they read aloud. "'The subject's removal was arranged per the principal's instruction. Witness to be isolated from corroborating memory indefinitely.'" They looked up. "*Principal.* The Ember ordered my exile. Before I even understood what I'd seen."

"It wrote your life like a ledger entry," Corvane said. "Line by line. And I countersigned."

Zephyrine struck the hearth-match then—quickly, before the mercy in them could intervene—and touched it to the contract, and the hooded broker's elegant clauses curled and blackened and went up the chimney flue in a ribbon of ash. The smell of burning ink filled the undercroft, sharp and clean.

"There," Zephyrine said softly. "One account closed."

From above them, faint through the old chapel floor, came a sound none of them had heard in the capital until that moment: a bell. Not the palace bells, which rang on schedule like a heartbeat too regular to trust. A small bell, cracked, human, rung by hand—three strikes, pause, three strikes.

Osk was up the stairs before anyone else, ear pressed to the door. Her face, when she turned around, had gone the color of the snow outside.

"That's the signal," she said. "Serah Vail's signal. The one we agreed on if the palace changed its posture." She swallowed. "It means the Ember knows we're in the city. It means the gates are sealing. And it means—" she glanced at Corvane, and something almost like pity crossed her scarred face— "it means it already knows *you* broke the deal, guildmaster. The message says it's asking for you by name. It wants its jailer back."

Corvane felt the old fear rise in him, the fear that had signed contracts and burned ledger entries and knelt in tollhouses. He let it rise. And then he let it pass through him and out, like weather.

"Then it can ask," he said. "But it sent the invitation to the wrong address. I don't keep accounts with that thing anymore." He rolled his shoulders, an old man's gesture, a fighter's gesture, and looked around the lamplit circle of the only family he had ever betrayed and been forgiven by in the same hour. "Someone fetch me a weapon. It seems the Guild of Impossible Things is about to attempt one more."
