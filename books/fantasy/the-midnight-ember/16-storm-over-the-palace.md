## 16. Storm Over the Palace

### Osk

The palace had too many doors.

Osk had spent thirty years learning the grammar of locks—every tumbler a syllable, every bolt a stubborn consonant—and she had never met a building that lied about its doors before. The servant ways beneath the Ember's throne were wrong in the specific way that made her teeth ache. Corridors doubled back on themselves like sentences that refused to end. Staircases descended into rooms they'd already been through. And every door she touched was locked from *both* sides, which was not how doors worked, which was exactly how fear worked.

"Talk to me, Osk." Bramble's voice came low behind her, tight with the effort of carrying her satchel of stolen memories up a stair that kept trying to become a wall. The vials clinked softly against one another, a hundred little glass hearts full of other people's yesterdays.

"The palace is dreaming," Osk said. She pressed her palm flat against a door of black oak banded in iron, and felt it press back. "Or something in it is. These aren't real locks. They're *insisted-upon* doors. Someone decided this passage doesn't exist, and the stone believed them."

"Can you argue with it?"

Osk smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile. Lock-witches did not pick locks; that was a burglar's trade, honest and mechanical. A lock-witch listened for the moment a door forgot what it was and reminded it, gently, that it had always been open.

"I don't argue," she said. "I flatter."

She found the seam—the hairline place where the door's conviction wavered, where some tired servant twenty years dead had still thought of it as *the way to the linen stores* no matter what the Ember insisted. She breathed warmth onto that seam, the way you breathe on a frozen window, and whispered: *You are a door. You have always been a door. Doors open.*

The lock sighed. The iron bands loosened like unclenched fists. Beyond lay a corridor lit by hearths burning blue and cold, flame without heat, fire that had forgotten its purpose—which was, Osk thought, the whole palace in miniature.

"Through," she said. "Quickly. It'll remember itself soon."

They moved—Bramble first, then Nell clutching the locket of frozen fire against her chest with both mittened hands, then Corvane at the rear walking backward as much as forward, watching the dark they'd come out of. Somewhere above them, muffled through seven floors of lying stone, the palace guard had begun to shout.

And somewhere ahead, deeper than all of it, Zephyrine walked toward the throne room with a storm held behind their ribs like a held breath.

*Hold,* Osk thought at them, uselessly, lovingly. *Hold until we're there.*

The next door tried to be a mirror instead. Osk talked it out of that too.

### Bramble

The guards came at them in the gallery of portraits—a dozen men in white-and-gold, moving with the terrible smoothness of people who had stopped needing to feel afraid, because the thing they served ate fear along with everything else.

Bramble had never fought anyone in her life. Her gift was smuggling: she could fold a memory into a vial, carry it across borders in her blood, decant it into another mind like pouring wine. It had made her a merchant of other people's pasts, and she had told herself for years that it was harmless work, neutral work, work that hurt no one.

She uncorked the first vial and threw it like a grenade.

It shattered against the lead guard's breastplate, and the memory inside him *woke*. Bramble had bought it off a dying soldier in Vellsmark two winters ago—his daughter's wedding, the whole of it, three hours of fiddle music and honey cake and his girl laughing so hard she couldn't say her vows. It bloomed inside the guard's skull all at once, uncompressed, unfiltered, a lifetime's worth of joy detonating in a man who hadn't felt anything in years.

He dropped his sword and sat down in the middle of the fight and wept.

"Oh," said Nell, wide-eyed, locket glowing faintly through her mittens. "Oh, that's *horrible*. That's wonderful."

"That's contraband," Bramble said grimly, and threw another.

This one was a mother's memory of a fevered child recovering—she'd paid a fortune for it, a genuine miracle-memory, the exact moment the fever broke—and the second guard caught it full in the face and began calling for his own mother in a voice cracked down the middle. The third got a fisherman's last perfect morning on open water. The fourth, mercifully, only got an old woman's recipe for plum bread, and stood there confused while Corvane shouldered past him.

"You're weaponizing grief," Corvane said, holding the corridor mouth alone, his broad back a wall between them and the sound of boots multiplying below.

"I'm weaponizing *everything they took*," Bramble answered. "That's the point. They banned music because music makes people remember they have insides. Well." She hurled a memory of a midsummer bonfire, and three guards staggered as if struck by sunlight. "So does this."

But her satchel was lighter than it had been an hour ago, and the gallery was long, and the palace kept making more guards—or perhaps it was simply emptying its pockets of every hollow man it owned. She saw Nell stumble, saw Osk catch her, saw the locket flare white through the wool.

Ahead, the throne room doors stood at the end of the gallery, tall as gallows, carved with a sun whose rays had all been chiseled off sometime in the last seven years.

Behind them, the shouting turned to running.

"Bramble," Corvane said quietly. "How many do you have left?"

She weighed the satchel in her hand. Counted the vials by their clink, the smuggler's arithmetic, the only ledger she'd ever trusted.

"Enough," she lied, and threw the wedding again.

### Serah

Serah Vail had trained Dain Marrow herself. She had taught him the blade-grip when his hands were too big for it. She had sat with him through the night after his first burning, both of them silent, passing a flask, neither saying the thing they were thinking. He had been the closest thing to family the Hunt allowed.

Now he came at her across the frozen fountain court with his sword drawn and his eyes like frosted glass, and she understood that whatever Dain had once been, the Ember had finished drinking him years ago. What remained moved beautifully and meant nothing.

"Dain," she said, circling. Their breath hung between them in twin plumes. "Step aside. This isn't your fight anymore. It hasn't been since Vellsmark."

"Commander." His voice was flat as slate. "You've been declared traitor. The register lists you among the unbound. Surrender your sword and the Ember will be gentle."

"The Ember doesn't know how to be gentle. It only knows how to *seem*."

He lunged. She caught the parry late and felt her forearm go numb to the elbow, and the cold of the court rushed into the gap like water into a hull. They broke apart, circled again. Around the fountain's rim, the statues of old kings wore caps of ice, and Serah thought absurdly that they looked like an audience waiting to see whether she'd die well.

"You know what it has," she said. "You know why I stay. It has Liss."

Something flickered behind Dain's frost. Small, buried, but there—the way a coal is there under ash. "Everyone serves it for something," he said.

"And what do you serve it for?"

He didn't answer, and the not-answer told her everything: nothing. He served it for nothing. He was one of the ones it had emptied just because emptiness was easier to command. Grief went through Serah like a blade going through snow—all the way down, leaving a clean bright wound.

So she stopped fighting like a commander and started fighting like a sister, which was to say: dirty, fast, and with her whole heart. She feinted high, swept his ankle on the ice where she'd led him, and put him down hard enough to crack the frost. Her knee on his chest. Her sword at the seam of his gorget.

"Dain," she said. "Listen to me. Tonight the Ember dies. Whatever's left of you when it goes—I want it standing."

His eyes cleared for half a heartbeat. In that half-heartbeat he said, so quietly she nearly missed it: "The throne room. It's expecting them. It's been expecting them for *seven years*, Serah—it built the whole winter around this night—" and then the frost came down over his face again and he bucked beneath her, and she had to choose: hold him, or run.

She chose to run. She owed him that much truth and no more delay. Behind her, Dain rose slowly to his feet and did not follow, and Serah carried his warning through the freezing corridors at a dead sprint:

*It knows. It has always known.*

### Nell

Nell ran because everyone else was running and because the locket was warm now, warm as a bird, and it beat against her chest like a small frightened heart saying *hurry, hurry, hurry*.

Inside it was a king. Inside it was a whole person folded up small, and Nell had held it for three days and three nights and had talked to it the whole time, because her mam had taught her that nothing alive should travel in silence. She'd told it about the roads and the villages with no singing in them. She'd told it about Bramble's plum-bread memory and Osk's terrible kind smile. She'd told it about Zephyrine, who looked at storms the way other people looked at home.

"Hold on," she panted now, skidding on marble, Osk's hand locked around her wrist. "We're nearly there, Your Majesty. Hold on."

The throne room doors were ahead—huge, black, sun-carved and rayless—and between them and the doors stood a wall of guards, and beyond the guards, coming up the grand stair with torches and chains, more of them, dozens, the palace spending everything it had left to keep four fugitives and a locksmith from one room.

Corvane reached the doors first.

He did not draw a weapon. Corvane had never needed one; he was a big man in the way that mountains are big, and he had spent seven years being a door himself, standing in guildhouse thresholds, keeping weather and hunters and hard truths on the outside. Now he planted himself in the doorway of the throne room, spread his arms, and became the last door in the palace that actually worked.

"None of you," he rumbled, "are invited."

The first guard hit him like a wave hitting a harbor wall. Corvane folded around the blow rather than resisting it, gave ground inch by inch, turned force aside with shoulders and elbows and sheer patient mass. Nell saw a chain come down across his back and heard him grunt and *not fall*, and something in her chest tore straight down the middle.

"CORVANE!"

"Keep *moving*, girl!" he roared, laughing even as the second chain landed. "I've held worse doors than this! I held the door against *tax collectors!*"

Then Serah came out of nowhere like winter itself, white cloak streaming, and the guards nearest the stair suddenly had a very different problem, and Corvane laughed again, bloody-mouthed, and held.

Nell ran through the gap he made. Osk ran through it. Bramble threw one last vial backward—Nell heard a guard start weeping with joy—and followed.

And the doors swung shut behind them on the sound of Corvane roaring like a landslide, holding, holding, holding.

### Zephyrine

The throne room was warm.

That was the first obscenity of it. Seven floors of frozen corridors, hearths that burned cold, guards with frost in their eyelashes—and here the air hung soft as June. Candles burned gold and upright. A fire crackled in a hearth the size of a barn door, and Zephyrine knew, with the certainty of the skin that had hidden static for seven years, exactly where all the realm's missing warmth had gone.

It pooled here, around the throne, like light around a wound.

"You came," said King Aldric, rising from the throne, smiling, spreading his hands. He wore the crown loosely, easily, the way a man wears a hat he likes. "Seven years I've waited, Zephyrine. Seven years, and my favorite witness walks in the door at last."

Zephyrine's storm rose against the backs of their teeth. Every candle in the room guttered toward them, hungry or afraid—they could not tell which, and suspected the difference didn't exist here.

"You're not him," Zephyrine said.

"No," the Ember agreed pleasantly. And then it stopped pretending.

The change was not violent. That was the horror of it. Aldric's face simply *loosened*, like wax near a flame, like a mask deciding it had no further need of a wearer. The smile stayed. The smile stayed as the cheeks ran and reformed, as the body stretched and thinned and grew tall as a doorway, as skin went the color of coals seen through ash—dark, and veined everywhere with lines of orange light, as though something enormous burned behind a curtain of cinders.

The Ember stood in its own shape at last, crowned still, because it liked the crown, and it smiled with a mouth that was mostly heat.

"There," it said, in a voice made of every voice Zephyrine had ever loved. Corvane's rumble. Nell's brightness. Bramble's dry smoke. Its own. "Isn't honesty better? Shall we begin?"

The great doors burst inward behind Zephyrine—Corvane, bleeding, triumphant, falling through them; Serah with her sword; Osk and Bramble and Nell with the locket blazing white against her chest, beating faster now, *hurrying, hurrying*—and the Ember opened its arms to all of them like a host welcoming guests.

"Wonderful," it said. "Everyone's here. Now we can talk about forgetting."
