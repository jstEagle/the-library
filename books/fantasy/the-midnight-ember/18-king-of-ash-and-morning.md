## 18. King of Ash and Morning

The first thing Aldric knew was warmth—and the shame of knowing it.

Warmth had not been his in seven years. He had been *of* it once, a hearth for something else's fire, and even now, crumpled on the cold stone of the palace vault with a locket of shattered glass beside him and frost melting off his skin in slow beads, some traitor part of him missed it. The Ember had kept him warm the way a lamp keeps oil warm: by burning him.

He tried to remember his name.

That was the horror of it—the first thing he reached for was not his name but the shape of the burning, the long slow sip of himself that had lasted seven years, and he had to climb over that memory like a man climbing over a fallen horse to reach the small signpost behind it. *Aldric.* Yes. *Aldric Veyran, third of that name, king of—* of what? The realm's name came slower, arriving like a guest who had forgotten the house. He lay on his back and looked up at vaulted stone webbed with ice, at torchlight guttering blue in air too cold to burn properly, and he said his own name aloud just to hear whether his voice still worked.

"Aldric."

It worked. It sounded like a hinge that needed oiling. Seven years of disuse will do that to a voice; seven years of being spoken *over*, spoken *as*, worn like a coat by something that smiled with his mouth. He began, absurdly, to laugh, and the laugh became coughing, and the coughing bent him double over his own knees—knees that ached, gloriously, specifically, *individually* ached. Pain. He could have wept for the pain. Pain meant he was nobody else.

Hands were on him. Small hands, chapped, certain—a girl's hands, pressing a cloak around his shoulders, saying words he only half caught: *your majesty, can you walk, we have to move, she's calling the storm.* A storm-caller. There had been a storm-caller once, hadn't there? He remembered—no. He remembered *a throne room*, and rain against high windows, and a young face looking up at him across the floor with an expression he now understood, from the far side of seven years, had been fear. Someone had seen. Someone had seen the thing crawl out of his own hearth and put on his crown while his body slept its last true sleep—or had it slept? Had he died? He did not know. The Ember had never told him, and he had asked, in the wordless way one asks inside a fire, every day for seven years.

"Can you stand?" the girl asked again. Nell, someone called her—from the dark of the corridor, a voice like gravel and kindness. "Nell, love, we haven't time—"

"I've got him," Nell said, and hauled, and Aldric Veyran, King of the Nine Valleys, Warden of the Long Road, Lord Protector of a realm whose winters used to end, stood up on legs as thin as winter branches and took one step, and then another, because that was what kings did. That was, he realized dimly, the only thing left that was actually his. Not the crown—it had been stolen. Not the name—it had been worn. But the standing up. The putting of one foot after the other. No usurper could do that for him.

He walked out of the vaults toward the sound of the world ending above.

* * *

The palace stairs were wrong in a way his body understood before his mind did. Frost climbed the walls in feathered patterns, beautiful and starving, and where the torches still burned their flames leaned sideways, all of them, toward the throne room, like iron filings obeying a magnet, like men bowing. The cold coming down those stairs was not weather. Weather did not have an appetite. This cold had an appetite, and it was eating, and beneath the groan of freezing stone Aldric could hear it chewing: a vast, slow, satisfied sound, the sound of a fire digesting a kingdom.

He had felt every meal. That was the thing no one would ever understand unless they had been the wood. Seven years of the Ember feeding—not on heat, heat was nothing, heat was cheap—but on the warmth underneath heat. On the way a baker hummed at dawn. On the specific joy of a child finding the first plum of summer. It drank those first, sipped them out of the city like cream off the top of milk, and then the people were left with the bare mechanics of living, smiling with dead eyes because the smile remained and everything behind it had been swallowed. Aldric had felt each swallow pass through him. He had been the throat.

And he remembered them anyway. That was the miracle buried in the violation: the Ember had poured itself through him for seven years, and somewhere in the burning he had become a ledger no one knew he was keeping. Every kindness it consumed, he had held. He did not know why. Perhaps fires cannot burn what they do not touch, and it had touched everything through him, so everything had left its mark. Perhaps grief simply refuses arithmetic. He only knew that when he closed his eyes he could see them, thousands of them, whole and warm: the baker's humming, the plum, a widow in the Lower Terraces who sang to her bees, two brothers who split one apple every night and each swore they'd gotten the bigger half—

"Your majesty." Osk, the lock-witch, at a door that was more suggestion than door, her clever fingers working a lock that screamed as it opened. She glanced back at him, and her face did something complicated—reverence and pity fighting for the same ground. "Beyond this is the antechamber. Past that, the throne room. I won't lie to you about what you'll see."

"What will I see?"

"Myself," he said, before she could answer. "I'll see myself."

She stepped aside.

* * *

The antechamber was full of snow falling indoors.

It drifted down through the rafters in fat, unhurried flakes, settling on the banners of nine generations of his ancestors, whitening the shoulders of the dead. Beyond the great doors—half melted into their frames, oak slumping like candlewax—the throne room glowed with a light that had no business existing: hearth-light, vast and orange and wrong, the color of a fire that has learned ambition.

Aldric stopped at the threshold and looked at himself.

The thing wore him well. That was the worst discovery, worse than the crown, worse than the smile. It had refined him, the way rot refines fruit into sweetness. Its Aldric was taller by a finger's width, straighter in the spine, his gray hairs gone, his face smoothed into the version of a king that exists on coins. It sat upon the throne with one ankle crossed over one knee, utterly relaxed, watching a storm-caller with lightning crawling up both arms like ivy made of noon.

He knew her. Zephyrine Dunmore. The witness. The one whose face had haunted whatever passed for his dreams these seven years—young, terrified, standing in the rain-lashed throne room on the last day anything had been real. They had exiled her for seeing. He had known that much even inside the fire; the Ember had gloated about it, the way a thief gloats over a lock picked cleanly. And here she was at last, come back with a storm in her fists, and the Ember was smiling at her with *his* smile, offering her—what? He could feel the shape of the offer without hearing it. It always offered. That was how it fed best: not taken warmth but given warmth, freely surrendered, which tasted sweeter.

Zephyrine said something he couldn't catch. The Ember's smile cracked.

And then the thing shed his face.

It happened the way a snake sheds skin, if the skin screamed. The false Aldric split along lines no anatomy allowed, and what came out was fire remembering it had once been hungry—an upright column of ember-light, crowned in cinders, wearing the throne room like a coat it had outgrown. Heat rolled off it in visible waves, and everywhere the waves touched, color drained. The banners went gray. The gold leaf on the pillars dulled to pewter. Snow fell faster.

Aldric's legs decided, without consulting him, to stop holding him up.

He went down onto one knee on the frozen flagstones, and Nell was there instantly—she seemed to be always there, this small fierce apprentice with bandaged fingers, catching his elbow, saying "don't look, your majesty, don't," and he shook his head and kept looking, because looking was the entire office to which he had been elected by catastrophe. Anyone could fight the thing. Anyone could run from it or curse it or die heroically against it. But he was the only soul in this palace who knew, item by item, exactly what it had eaten. If no one named the meal, the meal vanished twice: once into the fire, and once out of memory. He had spent seven years keeping the books.

It was, he thought with a flicker of his old dry humor, fitting that the work of a king should turn out to be accounting.

He got his other knee under him. He rose. His body protested in a chorus of small treacherous voices—seven years folded into a locket does not make for sturdy joints—but voices could be outvoted. He had outvoted louder ones.

"Nell," he said. "How do I look?"

She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Like a king," she said, which was generous and untrue and exactly right.

"Good," said Aldric. "Then watch closely. I'm about to commit treason against the crown."

* * *

He walked into the throne room the way a man walks into a river he knows will carry him off—deliberately, chin up, letting the current take what it takes. The cold hit him first, then the heat, the two of them warring over his skin like dogs over a bone. At the far end of the hall, Zephyrine stood with arms flung wide, hair lifting, the beginnings of something enormous gathering in the air around them—the pressure before lightning, the taste of copper and ozone, the windows beginning to hum. Beside her, Bramble shouted words he didn't understand into a decanted memory, weaponizing stolen pasts; Corvane, gray-faced and resolute, held a barricaded doorway against hunters who had turned their coats; somewhere above, steel rang against steel, Serah Vail dueling her own shadow.

None of it mattered yet. All of it hung on the woman calling the storm, and the storm was not ready, and between her and the Ember stretched thirty feet of dying marble that the fire-thing intended to cross.

So Aldric planted his boots in the middle of it, alone, and did the only thing left to a king who owned nothing: he spoke.

"You know me," he said.

His voice cracked on the second word. Seven years of rust. He raised it anyway, pitched it the way he had once pitched it across battlefields and coronation halls, and the strange acoustics of a room being unmade carried it to every corner.

"You wore my face for seven years. You ate my kingdom through my own chest. So you know me better than any man living—and I know *you*. I know every mouthful."

The Ember turned. Fire has no eyes, but it turned, and the turning was curiosity, and curiosity was the opening Zephyrine needed, and he prayed she'd take it, and he kept talking.

"The widow Maren of the Lower Terraces, who sang to her bees so they wouldn't be lonely. You drank her song on the fourth morning of the frost, and she has not hummed since, and her bees are dying, and *her name is Maren*—" his voice broke, steadied, "—and I am going to say it until you choke on it."

The fire flickered. Just slightly. Like a candle noticing a draft.

"Bram the baker, who hummed at dawn. The twins Osric and Fen, splitting one apple nightly, each swearing his half the larger. The lamplighter Joss, who gave children rides on his ladder. The schoolmistress Annet, who taught letters by lantern during the blackouts you caused. The gravedigger Hollen, who buried strangers' dead with flowers because no one should go under unwelcomed." Name after name, kindness after kindness, and with each one he watched the Ember flinch—that was the word, there was no other—the great flame contracting around some morsel that would not sit quietly in its belly. Because things that are named are *things*, and things have edges, and edges resist digestion. The consumed kindnesses were waking up inside the fire, crowding toward the sound of their own names like children toward a window.

"You cannot eat a thing unseen forever," Aldric said, and now he was weeping, and did not care, and suspected the weeping was itself a kind of weapon. "I saw. I was your cup, your pipe, your throat—and I *kept count*. Seven years of ledgers written in ash. Every warmth you stole, I hold. Every name, I speak. And when this storm breaks you—when she breaks you, and she will—everything you swallowed is going to spill back into the world, because I have testified, and testimony is the one fire you cannot drink."

The Ember shrieked.

There was no other word for it. The sound was the scream of a log splitting, scaled up to the size of a sky, and the temperature plunged so hard that the remaining windows burst inward in a bloom of glass-snow, and the great doors behind Aldric groaned as Corvane threw his whole weight and whole life against them, holding the line, buying seconds.

Above the din—thin, clear, impossible—Aldric heard Zephyrine Dunmore begin to speak a name that was not his, and felt the air itself lean in to listen.

He kept counting.

"Maren. Bram. Osric and Fen. Joss. Annet. Hollen. The midwife Sefa. The ferryman's daughter, Ivy, who sang to the oars..." His voice was failing now, fraying like rope over an edge, and his knees were failing, and none of it mattered, because the Ember had stopped advancing. It stood in a halo of its own stolen light, convulsing, trying to burn away a thousand small remembered joys that would not stop *shining* inside it, and every name Aldric spoke added another coal to that unquenchable heap. Witness, he thought wildly, through the roar and the cold and the tears freezing in his beard. *Witness is resistance.* Let history record that the last act of Aldric Veyran was bookkeeping.

Then the Ember found its footing. The convulsion stilled. The fire gathered itself with a terrible patience and fixed its attention—there was no other word—on the frail old man kneeling in the wreckage of the throne room, the loose thread, the loose tongue, the one witness it had failed to erase.

It drew breath, if fire can be said to breathe.

Aldric, on his knees in the ash of his own reign, lifted his chin and waited for the blow—and behind him, rising like dawn through the broken windows, he heard thunder answer a mortal voice.
