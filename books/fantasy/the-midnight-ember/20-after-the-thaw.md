## 20. After the Thaw

Bramble had smuggled a great many things in her life—stolen years, bottled lullabies, the last words of dead poets sealed in wax—but she had never once smuggled hope, because she had never believed it could be carried. Hope was not cargo. Hope was the thing that leaked out of you when the crate broke.

And yet here it was, leaking everywhere across the ruined capital of Vaelholm, three days after the thaw, and Bramble could do nothing to contain it.

She stood on a balcony of the palace's east wing with her arms full of bread—actual bread, baked that morning in ovens that had burned for the first time since autumn—and watched the city remember how to make noise. Somewhere below, a woman was laughing so hard she had to lean against a wall. A child ran past chasing a dog, both of them shrieking. Someone had opened a window and was beating dust out of a rug, and the rhythmic *thwack-thwack-thwack* of it sounded, to Bramble's smuggler's ears, like the most valuable currency in the world.

The snow lay everywhere still, but it was dying snow now, slushy and ordinary, retreating from the cobbles in gray rags. The frost that had climbed the palace walls like ivy for seven years—the frost no one remembered arriving, because remembering required feeling and the Ember drank feeling first—was melting off the stone in long bright threads.

Bramble counted things when she was afraid or happy or grieving, which were all, at present, the same weather inside her. She counted: one city waking up. One king, alive. One guild charter, formally dissolved as of yesterday noon. One storm-caller, asleep for two days, breathing shallow and even, with no thunder under their skin anymore.

One sister, returned.

* * *

Serah Vail sat by the sickroom fire with Liss's head in her lap, and she had not moved in an hour, and she would not move for another hour after this one either, if anyone was fool enough to ask her to.

Liss slept. That was the whole miracle, and Serah kept turning it over like a coin she suspected might vanish. Her sister's soul had been folded into a locket of frozen fire alongside King Aldric's—two hostages in one small prison—and when Zephyrine's storm had torn the Ember apart from the inside, the bindings had come loose all at once, like knots slipping in wet rope. Aldric had woken in the throne room, frail as ash. Liss had woken in the Ember's vaults, screaming her sister's name into the dark until Osk found her.

Now she slept in a proper bed, in a room with a fire, and every few minutes her fingers twitched against Serah's knee, and every few minutes Serah looked down to confirm the twitching was real.

"You'll cramp," Bramble said quietly from the doorway.

"I've held harder watches."

"I know. I carried your watch once." Bramble came in and set the bread on the table—a whole loaf, dark rye, still warm through its cloth. "From the market. The baker wouldn't take payment. First thing he'd baked since the Hunt began. He said—" She stopped, because her throat did something inconvenient. She tried again. "He said he'd forgotten his own daughter liked it burnt until this morning. He cried into the dough. It's probably the best loaf in the kingdom."

Serah almost smiled. Almost. On her face it looked like weather clearing over hard country. "How are they?" She nodded toward the far bed, where Zephyrine lay beneath three blankets, pale as ledger paper.

"Sleeping. Woke yesterday for about ten minutes. Asked me how much we owed the innkeeper." Bramble sat down heavily on the window bench. "That's how I knew they were going to live. Nobody asks about accounts unless they intend to keep living long enough to settle them."

"And the gift?"

"Gone." Bramble said it plainly, because Serah deserved plainness; the woman had spent years speaking in code and had earned a season of honest words. "Osk checked. Whatever door the storm lived behind—it isn't locked anymore. There's just... wall."

Serah looked into the fire. "I've taken gifts from people my whole career," she said. "Registration. Confession. The knife, when it came to that. I told myself it was mercy, most times. Registration instead of burning." Her jaw worked. "I never once thought about what it would be like to be the person standing there afterward, holding out their hands, empty."

"No," said Bramble gently. "But you're thinking about it now. That counts for something. Most people never get around to it."

Down in the bed by the fire, Liss murmured in her sleep and turned toward the warmth, and Serah Vail—commander of the Long Hunt, terror of three provinces, the woman whose warning scratched into the guildhouse doorframe had started everything—bowed her head over her sister and wept without any sound at all, the way soldiers do, the way she had probably learned to do in exactly the kind of rooms where crying loudly got people registered.

Bramble looked away and gave her the privacy of pretending not to see.

* * *

King Aldric abdicated on the fourth day, in a hall that still smelled faintly of scorched ice.

He stood before what remained of the court—half the nobility had fled during the battle, half of those who stayed couldn't meet his eyes, having spent seven years smiling at a thing that wore him like a coat—and he read from a single sheet of paper in a voice like dry leaves.

Bramble watched from the back, arms crossed, professional skeptic to the last. She had smuggled enough royal decrees to know how they usually went: gold thread, long titles, everyone's interests carefully wrapped in velvet.

This one was four sentences long.

"I was king," Aldric read, "and then I was fuel. For seven years something wore my face and fed on my people, and none of you knew, and neither did I, and I will not pretend that innocence and ignorance are different things when the bill comes due. Therefore I abdicate—not to a successor, for I have seen what happens when one vessel holds too much power, but to a council drawn from every province, every trade, and every gift. Yes," and here he looked up, and Bramble saw the tremor in him and the steel underneath it, "every gift. We have spent seven years learning what a realm becomes when it amputates its own strange children. Never again."

The silence afterward lasted long enough for Bramble to count her own heartbeat eleven times.

Then a wool merchant from Vellsmark—that burned city, that ash on the wind, whose survivors had drifted into the capital like embers ahead of a fire—stood up in the back rows and said, in a cracked voice, "Vellsmark claims a chair," and after that it was simply a matter of logistics, and logistics, Bramble thought, were the one form of magic even ungifted folk could work.

Aldric stepped down from the dais afterward and walked, unaided and unhurried, through the crowd that parted for him, and stopped in front of Bramble.

"You're the memory-smuggler," he said.

"Bramble Thornquist. Guild of Impossible Things. Formerly." She sketched a bow that was mostly attitude. "We're between charters at present."

"I'm told you have ledgers full of purchased memories. Things people sold. Things people stole. Things the Ember erased."

"All of them. Catalogued. Cross-referenced. Some of them are very strange and at least nine of them are crimes."

His smile was thin and real. "Then when your new guild opens, whatever shape it takes—I want the throne's first commission to be this: return every memory we can return. Let people have back what was taken from them. Even the painful ones." His voice roughened. "*Especially* the painful ones. A kingdom that only remembers its joys is a kingdom waiting for the next Ember."

Bramble studied him—this diminished man, this battery, this king of ash and morning—and found, to her considerable irritation, that she was going to cry in front of royalty.

"We'll need funding," she managed.

"Naturally."

"And a building with good locks. Osk is particular."

"Naturally."

"And someone will have to tell Corvane he's not in charge anymore."

Aldric's thin smile widened by a fraction. "I look forward to watching that."

* * *

Corvane dissolved the old guild charter in the guildhouse courtyard, in front of the entire roster, with the scorch mark from Zephyrine's lightning still blackening the flagstones like a signature.

He stood on the same step where he had announced neutrality all those weeks ago—the announcement that had felt like wisdom then and like a slow death sentence now—and he held the charter itself, the original document, heavy vellum with the seal of the Guild of Impossible Things pressed in green wax.

"Read it to them," said Osk, leaning against the doorframe with her arms folded, her lock-witch's rings clicking softly against each other. "All of it. Every clause. They deserve to hear what we signed."

So Corvane read it. Clause by clause, in his big careful voice: the neutrality oath. The secrecy clauses. The clause forbidding members from acting against crown interests—which had, Bramble realized with a fresh wave of nausea, legally bound every one of them to stand aside while Vellsmark burned. And near the bottom, in smaller script, the clause about sanctuary contracts, the one that had bound Corvane's tongue for seven years, the clause the hooded broker had written so cleverly that a decent man had become an unwitting jailer.

When he finished, the courtyard was silent except for the meltwater dripping off the eaves.

"This charter kept us alive," Corvane said. "It also kept us small, and quiet, and complicit, and it nearly cost us—" his voice caught on it, and he let it catch, didn't smooth it over, "-it nearly cost us Zephyrine. It cost Vellsmark everything. So." He turned to the brazier beside him, where Nell—two fingers bandaged where frostbite had claimed them, her other hand steady—held the document's corner over the coals.

Nell looked up at Corvane. "Together?" she asked, and it wasn't really a question about the paper.

"Together," he said.

They burned it together, the old master and the half-feral apprentice, and the green wax bubbled and the neutrality oath curled into black lace and the sparks rose past the scorch mark on the stones, and Bramble—who had spent her whole life believing that everything could be bought, sold, smuggled, and stolen—felt something in her chest come loose and drift upward with the smoke.

No one applauded. It wasn't that kind of moment. But afterward people started talking to each other in low voices about what the new charter should say, and the talking went on well past dusk, and that, Bramble thought, was applause enough.

* * *

Zephyrine woke properly on the fifth day, and Bramble was there, because Bramble was always there, sitting in the sickroom chair with a ledger open on her knees, pretending to work.

"Give me the bad news first," Zephyrine rasped.

"That's my line."

"You've been saying it over my sleeping body for five days. I heard some of it." Their eyes were clear and gray and terribly ordinary. No static in them. Bramble had not realized until now how often the air around Zephyrine used to shimmer faintly, like heat off summer stone. The room just looked like a room now.

"The bad news," Bramble said, closing the ledger. "Your gift is gone. Truly gone. Not sleeping, not buried. Spent. You called a storm through yourself, and storms don't come back through twice."

Zephyrine nodded slowly, the way people nod when the news confirms what the body already knew.

"The worse news," Bramble continued, because Zephyrine would want it in order of severity, they always had, "is that the thaw means spring is coming, which means mud, which means I will expect you to help me re-inventory the entire vault while our boots ruin the floorboards."

Something flickered at the corner of Zephyrine's mouth. "And the good news?"

"Liss ate half a loaf this morning. Aldric has appointed a council that includes a wool merchant. Nell has named her missing fingers 'Ferdinand' and 'Gerald' and insists they ache before rain, which she considers a promotion. And Corvane—" Bramble paused, choosing. "Corvane asked whether you'd receive him. He's been standing outside this door twice a day like a man outside a confessional with nothing in his pockets but sin."

Zephyrine was quiet a long moment. The fire popped. Downstairs, faintly, someone had begun to sing—an actual song, melody and all, the first music inside these walls since music became sedition—and the sound drifted up through the floorboards like water finding a channel.

"It's strange," Zephyrine said at last. Their voice was level, which meant it was costing them. "Seven years I hid it. Seven years I hated it, the static under my skin, the way candle flames leaned toward me, the way I had to count everything twice to keep the lightning down. I thought when it was gone I'd feel light." They lifted one hand and turned it over, palm up, empty. "I feel robbed. Isn't that ungrateful? It saved everyone. It killed the Ember. And all I can think is that my hand doesn't hum anymore."

"No," Bramble said. "It isn't ungrateful. I've carried stolen memories my whole life, Zeph. Do you know what it's like to give one back? To open the little bottle and pour somebody's mother's face back into their head? They weep. They thank me. And then they grieve the years they lived without it, and nobody calls *that* ingratitude." She reached over and took Zephyrine's empty humming-less hand. "You lost a limb. Limbs get mourned. Mourn it properly. I'll bring tea."

Zephyrine's composure held for approximately four more seconds. Then they turned their face toward the wall and shook, silently, and Bramble sat with them and held their hand and did not say *it's all right*, because it wasn't, and did not say *you'll be fine*, because she didn't know, and only said, when the shaking slowed, "For what it's worth—the room remembers you kindly. All the rooms do. Since the thaw, I can read them. What a place remembers. This room remembers someone who paid its price honestly."

"What does that even mean?" Zephyrine whispered, wetly, into the pillow.

"I have absolutely no idea," Bramble admitted. "But I think it's the beginning of a new kind of ledger. And I think you're going to be very good at keeping it."

* * *

On the sixth evening, they carried bread into the ruins of the throne room.

It had been Osk's idea, which surprised no one; lock-witches understood thresholds better than anyone, and she said a room needed to be re-entered on purpose or it stayed a wound. So Nell baked—badly, gloriously, with flour in her eyebrows—and Corvane carried the loaves, and Serah came with Liss leaning on her arm, and Aldric arrived alone and unannounced and sat down on the steps of his former dais like a man visiting a grave that had turned out to hold a garden.

The throne itself was gone. In its place was a scar of clean stone where the binding had been, and around it, radiating outward, the marks of the final storm—blackened fans, like wings, like ferns, like frost made of shadow. Zephyrine stood looking at them for a long time.

"I did that," they said quietly.

"You did that," Nell agreed cheerfully, tearing a loaf. "It looks like a flower. An angry flower."

"A very angry flower," Osk said.

They sat where they could—on the dais steps, on rubble smoothed with cloaks, Serah and Liss shoulder to shoulder, Corvane careful to leave space beside himself for Zephyrine and visibly relieved when it was filled. Bread went around. Butter, smuggled in from a farmstead two days' ride away, was spread thick and unreasonable. Liss, still thin and hollow-eyed, laughed at something Nell whispered, and the laugh echoed strangely in the high ruined room, and no one minded the echo, because echoes meant the room was learning sound again.

"So," Corvane said at last, chewing. He had the expression of a man assembling a speech and abandoning it mid-brick. "The new charter. We should—there are matters of—"

"Tomorrow," said Aldric.

Everyone looked at him. The frail king brushed crumbs from his knees. "I ruled a kingdom for twelve years and was absent from it for seven, and in all that time the wisest thing ever done in my name was done by a guild accountant with a storm in their chest and friends who refused to be sensible. Tomorrow there will be councils and charters and, I am assured, a wool merchant with opinions about tariffs." He raised his crust of bread like a toast. "Tonight there is bread, and the people who broke the winter. That is the whole agenda."

They ate. The fire Osk had built in the old hearth—hearth of hearths, the Ember's first doorway, now just a fireplace with a cheerful ordinary blaze—cracked and settled. Outside the tall broken windows, the sky did the thing it had been doing every night since the thaw: turning slowly through colors, purple and copper and deep forgiving blue, as though it were showing off, as though it too had been locked in a locket and had opinions about freedom.

Bramble sat with her back against the dais and let herself be, for once, entirely off duty. No crates to check. No seals to forge. Just bread and warmth and the sound of her strange family arguing about whether Ferdinand and Gerald could feel weather in fingers they no longer occupied.

Then Nell, butter on her chin, said suddenly: "Zeph. When the storm took your name—you gave it your name, right? To balance the ledger? What did it *do* with it?"

The room went quiet in the gentle way rooms do when a real question lands among them.

Zephyrine looked down at their hands. "I don't know," they said. "I assumed it was consumed. Prices usually are."

"Hm," said Bramble.

They all looked at her.

"Hm *what*?" demanded Osk.

Bramble pulled out her ledger—the new one, blank except for a few pages—and opened it to the entry she had been avoiding telling anyone about. Three nights ago, in the sickroom, the ink had moved. Just once. A line appearing below the account of the Unbinding, in handwriting that was not hers and yet was achingly familiar, in the neat upright script of a born bookkeeper:

*A debt remains outstanding. A name, given freely, seeks its keeper.*

"I was going to wait," Bramble said, "until everyone had finished healing. But the ledger's been humming at me all day, and I've smuggled enough impossible things to know when a package wants opening." She turned the page toward the firelight, toward the family, toward Zephyrine.

"Someone," she said, "has left us a delivery."
