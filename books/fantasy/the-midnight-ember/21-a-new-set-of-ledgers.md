## 21. A New Set of Ledgers

The guildhouse smelled different now.

Zephyrine noticed it every morning, the way one notices a missing tooth with the tongue—some old absence that the mouth keeps reaching for. Once, the halls of the Guild of Impossible Things had carried the scent of secrets: beeswax and tallow smoke and something faintly metallic, like the air after lightning or before a lie. Now it smelled of bread from Osk's oven, of ink and clean paper, of Nell's ever-present smell of woodsmoke and wet wool, of spring coming through windows they no longer boarded shut.

Seven months since the thaw. Seven months since the last lightning bolt left them like an arrow leaving a bowstring, and did not return.

Zephyrine woke before dawn anyway, as they always had, out of habit older than grief. The room they kept was small and faced east, and when the light came over the rooftops of the capital's poorer quarter it caught the scar on the back of their hand—the mark where the gift had exited, if such a thing could be said to exit anywhere, which the physicians disagreed about and Zephyrine had stopped asking. It looked like a branching burn, delicate as frost ferns, and on cold mornings it ached in a way that had nothing to do with weather anymore.

They dressed, went down, and lit the hearth with flint and steel like any ordinary person.

That was the part no one warned you about, Zephyrine thought, watching the kindling catch. Everyone imagined losing a gift would feel like losing a limb—dramatic, catastrophic, a single terrible severing. And for the first weeks it had been that: phantom storms, hands that curled around thunder that wasn't there, nights spent sitting upright in bed certain they could feel pressure building behind their ribs, a whole sky trying to be born inside them and finding the door closed. But limbs healed into stumps, and stumps learned their own shape. What remained was quieter and stranger. What remained was *ordinary*, and ordinary, it turned out, had its own weight.

"You're talking to the fire again," said Nell from the doorway.

"I'm greeting it."

"It's not listening. It's just fire."

"That," said Zephyrine, "is exactly what I'm greeting." They smiled at her—nine fingers now, the two the frost took gone forever, though she'd made a joke of it within a week, holding up her hand and announcing she'd finally have an excuse for bad arithmetic. She carried a basket of eggs she'd traded for at the market, real eggs, paid for with honest coin, and she set them on the table with the care of someone who still remembered hunger.

"Corvane wants you in the counting room when you've eaten," she said. Then, grinning: "He says it's urgent. He says everything's urgent. He stood there this morning worrying the ledger shelf like a man whose favorite child has run off to sea."

"He can worry. The charter's not going anywhere."

"No," Nell agreed, and her grin softened into something warmer, more careful. "It isn't, is it. We wrote it down and everything."

She went off toward the kitchen, humming. That was another thing that had come back to the world: humming. In the Ember's winter, sound itself had seemed to starve; people spoke less, sang never, and the silence between words had grown teeth. Now Nell hummed, the baker across the lane sang off-key through his open window, and twice a week someone in the guildhouse got drunk enough to attempt the old harvest rounds, badly, with feeling.

Zephyrine ate standing up, as they always had, then walked down the corridor to the counting room, trailing their fingers along the wall as they went.

And here was the thing—the quiet, impossible, unadvertised thing—that Zephyrine had told almost no one:

The wall remembered them doing it.

Not remembered in the way stone remembers anything, which is to say dully and without preference. Remembered the way a page remembers being written on. As Zephyrine's fingertips passed along the plaster, they felt the ghost-pressure of every other hand that had trailed that same corridor—Bramble's quick smuggler's touch, Osk's lock-witch caress that tested every surface for hinges, Corvane's heavy anxious palm pressed flat during the worst nights of the Long Hunt. Felt, faintly, the fear-sweat of hunters' gloves and the salt of Bramble's tears the night they'd all scattered, and underneath all of it, older and deeper, the patience of the mason who'd laid the stones two hundred years ago and been proud of his work.

Attention, it turned out, was not nothing.

The gift was gone—truly gone, poured out with the storm into the binding, spent down to the last copper of it. But attention had never been the gift. Attention had only been riding in the gift's cart all these years, unnoticed, the way the accountant rides in the army's wake. A storm-caller commanded sky. But Zephyrine had spent seven years keeping ledgers of impossible things, and ledgers are not really about numbers. Ledgers are about looking. About noticing what entered, what left, what changed hands, and who was lying about it.

A room was only a ledger written in dust and wear and the residue of feeling. And Zephyrine had gotten very good at reading ledgers.

* * *

The counting room had once held the guild's true business: bottled thunderclaps, borrowed years, smuggled memories, vials of other people's grief decanted and sold by the dram. Those shelves were empty now. In their place stood rows of plain oak ledgers, unglamorous, hand-bound by Bramble, who had discovered she liked bookbinding better than memory-running and was insufferable about it.

Corvane stood at the central table looking, as Nell had promised, like a man whose child had gone to sea. He was heavier than he'd been a year ago—he'd begun eating again, actually eating, instead of performing the ritual pantomime of meals—and his hair had gone fully white at the temples, but his eyes were the same eyes that had bought an exile contract seven years ago out of guilt and called it mercy.

"The printer came," he said, without preamble, and set a broadsheet on the table between them.

Zephyrine read it. THE CHARTER OF THE GUILD OF HONEST THINGS, the header ran, and beneath it the new articles, typeset and waiting for signature. No trade in memories. No trade in years, health, names, or love. No neutrality clause—neutrality had died on the palace steps along with the Ember, buried under the weight of everyone admitting how much their careful indifference had cost. Instead: witness, sanctuary, record. The guild would keep accounts of the realm's truths, protect those who carried dangerous ones, and teach the reading of rooms and records to anyone who came willing.

"It's good," Zephyrine said.

"There's a blank space." Corvane tapped the bottom of the page, where the keeper's name would go. "Article One requires the guild to name its Keeper of Records. Bramble says it should be her. Osk says it should be Bramble. Nell voted for the cat."

"The cat is dead, Corvane."

"Nell voted for it anyway. Sentimentally." He cleared his throat, and Zephyrine saw the old guilt move behind his face like a fish under ice—the guilt that had lived there since the day he'd confessed the bargain, the hooded broker, the contract that forbade him from ever telling Zephyrine why they'd been exiled. He would carry that fish under that ice for the rest of his life, Zephyrine suspected. They had stopped trying to melt it for him. Some penances were load-bearing.

"You want me to sign," Zephyrine said.

"I want you to consider signing. There's a difference, and after everything, I've learned to respect it." He pushed the quill across the table, then pulled it back. "No. First—there's something else. Something I found while cataloguing the vaults. The deep ones, under the burned wing. And I don't want to hand it to you the way things were handed to you before. In pieces. By brokers." His jaw worked. "So look at it yourself, and take whatever time you need."

He opened a plain wooden box and lifted out a ledger.

Zephyrine knew it immediately, the way you know your own handwriting on a stranger's letter. It was their first ledger—the one they'd kept as a junior clerk in the old guild, the one that had vanished the week of their exile. Its cover was water-stained. Its spine had been broken and badly re-sewn by someone in a hurry.

"I found it walled into the foundation," Corvane said quietly. "Under the counting room floor. Deliberately hidden, and recently—the mortar was newer than the stones. Someone put it there during the winter. After the thaw, I think, or right at the end of it." He paused. "There's writing in it that isn't yours."

Zephyrine opened it.

Their own entries, young and precise, marched down the early pages—one bottled thunderclap, three borrowed months, a memory of a wedding purchased by a widow who wanted to feel it fresh again. Then, halfway through, the handwriting changed. The new script was cramped and hurried, pressed hard enough into the page to emboss the leaves beneath, as if the writer had feared the words might otherwise refuse to stay.

*I am the room,* the strange hand had written. *I do not know how else to say it. You touched me every morning for seven years and I learned the shape of your attention, and when the storm went out of you, some of it stayed in me—in the walls, in the floors, in the dust of this place. I am what your family left behind when they scattered, and I have been holding it.*

Below that, entry after entry, in that cramped urgent script, the walls of the guildhouse had recorded what happened while Zephyrine was away: the night the hunters came and Osk locked doors that weren't doors. The night Nell stood between a crossbow and the person she loved most in the world and did not step aside. Corvane's confession, spoken aloud to an empty counting room because he could not yet say it to a full one. Bramble weeping over a ledger she couldn't bring herself to deliver.

And on the last written page, in that same pressing hand, a single line, underlined twice:

*You read rooms. So read this: there is one account still open. It is not ours. It is waiting in the west wing, in the room where the fire started, and it has been waiting to be closed since before any of you were born.*

Zephyrine sat very still for a long moment. Outside, the ordinary morning went on—a cart rattling past, Nell's humming, the baker's terrible singing—and inside them, where the storm used to live, something moved that was not quite longing and not quite dread.

"Corvane," they said at last, "when the Ember burned Vellsmark—where in the city did the fire start?"

He frowned. "I never asked. Why?"

"Because I think I'm about to."

* * *

The west wing had been sealed since the thaw. Not ruined—the fire that had taken it was small, contained, almost polite—but abandoned, the way people abandon rooms where something was said that can't be unsaid. Dust lay on everything in a fine grey velvet, undisturbed except by light.

Zephyrine went alone. This was deliberate. Whatever waited here, they needed to meet it the way they now met everything: with nothing but themselves, no storm to hide behind, no gift to do the work their courage should have done.

At the threshold of the burned room, they stopped, laid their palm against the doorframe, and listened.

Rooms remember in textures, Zephyrine had learned. Fear felt like grit. Grief like damp. Joy, rarely surviving long, felt like warmth trapped in north-facing stone. But this—this was none of those. This was *attention*. Dense, patient, layered attention, years of it, the residue of someone watching and watching and watching, and beneath it, unmistakable even now, the mineral chill of the Ember's passing.

They stepped inside.

The room had clearly once been a study. Char marks climbed one wall in a fan pattern—the origin point of the Vellsmark fire, brought here somehow, or copied, or commemorated; Zephyrine didn't yet know which, and the room's memory blurred frustratingly at exactly that point, as if the answer were a page torn out. In the center stood a desk. On the desk, arranged with terrible neatness, lay objects Zephyrine recognized one by one as recognition arrived like slow knives:

A storm-caller's focus-stone, cracked down the middle. Their mother's. Lost when they were twelve, mourned for a year, gone without explanation.

An exile contract, the original, the one the hooded broker had drafted—the one whose existence Corvane's confession had proven and whose text Zephyrine had never been permitted to see. Unfolded now. Waiting.

And beneath both, a single sheet of fresh paper in the cramped pressing hand of the house itself, dated to the night of the thaw:

*The Ember kept records too. Everything it erased, it wrote down first—it could not digest what it had not inventoried. Its accounting books were hidden in the walls of the places it emptied. This room was its counting room. There are others. The largest cache is not in the capital. It is in Vellsmark, under the ash, and it holds the names of everyone the Ember consumed, and it will not stay buried forever.*

*You are the Keeper of Records now. So keep them.*

Zephyrine stood in the dusty light with the paper trembling slightly in their hand—not from fear, they realized, but from the peculiar vertigo of an accountant discovering the books are bigger than anyone dreamed. Behind them, in the corridor, footsteps: Nell first, always Nell, and then the others, drawn by whatever instinct tells a family when one of its own has found the edge of something vast.

"Well?" Bramble called softly. "What is it?"

Zephyrine looked down at the exile contract, at the line where their own younger name waited beside a reason they'd been forbidden to learn. They looked at the doorframe where the room's long attention pressed against their skin like a hand on the shoulder.

"It's the next entry," they said, and reached for the contract.
