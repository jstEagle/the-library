## 1. The Valley That Forgot

The bone told Emrys it had been a boy before it was a bone.

He knew this the way he knew the weight of rain coming, or the particular silence a house keeps when someone has died inside it — not with his mind, but somewhere older than his mind, in the place where his hands met his heart. The femur lay across his leather apron, pale as a peeled birch branch, and through it ran a thin thread of voice like water under ice. *Cold,* the marrow murmured. *The pond was so cold and then it wasn't anything at all.*

Emrys set down his smallest knife — the one he called the Listener, though he'd never said that aloud to anyone — and let his thumb rest on the bone's knobbled end.

"I know," he said softly. "I know, little one."

The workshop smelled of tallow and juniper smoke and the faint sweet rot of the peat fire. Outside, the evening was doing what valley evenings did: folding itself gently over the rooftops of Hollowmere, laying a blue-grey blanket across the terraced fields, sending the first lamplighters up the crooked lanes with their long poles and their coughs. Somewhere a dog was complaining about something with great conviction. Somewhere Maren Ashwell's daughter was probably tormenting her mother's hens again. The world went on being ordinary, which was both a comfort and, these days, a kind of insult.

Because in here, a six-year-old boy named Tam Wicker was dead, and his femur was talking to Emrys Thornbury, and no one else in the whole walled-off world of the valley could hear it.

*Was I good?* the marrow asked. It asked this the way children ask things — urgently, as if the answer might change the shape of everything.

"You were good," Emrys said. "You shared your bread with your sister even when she didn't share back. You once tried to rescue a frog from the millrace and got so soaked your mother thought you'd fallen in. You were kind to animals and rude to turnips." He smiled at the bone, because the dead liked to be smiled at; they had spent their whole lives watching faces and it seemed cruel to stop now. "You were very good."

The marrow made a sound like a sigh settling into warm sand. Satisfied, then. Children were easy that way, once you learned their frequency. It was the old ones who lingered, the ones with unfinished ledgers, the ones who wanted to argue about wills and fences and who had said what at whose wedding forty years ago. Tam Wicker only needed to be told he'd been good, and to have his name carved where the living could see it.

So Emrys picked up the Listener again and began.

Bone-carving was slow work when done properly, and Emrys was nothing if not proper about it. He turned the femur in the lamplight and cut the memorial script into the shaft in fine linked letters: *TAM, SON OF BRIDGET AND OSKER, WHO LOVED FROGS.* Not *who drowned* — never that. The valley's custom, older than anyone could say, was to carve what the dead loved rather than how they died, and Emrys agreed with the custom. Death was an ending everyone got whether they wanted it or not. Love, at least, was chosen.

The knife whispered along the grain. And through the grain, faintly, other voices came.

They always came when he carved. He had long ago stopped pretending they wouldn't. Every bone in his workshop held somebody — that was the nature of the trade, the strange covenant-within-the-covenant that had made the Thornbury family necessary for two hundred years. A rib from old Hedda Voss still grumbled about her knees. A finger-bone of a shepherd named Col kept humming a tune nobody living remembered. And beneath them all, deeper than any single bone should reach, there was the general murmur — the great soft tide of the valley's accumulated dead, speaking all at once and none at all, like wind through a wheatfield, like rain on a roof.

Emrys had been hearing them since before he could talk. His mother said his first word wasn't *mama*. It was *Hedda*, spoken toward the shelf where Hedda Voss's ribs sat wrapped in linen.

*Carve us too,* the tide said, the way it always said, patient and vast and slightly sad. *We are here. We are here. We are here.*

"In a moment," Emrys told the room, as he always did, knowing they couldn't wait and wouldn't mind anyway. The dead were the most patient company a man ever kept. That was precisely the problem with them.

He worked until the last letter caught the light, then blew away the bone-dust — carefully, reverently, because bone-dust was not nothing; bone-dust was somebody's somebody — and held the finished memorial up for inspection.

"Well, Tam," he said. "What do you think?"

The femur hummed its small contentment. From the shelf, old Hedda observed that the boy's letters were better than her husband's had been, and her husband had been a surveyor, so there. Col the shepherd's finger-bone changed its tune to something almost celebratory. The tide rose and fell around them all, and Emrys sat in the middle of it, warm lamplit island, ferryman of names.

This was the part nobody understood. This was the part he could never explain to the living without sounding either blessed or cursed, and he'd never decided which one he was.

Everyone in Hollowmere mourned. Everyone grieved, buried, wept at gravesides, left offerings at the little door-shrines. But grief, for them, was a conversation with an empty chair. For Emrys it was a crowded room. When Bridget Wicker wept at her son's grave, she wept into silence. When Emrys stood beside her — as he would tomorrow, delivering the bone — he would feel Tam's presence flickering at the edge of his senses like a candle flame deciding whether to gutter out, and he would have to smile and say the customary words — *he is at rest, he is remembered* — while inside him a child's voice was asking *will my mama be all right?*

And what was he supposed to say? *Yes* was a lie. *I don't know* was cruelty. So he said *she loves you, and love is a kind of staying*, and hoped the dead found it as comforting as the living pretended to.

He was, he reflected, setting the femur in its cedar box lined with wool, the loneliest well-loved man in the valley. Everyone knew him. Everyone needed him. The bereaved came to his door with their sorrows bundled in cloth like loaves, and he took each one seriously, and carved each name, and sent each voice gently down into the great tide where the dead pooled together and forgot their individual coldness. He was essential. He was cherished. He was completely, utterly alone with it.

His mother used to say — before the tide took her voice too, before her own bones joined the shelves he could no longer bring himself to shelve — *you listen so they don't have to scream, Emrys. That's a mercy. But mercy is heavy. Set it down sometimes.*

He'd never learned how.

A knock at the workshop door — three quick raps, unmistakable.

"Emrys! I know you're in there. There's lamplight and I can smell juniper and you never leave a carving half-done, you're constitutionally incapable of it."

Emrys sighed, set down the knife, and opened the door onto Maren Ashwell, holding a basket covered with a cloth that was doing a poor job of hiding a pie.

"Maren."

"Don't 'Maren' me in that tone, I brought dinner."

"You always bring dinner when you want something."

"And it always works," she said, sweeping past him into the workshop with the ease of thirty years' friendship, "because you're a coward with excellent taste in pies. Goose and leek. Sit down."

He sat. She unpacked the pie, poured two cups of the elderflower wine he kept for occasions he dreaded, and looked around the workshop with the expression she wore whenever she was working up to something — chin lifted, eyes busy, mouth set like a fence gate latched against weather.

"It's Tam Wicker's bone," she said, nodding at the cedar box.

"Yes."

"Bridget's taking it hard."

"They always do."

"Not like this." Maren's voice dropped, lost its banter, became the low urgent thing underneath. "She hasn't slept in four nights. She sits by the pond. Osker had to carry her home twice. And today—" Maren stopped, pressed her lips together, started again. "Today she walked up to the old shrine, Emrys. The ruined one, above the falls. She knelt down in the wet moss and she prayed. Out loud. Begging. Anyone. Anything."

Emrys's hand stilled on his cup. "Nothing answered."

"Of course nothing answered!" Maren flared, then caught herself, lowered her voice. "Nothing answers. Nothing has answered in a hundred years, that's what everyone says, that's why the shrine is ruins and the offering-stones are green with moss and nobody goes up there except fools and the heartbroken. But she went anyway, because her boy is dead and the gods are supposed to be for exactly that." She leaned forward. "So I'm going to ask you, and I need you not to make that face."

"What face?"

"That face. The one like you've swallowed a lemon and are trying to be polite about it."

"That's just my face, Maren."

"Your face has opinions. Ask me what she wants."

Emrys looked into his wine. "What does she want?"

"She wants someone to go up to the shrine and pray properly. Really pray. Old words, new words, any words. Someone the dead favor." Maren's hand crossed the table and gripped his wrist, warm and rough and trembling slightly. "Someone who hears. Because if anyone's prayers would get through, it'd be yours. Everyone says so. Even people who don't believe in anything say so."

The tide in the bones stirred. Emrys felt it like a change in air pressure — the murmur sharpening, hundreds of voices leaning in to listen to what he would say. Even the dead, it seemed, were curious.

"Maren," he said carefully. "Prayer doesn't work like that."

"Then how does it work?"

"It doesn't. That's the point. The gods stopped answering a century ago. The shrine's been empty longer than the moot-hall's been standing. Whatever listened up there, it's gone, or sleeping, or never was. Praying there won't help Bridget. It'll only teach her to hope at something that can't hold it."

"But you don't *know* that."

"No," he admitted. "I don't know that. I know what I hear, and what I hear is the dead, and the dead don't mention gods. Don't you think that's strange? Two hundred years of voices, and not one of them has ever said *I saw heaven*. They just... pool. Fade. Stay." He turned his cup slowly. "If there were gods listening, Maren, I'd hear the echo of it somewhere. I hear everything else."

Maren stared at him. "You've never told anyone that."

"I'm telling you."

"Why?"

"Because you brought a pie and I'm weak."

But she didn't laugh. Her grip tightened on his wrist. "Emrys Thornbury. Bridget Wicker needs a miracle or she'll follow her son into that pond, and you're the closest thing this valley has to a man who talks to heaven. Go up to the shrine. Kneel down. Say the words. If nothing happens, nothing happens — you'll have lost an afternoon and gained some moss stains. But if something does—"

"If something does," Emrys said quietly, "then I'll have to live knowing what else might be listening."

He hadn't meant to say that aloud. Maren blinked. "What does that mean?"

It meant the dream he never spoke of — the one where the tide of the dead went suddenly silent, all at once, like birds before an earthquake, and something enormous turned its attention toward him through the floor of the world. It meant that his whole life he had felt heard by something, and had spent that whole life declining to find out what. It meant that listening, for Emrys, had always cost something he couldn't name, and he feared the bill was still coming due.

"It means I'm a bone-carver, not a priest," he said instead. "The answer is no, Maren. I'm sorry. Tell Bridget I'll sit with her tonight. Tell her grief isn't a prayer problem. Tell her—" He stopped. Rubbed his eyes. "Tell her I'll come at dusk and stay as long as she needs."

Maren withdrew her hand. She repacked the basket with slow, hurt movements, leaving the pie — she always left the pie; it was how she punished him and fed him in the same gesture — and at the door she paused, backlit by the last of the daylight, and said:

"One hundred years of silence, Emrys. My grandmother prayed her whole life at that shrine and got nothing. Fine. But my grandmother also said the valley wasn't always walled. She said there was a time before the border stones, and the founders built them for a reason, and prayers stopped the day the walls went up. She said it like it was connected." She shrugged, attempting lightness and missing. "Old women's stories. Probably nothing. But if you ever get curious about what your valley's actually sitting on... ask the bones. You're the only one who can."

The door closed behind her.

Emrys sat a long while in the lamplight. Around him the tide murmured — *we are here, we are here, we are here* — and beneath the murmur, for the first time in his memory, he noticed the shape of the silence underneath it. Deep. Round. Patient.

Like something holding its breath.

He looked at the shelf of bones, at the cedar box with Tam's name on it, at the untouched pie going golden-smelling in the cooling air.

"Just moss," he told the room firmly. "Tomorrow. Moss stains and wasted words, and then we never speak of it again."

From the shelf, old Hedda's ribs made a sound suspiciously like laughter, and deep beneath the workshop, beneath the valley, beneath a hundred years of answered-by-nothing prayer, something waited to see if he meant it.

* * *

Dusk found Emrys climbing the path above the falls with a lantern and a stubborn sense of having been managed — by Maren, by Bridget's grief, by whatever it was that made him unable to refuse a mourner anything. The ruined shrine sat in its bowl of mossy stone like a broken tooth, offering-stones furred green, the little altar cracked clean through. No god had lived here in living memory. The moss had won a hundred-year war and knew it.

Bridget Wicker knelt in the wet, whispering her son's name over and over, and Emrys knelt beside her, and put his arm around her, and said the customary words.

And then, because he was tired, and grieving secondhand, and because old women's stories are sometimes load-bearing, he bowed his head and prayed — one sentence, careless, almost annoyed:

*"Whoever's up there. If you exist. She needs you more than I need my silence. So speak."*

For a moment — nothing. Moss. Wind. Bridget's whispering.

Then every bone on every shelf in his workshop, half a mile below, went silent all at once.

Every single one.

And something beneath the valley drew a breath so vast that Emrys felt it in his teeth, and the moss beneath his knees grew warm.

*Oh,* thought Emrys Thornbury, the valley's last listener, as the warmth spread. *Oh no.*

The shrine began, very quietly, to glow.
