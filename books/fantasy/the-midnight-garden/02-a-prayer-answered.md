## 2. A Prayer Answered

The morning of the miracle began, as most mornings did in Thornmere Vale, with bread that refused to rise and a neighbor who refused to leave.

"Emrys." Maren Ashwick stood in his doorway with the particular posture of a woman who had rehearsed an argument on the walk over and intended to deliver all of it. "You didn't come to the shrine."

"I came to the shrine last month," Emrys said, not looking up from the workbench. The femur before him belonged to old Tam Hollis, who had died in autumn complaining about the price of lamp oil and had not stopped complaining since, at least not to anyone but Emrys. He was carving Tam's name into the head of it, small and careful, the letters no bigger than barley grains.

"You prayed for rain then, and it rained anyway, so you've decided your part was done." Maren stepped inside without invitation — she had been doing that since they were children, when she used to steal his plums off the sill — and folded her arms. "It's different this time. It's Lark."

At the name, Emrys set down his knife. Lark was Maren's daughter, seven years old, with a laugh like a cart rattling downhill and a fever that had come and gone three times since midwinter, each time leaving her thinner, paler, quieter. The doctor from Lowbridge had shaken his head with the practiced gentleness of a man paid by the visit.

"The fever's back," Maren said. "Third night running. She talks to people who aren't there. And I thought—" Her voice caught, and she smoothed it flat again with visible effort, the way one smooths a wrinkle out of a tablecloth. "I thought if you went with me. If you knelt at the shrine too. Two prayers instead of one. Like doubling a fence against wolves."

Emrys looked at her for a long moment. Forty years he had lived in this valley, thirty of them as its bone-carver, the man families came to when someone died so that something would remain — a name in bone, a charm for the grieving pocket. He knew what Maren was really asking. She wasn't asking him to pray. She was asking him to believe, and she wanted company in it, because believing alone in Thornmere Vale felt like standing outside in a storm insisting the sun was up somewhere.

"Maren." He wiped his hands on his apron, though there was little on them but bone dust, white as flour. "You know what happens at that shrine."

"Nothing. Nothing happens at that shrine. That's rather my point."

"Nothing has happened there for a hundred years. The founders built it, the founders blessed it, and then the gods stopped answering, and everyone agreed to pretend they'd simply moved away. Like relatives. You don't ask why they stopped writing; you just stop setting places for them at supper." He turned the femur over. Tam Hollis muttered something in the marrow — he always muttered around noon, hungry even now, or perhaps only remembering hunger. *Lamp oil*, he grumbled. *Robbery, what they charge.*

"And yet we keep sweeping the steps," Maren said. "Every spring. Why do we do that, Emrys, if the gods have moved away?"

He didn't have an answer he liked. The truth was that the sweeping was habit, and habit was a kind of hope wearing sensible shoes, and Emrys had long ago decided he could not afford hope. Not because he was a coward — though Maren might have said otherwise, fondly — but because listening cost him. When he knelt at the shrine, the silence there was not empty. It was *held*. Like a breath. Like a room where someone sat very still behind a door, waiting to see whether you would go away. He had never told anyone this. There are things a bone-carver learns to keep in the bone.

"Lark needs the doctor," he said finally.

"The doctor needs money and gives us shrugs. The shrine costs nothing but knees." Maren's eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "Come with me tomorrow morning. One hour. If nothing happens, I'll never ask again, and you may carve that promise into a bone and wave it at me every market day for the rest of our lives."

Despite everything — despite the held breath of the shrine and the muttering marrow and the low grey dread that had lived under his ribs like a stone since childhood — Emrys heard himself say: "One hour."

* * *

They went at dawn, because Maren believed dawn prayers were more punctual, and gods presumably kept farmer's hours.

The shrine sat on the hill above the millpond, a circle of nine standing stones around a worn altar, lichen-greened and patient. Nine stones for the nine founders, everyone said, though Emrys had noticed as a boy that there were ten sockets cut into the earth, one forever empty, and no one had ever satisfactorily explained why. He had asked his uncle Coram once. Coram had looked at him for a moment too long and said, *Some seats stay empty on purpose, boy,* and changed the subject to fence posts.

Maren knelt at the altar with the ease of long practice, hands open, head bowed. Emrys stood at the circle's edge like a man at the shore of cold water.

The silence rose up to meet him.

It was worse today. Usually the shrine's stillness was simply absence — no murmuring dead, none of the soft crowd-noise that followed him everywhere else in the valley, leaking out of graveyards and heirloom charms and, once memorably, a soup bone in Widow Pell's kitchen that had made him drop the ladle. Here there was only quiet. But this morning the quiet had texture. Weight. It pressed against his ears the way deep water does.

And beneath it — he would spend years trying to describe what came next — he felt something turn over. Vast and slow, like a whale shifting beneath ice, like the whole valley settling on foundations no one had ever seen.

*Someone is home.*

Emrys took a step backward. Then, because Maren was watching, another forward, and lowered himself awkwardly onto one knee beside her, feeling foolish and frightened and forty years old.

"Maren," he whispered. "Maybe we should—"

"*Hush.* I'm praying."

She prayed the way she did everything: thoroughly, with lists. She thanked whatever listened for the plum harvest, apologized in advance for anything she'd forgotten, and then got to Lark, and when she got to Lark her voice broke clean through and she stopped apologizing to anyone and simply begged, plainly, terribly, the way only a mother can beg — not eloquently, not formally, just *please, please, please*, repeated like a heartbeat.

Emrys closed his eyes. And because he did not know what else to do, and because he was, despite his best efforts, a believer of extremely modest means, he added silently: *If you're there, and if you can, and if it doesn't cost anyone anything — help her. She sweeps your steps.*

The vast thing beneath the valley drew breath.

He felt it happen. Not heard — felt, in his teeth, in the hollows of his bones, in the carved names of two hundred dead murmuring somewhere far below his awareness. A single enormous inhalation, slow as seasons.

Then the world *moved*.

Light came up out of the ground. Not firelight, not sunlight — light the way he imagined memory must look from the inside, gold-grey and gentle, rising between the nine stones like water finding its level. It pooled around Maren's knees, around her bowed head, and she gasped but did not flee, because her daughter's name was still half-spoken in her mouth.

And the fever broke. He knew it the way he knew weather coming — not evidence but certainty — and indeed, later, Maren swore she felt it leave Lark at that exact moment, three fields away, a heat lifting out of the child like a bird off a branch.

The light sank back into the hill. The stones stood ordinary and green. A wren sang somewhere with the smugness of a creature who has witnessed nothing.

Maren opened her eyes. They were shining, and afraid, and joyful, all three fighting for the same territory.

"Emrys," she breathed. "Emrys, it *answered*."

He could not speak. Because something else had happened, something only he knew, and it had turned his spine to winter.

For the first time in his life, the dead were silent.

All of them. Everywhere. Tam Hollis in his workshop, mid-complaint. The murmurs in the churchyard, the whispers in the heirloom charms, the chorus that had been his constant, maddening, beloved companionship since he was six years old and first heard his grandmother's voice in a sheep's knucklebone. Gone. Every voice. Not faded — *stopped*, all at once, like a hall of musicians lifting their bows together.

The valley had drawn a breath. Something vast had filled its lungs.

And Emrys understood, with the cold clarity that comes to men kneeling on frozen ground at dawn, that the silence was not peace.

It was attention.

* * *

By noon, half of Thornmere Vale had climbed the shrine hill, and by evening, the other half.

They came carefully, the way villagers approach anything miraculous — sidelong, hats in hand, ready to be delighted or devoured. But Lark Ashwick herself settled the matter. She came running up the hill ahead of her mother at sundown, pink-cheeked and loud, demanding to know why everyone was staring, and when the crowd saw her — saw the child who had been grey as ash for three weeks now chasing the Henderson boys' dog in circles — the dam broke.

People wept. People laughed. Old Sorrel Pike, who had not knelt voluntarily since his wedding, went down onto both knees with a crack that made bystanders wince and stayed there praying with his face in the grass. Someone started singing one of the old harvest hymns, the ones with the verses nobody remembered, and got stuck halfway and kept humming until others joined, and soon the whole hillside was humming a song whose words had rotted out of it a century ago, and it should have been comic, and partly was, and nobody cared.

Terrified joy. That was the only honest name for it. Emrys watched from the edge of the crowd, arms crossed against the evening chill, and saw both things living in every face — the joy of a prayer answered after a hundred barren years, and beneath it the animal fear of a village realizing the world was larger than it had agreed to be. He saw a woman clutch her children and also reach toward the stones. He saw men who had mocked the shrine that very morning now arguing fiercely about which of them had swept it best last spring, as though credit were due.

"They'll want to thank it," Maren said, appearing at his elbow. She looked ten years younger and entirely unhinged with happiness, and he loved her for it. "We should organize. Offerings. A proper rite. Coram will want to lead it—"

"Coram," Emrys said, "will want to *control* it."

"Well, yes. That's what Coram wants to do with weather, children, and soup." She bumped his shoulder. "You were there, Emrys. You knelt with me. Half the village saw you. The bone-carver who wouldn't pray, praying."

"One knee. Briefly. Under duress."

"Under *duress*," she agreed cheerfully. "The gods take what they can get."

He almost smiled. Then the smile died, because across the crowded hillside he caught sight of his uncle Coram Thornbury standing apart from the celebration, near the tenth socket in the earth, the empty one. The elder was not singing. He was not praying. He was looking at the stones with an expression Emrys had never seen on him before — Coram, who faced floods and failed crops and council squabbles with the same flinty competence — Coram looked *afraid*.

Not surprised. Afraid. The way a man looks at a door he had believed nailed shut, hearing, unmistakably, a key turning in the lock.

Their eyes met across the crowd. Coram's face shut like a ledger closing.

"Emrys?" Maren said. "What is it?"

"Nothing," he lied, warmly, badly. "Go on. Organize your offerings. Someone has to make sure Sorrel Pike hasn't fused to the grass."

She went, laughing, into the crowd, and Emrys stood alone at the hill's edge while the hymn-humming swelled below him, and let himself feel the thing he had been holding off since dawn.

Silence. Still silence. The dead had not returned. His whole life they had murmured around him — grief and grievance, gossip and love, the dead of Thornmere Vale chattering in the marrow of the world like sparrows in an eave — and now the eave stood empty, and the emptiness had a shape, and the shape was the shape of lungs full of air.

Something had answered Maren's prayer. Which meant something had *heard* it. Which meant something had been here the entire time, through a hundred years of unanswered prayers, unwept graves, and swept steps — listening, and not answering, and not explaining itself.

Prayers worked again. The valley would call it a blessing. The valley would sweep the steps twice as often, hold feasts, argue about rites, and slowly convince itself the gods had merely been away and were now returned, and everything was as it had always meant to be.

But Emrys was the bone-carver. He knew what things sounded like when they were alive, and he knew what things sounded like when they were pretending.

That night he walked home in the dark past the churchyard, past the graves of everyone he had ever carved for, and the silence walked with him, close as a coat.

In his workshop, Tam Hollis's half-finished femur lay on the bench where he'd left it, name half-carved, complaint half-muttered. Emrys picked it up and turned it in the lamplight — and frowned.

The bone was warm.

Not room-warm, not sun-through-the-window warm. Warm the way a hand is warm. Warm from *inside*, as though the marrow remembered blood.

And faintly, so faintly he might have invented it, at the very edge of hearing — not Tam's grumbling voice, but something else, something older, something that did not sound like a dead man at all —

— the beginning of a whisper.

*Famine,* it seemed to say. Or perhaps *family*. The word dissolved before it finished forming, like breath on cold glass.

Emrys set the bone down very gently, the way one sets down a sleeping animal, and did not sleep much himself that night.

Outside, for the first time in a century, the people of Thornmere Vale prayed as they fell asleep — hundreds of voices, hopeful and greedy and grateful, rising into the dark like sparks off a bonfire.

And beneath the shrine, beneath the hill, beneath the ninth stone and the socket cut for a tenth —

something drew breath, and listened to every word.
