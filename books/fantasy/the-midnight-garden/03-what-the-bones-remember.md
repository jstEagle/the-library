## 3. What the Bones Remember

The charm had been warm since Tuesday.

Emrys noticed it the way a man notices his own house has developed a creak — not all at once, but in the pause between one familiar sound and the next. It sat in the third drawer of his workbench, wrapped in wool, where it had sat for eleven years, and it was supposed to be cold. All heirloom bones were cold. That was rather their purpose: cold as riverbed stones, quiet as winter fields, holding nothing but the memory of whoever had loved them enough to keep them.

This one held something else.

He took it out after supper, turned the lamp up, and unwrapped the wool with the care of a man defusing something — though he couldn't have said what. The charm was a knucklebone, polished smooth by generations of thumbs, drilled through with a hole for a cord. His grandmother had worn it. Her mother before her. It had come down the Thornbury line like a bad knee and a talent for carving, and Emrys had always assumed it belonged to some ancestor whose name the family had worn soft from use.

It was warm. Not fever-warm. Hearth-warm. The kind of warm that meant someone had just been holding it.

"That's new," he told it.

The dead in the marrow said nothing, which was also new. Usually they murmured at once — the little chorus every bone carried, the way a shell carries the sea. This bone had always been quieter than most, its occupant long past caring about the living. Tonight it wasn't quiet. Tonight it was *listening*, and Emrys had the peculiar sensation, holding it under the lamp, that he was the thing being examined.

He set it down on the bench. He picked it up again almost immediately, because leaving it felt worse.

"Right," he said. "Let's find out who you are."

* * *

Bone-carving was slow work by design. Emrys had learned it from his father, who'd learned it from his mother, and the first lesson in every generation was the same: *you are not making anything, you are uncovering it.* A memorial bone already knew what it wanted to say. Your job was to shave away everything that wasn't that.

He clamped the knucklebone gently in the vise and took up his finest blade. And the moment steel touched bone, the whispering began.

Not the ordinary murmur. Ordinary was like rain on a roof — present, constant, ignorable if you tried. This came up through his fingers like water finding a crack in a wall, sudden and directional, and it did not belong to the bone's old tenant at all.

*—the snow came in the ninth month—*

Emrys pulled his hand back so fast he knocked the lamp. It rocked, righted itself, threw his shadow huge against the workshop wall.

He stood there breathing. The dead spoke to him; that was his whole strange trade and stranger birthright, and he'd made his peace with it the way you make peace with weather. But the dead he heard were *his* dead — valley folk, drowned children, old men finished with their feuds. They spoke of small things. A dog missed. A debt unpaid. The taste of blackberries the summer before the end.

They did not speak of snow in the ninth month. There hadn't been snow in the ninth month in the valley in living memory. In *anyone's* living memory.

He touched the bone again. Deliberately, this time, the way you touch a stove you've been warned about.

*—nine families, nine names, we swore it on our own—*

*—it was starving, we were starving, there is a difference only the living can afford—*

*—bind it beneath the shrine, bind it deep, bind it with blood and forget—*

*—forget—*

*—forget—*

The last word repeated until it stopped sounding like a word, and Emrys sat down hard on his stool because his knees had opinions of their own. Through the window, the shrine on its hill stood silhouetted against the early stars, the same broken-toothed ruin it had been all his life, the place where Maren had begged him just yesterday to pray. Where prayers had gone unanswered for a hundred years, and everyone agreed this was simply how the world was.

*Beneath the shrine.*

"No," he said aloud, to nobody, to the bone. "No, that's — those aren't my dead."

But the voice went on, patient as dripping water, and now that he listened he could hear how old it was. Not the settled quiet of the long-buried. This was older than burial. This was the voice of something that had never been allowed to finish dying.

*—they carved the covenant in us, child. Nine knives. We counted them. We count them still—*

Emrys looked down at his hands. Carver's hands, scarred and capable, hands that had made two hundred memorial bones and never once wondered why the valley needed a man who could hear the dead when the dead had been silent to everyone else for a century.

Why *he* could hear them.

The thought arrived the way ice arrives in a stream — not suddenly, but everywhere at once. His family. His line. One of nine names sworn over something bound beneath the shrine, and the binding written, the whisper said, *in bone-dust*, and who worked in bone-dust, generation after generation, keeping the craft alive like a pilot light under a boiler no one remembered building?

His grandmother's charm. Warm as a held hand.

"What did she do?" he whispered. "What did any of you do?"

The bone answered with warmth, and beneath the warmth, faint as a pulse under a wrist, a single new fragment:

*—the door remembers. Find the ash. Before the walls remember too—*

Then silence. True silence, the ordinary kind, and somehow that was worst of all, because it meant whatever had spoken had said what it came to say.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He made tea instead, three cups of it, and sat in his kitchen watching the coals settle, doing what carvers do when the work goes wrong: going back to the beginning.

The beginning was a famine. Everyone knew that much; it was the first story every valley child learned, along with the names of the founders. The Winter of Hollow Bells, ninety-some years ago, when the crops died twice running and the passes snowed shut and the people of the valley starved while — and here the story always got vague, the way stories do around their load-bearing parts — while *something happened*, and after it the valley was walled by the covenant and safe forever. Safe behind borders no one could see and no one crossed. The founders were heroes. Their descendants — the moot of nine families — governed still.

Every child learned the nine names. Emrys had recited them a thousand times at festivals, standing in the moot-hall square with other children, chanting: Vane, Coram-line, Ashfell, Merrick, Dune, Hollis, Brightwater, Sorrel —

Thornbury.

His own name, third in the roll, chanted with pride. He'd never once thought about why his family, of all nine, had been given the bone-craft. Why the carvers were always Thornburys, and why the craft passed down the female line, grandmother to grandson, skipping fathers like a stone across water.

Because someone had to maintain the binding. And the best way to make sure a jailer never questioned the prison was to raise him inside it, loving it, calling it home.

He laughed — one short, ugly syllable — and then pressed his palm against his mouth, because the laugh had come out wrong, had come out close to something else.

Outside, the night lay blue and ordinary over the rooftops. Somewhere a dog barked. Somewhere Maren was asleep with her daughter, both of them safe inside the covenant, inside the prison, inside the lie, and tomorrow she would come to his workshop with bread and gossip and ask him, again, kindly, to pray at the shrine — the shrine under which, if the bone spoke true, something starved and patient had been pinned for ninety years by his own blood.

He thought of the drowned child's bone he'd carved last week. Little Tam, four years old, blue at the millrace. Emrys had spent three evenings uncovering what the bone wanted to say — *mama, the wheel, I'm sorry about the wheel* — and had delivered it to Tam's mother, and watched her hold it and finally weep, and had called that his life's work. Bearing witness for the dead of the valley.

And all the while, beneath the town, beneath the shrine, beneath everything: the dead who were never given graves. Never given carvings. Never given names.

*We count them still,* the bone had said. Nine knives. Counted for ninety years.

Emrys put his head in his hands.

* * *

Dawn found him back at the workbench, which surprised him; he'd expected to spend the night pacing, or fleeing, or doing any of the dramatic things a man does when his world turns out to be built on a crime. Instead he'd slept two hours in the chair and woken with a carver's itch in his fingers, because apparently his body had decided that when existence becomes unbearable, the answer is more whittling.

So he worked. He took the knucklebone out of the vise and, instead of carving it, did the opposite of carving: he laid down his blades and simply held it, palms cupped, the way you'd hold water or a wounded bird. Listening without tools. His grandmother's way, she'd called it — though now even that phrase rang differently. *Her* way. Of course it was her way. She'd probably been listening to this thing her whole life.

The warmth pulsed against his skin like a second heartbeat.

"Talk to me," he said. "Properly. Start at the beginning."

And the bone, as if it had only been waiting for permission, gave him the beginning.

Not words this time. Worse — pictures. They rose behind his eyes unbidden, grainy and grey as old woodcuts, and Emrys gripped the edges of the bench and let them come because he was, whatever else he was, a man whose whole trade was bearing witness.

Snow. A valley white and silent, deeper in winter than any winter he'd known. A village — his village, but smaller, meaner, rooflines sagging under drifts. Empty gardens. Empty pens. Children with faces like paper lanterns. A bell tolling for burials so often it stopped meaning anything — hollow bells, hollow bells, the winter named for the sound of a bell rung over a grave with nothing left inside the coffin to bury.

Hunger walking upright, wearing his neighbors' faces.

And beneath it all, beneath the frozen fields, a presence. Vast and slow and *starving*, yes — but not cruel. That was the part that broke something loose in his chest. The vision showed him a god like a great grey root system under the earth, the kind of thing that had once made barley grow and rivers stay kind, and it was starving too, starving alongside them, giving what it had left in a trickle of mercy that kept perhaps one field in ten alive. It was not eating the valley.

The valley's ancestors had decided it should be eaten anyway.

Nine figures in a snowlit circle above the shrine-hill, knives drawn — and the bone-dust rising around them like breath, like incense, and the god below not fighting, not cursing them. Only asking. The vision didn't carry the words, but Emrys felt the shape of them the way you feel thunder through a floor: *why. why. we fed you your whole lives, why.*

And then binding. Cold and dark and pressure, and the god folding itself smaller and smaller around its own hunger, and the last thing it saw — the last thing it was *shown*, deliberately, cruelly, so it would know what it lost — was spring. Green coming up through the thaw. Barley heads nodding in a wind it would never feel again.

The vision snapped off.

Emrys discovered he was on the floor. He didn't remember sitting down. His tea, abandoned overnight, had gone the colour of bog oak, and morning light lay across the workbench like a hand he couldn bring himself to shrug off.

"They murdered it," he said to the empty room. His voice sounded borrowed. "To survive one winter, they murdered a god, and then they built a paradise on top of the grave and called it sanctuary."

The covenant. The border stones. The reason no one left, no one entered, no prayers were answered, no magic crossed — he'd grown up believing the covenant protected the valley *from* the world. It protected the world's gods from finding their imprisoned kin. Every prayer unanswered for a century hadn't been silence.

It had been gagging.

And the charm in his hands — his grandmother's charm, warm as a held hand — pulsed once, gently, like a nudge.

*Find the ash,* it had said. *Before the walls remember too.*

"The walls remembering," Emrys muttered, getting up on legs that mostly cooperated, "sounds like the sort of thing that happens to other valleys."

Even as he said it, he heard the lie in it. He'd been a carver too long not to recognize the signs of something working itself loose. The bone bleeding warmth. The whispers surfacing after decades of quiet. Whatever seal the founders had set was thinning — and things that thinned, broke.

He wrapped the charm back in its wool, then stopped, unwrapped it, and threaded it onto a cord instead. Around his neck. Against his sternum, where he could feel it with every breath. If his family had made this mess, then his family's craft would be the thing that unmade it — or at least the thing that refused, this time, to look away.

He was reaching for his coat, intending — he wasn't sure what. Walk. Think. Maybe go stand at the shrine and say something unspeakable at the ground. When three sharp knocks hit his door, and through it came Maren's voice, breathless and bright and entirely wrong for the hour:

"Emrys! Emrys, open the door — you'll want to see this — the shrine, Emrys, *someone answered my prayer!*"

The charm against his chest went very cold, then very hot, and the dead in the marrow whispered, clear as a bell over an open grave:

*It's waking.*
