## 4. The Covenant's Edge

Emrys had walked to the border stones every year on the anniversary of his father's death, and in all his thirty-two years the walk had never once been interesting.

That was rather the point of it. The border was where interest went to die. You stood among the grey standing stones at the valley's lip, you looked out at the wall — not a wall you could see, but a wall you could *feel*, a flatness in the air like the moment before a headache — and you turned around and walked home. His father had done it. His grandfather had done it. The Thornburys had been walking to the edge of the world and finding nothing there for six generations, and Emrys had always assumed he would die doing precisely the same thing, probably while complaining about his boots.

But this morning, a wren crossed where nothing should be able to cross.

He saw it happen. He saw the little bird hop along the top of the easternmost stone, cock its head at him as if to say *well, don't just stand there*, and then hop off the far side — into the air that was not supposed to hold anything, past the boundary that had held for two hundred years, and away over the dead brown hills beyond without so much as a ripple.

Emrys stood very still for a long time.

"Right," he said finally, to no one. "That's fine. That's perfectly normal."

The wren did not come back to reassure him.

He should have gone home. That was the sensible thing. There were orders waiting on his bench — old Tam Whitlock wanted a memorial piece for his wife, something in ash wood rather than bone because Tam had never quite gotten comfortable with the bone carvings despite forty years of neighborliness, and Emrys respected that; you couldn't carve grief for someone who flinched at your material. He had a fire to lay. He had bread rising under a cloth on the sill.

Instead he found himself stepping up onto the stone itself, boots scraping on lichen, and leaning toward the place where the world went flat.

It flickered.

There was no better word for it. The invisible wall — the thing he had felt pressing against his forehead every year of his life, patient and absolute as the underside of a lid — stuttered. For half a heartbeat the air beyond looked *thin*, like cloth worn through at the elbow, and Emrys saw, clearly, impossibly, the hills rolling away northward, real hills with wind moving through their grass, and then the flatness closed again and everything was the valley and only the valley, as it had always been.

Except that now Emrys knew what the hills looked like. And they had looked *hungry*. Brown and scrubby and vast, running to a horizon the valley didn't have room for.

His hands were shaking. He noticed this with mild academic interest, the way he noticed when a carving wasn't going well. Then he climbed down from the stone, sat on it instead, ate his bread, and decided that since he had already ruined one perfectly good day of denial, he might as well ruin the whole week.

* * *

Ilsa Vane had been waiting outside the wall for nine days, and she had opinions about it.

Her map called this place Vaelhollow, which was a lie, because the map also showed a river here and a road here and a town of four thousand souls here, and what Ilsa actually saw was a bowl of green fields cupped between hills like an egg in a fist, with maybe three hundred rooftops and not a single honest road in or out. Maps lied. That was the first lesson of door-seeking. The second lesson was that the lies were usually load-bearing: somewhere underneath this map's polite fiction of a river and a road, there was a Door, because doors were the only things worth lying about.

She sat on a boulder with her coat around her shoulders and her boots off, drying her stockings by a fire she'd built small enough to hide, and she watched the valley breathe.

That was the strange part. It *breathed*. She'd watched a lot of sealed places in her years on the road — warded keeps, god-tombs, one memorable monastery that turned out to be a very committed fraud — and they all had a stillness to them, a held-breath quality. This place moved. Smoke curled from chimneys and bent in a wind that stopped exactly at the tree line. Birds sang inside it. She'd seen children chase a dog down a lane yesterday, and the dog had bounded joyfully at the border and bounced off it like a moth off glass, and then trotted back, entirely unbothered, and Ilsa had laughed so hard she'd nearly given away her position.

Nine days. Her provisions said five more, her patience said three, and the debt-mark on her wrist said *as long as it takes*, in the way debt-marks do — not in words, but in that low persistent hum beneath her pulse that meant somewhere a god remembered she owed Him.

Then, this morning, the wall flickered, and a bird came out.

And shortly after that, a man came out too — well, came *up to* it, climbed the border stone, stared at the air like it owed him money, and sat down to eat bread.

Ilsa pulled her stockings back on. This was, she reflected, the eternal comedy of her profession: years of preparation, careful approach, coded inquiries across three countries, and in the end the door-seeker simply waited until somebody from the inside got curious and wandered out. Doors wanted finders. It was practically their whole personality.

She kicked dirt over her fire, shouldered her pack, and strolled up the hill as casually as a woman could who was approaching an invisible wall surrounding a hidden valley that appeared on no honest map.

The man on the stone watched her come. He had a long face built for worry, hands that looked like they'd been carved by someone who cared very much about hands, and the particular stillness of a person who talks to himself and has mostly made peace with it. When she stopped ten paces from the border — close enough to talk, far enough to bounce off, she'd learned that lesson the hard way in the monastery fraud — he looked her up and down with an expression she recognized from markets in border towns everywhere: the look of a man deciding whether she was trouble, and reluctantly concluding yes.

"You're outside," he said.

"Astounding," Ilsa said. "They told me the people in here were sheltered, but—"

"The bird crossed." He ignored her entirely, which took some doing, because Ilsa had never once been successfully ignored. "This morning. A wren. It went through the wall like the wall wasn't there."

"It wasn't, this morning. Not entirely." She spread her arms. "Which is why I'm talking to you instead of shouting at a hillside for another week."

The man's eyes narrowed. Up close she could see the tiredness in him, deep-set, lived-in, the tiredness of someone who does work nobody thanks him for. She filed that away. Tired people either helped you or hindered you, but they almost never surprised you.

"Who are you?" he asked.

"Ilsa Vane." She gave a small bow, road-style, ironic but sincere underneath, because sincerity was a tool too and she kept hers sharp. "Door-seeker. Third circle, licensed by nobody, answerable to less than nobody."

"A what?"

"A door-seeker. I find doors. Thresholds. Ways in and ways out of places like—" she gestured at the entire valley, the entire green impossible egg of it, "—this. Your wall is thinning. Has been for weeks, I'd guess. Whatever holds it is running out, and when it runs out completely, everything inside goes wherever unmade things go, which is nowhere, which is why I'm here early."

He was quiet for a moment. A cloud crossed the sun and she noticed he didn't watch the cloud; he watched the shadow of it move over his valley, the way a shepherd watches weather, with ownership.

"I'm Emrys Thornbury," he said at last. "And you should know that everything you've just said sounds insane."

"Oh, absolutely."

"There is no door here. There's a covenant. It's been in place since the founding. Nobody leaves, nothing comes in, and it has held for two hundred years."

"And yet." She tapped the air in front of her, gently, where the wall would be if walls were visible. Her knuckle met resistance — soft resistance today, though, like pressing a bruise. Even a month ago, she knew, it would have been iron. "Emrys Thornbury. That's a founder's name, isn't it? Nine families. I've read the fragments. Half of them are burned, the other half contradict each other, which in my trade means we're close to something true."

Something moved behind his face. She watched it happen — the flicker of a man who has spent his whole life being the keeper of other people's secrets suddenly hearing one of his own spoken aloud by a stranger.

"How do you know about the founders?"

"Because I'm hunting what they built. Or what they buried — accounts differ." Ilsa crouched, slowly, deliberately, and set her pack on the ground between them, open. An offering. Inside: dried meat, a coil of rope, a battered brass instrument like a compass that had been raised by wolves, and a strip of leather marked with a brand that glowed faintly, even in daylight, even now, even after everything. "I'll tell you the truth, Emrys Thornbury, because liars don't last long around doors and I'd like to last long. Something answered a prayer in your valley recently. I felt it from sixty miles off, like a bell rung underwater. Things are waking up all over the world — old things, bound things, things that were put away when I was a child. And every one of them wakes next to a threshold. Yours is called the Door of Ash. I intend to find it before your wall finishes dying, because when it does, you will need a way *out*, and I am the best way-finder in a country that no longer has any ways."

Emrys looked at the glowing brand on the leather. "What is that?"

"My debt." She closed the flap, quicker than she meant to. "A story for a story. You first. What happened at your shrine?"

* * *

Emrys hadn't told anyone about the silence.

Three days ago Maren's prayer had been answered — old Brannoc's withered arm made new, whole, strong enough that he'd carried his granddaughter on the healed shoulder through the village square while half the valley wept and the other half checked their own limbs for miracles. Joy, everyone called it. Terror wearing joy's clothes, Emrys thought, but terror wasn't a thing you said out loud in a valley that size.

And through all of it, the dead had gone quiet.

All his life they had murmured. Through the marrow of every bone he carved, through the long nights at his bench, a low tide of voices — complaints, comforts, fragments of songs, the endless small gossip of the departed. He had hated it as a boy, made peace with it as a man, and never once, not for a single hour, known true silence. Until the miracle. Until something enormous had drawn a breath beneath his feet, and every voice he'd ever heard had hushed at once, the way a forest hushes before weather.

He had slept that night without the murmur for the first time since childhood, and woken weeping, and could not have explained to anyone why.

Now a stranger stood outside the fabric of his whole life, poking holes in it, talking about doors and debts and dying walls, and the worst part — the part he'd never admit — was how much of it rang against things he already knew. The bones had been whispering differently lately. Fragments. *Famine.* *Bargain.* Words that didn't belong to any of his usual dead.

"There was a healing," he said carefully. "At the old shrine. A man's arm."

"Whose shrine?"

"The founders'. It's ruins now. Nobody prays there. Prayers haven't worked in a century."

"And then a prayer worked." Ilsa smiled, and it wasn't a happy smile; it was the smile of a woman watching a lock begin to turn. "Do you know what that means, bone-carver? It means whatever answers prayers in this country just stuck its hand through a hole in your wall. And holes in walls are doors. And doors are my business." She rose, brushing off her knees. "Your wall is coming down whether you like it or not. The question your valley needs to ask itself — soon, and out loud — is what comes in when it goes."

"And you want me to help you find your Door of Ash." He said it flatly. "Why would I trust you?"

"You shouldn't. Trust is for family and fools, and I'm neither." She grinned, and for one unguarded moment she looked young, and tired, and a long way from anywhere. "But I'll tell you what you can do. You can verify. Go to your moot-hall, Thornbury. Read what your founders wrote down the winter they built this place. If I'm wrong, you'll sleep fine and I'll go away. If I'm right—" she shrugged, and the motion took in the flickering air, the hungry hills, the wren that had crossed where nothing should cross, "—then you'll want to find me again fast. I'll be the woman camped outside your wall, arguing with a god."

She turned and walked back down the hill toward her cold camp, and Emrys stood on the border stone a long while, holding the crust of his forgotten bread, listening to the silence where the dead should have been.

Behind him, faint as a breath, the marrow-voice of some old dead man whispered a single word he'd never heard before:

*Ash.*

Emrys went home the long way, past the moot-hall, and stood looking at its locked archive window until the light failed.

He did not go in. Not yet.

But he memorized the lock.
