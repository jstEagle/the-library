## 5. The Founders' Ledger

The moot-hall of Thornbury Vale had two locks on its archive door, and Emrys had spent three days learning that it only needed none.

The first lock was iron, old and honest, and yielded to a copy of the key he'd carved from memory when he was eleven years old — because his uncle Coram had once made the mistake of asking him to fix the archive's broken drawer, and boys who carve bones for a living do not forget the shape of things they've held. The second lock was Coram himself, who kept the archive's only other key on a cord around his neck and slept like a man guarding a secret he'd never been told the contents of.

But Coram was at the shrine tonight. They all were. Since Maren's prayer had cracked open the sky over the ruined stones three weeks ago, half the valley prayed there nightly, kneeling in the frost, begging the newly attentive dark for rain, for healthy calves, for their children to stop coughing through winter. And the other half stayed home and bolted their doors, because answered prayers were either a blessing or a breach in a wall, and no one could agree which, and disagreement made people pray harder either way.

Emrys did neither. Emrys picked up his lantern, his father's old crowbar, and the bone charm that had started whispering to him like a guilty conscience, and he went to commit burglary against his own inheritance.

"You understand," he muttered to the charm as he crossed the square, "that this is entirely your fault."

The charm — a knucklebone of some beast no one had ever named, polished smooth by six generations of Thornbury thumbs — sat warm in his pocket. It had been warm for days now, warm as a held hand, and through its marrow came fragments: *famine*, and *bargain*, and *beneath*, and once, terribly clearly, the sound of nine voices speaking one word in unison, though the word refused to resolve into anything his waking mind could hold.

The moot-hall stood black against the stars, all timber and steep roof, built by men who'd wanted their decisions to look permanent. Emrys let himself in through the council door — the iron lock turning sweet as butter — and stood a moment in the dark hall where the valley had argued about mill-rates and marriage banns and, lately, whether the god answering prayers was a mercy or an infestation.

His boots echoed. He winced.

"Quiet," he told himself. "You're a bone-carver. You've crept past sleeping houses your whole life delivering charms to the dying." This was true. There was something almost restful about sneaking through a building you weren't supposed to be in. It was the closest thing to honest work a thief ever did.

* * *

The archive was a narrow room behind the council chamber, windowless, smelling of dust and tallow and the particular mustiness of paper that has been deliberately forgotten. Shelves ran floor to ceiling. Bundles of deeds. Tally-books going back two hundred years. A tax ledger with a dead mouse flattened inside it like a bookmark, which Emrys chose to interpret as a sign of the archive's general standards rather than an omen.

He set the lantern down and began at the oldest shelf, working by feel, reading spines by touch the way he read grain in bone.

The founders' records should have been here. Every child in the valley learned the names before they learned letters: Nine Who Stayed, Nine Who Fed Us, the nine families who had held the vale together through the Famine Winter and raised the border stones and written the covenant. Their portraits hung in this very hall — stern faces, good wool coats, the particular smugness of people painted after surviving something. Coram liked to point at them during moots when he needed authority. *Your great-great-grandmother didn't complain,* he would say, incorrectly, about someone else's great-great-grandmother.

Emrys found deeds. He found harvest tallies. He found, on the bottom shelf, wedged behind a bundle of fence disputes, a slim book bound in grey leather gone stiff as bark, unmarked except for a symbol pressed into the cover: a circle with nine points, like a wheel with too many spokes.

His hands were steady. That surprised him. He'd expected them to shake.

The clasp was bone-dust and tallow, fused hard. He worked his smallest carving knife under it — a desecration, using a tool meant for memorial work on a lock, but then, everything tonight was a desecration, and better his knife than his conscience — and the cover fell open with a sigh, as if the book had been holding its breath since before his grandfather's grandfather was born.

The first pages were ordinary. Accounts of seed stores. A list of livestock lost to the cold, written in a hand that deteriorated month by month until the numbers stopped being numbers and became scratches. Emrys knew this handwriting's cousins; he saw it in ledgers across the valley. It was the handwriting of a person writing while starving, keeping accounts because accounts were the last thing that still obeyed rules.

*Third month of snow. Forty-one souls in the vale. Seed gone. The low pasture is silent — nothing left to graze it.*

*Fifth month. Twenty-nine souls. We ate the shrine offerings. Forgive us. There was nothing else and we were so hungry we could not be sorry.*

Emrys sat down slowly on the floor, back against the shelf, lantern between his knees.

*Seventh month. Nineteen souls. Halvard says the thing beneath the shrine has woken with our hunger. Says it has always been here, older than our walls, sleeping in the root of the valley. Says it will feed us if we ask properly. I said there is no such thing. Then I helped dig.*

Then, several pages on, the handwriting changed. New hand, steadier, terrible in its composure:

*Ninth month. Eleven souls remain. The bargain is struck. What sleeps below will keep the vale fed through the years of hunger, and after, and always, and in return we give it what it asks: to be shut away from its own sky. It is tired, Halvard says. It has been hunted in every country we came from. It wants only to sleep somewhere it cannot be reached. We will build it a prison it will mistake for a sanctuary, and it will never again hear a prayer it must answer, nor see a war, nor feel a hunter's net. We are not murderers. We are wardens of its rest.*

*It believed us. That is the worst of it. It was so grateful.*

Emrys discovered he had stopped breathing and made himself start again, one long careful pull of air, the way he did when a dying client gripped his wrist too hard.

*We are not murderers,* he read again. The ink had been pressed hard enough there to emboss the next page. Someone had needed that sentence to be true so badly they'd driven it into the paper like a nail.

He turned pages with fingers that felt borrowed. The ritual came next — and it was written, as the whispers had promised, in bone-dust, ground fine and mixed with tallow so the characters stood slightly proud of the page and caught the lantern light like frost. Nine lines of covenant-law. He could read perhaps a third of it: words about *root* and *roof* and *silence*, about a wall drawn around a valley like a circle drawn around a name. At the foot of the ritual, nine signatures.

He read them aloud, barely a breath, because some things needed to be spoken to be believed:

"Halvard Vane. Sorrel Ashdown. Brigid Corvane. Tammas Larkspur. Odile Fenwick. Joss Merrow. Annis Hollow. Bertram Gale." His voice stopped working. He looked at the ninth name for a long time.

"Thornbury."

Not just any Thornbury. *Edric Thornbury.* His own line. The name carved above his family's plot in the churchyard. The name on the workshop lintel, worn nearly smooth by weather, that he passed under every morning of his life.

Edric Thornbury had signed a prison into being and called it mercy, and every generation since had inherited the signature without knowing they'd inherited the debt.

"Well," Emrys said to the empty archive, because humor was the only tool he had left that wasn't trembling, "that explains the family temperament."

The charm in his pocket pulsed warm, almost scolding.

"Yes, yes. Not funny. Nothing is funny." He dragged a hand down his face. "Though I'll note, for the record, that Uncle Coram lectures me yearly about honoring our ancestors' sacrifice, and apparently the sacrifice was committing a deicide and then filing it away behind the fence disputes."

But the laugh wouldn't come, and underneath the joke the truth sat like a stone in his chest: *his whole life*. Every charm he'd carved for the dying, every murmur he'd heard through the marrow, every funeral where he alone had heard the dead say goodbye — all of it had happened inside a lie shaped like a sanctuary. The dead of the vale spoke to him because they were kept. Kept like Edric's sheep. Kept like the god itself, sleeping beneath the shrine, grateful, believing itself safe.

And the famine — the whispers had shown him famine — and the founders had been starving too. Nineteen souls. Eleven. Emrys turned back to the early pages, the tally of the eaten seed and the silent pasture, and forced himself to sit with it, because he owed the dead that much honesty: they had not been monsters. They had been starving people offered a way out by something patient and kind, and they had taken it, and then — because starving people learn that kindness is a debt that compounds — they had betrayed it.

*It believed us. That is the worst of it.*

"No," Emrys murmured. "The worst of it is that it worked. Two hundred years of full bellies and safe births, all of it standing on that lie, and everyone happy, and me the only one who ever heard the walls humming."

He copied the nine signatures onto a scrap of paper, because names were evidence and evidence needed carrying, though he couldn't have said yet what he'd charge anyone with or in what court. Then he hesitated over the ledger itself. Take it? Coram would know within a day. Leave it, and it might vanish — archives had a way of losing precisely the pages that mattered, especially once elders started demanding renewed sacrifices at moots, especially once a man like Coram decided the past was best left buried under the shrine along with everything else.

He took it. Slid it inside his coat, where it lay against his ribs like a second, colder heart.

* * *

He was relocking the council door when the dead spoke.

They came through every surface at once — through the moot-hall's ancient timbers, through the flagstones, through the bone-handled knife in his pocket — not the usual murmurs, not the soft overlapping gossip of the recently departed, but a single vast attention, like the moment before a choir inhales. The lantern flame leaned sideways though there was no draft.

*You found it,* the bones whispered, in a hundred voices that were also somehow one voice, and Emrys recognized the tone. It was the tone of clients who had finally, near the end, decided to tell the truth.

"You knew," he said quietly. "All of you knew. All this time, every funeral, every charm — you knew what this valley is."

The silence stretched. Then, gently, the way one speaks to a child holding something breakable:

*We knew what was done. We did not know it would hold this long. Little carver — it is beginning to loosen. Can you not feel it? The wall is thin as eggshell now. And the one beneath the shrine is starting to dream.*

"Dream of what?"

The answer came not in words but in an image pressed briefly against the inside of his skull, delicate as a thumbprint: a door. Grey, ancient, standing alone in a country with no color, and around its edges, seeping out like smoke under a closed lid, the smell of ash.

Then the presence withdrew, leaving the ordinary night sounds — wind in the eaves, a dog barking two streets over, his own heartbeat going far too fast.

Emrys stood on the moot-hall steps with a stolen ledger against his ribs and looked east, toward the border stones he could not see, beyond which Ilsa Vane was camped somewhere in the dark. Vane. Halvard Vane's blood, come knocking at the wall her ancestor had built — and she'd known, he realized, or guessed enough to travel all this way hunting a Door of Ash.

He thought about waking her. He thought about burning the ledger. He thought, most of all, about the shrine at the center of the vale, where hundreds of his neighbors knelt nightly praying into the dark — praying, without knowing it, straight down into the ear of a chained god that had just begun, after two centuries of silence, to answer.

"Well," Emrys said, and this time the laugh came, small and bleak and entirely private. "I expect I'll want more than one night's sleep before I decide which of those thoughts to act on."

He pulled his coat tight around the ledger and walked home through the sleeping streets, past the workshop where, faintly, through the walls, the bones in their drawers had begun — very softly, very patiently, like an audience settling before the play — to sing.
