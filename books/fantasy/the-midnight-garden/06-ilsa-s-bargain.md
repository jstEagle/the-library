## 6. Ilsa's Bargain

The border of Thornbury Vale tasted like a held breath.

Ilsa Vane had crossed nine thresholds in her life — some marked by standing stones, some by rivers that ran the wrong way, one by a tollgate where a woman with too many fingers had weighed her name on brass scales and found it wanting. She knew what thresholds felt like from the outside: a pressure against the sternum, a whisper of *not yet*, a door-shaped absence in the world. But she had never felt one thinning before. This one shivered like ice over a spring creek, and when she pressed her palm toward it, the resistance gave the way old cheese cloth gives — reluctant, then suddenly not there at all.

She stepped through into a valley so green it looked embarrassed about it.

"Well," she said to no one. "That's obscene."

Outside the covenant, the world had spent three seasons learning what it meant for gods to answer again. Ilsa had watched a fishing village pray its harbor full of fish and then drown in pilgrims. She had watched a town burn its own granary because a voice in the smoke told them hunger was holy now. The old gods were awake, and they loved their faithful the way a drunkard loves his friends: loudly, generously, and without any regard for what happens next.

This valley had none of it. No answered prayers. No fanaticism. Just barley, sheep, whitewashed cottages, and a silence so complete it rang.

*Because something is holding the door shut,* she thought. *And whatever's doing the holding is starting to lose its grip.*

She kept to the hedgerows. Her boots were good, her coat was better, and her face — narrow, freckled, unremarkable except for the eyes, which her mother had said were the color of pond water and just as trustworthy — could pass for local if she didn't open her mouth. She'd learned the accent from a peddler who'd come out of this country years ago and talked about it the way men talk about an ex-wife: fondly, and with a flinch.

The shrine was easy enough to find. Ruined shrines have a look about them, even here — a hush, birds refusing to land, grass growing in a circle that's slightly too perfect. What was harder to find, and what she actually needed, was the bone-carver.

Everyone on the Road of Ash knew the story, or a version of it. The valley walled off by nine founders. A god that stopped answering. And in every version, one detail recurred like a burr in wool: *the dead still speak in Thornbury Vale. To one man. A carver.*

Gods answer prayers. The dead don't answer anything. Which meant the covenant wasn't keeping magic *out*. It was keeping something *in*.

Ilsa intended to find out what.

* * *

She found him at dusk, in a workshop at the edge of the churchyard, bent over a sliver of bone with a knife finer than anything she'd ever seen outside a jeweler's stall. He was younger than she expected — mid-twenties, ink-stained fingers, hair that suggested he owned a comb out of politeness rather than habit. A lamp threw his shadow long across the floorboards, and he was talking. Low. Continuous. To nobody.

"—and if you're going to keep humming, Wenna, I'll carve your memorial as a turnip, I swear it—"

"Turnip," said Ilsa from the doorway. "Bold choice."

Emrys Thornbury did not scream, which she noted with approval. He went very still instead, the way people do when they've grown up around things that reward sudden movement badly. Then he turned, and she watched him do the arithmetic: stranger, woman, travel-worn, standing inside a wall that should have stopped her.

"You came through the border," he said.

"The border came down long enough for me to come through. There's a difference." She stepped inside, uninvited, and hung her dripping coat on his peg — a small act of trespass that always told her more about a host than any question. "You're the carver."

"I'm a carpenter's nephew who does fiddly work."

"Mm. And the fiddly work talks back?"

His jaw tightened. Outside, the first rain began, soft as an apology. Somewhere in the room — and this was the part that made the hair rise on Ilsa's arms — something *answered*. Not words. A sound like a sigh moving through a bundle of reeds, coming from the shelf where rows of small carved bones lay in labeled niches, each one tagged with a name and a date.

"That's Wenna," Emrys said flatly. "She's been dead eleven years and rude for ten of them. Who are you?"

"Ilsa Vane. Door-seeker." She let the word sit. His eyes flickered — he knew it, or half-knew it, the way valley folk know the shape of things they've been taught not to look at. "I find thresholds. Dying countries shed them. Doors that used to lead somewhere stop leading anywhere, unless someone walks them open. I walk them open."

"And you think my valley has one."

"I think your valley *is* one." She pulled the stool out from under his workbench and sat, making herself at home in his unease. "Your border flickered last week. A bird crossed where nothing should cross. Your shrine — the ruined one on the south slope — answered a prayer two days ago. First miracle this country has seen in a hundred years, and everyone's calling it joy." She leaned forward. "You called it something else. I can see it all over your face. You called it *breathing*."

For a long moment the only sound was the rain and Wenna's faint, indignant humming, which had resumed as though determined to make a point about turnips.

"I heard the dead go quiet," Emrys said finally. "All of them. All at once. Like a room of people turning to look at the same door." He rubbed his thumb along the knife handle, a habit, a comfort. "You want me to say that frightened me. Fine. It frightened me."

"Good. Frightened is honest. Happy is what gets people burned." She reached into her coat — slowly, showing him her empty palm first, a courtesy she'd learned in towns where fast hands got cut off — and drew out a strip of oiled leather. Unrolled, it held a single bone charm, old and yellowed, carved with spirals worn nearly smooth. "Do you know this work?"

He went white.

Not pale — *white*, the way paper is white, the way bone is white. He stared at the charm the way a man stares at a debt collector.

"Where did you get that?"

"My sister wore it. Brigid. She was seven." Ilsa kept her voice level, which cost her; she could feel the old seam in her chest pull tight, the one that never fully healed. "We lived in a dying country too, before yours — Kellharrow, maybe you've heard of it. No? Nobody has. That's rather the point. When the famine came to Kellharrow, our gods were already gone. Dead, sleeping, fled — no one agreed. People prayed into a silence so thick you could chew it. My mother sold everything we had to buy Brigid a fever-charm from a hedge-priest who swore the old ways still worked." She smiled, and knew it was a bad smile. "It worked. On the third night, when the fever peaked and the doctor had gone home to write her death-list, Brigid sat up. Asked for soup. Lived to be eight months older than she should have been."

Emrys's voice was careful. "Eight months?"

"The fever came back. Whatever answered her prayer — it didn't cure her. It *bought* her. Time, borrowed against something." Ilsa turned the charm over in her palm. "In those eight months, she was the happiest child in three parishes. And in the last hour, she looked at me — clear-eyed, no fever, older than seven somehow — and she said, *'Ilse, he says thank you, and he says someone owes him, and he says it isn't us.'*" She looked up. "Then she slept, and didn't wake, and the charm started bleeding warmth. It hasn't cooled since. Twenty years, and it hasn't cooled."

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though the window was shut.

"A god answered her," Emrys said quietly. "A god that answers."

"One god. *The* god, I'd wager. The one your founders didn't manage to kill." She stood, and set the charm gently on his workbench between them, like a card laid down. "Here's my bargain, carver, and I'll make it plain because I've got no talent for scheming and less patience for it. I know where the Door of Ash lies. I've spent six years and two nearly-fatal mistakes finding it. But doors like this one don't open for door-seekers, or priests, or kings. Yours opens for blood — founder's blood, the blood that made the lock. Nine families. Nine names."

"And you've come to check mine against a list."

"I came to check yours against a list," she agreed. "Thornbury. Fourth name from the top, if the peddler's memory holds, and his memory held everything else." She let that land, watching him absorb it — the ledger he hadn't read yet, the inheritance he hadn't opened, the crime sitting under his barley fields like a body under floorboards. "I'm not here to threaten you. I'm here because I owe a debt to the thing beneath your shrine, and I pay my debts. It bought my sister eight months of soup and laughter and one impossible conversation. Everything I have is interest on that loan."

Wenna hummed. Somewhere in the shelf, another voice joined it — lower, older, a bass note through marrow — and both Emrys and Ilsa turned to look.

"They do that?" Ilsa asked.

"When they agree about something." He looked shaken. "They almost never agree about anything."

"So." She stuck out her hand, rough-palmed, nails bitten, a door-seeker's hand. "Partnership. You give me the blood and the nerve. I give you the road and the Door. We find out what your founders buried, and we decide — together, mind — whether it deserves to stay buried."

Emrys didn't take her hand. Instead he picked up the charm, turned it once in the lamplight, and held it near the shelf of the dead. The murmuring swelled — dozens of voices now, maybe hundreds, a congregation — and among them, impossibly, briefly, a child's voice, saying something that made him close his eyes.

"What did she say?" Ilsa asked. Her heart was doing something undignified.

"She said—" He stopped. Started again. "She said, *the tall one keeps her promises, tell her.*"

Ilsa laughed. It came out wrong, half-sobbed, and she didn't care. Twenty years. Twenty years of carrying a seven-year-old's last words like a stone in the boot, and some dead stranger in a shut-away valley had just handed her a receipt.

"Fine," Emrys said. He took her hand after all, gripping hard. "Partnership. But Vane — if this ends with me opening something that eats my neighbors, I will carve your name on the most insulting memorial bone in existence, and I know how to make them read."

"Noted." She grinned, and for the first time in months it felt like the real one. "Now pour me whatever passes for tea in this country, and start talking. Because tomorrow morning, the moot convenes — nine families, one panic — and if your uncle Coram is half the man the road-gossip says he is, we've got until then to learn what your family did."

Emrys was already reaching for the kettle. But at the word *Coram*, his hand paused, and Ilsa saw the fear come back into his face — not fear of gods or ghosts, but the smaller, sharper kind. Family fear.

"He knows more than the ledger shows," Emrys said quietly. "He's always known. And tonight of all nights, I think he's waiting up for me."

* * *

Outside the workshop, the rain fell harder, and beneath the shrine hill — beneath the barley, the bones, the hundred years of careful silence — something vast rolled over in its sleep, and dreamed, for the first time in a century, of doors.
