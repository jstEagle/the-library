## 7. The Moot of Nine

The summons came at dusk, carried by a boy who would not meet Emrys's eyes.

"Elder Thornbury's compliments," the boy recited, staring hard at a point somewhere past Emrys's left ear. "The moot convenes tonight. All nine lines are bidden. You're to attend."

"Am I," Emrys said. It was not a question, but he made it one anyway, because there was no version of this in which his attendance was anything but an accusation wearing the clothes of courtesy.

The boy shifted his weight. "He said you'd say that. He said—" and here the boy's voice dropped, embarrassed by the words even as he delivered them, "—he said blood sits at the table whether it wants to or not."

Emrys looked past him to the road, where the evening mist was coming up off the river in slow grey ribbons. Three days since he had stood before the assembled valley and named their founders murderers. Three days since the dead had begun screaming through every bone in his workshop, and had not entirely stopped — though they had gentled, of late, into something worse than screaming: a low, continuous murmur, like wind through a field of dry stalks, like ten thousand voices all saying *remember* at slightly different speeds.

"I'll come," he said.

The boy fled as if released from a snare.

---

Maren was waiting for him at the fork in the road, wrapped in her good shawl, her face pale in the last light. She fell into step beside him without asking, and he did not tell her she shouldn't have come, because he knew why she had. Her daughter Sefa was at home with Maren's sister — but not far from home, and not far enough from the mood that had settled over the valley like frost.

"They've been killing the livestock since dawn," Maren said quietly. "I heard it from the tannery. First the old ewes, then the oxen that went lame in spring. Blood on the shrine stones until the ground drank its fill and still they poured." Her hands worked at each other inside the shawl. "And now the moot."

"Now the moot."

"You'll speak again?" She glanced up at him. "Even after what happened at the well?"

What had happened at the well: half the valley turning away from him as he passed, the other half watching him the way one watches a crack in a dam. Old Hesta Rook spitting at his feet. But also — and this was the part he could not stop turning over — young Tam Ellery standing beside him in silence, and Widow Crane taking his hand briefly, fiercely, before vanishing into her house. The valley had split along a seam no one had known was there, and now everyone could feel the two halves grinding against each other whenever the community gathered, which was always, because the valley was small and grief made people cling.

"I don't know what I'll do," Emrys said honestly. "But I know what they'll ask. And I know what answering them will cost."

Ahead, the moot-hall rose against the darkening sky, its stone roof furred with moss, its single window lit gold. Nine benches ringed the hearth inside, one for each founder's line, and above the hearth hung the Nine Chairs — carved from the same oak as everything else that mattered in this valley, worn smooth by two hundred years of anxious hands.

Emrys had sat in the Thornbury chair exactly once before, when he was nineteen, summoned after his father's death so the elders could look at him and decide whether he was a danger. They had decided he was merely strange, and let him go back to his bones.

Tonight he suspected the decision would be different.

---

Inside, the hall smelled of woodsmoke and tallow and something else beneath — a sourness, animal and fresh, that clung to the elders' sleeves. Blood, carried in on their clothes from the morning's slaughtering. Emrys felt the dead stir at the edge of his hearing, drawn to it like moths.

Eight faces turned toward him as he entered. Coram sat at the head, in the eldest chair, and Emrys was struck — not for the first time, but more sharply than ever — by how much of his own face lived on his uncle's: the same deep-set grey eyes, the same heavy jaw. It was like being greeted across the room by a harder-edged version of himself, one that had been sanded down by forty more years of responsibility.

"Sit," Coram said. Not unkindly. That was the terrible thing about Coram; he was almost never cruel in tone. His cruelty was architectural. It lived in structures, in decisions, in the patient arithmetic of what must be done so that children might live through winter.

Emrys sat. The Thornbury chair received him with a creak that sounded, in the hush, almost like speech.

"The border thinned again," Elder Voss began, because Coram always let someone else open with the bad news. "Tam Ellery swears he watched a wren fly straight through the air where the wall ought to be. Straight through, and out the other side, singing." Voss was a round, sweating man whose family had kept the eastern stones for six generations, and fear had eaten him down to the sinew. "The barley's blackened in the low fields. The river ran grey Tuesday. Grey, like ash water. I don't need to tell this room what grey water means."

"It means He's waking," said old Mother Quill, who at ninety-one was the oldest person in the valley and said whatever she liked. Her voice was thin and clear as a needle. "It means the binding rots. We were fools to think otherwise. My grandmother told me the covenant was a roof, not a wall, and roofs fail."

"Then we re-tighten it." This was Coram, and every head turned to him the way iron filings turn to a lodestone. He leaned forward, firelight carving grooves in his face. "The founders bound Him once with nine names and nine offerings. The ledger is clear. What was done in famine can be done again, in lesser measure, to hold what remains. Livestock first. Then—"

"Livestock won't hold anything," Emrys said.

The word dropped into the room and lay there. No one moved. Somewhere in the walls, a settling timber ticked like a clock.

Coram regarded his nephew with an expression Emrys had seen him wear at funerals: sorrowful, composed, immovable. "Nephew. You sit in a founder's chair. Speak, then, as a founder's blood. Tell us what livestock won't do."

"They won't satisfy Him." Emrys heard his own voice come out steady, which surprised him, because inside he was shaking like a struck bell. "Because it isn't hunger He's dying of. I've listened to bones my whole life. I know the difference between a body that starves and a spirit that does. He doesn't want blood. He never wanted blood. Your grandfathers gave it to Him because blood is what men understand how to take."

"He," said Elder Voss, with the delicacy of a man handling something rotten. "You'd have us say it aloud. Name it."

"I'd have you say worse than that." Emrys stood. The chair scraped back. Around the ring, eight faces watched him — some hostile, some frightened, one or two, he thought, hungry for permission to believe him. "I'd have you say what I said at the well, since apparently we're all determined to pretend I didn't say it. Our founders murdered a god to survive a famine winter. Nine of them, and the ledger is in this building's cellar, and I have read it, and the ritual of binding is written in bone-dust in a hand I recognize because it's the hand my family has written with for two centuries. We didn't build a sanctuary. We built a prison, and we built our houses on top of it, and we called the silence peace."

Mother Quill laughed — a dry, rustling sound, not mocking so much as weary. "The bone-carver preaches. Well. My grandmother also said the Thornburys talked too much."

"And yet," Coram said quietly, "it was a Thornbury who volunteered first."

The hall went very still.

Emrys looked at his uncle. Coram met his gaze without flinching, and there was something in his uncle's face that Emrys had never seen there before — not triumph, nothing so simple. Grief. Old, load-bearing grief, the kind that holds up roofs.

"You know," Coram said. "Don't you. You found the ledger, so you know. The ninth name isn't written the way the others are. Read it again, nephew. The first eight were taken. Persuaded. Terrified. But the ninth knelt down willingly and offered his own blood to start the binding, because the ritual demanded a willing heart or it would fail — and because the alternative was watching his own children eat things no child should eat, and die anyway." His voice did not rise. It lowered, if anything, until Emrys had to lean forward to catch it. "That was your ancestor. Mine. Ours. So yes — I demand renewed sacrifice. Not because I love cruelty. Because I am the ninth line, and keeping the covenant is not our crime. It is our penance. It is the only thing left that we can give."

"That's not penance." Emrys's voice cracked at last, and he hated it, hated the tremor. "Penance is yours to pay. You keep spending other people's blood to settle a debt that's already owed. The ewes this morning — the oxen — and next? You'll stand in this room and tell me the arithmetic stops at animals?"

No one answered. In the silence, Emrys became aware of a sound only he could hear: the dead, pressing close, hundreds of them, murmuring *remember* like a tide. And beneath the murmur, fainter, deeper, from far below the hearthstone — something vast turning over in its sleep, attentive, listening to the argument above it the way a man listens to rain on a roof.

*They quarrel over Me,* it seemed to say, without malice, without hope. *As they always have.*

Coram rose too, and now the two of them stood facing each other across the hearth, the same face in different lights. "Next will be whatever must be next," Coram said. "I pray it is nothing. I have prayed every night since the barley blackened that it be nothing. But I will tell you what I told this room twenty years ago, and thirty, and forty, when the covenant creaked and we patched it: better one death freely given than a valley slowly erased. Better cruelty with an end than mercy that lets us all dissolve into nothing. You call it machinery, nephew — I can see it in your face, you think we are gears grinding. Perhaps we are. But gears keep a mill turning, and flour keeps children alive, and I have buried fewer children in my sixty years than my grandfather buried in one winter. That is the whole of my philosophy, and I will answer for it before any god, including the one under this floor."

The fire popped. Around the ring, the elders looked down at their hands.

"The moot votes at moonrise," Coram said. "On the question of renewed offering. Every founder's blood speaks, and every founder's blood is counted — including yours." For a moment, just a moment, something almost gentle crossed his features. "Vote however your conscience drives you, Emrys. That's why you were bidden. But know this: if the vote passes, and the rite requires more than beasts — I will stand between this valley and that necessity with my own throat. I am an old man. My arithmetic is nearly done. Let mine be the last sum, if it comes to sums at all."

Emrys stared at him. He had come prepared for a tyrant and found instead a man standing in a doorway he had guarded so long he could no longer remember what he was guarding it against — only that behind it were children, and cold, and the memory of a famine that had taught his family that survival and sin wore the same boots.

"I'll vote," Emrys said hoarsely. "But hear me, Uncle. There is a third door. Not the covenant, not the slaughter. I've seen it. There is a way to end this that doesn't cost the valley — but it costs the truth, all of it, and none of you are ready to pay that."

"Then teach us," said Mother Quill unexpectedly, her needle-voice thin in the dark. "Or get out of the doorway yourself, boy, and let the old man guard it his way."

At moonrise, the vote came: seven for renewal, one abstaining, and Emrys's lone voice against, ringing in the stone room like a bell in an empty house.

Walking home afterward through the silvered fields, Maren's shawl bright against the dark beside him, Emrys listened to the dead below the shrine murmur their one word, over and over, patient as erosion. And he understood, finally, that Coram was not the machinery of cruelty. Coram was a gear in it, the way Emrys himself was — the way every soul born under the covenant had been, turning and turning, keeping a mill that ground something no one had ever bothered to identify.

The crops were failing. The border was thinning. And somewhere beneath the valley, a murdered god waited to learn what nine families' children had decided He was worth.

Emrys walked faster, though there was nowhere to walk *to* — not yet. But there was a woman waiting beyond the border stones who claimed to know where a certain door stood, and tomorrow, he thought, tomorrow he would go and find her, and ask her the question that had begun to burn in him like a coal:

*How do I stop being a gear, and become a hand?*

The dead murmured. The mist closed behind him. And in the moot-hall, the fire burned low over a hearthstone older than every name spoken above it.
