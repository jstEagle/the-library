## 8. First Blood at the Shrine

The first blood was a ewe's, and it did not work.

Emrys heard about it the way he heard about everything in Thornvale now — too late, from someone who wanted something carved. Old Piet Hallow came to his workshop on a grey morning with rain still beaded in his beard, and he would not sit down, and he would not take tea, and he stood by the door turning his hat in his hands like a man wringing out a small animal.

"They took Bramble to the shrine," he said. "Coram's lot. Three nights past."

"Bramble." Emrys set down his knife. The ewe's name sat strangely in the room, among all his little carved bones. "Piet, that ewe was twenty years old. You used to let her sleep against your kitchen stove."

"Aye." Piet's voice cracked on it. "Aye, I know what she was to me. That's why I'm here, isn't it? They said blood freely given, blood of something loved. That's what the old ledger says — Coram read it out at the moot, loud as a sermon. *Blood freely given.*" He wiped his face with the back of his hand. "And nothing happened, Emrys. The crops keep yellowing at the edges. The border stones keep humming like they do. And Bramble is dead in a pit behind the shrine, and I gave her up for nothing."

Emrys looked at the man a long moment. There was no charm he could carve for this. He had charms for the dying and charms for the dead, but none for the living who had been made fools of by their own fear.

"I'll carve her name," Emrys said quietly. "On hazelwood, if you like. She wasn't a person, but she was somebody's somebody, and that counts."

Piet nodded too many times and left without taking the tea after all.

Emrys sat alone in his workshop, listening.

He had been listening all week, and he didn't like what he heard. For two hundred years the dead of Thornvale had murmured — a low, patient undercurrent through every bone he touched, like wind through wheat. Griefs mostly. Small unfinished things. A woman who'd never apologized to her sister. A boy who'd dropped his father's ring down a well. But beneath the murmuring there had always been something else, something older and deeper and very far away, so distant most carvers would have called it silence: a pressure, like the sea leaning against a seawall.

That pressure was gone. In its place was attention.

Something below the shrine had turned over in its long sleep, and now it was listening back.

* * *

The second blood was a goat's, and then an ox's, and then two pigs, and each time Coram Thornbury stood on the shrine steps and read from the founders' ledger in a voice pitched to carry across half the valley, and each time the crowd grew larger and hungrier and more frightened, which Emrys knew from long experience were three names for the same thing.

He went to the third rite intending only to watch. He told himself he owed the dead that much — to witness, if nothing else, since witnessing was the whole of his trade. Maren came with him, walking close, her shawl pulled up though the day was warm. Her daughter Sela trotted ahead chasing a stick, and every few steps Maren's eyes flicked to the girl the way a hand checks a wound.

"You shouldn't come," Maren said. "You know Coram will use you being there."

"He already uses me," Emrys said. "I'd rather he did it to my face."

The shrine had been swept clean for the occasion. Torches burned at noon, which struck Emrys as either theater or terror — fire lit when no darkness needed driving back. A pen had been built beside the altar, rough-hewn and new, and in it stood an ox with gentle brown eyes, chewing thoughtfully, entirely unaware that it had been elected.

Coram stood on the steps in his moot-hall robes, grey wool gone threadbare at the cuffs because Coram spent the valley's coin on grain stores and road stone and never once on himself. That was the thing about his uncle that made hating him difficult: he was not a hypocrite. He genuinely believed every word he was about to say.

"People of Thornvale!" His voice rolled out over the gathered crowd, three hundred souls at least, more than any harvest festival had ever drawn. "Our grandmothers' grandmothers left us a covenant written in their own suffering! A famine winter. A valley full of starving children. And nine brave souls — nine — who found a way to save them!"

Emrys felt Maren go stiff beside him.

"And now the covenant thins," Coram went on, "because we have grown lazy in our keeping of it. The border hums. The crops fail at the margins. Birds cross where birds should not. And what does the ledger command?" He raised the great book — Emrys had seen it only once, in the dark of the archive, and seeing it now in daylight made his stomach turn. "*Blood freely given, in season and out of season, keeps the wall between us and the wasting dark.*"

He lifted the axe.

Emrys moved before he decided to move. Later he could never quite reconstruct the decision; it was simply that his feet carried him forward through the crowd, and Maren hissing his name behind him, and then he was standing in the open space before the altar with three hundred faces swinging toward him like weather vanes.

"Uncle," he said. "Stop."

Coram lowered the axe an inch. His eyes, pale as winter water, found Emrys with something that was almost relief and almost grief. "Nephew. Come stand with your family, then. It's fitting. Your line kept the covenant longest of any."

"That's exactly what I've come to talk about." Emrys's heart was slamming against his ribs. He was aware, distantly, of how absurd he must look — the bone-carver, slight and quiet, in his ink-stained apron, facing down the most powerful man in the valley. "You keep saying *nine brave souls*. I've read the ledger, Coram. All of it. Not just the parts you read aloud."

A murmur ran through the crowd.

"The famine winter was real," Emrys said, louder. "The hunger was real. I don't doubt one hour of the suffering. But the ledger doesn't say the god beneath this shrine attacked anyone. It doesn't say the god cursed the fields or blighted the herds. Do you know what it says?" He turned, slowly, to face the whole crowd, and his voice cracked on the words and he let it crack, because these people trusted honest cracks more than smooth sermons. "It says the founders *found* the god. Sleeping. Beneath the hill. And it says they bound it — bound it with bone-dust and a rite — and then they took its strength. Its strength, that had been keeping this valley green since before there were founders. They stole from a sleeping thing to feed themselves, and when the theft started costing them, they dressed it up as sacrifice and called it mercy."

"That is enough—"

"It says *nine*," Emrys pressed on, over him. "Nine names. And mine is among them. Thornbury. So believe me when I tell you I have no pleasure in saying this: our ancestors weren't heroes. They were starving people who committed murder, and then built two centuries of prayers on top of the body."

The silence afterward had a texture to it, physical, like the moment after ice cracks on a pond. Emrys watched the crowd absorb it — watched old women clutch each other's hands, watched young men look at Coram for rescue, watched Piet Hallow near the back go white and then red and then settle into something grimly thoughtful, because Piet had loved an ewe and knew what it was to give something up for a lie.

Coram's face had gone the color of the ash in a cold hearth.

"You dare," he said softly, "stand on consecrated ground and call our founders murderers."

"I dare stand anywhere and say true things. It's my only skill."

"They *saved* us!" Coram's composure broke at last, and what was underneath was worse than anger — it was anguish. "You think the famine ended by luck? You think nine farmers out-thought a winter? They did what had to be done so that children — *children*, Emrys, your own blood three times back — would live to complain about us! If that is murder, then murder fed this valley for two hundred years, and I will spill every drop of blood in it before I let us starve again!"

"There it is," Emrys said quietly. "Say the rest of it, Uncle. Whose blood, exactly? Because first it was livestock. And Bramble Hallow died for nothing. And everyone here can count. Everyone here can see the pens getting bigger and the ledger getting read more often, and everyone knows what comes after oxen when oxen stop working."

He saw it land. Saw it ripple outward through the crowd — mothers pulling children closer, a farmer's wife covering her mouth, Sela tugging her mother's sleeve and asking why everyone had stopped moving. Coram heard it too. For one terrible instant his eyes flicked, involuntarily, toward the crowd, searching it the way a man searches a room where he has lost something valuable.

Searching it for children.

"No," Maren breathed, so softly only Emrys caught it.

"Enough rites," Emrys said, loud and clear, into that searching silence. "Go home. All of you. Bury your animals with names, not knives. If the wall fails, we'll meet it standing together — not feeding each other to it piece by piece."

For a heartbeat, three hundred people balanced on a knife-edge of history. Then a woman in the front row — Widow Ashun, whose son Emrys had carved a memorial for two winters past — stepped backward off the packed earth. Just one step. But it was a direction, and directions are contagious. Others followed. The crowd broke apart in slow, muttering streams, some glaring hatred at Emrys, some at Coram, some at nobody, at the world itself.

The ox lowed, bewildered, in its pen.

Coram descended the shrine steps until he stood an arm's length away. Up close, Emrys could see how much older his uncle had grown in a single month, how the skin had gone loose at his jaw, how his hands shook around the ledger he still clutched.

"You've split them," Coram said. "Half the valley thinks you're a prophet and half thinks you're a curse, and neither half will follow me now without question. Do you understand what you've done? Doubt is a hole in the covenant, nephew. Fear held this wall up as much as blood ever did. You've taken the fear and given them questions instead."

"Questions hold walls up too," Emrys said. "Better ones. Walls built on questions get inspected."

Coram looked at him for a long moment — and Emrys saw, with a chill, that behind the fury his uncle was afraid. Truly afraid, deep down, in the place where the family secret lived.

"When the wall fails," Coram said, "and it fails soon — remember this day. Remember that you chose words over keeping." He turned and walked away, robes snapping, leaving the ledger's shadow across the grass.

* * *

That night, Emrys understood what the cost of truth was.

It came for him around midnight, when the workshop was dark and the coals in the brazier had sunk to a sullen orange. It began as a vibration in the floorboards — faint, rhythmic, wrong. Then the bones began.

Every piece in the shop. Two hundred memorials waiting for families, a century of offcuts in the cedar chest, the half-finished hazel rod for Piet Hallow's ewe. They began to rattle against each other, gently at first, like teeth chattering in a mild chill. Emrys sat up in his cot in the back room and listened with his whole body, the way he'd listened his entire life, and what he heard made the hair rise along his arms.

Screaming.

Not the murmurs. Not the patient, wheat-wind whispers he had known since childhood, the small griefs and unfinished goodbyes. This was the deep layer, the old layer, the pressure-that-had-become-attention — except the attention had curdled. Something vast below the shrine had felt three hundred hearts turn against its keepers, had felt the covenant wobble on its ancient pins, and it was screaming — not in rage, Emrys realized, sick with a pity he hadn't asked for. In *hope*. The way a starving creature screams when it smells bread.

The bones screamed with it. Every carved name in the valley, every memorial he had ever made, resonating like struck glass, and threaded through the screaming came words, fragments, torn loose from whatever deep memory the bound thing kept:

*—they came with dust and knives—*

*—I was asleep, I was only asleep—*

*—the children, I would have fed the children, I was MADE for the feeding—*

Emrys pressed his palms over his ears, which was useless; the sound wasn't coming through air. It was coming through marrow, through the bones of his own skull, through the founder's blood that Coram had just reminded him ran in his veins.

"Stop," he whispered. "Please. I'm trying to help you."

*—then OPEN, little carver, little thief's-son, open what your fathers shut—*

The rattling crescendoed. A shelf bracket snapped; memorials cascaded to the floor, and even scattered they rattled, spinning slowly on the boards like compass needles all finding the same terrible north. Through the window Emrys saw light over the shrine hill — torches, dozens of them, Coram's faction keeping vigil, and he wondered wildly whether they could hear it too, whether the whole valley lay awake listening to its own foundation scream.

Then, near dawn, it stopped. All at once, like a held breath released. The bones settled. Silence poured back into the workshop, thick and total — and Emrys discovered, lying shaking in his cot, that he missed the murmurs. Missed them like a limb. Even the dead's ordinary grief would have been company now.

He got up, lit a candle, and knelt among the fallen memorials, gathering them one by one. Each name he lifted, he said aloud, the way he did for families: *Marta Ashun. Tam Wicke. Little Nell Fairrow.* Restoring order. Restoring witness.

When he reached the bottom of the pile, he found a bone he didn't recognize. Long, grey, older than any stock he owned — it must have been buried in the chest's depths for generations, a founder-era offcut perhaps, and he had never once heard it whisper.

Now it was warm. Warm as a living hand. And as his fingers closed around it, the murmur that rose from it was clear and single and terribly patient, and it said:

*"Come down, Emrys Thornbury. Come down and see what your family did to Me. I will show you the winter. I will show you everything. And then you will decide what your blood owes."*

Outside, the sky was going grey at the edges, and somewhere down the lane a rooster was crowing at a morning that smelled, faintly but unmistakably, of ash.

Emrys closed his fist around the warm bone, and knew he would go.
