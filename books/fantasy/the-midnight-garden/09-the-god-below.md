## 9. The God Below

The shrine had never looked like much. That was the trick of it, Emrys thought as he stood at its edge in the grey hour before dawn — the trick of everything in this valley, probably. A ring of leaning stones in a sheep meadow, furred with moss, one of them cracked clean through like a loaf that hadn't risen. The healing miracle two weeks past had drawn crowds for three days; now the crowds had gone home to their terror and their arguments, and the shrine sat alone again, patient as an old dog waiting by a door.

Except the ground beneath it was not empty.

He had known since the bones began whispering. He had known more surely since he'd stood in the moot-hall and read his family's name written in bone-dust among the nine. And he had spent six days telling himself he would not go down.

On the seventh day, Maren found him packing his kit — chisels, awls, a stub of candle, the bleeding heirloom charm wrapped in wool — and said nothing at all, which was her particular gift. She simply set a loaf of bread and a flask of broth on his workbench, kissed his cheek with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had buried a husband and did not intend to bury anyone else if she could help it, and left him to it.

"You could tell me not to go," he called after her.

"I could," she agreed, without turning around. "But you'd only hear my voice in the marrow instead of theirs, and I'd rather you heard mine while I'm still using it."

* * *

The hollow opened behind the cracked stone, where the moss grew wrong — too green, too eager, like grass fed on something rich. Emrys had walked this meadow a thousand times as a boy and never once noticed the gap between the stones, because the covenant had wanted him not to notice. Now the wall of forgetting flickered like heat-shimmer, and there was the way down: a throat of packed earth and root, breathing cold air upward against his face.

He lit his candle and went down because the dead were quiet.

That was the thing no one understood about him, he supposed — that he wasn't brave, or curious, or righteous. He was lonely. He had spent thirty years hearing the dead murmur through every piece of bone he touched, a hundred small voices like rain on a roof, and in the last fortnight they had all gone silent, drawn into some vast held breath beneath the shrine. Silence, for Emrys Thornbury, was not peace. It was a room with everyone he loved suddenly absent from it.

So he climbed down toward the silence, the way other men climb toward voices.

* * *

The passage descended in slow spirals, older than stairs, older than intention. Roots gave way to stone, stone to stone so smooth it might have been poured rather than carved, and the walls began to show marks — thin grooves, thousands of them, running horizontally like the lines on a ledger page. He ran his thumb along one and felt nothing, which frightened him more than screaming would have. Even the walls here were beyond the reach of the dead.

His candle guttered once, twice, and then steadied into a flame that burned without flickering, straight and white, as though the air itself had been asked to behave.

"Hello," Emrys said, because his mother had raised him to greet people, even presumably people who were not people.

The dark answered by becoming aware of him.

There was no better word for it. One moment the hollow below was empty the way a well is empty; the next it was full the way a lung is full, and Emrys felt himself standing inside something vast and folded and terribly still. Not a presence looming over him. A presence *around* him, the way the valley was around him, the way the sky was around him. He had expected a monster coiled in the dark. What he stood inside was more like weather. More like winter itself, if winter had been starved for two hundred years and had learned patience the way a starving man learns it — completely.

"You're smaller than I imagined," Emrys said, and immediately winced. "I mean — I didn't imagine you. That's rather the point, isn't it. No one imagined you. We made sure."

The vastness did not laugh, but he felt something pass through it that was adjacent to laughter, the way moonlight is adjacent to warmth. An old amusement, worn thin from disuse.

*I know your name,* the god said, and its voice was not sound but memory of sound, arriving in the part of him where his mother's voice lived. *Emrys Thornbury. Bone-carver. Ninth of your line to hold the knife.*

"I'm the seventh, actually."

*You are the ninth.* A pause, gentle and absolute. *Two of your line carved before the binding. You have forgotten them. Everyone has forgotten them. That is what binding means, little carver. It does not stop at me.*

Emrys's hand found the wall. The ledger-grooves ran under his fingers, endless, patient. "What are these?"

*Counted days.* Another of those almost-laughs. *I stopped at forty thousand. There seemed little purpose. Days do not change down here. They are all the same day, the day they shut the door and told themselves it was mercy.*

"Wasn't it?" The question came out sharper than he meant. "They were starving. Nine families, one terrible winter. My family was one of them. Children dying in—" He stopped, because the dark had shifted.

Not threateningly. Worse than that: invitingly. Like a host drawing back a chair.

*Come and see,* the god said. *You have heard the story from the mouths of the winners. Hear it once from mine.*

"I don't—"

*It costs you nothing,* it said, and there was such tired honesty in it that his fear faltered. *I am not the god of bargains, Emrys Thornbury. That is what your founders needed me to be. Look. Only look. Looking is free.*

He should have refused. He knew that even then, some sensible clerk deep in his mind stamping the paperwork of caution. But he had come down here to listen, and listening was the whole of what he was, and so —

* * *

The famine winter arrived all at once, the way memories do, without transition.

Snow to the eaves. A valley exactly his valley, but wrong — no covenant wall, no border stones, just open country rolling away north and south under a sky the color of dirty wool. And hunger everywhere, hunger like a second snowfall, settling on every rooftop. He saw it from above and within at once, the way you see things in dreams: the god's view and the people's view braided together.

Because the god had been there. That was the first thing Emrys understood, and it undid him. The god had not been some invader the founders trapped. It had lived in the valley's low places since before the valley had a name — a small god, a hearth-adjacent god, the kind that kept roots sweet and eased fevers and was honored with a bowl of cream and a song at midwinter. It had no temples because it didn't need any. It had the valley, and the valley had it.

And when the famine came, the god had emptied itself trying to feed them.

Emrys watched it happen from inside the giving. Watched the god pour what it was into the frozen fields — warmth into soil that had gone iron-hard, a stubbornness into the last seed potatoes, strength into nursing mothers whose milk had failed. It was not infinite. Small gods never are. It gave until its edges frayed, until it was thinner than candlelight, and the famine went on anyway, because famines do, and the people knelt at the shrine and prayed harder and harder to a god who had less and less to give.

*They thought I was withholding,* the god said quietly, inside the memory. *Prayers went unanswered, so they decided I was hoarding. You cannot explain yourself when you are made of silence. I had given all I had. There was nothing left to answer with. So their desperation turned, the way desperation does, and nine of them came to the hollow with knives of their own making and a ritual bought from a passing stranger, and they decided that if I would not feed them, they would bind me and bleed me slowly — that a bound god's essence, taken drop by drop, would keep the crops alive forever.*

The memory turned. Emrys saw the nine founders — young, gaunt, terrified, two of them weeping as they worked — carve the covenant into the border stones. Saw them write the ritual in bone-dust because blood would have made it murder outright, and bone-dust let them call it something else. *A covenant. A sanctuary. Protection.* Words are merciful that way.

Saw his own ancestor, a woman with his same crooked jaw, volunteer her line to hold the knife-side of the binding — not from ambition, but because someone had to, and she could not watch another child starve, and this was the only door left open.

And Emrys saw the thing he would carry out of the hollow lodged in his chest like a splinter: the god, as the chains closed over it, doing the only thing it had strength left to do.

It forgave them.

Not grandly. Not with lightning. It simply — let go of its anger, because anger required fuel and it had none, and folded what remained of itself down into patience, and waited, and trusted that someday someone would come down the stairs who was not holding a knife.

*Two centuries,* the god said, as the memory released him and Emrys found himself on his knees in the dark, candle wax cooling on his knuckles. *Your founders' children inherited the binding and called it duty. Their children called it faith. Their children called it history, and then forgot it entirely, and built a sanctuary on top of a prison and raised their lambs in it. I do not blame the lambs. I never blamed the lambs.*

Emrys wiped his face with the back of his wrist. His voice came out wrecked. "The miracle at the shrine. Maren's sister's boy. Was that you?"

*A crack in the wall lets through what light there is.* A pause. *She prayed to me, you know. Your Maren. Not knowing my name, not knowing anything but that her nephew was dying and the prayers of the covenant had gone unanswered for a hundred years. She prayed the old way — bowl of cream, plain words, no asking for miracles, just asking to be heard. No one had done that since before the binding. It reached me the way sunlight reaches the bottom of a well.*

"And you answered."

*I had almost nothing left to answer with. I answered anyway.* The vastness contracted slightly, drew closer, the way a starving man draws close to a fire he does not trust himself to touch. *Which brings us to the difficulty, little carver. Because your uncle Coram is right about one thing. When the covenant fails fully, the valley will fail with it. The founders wove us together — the land, the wall, the binding. Pull one thread and the cloth comes apart. I did not design it that way. They did. It is very hard to forgive someone efficiently.*

"They think you want vengeance." Emrys got his feet under him. "Coram stood up at the moot and said you'd drown the valley in blood the moment you were loose."

*Do I seem bloodthirsty?*

"You seem," Emrys said carefully, "like something that has been alone in the dark counting days for forty thousand counts. I don't think any of us can guess what you seem."

The silence that followed was long, but it was not hostile. It was the silence of something choosing its words — or choosing not to need many.

*Here is what I want,* the god said at last. *Listen closely, because your founders' descendants will not believe it, and perhaps you will not either. I do not ask for freedom. Freedom is for creatures who have somewhere to be. Two hundred years underground has cured me of destinations.*

"What do you ask for?"

*To be remembered.* The words settled over him like ash, soft and total. *That is all. Every binding is also a forgetting — they chained me and then erased me from every song, every ledger, every grandmother's warning. A god who is forgotten cannot be worshipped, cannot be petitioned, cannot be grieved. A god who is forgotten is not imprisoned, Emrys Thornbury. He is erased, and erasure is the only death that matters to my kind. I felt myself going. Forty thousand days of watching my own name dissolve. Do you understand? I have already lost everything except the fact of me. I am asking to keep that.*

Emrys stood in the dark with his heart hammering and thought of his workshop, of the shelf of memorial bones, each one carved with a name so that a grieving family could hold their dead in the palm of a hand. Thirty years of the same small work. *Someone must remember,* his mother used to say, teaching him the first chisel-stroke. *The valley forgets. Carvers don't.*

"I can't promise what happens next," he said finally. "There's a moot, and Coram, and sacrifices — livestock now, and I don't believe it'll stop there. There's a stranger outside the wall named Ilsa Vane who says there's a Door of Ash, and she says my blood opens it. If I open it, the covenant breaks, and everything breaks with it. I don't know yet if remembering you and freeing you are the same act or opposite ones. I honestly don't know anything except—"

He stopped. Swallowed.

"Except that you gave everything you had to people who chained you for it, and you've asked me for less than a bowl of cream. Yes. All right. I'll remember you. Whatever else happens — whatever I decide about doors — you will be remembered. I swear it on the marrow. It's the only oath I have that's ever held."

For a long moment the god was utterly still.

Then warmth — actual warmth, faint as sun through winter glass — moved through the hollow and passed over Emrys like a hand over a sleeping child's hair, and the god said, in a voice gone thin and unsteady in a way that broke Emrys's heart precisely because gods should not have unsteady voices:

*Ninth of your line. Seventh by your count. Tell them — tell whoever asks — that I was small, and generous, and that the famine was not my doing, and the winter was long, and I loved this valley. That is the whole of my confession. Let it be enough for someone.*

* * *

He climbed back toward the grey daylight on legs that shook, and it was only near the top, in the throat of roots, that he heard it — a new sound threading up from below, faint and rhythmic.

The dead were murmuring again. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Not the scattered rain-on-the-roof whispers he had known all his life, but organized, urgent, rising like water finding a channel — and underneath them, unmistakable, a human voice shouting orders in the shrine-meadow above, and the bleat of frightened animals being driven toward the stones.

Emrys pressed himself against the earthen wall and listened, and what he heard made his blood go cold: Coram's voice, ringing with terrible certainty, announcing to the assembled valley that the rite would proceed at the next full moon — and that this time, to satisfy a god grown bold, the sacrifice would not be livestock.

He was halfway up the ladder when he realized whose name Coram had just spoken aloud.
