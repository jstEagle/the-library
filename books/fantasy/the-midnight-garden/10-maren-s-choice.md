## 10. Maren's Choice

The morning after Emrys accused the founders of murder in front of half the valley, Maren woke before dawn to the sound of her daughter laughing in the garden.

This was not, in itself, alarming. Sef was six, and Sef laughed at most things — at the hens' outrage when she stole their eggs warm from the nest, at the way the rain fell sideways in a wind, at Maren's attempts to braid her own hair without a looking-glass. But this laugh was different. This was the laugh she'd had before the fever last spring, before the shrine, before the light that had poured out of the old stones and into her daughter's chest and closed up a sickness that should have killed her.

Maren went to the window.

Sef stood among the bean rows in her nightdress, barefoot in the frost, holding out both hands to something Maren couldn't see. The frost did not touch her. Around her small feet, in a circle perhaps as wide as a cartwheel, the ground was green — green in late autumn, green as midsummer, the beans themselves straining upward on their strings as if they believed it was June and were determined not to waste the lie.

"Sef," Maren called through the glass, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Come inside. You'll catch your death."

Her daughter turned, and there was dirt on her knees and frost-melt in her hair, and she said, "Mama, he's still here."

Maren dressed without hurry because hurrying would have meant admitting what she already knew, which was that everything since the healing had been a countdown, and she could feel the numbers ticking against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

* * *

She took Sef to Emrys's workshop that same afternoon, under the pretense of returning a borrowed kettle, which was a lie so transparent that even Emrys — who was many things, bone-carver and mourner and keeper of other people's griefs, but never once a fool about mothers — simply set the kettle aside and looked at her over the rims of his spectacles until she gave up.

"He's still here," Maren said. "The god. Or whatever leaked out of the shrine into my daughter. She talks to him. The beans are growing out of season. Yesterday she put her hand on the hip of Old Tam's lame cow and Tam swears the cow walked sound by evening." She stopped, because her voice wanted to shake, and shook anyway. "Emrys. They'll notice."

"They've noticed," Emrys said quietly. He was cleaning a burin, turning it in the lamplight, and his hands were steady the way they always were, the way they had been at every funeral since Maren could remember, as though grief itself had taught him stillness. "Elder Coram sent for me last night. He asked whether I thought the miracle at the shrine was a blessing or a breach." His mouth twisted. "I told him I didn't know. Which is true. What I do know is that Coram has started counting heads."

"Counting heads," Maren repeated.

"For the old rites. Livestock first. He's already taken three of Hallam's goats." Emrys set down the burin. "Maren, you know what comes after livestock."

"I know what the ledger says comes after livestock," she said. "I also know the ledger is two hundred years old and written by frightened people who murdered a god to keep their children fed."

"And yet here we are, two hundred years later, with frightened people and hungry fields and a child standing in frost-green circles." He rubbed his eyes under the spectacles. "Why did you really bring her here?"

Because, Maren thought, of all the people in this valley, only one man heard the dead, and only one man had stood up in front of the moot and said aloud the thing everyone else had spent two centuries pretending not to know. Because Sef had begun sleeping with a chip of carved bone under her pillow — one of Emrys's memorial charms, scavenged from who-knew-where — and when Maren asked her where she'd gotten it, Sef had said, *he gave it to me, mama, the quiet one under the hill, he says it's so I can hear him better,* and Maren had lain awake the whole night after doing arithmetic she couldn't finish.

What she said was: "Because you're the only person in this valley who isn't afraid of the truth. And I need someone unafraid to stand next to me while I pretend very hard that everything is fine."

Emrys looked at her for a long moment. Then he pulled a stool over beside his workbench, and patted it, and said to Sef, "Well then. I'm told you talk to someone under the hill. I talk to people under the ground myself. Perhaps we should compare notes."

Sef climbed onto the stool with the solemnity of a magistrate and produced, from the pocket of her dress, the little bone charm. And Emrys — who had carved ten thousand bones and never once, in all Maren's memory of him, flinched — flinched.

"That's mine," he said softly. "That's Tamsin Hale's memorial. She died forty years ago. I buried the offcuts myself." He turned it in his fingers. "It's warm."

"It's always warm," Sef said. "He holds it for me sometimes. When I sleep. He doesn't like being cold."

* * *

Over the following days, Maren learned the shape of the thing growing inside her valley, the way you learn the shape of weather coming over the ridge — not by seeing it, but by the behavior of everything smaller than yourself.

The hens stopped laying near the shrine road but laid double everywhere else. The well water tasted faintly of summer. Old Tam's lame cow walked sound, and Tam, who had cursed every god living and dead for thirty years, now left bread and salt at his gate each evening and would not say why. People began, hesitantly, shamefacedly, to pray again — not at the ruined shrine, not yet, that was still too frightening, but privately, in kitchens, with their hands cupped like beggars. And some of those prayers were answered. Small ones. A fever broken. A calf turned right way round in the womb. A letter, long lost, found behind a loose brick.

And the moot met, and met again, and Elder Coram's voice grew harder each time, and the sacrifices began.

Three goats, the first week. Then a bull, its throat cut on the shrine steps, blood running down into the cracks of the old stones — and Maren, watching from the crowd, saw the blood vanish into the stone as if drunk, and heard a woman near her whisper, *it's working, it's working*, in a voice full of relief and horror in equal measure, and understood that the valley had split cleanly down its middle, like a bone struck true.

Emrys stood in the middle of the crack. He had made himself the crack. Every market day he spoke — calmly, terribly, with the patience of a man who had spent his life listening to the dead and had run out of reasons to fear the living — about the famine winter, and the nine names, and the ritual written in bone-dust, and how the founders had not saved the valley but sold it. Half the valley hated him for it. The other half came secretly to his workshop at dusk, and stood in the doorway, and asked him in whispers: *is it true? Is our grandmother's name in the ledger? Did they really—*

And through it all, Sef grew greener. Not her skin — her *presence*. Plants leaned toward her the way sunflowers lean toward noon. Animals followed her at a respectful distance, like courtiers. Once Maren caught her standing at the edge of the family plot, talking to the headstones, and Sef reported afterward, matter-of-fact, that Great-Aunt Bess had complained about the drainage and asked whether anyone was ever going to fix the fence.

"What does he want?" Maren asked her daughter one night, sitting on the edge of her bed, because she had to ask someone and Sef was the only one being spoken to directly. "The one under the hill. What does he want?"

Sef considered this with great seriousness, chewing the end of her braid.

"He wants to be remembered," she said finally. "He's not angry. Mama, he's been down there such a long time and nobody ever says his name, and he can't remember it himself anymore, and that's the worst part, worse than the cold. He just wants somebody to know he was here."

Maren held her daughter until the child slept, and then she sat alone in the dark kitchen and let herself do the arithmetic she had been avoiding.

Here was the sum as she understood it: the covenant was cracking because the god beneath the shrine was waking, or leaking, or being remembered back into the world. Coram believed the cracks could be sealed with blood, starting with animals and — when the animals proved insufficient, and they would prove insufficient, because you cannot patch a broken promise with goat's blood any more than you can mend a shattered bowl with spit — moving on to people. And the old rites, the ones written in bone-dust in a ledger no one was supposed to read, specified exactly what kind of person. A child. Innocent, unbaptized by any god but the bound one, marked by proximity to the leak.

Marked, said the arithmetic, cold and clear as ice on a windowpane.

Her daughter.

There was another part of the sum. There always was, with terrible sums. If the god stayed bound, Sef might die on the shrine steps and the valley might survive another century on borrowed mercy. If the god went free — if Emrys and the door-woman and whoever else joined them actually managed it — then the covenant would fail entirely, and the valley, whatever that meant, whatever *unmade* meant, would come apart. Either her daughter might be sacrificed to keep a prison standing, or her daughter might be erased along with the prison and everyone in it.

Unless. Unless there was a third column in the sum, one Emrys kept hinting at and wouldn't fully explain, something about carrying memories out, about the difference between a place and the remembering of a place. Maren didn't understand it yet. But she had decided — lying awake, doing sums in the dark — that she would bet her daughter's life on Emrys Thornbury's understanding of it, because he was the only man in the valley who had ever treated the dead as people rather than property, and because he had looked at Sef's warm little bone charm and hadn't reached for it greedily or thrown it in the fire, but had simply handed it back and said, *tell him I remember Tamsin Hale. Tell her too.*

A mother's arithmetic came down, in the end, to choosing whose word you trusted with your child's life. Maren chose.

* * *

She told Emrys she was with him on a grey morning at the border stones, where he had gone to meet the door-seeker woman — Ilsa Vane, all sharp angles and sharper eyes, wrapped in a coat that had seen more countries than Maren had seen winters. Maren arrived with Sef on her hip, a sack of bread and cheese, and the expression of a woman who has already argued with herself and won.

"You're coming," Ilsa said. It wasn't quite a question. She looked Maren up and down — the flour on her apron, the child, the determination — with the frank assessment of someone pricing a horse. "You know the valley will try to stop us. You know your own kin will call you traitor."

"My kin," Maren said, "have been calling me stubborn since I was four years old. Traitor is a promotion."

Something that was almost a smile crossed the door-seeker's face. "And the girl?"

"The girl," said Maren, tightening her arm around Sef, "goes where I go. That is not negotiable, not discussable, and not, whatever you're about to suggest, a bargaining chip for anything."

Ilsa raised both hands. "Wouldn't dream of it."

But Emrys was watching Maren with an expression she couldn't read — or could, and didn't like. It was the look of a man who hears the dead murmur through marrow, who notices things others miss, who had spent a lifetime learning what people hid even from themselves. He looked at her the way he must have looked at a freshly carved charm, checking for flaws.

"Maren," he said carefully, "the moot meets tomorrow. Coram means to formalize the next stage of the rites. Whatever happens after that—" He stopped. Started again. "Is there anything you ought to tell me? Anything at all?"

The kitchen was warm and smelled of bread. Sef was asleep against her shoulder, breathing the slow breath of a child who trusted the world completely. Somewhere under the hill, a starved and patient presence waited to be remembered, and somewhere above it, men with knives and old books were deciding whose blood would buy another hundred years.

*No*, she almost said. *Nothing. Everything's fine.* The lie sat ready on her tongue, worn smooth from practice, comfortable as an old shoe.

Instead she said, "Not here. Not in front of the border, where anyone could be listening." She shifted Sef's weight. "Tomorrow. Before the moot convenes. Come to my house at dawn, and I'll tell you why I'm really here, Emrys. All of it."

He nodded slowly, and if he suspected how much of *all of it* she still intended to hold back — the marking, the rite, the exact and terrible specificity of what Coram had planned — he had the grace not to say so.

That night, Maren packed a single bag in secret, kissed her sleeping daughter's forehead, and lay awake listening to the house settle around her, doing and redoing the sum.

Dawn, she promised herself. Dawn, and then the truth.

But dawn was three hours away, and the moot had moved its meeting to midnight, and by the time anyone knocked on Maren's door it would be far too late to confess anything at all.
