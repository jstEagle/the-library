## 11. Coram's Reckoning

Coram Thornbury had not slept in the moot-hall for forty years, but tonight he made an exception. He spread his cloak on the long bench beneath the founders' portraits, banked the fire, and waited for his nephew to come to him — because Emrys would come, eventually. The boy had always been like his father that way: stubborn as a mule in a bog, but predictable as one too.

He was not disappointed. Somewhere past midnight, boots on the flagstones, and then Emrys stood in the doorway of the archive, lantern in hand, looking like a man who had been caught doing something wrong and hadn't yet decided whether to be ashamed.

"You picked my lock," Coram said mildly.

"The lock was already broken."

"By you. Yesterday." He gestured to the bench opposite. "Sit down, Emrys. You've been reading my ledger."

Emrys did not sit. "You knew I'd find it."

"I hoped. I prayed. Neither worked, which is about par for this valley." Coram poured two cups of cider from the jug he'd brought up from the cellar — good cider, the autumn pressing, saved for hard conversations. "The ledger's been in this hall since before either of us was born. Nine names. You found yours?"

"I found mine." Emrys set the lantern down carefully, the way he handled everything, as if bones might bruise. "Thornbury. Third from the top. *Voluntold* is written beside it. That's not a word, Uncle. Someone made it up so it wouldn't have to say *volunteered*."

"It's a perfectly serviceable word," Coram said. "My grandfather coined it. Sit down."

This time Emrys sat, though he held his cup without drinking it, which Coram chose to take as a small victory rather than a small insult. Outside, the wind worried at the shutters, and somewhere far off a dog was barking at nothing, which these days meant it was barking at everything.

"You want me to tell you the story," Coram said. "So I'll tell it once, properly, and then you can go back to accusing us of murder with all your facts in order. It's only fair. I raised you to be thorough."

"I haven't accused—"

"You stood up at the moot and called our grandmothers murderers in front of half the valley. Your words were gentler, but I know how you file things down. Your father did the same. Say what you mean and sharpen it twice." Coram drank. The cider was honest work; it steadied him. "Very well. Here is what happened in the famine winter, as it was told to me by my father, who had it from his mother, who was there."

* * *

"There were nine hundred souls in this valley when the frost came early and stayed late," Coram said. "You know the shape of it. Crops black in the field. Livestock dead by midwinter. And the prayers — we prayed the way people pray when they're starving, which is to say constantly and uselessly. The old gods didn't answer. Maybe they couldn't hear us. Maybe they were busy. It doesn't matter now.

"But there was something under the shrine hill. Something old, older than the valley, older than the covenant. The first settlers had felt it — a presence in the roots, patient as stone. They'd built the shrine over it deliberately, the way you'd put a lid on a well. Not to trap it. To be polite. To say: we know you're there, we'll keep quiet, don't mind us.

"In the famine winter, someone went down and knocked on the lid."

Emrys's cup had stopped halfway to his mouth. "Who?"

"A woman named Sera Vane." Coram watched his nephew absorb that. "Yes. A Vane. There have always been Vanes at the edges of things. She went down into the hollow and she asked the thing below for bread. And it gave it. Grain that sprouted overnight in frozen fields. Milk where there had been dry cows. The famine broke in nine days.

"And the price," Coram continued quietly, "was memory. The god — call it a god, it never asked for the title — fed on remembrance. Every miracle it worked, it ate a little of the valley's past. Names off gravestones. Faces out of minds. By spring, half the valley couldn't recall their own grandparents' names, and the children born that year came into the world while their mothers forgot their own childhoods. Sera Vane went down to thank it and came back up not knowing why she'd gone."

The fire popped. Emrys had gone very still, in the way he did when he was listening to marrow instead of men. Coram pressed on.

"So the founders met in this room. Nine of them. And they faced a choice that I hope to God no one I love ever faces. Let it feed until the valley was a country of strangers who couldn't remember each other — or bind it. Close the covenant. Wall the magic out and wall it in, and starve it slowly, and live small and forgetful of wonder but full of each other.

"They voted. Seven to bind. Two refused — and here is the part your ledger doesn't soften, because my grandfather wrote it honestly — the seven bound the other two's objections into the covenant itself. Their dissent became part of the wall. That's why the border stones hum when you touch them. That's Aldous and Petra, still saying no after two hundred years."

"And Thornbury?" Emrys's voice was thin. "Third from the top. Voluntold."

"My grandmother Maren — yes, the same name, we're an unoriginal family — volunteered her line for the binding rite. The ritual needed blood freely given, blood that would keep giving, generation after generation, or the covenant would rot in a century. Someone had to promise their children and their children's children to the keeping of the door. Seven families shared the weight, but the Thornburys took the keystone. Because," and here Coram's voice did something he hadn't planned, roughened despite himself, "because my grandmother had four children, and three of them had already forgotten her face. She said — I can recite it, I've said it at every moot of my life — *I will give what I have left, so that what remains remembers.*"

Silence. The dog outside had stopped barking. Emrys stared into his untouched cider.

"You think that makes it better," he said at last.

"No. I think it makes it *ours*. There's a difference."

* * *

"She wasn't a murderer, Emrys. None of them were." Coram rose and crossed to the portraits — nine grim faces in fading oils, hung so long they seemed less like paintings than weather stains. He touched the frame of the third. "They were starving parents who looked at a hungry god and chose to go hungry instead. Do you understand what that covenant bought? Two hundred years. Two hundred years of children who grew up knowing their grandmothers' voices. Of graves whose names stayed carved. Of a valley where nobody woke up a stranger in their own life. That is not a prison. That is a *promise*."

"And the god?" Emrys asked. "What do you call what we did to it?"

"I call it mercy," Coram said, "and I will defend that word with my last breath, because I've read what happens to the places that didn't choose it." He came back to the bench. "You've been outside the stones lately — don't look surprised, boy, I'm old, not blind. You've seen what the world is becoming now that the old gods are answering again. Towns tearing themselves apart over which prayer got answered first. Whole villages kneeling in the road waiting for a miracle that never comes, while their crops rot in answer to a different man's asking. Out there, the gods eat *everything* — hope, grief, memory, all of it, whatever's nearest. In here, we starved one god gently for two centuries so that eight hundred people could remember their own names. If that's murder, then it's the kind of killing a mother does pulling a child from a burning house — you break the arm to save the body."

Emrys was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, it was barely above the fire's crackle. "It talked to me. Down in the hollow. It showed me the famine winter from its side."

Coram's chest tightened. "And what did its side look like?"

"Hunger." Emrys's hands curled around the cup at last, though he still didn't drink. "Not hunger for food. For remembering. Uncle — it doesn't want worship. It wants to be *remembered*. That's all it ever wanted. And we built a wall between it and every memory in the world, and called ourselves merciful."

"It told you that," Coram said flatly. "A starving creature, alone in the dark for two hundred years, told you what it wanted, and you believed it."

"You don't believe it could tell the truth?"

"I believe," Coram said, "that hunger is the oldest liar in creation. My grandmother was hungry too, Emrys. Hungry enough to watch her children forget her face. And even starving, even mad with it, she chose the wall. What does that tell you about what she saw down in the dark?"

"That she was afraid."

"Yes! Yes, she was afraid, and fear is a fine honest counselor, and she listened to it, and because she did, I exist, and you exist, and Maren Cooper's daughter exists, and none of us woke up this morning unable to name our own mothers." Coram heard his voice climbing and reined it in. He was tired. God help him, he was seventy-one years old and tired down to the marrow. "I am not telling you the founders were saints. I'm telling you they were *parents*, which is harder and less flattering. They did an ugly thing in an ugly winter so that beauty could happen afterward, forever, on purpose. And every Thornbury since has carried the keeping of it. Including you. Especially you."

"Me?"

"Who do you think keeps the names?" Coram gestured at the workshop across the lane, visible through the shuttered window, lamplit even now. "Two hundred years of bone-carvers, one in every generation, and every single one a Thornbury. You thought that was coincidence? You carve the dead into bone so the covenant has something to hold onto — memory given a shape, a handle, something the wall can grip. Your whole life, boy, you've been doing the binding work with your little knife, and you never once asked why the dead talk to *you*."

Emrys stood abruptly. The bench scraped. For a moment Coram thought the boy would shout — and some traitorous part of him wanted it, wanted the fight, wanted the clean simple anger of youth. But Emrys only stood there, lantern-light trembling on his face, and said:

"If it's mercy, Uncle, why does it need sacrifices again? Why is Cora Cooper marked? Mercy doesn't eat children."

"The moot hasn't decided—"

"The moot met tonight. Don't lie to me in a room where our grandmother's portrait can see you." Emrys's voice cracked on the last word, and Coram understood, with a lurch, that the boy loved him still — that was the worst of it, that he could wound and be wounded and love through both. "Nine days, Coram. Nine days and they put a child on the shrine hill. Tell me that's what Maren Thornbury died for. Tell me that's the promise."

Coram looked at the portrait. His grandmother's painted eyes were the color of old bone, and like every founder, she had been painted unsmiling, because the painter had known them after, not before.

"The vote isn't final," he said quietly. "There are two elders against it. There have always been two."

"And if the vote goes the other way?"

"Then it goes the other way." He drained his cider. "But Emrys — listen to an old man for one more minute. Whatever you're planning, whoever that Vane woman is you've been meeting at the border stones — and yes, I know about her too, the whole eastern fence knows about her, she talks louder than she walks — think about what stands on the other side of that Door. Not freedom. Hunger. The same hunger, loose in a world already drowning in answered prayers. You'd trade a walled garden for a wolf-country and call it justice."

Emrys paused at the doorway, lantern lifted.

"Maren Thornbury gave what she had left," he said, "so that what remained remembered. I keep thinking about that, Uncle. About what she actually promised. Not to keep the god locked. Just — to remember. We've done one of those things for two hundred years." He pulled his collar up against the draft. "Maybe it's time we tried the other."

Then he was gone, boots fading down the lane, and Coram sat alone with nine painted ancestors and two empty cups.

After a while, he noticed the ledger lying open where his nephew had left it. He went to close it — and stopped. Beside the word *voluntold*, in fresh ink, in handwriting he recognized from a thousand carved memorial bones, someone had added a second line beneath his grandmother's vow:

*So that what remains remembers.*

And beneath that, smaller, almost apologetic:

*I'm sorry, Uncle. I have to try the other thing. — E.*

Coram stood for a long time with his thumb pressed against the wet-inked page. Then he snuffed the lantern, took down his heavy coat from the peg, and went out into the dark — not toward the moot-hall bell, and not toward the shrine hill, but toward the eastern fence, walking as fast as seventy-one-year-old legs would carry him, before his nephew did something that could not be carved over.
