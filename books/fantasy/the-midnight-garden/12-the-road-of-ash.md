## 12. The Road of Ash

The border let them through the way a rotten tooth lets go — all at once, and with less pain than Ilsa had braced for.

One moment she was pressed flat against the cold of the boundary stone, feeling that familiar pressure like swimming against a current, and the next there was nothing at all. Just air, and the smell of outside: woodsmoke, wet ash, and something sweeter underneath that she couldn't name until Maren did.

"Lilac," Maren said, puzzled. "Out here?"

"Gods," Emrys said quietly.

Ilsa had been out here for two years. She'd forgotten what the first breath felt like — the way the whole world seemed to lean toward you now, attentive, as if every prayer were being overheard by something with excellent hearing and questionable judgment. The lilacs bloomed where they shouldn't. Rivers ran uphill in three provinces she knew of. And everywhere, everywhere, people prayed.

"Keep moving," she said, because if she didn't say it aloud she'd stand there breathing like a fool. "We want to be past the shrine-road before dark."

"What's on the shrine-road?" Maren asked.

"Pilgrims." Ilsa shouldered her pack. "And worse, pilgrims who've been answered."

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a tollhouse, its roof half-collapsed, its little stove still drawing well enough to heat water. Emrys built the fire with the fussy competence of a man used to working alone, arranging kindling like he was carving it. Maren dozed sitting up, exhausted from the crossing — the border had taken more out of her than either of them admitted, though Ilsa had seen it: the moment they passed through, Maren had clutched her chest like something inside her had knocked loose.

"You should sleep," Ilsa told her.

"In a minute." Maren stared into the flames. "It's strange. Inside the valley, when I prayed at the shrine, nothing answered. It felt like shouting down a well. Out here—" she flexed her fingers, "—it feels like someone is standing just behind my shoulder. Listening. Politely."

"That's exactly how it feels," Ilsa said. "Politely. That's the word."

"And is that bad?"

Ilsa considered lying, decided against it. Lying to these two was like lying to a pair of sheep; it would work briefly and then everyone would be embarrassed. "My sister thought it was wonderful. Right up until it wasn't."

Emrys looked up from the fire. He'd been quiet since the border — quieter than usual, which for him meant nearly silent, his long carver's hands wrapped around his knees. "Tell us about her," he said. "Your sister. You've never actually—"

"No," Ilsa agreed. "I haven't."

She fed a stick to the fire. Outside, far off across the dead fields, something sang — a human voice, high and looping, one of the road-prayers that traveled from town to town like weather.

"Her name was Sef. Younger than me by four years, which she reminded me of weekly, right up until the fever took her lungs. Our mother prayed to every god whose name hadn't been struck from the rolls. Nothing. Then an old woman told her about a god with no name left — a small god, a gutter-god, the kind that lives in doorways and under stairs. My mother prayed to it because it was the only one listening anymore." Ilsa smiled without warmth. "Sef woke up the next morning coughing up black blood, but alive. Alive for eleven more years."

"That sounds like a mercy," Maren said carefully.

"It was. That's the trap of it." Ilsa drew her sleeve back. In the firelight, the mark on her forearm showed dark as a brand: a small archway, a debt-mark, the sign of a god who kept accounts. "Eleven years of Sef being healthy, and every year the god came for its tithe. Not much, at first. A lock of hair. A memory — she lost the taste for honey, then the memory of our father's face, then whole seasons. By the end she was praying to die, and the god, ever polite, obliged. And it marked me after, because grief is a currency too, and I'd watched the whole transaction and learned the exchange rate."

Emrys was looking at her arm with an expression she recognized — the same look he got around bone. Like he could hear something in it.

"You can't burn it off," he said. "Can you."

"I know six people who tried. I've seen what's left of five of them." She pulled her sleeve down. "The point, Thornbury, is this: your valley locked the gods out and called it a prison. Out here, we let them in and call it grace. Neither one is freedom. Freedom would be a world where nobody has to bargain with anything to keep their children breathing."

Maren was quiet a long moment. Then, in a voice smaller than Ilsa had yet heard from her: "My daughter's fever broke last spring. After I prayed at the ruined shrine. Everyone said it was a miracle."

Ilsa met Emrys's eyes across the fire and saw him arrive at the same place she already was. Nobody said it. Some things you carry unspoken or they get heavier.

"Sleep," Ilsa said again, gently this time. "Tomorrow we walk the Road of Ash, and you'll want your legs under you."

* * *

The Road of Ash earned its name honestly. It ran through country that had burned — not recently, but thoroughly, in the way of fires that are started deliberately and blessed before lighting. Charred fence posts stood in rows like a congregation waiting for a sermon that never came. Every few miles there was a wayside shrine, and every shrine was crowded.

That was the thing outsiders never understood about the old gods' return, Ilsa reflected as they picked their way past the fourth such shrine. It wasn't the miracles. Miracles were rare enough, and mostly small — fevers broken, wells refilled, one notorious incident involving forty pounds of butter. No. It was the *certainty*. When prayers started getting answered, people stopped tolerating doubt, and doubt was the only thing that had ever kept any of them decent.

A crowd had gathered at the fifth shrine — a squat cairn of river stones draped in ribbons, tended by a woman in grey robes who was holding forth in a voice trained to carry over wind and skepticism alike.

"—and the god does not ask for gold! Does not ask for blood! Only for witness! Bring your griefs and lay them down, and be *seen*, sisters, be *known*—"

"We could go around," Emrys murmured.

"The road goes through," Ilsa said. "Head down. Don't make eye contact with the robed ones. If anyone asks, you're my brothers."

"I'm clearly not your brother," Emrys said. "You're—"

"Tall? Yes. Genetics are a mystery. Walk."

They almost made it. They were twenty feet clear when a small boy detached himself from the crowd and fell into step beside Maren, gazing up at her with the frank adoration children reserve for tired women carrying bundles.

"Are you a pilgrim?" he asked.

"I'm traveling," Maren said.

"To the Door?" The boy's eyes went wide. "Everyone's walking somewhere now. Papa says the roads are all going soft. Mama says the Door-seekers will crack the world open like an egg and we'll all fall out." He said this cheerfully, the way other children reported the weather.

"Mama sounds sensible," Ilsa said.

"She prays to the Grey Lady now. Before the Answering, she prayed to Saint Vessal, but Saint Vessal stopped answering so Mama says he's dead, and you don't pray to dead saints, that's how you get hollowed." He trotted along happily between them. "Are you hollowed? You look a bit hollowed."

"Charming child," Ilsa said through her teeth.

"He's not wrong, though," Emrys said quietly. "About the roads going soft. I can feel it. The bones in my pack have been humming since we crossed."

That was true, and Ilsa had been pretending otherwise. She'd heard it in the night watches — a faint resonant note from Emrys's satchel, like a wineglass touched at its rim. The dead man's fingerbones he carried, and the memorial charms, all of it singing faintly out here where the air itself remembered gods.

The boy tugged Maren's sleeve again. "If you meet the Grey Lady, don't tell her your true name. Papa gave ours away at the harvest rite and now Mama cries whenever anyone says it." He waved and peeled off toward the shrine, and none of them spoke for a quarter mile.

"Well," Maren said finally. "He seems well-adjusted."

"They usually do," Ilsa said. "That's the horror of it. Give a child a god that answers and they'll build their whole soul around the answering. Take the god away later and there's nothing left but the shape of it." She glanced at Maren. "You asked once whether the valley's covenant was cruel. Ask me again sometime whether it wasn't the kindest wall anyone ever built."

Maren didn't answer. But she walked a little closer to Emrys, and Ilsa pretended not to notice that either.

* * *

They reached the crossroads town of Hallowmere on the third evening, and Ilsa hated it immediately, which she took as a good sign. Places she liked were places that lied to her.

Hallowmere had been a market town once; now it was a bazaar of the sacred and the desperate. Stalls sold prayer-ribbons by the yard, bottled dreams, indulgences from nine competing pantheons, and — Ilsa noted with professional interest — maps. Dozens of maps, hanging like flayed skins, most of them wrong, several of them drawn by door-seekers who had never found a door in their lives but had discovered that hope sold better than accuracy.

"We need supplies," she said. "Food, lamp oil, and a map that isn't garbage. Emrys, you have coin?"

"Some. Valley mint won't pass here."

"It'll pass. Everything passes in Hallowmere; that's the problem." She split them up with a jerk of her chin. "Maren, with me. Emrys — buy food, stay visible, talk to no one about bones. Can you manage 'talk to no one about bones'?"

"I once managed an entire winter funeral season without discussing bones," Emrys said with dignity, "except professionally."

"Then this should be restful."

But it wasn't, and Ilsa knew within the hour that it wouldn't be. The trouble announced itself not with violence but with a hymn — a low, rolling chant coming up the main street, and a procession of the Witness-Bound, the sect from the roadside shrine, now swollen to thirty strong. They moved through the market laying hands on stalls, on strangers, on a crying mule, murmuring *be seen, be known*, and the crowd parted for them the way crowds part for anything that might be holy or might be armed, which in Hallowmere amounted to the same caution.

At their center walked the grey-robed woman from the shrine, and her eyes swept the square and snagged — Ilsa felt it physically, like a hook setting — on Maren.

"There," the woman called, voice ringing off the burnt rooftops. "Sisters — see her. See how she walks half-in-shadow. This one has been *touched* and hides it."

Half the market turned to look. Maren froze with the particular helplessness of a woman who has spent her life being respectable and has no script for being accused.

Isla stepped into the space between them. "We're just travelers."

"Travelers." The woman smiled, and it was a good smile, warm as banked coals, which was the worst thing about it. "Child, nothing travels through Hallowmere by accident anymore. The roads themselves are prayers now. And you—" her gaze slid past Ilsa, found Emrys at the provisioner's stall, and lingered on the satchel at his hip, on the shape of it, on the faint hum that Ilsa realized with a lurch was audible to anyone standing close. "And your brother carries the dead. Oh, sisters. Oh, *see* him."

The murmur that went through the crowd was soft and hungry and Ilsa had heard it before, in three towns, always just before things went badly. She put her hand on the knife at her belt — not drawing it, just introducing the concept — and pitched her voice to carry.

"This one's marked," she said loudly. "Grey Lady's own. Anyone wants to handle her business handles mine first."

It worked the way bluff works: completely, and only for a while. The grey woman studied Ilsa's bare forearm — the mark hidden beneath the sleeve — with eyes that had weighed a thousand lies, and Ilsa understood with sinking certainty that she was going to have to show the debt-mark or start a fight she couldn't finish with two civilians to protect.

Before she could choose, a bell rang from the town's watchtower — three strokes, urgent — and a crier's voice cracked across the square:

"Witness-Bound to disperse! Disperse by order of the Toll-Keeper! And — hear this — the Toll-Keeper announces passage south is closed tonight! Closed for weighing! All door-seekers report to the toll-house for marking!"

The crowd groaned, scattered, reformed into knots of argument. In the confusion Ilsa grabbed Maren's elbow, collected Emrys with a look, and steered them both into the shadow of a map-seller's awning.

"Toll-Keeper," Emrys said. "Friend of yours?"

"No. Worse." Ilsa exhaled slowly. "Her name is Vessa Crane. She runs the southern passage, and she's a door-seeker of the old school — she doesn't hunt doors for wonder, she hunts them for tolls. And two years ago, in a town very much like this one, she looked at my debt-mark and told me she knew what god held my paper." Ilsa rubbed her face. "She also knows the Road of Ash ends at a threshold of grey bone. She's been selling maps to it for a decade. Wrong maps. Because Vessa Crane doesn't want anyone reaching the Door of Ash."

"Why not?"

Ilsa looked at the satchel at Emrys's hip, still humming its thin, ghostly note, and felt the whole shape of the next few days settle onto her shoulders like a yoke.

"Because a founder's blood opens it," she said, "and Vessa has been looking for someone with founder's blood for ten years. We just walked into her market square carrying exactly that. And by morning, Emrys, she's going to know it."

Across the square, above the toll-house, a lantern was being lit — and beneath it, watching the map-stalls with the patience of a spider who owns the web, stood a tall woman in a coat stitched all over with small brass keys.
