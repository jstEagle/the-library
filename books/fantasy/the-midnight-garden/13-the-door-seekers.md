## 13. The Door-Seekers

Ilsa had told them the town would be ugly. She had not quite managed to convey *how* ugly.

Ashfall sat in a bowl of grey hills like a coin at the bottom of a beggar's cup — a sprawl of tents and leaning timber houses stitched together with rope and hope, lit by lanterns that burned everything from oil to tallow to, if the rumors were true, rendered prayer-candle wax. The smell hit first: smoke, dung, wet wool, and under it all something sweeter and wronger, like incense left burning over a corpse.

"Charming," Emrys said.

"Don't be sarcastic until you've been here a full hour," Ilsa said. "Sarcasm is a resource. You'll need all of it."

Maren pulled her hood lower. In three days on the Road of Ash she had learned to walk like someone who wanted nothing noticed about her, which was a skill Ilsa privately admired; it had taken Ilsa ten years and two beatings to learn it.

The trouble was that Maren walked like that *now*, but she still looked like a valley woman — clean hands, soft voice, eyes that went to children first and doorways second. Ashfall ate women like that before noon.

"Rules," Ilsa said, stopping them beneath a signpost made from a ship's mast driven into the dirt. Someone had nailed seventeen arrows to it, pointing in seventeen directions, each labeled with a country that no longer existed. One arrow pointed straight down. "One: don't touch anything hanging from a lintel. Two: don't answer anyone who calls you by name unless they're me. Three—"

"You're going to say don't make bargains," Maren said.

"I was going to say don't drink the well water, but yes, also that."

They went down into the town.

* * *

The door-seekers were exactly what their name promised and nothing what strangers expected. They were not mystics or madmen, mostly. They were cartographers of endings — surveyors, tally-keepers, salvagers of thresholds. When a country died, its doors outlived it by days or years, standing open onto nothing, humming. Door-seekers found them, mapped them, sold passage through them to whoever could pay, and fought viciously over the good ones.

And above them all, in a hall built from the ribs of a collapsed cathedral, sat the Tollmother.

Ilsa had met her once, six years ago, when Ilsa had been younger and more desperate and considerably less marked. The woman had aged since then the way rivers age stone.

"She'll know me," Ilsa said, as they climbed toward the hall. "That's not the problem."

"What's the problem?" Emrys asked.

"The problem is what she knows *about* me." Ilsa flexed her left hand inside its glove. Under the leather, the debt-mark lay along her wrist like a vein of dark glass — her god's claim, written into her skin the night it answered her sister's prayer and saved her sister's life and took payment in advance. Every god-touched thing in the world could read it. So, unfortunately, could every door-seeker with a good eye for scripture.

The hall smelled of old incense and older politics. Inside, lanterns hung from the rib-vaults like fruit, and beneath them, tables stretched the length of the nave, crowded with seekers hunched over maps drawn on hide, slate, and in one memorable case, a tablecloth. At the far end, on a chair made from an actual church door laid flat across two barrels, sat the Tollmother.

She was smaller than Ilsa remembered. That somehow made it worse.

"Well," said the Tollmother, as they approached, "the ash has blown something interesting in." Her eyes — pale, unhurried, appraising — moved from Ilsa to Emrys to Maren and back. "Vane. Six years. And you've come up my road without paying toll, which means either you've forgotten our customs or you intend to remind me why I liked you."

"I remember both, Mother."

"Do you now." She rose. She was barely taller standing than sitting, but the hall quieted around her anyway, the way water quiets around a dropped stone. "Show me your wrist."

There it was. No preamble, no pleasantries about the weather. Ilsa had hoped — foolishly, she supposed — that the mark might have faded on the road. It hadn't. It never did. She stripped off the glove and turned her arm over, and the debt-mark caught the lantern light and threw it back dark and deep, like looking down a well.

The Tollmother studied it for a long moment. Around them, the tables had gone quiet. Door-seekers did not see god-marks often. They saw them often enough to know what they meant.

"A god paid you," the Tollmother said. "Or rather, a god *loaned* you, and the note is outstanding." She smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "You know what that makes you, Vane? Not a woman. A collateral instrument. Walking currency."

"I'm aware."

"Are you?" The Tollmother stepped closer. Up close Ilsa could see the fine network of faded marks on the old woman's own throat — debts long since paid, or defaulted, or traded away. A career written on skin. "Because here's my problem, girl. My town is starving. My maps are dying — every threshold within a hundred miles is going dark as the countries behind them finish rotting. And you walk in carrying a live founder of a sealed valley, blood-kin to a murdered god, wearing a debt-mark that any collector would pay a fortune to hold." She spread her small hands. "You've brought treasure into a poor house and asked me to pretend it isn't treasure."

"He's not for sale," Ilsa said.

"No," the Tollmother agreed pleasantly. "*He* isn't. But your *passage* is. And your passage costs his blood."

* * *

They were given a room — a cell, really, in the hall's undercroft, with a door that locked from the outside, because the Tollmother was courteous enough to be honest about holding them.

Emrys sat on the cot with his elbows on his knees. Maren stood at the barred window, watching the lantern-light of the town. Ilsa paced, because sitting still felt like agreeing.

"His blood," Maren said finally. "What does that even mean, practically? They can't mean kill him. He's worth nothing dead."

"They don't want him dead," Ilsa said. "Dead men's blood stops being useful the moment it stops moving. They'd take it slow. Bowls at a time. Enough to weaken him so the mark reads clearly through him — a founder's blood opened up is a key to every lock his family ever sealed." She stopped pacing. "It's how half the dying thresholds got forced open these last years. Somebody's descendant, bled at somebody's ancient door."

"That's horrifying," Maren said.

"Yes."

"And common?"

"Common enough." Ilsa leaned against the wall. "It's the whole economy, Maren. Gods came back, countries started dying, and it turns out the only things that still open doors are bloodlines and debts. People like me — we're just walking keys with legs."

Emrys laughed. Short, surprised, genuine.

"What?" Ilsa demanded.

"You apologize for existing the way I apologize for listening to the dead," he said. "Like it's a personal failing instead of something done to us." He rubbed his jaw. "Here's what I don't understand. Your god saved your sister. That was real — I felt the truth of it on the road. So why does everyone treat the mark like a chain?"

"Because it is one," Ilsa said. "The saving was real. So is the owing. Both things are true at once, and nobody — not the priests, not the seekers, not the gods themselves — will tell you where one ends and the other begins." She heard her own voice go flat and stopped. Gentle humor, she reminded herself. The valley man needed gentle humor or he'd start carving something out of the cot frame. "Besides, there's a practical matter you're both dancing around."

"Which is?" Maren said.

"The Tollmother doesn't actually need to hurt Emrys. She needs *leverage*. And she thinks I'm the lever." Ilsa pushed off the wall. "So we don't give her a lever. We give her a better deal."

Emrys raised an eyebrow. "A better deal than your blood and mine?"

"A map," Ilsa said. "Not a map *of* a door. A map to the hollow country — the erased lands. No seeker has charted it because none of them have come back. If the Tollmother gets the first survey of territory that exists on no other map in the world, that's worth more than your veins. That's a dynasty."

"And how do we draw it?" Emrys asked. "We haven't crossed it yet."

"We will. And I have a memory like a ledger." She tapped her temple. "My mother was a surveyor before the wars. I grew up triangulating distances off church spires and gallows posts. Give me landmarks and I'll give the Tollmother a chart accurate to within a mile, and she can spend twenty years sending fools after it."

"That's dishonest," Maren observed.

"It's *commerce*," Ilsa said. "In Ashfall those are different words entirely."

Emrys was smiling again, that dry, tired smile she was beginning to suspect he rationed like bread. "And if she refuses?"

Ilsa looked at the barred window, at the town beyond it, at the debt-mark she could feel like cold weather under her glove.

"Then tomorrow morning," she said, "we find out what I'm actually worth."

* * *

The Tollmother received her alone, in the small hours, in a room behind the great hall that held nothing but a table, two cups, and a lamp. Negotiation furniture. Ilsa had sat in rooms like it before, though usually on the other side of the table, selling someone else's secret.

"You have a counteroffer," the Tollmother said. It wasn't a question. "Good. I hate wasted mornings."

"A map," Ilsa said. "Of the hollow country. First survey ever drawn. Exclusive to you."

The Tollmother poured wine into both cups without asking. "The hollow country is a rumor."

"The hollow country is the valley's original lands, unmade two centuries ago when its founders bound a god. I've seen its edge. I'm standing in this room because I'm going to cross it." Ilsa kept her hands flat on the table, casual, while her heart hammered somewhere up near her teeth. "No seeker has charted it because everyone who goes in drowns in grief or comes out mad. I have a bone-carver with me who *hears* the dead, Mother. He can walk that ground and stay sane. That's not a rumor. That's a monopoly."

The Tollmother drank. Set the cup down. Looked at Ilsa for a long, silent stretch in which the lamp flame seemed to lean closer to listen.

"Clever," she said at last. "Genuinely clever. Your mother would be proud — I bought charts off her once, you know. Before the wars took her." Something moved behind the pale eyes and was gone. "But here is my difficulty, Vane, and I'll be honest with you because honesty is cheaper than violence. A map is a promise. Blood is a fact. Promises can die in the hollow country along with the people making them." She folded her small hands. "So. I'll take your map *and* the mark."

Ilsa went very still. "The mark isn't mine to give."

"Everything is yours to give. That's what a debt is." The Tollmother's voice stayed gentle, which was the frightening thing about it. "Your god holds paper on you. I hold paper on half the collectors between here and the coast. I can buy your debt, transfer it, sell it onward — or I can burn it outright, and you walk free of your god for the rest of your life. Freedom, Vane. Real freedom, the kind you've never had since the night it wrote on you. That's what I'm offering. In exchange for the founder's blood — bowls of it, taken gently, over weeks — and your mark transferred to me as surety."

The room tilted slightly. Ilsa gripped the table's edge.

Free. The word landed in her chest like a swallowed coal. Six years she had carried the mark, woken to it, planned around it, hated it, hidden it, been defined by it. And here it was, priced. All she had to do was hand over the quiet, stubborn, bone-carving man upstairs who had apologized to *her*, of all people, for the weight of what had been done to him.

She thought of Emrys laughing at his own joke in the cell. She thought of Maren at the window, counting children through the bars. She thought of her sister, alive because of the mark, asleep somewhere safe because of the mark, who would never know what Ilsa was offered in this lamplit room.

And underneath all of it, quieter, colder: she thought about what a debt truly was. What her god had written into her wrist. Whether freedom purchased by selling another person was freedom at all, or just a bigger cage with better light.

"No," Ilsa said.

The Tollmother sighed, genuinely regretful, and reached beneath the table.

"Then I'm sorry, girl," she said. "But at dawn, the founders' blood comes out of him whether you've agreed or not. And you—" the lamp flared as she rose, and her shadow went up the wall enormous, "—you can watch, and learn what your loyalty costs. Or you can stand in front of the knives. I find most people choose watching."

"Most people," Ilsa agreed, "haven't met me."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She spent the night doing arithmetic — guards at the gate, guards at the stairs, the layout of the undercroft, the position of the lamp-oil stores — and near the grey edge of morning she sat down across from Emrys, who was awake too, because of course he was, and told him everything.

He listened the way he listened to the dead, she realized. Completely. Without flinching.

"So," he said at the end. "Dawn."

"Dawn."

He nodded slowly. Then he rolled up his sleeve, bared his forearm, and looked at the blue tracery of his own veins with the detached interest of a man examining a carving blank.

"There's a version of this where I let them take the blood," he said. "Bowls of it, gently, over weeks. Nobody dies. You get your passage, Maren gets her road home eventually, the Tollmother gets her dynasty. It's almost reasonable."

"It's not reasonable," Ilsa said sharply. "Once they've tasted what you are, they'll never stop. You know that. I know that."

"I know that." He lowered his sleeve. "I just wanted to say it out loud, so you'd know I chose the unreasonable thing with my eyes open." He looked at her then, and there was something in his face she'd seen only once before — in the shrine hollow, facing the god. "Whatever you're planning, plan fast. And Ilsa — whatever it costs you—"

"Don't," she said. "Don't do the noble speech. I haven't decided anything yet."

"You decided last night," Emrys said quietly. "In the lamplit room. You just haven't admitted it to yourself."

Outside, somewhere over the grey hills, the first light came up the color of old bone. And beneath the floorboards, faint but unmistakable, Ilsa heard the sound she had dreaded all night: boots. Many of them. Coming down the undercroft stairs.

She stood, and her hand closed over the little knife at her belt — not the one for fighting. The thin one. The one for marks.

"Emrys," she said, "how attached are you to my god's handwriting?"
