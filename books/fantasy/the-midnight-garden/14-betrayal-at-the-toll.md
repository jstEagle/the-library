## 14. Betrayal at the Toll

The town was called Grudge's Rest, which Ilsa thought was the most honest name she'd ever heard.

It sat in a bowl of dead hills like a coin at the bottom of a pocket — three crooked streets, a toll-gate made of wagon axles welded together by someone who had strong feelings and no plan, and a market that sold everything except hope. Door-seekers, god-haunted pilgrims, debt-marked wanderers, and the usual assortment of people who'd run so far from their old lives they'd forgotten what they were running from. Ilsa knew the type. She was the type.

"You're staring," Emrys said quietly.

"I'm assessing."

"You're staring at the man with the ledger."

"I'm assessing the man with the ledger." She kept her voice low. The man in question sat on a stool beneath the toll-gate's central arch, a fat book open on his knees, and he had not looked up once since they entered the town. That was the trick of it. Men who watched you constantly were bluffing. Men who never looked at all already knew everything.

"Who is he?" Maren asked. Her daughter's ribbon was still tied around her wrist; she touched it the way other people touched amulets.

"That's Hobb Undermarch," Ilsa said. "Keeper of the Toll. He doesn't charge coin here. He charges debts. You want passage through his gate toward the Hollow Country, you pay with something you owe. A favor. A secret. A mark." Her hand drifted to her wrist without her permission, where the brand sat under her sleeve like a second pulse. "Or a person."

Emrys went very still beside her. He'd been doing that a lot lately — going still, listening to some frequency Ilsa couldn't hear. In the valley it had been his dead murmuring through bone marrow. Out here, in the lawless country where answered prayers curdled in the air like milk left in the sun, she suspected he was hearing something worse: nothing. Silence where there should have been silence anyway, and finding it strange.

"He charges *people*?" Maren said.

"He charges *founders*," Ilsa corrected, and hated how easily the word came out. "Blood of the nine. There are collectors all over the dying countries paying good silver for founder's blood. Nobody knows why yet. Everybody assumes it opens something."

"And you didn't mention this before we walked into his town because—"

"Because if I'd mentioned it, you'd have turned around, and I need that gate, Thornbury." She met his eyes. They were tired and kind and entirely too trusting for a man whose ancestors had murdered a god, and she found she couldn't hold them. "Because I owed my sister's god a debt, and this was the fastest road to paying it."

That wasn't quite true either, and she knew it. The truer thing — the thing she'd been carrying since the valley's border stones — was that she'd known what Emrys was worth from the moment she'd seen him. Not to her. To the market. And every mile east, the arithmetic had gotten louder in her head: one founder's blood buys passage through three gates, maybe four. One founder's blood clears a debt-mark. One founder's blood buys back the protection of a god who answers prayers, and Ilsa Vane, who had spent six years being nobody's miracle, could be somebody's again.

She was still doing the arithmetic when Hobb Undermarch finally looked up.

* * *

"Well," Hobb said, closing his ledger with the softness of a man setting a trap rather than springing one. "Ilsa Vane. Six years, and you walk into my town wearing another woman's coat and two strays."

"The coat was a gift."

"The strays aren't." His eyes moved past her, unhurried, cataloguing. Emrys with his carver's hands and his careful face. Maren with her healer's posture and her mother's worry. "A bone-carver from behind a covenant wall. A valley-woman. And you." He tapped the ledger once. "Still marked, Vane?"

Ilsa lifted her chin. "Still marked."

"Pity. The god you owe has been generous lately. Answered three prayers in this town last month. People are lining up to sell themselves back into service." He smiled, and it was almost warm, which was how she knew she was in danger. "You could clear your slate today. If you brought me the right trade."

"Don't," Emrys said quietly. Not to Hobb. To her.

She hadn't even looked at him. That was the humiliating part — he'd simply felt it coming off her, the way you feel weather change. He stood there with his ink-stained fingers and his terrible honest eyes and said *don't* as if he already forgave her for considering it, and somehow that was worse than an accusation.

"I don't know what he means," Maren said.

"She does," Emrys said gently. "She's known since the border."

The market noise seemed to recede. Somewhere a child laughed at nothing. Ilsa became aware of her own heartbeat, steady as a debt-collector's knock.

"It's true," she said. Because lying now would be its own kind of sale. "Your blood is worth more than anything I own, Thornbury. Enough to buy my way through every gate between here and the Hollow Country. Enough to burn this mark off my arm and put me back under protection." She swallowed. "I've been turning the sum over in my head for two hundred miles. I want you to know that. I'm not going to pretend I'm better than I am."

Maren stepped back. Emrys didn't.

"And now?" he asked.

"Now—" Ilsa stopped. Here was the thing about debts: they were simple. Debts told you exactly what you owed and to whom, and paid in full meant free. But Emrys had crossed a failing covenant-wall for her. Maren had stitched her shoulder after the toll-fight at the last crossroads and hadn't charged. And her sister — her sister had died whispering a prayer to a god who bothered to listen, and had Ilsa ever once wondered why that mattered? Why being heard was worth more than being safe?

Because she knew what it was to be unheard. Everyone out here did.

"Now I'm tired of arithmetic," she said.

Hobb sighed, genuinely regretful, the way a merchant regrets a customer walking away from a fair price. "Vane. Sweetheart. You don't get to *not* do arithmetic. Not in my town." He raised two fingers, and the market — casually, the way water finds cracks — began to close around them. Big men putting down baskets. A woman climbing down from a cart roof. "Founder's blood is the toll. Either you sell him, or the town takes payment in kind from all three of you, and I promise our rates are less gentle than mine."

"You'd do that?" Maren whispered. "Just take us?"

"It's not personal," Hobb said, and he sounded like he meant it kindly, which was somehow the most frightening thing anyone had said all day. "Nothing here is personal. That's what makes it work."

Isla looked at the closing market. At Emrys, who was already — gods help him — reaching for his knife instead of running. At Maren, who had positioned herself slightly in front of both of them, a healer's instinct turned inside out.

Then Ilsa did the only thing left that wasn't arithmetic.

"Burn it," she said. "Fine. You want payment, Hobb? Here's mine." She shoved up her sleeve, baring the debt-mark — a spiral of grey scar-tissue, six years old, humming faintly with borrowed grace. "But I'm settling it myself."

"You can't—" Hobb started, and then his face changed, because he realized she could. Every debt-marked soul in the dying countries knew the old ugly truth, whispered like a curse: the mark could be burned off. It just took the fire of a vow renounced, and the vow's protection burned with it, and whatever came for you afterward came alone.

"Vane," Hobb said, and for the first time he sounded like a man watching money catch fire. "Don't be stupid. That mark is the only reason your god hasn't collected your sister's interest. Burn it, and you're just meat like the rest of us."

"I've been meat with paperwork," Ilsa said. "Let's try the other thing."

She pulled the stub of candle from her pocket — Maren's, stolen that morning, though she'd tell her later it was borrowed — and pressed the flame to the spiral on her wrist.

It hurt like honesty.

The mark screamed — not her, the mark itself, a thin whistling note like a kettle or a soul — and the smell of it filled the street, hot iron and incense and something sweeter underneath, like the last breath of a prayer. For one instant the air above the market *bent*, as if something vast had turned its head to look at her. Ilsa felt it look. She felt six years of borrowed safety go up in smoke and stink, and underneath it, where the grace had been, there was nothing at all — just Ilsa Vane, unarmored, unclaimed, unloved by any power in heaven or under hill.

And it was, unexpectedly, the freest she had ever felt.

"There," she said, voice shaking, wrist smoking. "Paid. Now open your bloody gate."

* * *

Hobb Undermarch, to his credit, honored the letter of his own rules.

To his discredit, he interpreted them the way merchants interpret everything.

"The toll is paid," he announced to the market at large, spreading his hands, "in spirit! In *sacrifice*, even — very moving, very old-fashioned, I nearly wept. But the *letter*, friends, the letter says founder's blood, and this gentleman is standing right here full of it." He clicked his tongue. "So. Take them."

What followed was less a battle than a very crowded argument conducted with elbows, chair legs, and one memorable fish.

Ilsa fought the way she always had — dirty, fast, and with a running commentary she mostly kept internal. Six years of divine protection had made her lazy; she'd grown used to knives missing her by a finger's width and arrows losing interest mid-flight. Now the world was honest again, and honest meant the big man with the chain hook actually connected, and pain arrived like an old friend who'd let himself in.

"*Left!*" Emrys shouted — and drove the flat of his carving-knife into the elbow of the man swinging at her blind side. It should not have worked. Carving knives are small things, delicate things, made for bone and patience. But Emrys Thornbury had spent his life learning exactly where things broke, and the man folded around his wrist with a sound like green wood.

"You fight?" Ilsa gasped, hauling him backward.

"I carve. It's adjacent." He ducked a bottle. "Also I grew up with Coram. You learn to read swings."

They got ten feet. Fifteen. The crowd was thick but half-hearted — most of these people were scavengers, not soldiers, and there's no profit in a corpse. Then someone threw the fish, which hit Maren square in the chest and startled her into stepping off the cart-edge she'd been using as a vantage point, and she came down hard on the cobbles with a wet crack Ilsa heard over the whole riot.

"Maren!"

"I'm fine—" Maren tried to stand, went white, and sat back down with the particular dignity of the badly injured. "I'm *mostly* fine. Go."

"Nobody's leaving anybody." Ilsa got an arm under her — Maren was lighter than she looked, or the fear was doing its usual work — and Emrys cleared a path ahead of them with surprising, apologetic efficiency, murmuring "*sorry, excuse me, terribly sorry*" to men he was breaking.

They might even have made the gate clean, if Hobb hadn't blocked it personally.

He wasn't armed. He didn't need to be. He simply stood in the gateway with his ledger held against his chest like a shield of scripture, and the crowd slowed around him, because everyone understood what he represented. Rules. Rates. The comfort of knowing the price of everything.

"You burn a six-year mark to avoid selling one man," Hobb said, almost admiring. "Do you know what that costs? Do you know what comes for unmarked souls who travel the hollow roads? There are things out there, Vane, that eat the unprotected first."

"Good," Ilsa panted. Maren was shaking against her side. Emrys's knuckles were bleeding. "Then they'll have to queue behind everything else."

She hit Hobb with the fish. It was, in fairness, a large fish, and grief makes people resourceful.

Then they were through the gate and running, and the town's shouting fell away behind them like a tide going out, and the Road of Ash opened eastward across the dead hills — grey, wide, and empty as an unwritten page.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen milestone, because Maren couldn't walk further and wouldn't say so twice.

Ilsa set the bone — Maren's forearm, cracked clean, not shattered, the healer had assessed it herself with grim professionalism while Ilsa splinted and Emrys hovered uselessly offering water. Now Maren slept, feverish but stable, her daughter's ribbon tied around the splint for reasons she hadn't explained and nobody had asked.

Emrys sat across the small fire, cleaning blood off his carving-knives with a rag, methodical as prayer.

"You should have sold me," he said. No accusation in it. Just the fact, laid on the coals.

"Yes," Ilsa agreed.

He glanced up. Whatever he'd expected, it wasn't agreement. "Most people would argue."

"I'm done arguing with true things. It's exhausting." She fed a stick to the fire. "You want the whole confession? Fine. I ran the numbers every day since the border. Your blood for my freedom. Some days the numbers won. Today they didn't." She turned her bandaged wrist in the firelight — the raw pink spiral where the mark had been, already scabbing into ordinary scar. "You know what I figured out? My sister prayed on her deathbed and got an answer. One answer. And I've spent six years thinking that made her lucky. But she wasn't lucky, Thornbury. She was *heard*. Once. By something enormous. And I've been trying to buy my way back into that ever since — protection, marks, debts, anything so long as somebody powerful was keeping accounts on me." She laughed, short and bitter. "Burning it hurt less than keeping it."

Emrys was quiet a while. The fire popped. Far off, something howled — not a wolf; wolves had been extinct in the hollow lands longer than the valley had existed — and neither of them flinched, because after today, ordinary monsters seemed almost homey.

"In the valley," he said at last, "I used to think my gift was a burden. Hearing the dead. Being the only one." He turned a knife over in his fingers. "Out here, watching people sell each other by weight... I think maybe the burden isn't hearing. It's deciding what to do with it." He looked at her, and there was no judgment in it, which was somehow harder to bear and easier at once. "You chose. Late, loudly, and at ruinous cost. That's more than most manage."

"Flatterer," Ilsa said, and discovered her voice had gone rough. She cleared her throat. "Sleep, Thornbury. I'll take first watch. Unprotected meat watches well; it's got nothing else to rely on."

He smiled and lay back, and within minutes his breathing went slow — though Ilsa noticed he'd arranged his knives within reach first, and slept on his side facing the dark hills, the way people sleep when they've learned the world keeps ledgers too.

She sat watch under unfamiliar stars and did the only arithmetic she trusted anymore: one healer, broken but breathing. One carver, guilty and kind. One door-seeker, freshly ruined, owing nothing to anyone.

Three souls, no protections, heading into the Hollow Country where the famine dead lay thick as ash.

It was near dawn when she saw it — far off on the eastern horizon, where the maps gave up — a light. Not firelight. Not dawn. A pale glow rising from the ground itself, steady as a held breath, in the exact shape of a doorway seen edge-on.

And along the wind, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of thousands of voices mourning — patient, ancient, and waiting for someone who could hear them.

Ilsa stood, heart hammering, and woke Emrys with a hand on his shoulder.

"We're close," she said. "And Thornbury — I think they already know your name."
