## 15. The Hollow Country

There is a particular silence that comes from a place that was never supposed to exist, and Emrys heard it before he saw it.

They had been walking for two days past the border stones — Ilsa ahead with her map that wasn't a map but a strip of bark scored with lines only she could read, Maren behind him with her pack straps cinched too tight because she refused to let anything ride loose — when the sound of the world simply stopped. Not quiet. Quiet still had edges to it, the tick of a beetle in dead grass, the whisper of wind finding its way through a coat seam. This was the absence of edges.

"Stop," Emrys said.

Ilsa held up a hand anyway, as though she'd felt it first. She probably had. "We're here."

"Where's here?" Maren asked. "There's nothing here."

"There isn't," Ilsa agreed, with the cheerful grimness of someone who found bad news nourishing. "That's rather the point. Welcome to the Hollow Country. Population: none. Ever. Officially." She tapped the bark map. "No kingdom claims it. No cartographer has ever drawn it. Every road that should cross it bends around it, and every traveler who tries to walk straight through it finds themselves arriving somewhere else entirely, and remembering it wrong afterward."

"And us?" Maren said.

"We're not travelers," Ilsa said. "We're carrying something the country recognizes." She glanced at Emrys, and her look was not unkind, which from Ilsa Vane was nearly alarming. "Him. What he hears. It'll let us in because he's already half inside it."

Emrys did not care for that sentence at all.

The land itself looked ordinary enough at first glance — grey-brown hills, a dry streambed, the skeletons of trees that might have been dead a season or a century. But nothing grew *over* anything else. No moss on stones. No lichen climbing bark. The world here had been interrupted mid-sentence and never finished, like a letter left half-written by someone who would never come back to the desk.

And under all of it — under the hills and the dry water and his own boots pressing into soil that gave back no sound — Emrys could feel them.

Not one voice. Not the familiar murmurs of the valley dead, who spoke through marrow and memory, one soul at a time, patient as weather. This was different. This was a crowd. A congregation. Thousands of voices layered so tightly they made not words but a pressure, like standing at the bottom of a lake and feeling the whole weight of the water above you, waiting.

He had spent his whole life believing himself alone with the dead.

He had never once imagined there were this many of them.

* * *

They made camp that first night in the lee of a hill that had no name because names were things countries gave their places, and this place belonged to no country. Ilsa built a fire small enough to hide in a teacup and cooked something involving dried fish and an amount of pepper she described as "medicinal."

Maren ate it without complaint, which told Emrys more about her mood than any confession could have. His neighbor was a woman who complained about salt the way other people complained about taxes — thoroughly, cheerfully, and at length. Tonight she ate in silence and watched the dark between the dead trees.

"They're out there," she said finally. "Aren't they. You can hear them."

Emrys set down his bowl. "Yes."

"How many?"

"I don't know." He rubbed his sternum, where the heirloom charm hung against his skin. It had been warm since the shrine; now it was warm as a sleeping bird, and it pulsed faintly, in time, he realized with a lurch, with the pressure of the voices. As if the bone remembered where it came from. "More than the valley has people. More than the valley has ever had people."

Ilsa looked up sharply. "That many?"

"The famine winter," Emrys said quietly. "The ledger said nine founders. It didn't say how many died while nine people decided what to do about it."

The fire crackled its tiny medicinal crackle. Somewhere far off — or perhaps not far off at all, distance being unreliable here — something vast shifted its weight.

"Sleep in shifts," Ilsa said. "And Emrys — if you start hearing things, you wake me. Don't be brave about it. Bravery out here gets people eaten by geography."

"That's not a real thing," Maren said.

"You're walking through a place that doesn't exist," Ilsa said. "Everything's a real thing. That's the problem."

* * *

It happened on the second day, at the bottom of a valley within the hollow country — a fold in the land shaped almost exactly like home, which Emrys understood, with a cold settling in his chest, was not coincidence at all. This *was* home. Or had been. Before the covenant, before the binding, before nine frightened founders drew a circle around a stolen god and called the theft a sanctuary. These were the valley's original lands, and the god's imprisonment had unmade them, unwritten them, erased every field and fencepost and grave.

Which meant the graves were erased too.

But the dead were not.

Emrys felt it come like weather — like the storm-front rolling off the mountains in autumn, visible for hours before it arrives, giving you just enough time to decide whether to run or stand and take it. He stood. He would always, he suspected, stand. It was his single flaw and his entire profession.

The voices resolved.

Not gradually. All at once, the way a chord resolves, thousands of murmurs snapping into distinctness — and oh, they were so *ordinary*. That was the horror of it. He had braced for wailing, for fury, for the shrieking of the murdered, and instead he heard:

*— the turnips froze in the ground, I told him, I told him we should dig them early —*

*— if the miller's boy takes bread again I'll not report him, let the child eat —*

*— my hands hurt. Why do my hands hurt? I'm not even—*

*— Mama said spring. Mama promised spring.*

*— I buried three of mine under the north elm and then I couldn't remember why the elm looked wrong until I remembered there were no elms anymore—*

A woman counting her remaining children aloud, over and over, because counting was the only prayer she trusted. An old man reciting the names of his neighbors who had gone into the winter strong and come out of it as entries in a ledger. Children asking questions. So many children, asking questions that had waited two hundred years for an answer, patient as stones, certain as stones, and Emrys stood in the middle of it with his hand pressed flat over the warm charm and felt the grief arrive like floodwater.

It didn't ask permission. Grief never does.

It came up through his boot-soles and into the bones of his legs, and he thought distantly, *so this is why the carvers' guild taught grounding exercises*, and then he stopped thinking in words at all.

He went under.

Not drowning as such — there was no water — but the sensation was exactly that of being pulled beneath a river in flood: the roar, the tumbling, the terrible intimacy of it, thousands of voices pouring through him as though he were not a man but a channel cut precisely to their shape. Because he was. Because the bone-carver's gift had never been his alone; it was a thread running back through his family line to the founding, to the binding, to the bargain struck in blood and bone-dust — and these dead, the unmade dead, the ones whose graves had been deleted along with everything else, recognized the thread. They knew what he carried in his blood. They had been waiting two hundred years for anyone from the nine families to walk back into the hollow and *listen.*

*You hear us,* they said, in ten thousand overlapping mouths, gentle as snowfall. *At last. At last. At last.*

He heard the famine from inside it. A mother boiling leather for soup and apologizing to the child watching her, apologizing as though hunger were her personal failure. A farmer leaving his own ration at a neighbor's door and lying to his wife about where it went. A priest — no, older than any priest Emrys knew, a keeper of the old shrine — praying desperately to the god below for mercy and receiving it, receiving grain from empty storehouses, receiving warmth in the killing cold, and then the nine coming to the shrine with rope and iron and bone-dust, and the keeper begging them to stop, *he answers, can't you feel it, he answers*—

*—and they bound him anyway. They bound the answering. They chose a closed door over an open hand, because an open hand might someday close, and a closed door asks nothing of anyone.*

Emrys came back to himself on his knees in the dry grass, nose bleeding freely, Maren gripping his shoulders and shouting his name, Ilsa crouched nearby with a knife drawn and pointed at nothing, at everything, at the air.

"He's breathing," Maren was saying, to herself or to Ilsa. "He's breathing. Emrys. Emrys, look at me."

"I'm looking," he managed. His voice came out scraped hollow. "I'm fine."

"You are transparently not fine."

"No," he admitted. He wiped his nose on his sleeve and looked at the smear of red, absurdly bright against the grey country. "But I'm *here*, and that's more than most people get to say about this place."

* * *

They rested through what passed for afternoon. Ilsa insisted. Even she had gone pale around the mouth, and Ilsa Vane going pale around the mouth was roughly equivalent to another person taking to their bed with a wet cloth over their eyes.

When Emrys could speak without his voice shaking, he told them what he'd seen. All of it. The leather soup. The farmer's lie of charity. The keeper at the shrine. The binding.

"So it wasn't just murder," Maren said slowly. Her hands were wrapped around a cup of nothing-hot tea she kept drinking anyway, for the ritual of it. "It was — they were given help. The god was helping them. And they bound him for it."

"They bound him because he was helping them," Ilsa corrected softly. She was sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening, which Emrys had learned was how she prayed. "Think like a frightened founder. If the god feeds you this winter, you owe him next winter, and the winter after that. Gratitude is a leash you put on yourself. Better to bind the hand that feeds you than live beholden to it. Cruel arithmetic. But arithmetic."

"It's cowardice wearing arithmetic's coat," Maren said.

"Yes," Ilsa said. "Also that. The two wear the same coat more often than anyone likes to admit."

Emrys stared out across the hollow hills. Somewhere among them, under them, through them, ten thousand souls sat in the unmade ruins of their lives and waited. Not screaming. That was somehow worse. Screaming you could run from. Patience you had to carry.

"When the covenant breaks," he said, "when the valley starts to come apart like this place did — those people will be there too. Everyone the valley ever lost. Everyone the founders' crime touched, directly or downstream, two hundred years of deaths, all of them caught inside the circle when it closes." He turned to look at his companions, and heard his own voice go quiet and level, the voice he used for telling families the thing they already knew. "I felt the scale of it today. I need you both to understand: it is bigger than us. It is maybe bigger than anything. And we are going to have to carry it anyway."

Nobody answered for a long moment. The dead trees stood in their patient rows.

"Well," Ilsa said at last, sheathing her knife. "Good news is I've been carrying impossible debts since I was nineteen. Bad news is I've never once figured out how to put one down." She jerked her chin eastward, toward the low line of hills. "Two more days, if the country lets us. The Door's in the heart of the hollow — of course it is, thresholds always nest inside each other like spoons."

Maren reached over and squeezed Emrys's hand once, hard, the way she did when words would only make things worse, and Emrys squeezed back.

They walked on.

And the dead of the hollow country walked with them — not clinging, not dragging, just *present*, a vast soft congregation keeping pace, and Emrys understood with the last of his strength that day that they were not following him.

They were leading him somewhere.

By evening, the hills ahead had begun to change. The grey of the unmade land gave way to something paler, finer, catching the dying light like the inside of a shell. Bone-white stone, miles of it, rising from the earth in shapes that were almost architecture.

And among the pale stones, small and sharp against the sky, Emrys saw figures moving — and heard Maren's breath catch beside him, and heard her say, very quietly, in a voice he had never heard her use before:

"Oh no. Oh, no, no — they moved the rite. They must have moved it forward, Coram must have—" Her hand flew to her mouth. "My daughter. Emrys. My daughter was marked, and I never told you, and they're going to start without us."
