## 16. Maren's Confession

Maren had learned long ago that a lie told to yourself was still a lie, no matter how many times you watered it.

She stood at the edge of the hollow country with her hands buried in her apron pockets and watched Emrys kneel in the grey grass, listening to the dead of a land that no longer existed. His shoulders were bowed the way a man's shoulders bow when he has been carrying water for years and has forgotten that arms are allowed to hang empty. He had been down there among the famine-dead for most of the morning. Each time he rose, he looked older — not tired, exactly, but *carved*, as if the grief were working him like bone and taking away everything that wasn't necessary.

Ilsa sat apart on a broken milestone, sharpening a knife she never seemed to need to sharpen. She glanced up when Maren approached, read her face in one look, and — mercifully — said nothing. She just moved her pack over and made room on the stone.

Maren did not sit.

"You should hear what they're saying," she said instead, to fill the silence. "The dead here. When the wind comes through." It was true; even she could catch fragments now, thin as smoke, though only Emrys heard whole sentences. "Some of them sound angry. Some of them sound like my mother after a harvest festival, trying to remember where she put her shoes."

That got the ghost of a smile out of Emrys. Good. She needed him soft for this. She hated herself for needing that, and she did it anyway, because that was the arithmetic mothers lived by: you spent whatever coin you had.

"Emrys," she said.

He looked up. The wind moved through the grey grass behind him, carrying its thousand whispers, and his eyes went distant for a heartbeat before he dragged them back to her. "What is it?"

"There's something I've been keeping."

* * *

It had started, as most terrible things do, with an ordinary evening.

The moot had come to Maren's house on a Tuesday, which she would always remember because Tuesdays were bread days and Elder Coram had gotten flour on the hem of his black coat and pretended not to notice. There were four of them, which was two more than courtesy required and three more than honesty ever wore. They'd sat in her kitchen, under the dried herbs, and drunk her tea, and Coram had said, "Serra is eleven now?"

"She'll be twelve at midwinter."

"The rite requires a child who hasn't flowered," said one of the others — Hesta Quill, whose family name was also on that ledger Emrys kept quoting like scripture. "It always has."

Maren had set down the teapot very carefully, because if she set it down carelessly she would have thrown it. "The rite," she said, "requires livestock. A bull calf, a brace of geese. That's what the old books say. My grandmother attended one."

"The old books say blood freely given from the founder lines," Coram said. "Livestock was always a substitute, Maren. You know that. Everyone knows it, deep down, the way you know the cellar is cold before you open the door." He folded his flour-dusted hands. "The covenant is failing. The god is waking whether we feed it geese or not. We believe — the moot believes — that it wants recognition. Kinship. A descendant of the nine, offered back."

"And Serra is descended from how many of them?" Hesta Quill asked, with the gentle precision of a woman counting coins. "Three lines, isn't it? Quill, Vasser, and Thornbury besides. Through your late husband." She smiled as if this were good news. "No child in the valley is better suited."

*Suited.* Like a glove. Like a shoe.

Maren had said yes. That was the part she could hardly forgive. Coram had laid it all out — the failing crops, the thinning border, the birds crossing where nothing should — and then he had said, quietly, "Or we can wait, and hope, and if the covenant breaks with no offering at all, the god takes what it likes," and Maren had heard her daughter's name in every word he didn't say, and she had said, "When?"

"After the next full moon," Coram said. "There will be a ceremony. It won't hurt her. I give you my word as a Thornbury."

*A Thornbury.* Emrys's uncle. And Maren had nodded, and shaken their hands, and walked them to the gate, and gone inside and kissed her sleeping daughter's hair, and then sat on the kitchen floor until dawn because her legs had decided lying down was the only honest posture left.

She hadn't told Emrys. Not in the moot-hall, not on the road, not in any of the hundred moments since when he'd looked at her and asked, kindly, if she was eating enough. Because telling him meant admitting she had agreed. And worse — far worse — it meant asking him to choose between a god and a girl, between justice and a child who liked to braid flowers into the mane of a horse that bit everyone else.

* * *

"—and so the moot moved it forward," Maren finished. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Not after the full moon. Before. Three days from now, at the shrine. Coram says the border is thinning faster than the omens promised. He says there's no time left to be careful." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "*Careful.* As if any of this has ever been careful."

Emrys had risen to his feet somewhere in the middle of it. Now he stood very still among the grey grass, and the wind whispered around his knees, and his face was doing something complicated that Maren couldn't read — grief and fury and something underneath both that might have been fear.

"Three days," he said.

"They'll have taken her to the moot-hall already. Kept apart. Fed well. They're kind about it, Emrys, that's the worst part, they're *gentle*, they've all convinced themselves—" Her voice finally broke. She let it. "I signed her death warrant with a nod. I stood in my own kitchen and nodded."

"Maren." He crossed to her and took her hands, and his palms were rough with bone-dust and cold from kneeling in the hollow grass. "You were cornered. They designed it that way. A choice with no right answer isn't consent, it's a trap with better manners."

"It doesn't feel like better manners at three in the morning."

"No," he said softly. "It wouldn't."

From the milestone, Ilsa spoke without looking up from her knife. "So the moot's moved the rite ahead of us reaching the Door. Clever bastards. Or frightened ones. Usually both." She sheathed the blade and stood. "Question is what we do with three days, and I want to say clearly, before anyone gets noble at me: the Door is two days' walk past the worst ground in this entire country, and opening it is the only thing that ends this permanently. For everyone. Every valley, every village, every soul currently being prayed at by gods who've remembered they exist."

"I know what the Door does," Maren said. "I'm not stupid, Ilsa Vane."

"I didn't say you were. I said someone's about to get noble, and it's usually the person standing closest to me when things get difficult."

But Emrys wasn't looking at Ilsa. He was looking at Maren, and his expression had settled into something quiet and terrible, and she knew — she knew the way she'd known in her kitchen, the way she knew weather coming through a bad tooth — what he was going to say before he said it.

"I can't leave her," he said.

* * *

They argued for an hour, or argued adjacent to arguing, which amounted to the same thing with better grammar.

Ilsa laid it out flat: the Door, the timing, the mathematics of it. Two days to reach it. One day, if they killed the horses, and the horses were the only reason they'd survive the Road of Ash at all. If Emrys turned back alone — and he couldn't go back with them, not fast enough, not through the hollow country — he'd arrive with hours to spare and no plan and no leverage, and the moot would simply lock him up, because they'd been waiting for him to come back since the day he ran.

"If you go back," Ilsa said, "you trade the whole valley for one girl. And the moot will take her anyway, Emrys. You think Coram hesitates? He's been rehearsing this sacrifice his whole life. You'd be handing them their founder's blood *volunteering itself*, which is the best thing that could possibly happen to their story."

"Then I'll go back and I won't be caught."

"You're a bone-carver."

"I'm a founder's blood," Emrys said, "which turns out to be worth considerably more than a knife."

Maren stood between them with her heart going like a bird in a shuttered room, and understood suddenly that she had done this — that her confession had set these two against each other like weights on a scale, her daughter on one side and a nation of ghosts on the other, and whichever way it tipped, someone would spend the rest of their life staring at the bottom of the pan.

And that was when she understood something else, something that made her sick and free in the same breath.

"It doesn't have to be either," she said.

They both turned.

"Serra," Maren said, and her voice shook but held, "is my daughter. Mine. Not the moot's. Not the ledger's. Not the god's." She pulled the cord from around her neck — she'd worn it hidden all these weeks, a little carved charm, a sparrow Emrys had made her years ago from the wing-bone of a bird that died of old age, back when his carvings were gifts instead of memorials. "This is founder-line bone. Thornbury work, from your own hand, given freely to me. Blood freely given from the founder lines — isn't that what Coram said the rite requires?"

Emrys went very still. "Maren. What are you saying."

"I'm saying the rite wants kinship. Recognition. A piece of the nine offered back." She closed her hand around the sparrow until the edges bit her palm. "It doesn't say the piece has to breathe. And it certainly doesn't say the piece has to be *given by the people holding the knife*." She looked at Emrys, and felt the tears come and let them, because hiding them had never once worked. "You could make it. Something of yours. Given freely — truly freely, by a founder's line, carried by someone who loves her. You could unmake their rite from the inside, Emrys. Give the god a gift instead of a hostage."

For a long moment nobody spoke. The wind moved through the hollow grass, and among all its thousand whispers, Maren would have sworn some of them had stopped to listen too.

"That's either brilliant," Ilsa said slowly, "or it's the kind of thing that makes gods curious, and I have personally never once benefited from making a god curious."

"I don't know if it works," Emrys said. His voice was strange — hoarse, wondering. "I don't know what the god wants anymore. It showed me the famine. It showed me its own starving. It asked to be *remembered*, Maren. Not fed." He pressed his hand flat against his chest, where — she'd noticed it for days now — he sometimes flinched as if something knocked from the inside. "But I know bone. And I know that a gift freely given weighs different than a life taken. Any god worth the name can tell the difference. Maybe that's the whole point. Maybe it always has been."

"Then you'll do it?" Maren grabbed his sleeve before he answered, hating the begging in it and unable to stop it. "You'll carve it tonight, and I'll carry it back myself—"

"You're not going back alone."

"—and I'll put it in her hand before the moot ever gets her near that shrine—"

"*Maren.*"

"—because I have done the arithmetic, Emrys, I have done it every night since my kitchen floor, and I am telling you that I would burn the Door, the god, the covenant, and every ghost in this grey grass down to the last whisper before I let them touch one hair on that girl's head, and if that makes me small, then let the historians write *small* on my grave—"

"Maren." He took her by the shoulders, gently, the way he handled green bone. "Yes. I'll carve it. Tonight, by firelight, with everything I have left in me. But you're not carrying it back alone, and I'm not letting you walk into that moot-hall to play smuggler." He exhaled, long and slow, and when he looked up there was something new in his face — resolve, but also a strange relief, as if she'd handed him permission for a thing he'd wanted to do since the day they left. "Because I've been thinking, these last miles, about what the god actually asked me for. To be remembered. Carried out. And I keep wondering—" he touched his chest again, and winced, "—whether it's already chosen how."

Behind him, the wind rose through the hollow country, and this time the whispers weren't fragments. This time, faint and vast and patient as roots under stone, they were all saying the same thing, and even Maren could hear it:

*Three days. Come home, bone-carver. Come home.*

Isla was already saddling the horses.
