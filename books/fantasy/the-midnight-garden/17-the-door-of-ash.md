## 17. The Door of Ash

The hollow country did not want them to arrive anywhere.

Emrys had learned this over three days of walking through land that unmade itself behind them like a tide going out. Paths they had crossed in the morning were gone by noon—not washed away, not overgrown, simply *absent*, as if the ground had forgotten it had ever been asked to hold their weight. Ilsa navigated by debt-marks and dead stars. Maren navigated by stubbornness. Emrys navigated by listening to the bones in his satchel, which murmured directions the way drunk men give them: *left at the weeping place, no, right, right at the second weeping place.*

"Your bones are lost," Ilsa said, watching him turn the satchel upside down and shake it gently, as one might shake a nephew.

"My bones are *grieving*," Emrys corrected. "It's different. Grief gets lost all the time."

They crested a ridge of grey dust at midday—the only midday the hollow country seemed willing to offer, its sun a pale coin seen through smoke—and there it was.

The Door of Ash.

Emrys had expected, without admitting he'd expected it, something grand. A gate. A wall. A monument with the founders' names carved in self-satisfied rows. Instead it stood alone in a basin of white dust, and it was small—smaller than his workshop door, smaller than Maren's kitchen doorway, a threshold of grey bone rising perhaps eight feet from the ash with no frame around it and nothing beyond it but more hollow country, visible straight through the empty middle as though someone had cut a doorway out of the air and forgotten to hang anything in it.

Except it wasn't empty. When Emrys looked directly at the opening, his eyes slid off it, the way a finger slides off wet glass.

"Well," said Ilsa. "That's either it or the world's most elaborate disappointment."

"It's it." Emrys's mouth had gone dry. He could feel the Door the way he felt the shrine back home—a pressure in the marrow, a hum beneath hearing. But where the shrine's hum had always felt like a held breath, this was... open. Waiting. A lung ready to exhale after two hundred years.

Maren touched the edge of the bone frame. She snatched her hand back. "It's warm."

"Everything here is warm," Ilsa said. "The whole country's been bleeding heat for centuries. That's what unmaking looks like from the inside, probably. Cozy, right up until you stop."

But Maren was shaking her head slowly, and Emrys stepped forward and laid his own palm against the grey bone, and understood.

It wasn't the hollow country's warmth. It was *familiar* warmth. It was the warmth of a charm fresh from the knife, of a memorial bone an hour finished, of marrow that remembered being alive. The Door was made of bone that had once belonged to something living, and whatever it had been, it had loved the ones who built this threshold—or forgiven them, which in Emrys's experience was rarer and cost more.

"I know this feeling," he said quietly. "I've carved a thousand pieces of bone and never felt it come back like this."

"Then knock," Ilsa said. "Or whatever you do. You're the founder's blood. It answers to you, not us."

Emrys did not move. Behind them, far off across the ash, the dead were gathering—he had heard them since dawn, thousands of famine-voices drawn toward the Door like iron filings toward a lodestone, patient and terrible and *hopeful*, which was the worst part. The dead of the hollow country had not hoped for anything in two hundred years.

"Maren," he said. "When we came over the ridge. Did you see anyone else?"

She frowned. "No one. Why?"

"Because I did." He turned to face them both, and found that saying it aloud made it worse, so he said it anyway. "Standing in front of the Door. Nine figures. They didn't look at me. They looked at the opening, and they were waiting too, and they looked—" he searched for the word, hating each candidate— "*ashamed.*"

* * *

They made camp within sight of the Door, because none of them could quite bear to leave it and none of them could quite bear to approach it again. Ilsa built a fire from ash-wood she swore was safe, "mostly," and Maren produced the last of the hard bread and divided it into three unequal portions with a mother's flawless arithmetic: largest for Emrys, who had done the most walking and the least eating; medium for herself; smallest for Ilsa, who complained about it professionally and ate every crumb.

It was while the fire crackled its thin, apologetic crackle that the truth arrived.

It came through the bones, of course. It always came through the bones. Emrys had his satchel open across his knees, sorting his charms by weight—an old habit, a carver's fidget—when the memorial bones began to whisper all at once, and for once the whispers agreed with one another, which frightened him more than any argument among the dead ever had.

"They're telling me something," he said.

Ilsa looked up. "Telling you or warning you? There's a difference. My god used to do both, usually in the same sentence."

"The Door isn't just a way out." Emrys's hands had stopped moving. He watched them as though they belonged to a calmer man. "It's the *lock*. The founders didn't build a prison and then build a separate exit. The binding runs through the threshold itself. The covenant, the border stones, the valley—all of it is anchored here. This is the keystone."

"So we open it, the god walks free, we walk out. That was always the plan."

"And the valley?"

Ilsa paused. In the firelight her face did something complicated. "What about the valley?"

Emrys lifted one of the memorial bones—a child's femur, polished smooth by two hundred years of other hands before his. "The dead are showing me. The founders bound the god *into* the covenant. Its strength holds the walls; its patience holds the fields; its memory holds the names. Every prayer the valley ever thought went unanswered went into keeping it fed enough to keep the borders standing. And when the Door opens—" His voice caught. He made himself finish, because Maren deserved to hear it from him first, plainly, the way he told families about their drowned children. "When the Door opens, the god leaves, and everything it held up comes down. The valley won't be destroyed. It will be *unmade*. Like this country. Like the lands that were erased when they did the binding. Every field, every house, every grave. Gone from the earth and gone from memory, unless—"

"Unless what?" Maren's voice was very steady, which is how Emrys knew she was holding herself together with both hands.

"Unless the memories are carried out." He looked down at his own arms, at the fine white scars along his forearms where he had tested his knives over the years, and felt suddenly certain of things he did not want to be certain of. "Living memory. Someone who remembers must walk out carrying the dead—every name, every face, every grief the valley has ever set down in bone. Two centuries of them. If no one carries them, the people themselves will pass through the Door and forget they ever lived there. Forget the dead entirely. Forget—" he swallowed— "forget their children. Forget their parents. The covenant doesn't just hold the god, Maren. It holds the *record*. Break the lock and the book burns."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark ash, the famine-dead murmured closer, drawn by the words, drinking them in.

"That's why it's called the Door of Ash," Ilsa said softly. "Not because it stands in ash. Because of what it makes."

* * *

Emrys did not sleep. He sat apart from the others with his satchel in his lap and did the arithmetic he had spent his whole life avoiding.

One carver. One pair of hands. Two hundred years of the dead.

He had carried grief his entire life—one voice at a time, one family at a time, the murmur through the marrow that had made him strange and necessary and desperately lonely. He had thought that was the burden. He had thought himself, some bitter nights, the most put-upon man in the valley, the sole mourner of a whole country, doing work no one thanked him for because no one else could bear to know it existed.

And now he knew the true shape of it. To carry out the valley's dead in living memory meant taking them *into himself*—not hearing them through the bones but holding them, name by name, face by face, grief by grief, until he reached the far side of the Door. Thousands of voices. Perhaps tens of thousands. Every soul the bone-carvers' guild had ever served, every soul the guild had served *before* the guild, back to the famine winter itself, back to the nine founders kneeling in the snow with their knives and their guilt.

He thought of the hollow country around him, and understood at last why it was empty. Someone had been meant to carry these dead out too. No one had. So the land forgot, and the dead remained, and hope curdled into haunting.

"You're doing your mother's face," Maren said.

He started. She had come to sit beside him without a sound, wrapped in her shawl, her breath fogging in the cold.

"What?"

"When you're deciding whether you can survive something. You get a look like you're carving her. I used to see it on my husband, before the fever. Men think they grieve quietly." She settled beside him and pulled half his blanket over her knees without asking, because Maren. "Tell me the worst of it. Not the part you've already said. The part underneath."

Emrys looked at the fire, at Ilsa asleep sitting up against her pack with her knife still in her hand, at the pale outline of the Door against the starless sky.

"The part underneath is that I don't know if I can hold them all and stay myself." He said it plainly, because lying to Maren was like lying to a river. "The dead in the hollow country nearly took me under, and those are strangers. These would be mine. My neighbors. The child I carved last spring. My mother. My father. Coram, someday, though the saints preserve me from carrying *him*." He heard Maren huff despite herself, and was grateful for it. "If I take them all in and the weight breaks me halfway across the threshold, then everyone forgets anyway and I'll have opened the Door for nothing. If I refuse to open it, the moot keeps sacrificing, the covenant keeps thinning, and the valley dies ugly instead of vanishing clean. There's no version where nobody pays."

"There never is," Maren said. "That's what they don't tell you about love. Everybody sells it as a shelter. It's actually a bill." She was quiet a moment, turning something over, and Emrys saw her decide to say it. "My daughter's name is Wren. Have I told you that?"

"You have. Twice. She likes frogs."

"She *loves* frogs. She catches them and lectures them and lets them go." Maren smiled at the fire, and the smile broke his heart with its precision. "If we go home—if any of this ends with us walking back through the border—I need you to remember her frogs. Whatever else burns. Whatever the price turns out to be. Remember Wren and the frogs."

"I remember everything," Emrys said. "That's the whole curse of me."

"No, carver." Maren patted his knee, twice, the way she'd pat a good loaf. "That's the whole *cure*. We just haven't gotten to that part yet."

* * *

Near dawn, the Door spoke.

It did not use words. It did not need to. Emrys felt it the moment he woke—or rather, the moment he realized he had never slept—a great slow attention turning toward him through eight feet of grey bone, vast and starved and gentle the way a starving thing is gentle, and beneath it, threaded through it, the familiar presence from the shrine. The bound god. Reaching through its own prison to touch the lock.

And the dead of the hollow country began to sing.

Not the murmuring, not the whispering—singing, thousands of famine-throats finding a note they had not managed in two centuries, and Emrys pressed his palms over his ears and it did nothing, because the song was not in the air. It was in the marrow. It was in *his* marrow, in every charm in his satchel, in the founder's blood running thin and dutiful through his veins, and the meaning needed no translation:

*Come. Open. Carry. Before it is too late.*

Ilsa was already on her feet, staring east across the ash, and her face was grey as the Door. "Emrys," she said. "How fast can you carve a message?"

"Why?" But he was already reaching for his knife.

"Because the wind changed in the night, and I know that smell, and that smell is *blood*—a lot of it, old stone, burning fat. Your uncle's moot isn't waiting for you to come home." Her eyes found his, and there was no mockery left in them at all. "The sacrifice has already begun."
