## 18. The Sacrifice Interrupted

Emrys had never run like this. Not when the flood took the mill bridge and old Tam was caught on the wrong side of it. Not the night the fever came through the low farms and he'd carved forty-one names in a single week, his hands bleeding from the knife. He ran now the way animals run, thoughtlessly, his lungs burning, the Road of Ash crumbling behind him as if the hollow country itself were trying to hold his ankles.

Behind him: Ilsa's voice, hoarse with shouting, telling him he was a fool, that they could still reach the Door together, that one girl against a whole valley—

He did not stop. Some arithmetic does not permit debate.

Ahead of him, past the flickering border stones and the thinning fields, the shrine glowed on the hill like a wound that had finally decided to open.

* * *

He came over the last rise at dusk, and the sight stopped him so hard he nearly fell.

The moot had gathered every soul in the valley. Torches ringed the shrine in a circle of orange light, and inside that circle stood Coram — tall, grey-bearded, wearing the founder's stole that Emrys hadn't seen since his grandfather's funeral — and beside him, on the altar stone, sat Wren.

Maren's daughter was nine years old. She had a gap where her front tooth used to be, and she used to come to Emrys's workshop to ask if bones tickled when you carved them, and once she'd brought him a dead sparrow and demanded he make it into a charm that would let her fly. He had told her, gravely, that flight charms only worked on people who were very good at falling, and she had spent an entire summer practicing falls off the woodshed roof until Maren caught her and confiscated both the charm and the child.

That was the girl on the altar. That was the girl the moot had decided to feed to their guilt.

Emrys's legs carried him down the slope before his mind agreed to anything.

"—in accordance with the covenant," Coram was saying, his voice carrying the flat steadiness of a man reading words he has rehearsed until they no longer mean anything, "the nine lines give freely, that the bound one stays bound, that the valley endures—"

"The valley is *dying*, Uncle."

Every torch-flame seemed to flinch. Heads turned. Emrys walked into the circle of light with ash to his knees and blood on his palms from the road's broken stones, and three hundred people looked at him the way people look at weather they've been pretending isn't coming.

Coram lowered the ledger. "Emrys." Just his name, but it carried a whole argument in it: *not here, not now, not in front of them.*

"You're going to cut a child," Emrys said. "You're going to do it in front of her mother's empty house because you couldn't even find the courage to tell Maren what day you'd chosen. And you think that will hold the border? Uncle, I've stood at the stones. The wall isn't leaking because we've been stingy with blood. It's failing because what's underneath it is waking up, and no amount of—" he gestured at the altar, at the knife, at the whole machinery of it "—no amount of *this* will put it back to sleep. It doesn't want blood. It never wanted blood."

"Then what does it want?" someone shouted from the crowd.

"To be remembered," Emrys said. "And we have two hundred years of forgetting to answer for."

* * *

It went badly after that, in the way such things go. There were shouts about traitors and madmen, about the Thornbury name and what it owed. Old Hesta Pell threw a turnip at him, which under other circumstances would have been funny, and which he would remember later, absurdly, with something like fondness. Coram ordered him seized, and four men who had known him all his life — men whose fathers' bones he had carved, whose mothers' last words he had carried — took his arms and held him while Coram turned back to the altar.

"I'm sorry, boy," Coram said quietly, and the terrible thing was that Emrys believed him. His uncle's face was not cruel. It was the face of a man standing in a flooding cellar deciding which heirloom to save. "You've read the ledger same as me. Our line volunteered. We chose this burden so that others' children might live. If the price must be paid again, then it must, and I will carry the shame of it to my grave so that no one else has to carry worse."

"You don't understand what you're apologizing for," Emrys said. "You never did. None of us did."

Coram raised the knife.

And the dead screamed.

Not through Emrys alone this time — through everything. Through the bones in his satchel, through the marrow of the altar stone, through the ground itself, a sound like a thousand voices drawn through a keyhole, and every person in that circle heard it, and every torch guttered blue.

Wren, to her eternal credit, did not cry out. She looked at the knife, looked at the crowd, and said, in a small clear voice that carried further than any sermon: "I don't want to be a sacrifice. I want to go home and have supper."

Something in the crowd broke — not loudly, but visibly, the way ice breaks, all at once along a hidden seam. Mothers began to move forward. Farmers lowered their eyes.

But Coram's hand was already descending, and grief makes men fast, and Emrys did the only thing left.

"Take mine instead!"

The knife stopped a hand's width above Wren's collarbone.

"Founder's blood," Emrys said, and his voice cracked, and he let it crack, because some things should be said cracked or not at all. "Full blood. Not borrowed, not diluted by nine generations of careful marriages. My name is in the ledger, Coram. You know it is. And I'm offering it freely — not taken, not tricked, not rationed out by a council vote. Freely. The god below knows the difference between a gift and a theft. It has had two hundred years to learn exactly that difference."

Silence, except for the wind coming down off the hills, which sounded, for the first time in Emrys's life, like breathing.

Coram stared at him. "You'd die for a Pell brat?"

"I'd die for the truth," Emrys said. "She just happens to be standing in front of it."

* * *

They untied Wren first. Emrys insisted, and to his lasting surprise the crowd insisted harder — Hesta Pell of the turnip actually elbowed her way up onto the altar and physically hauled the girl off, muttering about idiots and children and supper, and Wren fled down the hill toward the lights of home without looking back, which Emrys counted as the wisest act performed that evening by anyone.

Then he climbed onto the altar himself.

The stone was warm. Not sun-warm; body-warm, the warmth of something vast lying beneath the hill with its cheek pressed against the underside of the world. Emrys knelt. He took out his carving knife — the one he'd used on every bone in the valley for twenty years, the one his grandfather had put in his hand at age seven and said, *gentle, boy, gentle, they can feel it* — and he opened his own palm along the old scar-line where carvers learn their trade.

Blood ran into the grooves of the stone, into channels worn there long ago by other palms, other winters.

"Here," he said. "I'm here."

The hill opened.

Not violently. The altar simply became a doorway, the way a word becomes a sentence, and Emrys stepped down into the hollow beneath the shrine, and the crowd above watched him descend until the earth closed over him like a book.

* * *

He had expected a cave. What he found was a memory of a country.

Grass grew down there — grey grass, soft as ash. A sky hung overhead that was not sky but the remembered idea of one, dim and patient. And in the middle of it stood the god.

It was not large. That was the first shock. Emrys had imagined something coiled and monstrous, something that required walls and covenants and knives. Instead it was roughly the size of a tired farmer, made of the same grey light as the grass, with a face that kept almost resolving and never quite did — a face like everyone's and no one's, like the feeling of being known.

It was starving. He could see that the way you can see hunger in a dog. Two hundred years of worship withheld, of prayers unanswered by design, of being fed nothing but fear and the occasional goat, and it stood upright anyway, patiently, the way the proud starve.

"You," it said. Its voice was not sound but recognition. *You are the one who listens.*

"Yes," Emrys said. His legs wanted to kneel. He let them.

*We have spoken before,* the god said. *Through the marrow. Through the small deaths. You thought you heard the dead.*

Emrys's stomach dropped. "The dead speak to me. They always have."

*Some,* the god said, gently. *The true dead, yes. But many of those voices were mine, leaking through the binding, calling to the only ears in the valley that were open.* A pause, and in the pause Emrys felt two decades of his life quietly rearrange themselves. *You mourned beautifully, bone-carver. Half of what you mourned was me.*

"That's—" He laughed, and it came out wrong. "That's a terrible joke."

*I have had two hundred years,* the god said, *and very little material. One develops humor.*

Despite everything — the blood running down his wrist, the council of frightened men above, the girl he'd traded his life for — despite all of it, Emrys felt his mouth twitch. Warmth, in the worst hour of his life, from the least likely source. Perhaps that was mercy too.

"Why did the founders bind you?" he asked. "The famine. Was it your doing?"

The grey grass shivered.

*Come and see,* the god said. *Since you offer your blood, see what it buys. See what they would not look at.*

It touched his forehead, and Emrys fell into the famine winter.

* * *

He saw it from below. From the roots. From the god's own eyes, which had been the valley's eyes then, before there was a valley — before there were borders and covenants and nine careful names in a ledger.

There had been green here once. Rivers. The land had been generous because the god had been awake in it, turning the seasons like a gardener turns soil. And then the frost came — not the god's frost, a real frost, a hard century-frost, the kind that comes whether gods are watching or not — and the crops died, and the founders, nine families huddled around a fire eating their boot leather, had done what frightened people have always done.

They had found someone to blame.

The god had not caused the famine. The god had *refused to fix it* — refused to force spring, refused to cheat the winter, because some things, it had told them, kneeling in the snow before their angry faces, must simply be endured. *Endure,* it had said. *I will endure beside you. Spring will come. I promise you spring.*

And they had heard refusal. Frightened, starving people heard a god telling them no, and they decided that a god who says no is a god who can say yes and chooses cruelty, and they built the ritual of binding out of bone-dust and desperation, and they poured their fear into it like mortar, and they buried the season-turner under the shrine and stole its office for themselves.

Spring had come that year. Of course it had. It always comes. But the founders had believed, afterward, that their knife had bought it, and so they taught their children the lie, and the lie grew a covenant, and the covenant grew a wall, and for two hundred years the valley had lived in a prison built from a misunderstanding, feeding it blood to keep the truth asleep.

Emrys came back to himself on his knees in the grey grass, sobbing without dignity.

"They murdered you for surviving them," he whispered.

*No,* the god said. *Worse. They murdered me for being honest. And then they called the murder mercy, and called the prison sanctuary, and raised their children inside the lie until the lie was the only country anyone knew.* It crouched before him, that not-quite-face level with his, and its hunger rolled off it like heat off a forge. *Your uncle believes he keeps a monster down. Your ancestors knew better. They knew exactly what they buried. That knowledge is the heaviest thing in this valley, bone-carver. Heavier than me.*

"What happens now?" Emrys asked. "Do you take my blood and break free and — what? Unmake us? The Door, the hollow country, Ilsa said—"

*Your blood I take gladly,* the god said, *as a gift, which is the only coin I have ever valued. But blood alone unbinds nothing. Here is the truth your door-seeker only half knows:* it leaned closer, and Emrys felt two hundred years of patience press against him like deep water. *A binding made of forgetting can only be broken by remembering. Every prayer they refused to hear, every death they blamed on me, every name struck from the ledger — all of it lives in me. All of it. If I rise, I rise *carrying* it, and the covenant cannot survive my rising because the covenant IS the forgetting. And when it breaks—*

"The valley forgets everything," Emrys said slowly. Understanding arrived like cold water. "Everything it's forgotten to keep the secret. The dead. The graves. The names. The people won't just lose their history. They'll lose—" his voice failed "—they'll lose everyone they've ever mourned. Like the mourning never happened."

*Unless someone carries it out,* the god said. *Someone who remembers. Someone whose whole life has been remembering. Someone who has been, without knowing it, preparing for this work since he was seven years old and an old man put a knife in his hand and said: gentle, boy. Gentle. They can feel it.*

The grey grass went very still.

*Memory must be carried in living vessels,* the god said, *or it dies at the threshold. I am not living. But you—*

"No," Emrys said. Then, because honesty had gotten him this far: "Yes. Obviously yes. I just needed a moment to panic first. Give me a moment."

The god waited. It was very good at waiting.

Emrys looked at his palm, at the blood still seeping along the old carver's scar. Twenty years of listening to the dead. Twenty years of loneliness mistaken for duty. He had thought the cost of his gift was solitude. It turned out solitude had been tuition.

"How much?" he asked.

*All of it,* the god said, not unkindly. *Every death. Every name. Two centuries of the valley's grief, passed through you, memory by memory, as we walk to the Door. It will hurt. It will hurt in ways you do not yet have words for, though I suspect you will invent several. And when we reach the threshold you may set it down or keep it. That choice will be yours. I only ask passage. I only ever asked passage.*

Emrys closed his eyes. Above him, muffled by the earth, he could hear the murmur of three hundred people standing around a closed altar, waiting for news of what they'd done. Somewhere among them was Maren, arriving home to find her daughter safe and her friend gone. Somewhere was Coram, holding a ledger full of lies, learning for the first time in his life how heavy a book could be.

"Before we begin," Emrys said, opening his eyes, "one condition. When you pass over the valley — whatever you are, when you rise — you pass over them *gently*. No weather of wrath. No judgment. They're fools, not monsters. Mostly fools. I've met actual monsters and they mostly run councils."

The not-quite-face did something that was almost a smile.

*Bones-carver,* the god said, *after two hundred years in the ground, do you truly believe I have rage left to spare?*

And it reached out, and laid its grey hand over his opened palm, and said:

*Then we begin. Hold still, Emrys Thornbury. Remembering is coming. All of it. All at once, and then forever.*

Far above, through miles of stone and soil and covenant, the first crack spread across the valley like light through ice —

— and the dead began, at last, to speak their names.
