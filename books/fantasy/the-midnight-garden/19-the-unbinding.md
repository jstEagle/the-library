## 19. The Unbinding

The god did not rise so much as the valley remembered it had always been falling.

Emrys felt it first in his teeth — a deep, subterranean hum, like the lowest note of a bell struck somewhere beneath the world. Then the shrine above him shuddered, and dust sifted down through the light of Maren's lantern, and the hollow that had held a starved and patient presence for two hundred years simply... opened.

He had offered his blood on the altar-stone with his daughter's name still wet in his mind — not Maren's daughter, thank every merciful bone, but his own: the founder's line, the carver's hand. He had expected pain. What came instead was *passage*.

The god rose through him.

It was not violent. That was the horror and the grace of it. It moved through his chest the way spring moves through frozen ground — inevitable, gentle, total. And in its wake came the dead.

All of them.

"Oh," Emrys said aloud, to no one, because there was nothing else to say. "Oh, there are so many of you."

They had been murmurs his whole life. A drowned child's small voice in a femur. An old woman's complaint about her son's choice of wife, carried in a knucklebone. The famine dead, nameless and many, a cold pressure at the edge of every carving he'd ever made. Now they were not murmurs. They were a river, and he was the riverbed, and they poured through him toward the opening sky.

Each one passed through him *complete*. Not a whisper — a whole life, lived at full speed, pressed into him like flowers into a book. He felt Tomas Ellery's wedding day and the fever that took him three winters later. He felt the drowned child — little Wren Ashcombe — feel the water go from cold to nothing at all, and then feel her mother's arms one last time as they pulled her from the weir. He wept without stopping, and it did not matter, because grief was simply the shape the passage made.

Above him, the covenant shattered.

He heard it rather than saw it: a sound like the world's largest pane of glass breaking in slow motion, note after note after note, ringing out across the valley. The border stones, he would learn later, cracked clean through. The invisible wall that had stood for nine generations flickered once, twice, and went out like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

And then the valley began to dissolve.

Not all at once. The edges first. From the shrine's mouth Emrys could see the western hills going soft — not crumbling, not burning, just *unwriting*, the way ink lifts off a page when it meets water. A farmhouse he had known all his life was half-gone, its far wall already a memory, and old Dagny Hobb stood in her doorway watching her own garden forget itself, row by row, beet by patient beet.

"Emrys." Maren's voice, raw from shouting. She was at the hollow's lip with her lantern held high, and behind her the sky was doing something the sky should never do. "The moot — Coram's people — some of them are running for the high pasture, they think height will save them—"

"Nothing saves them," Emrys said, and heard how strange his own voice sounded, layered now, thrummed-through, a chord instead of a note. "Nothing *keeps* them. Only carrying does."

"Then carry," Maren said simply. She had always been good at the simple, impossible things. It was why she prayed when no one else bothered. "Tell me what to do."

He climbed out of the hollow on legs that shook, and the god rose out of him and stood over the valley like weather — which is exactly what it was, exactly what it had been before nine frightened people with bone-dust and good intentions had nailed it under a shrine. It did not speak to Emrys. It had already asked everything it needed. *Remember us.* That was the whole prayer. Two words, two hundred years old, and it had waited this long to say them.

Now it was time to answer properly.

* * *

Coram Thornbury found him at the workshop.

That, more than anything, told Emrys how far the world had tilted. His uncle had not entered the workshop since Emrys was eleven years old, since the day he'd caught the boy listening to a sheep's knucklebone and gone white as tallow and said, *Don't. Don't ever let me see that again.* Now here he stood in the doorway, hat in his hands like a penitent, ash in his hair from the collapsing moot-hall, and his face was the face of a man watching his life's architecture come down.

"The ledger," Coram said. "You read it."

"All of it."

"Our name is in it."

"First of the nine," Emrys said gently. "Yours signed beneath theirs."

His uncle sat down on the carving stool — Emrys's stool, worn smooth by four generations of Thornbury backsides — and put his head in his hands. For a long moment the only sound was the valley unwriting itself outside, soft as snowfall, terrible as anything Emrys had ever heard.

"I believed it was mercy," Coram said finally. "My father told me it was mercy. His mother told him. You feed your children or you feed the god, and any parent alive chooses the children. I built my whole life on that sentence, boy. I raised you inside it. I nearly fed that girl to it tonight because I could not bear for the sentence to be a lie."

"It isn't mercy," Emrys said. "But it wasn't only murder either. Both things were true. That's the worst part." He crossed the room and put his hand on his uncle's shoulder, and felt the old man shake. "I need you, Uncle. Not your guilt. Your hands. There are four hundred people in this valley and the ground is forgetting them, and I cannot hold all their names alone."

Coram looked up. Something in his face rearranged itself — pride, maybe, or its corpse — and then settled into something better than either. Purpose.

"Tell me what a bone-carver does," he said, "when the bones run out."

* * *

Here is what a bone-carver does: he turns inward.

Emrys laid his left forearm on the workbench, took up his finest blade, and set the point against his own skin. Through him, still, the dead poured — slower now, a broad river rather than a flood, each soul pausing a moment in his chest as if catching its breath before the long walk out.

"You can't—" Maren started.

"I can. The Door answers founder's blood; the memory needs a founder's bones. Same principle. Cheaper materials." He tried for a smile and mostly managed it. "Besides. I've spent my whole life carving other people into keepsakes. Seems only fair somebody carves me."

He cut, and it hurt, and the hurt was nothing beside the names.

Because here was the secret no one had ever told him, the thing the dead knew and the living had forgotten: a name carved into living bone does not fade. Wood rots, stone weathers, paper burns. But a mark cut into a body that carries it willingly — that mark *travels*. It walks. It gets up in the morning and drinks tea and complains about the weather, and everywhere it goes, the name goes too.

So he carved Wren Ashcombe into the meat of his thumb, where she had drowned, so she would never be dry again. He carved Tomas Ellery along his wrist, next to the pulse, for the wedding day. He carved the famine dead — ninety-one souls the ledger had reduced to a single line, *the winter's cost* — down the length of his forearm, one by one, asking each as it passed through him what it wished to be called, because most of them had never been called anything.

*I am Hedda,* said a woman who had died holding her sister's hand. *I am Bren,* said a boy of maybe eight. *We had no stone. We had no stick even. Everything burned.*

"Then be bone," Emrys whispered, and carved, and wept, and carved.

Maren worked beside him. When his hands began to fail — and they failed often, shaking, slick — she steadied them. When he forgot whose name he was cutting, she read back the list they were building together, her voice steady as a plow-line. Once, in the worst hour, she laughed — actually laughed — because old Dagny Hobb, passing through on her way to oblivion, had paused in Emrys's chest long enough to complain that his workshop smelled of marrow and that *she'd told his grandmother a hundred times to open a window.*

"She's right," Maren said, wiping her eyes. "It's dreadful in here."

"Tell her," Emrys gasped, "that I'll open a window in the next world."

And Coram — broken, remade Coram — organized the exodus.

That was the part Emrys would remember longest, in the years when remembering was his whole occupation. His uncle walked the dissolving streets of a dying valley with a handbell taken from the moot-hall, ringing it and calling families by name — and he knew every name, every child, every goat, because keeping the rolls of the valley had been his duty and his pride for thirty years. *Hobbs, to the shrine road! Ellerys, take the north lane, mind the well! Ashcombes—* and here his voice broke, once, only once, *—Ashcombes, all of you, together, now.*

People obeyed him. That was the miracle nobody prayed for. In the middle of the unmaking of the world, four hundred frightened people looked at Elder Coram Thornbury — architect of the sacrifice, keeper of the cruel arithmetic — and followed him, because he was walking toward the danger instead of away from it, and because his voice, though cracked, did not waver.

By midnight the high pasture was gone. By the second hour past, the mill. Emrys worked by lantern-light while his homeland quietly ceased, carving until his arm was more name than skin, until the dead passing through him slowed from a river to a stream to a last few stragglers who lingered at his shoulder like guests reluctant to leave a warm kitchen.

One stayed longest. A woman he didn't know, from a generation before the ledger, before the famine even. She had been the valley's first bone-carver, she told him — the one whose craft the founders had stolen and twisted into chains.

"We meant it kindly, at the start," she said, in the accent of a country two centuries dead. "Binding, I mean. We thought we were saving everyone."

"I know," Emrys said. "Everyone always does."

"Mm. Clever lad." She touched — or seemed to touch — the fresh-cut names along his forearm. "You'll do. Go on, now. The door won't wait, and neither will the little Ashcombe girl. She's asking after you."

* * *

Dawn came grey through the workshop window, and the workshop was the last thing left.

Outside, the valley was a meadow. That was all. No shrine, no moot-hall, no lanes or walls or wells — just a wide green bowl of land with morning mist on it, as if nine generations had been a season's crop, harvested and cleared. The people stood at its edge with what they could carry, which was almost nothing, looking back at the place that had been their entire world.

The god passed over them one final time, going east, going home to whatever country had been robbed of it. It moved like weather, like light through cloud, and the people neither thanked it nor feared it, because both seemed suddenly beside the point. A few of the older ones bowed, out of habit. The god did not notice. Gods, it turns out, are very bad at noticing gratitude. It was prayers they'd always been good at.

At the workshop door, Coram stopped.

"You'll want these," he said, and handed Emrys the founders' ledger — the true one, the crime written plain. "Evidence," he said. "For whatever world we're walking into. Somebody should know what we were." He hesitated, and then, in the voice of a much younger man: "And somebody should know what we *did* about it. Write that part too, nephew. Write it plain."

Emrys took the ledger and his uncle's hand, and held both.

Behind them, at the head of the ragged column of survivors, Maren lifted her daughter onto her hip and looked back one last time at the empty green bowl of their vanished home. Then she turned east, toward the Road of Ash, toward the Door, toward whatever waited on the far side of erasure — and called out, clear across the mist:

"Emrys Thornbury! Are you coming? The dead of two hundred years are waiting for you to introduce them to the sunrise!"

He slung his satchel over his shoulder — blades, bone-dust, ledger, and one last blank charm, saved for reasons he couldn't yet name — and rang shut the door of the last house in a country that no longer existed.

"Yes," he called back, and smiled through the grief, because the grief was load-bearing now and he intended to carry it well. "Coming. Someone has to walk at the back and count."

And the last bone-carver of the valley stepped onto the Road of Ash, his arms full of names, leading his people out of the world that had forgotten them — into the world that would have to remember.
