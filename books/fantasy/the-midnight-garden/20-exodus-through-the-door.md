## 20. Exodus Through the Door

Ilsa Vane had walked through plague towns, war camps, and one particularly unpleasant village where the god of smallpox had been answering prayers a little too enthusiastically. She had seen people flee their homes before — in ones and twos, in terrified clumps, dragging children and chickens and whatever silver they could pry out of floorboards.

She had never seen an entire valley pack itself up and walk into the end of its own world.

They came down the Road of Ash in a line so long she could not see the beginning or the end of it from her place near the front. Old men on carts pulled by the last surviving oxen. Women carrying looms taken apart board by board because no one could bear to leave them. Children asleep in baskets, swaying with each step. The schoolmistress, a stern woman named Odile who had once threatened to have Ilsa arrested for looking at her wrong, walked with her entire library strapped to her back like a turtle that had decided to become a scholar.

"They're singing," Maren said beside her.

They were. Low, at first, half-embarrassed, as though singing were something you only did when you thought no one was listening. A harvest song, Ilsa guessed, though the words were worn smooth by generations of mouths and had gone soft in the middle. Then more voices joined, and the song stopped being shy.

Ilsa found she had to look at the horizon for a while.

"Don't," Maren said gently. "Don't look away. You'll want to remember this."

"I don't want to cry in front of a schoolmistress."

"Odile cried first. Before we even reached the border stones."

"That doesn't surprise me. She has the look of a woman who cries efficiently and grades harshly afterward."

Maren laughed — a wet, cracked laugh, but a real one — and Ilsa was glad of it. Maren had not laughed much since the hollow country, since her confession, since the terrible arithmetic of a mother who had almost traded a god's freedom for her daughter's breath. The girl herself, little Sefa with her crooked braid and her habit of collecting interesting rocks, marched along somewhere in the middle of the column with Emrys's sister's family, solemnly distributing pebbles to anyone who would take them. Ilsa had three in her pocket already. They were, Sefa assured her, *lucky*, which under the circumstances felt less like superstition and more like a strategic reserve.

Above them, the sky did something the sky should not do.

The freed god passed over the road like weather — there was no other word for it, and Ilsa had spent years learning words for gods, so she chose carefully. It came as a great slow shadow with light inside it, the way clouds carry rain but this carried something warmer, older. It moved without hurry. Where it passed, the ash on the road stirred upward in long ribbons, and for a heartbeat Ilsa saw faces in the drifting grey — thousands of them, two centuries of them — not screaming now, not pleading. Just *passing*. Going home by way of the only door left open.

Nobody thanked it.

That was the thing that undid Ilsa most, watching it. Not fear — the valley folk had made their peace with fear generations ago, and fear was a coin they'd stopped carrying. But no one fell to their knees either. No one shouted praise or built a shrine on the roadside or begged for miracles. An old farmer took off his hat as the shadow went over, the way you'd nod to a neighbor across a fence, and put it back on, and kept walking.

"They're not worshipping it," Ilsa said.

"No." Maren watched the light-shadow recede up the valley behind them, toward the lands that were already unmaking themselves, folding back into the maps like ink returning to water. "Emrys says it doesn't want worship. It wants company, maybe. Or just... passage. Like us."

"It murdered your founders' grandparents."

"The founders murdered it first." Maren said it simply, without heat, and Ilsa remembered why she liked this woman: she had the rare courage to say true things in a flat voice. "Though I think Emrys would say everyone starved that winter, and starving people do monstrous things, and then they build walls around the monstrous thing and call it safety until their grandchildren forget what the wall is for."

"Your Emrys is a wise man."

"He is not *my* Emrys, and he'd be embarrassed to hear you call him wise. Last night he tripped over his own workshop bench while carrying it on his back and blamed the bench for being where he left it."

Ilsa grinned despite everything. That was the other thing about the bone-carver: he heard the dead, he carried two hundred years of grief in his marrow, and he could still be made flustered by furniture. Somewhere in the last weeks — somewhere between the toll-town and the burning of her debt-mark and Maren bleeding in her arms — Ilsa had stopped thinking of him as a mark, then as a tool, then even as a friend, and started thinking of him as something closer to family. Family being, in Ilsa's experience, the people you would set fire to your own soul for and also steal bread from without remorse.

Ahead, the road bent around a hill of grey stone, and Ilsa saw the problem coming before the column did.

* * *

The problem was the river.

Or rather, the place where the river had been. In the hollow country, geography was a suggestion, and today the river had decided to be elsewhere — leaving behind a gorge of soft grey ash-silt forty feet deep, spanned by nothing except the skeleton of an old bridge that had been dead longer than the valley had been walled. Its stones were fused into shapes like vertebrae. Everything here was. The whole country was bone that didn't know it was bones yet.

The column slowed, murmured, and began, with the instinctive genius of crowds, to jam itself into a bottleneck.

"All right," Ilsa said, climbing onto a fallen pillar. "Everyone stop pushing. Yes, you, Master Hobb, I can see you, the oxen cannot climb vertical ash, this is a fact about oxen."

"How would you know anything about oxen?" Hobb called up. He was a miller, wide as his own door, with a voice that had ended many arguments before they started.

"Because I've been walking ahead of your oxen for two days, and I've developed opinions."

This got the laugh she wanted. Laughter was a tool; Ilsa had learned that young, in a household where the alternative tools were worse. You could move a frightened crowd with fear or with laughter, and laughter got better mileage.

"We go single file across the bridge spine, oldest and youngest first, everyone else waits their turn, and if anyone starts a panic I will personally introduce them to the schoolmistress, who has been looking for someone to grade all morning."

Odile, passing below, said dryly, "I have my slate ready," and the crowd laughed again, and the knot loosened, and it worked. It kept working. Ilsa stood on her pillar for three hours directing traffic — *you, then you, hold the child's hand, madam, the goat goes before the goat's opinion does* — and somewhere in the middle of it she realized she wasn't performing anymore. She cared. When the old ferryman Tam slipped on the fourth vertebra and caught himself, her heart actually jumped, and when a toddler dropped a wooden duck into the gorge and howled, Ilsa climbed down twenty feet of crumbling silt on pure reflex and retrieved it, and accepted a lucky rock as payment with full solemnity.

Her debt-mark had burned off her wrist days ago, taking her god's protection with it. She had expected to feel naked forever after. Instead she felt — she searched for the word while hauling herself back up the gorge, duck in hand —

Responsible. That was the word. Ugly, heavy, responsible.

"You know," Maren said, falling into step beside her on the far bank, "the moot used to pay people to do what you just did."

"What, rescue ducks?"

"Keep order. Guide people. Coram called it stewardship, and it meant deciding things for everyone from a big chair." Maren glanced at her. "You do it standing on a rock, covered in ash, holding a duck. I prefer your version."

"I charge less, too. One rock per duck. I'm being robbed, frankly."

* * *

They made camp that evening in the lee of a hill shaped like a folded hand, and Ilsa walked the perimeter out of habit and found Emrys sitting alone at the edge of the firelight with a knife and a length of bone, carving.

He was always carving now. Since the unbinding, since the dead had begun passing through him — she still couldn't think about that for too long or her chest did something unpleasant — his hands needed to be busy. Each figure was small, the size of a thumb: a name given shape. He'd done hundreds. He'd do hundreds more. He said the names came to him like weather now, gentle instead of screaming, and that carving them was the only way to keep them from pooling.

"Room for a door-seeker?" Ilsa asked.

"There's room for anyone who doesn't ask me to pray for them. I'm off duty."

"Perish the thought. I only came to mock your whittling."

He tilted the bone toward the firelight. It was a woman holding a loaf of bread. "Marit Ossel. Baker. Died eighty-one years ago, winter fever. Her granddaughter's granddaughter is asleep in that cart over there, third from the left."

"You know all of them?"

"I'm starting to suspect I will." He said it lightly, but his knife paused, and Ilsa saw the weight settle on him the way ash settles: slowly, everywhere, unavoidable.

"Emrys." She sat down beside him. "Tomorrow we reach the Door. Have you thought past it? What happens to you, on the far side?"

"Silence, I imagine." He turned the baker in his fingers. "Two hundred years of voices, and then quiet. Everyone keeps telling me I'll finally rest." His mouth twisted. "I keep catching myself listening for them anyway. Like an ear still ringing after the bell stops. What if the silence is worse?"

"Then you fill it. That's what silence is for. It's a room, not a grave." She nudged him with her shoulder. "Besides, you'll have work. Someone has to teach those children which rocks are lucky. Sefa has cornered the market, and her prices are extortionate."

That earned a real laugh, short and startled, and Ilsa counted it a victory worth more than any toll she'd ever dodged.

Behind them the camp settled into sleep by firelight, hundreds of fires, a constellation laid down on the ash. And above the sleeping valley-folk, far off toward the country that no longer existed, the god's light rose and fell against the horizon like slow breathing — neither thanked nor feared, merely witnessed, going about whatever business a murdered-and-mourned god conducts on its last night above the world.

Ilsa slept badly, as she always did before thresholds. Door-seeker's superstition: never dream on the eve of a crossing, or the door dreams back.

* * *

On the sixth day of the walk, the Road of Ash ended.

Not gradually. One step the road was there, worn and grey and real beneath their boots, and the next step it simply stopped, cut clean as a sawn plank, and beyond it lay a plain of white bone-stone under a sky the color of unpolished silver. And rising out of that plain, half a mile distant, stood the Door.

Ilsa had hunted doors her whole adult life. She had crawled through a mountain's armpit to find one, bribed a corpse-talker in Vell, sold three years of her luck for a map that proved false. She had imagined the Door of Ash a thousand times: vast, terrible, wreathed in warning.

It was none of those things. It was a doorway of grey bone standing alone on the plain, tall enough for a cart and oxen, framed in carved stone worn smooth as river pebbles. No flames. No guardian. Just a rectangle of deeper dark, patient as a held breath, waiting the way honest things wait.

The column stopped. The singing stopped. For a long moment the only sound was wind moving through four hundred people's clothing, and a child asking, very reasonably, "Is that it?"

"That's it," Ilsa said.

And then, because she was who she was, she raised her voice and added, "Single file! Elders and children first! And *nobody* touches the frame, I don't care how interesting it looks!"

From the middle of the crowd, Sefa's voice piped up: "Can I touch it a *little*?"

"*No.*"

Behind the crowd, far back down the road they had come, the horizon flickered — the last edge of the valley, thin as paper now, dissolving into silver. The god's light gathered there, low and bright, beginning its final passage. Whatever waited through the dark of the Door, it was time.

Ilsa looked at Maren. Maren looked at Ilsa. Neither of them saw Emrys anywhere in the column.

Then, at the head of the road, apart from everyone, he was already walking toward the threshold — the last bone-carver, carrying his people's names in his hands and his own grief on his back — and Ilsa understood, with a lurch, that the Door answered to founder's blood, and that the man who opened it would be the first through, and might mean to be the last.

"Emrys!" Maren called, and broke into a run.
