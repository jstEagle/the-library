## 22. The Last Bone-Carver

The silence was the strangest thing.

Emrys had spent his whole life with voices in his ears — the murmuring dead, patient as rain, filling every quiet hour with their small griefs and longings. He had complained about it, privately. He had wished, in weak moments, for a single morning without them. Now he had that morning, and every morning after, and he woke each day reaching for a sound that was not there, like a man patting the empty side of a bed.

He had carried two hundred years of names out through the Door. He had felt each one pass through him on the far side of that grey threshold — not painfully, though pain had been part of it — more like swallowing a winter whole and being asked to keep it warm. The god had gone over the exodus like weather, as Ilsa said afterward, neither thanked nor feared, and then it was gone into whatever country gods go to when they are finally free, and Emrys had stood in the ash-road dust holding every name the valley had ever loved.

The names were still there. That was the mercy of it. The memories lived in him now, folded small, the way seeds live in a closed fist. He could open his hand whenever he chose.

* * *

The survivors built at the foot of a range of hills that no map had ever bothered to insult, in a green country where the rivers ran cold and clear and nobody's prayers had been answered yet, which everyone agreed was a fine thing to have in common.

They named the settlement New Thornbury first, out of gratitude or guilt, and Coram Thornbury — who had walked out of the dissolving valley hollow-eyed and bent, an old man made older by truth — refused it so violently that he wept in front of the whole moot. So they called it Havenstead instead, which Maren suggested, because, she said, "we haven't earned 'haven' yet, but we're steadying," and that got the only laugh anyone had managed in weeks.

Coram did not rebuild much after that. He kept to himself, chopped wood for widows, sat at the edge of the evening fires. But once — only once — he came to Emrys's workshop door and stood there a long while before speaking.

"I've been trying to remember my grandmother's face," he said. "I can't. I know I loved her. I remember loving her. But the face is gone." His hands shook around nothing. "Is that what we deserved?"

Emrys thought about lying, decided against it — they had both had enough of lies to last several lifetimes. "The founders took the faces when they bound the god. I carry some of them now. Your grandmother is among them."

"Will you tell me about her? Someday?"

"Someday," Emrys said. "When you can bear it."

Coram nodded slowly, and Emrys understood that the old man knew perfectly well which of them would need the bearing. Then he left, and his footsteps going down the lane sounded lighter than they had in twenty years, which was its own kind of grief.

* * *

Emrys opened the workshop in the second spring.

It was smaller than the old one, with a north window that caught good light and a hearth that smoked if you looked at it wrong. He hung no sign. Word traveled on its own in a place this size, and besides, the work had changed.

In the valley, bone-carving had been for the dying: charms to ease the passage, tokens to hold a name past death, because the dead there spoke and needed things to say. Here, the dead were silent. Here, the living were the ones who needed help remembering.

So he carved memorials. Slender tablets of bone-white stone he quarried from a seam in the hills — stone the color of marrow, cool to the touch, which seemed right. On each one he cut a name, and beneath the name a single line of the person's life. Not an epitaph. A seed-line. Something that would grow in memory rather than close over it like sod.

*BRAMWELL OTTLEY, SHEPHERD. HE SANG TO HIS DOGS AND THE DOGS SANG BACK.*

*ODILE FENN, WEAVER. HER LOOM WAS THE FIRST SOUND OF EVERY MORNING.*

*TAM HOLLIS, SIX YEARS OLD. ASKED WHY THE MOON FOLLOWED HIM HOME.*

People came shyly at first, then steadily. They sat across the bench from him and talked, haltingly, about their mothers and fathers and children, and Emrys listened — truly listened, the way he always had, though now it cost him nothing but attention — and from their stumbling words he coaxed the line. When he found the right one, something happened in their faces. Recognition, relief. As if he'd found the loose thread that let the whole garment lie flat again.

"You have a gift," Odile's daughter told him, wiping her eyes.

"It's not a gift," Emrys said. "It's just paying attention. Anyone can do it. Almost no one does."

That was the closest thing to a philosophy he ever offered.

* * *

The garden began by accident.

A woman named Petra Voss, whose husband Emrys had memorialized, planted flowers around his tablet without asking anyone. Then someone else did. Then someone else. Within three seasons the hillside behind the workshop — terraced, south-facing, absurdly fertile — had become a garden of white stones among green and blooming things, each stone bearing a name, each name surrounded by whatever flowers the family favored.

Emrys walked it most evenings. He knew every stone. More than knowing them, he remembered them — all of them, the drowned child from the first chapter of his sorrow, the famine dead, the generations between. When he forgot nothing, the forgetting of others hurt less. That was the arithmetic he had chosen at the Door, and he had never once regretted it, though some nights the weight of two centuries pressed on his chest like a sleeping cat grown enormous, and he lay awake cataloguing faces until dawn.

Maren visited often. She had married a stonemason named Garrick — quietly, sensibly, with Emrys's blessing and Ilsa's raucous heckling from the back row — and she brought Emrys bread and gossip in equal measure, refusing to accept payment for either.

"You're thinner," she'd say.

"You say that every time."

"And every time you're thinner. Memory is heavy work. Eat the bread."

Ilsa Vane came and went as Ilsa Vane pleased, which was the only way Ilsa Vane did anything. She had burned her debt-mark at the Toll and severed herself from her god's protection, and the world had not punished her for it, exactly — but it had stopped smoothing the road ahead of her. She took dangerous work. She mapped thresholds. She turned up at Havenstead twice a year smelling of far countries, dropped outrageous gifts on Emrys's bench, argued with him about everything, and left before anyone could grow attached.

"You could stay," Emrys said, on one such departure.

"Could I." She squinted at the horizon the way other people squinted at fine print. "You know what I am, Thornbury? I'm a door-seeker with no door worth seeking. If I stop moving, I'll have to find out who I am when I'm not running, and frankly the suspense is unbearable."

"One day you'll run out of world."

"No," Ilsa said cheerfully, shouldering her pack. "But it's sweet of you to hope." At the gate she paused, uncharacteristically. "The garden looks good. My sister would have liked it." And she was gone down the road before he could answer, which was how Ilsa said almost everything important.

* * *

On a bright morning in early summer, five years after the Door, Emrys heard footsteps on the garden path — small, quick, deliberate footsteps he knew very well.

Liss Maren's-daughter — Liss Garrick now, technically, though she informed everyone that names were just labels and hers was Liss — came up the hill carrying a bundle of flowers wrapped in damp cloth. She was eleven, gap-toothed, sun-browned, with her mother's stubborn chin and a habit of looking directly at people that unnerved adults and delighted Emrys.

"These are for the new stones," she announced, holding up the bundle. Marigolds, mostly, and something blue he didn't recognize. "Garrick cut four last week. The ones near the wall."

"The famine year," Emrys said.

"Yeah." She knelt at the low wall where four new tablets stood pale against the green, and she arranged the flowers with the seriousness of a coronation. Emrys watched her work and felt the familiar fullness in his chest, the weight that had become, over the years, less burden than ballast.

"Auntie Ilsa says you used to hear the dead," Liss said, not looking up. "That they talked to you all the time."

"They did."

"What was it like?"

Emrys considered. Most adults asked that question carefully, apologetically, afraid of the answer. Liss asked it the way she asked everything — as though the world owed her straight talk and she meant to collect.

"Loud," he said. "And lonely. Everyone thinks hearing voices means you're never alone. It's the opposite. You're alone with everyone else's sorrows, and none of them can see you."

"That sounds awful."

"It was. And it was also—" He searched. "It was also the reason I knew what to do. Every voice I ever heard taught me something about what people need. Grief needs somewhere to go. That's mostly it. That's the whole secret."

Liss finished with the marigolds and sat back on her heels, studying him with those frank eyes. "Do you miss it? The hearing?"

"Yes," Emrys said honestly. "Every day. Like missing a limb that ached."

She nodded, satisfied, as though he'd passed some test. Then she dug in her pocket and produced a small object, holding it out to him on her palm.

It was a chip of bone-white stone, no bigger than a thumbnail, rough-cut. "I found it by the wall. Can you carve something on it? Something little. For me."

"For whom?"

"For me. To keep." She said it simply, without shame, and Emrys understood that this was the truest kind of request — not a memorial for the lost, but a charm for the living. The old work and the new, meeting.

He turned the chip in his fingers. He thought of a valley walled off by covenant, a god starved beneath a shrine, nine names in a ledger written in bone-dust, a Door of Ash standing open for one day and closing forever. He thought of a girl marked for sacrifice who had grown into a woman who grew flowers on graves, and of the daughter of that woman asking for a word to carry.

He carved one line, small and clean:

*LISS. REMEMBER OUT LOUD.*

"There," he said, pressing it into her hand. "That's the whole craft, really. Everything else is technique."

Liss read it, frowned, read it again. Then her face cleared the way dawn clears, and she grinned — gap-toothed, radiant — and threw her arms around his neck, and ran down the hill with her stone clutched tight, shouting something to somebody below, already keeping her promise.

Emrys stood alone in the garden among the white stones and the blooming names, in the warm silence where the dead had been, and found that it was not empty after all. It was full. Full of marigolds and blue flowers he didn't know the name of yet, full of bread and gossip and raucous parting gifts, full of a child's shout fading down the hillside, full of two hundred years held gently in a pair of working hands.

Somewhere beyond the hills, he suspected, a freed god was passing over some other country like weather. Somewhere, Ilsa was running toward the edge of the world. Somewhere, Coram was learning his grandmother's face a piece at a time, weeping and grateful.

Here, there was a workshop with a crooked chimney, a bench worn smooth, and work enough for the rest of his life: the slow, patient justice of remembrance, which outlasted law, outlasted covenant, outlasted even the gods — the only justice, he had come to believe, that asks nothing of the powerless and everything of the willing.

Emrys picked up his chisel.

There were stones waiting.
