## 1. The Color of Ash

The heralds came to Vell at dawn, which Wren thought was a coward's hour, though he supposed that made two cowards in the city that morning, because he had been awake since midnight with his ear pressed to the floorboards, counting the footsteps of the night watch.

He watched from his window as three riders in royal livery dismounted before the Iron Gate. Their boots rang against the cobbles. One carried a scroll sealed with black wax; another carried a hammer and a fistful of iron nails, because a proclamation meant to terrify must first be made to stay put in the wind. The third rider simply stood there, looking bored, and Wren found this the most frightening detail of all. Terror, administered routinely, by a man who would rather be eating breakfast.

The scroll went up beside the grain tariffs and a playbill for something called *The Lamenting Duchess*. The Proscription of Unlicensed Works, it read, in letters tall enough to read from horseback. Henceforth, all charms, cantrips, workings, bindings, blessings, small mercies, and kind intentions performed without crown sanction were declared crimes against the realm. Penalties to follow. Rewards for informants listed below.

*Rewards,* Wren noted. They'd learned that much since the last purge. Fear only got you so far. Greed got you your neighbors.

Below him, in the gray light, old Marta Voss came out to sweep her step, read the scroll twice, and then — Wren's heart stopped — reached up and unpinned her shawl, from which hung a charm no larger than a button. A little knotted thing of thread and river-clay, the kind grandmothers had been tying onto babies' wrists since before there were kings to object. A keeping-charm. It kept away fevers, mostly, and bad luck occasionally, and it worked about as often as a coin landing kind.

Marta held it in her palm for a long moment. Then she kissed it, tucked it back under her collar, and picked up her broom.

"No," Wren whispered. "No, you stubborn old woman."

They took her within the hour. He heard the shouting from three streets over, and he did not go look, because going to look was how watchers became witnesses and witnesses became accomplices. But he heard it: the boots on her stairs, the crack of her door, her voice rising in that imperious tone she used on fishmongers — *I have worn this since my wedding, you absolute infants* — and then the sound that stayed with him all day, all week, maybe forever: the crowd's silence. Not approval. Not protest. Just silence, the collective sound of a city deciding not to know what it was seeing.

By noon they had nailed her button-charm to the gate beside the Proscription, as evidence, like a moth pinned to a board.

Wren Okafor closed his shutters and began to count what he had to lose.

* * *

His house on Candlewick Lane looked, from the outside, like nothing worth burning down, which had always been its chief virtue. Inside it held a household that could not have explained itself to a census-taker if their lives depended on it, which, as of that morning, they did.

There was Tamsin, eleven years old, currently sitting on the kitchen table because chairs were a suggestion she declined to entertain, drawing maps on the backs of delivery receipts. She drew the way other children breathed: constantly, compulsively, with her tongue between her teeth. There was Fen, who had arrived four winters ago half-frozen and mute and had never entirely thawed into speech but could fix anything with gears or hinges and communicated chiefly in gestures of tremendous eloquence. There was Brother Aldous, defrocked, unrepentant, who blessed things quietly when he thought no one was listening, and who had once told Wren that if blessing bread was a crime then the crown could pry his loaf from his cold hands.

And there was Wren himself, keeper of the largest collection of harmless charms in Vell, none of which had ever harmed so much as a housefly, all of which now constituted, legally speaking, treason.

"You're doing the face," Tamsin said, not looking up from her map.

"What face?"

"The one where you're deciding something terrible and hoping nobody notices." She shaded in a coastline. "You've been doing it since breakfast. Also we're out of honey, which might explain it."

Wren laughed despite everything, which was the household's whole trick, its founding magic, older than any charm he owned. Then he rolled up his sleeves and did the terrible thing.

He built a fire in the yard behind the kitchen, in the old copper basin, and he brought out his stock.

Twenty years of work lay in that cedar chest. Sleep-easy sachets for colicky infants. Knots against nightmares. Salt-and-rosemary packets that kept bread from molding, more or less. Little stitched stars that farmers nailed over barn doors, which did absolutely nothing except remind the farmer that someone had wished him well, which Wren maintained was itself a working, whatever the Registrar's Office said. Every piece of it warm, small, human, and illegal by sundown.

Tamsin came to the doorway and watched him feed the chest's contents into the flames. She didn't ask why. Children always understood burnings faster than adults did; they'd seen the shape of loss before and recognized it.

"Will it hurt them?" she asked. "The charms. When they burn."

"They're not alive, love."

She gave him a look of devastating pity. "Everything you make has a bit of you in it. You say so yourself. So yes, they're a little bit alive, and you're burning them, and you're making the face again."

He crouched beside her, smoke stinging his eyes in a way he chose not to examine. "Listen to me. Tonight, after dark, we're leaving Vell. All of us. I need you to pack only what you can carry, and I need you to be clever about it, because clever is the one thing they haven't outlawed yet."

"Where are we going?"

"Somewhere the maps give up," Wren said. "And I'm going to draw us a path anyway."

Fen appeared in the doorway, took one look at the fire, one look at Wren's face, and went upstairs without a word to begin packing. That was the whole conversation. It had always been enough.

Brother Aldous came down last, carrying his loaf, which he set on the table with ceremony. "I heard," he said. "About Marta Voss." He crossed himself, an old habit the church had also tried to proscribe, and failed. "We should take the south road—"

"No," Wren said. "South roads have tolls, and tolls have ledgers, and ledgers have names. We go through the Hollow."

Aldous went pale in the way of a man who had heard fireside stories about the Hollow his entire life and had hoped never to fact-check them. "No one comes out of the Hollow."

"One person does," Wren said. "Occasionally. With maps."

* * *

It happened while they packed, in the last hour of daylight, when the sun came slanting gold through Tamsin's window and caught her wrist as she reached for her satchel.

The mark glowed.

Wren had seen it a thousand times and thought it a birthmark, a whorl of pale silver like a fingerprint gone strange, sitting just above her pulse. He had kissed it goodnight. He had never once let himself think too hard about it, because thinking too hard was how a careful man survived, and he had survived twenty years by not-thinking very skillfully indeed.

But in that light it wasn't pale anymore. It burned like a coal wrapped in skin — a sigil, fine-lined and deliberate, a registrar's seal, a ledger-entry written in flesh. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, Wren felt the pull of a bargain. Old. Binding. Hungry.

He took her wrist gently and turned it to the light, and Tamsin watched his face change.

"It's been doing that sometimes," she said, in a very small voice. "Since my birthday. I didn't tell you because you'd do the face."

"Tamsin." His mouth was dry. "Who sold you?"

"I don't—" She stopped. Something moved behind her eyes, some memory kept carefully in a drawer, and he saw her decide, eleven years old and already brave past all sense, to open the drawer. "My mother. Before you. Before the Hollow. She said it was to keep me safe. She said they'd hold a part of me until it was paid for, and no one could ever take the rest." Her chin came up. "What did she sell it *to*, Wren?"

And Wren, kneeling on the floorboards of the only home he'd ever truly had, holding the wrist of a child whose soul had a lien on it, understood that invisibility was finished. He could burn every charm, keep his head down, survive another twenty years of not-thinking — but not with *that* glowing on her skin, not with the court beneath the mountain already holding its claim.

Fear had been a good servant. Now it stepped aside, and something harder stood up in its place.

"We're not taking the south road after all," he said quietly. "Pack your ink. Pack every map I own. We're going somewhere tonight, Tamsin — and on the way, I'm going to teach you exactly what your mother bought, and what it cost, and who holds the debt."

Outside, the heralds' nails gleamed in the gate of Vell, and the first stars came out over the direction of the Hollow — unmapped, unmapping, waiting.

"Is it far?" Tamsin asked.

Wren looked at the mark on her wrist, still faintly burning, pointing home to its master like a compass needle.

"No," he said. "But it will remember us."
