## 2. What the Maps Refuse

The Hollow did not have a door so much as an apology. The treeline simply stopped pretending, and the forest beyond it was gray as old dishwater, its trunks bent like question marks, its leaves the color of a bruise deciding whether to heal.

Wren Okafor stood at the edge of it with his map case hugged to his chest and tried to look braver than he felt.

"It's smaller than I imagined," said Bramble Fenn, the hedge-witch who had shared his roof for eleven years and his bread for ten. She was fifty-three, built like a well-fed sparrow, and had packed for their flight a single satchel containing three dresses, no food, and forty-seven jars of ointment. "The Hollow. In your stories it's enormous."

"Stories exaggerate," Wren said. "It's actually bigger on the inside. Like a liar's purse."

Behind them, Vell's smoke still smudged the horizon. Behind them were the nailed Proscription, the empty hook where Marisol the button-charmer had been dragged past, the burned stock of harmless charms that had kept Wren fed and forgotten for twenty years. Ahead was nothing mapped by anyone but him.

Tamsin stood at his elbow, twelve years old, chin up in the way she adopted whenever she wanted people to believe she wasn't afraid. Her wrist, where the soul-mark glowed faintly under her sleeve like a coal refusing to die, ached in this place. Wren could see her rubbing it without noticing she was doing it.

"Stay between me and Bramble," he said. "If the ground asks you a question, don't answer."

"The ground *asks questions?*"

"Rhetorically. It wants to know what you remember. It likes to rearrange itself around the answer." He unrolled the first of his charts — inked over two decades of secret study, annotated in a hand small enough to fit a whole life in margins. "That's why only my maps work here. They're not maps of the land. They're maps of what the land pretends to be."

Bramble snorted. "You've always been insufferable about being the only one who can do things, Wren."

"I contain multitudes of insufferable," Wren agreed, and led them into the gray.

* * *

Tamsin counted her family the way she'd learned to count anything frightening: quickly, and twice.

Wren, ahead, arguing with the fog. Bramble, beside her, humming the off-key walking song she used to keep children brave. Old Sef the lamplighter, whose gift was making shadows hold still, currently using it to keep his own shadow from wandering off in sulky directions. Juniper, seventeen, who could coax sweetness from rot and was therefore in charge of the rations nobody liked. And Tamsin herself, keeper of nothing yet, carrying the whole group's spare stockings because someone sensible had to.

Five of them. She counted again anyway. Five.

The trouble with counting was that it gave her time to think, and thinking was the one thing everyone in this family seemed determined to protect her from.

She'd heard it through the floorboards eight months ago. Wren's voice low, Bramble's lower, the words rising through the gaps in old wood like water through a cracked jug: *sold. Before you ever came to us. Her own mother sold her soul to buy her safety, and the mark is proof, and if the crown learns what Tamsin carries they won't stop at proscription—*

*She can never know,* Wren had said. *Not while there's any chance I can fix it quietly.*

*Tamsin is going to be a grown woman one day, Wren Okafor, and grown women remember being lied to kindly.*

Tamsin had sat on the cold stairs with her heart hammering and thought, absurdly, about supper. Then she'd thought about the mark on her wrist, which she'd always assumed was a birthmark shaped like a curling fern. A fern. She'd traced it a hundred times thinking it was pretty.

She hadn't told anyone she knew. That was her secret now, folded up inside her alongside the other one — the one where she'd also heard Wren say, later that same night, alone at his table, speaking to no one: *I'll get it back for her. Whatever it costs. Whatever it costs.*

Adults said *whatever it costs* the way merchants said *free sample.* Tamsin had decided long ago that it was a phrase to be distrusted.

The path narrowed. On either side, the gray trees had begun doing something she didn't like: leaning inward, arranging themselves into shapes almost familiar. One trunk curved exactly like the crooked stair rail back home in Vell. Another forked the way the plum tree in Wren's courtyard forked, the one he'd promised to graft sweeter fruit onto someday, when it was safe to have courtyards again.

"Stop looking at the trees," Wren called back without turning. "They're fishing."

"For what?"

"Memories. They show you home so you'll want to go toward it." His voice tightened. "Nobody goes toward anything. We go where I say, even if it looks wrong. Especially if it looks wrong."

The pass ahead looked very wrong. It was a slot between two hills that hadn't been there a moment ago — Tamsin was nearly sure it hadn't — and it was lit with a soft amber glow, warm as lamplight, warm as kitchen windows, and drifting out of it came the smell of Bramble's cinnamon buns, which was impossible, because Bramble's cinnamon buns required an oven and a city and a life that had burned along with Wren's charm-stock.

"Oh," said Bramble softly, and her humming stopped. "Oh, that's *cruel.*"

"Don't look at it," Wren snapped. "Sef, shadows tight. Juniper, take Tamsin's hand—"

But Tamsin was looking, because she was twelve and it smelled like every good morning she had ever had, and in the amber light of the pass she saw something that made her stomach drop: a figure, small, moving away from them into the warmth. Moving like someone who knew where they were going.

"Sef!" she screamed. "SEF'S IN THE PASS!"

Old Sef had always been a quiet man; his shadow was the loud one. Now both of them were twenty paces into the amber, walking with the unhurried certainty of sleepwalkers, and the walls of the pass had begun to lean together like a book closing.

Wren moved faster than Tamsin had ever seen him move. He didn't run so much as *unroll* — flinging his arms wide, and the chart he'd carried went wide with them, paper snapping taut in air that had none, and he shouted words that weren't in any language Tamsin knew but which the ground clearly understood, because the hills flinched.

He caught Sef by the collar one stride before the pass sealed shut with a sound like a swallowed word.

For a moment nobody breathed. Then Sef blinked, looked around, and said mildly, "Was I dreaming about buns?"

"You were dreaming about becoming a bun," said Bramble, shaking.

Juniper burst into tears and immediately apologized for it. Tamsin discovered her hand hurt from gripping Juniper's.

And Wren stood there holding his trembling chart against the wind, looking older than his thirty-odd years, looking like a man who had just spent something he couldn't count, and Tamsin thought: *he'll lie now. He'll pat us all and say it's fine.*

Instead he sat down on a rock.

"Sit down," he said hoarsely. "All of you. I'm done keeping things quiet. Quiet nearly cost us Sef, and quiet has been costing me something else for years, and I'm tired, and we may all die in here tomorrow, so tonight you get the truth, and then you can hate me while we walk."

"We're not going to hate you," Bramble began.

"You might. Sit."

They sat. Even the fog seemed to sit, drawing back politely, and Wren took a breath like a man diving, and told them everything: the Hollow, the shifting paths, why only his maps held. And then — his voice dropping to the register Tamsin remembered through floorboards — the rest. The court beneath the mountain. The Registrar who bought and sold souls. The bargain made by a desperate mother eight years before Tamsin ever knocked on Wren's door, hungry and hollow-eyed, with a fern-shaped mark she thought was pretty.

Tamsin watched the family learn it. Bramble's face going white, then fierce. Juniper's hand finding hers. Sef reaching out to grip Wren's shoulder hard enough to creak.

And she watched Wren look at her last of all, and she understood suddenly that everything he'd done — the secrecy, the gentleness, the infuriating way he answered her real questions with questions about supper — had been a man trying to carry a debt alone so no child would feel like a price tag.

"I already know," Tamsin said, before he could apologize. "Eight months ago. Through the floor. You talk too loud when you're worried."

Wren stared at her. Then he laughed — a cracked, astonished sound, half sob — and Bramble laughed too, wetly, and even Sef huffed, and the fog around them shivered, because the Hollow feeds on secrets and five people had just emptied theirs out onto the rocks.

"Then hear the rest of it," Wren said, and his eyes were steady on hers. "There's a way to buy your soul back. There's only one thing down there worth more than a soul, and I intend to offer it. But the price—" He hesitated, and Tamsin saw him choose honesty over kindness, maybe for the first time. "The price will hurt you all more than it hurts me. And I can't tell you what it is until we stand in front of the Registrar, because if I say it aloud here, the Hollow will hear, and the Hollow tells tales."

"That's a terrible rule," said Tamsin.

"It's the mountain's rule. I just live under it." He rolled up his chart and stood, and pointed down the darkening defile to where, far below, faint as breath on glass, lights glittered beneath the roots of the world. "We camp here tonight. Tomorrow we descend."

Tamsin lay awake long after the others slept, her wrist glowing against the blanket like a coal refusing to die, watching Wren sit his watch with his maps spread across his knees, guarding a secret she now knew the shape of even if she didn't know its name.

Whatever it costs, she thought. Whatever it costs.

Down in the deep, the lights beneath the mountain pulsed once — slow, patient, like something opening an eye.
