## 3. The Court Beneath

The mountain did not have a door so much as a throat.

Wren felt it the way he felt all his maps — in the soles of his feet, in the small bone behind his ear that had never once been wrong about direction. The Hollow's shifting paths had herded them for three days, mockingly rearranging valleys they'd crossed an hour before, and now, at last, the terrain stopped playing games. It simply opened: a cleft in black stone, breathing out air that smelled of cold ink and old paper.

"Cheerful," muttered Brannick, who carried their supplies and most of their complaints.

"It knows we've come for it," Wren said quietly.

Tamsin walked closest to him. She had not spoken much since the pass, since he'd told her everything — the bargain her mother made, the mark on her wrist, the debt riding under her skin like a second heartbeat. She hadn't cried. She had looked at him with an expression far too old for eleven years old and said only, "You should have told me sooner. I'm not made of glass." Then she'd taken his hand, which was somehow worse than crying.

Now her wrist glowed faintly through her sleeve, a pale seam of light, like a candle behind linen.

The passage descended in spirals. Their footsteps echoed wrong — too many echoes, as though other feet walked just behind them, patient ones. Sable, the youngest of them after Tamsin, kept turning around to look. "There's nothing there," she reported each time, disappointed.

"There's always something there," said Merrin, who read fortunes badly and people well. "It's just being polite."

They slept when they could not walk further, huddled in a chamber where the walls were shelved floor to ceiling with ledgers whose spines had no titles. Wren sat apart, watching Tamsin sleep with the soul-mark glowing gently at her pulse, and made the decision the way you make any true decision: not all at once, but in pieces, until it is whole.

He knew what the court would ask. He had known since Vell, since the heralds' nails went into the Proscription and his neighbor's button-charm bought her a cell instead of safety. Everything beneath the mountain ran on exchange. He'd spent twenty years trading small magics for bread and silence. He understood the currency.

The question was only what he could afford.

* * *

The Pale Registrar received them in a hall of white stone that seemed to have been carved from one unbroken thought. There were no torches; the light came from the Registrar itself, or perhaps from the vast ledger open upon its desk, its pages turning of their own accord with a sound like slow applause.

The Registrar was tall the way winter is tall — not a height but a quality. Its face was smooth as an unwritten page. When it spoke, its voice was dry and courteous and terribly tired.

"Wren Okafor," it said. "Charmwright of Vell. Keeper of the Hollow's only honest map. Father to four strays and one ward."

"You know me," Wren said.

"I keep accounts." The Registrar gestured, and a page turned. "The girl's soul, held in trust against a debt of desperation, fourteen years accruing. You wish to redeem it."

"I wish to take her home whole."

"All debts may be redeemed here. That is the courtesy of this court." The smooth face inclined. "But nothing is given, Wren Okafor. Only traded. And you own so little."

"I own my craft."

"Your craft is written into your hands. Take it and you cannot sign." A pause. "Your memories? We have shelves of those. They are common as rain."

Behind him, Wren heard Brannick shift, Merrin's breath catch. They were listening. Of course they were. He had told them everything in the pass except the one thing he'd decided alone, in the dark of the ledger-room, and he would tell them nothing now either, because some mercies are also lies.

"What about my name?" Wren asked.

The Registrar went very still. Stillness, on that unwritten face, was an expression — and it was hunger.

"A name freely given," it said, softer. "Do you know how long it has been since anyone offered one? Names are stolen here, inherited here, squandered here. But given—" The pages of the great ledger fluttered, eager. "A given name is struck from every record. From every memory. Every mind that holds it would wake tomorrow unable to find it, because it will not be lost. It will be *unwritten*. Maps will show no trail of yours. Letters will bear no signature. Your mother's lullabies will carry a gap shaped exactly like you."

"And Tamsin?"

"Whole. Mark dissolved. Debt paid in full, with interest, for a name freely given is worth more than the sum of every charm you ever sold." The Registrar leaned close, and its voice dropped to something almost tender. "I will be honest with you, charmwright, because honesty is rare down here and I am starved of it. I do not want your name for its power. I want it because I have been no one for so long that I had forgotten being someone has a taste. Your name would be the first thing I have ever been *given*."

"That's very sad," said Tamsin.

Everyone turned. She had stepped forward out of the family knot, chin up, wrist glowing like a small dawn.

"Tamsin—" Wren began.

"No. Listen." Her voice shook and held. "Fourteen years ago my mother sold me because she loved me and couldn't feed me. Now you're going to sell yourself because you love me and can't lose me. This court runs on desperate love and I think it's disgusting and I think it's the bravest thing there is." She wiped her nose with her sleeve, fiercely. "But if you do this, I'm keeping your maps. All of them. Every one. And I'm going to learn your handwriting better than you ever did."

The Registrar watched this with the rapt attention of a creature reading its favorite book for the hundredth time.

"Those are the terms," it said. "She keeps the maps. Fitting. Paper remembers longer than flesh."

Wren looked at his strange, ragged, impossible family — Brannick scowling because scowling was how he loved people, Merrin already crying without shame, Sable clutching Tamsin's hand — and he smiled.

"Then I accept," he said. "Freely. Gladly. Write it down."

* * *

They signed it at midnight, because courts beneath mountains keep honest hours even when they deal in dishonest goods.

The family slept in the white antechamber, exhausted by mercy, and Wren sat alone at the Registrar's desk with a pen that weighed as much as a promise. The contract lay before him, its terms simple and enormous. At the bottom, a single line waited for his name.

"Do you understand what happens when you finish?" the Registrar asked. It stood beside him, and up close Wren could see that its blank face was not empty at all, but full — crowded with every name it had never been given, pressing against the inside like faces at a window.

"I stop being remembered," Wren said. "And you start being... something."

"Yes." A long silence. "When I take it, I will say your name aloud, once, properly, the way no one ever bothered to. Consider it a receipt."

Wren dipped the pen.

He thought of burning charm-stock in a copper pot while Vell's bells rang the Proscription. He thought of Tamsin arriving on his doorstep with a suitcase and a stare that dared him to send her back. He thought of Merrin's terrible fortunes, Brannick's grumbling kindness, Sable's laugh. He thought: *they will forget me, and it will hurt them, and they will be safe, and that is the trade I have been training for my whole life.*

He wrote his name.

The ink did not dry. It sank — down through the page, through the desk, through the mountain, and out into the world beyond, where twenty years of small kindnesses quietly came unstitched from every mind that held them. In Vell, a neighbor mid-sentence about a charming man downstairs forgot the middle of her story and blamed the wine. On a shelf in a rented room, a map's careful annotations faded to blank margins.

The Registrar lifted its head.

"*Wren Okafor,*" it said aloud, tasting every syllable like spring water after drought. Then, gentler, almost kind: "Sleep now, unnamed one. You did a brave thing in the dark, which is the only place brave things are truly done."

Wren laid down the pen. The world felt lighter, which frightened him more than any weight ever had.

In the antechamber, Tamsin stirred in her sleep, frowning at some dream-shaped absence, her hand closing around the strap of the map case she had claimed as hers.

Morning was coming. Someone would have to tend the fire.

No one would remember who usually did.
