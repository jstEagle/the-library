## 4. Anew

Tamsin woke the way she imagined a bell must feel after it had been rung — hollowed, resonant, and strangely light. The ache behind her ribs, the one she had carried so long she'd stopped noticing it, was gone. Her wrist was bare and unmarked. She pressed two fingers to the place where the soul-mark had glowed like a banked coal and found only smooth skin.

*Whole,* she thought. *I'm whole.*

Then she saw the stranger.

He sat at the edge of their campfire with his sleeves rolled up, turning a spit of river fish with the absent competence of someone who had done it a thousand times. He was ordinary in every way a man could be — brown hair going grey at the temples, ink stains on his fingers, a face you would forget on purpose. And yet her chest did something complicated when she looked at him, the way a compass needle does when it finds north after years of spinning.

"Good," he said, without looking up. "You're awake. Eat before Brannick does; he's been circling that fish like a heron all morning."

Tamsin looked around the fire. Brannick was indeed there, sitting beside Maren and old Sef, and all three of them were watching the stranger with the polite wariness of people who have decided not to mention the obvious.

"I'm sorry," Tamsin said carefully. "Who are you?"

The man's hands went still on the spit. For a moment something moved behind his eyes — not hurt exactly, but the shape of hurt, worn smooth from handling.

"No one," he said. "A traveler. You needed sleep. I kept the fire."

"He's been here since dawn," Maren said slowly, frowning, as though the sentence didn't fit in her mouth. "I think. I can't quite — isn't it strange? I feel as if I ought to know whether he takes sugar in his tea."

"I don't take tea," the stranger said gently. "But thank you for trying."

Brannick stood up, hand drifting toward his axe out of habit rather than threat. "Right. That's enough. A man we don't know, cooking our fish, sitting in our camp like — " He stopped. His brow furrowed deeply, painfully. "Like Wren used to."

The name dropped into the fire and made no sound at all. But everyone flinched.

"Wren," Sef repeated, testing the word like a loose tooth. "Why do I know that name? I don't know that name. I know there's a hole where a name should be, and the hole is shaped like *Wren.*"

Tamsin's heart was hammering now, some deep animal part of her understanding before her mind caught up. She looked at the stranger — really looked — and saw what her family could not: the way he held his shoulders slightly turned, always half-facing the horizon, a cartographer's habit of keeping the map in view. The ink-stained fingers. The small scar on his jaw she had watched form, aged nine, when he'd sliced himself sharpening a quill because he'd insisted on doing everything himself rather than ask help.

"You gave your name," she whispered.

The stranger — Wren — met her eyes. "Ah," he said softly. "You too, then?"

"My whole," she said. "My soul came back. And yours — "

"Is still mine. Names are different from souls, little bird. They wanted the name. It seems they took it thoroughly." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm and rueful, but it wobbled at the edges. "Twenty-two years I spent making sure nobody important knew me. I suppose I finally succeeded beyond all reasonable ambition."

Nobody laughed. It was the kind of joke that needed a shared history to land on, and that was precisely what had been stolen.

Tamsin stood up. Something was rising in her, fierce and clear, and she recognized it as the same feeling she'd had in the Hollow when the pass shifted and she'd refused to let go of Sef's hand. She crossed to the sledge where they kept their most precious cargo: the maps. Twenty years of Wren Okafor's cartography, rolled and oiled and annotated in a hand so distinctive it might have been a signature.

She unrolled the first one across her knees.

"This," she said loudly, "is the ferry road south of Vell. There's an annotation here — see? — *'Maren insists the current is safe. Maren is wrong.'* Dated eleven years ago."

Maren blinked. "That's... my handwriting next to it. *'The current is perfectly safe.'*" She laughed, startled. "We argued about that ferry for a week. Who wrote this?"

"A man named Wren," Tamsin said. "He pulled you out of the water anyway, and you never let him forget it, and he never once said *I told you so*, which if you knew him was an act of heroic restraint."

She unrolled another map. Then another. She worked outward from the paper into memory, reading aloud not just the annotations but the stories stitched between them.

"Here, the mill town at Corrow's Ford. We hid in the granary for three days. Brannick taught me dice and cheated shamelessly. When I caught him, he said cheating was a survival skill and I owed him rent for the lesson." 

Brannick's frown cracked down the middle into something helpless. "I remember the granary. I remember dust and laughing and being told to be quiet by — " He pressed his fists against his eyes. "By someone. Someone short-tempered and clever who smelled like ink."

"That's him," Tamsin said. "That's Wren. He smells like ink and burnt charm-paper and, when he's nervous, like the inside of his own hat."

Sef was crying quietly, without seeming to know it. "The night market," the old man murmured. "There was a night market. Someone bought me honey-cakes when I hadn't eaten in two days. He told me they were stale so I wouldn't feel beholden." He looked up. "Was that him?"

"It was him," Tamsin said.

One by one, she fed them back to themselves. Not the great things — not the flight from Vell, not the bargain beneath the mountain, which lived nowhere in any mind now, including hers, save as a shape of gratitude with nothing at its center. But the small things. The ferry. The granary. The time Maren had mended his coat with thread three shades wrong and he had worn it for a year and called it his lucky coat. The way he hummed when he drew coastlines and went silent when he drew mountains, because mountains, he said, deserved respect.

And piece by piece, like frost melting off a window, they remembered him.

Not all at once. Memory, Tamsin discovered, was less like a door reopening than like a fire being relaid stick by careful stick — and she was willing to sit in the cold as long as the building took. By midday Brannick had crossed the camp and gripped Wren's shoulder hard enough to creak. By afternoon Maren had remembered the argument about the ferry well enough to restart it, and Wren had nearly wept with joy at being told *I told you so* was overdue.

Only Sef came last, standing apart, working through it like a man solving a knot. Finally he said: "If we forgot you, others will too. Everyone who ever knew you. Every debt you're owed, every kindness you paid — unwitnessed now. Is that what you traded? For her?" He nodded at Tamsin.

Wren looked at Tamsin — at the girl whose soul he had carried home through a court older than kingdoms — and answered honestly, as he had learned to do in the dark places.

"Yes."

"Then we'll simply have to witness it again," Sef said briskly, wiping his eyes. "All of it. Starting tonight. I'll expect the honey-cake story told properly, with embellishments."

They broke camp late, walking north out of the shadow of the mountain into country no map had ever charted — which meant, Wren said, settling his satchel on his shoulder with the air of a man handed a gift, that somebody was going to have to draw it.

Tamsin fell into step beside him and pulled a fresh sheet of vellum from the sledge, along with the spare quill she'd started carrying weeks ago, back when she couldn't have told you why.

"What are you doing?" he asked.

"Learning," she said, and began to draw the ridge line. Beneath it, in careful letters, she wrote: *Charted by Tamsin, keeper of the maps of Wren Okafor.*

Wren read it upside down and went very quiet.

"You know," he said at last, voice rough, "most people would have just called me a stranger and taken the fish."

"You're not a stranger." Tamsin shaded in the ridge, steady-handed. "Strangers are people you haven't decided to know yet. This—" she tapped the page, then his arm, then her own chest— "this is deciding. Over and over, if we have to. Every morning, if it comes to that."

Behind them, the mountain kept its silence, and somewhere in its deep courts a lonely Registrar held a name like a candle it could not light. Ahead, the unmapped country opened gold and green in the evening sun, waiting for its first lines.

Tamsin drew them.
