## 1. Charms and Cinders

The rats of Vell knew Rosalind Quintana before any customer did.

They came at dawn, before the shuttered stalls had even yawned open, whiskered faces poking from the drain grates along Copperlane Row. This morning a fat one with a torn ear sat on her doorstep like a tax collector.

"You're early," she murmured, crouching to its level. "That means trouble."

*Grain carts late,* the rat said, in the way rats said things — a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of words that weren't quite words. *Guards took the baker's son. He talked back.*

Rosalind fed it a crust of yesterday's bread and filed the information away. Twelve years in this city, and she'd learned that rats heard everything worth hearing, long before heralds bothered shouting it.

Her stall was modest by design — a folding table of charms and trinkets, nothing that smelled of real magic. Copper bells to ward off headaches (they didn't, but hope was sellable). Knotted red thread for safe travel. Dried lavender sachets against nightmares. A tray of polished river stones that pigeons had told her — for the price of three crumbs each — were considered lucky this season. She priced them accordingly.

"Pigeon intelligence," she said under her breath, arranging them, "is an underrated market indicator."

A gray pigeon on her awning cooed something smug.

By midmorning the row filled with its usual noise: fishwives arguing over eels, a knife-grinder's wheel screaming, children weaving between legs with stolen apples. Rosalind sold a headache bell to a clerk with a tyrant of a supervisor, knotted travel-thread for a grandmother whose grandson sailed north, and lied warmly to everyone about how effective her wares were.

It was good work, harmless work. That was the point. A woman who sold lucky stones could not possibly be the thing the crown called *gifted* — could not hear the mice in her walls complaining about the cold, could not ask the gulls which ships carried plague, could not, on one terrible night six years ago, have begged a sparrowhawk to lead a lost child home and then spent a week sick with fear that someone had seen.

No one had seen. She'd made sure of it ever since. Small bargains only. Quiet ones. Never more than two animals in a day.

She was wrapping a lavender sachet when the pigeons went up all at once — every bird on Copperlane Row bursting skyward like a handful of thrown gravel.

Then came the drums.

They came down the Processional Way in formation: six crown heralds in white-and-gold, flanked by a dozen soldiers, and behind them — Rosalind's stomach dropped before her mind caught up — the witch-finders. Three of them, gray-robed, faces like closed doors. And hounds. Four great beasts on chain leads, black-coated, yellow-eyed, bred for one purpose: to smell what the crown called *the taint*.

The crowd hushed the way water goes still before it freezes.

A herald mounted the steps of the assize hall and unrolled a scroll sealed with the thirteenth moon — the black disc Vell's children were taught to fear in bedtime stories.

"By decree of the Crown," he read, voice trained to carry, "the Edict of the Thirteenth Moon. Whereas the gift of beast-speaking is a corruption of soul and a treason of nature, let it be known: all persons so gifted are hereby named anathema. They shall be sought. They shall be tried. They shall be burned." A pause, letting it settle into the crowd like ash. "Reward of one hundred crowns for information leading to the taking of any speaker. The hunt begins at moonrise."

One hundred crowns. Rosalind kept her face pleasant and vacant, the face of a charm-seller with nothing inside her head but profit margins. Around her, neighbors glanced at neighbors — and she saw it begin, the arithmetic behind people's eyes. What was old Marta's cat obsession worth? Why did the tanner's boy never flinch around dogs? A hundred crowns bought a lot of neighborliness.

*This is how it starts,* she thought. *Not with fire. With prices.*

The procession moved on. And as it passed her stall, one of the hounds stopped.

It turned its great head — slow, deliberate — and looked at her. Not at the crowd. At *her*. Its nostrils flared. Its ears pricked forward. And then, softly, horribly, it whined. A sound of recognition. The way a dog whines at the grave of someone it loved.

Rosalind's hands went still on the counter. *Easy,* she told herself. *Dogs stare at everyone.* But this wasn't staring. This dog's gaze held something older than training, something that reached through her pleasant vacant face and found the girl underneath who spoke rat.

Its handler yanked the chain. "Move, curse you." The hound went, but its head stayed turned until the crowd swallowed it, and its last look was almost apologetic.

Rosalind sold nothing else that afternoon. Her hands shook too badly to knot thread.

* * *

The rat with the torn ear came back at dusk, and it brought friends.

"They're going door to door in Tanners' Quarter," she translated, kneeling in her darkened shop. "*Taken four already. The hound smells through stone. Through walls.*"

"Can they smell me?" she asked. "Did they mark this street?"

The rats conferred in a rustle of claws. *The yellow-eyed one remembers a name,* one said. *It keeps saying it, inside itself. Quintana. Quintana.*

The floor tilted. Rosalind gripped her table.

Her grandmother's name. Her grandmother, who had vanished from Vell twelve years ago, the night Rosalind fled her childhood village, the night her mother pressed a purse of coins into her hands and said *never tell anyone what you can do, never say your true surname aloud, be Rosalind the charm-seller and nothing else.* Her grandmother, whose last letter — burned within the hour — had spoken of a duty, a door, a country that no longer appeared on maps. Rosalind had spent half a lifetime telling herself those were the fevered words of a dying woman.

But a witch-finder's hound remembered her name. Hounds didn't remember names. Unless they'd been taught them, generation after generation, bred to one family's scent like a key cut for a single lock.

"Oh," she said quietly. "Oh, they're not hunting speakers at all."

The rats waited, patient, for her to explain. She couldn't. She was too busy watching twelve years of careful life come apart in her mind like wet paper.

She worked fast through the evening, pretending it was an ordinary night. She packed her valuables into a go-bag she'd kept ready since her second year in Vell — coin, a knife, dried food, the one true thing she owned: her grandmother's letter, hidden in a false bottom these dozen years. She fed the pigeons extra and asked them to watch the Processional Way. She paid the rats in bread and cheese to tell her if boots came up Copperlane Row.

And all the while she told herself: *maybe the hound was wrong. Maybe it whined at nothing. Maybe—*

The pigeons screamed at midnight.

*Fire,* they said, all together, a shriek of wings and warning. *Fire in the market!*

She was out her door in seconds. Down the lane, the great canvas awnings of the Grand Market stood silhouetted against an orange glow — and the glow was spreading, licking up the fishmongers' quarter, swallowing the spice stalls, painting every window in Copperlane Row the color of ruin. Figures moved through the smoke with torches. Not looters. Soldiers. Setting fires with methodical calm while other soldiers blocked the streets and turned back the bucket chains.

"It's the edict," a man sobbed beside her. "They say a speaker was seen here. They say the whole market's *tainted*—"

The Grand Market. Where she'd traded gossip with gulls for eleven years. Where old Marta sold herbs and hummed to her bees — and the bees always landed on her first, always. Where the tanner's boy could quiet a rearing horse with a murmur. Where half the vendors were poor and strange and kind, and every one of them now knew that a speaker had been *seen*, and every one of them would remember the charm-woman whose stall the hound had stared at.

Rosalind backed away from the light, her shadow stretching long and guilty behind her.

There was no maybe left. There was only the arithmetic she'd watched bloom in her neighbors' eyes, and a hundred crowns, and a hound that knew her name, and a city closing like a fist.

She ran. Not toward the fire — away from it, down the drainage alleys where the rats guided her through the dark, past the burning market's heat on her cheek, out along the river road where the mud hid her footprints and the fog swallowed her shape. Behind her, Vell burned orange against the stars, and somewhere in that glow a witch-finder's hound lifted its head and began to bay.

Ahead, past the last lamps of the city, the road ran north into darkness — toward the borderlands, toward the fens, toward the place on no map that smugglers whispered about when they thought no one was listening.

Rosalind had listened. Rosalind had always listened.

She pulled her grandmother's letter from her coat, and in the light of the burning city behind her, read the words she'd never dared believe:

*If they come for you, little bird, run north. Find the Hollow. Knock, and speak, and the door will know your blood.*

The hound's bay rose behind her, closer now.

Rosalind Quintana ran.
