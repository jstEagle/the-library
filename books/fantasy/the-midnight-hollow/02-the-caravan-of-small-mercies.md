## 2. The Caravan of Small Mercies

The ashes of Vell's market still clung to Rosalind's hair when she found the caravan.

It waited in the shadow of the tannery wall on the city's western edge, where the cobbles gave way to mud and the mud gave way to the old road that no respectable person traveled. Six wagons, tarred canvas over their loads, mules stamping in the cold. A man stood at the lead wagon with his arms crossed, watching her come with the flat patience of someone who had watched a hundred desperate people approach and turned away ninety-nine of them.

"You're burning daylight," he said. "And you're not buying."

"I'm buying." She stopped three paces off — close enough to be heard, far enough to run. "Passage west. As far as the road goes."

"The road doesn't go anywhere. It stops at the Hollow, and then it stops being a road." He was younger than his voice suggested — thirty, maybe, dark-haired, with a scar splitting one eyebrow and hands that looked like they'd broken things professionally. "I'm Dario Voss. I move cargo and people who don't want to be counted. I don't move people who can't pay."

Rosalind opened her satchel and showed him what remained of her life: a handful of silver, a charm-bracelet that had survived the fire because it had been on her wrist when the market went up, and a small tin of dried lavender that smelled, even now, like every ordinary morning she'd ever pretended to have.

Dario weighed the silver in his palm without counting it. His eyes flicked up — quick, assessing, cataloguing her the way he'd probably catalogue contraband. Burned hem. Soot under her nails. No luggage worth stealing.

"Vell market burned last night," he said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"They're saying the gifted did it. That a beast-speaker set the whole row alight rather than be taken." He said the word *beast-speaker* the way a man tests ice before stepping on it. "You hear anything about that?"

"I heard screaming," Rosalind said. It wasn't a lie. "I heard the heralds reading their pretty edict while the stalls were still smoking. I heard that anyone with a gift gets a rope now, and anyone who hides one gets a worse one."

Something moved behind his eyes — not sympathy exactly, but recognition, the look of a man checking a lock he already knew worked. "Fifty silver for passage to the edge of the Hollow. You walk from there. You eat what we eat. And if the witch-finders stop us, you keep your mouth shut and let me do the talking, because my tongue has bought off more soldiers than your silver ever will."

"Forty."

He almost smiled. "Forty-five, and you take a shift driving."

They shook on it. His grip was careful, deliberate — a smuggler's handshake, sealing terms rather than greeting. Rosalind climbed onto the second wagon between a woman clutching an infant and an old man who smelled of river water and fear, and told herself the tightness in her chest was relief.

It was not relief. Relief didn't taste like this — like a door closing behind her with her own hand on the latch.

* * *

The caravan traveled by night and hid by day, which suited Rosalind fine, because night was when her gift got loudest and she'd learned long ago that silence came easier in motion.

The horses were dying.

Not all of them — but two of the six draft animals had been driven too hard on some previous run, and their lungs were full of fluid that no ostler would spend coin fixing. She heard them from her first hour in the wagon: a wet, labored whisper beneath the creak of axles, the way drowning men talk to themselves.

*Cold,* said the bay mare on the left trace. *Cold in the chest. Cold going down. The tall one feeds us but does not look.*

*Does not look,* agreed the gelding behind her, whose voice was slower, thicker, a mind going gray at the edges. *Looking costs. Everything costs.*

Rosalind stared straight ahead at the back of Dario's head and said nothing. This was the discipline of twelve years: hear everything, answer nothing. Beasts talked to her the way weather happened — constantly, whether she wanted it or not — and every word she answered was a thread some sharp-eyed person might follow back to the gallows. In Vell she'd built a life out of small permissions. Rats traded gossip for crumbs. Pigeons carried messages for seed. The transactions were tiny, deniable, invisible. But these horses were asking for nothing. They were just dying, out loud, and she could do nothing about either fact, and the effort of *not listening* sat on her chest like a stone all through the first night's drive.

Near dawn they halted in a birch stand off the road, canvas down, fires unlit. Rosalind walked the line of wagons on the pretext of stretching her legs and stopped beside the bay mare.

The horse's eye found her immediately. Animals always knew. It was the first thing she'd learned as a girl — before she learned to hide it — that beasts saw her plainly, without any of the costumes humans required.

*Small one,* the mare said. *Your hands are kind-hands. Will you fix the cold?*

"No," Rosalind whispered. The word hurt coming out. "I'm sorry. I don't know how to fix the cold."

*Then will you stay? Staying helps. Kind-hands help, even empty.*

So Rosalind stayed. She pressed her palm to the mare's sweat-dark neck and hummed something tuneless, an old thing her grandmother used to sing, and felt the animal's breathing ease by a fraction — not healed, not saved, just less alone. It cost her nothing and it cost her everything. Behind her, boots crunched through dead leaves.

"She won't make the Hollow," Dario said.

Rosalind didn't turn. Her hand was still on the mare's neck; there was no hiding what she'd been doing, only what she'd been doing *with* it. "No."

"You knew that?"

"I guessed. She breathes like a bellows with a hole in it."

Dario was quiet a moment. "There's a vetting station at Marrow Ford. I'll trade her there for a sound one. Better than letting her drop on the road."

It was practical. It was even, in its way, merciful. Rosalind nodded and walked back to her wagon, and only when she was seated did she realize her hands were shaking — not from fear but from the strain of having spoken gently to a dying animal while a stranger watched, of measuring every syllable so that kindness sounded like commerce.

This was the price. Nobody told you the price of silence was paid hourly, forever. Twelve years of it, and the bill never shrank.

* * *

Marrow Ford announced itself by its checkpoint.

They saw it an hour before they reached it — a palisade thrown across the ford where the road narrowed between the river and a rockfall, lanterns strung along its face, and the particular bored cruelty of crown soldiers visible even at distance. Dario reined up the column on a low hill and studied it through a spyglass for a long time.

"Four men. One officer. They're shaking down traffic, not hunting anyone specific." He lowered the glass. "We go through as a wool convoy. Papers say we're bound for Hessel. Everyone keeps their eyes down and their mouths shut."

"And if they don't believe the papers?" the woman with the infant asked.

"Then I believe harder." Dario passed the wagon reins to his driver and moved down the line, murmuring instructions. When he reached Rosalind's wagon he paused. "You especially. New faces draw questions. If anyone asks, you're my cousin's widow. Grief keeps people quiet."

"I'll manage."

"Don't manage. *Nothing.* Nothing is the safest thing a person can be at a checkpoint."

She hated how well she understood that instruction. She had been *nothing* for twelve years — a harmless woman selling harmless charms, a shopkeeper so forgettable that even the neighbors misremembered her name. She could do nothing in her sleep.

Except the mule wouldn't let her.

It happened fast. They rolled down toward the ford in loose order, papers ready, and the soldier at the palisade gate waved the first two wagons through with the lazy greed of a man collecting his unofficial toll — and then the wind shifted. Rosalind smelled it a heartbeat before the mule did: blood, from the butcher's cart ahead of them, a slaughtered pig lashed to its bed, draining as it bounced.

The mule screamed.

It reared so hard its front hooves cleared the wagon bed, wrenching the traces, slamming its skull against the shafts, and the driver — a boy of maybe sixteen — lost the reins entirely. The animal spun, shrieking, blind with terror, and the whole careful choreography of the convoy shattered around it. Soldiers turned. The officer's hand went to his sword. Somewhere behind Rosalind, a child began to cry.

*Fire-fire-fire-run-run-* the mule's mind roared, all sense gone, pure panic with nowhere to go.

Every eye was on the animal. Which meant in ten seconds the soldiers' eyes would travel from the animal to the boy who couldn't hold it, to the wagons, to the papers, to the people. Dario was already moving, saying something smooth about a young teamster, offering coin — and the officer was refusing it, because panic made officials suspicious, and suspicion made them thorough.

Rosalind didn't decide. That was the truth she would carry afterward: there was no decision, no weighing of risk, just her body moving off the wagon seat into the space between the mule and the drawn sword, her hands rising, and the words coming out of her in the deep speech — not loud, barely above a breath, but pitched the way her grandmother had taught her, down where beasts listen with their bones.

*Easy. Easy, brother. The fire is past. The fire is years ago. You are safe, and I am here, and here is safe, and you may put your fear down now.*

The mule stopped.

It stopped the way a storm stops — suddenly, completely, mid-shriek, its ears swinging forward, its wild eye fixing on her with an expression no one standing nearby could mistake for anything natural. Then it sighed, a great shuddering exhale, and lowered its head against her shoulder like a child, trembling, quiet.

The ford went silent except for the river.

Rosalind became aware of the silence the way one becomes aware of falling. The officer staring. The soldiers staring. Dario, five feet away, absolutely still, looking at her with an expression she couldn't read and would think about for days.

"Witch-work," someone said softly. Not an accusation. Almost a question.

"It's a trick of handling," Rosalind said, her voice steady while her pulse hammered. "My father broke mules. You get your body between them and the thing they fear, and you breathe slow, and they borrow your calm." She kept her hand on the mule's neck, kept breathing slowly and visibly, making a performance of the mundane explanation. "Anyone can learn it. Most people don't bother."

The officer chewed on that. Witch-hunts made everyone jumpy, but they also made everyone eager to disbelieve — nobody wanted to find a witch in a wool convoy and deal with the paperwork. He looked at Dario. "Your teamster?"

"My cousin's widow. Grief makes her strange with animals." Dario's voice didn't change at all. "The mule's calm now. We're holding up your road."

A long moment. Then the officer waved them through with two fingers, already turning to the next cart, the next toll, the next small extortion.

They crossed the ford and climbed the far bank, and only when the palisade dropped out of sight did Dario rein alongside Rosalind's wagon and bring both teams to a walk.

"That was a good story," he said quietly. "About your father."

"It was a true story."

"Mm." He looked forward at the road, at the fog beginning to gather in the distant folds of land where the maps grew vague. "My father sold me to a caravan master when I was nine. Also true. Doesn't mean I tell it at checkpoints."

Rosalind said nothing.

"You breathed at that animal," Dario said. "That's all. You leaned close and breathed, and it forgot it wanted to die. I've seen horse-whisperers, lady. That wasn't whispering." He clicked his tongue and his wagon drew ahead. "Sleep. Long road tomorrow."

She slept badly. The mule's borrowed calm stayed with her like a warmth she couldn't put down, and beneath it ran a colder current: he'd seen. Whatever he chose to believe, whatever he chose to do with what he believed, he had seen.

On the third night, when the last farms had fallen away and the world dissolved into the grey breath of the Midnight Hollow, Rosalind noticed the crows.

They flew alongside the caravan for an hour — not following, *accompanying*, wheeling in perfect formation above the lead wagon. And when she listened, half against her will, she caught the shape of what they were saying to each other, over and over, like a name passed down a line:

*The keeper comes. The keeper comes. Tell the ones inside: the keeper comes.*
