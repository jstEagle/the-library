## 3. Where Maps Go to Die

Dario Voss had a rule about lying: never lie to someone whose life depended on your honesty. It was the reason he'd never married, never taken a partner, never let anyone ride in the front of his wagon but himself. The road was honest work for a dishonest man — that was the arrangement he'd made with whatever gods still bothered to watch smugglers.

Then Rosalind Quintana climbed into his caravan with ash still in her hair and paid her passage in coins she'd pulled from a rat's nest, and every rule he had started dying quietly.

* * *

The checkpoint at Grey Ford was where it began.

He'd watched her from the driver's bench while the crown's soldiers picked through his wagons with their stupid, thorough hands. One of his mules — Bramble, a gray gelding with a bad heart and a worse temper — had smelled the soldiers' torches and come apart screaming, kicking the cart rails hard enough to crack one.

Dario knew what happened next. He'd seen it a hundred times: the soldier would put a bolt in the animal, the caravan would lose half its goods, everyone would learn again that fear cost money.

Instead Rosalind stepped out of the line of refugees, walked straight up to a panicking mule surrounded by armed men, and put her hand on its neck.

She didn't whisper. Dario was close enough to know she didn't whisper. Her lips moved, yes, but no sound came out that he could hear — and yet the mule heard something. Bramble went still like a candle pinched out. Leaned into her palm. Breathed.

And then the crows.

There were crows on the checkpoint's hanging cage — the cage they kept for smugglers who argued — and when Rosalind turned away from the mule, every crow on that cage turned with her. Six black heads, tracking her like compass needles finding north. She looked at them once, briefly, the way you glance at an old friend across a crowded market, and they lifted off together, wheeling south.

Dario stood on his bench and told himself he'd imagined it. Told himself the mule had simply tired. Told himself anything except the truth, which was that he was carrying a witch into the Hollow on behalf of the crown's witch-finders, and the crown's witch-finders would burn her alive if they ever learned what he now suspected.

That night, writing his report by the coals — *caravan intact, twelve refugees, no sign of the Quintana woman's gift* — his pen hovered over the page for a long time before he lied in ink for the first time.

It did not feel like freedom. It felt like stepping off a cliff and discovering, mid-fall, that you were still falling.

* * *

Three days later, the road ended.

Not metaphorically. Dario had made this run twice before, and both times the map simply gave up — the parchment fading to blank parchment past a place called the Weeping Stones, as if the cartographer had run out of nerve. Beyond that, there was only the Midnight Hollow, and the Hollow had no road because roads are agreements, and nothing on the other side of those stones had ever agreed to anything.

He gathered them around the last fire on the last legal mile of earth. Twelve refugees plus Rosalind, thirteen souls, and he hated that arithmetic more than he could say.

"Past the Stones," he said, "there's no turning back. Not because I'm a tyrant. Because the fog doesn't permit it. You'll see." They always thought he was being dramatic. They always stopped thinking that within an hour.

A thin woman with a feverish child asked him what was out there. Dario looked at Rosalind, who was watching him with those level dark eyes, and said, "Out there is the country that isn't."

"And you've been?" she asked.

Twice, he almost said. And both times I came back with wagons full of Ostravan silver and no memory of how I'd earned it, and both times the chancellor's men were waiting at the border with questions I couldn't answer, and my sister has been in their cells ever since the second time.

"I've been," is what he actually said.

* * *

The fog took the road behind them exactly the way he'd promised, and it was worse than he'd remembered.

They crossed the Weeping Stones at dusk — great leaning monoliths slick with condensation, weeping real tears of water that ran uphill — and by the time the last wagon cleared them, Dario turned on his bench and saw the way back dissolving. Not covered by mist. Dissolving. The ruts of his own wheels smoothing out behind him like a scar closing. The Stones themselves softening at their edges, going grey and uncertain, becoming the idea of stones rather than stones.

One of the refugees started to scream. Dario let her. Screaming was honest.

Rosalind didn't scream. She stood in the back of his wagon with her arms folded against the cold, watching the world unmake itself behind them with an expression he'd seen once before — on a man watching his own house burn with his family inside, who had somehow already known it would happen.

"You knew," he said to her, low, under the crying. "You didn't flinch."

"I guessed," she said. "Places that aren't on maps have to keep themselves that way somehow."

"That's not how geography works."

"No," she agreed. "I don't imagine it is. How does *your* geography work, Dario? Who taught you the way through?"

And there it was — the question he'd been dreading since Grey Ford, arriving without anger, which was so much worse than anger. He kept his eyes on the fog ahead. "I follow the old markers. My father's trade. Before him, my grandfather's."

"Liar," said a voice above them.

Both of them looked up. A crow sat on the wagon's rail, feathers ruffled, regarding Dario with one flat eye. More were settling along the top of the canvas — a dozen, two dozen, silent, riding the caravan like passengers.

"The birds have opinions," Dario said carefully.

"The birds have *memories*," Rosalind said softly, and Dario watched her tilt her head, listening to something he could not hear, and the look on her face changed — sharpened, saddened. "They say you've made this crossing four times, not two. They say you stop at a cairn on the third night and leave a letter under a stone. They say men in grey take the letters and leave coin."

The fog pressed close. Somewhere behind them, the last of the road finished forgetting itself.

"They say," Rosalind continued, very quietly, "that the coin goes to a prison in Vell. To a woman named Sera Voss."

Every muscle in Dario's body locked at once. His hands stayed easy on the reins — twenty years of smuggling had taught his hands treason against his own terror — but inside, everything was falling.

"You've known since Grey Ford," he managed.

"I suspected. The crows confirmed it tonight." She didn't sound triumphant. That was the unbearable part. She sounded tired, and kind, and that combination made him want to put the wagon in a ditch. "Why haven't you turned me in, Dario? Your handlers want the keeper's bloodline. I'm sitting in your wagon. One letter under one stone."

"Because—" He stopped. Started again. "Do you know what they do to traitors' sisters? Do you know what Sera looks like after three years? I do. I visit twice a year. I sit across a table and she asks me how the business is and I tell her the business is fine and she knows, she's always known, what I do to keep her breathing—"

"So you feed them a witch instead."

"I *was going to*." The words tore out of him raw. "The first night. At Grey Ford, when I saw the mule — I wrote it down. I wrote everything down. And then I sat there with the pen in my hand and I thought about you giving your coins to the rat's nest woman at the market, I thought about you walking up to a mule that could have broken your skull, I thought — what is she, what did she ever do to the crown except be born — and I lied. First lie I've ever told them in ink. And now I can't stop, because every report I falsify is another day they don't find her body in the river, and every day I don't turn you in is another day I choose between the person in front of me and the person behind me, and I am *so tired*, Rosalind, of choosing."

The silence that followed was long. The crows rode the rails, saying nothing, which from crows was a kind of mercy.

"The person behind you," Rosalind said at last, "is being held by men who erased an entire country from existence. Did you know that? That's what's out here. That's what the Hollow hides. Not smuggler roads. A whole nation the crown decided never happened." She shook her head slowly. "I don't forgive you, Dario. But I understand the shape of the cage you're in. I've lived in one myself for twelve years."

Ahead, the fog was beginning to glow. Faintly, impossibly, like moonlight stored in milk — though the moon hadn't risen yet.

"What happens when we reach the end of your markers?" he asked.

"I don't know," she said. "But the crows do. They've been trying to tell us since the Stones." She lifted her face to the birds along the rail and spoke — aloud this time, no hiding, no shame, her voice ringing strange and clear in the fog. "*Lead us.*"

And every crow rose at once, a black river pouring forward into the glowing mist, and the fog opened ahead of the wagons like a door.

Behind them, where the road had been, there was nothing at all.
