## 4. The Country That Isn't

The fog did not lift. It opened.

Rosalind had no better word for it, standing at the front of the stalled caravan with Dario's lantern guttering in her hand. For three days the Midnight Hollow had swallowed them — road dissolving behind the last wagon like a sentence someone kept forgetting to finish — and then, without warning, the gray parted down the middle, the way a curtain parts for a play that has already begun without you.

Moonlight poured through.

Not the thin, borrowed light of Vell's nights, but moonlight the way honey is sweet — thick, deliberate, filling the valley below them from rim to rim. Fields of silver grass. Orchards heavy with pale fruit. A river running slow and luminous as spilled milk. And rising out of it all, on the far side of the water, a city: towers of white stone, bridges strung with lamps that burned without flame, streets laid out in patterns Rosalind's eye kept trying to follow and failing, the way you can't quite trace a face in a dream.

"Tell me I'm not the only one seeing this," Dario said quietly beside her.

"You're not."

"That's unfortunate." He rubbed his jaw. "I was hoping it was the water."

Behind them, the caravan had gone silent — forty-odd refugees pressed into seven wagons, people who had paid him everything they had to be carried toward a rumor. An old woman clutching a birdcage with nothing in it. Twin boys who hadn't spoken since Vell. A one-eyed soldier from a war nobody could name. They stood now among their bundled lives and stared down at a country that could not exist.

Because it didn't. Rosalind knew the maps. She'd copied them often enough in her shop, tracing coastlines for charm-bottles, and there was nothing here. The Hollow appeared on royal charts as a smudge, an ink stain shaped like a warning: *Here be nothing.* The crown taxed the road around it and pretended the road through it was superstition.

Yet here was a river. Here were lamplit bridges.

And here — she felt it before she heard it — here were voices.

Not human ones. The grass itself was murmuring, a low green whisper rolling up the hillside like tide. The orchard trees creaked conversationally to one another. Somewhere downslope, something large and patient exhaled.

"They're talking," she said before she could stop herself.

Dario looked at her. "What is?"

"The grass. The trees. Everything here is—" She shut her mouth. Three days she'd held the silence, letting the dying horses' complaints pass through her unanswered, biting her own tongue bloody rather than let Dario hear her answer them. But this place pulled at her gift like a hand closing around hers. "Nothing," she finished. "It's nothing."

"It doesn't sound like nothing."

"Then you should get your ears seen to along with your eyes."

He almost smiled. He'd been doing that more since the checkpoint — since the mule, since the moment her voice had slipped out of her like a blade leaving its sheath and the animal had gone calm and every refugee in the line had stared at her, and Dario had said nothing at all. She didn't know which was worse: his silence or what it might contain.

She left him with the wagons and walked down into the light alone.

* * *

The grass parted around her feet as though it had been expecting her.

That was the first wrongness she let herself notice. In Vell, beasts flinched from her unless she worked at them — pigeons scattered, dogs bared teeth, rats kept to the walls. Here everything leaned *toward* her. The whispering rose as she descended, words forming inside it, half-heard:

*—the keeper's blood, the keeper's blood, told you, told you—*

"I'm not a keeper of anything," she said aloud, and the whole hillside shushed her like a congregation.

At the river's edge stood a ferry with no ferryman, a flat barge of white wood tied by a rope of braided silver. As she approached, the rope untied itself. She stepped aboard because there seemed nothing else to do, and the barge slid out across the milk-slow water without being asked, carrying her toward towers that grew larger and stranger the nearer she came — windows lit warm gold, laundry strung between spires, smoke rising from chimneys in curls that drifted *upward against the wind*, politely ignoring physics.

People lined the far bank when she landed. Dozens of them. Hundreds. They had come out of the lamplit streets in nightclothes and work aprons, and they were staring at her the way starving people stare at bread.

A woman pushed through the crowd — tall, gray-haired, wearing a soldier's coat cut down for civilian shoulders. She took Rosalind's hands and turned them over, palms up, examining them as if reading something written there.

"You crossed the fog," the woman said. Her accent was old-fashioned, vowels rounded like river stones. "Alive. With a caravan. How many?"

"Forty-one. Maybe forty-two. I've stopped counting the children."

"Alive?" Something moved through the crowd at that word, a shudder, like wind through wheat. "All alive? Through the Hollow?"

"The Hollow didn't seem interested in killing us. It seemed interested in—" Rosalind hesitated. "*Erasing* us. The road behind us is gone. I watched it go."

"Yes," the woman said softly. "It does that. It keeps us safe by keeping us forgotten." She straightened, and her voice changed, lifted, took on the cadence of announcement. "Strangers have come through the door-country! Forty-one souls, alive and whole!"

The crowd made a sound Rosalind had never heard a crowd make — not cheering, not weeping, but both at once, tangled together, joy with grief running through it like a vein of ore. People reached out to touch her sleeves, her hair, the hem of her coat, gently, reverently, as if she were proof of something.

An old man knelt in the mud of the bank and began to pray — not to any god she recognized. "Blessed be the thirteenth turn," he murmured. "Blessed be the door."

"What door?" Rosalind said. "What are you talking about?"

But the woman in the soldier's coat had gone very still, and she was looking at Rosalind with an expression that made the skin of her arms prickle. Not wonder. Recognition.

"Your name," the woman said. "Give me your mother's name. Your grandmother's."

"Why?"

"*Give it to me.*"

"Quintana," Rosalind said. "My name is Rosalind Quintana. My grandmother was Alma Quintana, and if you're about to tell me she owed your family money, I should warn you I'm in no position to—"

The woman staggered back a full step. Around her, the crowd went silent so completely that Rosalind could hear the river lapping, the lamps humming, the grass whispering its one insistent phrase: *the keeper's blood, the keeper's blood.*

"Maren," the woman called, not turning her head. Her voice had gone strange and thin. "MAREN. Come out of the tower. Now."

* * *

They brought her to the tallest of the white towers, up stairs worn hollow by centuries of feet, to a chamber at the top where the ceiling opened to the sky through a circular oculus, and where an old woman lay dying on a bed of woven moonlight.

That was the only way Rosalind could describe it later, and she hated descriptions she couldn't defend. But the bed glowed. It held the old woman the way water holds a leaf. Her skin was translucent at the edges, her hands like paper held up to a lamp, and her eyes — when she turned them on Rosalind — were the pale silver of the moon itself.

"This is Maren," said the woman in the soldier's coat, whose name, Rosalind had learned on the stairs, was Queen Isolde, and who was dying too, though slower, some rot in her lungs that the luminous air could not cure. "Keeper of the Door. And you will explain to me, girl, exactly how much you know."

"About what?"

"About your grandmother."

"My grandmother sold charms." Rosalind's voice came out flatter than she intended. "Harmless ones. Love knots and luck-bringers and little glass bells. She taught my mother, and my mother taught me, and none of it ever amounted to anything except a living and a lot of practice pretending I was ordinary."

Isolde laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it. "Ordinary. Yes. The crown taught your family to say that word so well you believed it." She lowered herself onto a stool beside the bed, wincing. "Girl, your grandmother was Alma Quintana, last Keeper of the Door of Ostrava, and she did not sell charms. She sold *exits*. Every trinket in that shop of yours was a key that didn't know what it unlocked. Why do you think the gifted of Vell found their way to you? Why do you think the hunted always seemed to find your street?"

Rosalind sat down. Her legs simply declined to hold her.

"She used to say things," Rosalind managed. "When I was small. She said our family kept something the world wasn't allowed to remember. I thought it was guilt. I thought she meant a crime."

"A crime." Isolde nodded slowly. "In a sense. Ours, or theirs, depending on where you stand. Two hundred years ago this country stretched from the Hollow to the sea. Then a chancellor of the crown committed an atrocity here — I will not sully your first hour with its details — and rather than be remembered for it, he did something worse. He hired the greatest speakers of his age and they spoke Ostrava *out of the world*. Off the maps. Out of the songs. Out of memory itself. Everyone who ever heard of us forgot us mid-sentence. The borders closed like a wound."

"But you're here."

"We are what was inside the wound." Isolde gestured at the glowing room, the tower, the moon-drowned fields beyond the window. "This pocket between moonbeams. Sustained by the door your grandmother's line kept — a door that opens once every cycle, on the thirteenth moon-turn, wide enough to let the lost slip in. Refugees. The disappeared. The ones the crown makes vanish." Her eyes glittered. "You've been selling passage to a country, Rosalind Quintana. You just never knew it was yours."

Through the oculus above, the moon hung fat and white, and Rosalind saw — with the vertigo of a floor giving way — that around its edge, faint as a watermark, ran a second rim of light. A ring within the moon. Growing.

"How long until the thirteenth turn?" she heard herself ask.

"Nine days," Maren said. Her voice was rust and silk, and Rosalind started — she had thought the old woman asleep. Those silver eyes had been open the entire time, fixed on her face. "Nine days, and the door stands open for one night. And on that night, child, I need you to understand what the witch-hunters truly hunt."

"Not witches," Rosalind said slowly. The shape of it was assembling itself in her mind, terrible and obvious. "Not the gifted. Or not only. They're searching for—"

"The key," Isolde said. "The door answers one bloodline. One voice. If it opens on the thirteenth turn and no Quintana speaks to it, the pocket begins to fail. We fade. All of us — every soul the crown ever erased, every refugee your smuggler carried in — gone like breath off a mirror. And if the crown finds the last Quintana first, they won't ask her to open anything." The queen leaned close, and her breath rattled like a dying fire. "They'll make sure the door never speaks again. They will kill the bloodline, Rosalind. They have been trying for two hundred years. Your grandmother hid well. You hid better. But the hounds have caught the scent at last, and nine days from tonight—"

Above them, faint and far away, across the silver fields, a sound rolled over Ostrava that did not belong to it.

A dog, baying.

One note only, deep and rhythmic, threaded through with something that made the grass fall silent and the lamps flicker: recognition. Longing. Grief.

Rosalind knew that voice. She had heard it whine outside her stall in the burning market, staring at her through the smoke with knowing amber eyes.

Maren's translucent hand closed around Rosalind's wrist with shocking strength.

"It found you," the old keeper whispered. "It always finds the keepers. Child — how soon can the caravan reach the tower?"
