## 5. Lessons in a Vanished Tongue

The deep speech did not sound like words.

That was the first thing Maren taught her, standing before the door at dawn — if Ostrava had dawns, which was debatable, since the light here came from everywhere and nowhere, silver as spilled milk. The door itself was not wood or iron but a seam in the air, tall as a cathedral arch, humming faintly, like a held breath.

"Beasts listen with their bellies," Maren said. She was small and bent, older than the queen, older perhaps than the country, with hands like knotted roots and eyes the pale gray of fog. "They feel your meaning before they hear it. That is why your gift works. But stone listens with its memory. And the moon listens with its patience. You must give them something to hold."

"And what's that?"

"A name. Everything true has one."

Rosalind tried. She stood before the door, planted her feet the way she'd stood before rats in Vell cellars, and spoke the way she'd spoken for twelve years — soft, coaxing, warm. *Open,* she said, in every gentle register she owned.

The door hummed on, indifferent.

* * *

The days in Ostrava had no names, so the people made their own. They called this one Bread Day, because the ovens of the Lower Quarter fired at first moonrise, and Rosalind learned to follow the smell through streets that curved in ways streets shouldn't, past houses built from salvaged moonlight and ship timber and, once, an entire Vellish chapel, its saints chiseled off and replaced with carvings of doors.

She was given a room above the cooper's shop. A bed with a real mattress. A window that looked out on a square where children played a game with chalk lines that only worked because the chalk remembered where you'd drawn them.

On her third morning, an old woman pressed a loaf into her hands and said, "You have her chin. Ilse Quintana. She pulled me from the fire at Kellin Ford when I was younger than you."

"You knew my grandmother?"

"Everyone knew the keepers." The woman smiled with half her teeth. "We just didn't know we'd lost them until the door started closing."

By evening there were gifts outside her door. A jar of honey. A scarf dyed the color of dusk. A child's drawing of a woman with a lantern, labeled in careful letters: *THE NEW ONE.*

Rosalind sat on the bed with the drawing in her lap and cried for the first time since the market burned — not from grief, though grief was in it somewhere, but from the sheer disorienting weight of being wanted. In Vell, being known meant being hunted. Here, being known meant being handed bread.

It terrified her more than the witch-finders had.

* * *

"Again," Maren said.

They stood at the door each day while Ostrava went about its business around them. The refugees had learned to ignore the two women muttering at a seam in the world; some of them nodded as they passed, the way Vellers nodded at a priest.

"The beasts hear me fine," Rosalind said. "I talked a crow out of stealing Maret's wedding ring yesterday. He gave it back. He *apologized.*"

"The door is not a crow."

"No," Rosalind said. "The door is a snob."

Maren laughed — a dry rustle, like wind through winter reeds — and then went grave again. "Say what I say. Not the meaning. The sound."

And she spoke, and it was like nothing Rosalind had ever heard. Not Ostravan, not Vellish, not any tongue. It sounded the way starlight would taste — cold and sweet and impossibly old. The syllables didn't move through the air so much as settle into it, the way dust settles, the way snow settles, and the door *leaned toward Maren*, the way a horse leans toward the hand that feeds it.

Rosalind repeated it. Or tried. Her mouth shaped the sounds and they fell flat, dead on arrival, coins of lead where Maren's were silver.

"It hears you trying," Maren said. "It doesn't hear you meaning."

"How do I mean harder?"

Maren turned those gray eyes on her, and for a moment Rosalind saw how tired the old woman was — tired down to the marrow, tired the way a wick is tired when the candle is nearly gone. "You've spent twelve years making your voice small, child. Small enough to hide in. Now you need it large enough to hold a country. That isn't practice. That's unlearning everything that kept you alive."

* * *

That night she walked to the edge of town, where the moonlight thinned and the Hollow began, fog pressing against Ostrava's borders like a sea against glass. Somewhere beyond it, Dario was out there. The thought arrived and she put it down quickly, the way you set down something hot.

A hound found her there.

She heard it before she saw it — the particular silence of a large animal moving carefully — and turned with her heart in her throat. But the dog that stepped out of the silvered dark was old, gray-muzzled, moving stiffly, and it looked at her with eyes that were not a witch-finder's eyes. They were wet and apologetic.

"*Quintana,*" it said, in the wordless way dogs speak names. "*Ilse's girl.*"

"My grandmother's dog," Rosalind breathed. "But that hound belonged to—"

"The finder took me when your grandmother burned." The hound lay down heavily, sighing. "I have run for her ever since. Smelled for her. Twelve years of smelling. And now here—" Its tail moved once, uncertain. "Here you smell like her. Like home. Like the door."

"You're hers."

"I'm mine," the hound said. "But I remember whose voice opened things."

Rosalind crouched and let the old animal push its head under her hand, and together they watched the fog churn at the border of the erased world.

"Maren says the keeper can't leave," she said, to the dog, to herself. "Once the door opens for others, the one who holds it stays holding it. Forever. That's the price."

The hound said nothing. Dogs never lied about prices; they'd been paying them too long.

* * *

"Ask me why I do it," Maren said, another day, another dawn, another failure at the door. Rosalind's throat was raw. Her voice felt like a borrowed coat that fit no better than it had twelve years ago.

"Why do you do it?"

"Because I opened it once," Maren said simply. "Sixty years ago. I let three hundred people through, the night Chancellor Aldric's men came to finish the erasure. Three hundred. And then the door shut behind the last of them, and I have stood here ever since." She looked up at the humming seam. "Do you know what I miss? Rain. Real rain, that falls on you without asking permission. I would trade every year of keeping for one hour of weather."

"Then why—"

"Because someone must be the hinge," Maren said. "Or everyone becomes the wall." She took Rosalind's face in her root-knotted hands. "Listen to me, Quintana. Your gift works on living things because living things want to be understood. Stone and moon don't want anything. So you cannot coax them. You cannot bargain. You have to *tell*. With all of yourself. No hiding. No smallness. The whole truth of you, laid open, or the door will know you're flinching and stay shut."

"And if I can't?"

"Then Ostrava fades," Maren said. "Not burns. Fades. The thirteenth turn comes, the door opens whether we speak or not — a crack, a sliver, one night — and if no keeper's voice holds it wide, it closes for good, and this place goes back to being nothing. And everyone who chose to believe in a country that isn't on any map will quietly stop having ever been."

The words settled over Rosalind like snowfall.

Somewhere across the square, the children were playing their chalk game, shrieking with laughter. The ovens smelled of tomorrow's bread. An old woman was singing in the cooper's yard, a song about a river that no longer existed, in a language that officially did not exist, and every note of it was stubbornly, gloriously real.

Belonging, Rosalind thought. It had taken her twelve years to find it, and now it came with a bill attached: stay forever, or lose it all.

*Stay,* whispered something inside her, small and hidden, the same voice that had kept her alive in Vell. *Take the room above the cooper's shop. Take the bread. Let the door close. Be safe.*

And underneath it, another voice — newer, rougher, still bleeding from the checkpoint and the burning market and Dario's face in the firelight — whispering: *Dario is out there. The witch-finders are out there. Every refugee still running is out there.*

The door hummed between the two voices, patient as the moon.

Maren squeezed her hands once and released her. "Rest tonight. Tomorrow we try again."

They walked back through the silvered streets, and Rosalind noticed what she should have noticed days ago: Maren walked slower now. Stopped twice to catch her breath. Leaned once, briefly, against a doorpost, and straightened before anyone could see.

The old keeper was fading faster than the country.

And at the edge of the square, where the fog pressed close against the border, the mist was moving wrong tonight — swirling inward, parting, folding back — like something on the other side was testing the seams. Like something had caught a scent.

The hound raised its head from the doorstep where it waited for her, hackles lifting, and growled low at the dark.

"They're coming," it said. "I know that smell. It's coming."
