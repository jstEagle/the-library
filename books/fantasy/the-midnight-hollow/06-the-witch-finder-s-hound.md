## 6. The Witch-Finder's Hound

The hound would not eat.

Serane Kolt stood at the edge of the firelight and watched it — a great gray beast with frost on its muzzle, ribs like a washboard, and eyes that had seen too many years to be fooled by anything. It lay beside the dead fire of the last checkpoint and stared east, toward the Hollow, as if the fog out there were a person it had been waiting for.

"Quintana," Serane said.

The hound's ears lifted.

That was the whole of her tracking method, and she hated how much it looked like witchcraft. No compass. No scrying bowl. Just a name spoken into the dark, and a dog that turned its head like a compass needle finding north. The men in her company called the animal cursed. Serane had stopped correcting them. Cursed was useful. Cursed made the men keep their distance from both hound and handler, and Serane Kolt had spent eleven years in the Office of Edict learning that distance was the only armor that never dulled.

She crouched, offered the dried meat again. The hound took it this time, gently, the way old things take things — apologetically, as though chewing were an imposition.

"You knew her," Serane said. "The grandmother."

The tail moved once against the dirt.

The files said the beast had belonged to Ilse Quintana, keeper of something the archives could not name because the archives themselves had been cut. Page after page in the chancellery records ended mid-sentence, ink blotted over whole paragraphs by a censor's hand two generations dead. Serane had read those mutilated pages by candlelight until her eyes ached, and what remained between the black lines told a story the crown would have burned her for repeating: there had been a country east of Vell. There had been a door. And a chancellor whose name was now a street and a holiday had ordered both unmade, because a country that exists can be audited, and an audit is a dangerous thing when your grandfather built his fortune selling the land twice.

So they erased it. Not with armies — armies leave graves, and graves leave questions — but with edicts. The Thirteenth Moon. The hunt for the gifted. Every speaker flushed out of every market and village, because a speaker's voice was the only key that fit a door no map remembered. The chancellor's successors hadn't even needed to know why anymore. They hunted on momentum, like a wheel still spinning after the cart has crashed. Serane had spent years believing the wheel was justice.

Then she'd read page 214.

* * *

They crossed into the Hollow at dusk, and the road behind them stopped existing.

Not faded. Not fogged over. Stopped — as if the world were a ledger and some clerk had drawn a line through the entry. Serane watched her own hoofprints vanish behind the column, watched the checkpoint tower they'd left an hour ago simply not be there when she turned to check it, and felt something cold settle behind her sternum that had nothing to do with the weather.

Her lieutenant, Brakkus, a wide man with a soldier's superstitions and a coward's piety, rode up alongside. "Witch-country," he said, spitting to the side. "Ground doesn't hold a shape here."

"The ground holds exactly the shape it's told," Serane said, "by someone with the voice for it."

Brakkus crossed himself with the wrong hand, the crown's gesture, thumb to sternum then outward. "You believe all that? Speakers? Doorways?"

"I believe in ledgers." She nodded at the fog swallowing the road. "And somewhere ahead of us is a debt sixty years past due."

Ahead, the hound pulled at its lead, not straining but leaning, the way a man leans toward a door he recognizes. Serane had raised the animal from its mother's litter — or rather, inherited it, along with the post, the office, and the mutilated archive, when the last witch-finder died with frostbite in his lungs and a Quintana name still on his lips. The beast was older than her career. Older, she suspected, than several careers combined. It did not behave like a dog. It behaved like a witness.

At midnight they found the caravan's leavings: a cold camp, mule droppings, a child's carved wooden bird dropped in the dirt and left behind — which told Serane the party was moving fast and frightened, or moving fast and hopeful, and in her experience those were the same thing at speed.

The hound put its nose to the bird and whined.

Low. Long. A sound Serane had heard it make only once before — at the burned market in Vell, staring at a charm-seller's stall while the ash came down like dirty snow. At a stall where, according to the surviving vendors, a woman named Rosalind Quintana had kept shop for twelve years without so much as a sneeze of magic, until the night everything burned and the hound had sat down in front of her stall and refused to move.

Refused to hunt. Refused to bark. Sat there like a mourner at a grave, while the woman herself slipped away through the smoke.

Serane had reviewed that report forty times. Every other finder in Vell read it as failure. Serane read it as recognition. The beast had smelled the bloodline. Grandmother's scent, carried down twelve years and one generation, sitting behind a counter of harmless charms.

"Where is she?" Serane asked the animal now, kneeling in the dirt of a road that wasn't sure it existed.

The hound stood, faced east-northeast into the fog, and waited for her to catch up.

* * *

They made camp in the throat of the Hollow, and that was when the messenger came.

No rider. No horse. Just a man standing at the edge of the firelight who had not been walking a moment before, dressed in chancellery gray with the seal of the Second Bureau on his collar, and Serane's hand went to her knife before her mind caught up — you did not survive eleven years in the Office of Edict without learning that Bureau men arrived like weather and left the same way.

"Witch-finder Kolt." He had a voice like a filed document. "Progress?"

"We're a day behind a smuggler's caravan carrying perhaps thirty souls, among them a marked speaker of the Quintana line." She kept her tone flat, administrative. "We'll have them within the turn."

"The Quintana woman is not to be arrested."

The words landed wrong, like a stair missing from a staircase. "The Edict of the Thirteenth Moon—"

"Is a net, witch-finder, and you are welcome to keep casting it over the small fish. But the Quintana woman comes to the chancellery alive, and alone, and before the moon completes its turn." He produced a folded paper. "Or the Bureau will conclude that you have been compromised by prolonged exposure, and your commission will end the way Finder Aldous's did."

Aldous. Her predecessor. Frostbitten lungs, dead in a rented room, with a Quintana name on his lips — and Serane had spent three years assuming the frostbite was the Hollow's work. Looking at the messenger's face now, she understood it had probably been the Bureau's.

"What is in Ostrava?" she asked.

The man didn't flinch at the name. That itself was an answer; half the chancellery claimed never to have heard it. "Nothing," he said. "There is no Ostrava. There has never been an Ostrava. Say the word again in a report and you'll be writing it from a cell."

"And if the woman opens whatever it is you're afraid of?"

Something moved behind the messenger's eyes — fear, genuine and fresh, the first honest thing he'd shown. "Then sixty years of careful bookkeeping burns, witch-finder. Chancellor Veyren sold Ostrava's borderlands twice over to fund a war he lost, then buried the country rather than bury the receipts. There are living families in Vell whose titles trace back to that theft. Estates. Names on coins. If that door reopens, if anyone hears the word *Ostrava* spoken in the capital with proof attached to it—" He stopped himself, smoothed his coat. "You are a servant of order, Kolt. Order requires that some pages stay blank."

"Order," Serane said, "requires that pages stay true."

"Then you understand each other perfectly." He stepped backward into the dark and was not there anymore.

Brakkus emerged from behind the supply cart, where he had heard every word and pretended otherwise. "Finder? Do we... do we take her alive, then? For them?"

Serane looked at the fire. At the hound, lying awake, watching her with those too-old eyes as though it already knew what she was going to say and had decided, charitably, to wait.

Sixty years ago, a chancellor stole a country and burned the evidence, and the crown had built an entire inquisition — had built *her* — to guard the ash. She thought of the market in Vell, the charms, the burning, the woman running through smoke while a dog sat and mourned. She thought of page 214, and the censor's ink, thick as a scar.

Every instinct she had trusted for eleven years was a hand on someone else's ledger.

"Brakkus," she said slowly, "when we find the caravan — we find it alone."

"Alone?"

"No Bureau escort. No dispatch riders. If the chancellery wants the Quintana woman delivered like stolen property, they can come collect her themselves and explain to her face why." She fed the rest of the dried meat to the hound, who took it without gratitude, as one colleague to another. "Besides. I want to hear what she says first."

The hound stood. Turned east-northeast, deeper into the fog where the road forgot itself, and this time it did not wait for her to catch up — it walked, and the direction it chose was not toward any campfire smoke or caravan track.

It walked toward moonlight, pooling in the Hollow ahead like water through a crack in a wall. Toward something bright enough to erase the dark around it.

Toward a door.

Serane Kolt tightened the girth on her saddle, drew her knife, and followed the beast of a dead woman into the country that did not exist — knowing, with the cold clarity of a page finally read to the end, that by morning she would either be a loyal servant of the crown or the first witness in sixty years to tell the truth about Ostrava.

Behind her, far off in the fog, she heard the faint sound of hooves turning to follow.

Bureau hooves.

They had sent a second column after her after all.
