## 7. What Dario Carried

The letter came folded inside a wheel-grease tin, and Dario Voss knew what it said before he broke the seal, because the crown always wrote the same way: three sentences and a threat shaped like a courtesy.

*Your sister's rations continue. Her health is noted. The thirteenth turn rises in nine days; be certain you are where you were hired to be.*

He read it twice by the light of a shuttered lantern in the back of the lead wagon while the caravan slept around him like cargo. Nine days. He'd known it was coming — he'd been sending his reports east with every crow that would take a bribe of dried meat, little notes tucked in oilcloth: *caravan intact, heading for the Hollow, no sign of the Quintana woman's gift.* Lies stacked like coins, each one buying Sera another week of bread and straw in a cell she didn't deserve.

And now the coins had run out.

The last line wasn't courtesy at all. *Should the gate fail to open from within, arrangements for the prisoner will be revised.*

Revised. He'd grown up learning the crown's vocabulary in the gutters of Vell, and *revised* meant one thing when applied to a person.

Dario put the letter in the stove and watched it curl. Then he sat in the dark for a long time, listening to Rosalind breathe in the wagon beside his, and understood that whatever he did next, he was going to lose something he could not buy back.

* * *

They made the inner valley on the sixth day, where the fog ran silver and the road forgot itself behind them. Ostrava rose out of the moonlight like a memory the world had tried to drown: terraced fields under an impossible moon, lantern-lit streets built by hands that maps had never acknowledged, and everywhere people — the disappeared, the taken, the fled — moving through their resurrected city with the careful joy of those who had learned not to trust good fortune.

Rosalind walked ahead of him through the gate arch, and the crowd parted for her the way water parts for a keel. *Quintana,* they whispered. *The keeper's blood.* She'd stopped flinching at the name. That frightened him more than anything, because it meant she belonged here, and things that belong cannot be smuggled out.

His handlers' instructions had been simple, because crowns only deal in simple: there would be a cairn on the ridge above the eastern fields, a white stone on top. Under it, a signal mirror. Flash the hollow-moon pattern three times on the night before the turn, and the witch-finders would know the gate could be opened from within. They would come with fire and writs, and they would need only one door held open by one traitor.

Sera's face floated up in him, fourteen years old, laughing at the harbor with fish scales on her wrists. Then Rosalind's face, asleep across the wagon from him for eleven days, trusting him with her breathing.

He climbed the ridge alone at dusk, telling himself he was only looking at it.

The white stone was exactly where the letter said it would be. Of course it was. The crown had planned this since before the caravan ever left Vell; Dario Voss had never been a guide, only a delivery mechanism wearing a man's shape.

He knelt. He lifted the stone. The mirror caught the first star and threw it back at him like an accusation.

He flashed the signal.

Three times. Hollow-moon pattern. It took four seconds, and it was the longest thing he had ever done.

Then he sat down in the bracken and was sick, and after that he just sat, watching the lights of Ostrava come on below him — a whole country lighting its lamps against the dark, not knowing it had just been sold.

* * *

He found Rosalind at the well in the lower square, drawing water for the caravan's mules. Maren's students hovered nearby — they always did now, the old keeper's apprentices treating Rosalind like a relic that might still learn to speak. She looked up when his shadow crossed hers, and she smiled, and that smile went through him like a blade finding the gap in armor.

"We leave the day after tomorrow," she said. "Maren thinks I might be ready by the turn. Dario — she says I might actually be ready."

"Ros." His voice came out wrong. He cleared his throat. "We should talk. Not here."

Something in his face must have told her, because her smile dimmed by degrees, like a lamp being shuttered. "Talk about what?"

"Somewhere without ears."

She set down the bucket. Behind her, at the edge of the square, a crow shifted on the well post and cocked its head at him with an attention he had learned to dread. Beasts watched him lately. As if they knew.

They walked to the caravan camp, and by then it was too late for anywhere without ears, because the caravan was there — Tam and the twins, old Wren the farrier, the Ferro family with their two quiet children, a dozen others who had trusted him with their lives and their route and their fear. They looked up as he entered the firelight, and he realized he had brought Rosalind here because some part of him already knew what absolution cost, and who it had to be paid in front of.

"I have to tell you all something," he said. "And then you're going to want to kill me, and I'm asking you to hear it through first."

The fire cracked. Rosalind stood very still.

"I work for the crown."

It came out flat, and once it was out, the rest followed the way water follows a breach. Eleven years of reports. The arrest of Sera Voss on a charge of harboring, which was true only in the sense that she had once hidden a wounded boy who turned out to be gifted, which meant Dario had done that too, handed the boy's name over in the first month to prove his usefulness, and they'd taken Sera anyway as collateral. The letters in wheel-grease tins. The signal on the ridge, four seconds ago yesterday, already flashing its way east to Serane Kolt and her hounds.

"I opened nothing," he said. "I told them the gate could be opened from within, and they believe me, and they are coming. Before the turn. With fire."

Silence has weight, and this silence fell on him like a collapsed roof.

Tam moved first. The big teamster crossed the fire circle in three strides and hit Dario open-handed, a blow that spun him half around, and then the twins were up, and Wren was shouting, and someone had a knife out — the Ferro children crying, the whole small nation of the road breaking apart along the fault line he had drawn through it.

It was Rosalind's voice that stopped it.

Not the deep speech. Just her own voice, cracked and terrible: "Enough."

The knife lowered. Tam stepped back, chest heaving, staring at Dario with an expression worse than hatred — the expression of a man doing arithmetic on every mile, every checkpoint, every kindness, wondering which ones had been real.

"The hound," Rosalind said quietly. "Serane Kolt's hound. It answered to my grandmother's name. That's how they'll track us — how they've always tracked the keepers." She looked at Dario, and her eyes were wet, but her voice wasn't. "You signaled them. You gave them me."

"I gave them a gate," he said. "I told them it could be opened from within. Rosalind, they have my sister. They will revise her. You know what that word means. I have carried this since Vell, I have lied to your face every day since the checkpoint, and I am telling you now because—" His voice failed and he hated it. "Because you deserved better than to find out from the fire."

Wren spat into the flames. "He confessed so we'd know whose throat to cut."

"He confessed," Rosalind said, "because it was eating him alive. There's a difference." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, like closing a ledger. "I've spent twelve years hiding what I am because hiding felt safe. It isn't. Hiding is just dying slowly, in installments." She stepped closer to Dario, close enough that he could see his own reflection in her anger. "So no, nobody cuts his throat. But he doesn't stay. Take your wagon, take your lies, take whatever's left of you, and go. If the witch-finders reach the border and find you standing with them, I won't spare you twice."

"Rosalind—"

"You loved me," she said. "Or you loved the woman you thought you were smuggling. Either way, that man is cast out with you."

* * *

He packed in the dark. Nobody helped him, and nobody stopped him. The twins watched from the fire with their arms folded. One of the Ferro children — the small girl, the one he'd taught a road song outside Vell — watched him from between the wagon wheels and didn't wave.

At the edge of camp, Rosalind was waiting. Not blocking the road. Just there, a lantern in her hand, the moonlight of Ostrava pooling silver around her feet.

"Sera Voss," she said. "Your sister. Where do they hold her?"

"Blackspire. The cells under the chancellery." He stared at her. "Why?"

"Because in nine days," Rosalind Quintana said, "every ear in the crown's city is going to hear a country speak. And when it does, I want to know exactly which cell to call her name toward."

She turned back toward the fires, and Dario stood alone on the road with his one wagon and his whole ruined heart, watching the lights of a nation that wasn't supposed to exist burn brighter against the coming dark — and far off, beyond the eastern ridge, he could swear he heard the first baying of hounds.
