## 8. The Thirteenth Turn Rises

The moon began to turn at dusk, and the whole of Ostrava felt it like a held breath.

Rosalind stood on the keeper's terrace above the gate and watched the sky do something the sky was never meant to do. The thirteenth moon — the one that lived only in this country, the one no map or almanac in Vell had ever named — slid out from behind its twelve pale sisters and began, slowly, to cross their faces. Where it passed, the moonlight bent. Shadows fell upward. The fog that ringed the border of the Hollow pulled back like a curtain drawn by careful hands.

"They're coming," Maren said beside her. The old woman's voice was steady, but her hands were not; they trembled against her staff the way leaves tremble before a storm they cannot outrun. "The fog only parts for the turn. It parts for us, too."

Below, the city of the erased had emptied into the streets. Rosalind could hear them — hundreds of voices, the disappeared of three generations, all of them looking up at the same impossible moon. Some were singing an old song in a tongue Rosalind almost understood now. Some were simply standing, holding their children, saying nothing.

The beasts had gone quiet. That was the worst part. All day the crows had wheeled overhead without speaking, and the rats in the granary walls had pressed themselves flat and silent, and when Rosalind reached for them with her gift she touched only a vast, animal refusal — the way a dog refuses to look at the thing in the corner of the room.

*They know,* she told Maren. *Everything with ears knows.*

*Then we listen to what they're not saying.* Maren turned from the terrace. *Come. A keeper spends her last quiet hour at the door, not watching the sky.*

* * *

The door did not look like a door. It looked like a wound in the air at the end of the Hollow Road — a seam of silver light where the world had been stitched shut and never healed quite right. Standing close to it made Rosalind's teeth ache. Her gift, which had failed her again and again in these weeks of training — beasts heard her, but stone would not, moon would not, the deep speech sliding off her voice like rain off glass — stirred uneasily near it, the way a cat stirs near a door it cannot open.

"Say your name to it," Maren said. "Again."

"Rosalind Quintana." The words fell dead. The seam didn't flicker.

Maren sighed and set her palm flat against the light. It flared gently, recognizing her, the way a fire recognizes the hand that built it. "Stone is honest," she said. "Stone hears what you mean, not what you say. You still mean to be somewhere else, child. Part of you is always calculating the distance to Vell."

"I don't—"

"You do. And there's no shame in it. But the door will not open for a woman who is half out of the country already." She drew her hand back. "When the turn completes, I will hold the gate while the refugees cross. When it closes—" she said it lightly, as if discussing weather, "—I will be on the wrong side of it. That is the arithmetic of keeping. One keeper per door. No exceptions."

"You told me a keeper can never leave."

"I told you the truth twice, then." Maren smiled, and it was such a warm, crooked smile, so like the ones Rosalind had spent a lifetime inventing for customers buying charms against bad luck, that Rosalind's throat closed around whatever she'd been about to say. "You have your grandmother's stubbornness. I knew her, you know. Before. She stood where you're standing and hated every word I said, exactly the same way."

The first horn sounded from beyond the border.

* * *

They came through the parted fog like iron through silk.

Witch-finders — dozens of them, in the crown's grey-and-scarlet, mounted on horses whose eyes had been bandaged with sigil-cloth so they could not see the country they were violating. Behind them came men with crowbars and chains and one thing Rosalind recognized from her grandmother's stories: a bell of black bronze, big enough to hang in a cathedral, silent as it swung.

"The Unmaking Bell," Maren said quietly. She had come up onto the wall with the rest of them, because there was nowhere else to be. "If it rings inside the border before the door opens, Ostrava doesn't fall. Ostrava *ends*. Everyone in it forgets themselves mid-breath. The queen forgot once, for an afternoon. She said it was like drowning in a room full of strangers."

"And if it rings after?"

"After the door opens? Nothing. An open door cannot be unmade. Sound needs silence to work in." Maren's knuckles whitened on her staff. "So they will ring it early, or die trying. And we will keep them from ringing it until the turn completes, or die trying. There is the whole war, child. Fought over the space between two bells' worth of time."

The witch-finders reached the outer farms. Rosalind watched, helpless, as the first houses went up — not burned, but *unlit*, their windows going dark as if the idea of light had been confiscated. Somewhere down in the streets below, someone began to scream, and then many someones, and then the screaming organized itself into something worse: movement. Hundreds of the erased pressing toward the gate, toward the seam of silver light, carrying what they could carry of three stolen generations.

And through it all, cutting against the current of refugees, moving up the road toward the wall with a gait Rosalind would have known anywhere —

The hound.

Serane Kolt walked beside it, witch-finder's coat immaculate even here, at the end of the world. The hound stopped at the base of the wall and lifted its grey muzzle and howled one word, one name, in a voice like a struck bell:

"*Quintana!*"

Every head on the wall turned to Rosalind.

"She smells you," Maren said. "She smelled your grandmother the same way. That hound is older than both of us, and it has been looking for its keeper's line since before you were born." She looked at Rosalind, and her eyes held something terrible and gentle. "It isn't hunting you, child. It's calling roll."

"I can't—" Rosalind's voice broke. Below, Serane raised her hand, and the first ladders went up against the wall. "Maren, I can't reach the stone, I've tried everything, my voice just—"

"It reaches the stone when you stop performing." Maren gripped her shoulders, hard, and the strength in those old hands was astonishing. "You've spent twelve years selling lies in pretty boxes. This is not a market stall. The door does not want your best voice. It wants your *truest* one — the ugly one, the frightened one, the one you use at three in the morning when nobody is listening. Say what you actually mean, Rosalind Quintana, and the whole country will hear it."

The ladders crested the wall. The siege began.

* * *

It lasted, by Rosalind's later reckoning, less than an hour, though she would remember it as several lifetimes.

She remembered the queen going down at the gatehouse stairs, still giving orders, her erased blood pooling black in the moonlight. She remembered Dario — Dario, cast out, sworn off, gone — appearing at her elbow with a smuggler's cutlass and no explanation, fighting like a man paying down a debt he could never finish. She remembered the refugees surging through the streets, and the black bell swinging closer, closer, carried on the shoulders of six men through the smoke.

And she remembered Maren walking out the gate alone.

The old woman planted her staff on the road between the fleeing people and the advancing witch-finders, and she spoke — not to the stone, but to the moonlight itself, and the moonlight obeyed her. It came down like spears. It wrote burning words across the ground that the witch-finders' horses refused to cross. She held them there, all of them, a hundred armed men pinned by one small fierce voice and a dead language, and over her shoulder she shouted, without turning:

"Now, girl! Speak! Speak like it's the last true thing you'll ever say!"

And Rosalind understood, finally, what Maren had been trying to teach her for weeks.

She dropped to her knees at the seam of light. She stopped reaching for the market voice, the charming voice, the voice that had kept her alive in Vell. She let herself be afraid. She let herself be furious and grieving and homesick for a home that had never existed, and she spoke straight from the wreck of it:

"Open. *Please.* They are dying out there and I am done hiding and I don't care what it costs me — open, open, OPEN—"

The stone heard her.

The seam of silver light split wide — wider than a door, wider than a gate, wide enough for a nation — and the deep speech poured out of Rosalind like water finding its level, and she felt the door answer her, *know* her, accept the burden settling onto her shoulders like a yoke.

But the burden came with its arithmetic, and the arithmetic arrived all at once, whole and merciless, the way the deepest truths do.

She felt both ends of the door. She felt the refugees streaming toward it, needing it open on the far side, needing passage out into the world that had erased them. And she felt Ostrava itself — the whole country, the moonlight and the terraces and the granaries and the graves — leaning on that same opening, needing it *anchored*, needing a keeper's whole self poured into it, or the Unmaking Bell's silence would pour in through the gap and end everything anyway.

One keeper. One voice. One door.

Hold it open for the exodus, and the country behind her drained away like water through her fingers — she could feel it starting already, the edges of Ostrava graying, the moonlight thinning, the erased forgetting their own street names even as they ran. Anchor the country, and the door narrowed, sealed, became a wall — and everyone running toward freedom ran into stone instead.

Both. Or neither. Never both.

Down the road, Maren's moonlight spears were failing. The old woman sank to one knee, then rose, then sank again, holding, holding, buying seconds with years she did not have. The bell was thirty paces from her. Twenty.

"Maren!" Rosalind screamed. "How do I hold *both*?"

The old woman looked back at her — through smoke and moonlight and the last of her strength — and smiled that crooked, warm, terrible smile one final time.

"You don't, child. You *choose*. That's what a keeper is. Choose fast."

The Unmaking Bell swung forward for the strike. Maren braced her staff against the ground, spread her arms, and caught the sound of it in the hollow of her own body — took the Unmaking into herself rather than let it touch her country — and came apart into moonlight.

The silence where she had been was the loudest thing Rosalind had ever heard.

Grief hit her like the bell should have. And out of the grief, horribly, beautifully, came clarity — cold and total, Maren's last lesson arriving late: *a keeper who opens the door can never leave*, and Maren had known, had always known, and had chosen this ending the way she'd chosen everything, plainly, without flinching.

Rosalind rose from her knees. The door blazed at her back. The refugees ran toward her. The country leaned against her heart.

She opened her mouth, and began to choose.
