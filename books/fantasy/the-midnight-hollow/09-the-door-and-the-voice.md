## 9. The Door and the Voice

The moon stood full over the Hollow, and the door stood open, and the witch-finders were coming.

Rosalind could hear them through the stone — not their voices, which were still too far away, but their hounds. Dozens of them now, baying in a language older than crowns, a chorus of *found, found, found* rolling up the valley like a tide. Behind her, the refugees of Ostrava pressed toward the gate with everything they owned bundled against their chests. The queen's body had been carried to the orchard at dawn. Maren's lay where she had fallen, one hand still resting on the doorframe, as if even death had refused to let go of her post.

Rosalind knelt beside the old woman and closed her eyes. Then she did what keepers do: she took up the burden.

"Go," she said to the crowd, and her voice carried strangely, as it always did now, as if the stone repeated it for her. "Through the gate. Into Vell. The soldiers won't stop you."

"They'll kill us," someone said. An old man with a fishmonger's hands. "They've been sent to kill us for two hundred years."

"No." Rosalind rose. Her legs shook. She was twenty-six years old and she had never been more certain of anything. "They've been sent to arrest a witch. And I'm about to make sure there are no witches left for them to find. Only witnesses."

She turned to the door.

It was not a door the way doors are usually doors. It was a seam in the world, a place where the moonlight pooled thick as milk, and it opened onto a hillside above Vell — the same hillside where heralds had nailed up the Edict of the Thirteenth Moon twelve days ago. Through it she could see the city burning its evening lamps. She could see the market square where her stall had been. She could see the gallows they'd built, because crowns build gallows quickly when they're frightened.

Maren had told her once, in the last lucid hour before the siege broke her, what a keeper's voice could do. *Stone listens,* Maren said. *Moon listens. But people only listen when you give them something they cannot unhear.*

Rosalind put her hand flat against the doorframe. The deep speech came slowly at first, like blood returning to a numb limb. *Open,* she said to the stone, and the seam widened until the whole hillside showed through, wide as a cathedral arch, spilling moonlight down into the dark of Ostrava like a waterfall running backward.

Then she did the thing that no keeper had ever done, because no keeper had ever needed to.

She stopped speaking to the stone and started speaking to the country on the other side.

* * *

It began with names.

"Ostrava," Rosalind said, and her voice went out through the door and down the hill and into the streets of Vell the way water goes into cracks — everywhere, unstoppable, finding every ear. "There is a country by that name. You have never heard of it because you were made never to have heard of it. Your grandparents' grandparents watched it erased. They watched the chancellor's scribes burn the maps and melt the seals and strike the name from every ledger, every gravestone, every prayer book. They watched because they were afraid. And then they forgot, because forgetting is what fear does when fear gets tired."

In the square below, a baker dropped his tray.

"There were eleven thousand souls," Rosalind said. "Fishermen on the Sere coast. Weavers who dyed cloth blue with a flower that grew nowhere else. A queen named Alzbeta who sheltered plague orphans from three kingdoms and asked nothing in return. There was a scandal — yes, I will say that word too, since your crown has hidden behind it for nine generations. The chancellor's brother stole Ostrava's treasury and blamed the queen, and when she proved him guilty before witnesses, the chancellor did not punish his brother. He punished the witnesses. He erased them all."

A soldier on the gallows platform lowered his crossbow. He didn't know why. His arms simply wouldn't hold it up anymore.

"I am standing in Ostrava now," Rosalind said. "I am speaking to you from inside the country that isn't. And every person coming through this door tonight is a soul from that count of eleven thousand — the grandchildren of the disappeared, born in exile, raised in fog, kept alive between moonbeams by a door that opens only to a speaker's voice. You cannot erase them again. Erasure requires silence. And I am *loud.*"

And here is the thing about truth spoken in the deep speech, the speech that beasts understand and stones obey and moonlight remembers: it cannot be unheard. It lodged in the ears of Vell like a splinter. Fishermen on the docks heard it. Guards at the palace heard it, and the chancellor himself heard it, sitting alone with his ledgers, hearing his own family's crime recited back to him in a girl's clear voice, generation by generation, theft by theft, lie by lie, until the whole rotten architecture of nine decades stood exposed in the moonlight like a house with its walls torn off.

"The hunt is over," Rosalind said. "Not because anyone has won it. Because there is nothing left to hunt. Go home. Look at your neighbors. Ask them what else you've all been made to forget."

* * *

The first refugees came through the door at moonrise-plus-one-hour, blinking, terrified, clutching their bundles.

The soldiers did not stop them. Some wept. One — a young sergeant whose grandfather had been a Sere fisherman, though he'd never known why his family sang songs in a language no map explained — fell to his knees in the road and began, haltingly, to sing along.

Serane Kolt arrived an hour later with thirty witch-finders and the old hound. She stood at the edge of the crowd and looked at the open door, at the moonlit country visible through it — orchards, rooftops, a bell tower — and Rosalind watched something break and reassemble behind the witch-finder's eyes. Serane had spent her life serving a lie so large it had become the ground she walked on. Now the ground had spoken.

"Hound," Serane said quietly. "Quintana."

But the hound was already moving — past Serane, past the soldiers, tail low and wagging, straight to the woman at the door who smelled of smoke and grandmother and home. It sat at Rosalind's feet like a sentence finally completed.

Serane looked at her hound looking at Rosalind, and she unbuckled her badge of office and let it fall into the mud.

"We were sent to find a key," she said. "We found a door instead. And a voice." She turned to her thirty witch-finders. "Stand down."

* * *

Dario Voss came through the crowd near midnight, leading the caravan horses, his face gray with exhaustion and something worse than exhaustion.

He had gone, after she cast him out. He had gone to the witch-finders' camp as ordered, to open the gate from within. Rosalind knew it the way she now knew many things — the way the door knows things. And she saw the moment he'd chosen otherwise, replayed in the memory of the stone: Dario standing at the camp's edge, the signal lantern in his hand, and then setting the lantern down in the mud and walking away from it, away from his sister's safety, away from everything the crown held over him, because some debts cannot be paid with other people's countries.

"You came back," Rosalind said.

"I came back." He stopped an arm's length away. He didn't touch her. He had no right to touch her, and both of them knew it, and both of them also knew that wasn't the same as nothing. "My sister—" His voice cracked. "The chancellor's agents moved her this morning. To the palace cells. But the palace is listening to you right now, Rosalind. Everyone is listening to you. So I'm asking."

Rosalind looked at him for a long moment. Twelve days ago she would have said no. Six hours ago she might have. But she had just spent the evening teaching an entire nation that erasure is a choice, made fresh each day, and can be declined.

"The cells under the west wing," she said to the door, to the night air, to Vell. "There is a prisoner there named Livia Voss. She committed no crime except loving a man who loved the wrong woman. Release her. Carry her to the hill road. She'll be met."

And because the whole city was listening, and because half the guards had already decided the hunt was a shame they wanted to be clean of, they released her. They carried her to the hill road. Dario's caravan met her at moonset, and Rosalind watched from the doorway as brother and sister clung to each other in the fog, and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since the market burned.

Dario came back to her after. Stood beside her at the door as the last of the refugees crossed, and the soldiers helped them now, offering hands and bread.

"The line holds till dawn," he said. "Me and mine. Then we run goods — real goods, both directions. First honest trade route into Ostrava in two hundred years." He glanced sideways at her. "That is, if the keeper allows commerce."

"The keeper," Rosalind said, "allows almost anything except forgetting."

He smiled, tired and true. "Then we'll get along fine."

Below them, Vell glittered, changed forever, its people awake inside their own history. Behind Rosalind, Ostrava breathed — orchards and bells and eleven thousand names growing back like grass after rain. The hound leaned warm against her leg.

And far off at the edge of the crowd, at the border of the firelight where the fog still pooled, a figure in traveling clothes stood watching the door — a figure with Maren's staff, though Maren was dead, and a face Rosalind did not know, and when their eyes met across the distance, the stranger smiled and mouthed two words in the deep speech:

*Nice work. Now learn what the door is actually for.*
