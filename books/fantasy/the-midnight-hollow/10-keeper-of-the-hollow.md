## 10. Keeper of the Hollow

The maps arrived on a Tuesday, which felt absurd. Rosalind had expected something ceremonial — trumpets, banners, a herald in gold reading from a scroll — but instead a cartographer named Pell came through the door with ink-stained fingers and a rolled sheet under his arm, and asked her where she wanted the mountains drawn.

"Wherever they actually are," she said.

"That's the trouble," Pell said, unrolling the map across her worktable. "They keep moving."

He wasn't wrong. Ostrava had spent three hundred years as a pocket between moonbeams, and now that its name was spoken in every market square from Vell to the coast, the country was remembering how to have edges. The river that fed the terraces below the keeper's house had shifted two hundred paces east overnight. A forest of silver-barked trees had grown up along what had been bare hillside when Rosalind first crossed over. The land was stretching like a sleeper waking.

Pell worked for six days, walking the borders with a measuring chain while Rosalind's voice anchored each boundary stone. It turned out that naming a place and keeping it named were different labors entirely. She spoke to the stones in the deep speech Maren had taught her, and the stones held the words the way walls hold heat.

When he finished, he showed her the final draft: Ostrava, rendered in careful ink between Vell and the eastern sea, with cities marked — Lumen, Cael Row, the terraced villages along the Moonwash — and a small star at the border crossing labeled, in the cartographer's neat hand, *The Door*.

"No one's drawn it before," Pell said. "It feels strange, putting it down."

"It feels stranger not to," Rosalind said.

She kept a copy pinned above her worktable. Below it she kept another paper — the old Edict of the Thirteenth Moon, torn down from a post in Vell by one of the first refugees to cross back over, its seal stamped through with the word *RESCINDED*. She had thought about burning it. Instead she kept it where she could see it, because forgetting how close it had come seemed like a way of inviting it back.

* * *

The refugees came and went all that autumn, and Rosalind learned their names the way she'd once learned the names of rats and pigeons — patiently, one at a time, each voice distinct.

Some stayed. An old clockmaker from Vell who hadn't been able to afford passage out during the purges wept the first time he heard his own language spoken openly in a street. A young woman named Ilsa, whose gift was knowing when rain would fall, took over the orchards and tripled their yield by simply asking the trees what they needed.

Some left. That was the harder thing, at first — watching people she'd helped shelter walk back through the door into the world that had hunted them. But they walked out straight-backed, carrying Ostravan seed grain and Ostravan songs, and every one of them paused at the threshold to touch the doorframe, the way you'd touch a parent's shoulder before a long journey.

"You could go too," Ilsa told her one evening, on the terrace above the gate. "Just to look. Maren's gone; no one would blame—"

"I'd be leaving the door unlocked," Rosalind said. "A keeper who opens the door can never leave." She smiled so the words wouldn't land heavy. "Besides. I did my traveling this year. I'm full up."

But she understood what Ilsa was really asking: whether the price still felt like a price. Some days it didn't. Some days it felt like standing in the exact center of a room built just for her. Other days — the days when Dario's caravan was due and the fog off the Hollow hung low and gray — it felt like standing at a window, watching weather she couldn't walk into.

* * *

Dario's caravan departed on the first of the month, forty wagons reduced to twelve, most of his passengers choosing to settle rather than return. Rosalind stood at the gate to see them off, and Dario stood beside his lead wagon looking at her with an expression she'd learned to read in the months since the betrayal and the confession and the long, slow work of forgiveness — an expression that was apology and promise braided together so tightly she could no longer tell which strand was which.

"The sister?" she asked.

"Sela's in Lumen," he said. "Learning to bake bread. Says she never wants to see a crown official again as long as she lives." He hesitated. "She wants to meet you. When you're — if there's ever time."

"There's always time now," Rosalind said. "That's the whole point of the place."

He laughed, and it cracked halfway through, and he covered it by pretending to check a harness buckle that didn't need checking.

"I'll be back with the full moon," he said.

"I know."

"Rosalind." He stopped fidgeting. "I reported on you. I opened the gate for the people who burned your market. You cast me out, and you were right to, and then you let me come back and hold the line beside you, and I still don't understand why."

She considered the question honestly, because he deserved honesty even if he couldn't stand hearing it.

"Because you confessed when no one made you," she said. "Because truth is the only currency I deal in anymore. And because—" she gestured at the road behind him, the long gray stretch of the Hollow where maps used to die, "—someone has to carry word back and forth. Someone the worlds both trust. You're the hinge, Dario. I'm just the door."

"A door can't be 'just' anything," he said quietly.

Then the wagons rolled, and the fog took them, and Rosalind stood alone at the threshold until the last wheel-sound faded — which was sooner than it should have been, because the Hollow had learned mercy lately, and swallowed distance gently now instead of cruelly.

Twenty-eight days. She counted them without meaning to. She suspected she always would.

* * *

On the ninth day after the caravan's departure, the hound came back.

Rosalind found her sitting at the gate at dawn, gray-muzzled and enormous, tail sweeping the stone in slow, deliberate arcs — the same animal that had once stared at her across a burning market and whined her name to a witch-finder. Serane Kolt's hound. Her grandmother's hound, before that. A creature passed hand to hand down three generations of hunters, and now, apparently, hers.

"You're late," Rosalind said. "You missed the caravan."

*I don't follow caravans,* the hound said. Her mind-voice was deep and unhurried, like water moving under ice. *I follow you.*

"Flatterer."

*Accurate.*

Rosalind sat down on the cold step beside her, and the hound leaned her warm weight against Rosalind's leg, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was the best kind of conversation either of them knew.

It had taken weeks for Rosalind to stop flinching at the sight of her. Longer to stop hearing witch-finder boots in the rhythm of her paws. But grief and fear, she was learning, were both just love wearing the wrong clothes, and underneath everything the hound had ever done for hunters and liars ran one simple thread: she had answered to the Quintana name because Quintanas spoke true, and a beast-speaker's grandmother had been the first person in thirty years to ask the dog what *she* wanted.

*The chancellor's trial begins next week,* the hound said. *In Vell. They say half the court will testify about the erasure. They say Ostrava's delegates will sit in the front row.*

"I know. Dario wrote."

*Will you watch?*

"Can't leave." Rosalind tapped her own chest, where the keeper's burden lived now, warm and permanent as a second heartbeat. "But Ilsa's going as envoy. She'll tell me everything. And besides—" she nodded toward the gate, where the morning light was coming through the arch in a long gold bar, "—the whole point of what we did was that I don't need to be in the room for my voice to reach it. It's already there. It'll be there when they read the verdict, too."

*You sound like Maren.*

Rosalind blinked. "Did you know her?"

*Every keeper,* the hound said. *For three hundred years. Someone has to remember the doorkeepers when the countries forget.* She rested her great head on Rosalind's knee. *You're the fourth Quintana I've served. You're the first one who chose it.*

That sat with Rosalind for a long moment. Her grandmother had inherited the door the way Rosalind might have inherited brown eyes — blood, obligation, no vote in the matter. Maren had died holding a gate shut against soldiers, passing the burden mid-scream. Every keeper in the ledger had received the door like a sentence.

Rosalind had walked away from it. Had hidden in Vell for twelve years selling harmless charms. Had fled across the Hollow kicking and lying and bargaining with rats. And then, at the end, with the moon completing its turn and the choice between safety and truth laid out before her like an open hand — she had chosen. Not the door. The people the door was for.

Maybe that was the inheritance after all. Not the burden. The choosing.

"Well," she said aloud, scratching the hound's ears, "someone had to do it right eventually."

The hound made a sound that was almost laughter.

* * *

Winter came to Ostrava for the first time in living memory — real winter, snow on the terraces, breath hanging white over the Moonwash — because a country with edges gets seasons, and everyone agreed this was marvelous right up until the pipes froze. Rosalind spent December learning plumbing from the clockmaker and diplomacy from Ilsa's letters and patience from the hound, who believed all problems could be solved by lying down near them.

On the night of the full moon, Rosalind climbed to the gate an hour early. The fog was doing its slow silver dance in the Hollow beyond, and the door itself hummed faintly under her palm, ready to widen at her word. She had gotten good at this part — the opening, the greeting, the lantern lit and the tea warming for whoever stumbled through cold and afraid.

The wheels reached her ears before the shape of the wagons did.

Twelve became fourteen — two new wagons, painted with fresh trade colors, refugees or goods or letters from Sela's bakery, whatever the worlds were trading through him now. The lead driver stood up in his box as they came into the lantern light, and Dario lifted one hand, casual as anything, as if he were arriving at a market stall and not at the border of a country that hadn't existed last year.

"You're early," Rosalind called.

"You're waiting," he called back.

The hound rose and stretched beside her, and the door swung wide at Rosalind's voice, and the first wagon rolled through into the moonlight of a country that was finally, irrevocably, on the map.

Somewhere behind her, deeper in the Hollow, past the edge of the fog, something else stirred — a sound none of the beasts could name yet, carried on wind that smelled of iron and far-off bells. The hound's ears pricked toward it. Rosalind's hand tightened on the doorframe.

Whatever was coming, it would find the door open, and the keeper awake, and every voice in two countries ready to answer.

She smiled, and stepped forward to greet the wagons.
