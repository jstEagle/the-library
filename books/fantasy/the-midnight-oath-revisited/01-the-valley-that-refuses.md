## 1. The Valley That Refuses

The sword did not need sharpening. It had not needed sharpening in nineteen years, and it would not need it tomorrow, and August Montrose knew both of these things with the certainty she knew her own heartbeat—and still, every morning, before the kettle sang and after the hens had been fed, she sat on the back step with a whetstone in one hand and the sword across her knees and went through the motions.

She told herself it was maintenance. She told herself it was habit, and that habits were the bones of an ordinary life, and an ordinary life was the entire point.

The truth was smaller and stranger: she liked the sound. Steel on stone, long slow strokes, *shhh—shhh—shhh*, like breathing. Like someone else in the house breathing. The Hollow was quiet in a way that could go stale if you weren't careful, and the whetstone was the loudest thing she owned besides herself.

This morning the sound was wrong.

Not broken-wrong. Not dull-wrong. It was wrong the way a familiar room is wrong when a chair has been moved two inches: everything accounted for, nothing where the ear expected it. August stopped mid-stroke and listened to the valley.

Wind in the pines above the terraces. The creek doing its patient arithmetic over stones. Old Marta's dog announcing the mail cart three miles early, as always. All of it present. All of it correct.

And underneath it, so faint she might have invented it—a hum, like a struck bell heard through a wall. Like something remembering how to ring.

"Stop that," she said aloud, to the valley or the sword or herself, and resumed sharpening, and the hum did not come back, which was somehow worse than if it had.

* * *

The Hollow did not appear on maps.

August had checked, once, years ago, trading three weeks of labor to a caravan man for the loan of his atlases, turning pages by lamplight while he drank her cider and talked about his daughters. The valley sat between the Greywater range and the coast hills like a cupped hand, fertile and mild, and on every map in every atlas it was simply—the space between. Cartographers had drawn mountains there, or rivers, or nothing at all, but none of them had drawn the valley, because the valley did not permit itself to be drawn. Magic could not cross its rim. Whatever old bargain had made the Hollow what it was, it extended to ink and intention alike. A surveyor who climbed the eastern ridge intending to chart it would descend having charted a landslide instead, and be grateful for the work.

That was the whole architecture of August's life: a place that refused to be recorded, holding a woman who refused to exist.

She was careful with the refusing. She paid her tithe in eggs and mended fence lines for neighbors without being asked. She let people believe she'd come down from the northern hill country after some unmentioned grief, because *unmentioned grief* explained a woman who lived alone, kept to herself, and flinched—not visibly, never visibly—when anyone said the word *sword* too warmly. There were other swords in the Hollow; farmers wore them against wolves. But nobody here loved a sword. Nobody here spoke *to* one. She had made sure, in nineteen years, that no one ever saw her lips move near the blade.

Because she was the last swordspeaker, and being last meant being careful, and being careful meant alive.

Most days she believed this completely. Most days the routine held like good mortar.

This was not most days.

* * *

The peddler came at dusk, which was itself unusual. Caravans entered the Hollow through the southern pass twice a season, set up their stalls on the green by the well, stayed four days, and left. This man came alone, off-schedule, driving a mule whose coat had gone grey around the muzzle, and his cart creaked under goods that seemed to argue with each other—brass kettles stacked beside birdcages, bolts of cloth in colors that didn't sit still quite right when you looked away and back.

He was a narrow man, weathered to the color of his own saddle, with eyes that had done a great deal of looking and very little telling.

"Evening, mistress." He touched his hat brim. "Heard you buy odd things."

"I buy eggs," August said. "And salt. And rope."

"Mm." He looked past her shoulder into the yard, unhurried, cataloguing. "Heard you pay fair for books, though."

Her spine went cold in a way she trained out of herself decades ago, so that externally nothing happened at all. "Who told you that?"

"A dead woman," he said pleasantly, and then smiled to show it was a joke, the kind of joke travelers made, and August laughed the required amount and hated herself for the laugh.

He began unpacking the cart's lower shelf without waiting for permission, laying things on the tailgate: a cracked astrolabe, a jar of honey gone to sugar, a child's shoe, a bundle of letters tied in ribbon that had been red once. And beneath all of it, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft as skin, a book.

It was small. Octavo, maybe, bound in leather the brown of dried blood, corners rounded by a century of hands. Water had gotten into it somewhere in its long life; the cover was warped, the page edges tide-marked, and when the peddler opened it for her—carefully, the way you open something you've decided not to read—the smell came up off it like a cellar: damp, stone, and something older than either. Ink. Old ink, iron-galled, the kind that bites paper.

"What is it?"

"Prophecies," he said. "Or the leavings of them. Bought it off a drowning estate in Vess—" He stopped. Cleared his throat. "Off an estate. Up north. Fell through."

Vessany. He'd caught the word behind his teeth like a man catching a wasp, but August had already heard it whole, and the hearing went through her like cold water through cloth. Nineteen years since she'd heard the name spoken in the open air. Nineteen years since there had been open air to speak it in.

"It's damaged," she said, because her voice needed something ordinary to do.

"Water-stained," he agreed. "Which is why it's cheap. Which is why I'm offering it cheap to a woman who pays fair." His eyes came up and rested on hers, mild as milk. "Some stains, though, don't fade. Some just dry in deeper. You'll know what I mean if you've ever tried to get a mark out of anything that wanted to keep it."

"That's a lot of talk for a book."

"That's a lot of book for talk," he said, "so we're even," and he named a price that was insultingly low, and she paid it without haggling, and they both pretended this was normal commerce between strangers.

At the gate, leading the mule out into the blue dusk, he paused. Didn't turn all the way around. Just enough that she saw his profile against the last light.

"Funny thing about that valley of yours," he said. "Every road I've ever traveled, I've met folk running from something or toward something. Mostly can't tell the difference from the outside." He clicked his tongue and the mule started forward. "You keep well, mistress. Mind your dates."

Then he was gone down the lane, cart creaking, and the dark took him the way the Hollow took everything: gently, completely, without record.

*Mind your dates.* August stood in her yard with the book under her arm and told herself it was nothing. Peddlers talked in riddles because riddles sold better than sense. The wind was rising. There were chores.

She went inside. She lit the lamp. She unwrapped the book on her kitchen table as if disarming it, which, in a sense, she was.

* * *

It was worse than prophecies. It was worse because it was orderly.

Page after page of entries in a dozen hands, some cramped, some flowing, some written by fingers that had clearly been shaking. Dates down the left margins like a ledger. Predictions beside them, most banal—hail in the third week of harvest, a birth in the miller's family, war coming from the east—and most, she noticed with a crawling feeling she couldn't immediately name, annotated afterward in a different ink. *Came true. Came true, early. Did not come true, but something worse did.*

A century of watching. A century of writing down what the world intended to do, and grading the world's accuracy.

She turned pages slowly. Outside, the wind worried the eaves. The kettle she'd forgotten to tend ticked as it cooled.

The water stain lived mostly in the middle third of the book, a brown bloom that had swallowed whole entries and left others untouched, arbitrary as shellfire. She was skimming past it, half-relieved to reach clean paper, when the light shifted.

Not the lamp. Something in the page.

She bent closer. In the gutter of the spread, along the crease where the binding gathered its shadows, there was a faint warmth to the paper. A glow, if she was honest—if she used the word she'd spent nineteen years forbidding herself. Faint as foxfire. Faint as the last image burned on the eye.

One line, in a fine, upright, deliberate hand, sat centered in the stain's clear heart like a stone in a flooded stream:

*On the thirteenth turn of the moon, the borders close.*

No year. No month. Just the moon-count, and beneath it, smaller, almost an apology:

*All oath-bound blood within, forever.*

August sat down. Her legs made the decision without consulting her, which frightened her more than the words did.

She knew the moon-count. Of course she knew it; a woman hiding from time learns to count it anyway, the way a prisoner counts bars. Tonight was the first night of the new moon. She had marked it that morning without thinking about why she still marked moons at all—thirteen turns from tonight, the cycle would complete, and the thirteenth moon—

The thirteenth moon was a story. Ilse had told it to her once, when she was small enough to be carried, in the voice Ilse saved for stories that were also warnings. *When Vessany was young, they sealed the borders with an oath so the old enemies couldn't follow us in. Thirteenth moon, the seal renews. It's just ceremony now, little owl. Ceremony is what magic looks like when it gets old and tame.*

Ceremony. Tame.

August put her palm flat on the page, over the glowing line, as if she could hold it down. The warmth of the ink pressed back against her skin—no, not the ink's warmth. The paper wasn't warm. The *line* was warm, the way a wire is warm when something far away is moving through it.

*Forever,* the margin said, in a hand that had known what it was writing.

She thought: it's a trick. She thought: peddlers' tricks, glimmer-paint, a lure for lonely women in empty valleys. She thought nineteen sensible things in sequence, each one solid, each one load-bearing.

Then she turned the page.

In the bottom corner, small enough to miss, drawn in the same fine hand—a sketch. Quick strokes, confident, the work of someone who drew often. A door. Freestanding, frameless, standing in a field rendered entirely in stippled dots: ash, or snow, or stars. The door was open. Nothing showed through it. Whoever had drawn it had left the space beyond the threshold blank—as if whatever waited there hadn't been decided yet, or as if the artist had looked at it and declined to say.

Underneath, in tiny letters, a single word:

*Out.*

The lamp flame leaned in a draft that hadn't entered the room. On her knees, where she'd left it that morning, the sword lay silent as it had lain silent for nineteen years—but August felt, distinctly, unmistakably, the sensation of being listened to. Of being, at last and after all this time, found legible.

She had built her life on a simple equation: the Hollow keeps magic out; therefore the Hollow keeps me safe; therefore I am invisible. She had never once turned the arithmetic around. Never asked what it meant that safety and invisibility arrived together, bundled, like goods from a peddler's cart. Never wondered whether a hiding place that worked this well might have been provided rather than discovered. Whether borrowed things could be called in.

The glow on the page pulsed once, gently, patiently, like a knock at a door.

Twelve turns of the moon. Twelve turns to learn what oath-bound blood meant when the borders closed around it—whether *forever* meant imprisonment or erasure or something with teeth—and twelve turns to find a door standing in a field of ash that she had no map to, in a country that officially had never existed.

August closed the book. The glow went out obediently, which was the most frightening thing yet: it hadn't needed to stay lit. It had only needed to be seen.

Outside, high over the southern pass, the new moon hung thin as a paring of steel, and the valley below it lay quiet, fertile, refusing—as it had always refused—to be written down.

August Montrose sat alone at her table, in the one place on earth that could not be mapped, holding the only book that had ever found her, and understood with perfect, dreadful clarity that her invisibility had never belonged to her at all.

Somewhere out there, the count had already begun.
