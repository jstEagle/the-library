## 2. Ink Older Than Grief

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when August finally admitted she wasn't going to sleep.

She sat at her kitchen table with the peddler's book open before her, and the water stain that bloomed across its cover looked, in the low light, like a map of some drowned country. She had turned it over three times looking for a title. There was none. There was no author's name either—only an inscription inside the front cover, written in a hand so small and precise it might have been done with a needle:

*For whoever is left to read.*

Her own handwriting.

Not exactly. Not letter for letter. But close enough that her stomach had gone cold when she first saw it, the way a body goes cold when it hears its own voice played back from somewhere it never stood. Her hand had changed since she was a child. Hands do. But the *shape* of the letters—the way the ascenders leaned slightly east, the way the crossbars were struck fast and low—that was a family resemblance, not a coincidence.

She hadn't written this book. Which meant someone who wrote like her had. Someone in her line. Someone of her order, if order was even the right word for what remained of it: one woman in a valley where magic couldn't follow, sharpening a sword that didn't need sharpening, keeping a dead language alive the way you'd keep a seed alive—not planting it, just refusing to let it rot.

August turned the page again and made herself look at it properly this time.

The paper was vellum, old but not fragile, the kind of skin-stuff that outlived its owners by centuries. The text was dense, arranged not in lines but in rings—circles within circles, words spiraling inward toward a center like water finding a drain. She recognized the script. Of course she did. Ilse had taught it to her, or tried to, in the years before the end: *swordspeak*, the written twin of the spoken art, a language designed to be read aloud because reading it silently was like reading music without ever hearing a note. Ilse used to say that swordspeak wasn't a language you knew. It was a language that knew *you*, and the writing was only the shape of its attention.

*You'll know it when your blood remembers it,* Ilse had said, tapping her sternum with one scarred finger. *The head's the slowest part of you. Let it catch up.*

August had been nine. She had thought it was a joke. She had spent twenty years being grateful, in a dull and unexamined way, that it had stayed a joke.

Now she read, moving her lips without sound because sound felt dangerous, and the spiral gave up its meaning one ring at a time.

The outermost ring was calendar-work. Dates. Dozens of them, marching around the circumference like sentries, each inscribed in the old reckoning and then, beneath it, in the common numbers any trader would recognize. Most were long past. A few sat ahead, patient as traps. And one—

One glowed.

It wasn't firelight doing it. She checked; she carried the candle around the table, held it at angles, cupped her hand to block every reflection. The glow came from inside the ink itself, a faint warmth like coals seen through ash, and it clung to a single date with the stubbornness of a thing that has waited a very long time to be noticed.

Tonight's date.

She counted anyway. She counted twice, because her hands were shaking and she wanted to give herself the dignity of having been wrong. She marked the common numerals against the almanac nailed above her door—the one she consulted for frost, for lambing, for when to turn the garden beds—and there was no argument in it. No discrepancy. The ring on the page and the day in the world agreed with each other completely, and their agreement felt less like information than like a finger laid gently against the back of her neck.

The second ring told her why.

It was harder going. The grammar of swordspeak bent time the way a river bends stone—not quickly, not kindly, but inevitably—and half its verbs had no clean translation into the valley's plain tongue. She worked through it the way Ilse had taught her to work through anything: aloud in her head, slowly, trusting the blood to do the remembering the head couldn't.

*On the thirteenth turning of the moon, the borders of Vessany shall close.*

Vessany.

She said the name and nothing in the room answered it, which was somehow worse than if something had. Vessany was a word she hadn't spoken in fifteen years—not because it was forbidden, but because names are doors and she had learned early to live in rooms without them. In the Hollow, she was August, the woman up the slope who mended fences and sold good cheese and kept to herself. Her country had been erased so thoroughly that even grief had nowhere legal to stand. There had been no war, officially. No purge, officially. Officially there had simply never been anything to erase.

But the book said otherwise. The book said Vessany the way you say the name of a sleeping animal—carefully, respectfully, aware that saying it might count as touching it.

*The borders shall close,* the ring continued, *and what is closed shall not open. Every soul bound by oath shall remain within, world without end, until the words return to their speaker.*

She read that last clause four times.

*Until the words return to their speaker.*

There was a marginal note beside it, tiny, angled into the curve of the spiral as though the writer had been afraid to let it interrupt the main text—a whisper squeezed between the lines. The same needle-fine hand. The same eastward lean.

*The Door Out stands where the last field burned. It wants what was scattered. Gather all, or gather none.*

Below that, smaller still, almost apologetic:

*I am sorry about the price.*

August sat back. The chair creaked. Outside, the Hollow did what the Hollow always did at night: nothing, beautifully. Wind in the pines. A dog barking twice down in the village and then thinking better of it. The particular silence of a valley that had made a career out of keeping things out—magic, history, consequences—and had never once, in all her years here, let her hear what it cost to keep them out.

She had assumed the silence meant safety. Sitting there with the book breathing its coal-warm light onto her table, it occurred to her for the first time that silence could also mean holding its breath.

* * *

She took the book outside because the kitchen had become too small to contain what she was thinking.

The night was clear enough to show the whole slow machinery of heaven, and she found the moon without trying—low over the eastern ridge, a clean silver coin bitten once at its edge. Three days past new. Working its way toward full, and then past full, and then waning, and then gone, and then new again. Thirteen times.

Thirteen turns. She did the arithmetic standing in her own yard in her bare feet, and the answer was just under a year. A year was a long time. A year was seasons. A year was enough time to convince yourself, day by day, that a glowing date in a dead language was a curiosity, a coincidence, a trick of old vellum and wishful light.

And yet her feet had already turned her toward the eastern fence, and her body walked the familiar track along the boundary with the unhurried certainty of someone who already knows how the argument will end.

The boundary wasn't a wall. That was the first thing anyone in the Hollow would tell you, if anyone in the Hollow ever talked about it, which they didn't. It was more like weather. You crossed an invisible something and the air changed texture—the way a room changes when a window closes—and after that you were inside, and whatever you'd been carrying of the wider world's strangeness simply stopped working. August had tested it exactly once, in her first year, young and stupid with grief. She had stood on the line and tried the smallest thing Ilse had ever taught her, a cantrip really, a breath of swordspeak that would make a dropped knife land hilt-up.

Inside the Hollow: the knife fell like a knife. Gravity got its way. Magic got nothing.

That was the bargain of the valley. Leave your power at the border and be forgotten. Be so thoroughly forgotten that even the things hunting the powerful would skim right past you, the way an eye skims a blank space on a page. Ilse had found the Hollow, or the Hollow had found Ilse—it was never entirely clear which—and Ilse had brought her here half-dead and sworn her to dullness. Tend goats. Mend fences. Be ordinary so hard that ordinariness became armor.

*They can't erase what they can't find,* Ilse had said, in the last lesson August ever got. *Be a blank space, child. Blank spaces survive.*

August reached the eastern fence and put her hands on the top rail, and the rail was cold, and beyond it the land rolled away dark and innocent toward the ridge, and beyond the ridge was the rest of the world, and somewhere out there—she had never let herself calculate how far—was a country that officially did not exist, full of people who officially had never been conquered, and a door standing in a field of ash.

She looked at the moon. The moon looked back with the flat patience of all celestial bodies, and for the first time in fifteen years August understood that patience as a threat.

Because here was the thing she kept circling and couldn't land on: the prophecy wasn't addressed to everyone. It was addressed to *oath-bound blood*. Hers. The sealing wouldn't fall on the Hollow like a lid on a jar; it would fall on her specifically, threading down through her veins to the vow she'd taken at nine years old in a burning schoolroom with Ilse's blood-wet hand gripping hers—*say it, child, say it and mean it, the oath will keep what's yours yours.* She had said it. She had meant it. She had spent two decades believing the oath was a keepsake, a locket, a dead woman's gift.

The book said otherwise. The book said the oath was a hook, and hooks wait.

And the humiliating thing—the thing that made her grip the fence rail until her knuckles ached—was how little she actually knew. She knew one phrase of her oath, the opening, the part you never forget because it was the part you screamed while the world ended: *Steel, remember me.* Four words. Four words and then smoke, and running, and Ilse's voice telling her *don't look back, looking back is how they follow you*—and then the Hollow, and dullness, and twenty years of deliberately not asking questions, because asking questions was the first step toward answering them and answers were dangerous and—

And now a book had asked them for her.

Where was the rest of the oath? Who had scattered it, and why, and where? What was the Door Out, and what price did a margin-note apologize for in advance? Who had written this—who in her bloodline, her order, her murdered country had sat somewhere safe enough to write and small enough to hide, and set a date glowing like a coal, and trusted it across a century to find the one reader left?

She knew almost nothing. That was the vertigo of it, the true vertigo—not fear of the prophecy but shame at the size of the hole it exposed. She had survived the erasure of her entire nation and she had let herself be erased too, cooperatively, gratefully, mistaking blankness for safety for so long that she'd forgotten blankness was something done *to* you. Ilse had taught her to be a blank space. Ilse had never once, in all those years, taught her what had been written there before.

*Why didn't you teach me the oath?* she said aloud, to the fence, to the dark, to a dead woman who couldn't answer. Her breath hung in the cold. *You had twenty years. You had all the time in the world.*

The dark didn't answer. But the sword—

The sword was leaning against the fence post where she'd left it that evening, out of habit, though it needed no oil and no edge and no care. It had come out of Vessany with her, wrapped in a blanket, and it had never once in fifteen years made a sound. Swordspeakers' blades whispered, Ilse had said. A living blade murmured the oath back to its bearer, kept it warm, kept it honest. Hers had been silent since the night it was forged—or since the night everything else was unmade, she'd never known which—and she had come to think of its silence as another form of the valley's mercy.

As she stood there grieving her own ignorance, she would have sworn—later, always later, when it mattered—that the silence shifted. Not a sound. Nothing so kind as a sound. Just the sense of a held breath, deep in the steel, easing by a fraction. Like something turning over in a sleep that had lasted too long, beginning to dream.

August picked up the sword. It weighed what it always weighed, which was both almost nothing and far too much. She carried it back to the house, and she did not sleep, and near dawn she made the decision the way you make all the decisions that matter: not with courage but with exhaustion, arriving at the inevitable and sitting down inside it.

A year. Thirteen turns of the moon. After that, no way out for oath-bound blood, forever.

Which meant she had to leave the Hollow. Before the thirteenth turn. Through the border that kept magic out—which meant crossing into a world where magic would come rushing back into her like floodwater into a dry channel, into a country that had been edited out of existence, carrying a sword that had begun, after fifteen years, to dream.

Ilse had died teaching her the opposite rule. *Never leave. Leaving is how they find you.*

"I'm sorry," August said to the grey morning, to the memory, to the ghost-shaped absence at her shoulder that fifteen years hadn't dulled. "I have to break it."

Outside, the moon was setting behind the western hills, thinning as it went, and August watched it go with the specific horror of a woman watching an hourglass that had just noticed her watching.
