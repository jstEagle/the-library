## 3. What the Hollow Keeps Out

The Hollow did not believe in weather worth mentioning. It believed in barley, in roof-thatch, in the slow arithmetic of seasons. It believed in August Montrose the way it believed in everything else: without ceremony, without questions, and without any suspicion that the woman who mended fences and sold late apples had once been something the world had gone to war to erase.

She spent the morning pretending otherwise. That was the ritual now—pretending, performed with such discipline it had become indistinguishable from living. She split kindling until her palms burned. She argued with a ewe about a broken gate latch. She ate bread that tasted of nothing because she'd forgotten to salt it, which was itself a kind of proof: a swordspeaker would have noticed. A swordspeaker would have felt the absence of salt on the wind three fields away.

August chewed the flat bread and let herself be nobody, thoroughly, all the way down.

Except the book sat on her table under a folded cloth, the way you cover a mirror in a house of mourning. And except that every hour or so, her eyes went to it anyway, the way a tongue goes to a cracked tooth.

*On the thirteenth turn of the moon.*

She counted without wanting to. Twelve nights left. Eleven, if the peddler's arrival had eaten one already, and she suspected it had; the ink had glowed at dusk, and dusk was when he'd come.

By midday she could no longer stand the sound of her own pretending. She hung her sword—the plain one, the quiet one, steel that had never needed sharpening since the day she'd carried it over the mountains as a child—and she took up instead a walking staff, a skin of water, and the book wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heart.

"I'm going to check the fence," she told her nearest neighbor, old Marta, who was beating a rug and did not look up. "The north one."

"Take the mule if it's the far post," Marta said. "And tell your mother I've honey for her."

"My mother's dead, Marta."

Marta beat the rug again, unhurried. "Then tell her anyway. She'll hear it."

It was the closest thing to philosophy anyone in the valley ever offered, and August took it, because it was free.

* * *

The northern edge of the Hollow was a day's walk if you dawdled, and August did not dawdle. The land climbed gently out of the barley country into scrub and grey stone, and by late afternoon she stood where the valley ended—not dramatically, not with cliffs or walls, but the way a sentence ends: simply, and then no more valley.

There was a line there. Everyone in the Hollow knew about it the way children know about the line between the floor and the wall, without ever being taught. Above a certain contour, the grass grew slightly wrong—a shade too pale, a half-step out of season. Below it, everything belonged. Crossing it was possible; people did it, going down to market towns beyond the hills, trading, marrying out sometimes. But they came back changed in small ways that were really large ways: they came back *forgetting*. Not memories exactly. Something subtler. A trader who'd spent a winter beyond the edge would return unable to hold a tune he'd known all his life, or would find his own handwriting slowly turning into someone else's. The valley kept what it kept, and what it kept, it kept whole. What left got sanded down.

Magic could not cross the line at all. That was the whole point of the place. Whatever the Hollow was—sanctuary, scar, wound dressed as geography—it had been made for people like her, the last dregs of orders that had been purged, bloodlines hunted past extinction into hiding. Inside the line, a witch's fire was only warmth. A curse was only bad luck. A swordspeaker's gift was only silence, and an oath was only words.

August had tested the line exactly twice in her life. Once at nine years old, holding Ilse's hand, when Ilse had pressed August's palm flat against the empty air above the pale grass and said, *Feel that? Nothing. That's what safe feels like. Learn its shape better than your own name.*

She hadn't understood then why Ilse's voice had shaken.

The second time had been alone, at nineteen, drunk on plum brandy and grief, daring the world to notice her. The world had declined. She had walked home humiliated and sobered, and had not spoken of it since.

Now she stood a third time, and this time she did it properly.

She unwrapped the book first. The prophecy page still held its faint light—not glowing now in daylight, but wrong somehow, the way a pond is wrong when something moves beneath it. She read the passage again, mouthing the archaic Vessani dialect, letting the syllables sit in her mouth like river stones:

*When the thirteenth moon has turned her face, the borders of Vessany shall close, and all bound by oath shall be gathered in, and the Door shall be shut, and the country that is not a country shall keep its own forever.*

Gathered in. Kept forever. She looked up at the unremarkable line where the grass went pale.

"You're talking about me," she told the book. "You've been talking about me for a hundred years, and I'm only hearing it now."

The book, being a book, said nothing. But the ink seemed to darken slightly, the way ink does when it dries—as if her reading it was finishing some process long begun.

She walked up to the line.

The crossing felt the way it always had: like wading through cold water that wasn't there. Pressure on the skin, a momentary deafness, a taste like coins—and then she was through, standing in ordinary hill country under ordinary sky, magic-less and hollow-named, and the valley lay behind her like a cupped hand.

She waited.

That was the test, and she knew it, and her hands shook while she did it. Because the book had said the borders would close on the thirteenth moon, and tonight was not the thirteenth moon, and so the border should have been open. Should have been the same tired, patient absence it had been her whole life.

But the air on the far side of the line was not absent anymore.

She couldn't have said how she knew. There was nothing to see—no figure, no light, no sound. The hills rolled away brown and gold toward the lowlands, empty as ever. Yet the emptiness had acquired texture. Standing there was like standing in a doorway on a summer evening and feeling, without hearing, that someone had stopped walking somewhere behind you and was simply waiting for you to turn around.

Patient as weather. That was the phrase that came to her, and it fit. Weather doesn't hurry. Weather doesn't need to. Weather knows that everything outside is eventually inside.

"Hello?" she said, and hated how small it sounded.

The nothing did not answer. But it *registered* her—she felt that distinctly, the way you feel eyes on the back of your neck, the way a deer feels a bow drawn somewhere beyond sight. Something on the other side of the border of a dead country had noticed August Montrose, and it was in no rush whatsoever, because it already knew where she lived.

She stepped back across the line. The pressure passed over her, the coin-taste faded, and the valley closed around her again like a hand around a candle flame.

Safe.

The word arrived and curdled instantly, because she saw it clearly now, standing inside the thing she'd called home for twenty years: *safe* and *kept* were the same word wearing different clothes. The Hollow had never been a sanctuary. It had been a shelf. And things on shelves are not protected—they are stored, until someone comes with a use for them.

Her legs decided before she did. They carried her ten paces back toward home before her mind caught up, and then she stopped, in the scrub grass, in the long amber light, and made herself think it through because thinking was the one discipline Ilse had beaten into her that no purge could touch.

*If the border closes on the thirteenth moon, and I am inside it, I do not die. Worse. I stay. Forever. Bound blood, sealed in a country that officially never existed, filed away with every other erased thing.* Sable—that was the name the peddler had coughed out along with the book, half-delirious with fear, saying *find Vane, find the cartographer, she owes the order a debt* —Sable might know a road out. Or might not. Either way, the arithmetic was simple and obscene: twelve nights, one door, and a waiting thing outside that already knew her face.

Against that: one rule. The rule. The only commandment the Hollow had, written nowhere, enforced by everyone, drilled into her so deep it had become marrow.

*Never leave before your time. The ones who leave early are the ones who are found.*

Ilse had died teaching her that. Not in battle—there had been no glory in it. Ilse had died in a ditch outside a burning village with August's nine-year-old hand gripped hard enough to bruise, gasping the rule out between breaths that wouldn't finish: *Don't run yet. Running is a signature. Wait until they've stopped looking. Promise me. Promise me, little loud-one.*

August had promised. She had built twenty years on top of that promise, brick by careful invisible brick.

And now she stood on the promise's grave and found she meant to dig it up.

Because Ilse had also taught her other things, and one of them surfaced now with terrible clarity: *A rule that keeps you alive is wisdom. A rule that keeps you afraid is just a cage with good manners.* Ilse had laughed when she said it, that dry rasp, and young August had thought it was a joke. Twenty years later, alone on a hillside with a cursed book against her chest, she finally heard it as what it had been: an instruction. Ilse had always intended, someday, for the rule to break.

Or—said a colder voice, the voice she didn't want—the rule had been Ilse's, and Ilse was ash, and ash gets to decide nothing.

Either way.

Either way, she was going.

She turned south, toward the lowlands, toward whatever cartographer dealt in roads that weren't, and the waiting thing beyond the border watched her decide. She felt it the whole walk home: not pursuit, not threat—just attention, steady as barometric pressure, patient as the thirteenth moon counting down behind the clouds.

At the fence line she stopped once more and looked back at the valley: the smoke of hearths rising straight in the still air, the barley going gold, Marta's rug still beating somewhere out of sight. Everything she had chosen. Everything she had been given instead of a country.

"Twelve days," she told it quietly. "Then you're either a home or a cage, and I intend to find out which."

Behind her, far off along the northern edge, the pale grass stirred though there was no wind inside the line at all.
