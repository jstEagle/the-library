## 4. The Cartographer of Roads That Aren't

The map shop had no address, because addresses were a kind of testimony and Sable Vane had spent eleven years refusing to testify.

It sat where the river road forgot itself—a lean wooden building wedged between a cooper's yard that hadn't produced a barrel in a decade and a chapel whose bell had been requisitioned for the war that everyone insisted had never happened. The sign above the door read simply: SURVEYOR. No name. Surveyor was safe. Surveyors measured what existed, and no one had ever hanged a woman for measuring.

Sable drew other things.

She was at her drafting table when the knock came—three raps, evenly spaced, the knock of someone who had thought about knocking before doing it—and she did not look up from the vellum under her pen. The map taking shape there was of a road that began in a village called Merrow's End and ran east into country that every atlas in the world agreed was empty grassland. It was not empty. Sable knew it was not empty because she had walked it once, before her exile, when she still held a commission with a seal on it, and the road had run all the way to a city called Vessany-with-its-name-on, and she had drunk wine in its taverns and argued geometry with its bridge-builders.

Then the Custodians came with their ledgers, and the city went into the column marked ERASED, and the road went with it, and Sable's beautiful commissioned survey of the eastern provinces developed a hole in it shaped like everything she'd ever loved about her work.

She kept drawing the road anyway. That was the crime, when they finally charged her. Not treason. Not forgery. *Cartographic revisionism*, the clerk had written, in ink so black it looked wet even after it dried. Drawing places that do not exist. She had wanted to tell him: they exist. They exist exactly as much as they ever did. You're the ones who moved them. I'm just refusing to follow.

"Shop's closed," she said to the door.

"It's midmorning," said the door. A woman's voice, low and careful, with an accent Sable placed immediately—the soft swallowed vowels of the deep valley country, the Hollow, or somewhere pretending to be it. "Your lamp is lit. Your shutters are open."

"My mistake."

"I'll wait until you've made more."

Sable set down her pen. There was something in the voice worth looking at—not the words but the weight of them, the way they were laid down one at a time like stones across a flooded ford. She crossed the shop and opened the door on a stranger who was trying very hard to be unremarkable and failing at it in ways only another professional would notice.

The stranger wore a gray traveling cloak gone green at the shoulders with moss and mending. Ordinary boots. Ordinary face, ordinary brown hair cut short in the way of women who don't want hair anyone can grab. But she stood like a fencer stands when she thinks she isn't standing like a fencer—weight balanced over both feet, left side angled back, hands loose and ready at her sides—and there was a sword at her hip wrapped in oilcloth, which no honest traveler did anymore, because swords had been *collected* a generation ago, all of them, catalogued and melted or locked away by people who kept excellent records.

"You're armed," Sable said.

"You're not," the woman said, "which is interesting, given your window."

Sable followed her glance. In the window, displayed among the honest maps—coastlines, trade roads, the boring cartography of permitted things—was her private piece. The road out of Merrow's End, running east into nothing, drawn in silver-point so fine it looked like a scratch of light. She'd put it up months ago as a message in a bottle. Nobody had ever answered it.

"That's not for sale," Sable said.

"I know." The woman's eyes came back to hers. Gray eyes, watchful, tired around the edges. "It's not a map. Maps show you how to get somewhere. That shows you how to get to a place that officially can't be gotten to. That's not a map. That's an accusation."

Sable leaned against the doorframe and studied her. "You have me at a disadvantage. You know my work. I don't know yours."

"No," the woman said. "And you won't. Not my name."

"Everyone has a name."

"Not everyone keeps theirs." Something moved behind the woman's face—grief, or its fossil. "I'm told you draw roads into erased country. I'm told you've done it for years, for pay, and never once been caught because you take payment in things that can't be traced. Barter. Favors. Secrets."

"Told by whom?"

"A peddler. Water-stained coat, water-stained books, a laugh like a hinge."

Ah. Corvess. Sable felt her stomach tighten with an emotion she refused to name, because naming it would make it hope. Corvess sold prophecy books to lonely women in valleys and then came through the capital selling their descriptions to whoever paid for intelligence about the desperate. Or maybe he didn't. Maybe he was just an old man who liked hinges. Eleven years of exile taught you that every kindness might be a ledger entry; you survived by assuming the worst and being pleasantly surprised roughly never.

"What do you want drawn?" Sable asked.

The woman reached into her cloak and brought out a book.

Even half-ruined by water, Sable knew it at once—the way a musician knows a broken instrument, the way a priest knows a desecrated altar. *The Vessany Almanac of Turns*, third printing, the last thing published by a nation that had since been unpublished. Her fingers actually twitched toward it before she stopped them. She had drawn maps *from* that book. She had learned coastlines from it that no longer appeared anywhere else on earth.

"Where did you get this?"

"Bought it tonight. Off the same peddler."

"And?"

"And tonight's date is in it." The woman opened the book to a page near the middle and held it out. "Glowing. Like a coal that won't go out. I've read the entry four times and each time it reads me back harder. Thirteenth turn of the moon, the borders seal, and every oath-bound soul inside Vessany—" her voice caught on the country's name, just barely, the way a wheel catches on a rut, "—is sealed inside forever. I want the Door Out. The book draws it here." She turned the page: a marginal sketch, quick and sure, a door standing free in a field of ash. "I'm told you're the only person alive who could find a place that exists only in a margin."

The shop was very quiet. Outside, the river made its one long sentence over and over.

Sable should have said no. Every instinct honed across eleven years of survival said *no, say no, strangers carrying glowing almanacs are how exiles end up in ledgers.* She heard herself say instead:

"Come inside. And wrap that book like it's contraband, because it is."

* * *

Over tea she didn't drink—she'd stopped drinking tea served to guests after the second betrayal, a rule she'd never explained to anyone and didn't intend to start with a stranger—Sable spread her materials and listened.

The woman—August, she offered, first name only, and Sable noted the offering itself as data, because people who gave partial names had calculated exactly how much of themselves each syllable cost—August talked the way soldiers march: steady, economical, no wasted motion. She'd grown up in the Hollow. Magic couldn't cross the valley's rim; that was its whole purpose and its whole prison. She'd believed herself safe there, invisible, the last of a dead order living a dead woman's quiet life. Then a book glowed at her, and now the thirteenth moon hung overhead like a signature waiting to be signed.

"The oath-bound," August said. "That's what the entry says will be sealed. Oath-bound blood. I'm the last of the order. If the borders close—"

"You stop existing twice," Sable finished. "Once in body, once in record. The Custodians' favorite arithmetic."

August's head came up sharply. "You know them."

"Everyone knows them. Most people just don't know they know them." Sable pulled a drawer open and took out her silver-point pens, her good vellum, the little brass compass that had been her mother's. "They don't wear masks. They wear clerks' collars. They arrive in a town with a wagon of ledgers and stay three days, and when they leave, the town has always been exactly as it is now, except smaller. Names gone. Streets renamed to nothing. People remember less than they did. The terrible genius of it is that they don't kill the past—they *file* it. And a filed thing can't be missed, because missing requires knowing what you had."

"You sound like you hate them precisely."

"I sound like someone who used to work for them."

There it was. Sable watched it land—the small recoil, the recalibration, the hand drifting toward the wrapped sword. Good. Let her be afraid. Fear was honest; charm was how the Custodians got in everywhere.

"You mapped for them," August said flatly.

"For six years. Before the erasure, when they were just the Office of Boundaries and Records, and the work was dull and honorable. Then the commission changed. New directives. We stopped surveying what *was* and started producing maps of what *was decided*. I was ordered to redraw the eastern provinces with a hole in them. A Vessany-shaped hole. I was ordered to certify, under seal, that no such country had ever existed, and to burn my original survey." Sable turned her pen in her fingers. "I certified the lie—it was that or the ledger myself—but I didn't burn anything. I hid the originals, all of them, in places only I know. Then I ran. They've been looking for me for eleven years, which sounds flattering until you learn they aren't looking hard. Why chase a mapmaker? Every road she knows leads to a country that doesn't exist. As far as they're concerned, I'm mad. A woman who insists on the reality of a ghost."

"And are you?" August asked. Quietly. "Mad?"

Sable laughed—one short bark, surprising her own throat. "Daily. But not about this." She flattened a sheet of vellum and pinned its corners. "Here's what I know, and understand I'll only say it once. The Door Out isn't a metaphor and it isn't in the Hollow, because the Hollow keeps magic out and the Door is the most magical object left standing in the world. It's in the ash-field east of Merrow's End—in the old country, in what the ledgers call the Empty Provinces. I can get you within a day's walk of it using roads I've kept alive in ink for eleven years. Roads that exist only because I keep drawing them. Some nights I swear the ground remembers my lines before my boots reach them." She met August's eyes. "But there's a problem, and you've already guessed it."

"The moon," August said. "Thirteen turns."

"How many are gone?"

"One."

Sable did the arithmetic aloud, because arithmetic was a comfort and the numbers were ugly. "Twelve turns to walk into erased country, find a door that may be a drawing, and open it with—I'm guessing—an oath you don't fully know, belonging to an order you've already admitted you know almost nothing about." She let that sit. "I can give you the roads. I can't give you time."

"Then we leave soon."

"*We*." Sable raised an eyebrow. "Bold pronoun, for a woman who wouldn't give her surname."

"I need a guide. Guides come with the package." But August's jaw tightened, and Sable recognized the expression because she wore it daily: the look of a woman doing sums about trust and finding the columns wouldn't balance.

"Let's speak plainly, then," Sable said, and set down her pen. "Plainly is the only currency I trade in. You want my roads. Fine. But my roads are the most dangerous documents in the world right now, and if the Custodians catch me carrying them toward the Empty Provinces, they won't exile me twice. They'll file me. So I'm not selling passage, swordswoman. I'm selling my life's remaining safety. And I won't take coin, or favors, or secrets. I'll take one thing only."

"Name it."

"Your true name. Spoken aloud, here, once, with intent. Not 'August'—that's a handle, a thing for calling across rooms. Your *true* name. The one your order bound into whatever oaths it bound. The name the ledgers would want." She folded her hands. "In exchange, I guide you to the Door Out and stand beside you when you reach it. That's the price. Take it or leave it."

The silence stretched long enough for the river outside to complete several more sentences.

"Why?" August asked at last. "Why would you want it? Names are liabilities. You of all people—you live unrecorded, you *preach* unrecorded—why buy the one thing about me that could destroy us both?"

Because names are the only real estate left, Sable almost said. Because I have spent eleven years owning nothing that couldn't be denied, and I am so tired of holding nothing. Because if we're going to walk into the mouth of the world's biggest lie together, I want to hold one true thing about my companion, the way a climber holds rope.

What she said was: "Insurance. If you die out there, someone should know who died. If you betray me, I'll have something to curse properly. And if the Door needs blood or truth to open, I'd rather carry truth than blood." She shrugged. "Also, frankly, because you took the peddler's book and came straight to the one shop in the world that sells impossible roads. That took either desperation or judgment. I want to know which. A true name tells me. Names always do."

August stood. She walked the length of the shop slowly, reading Sable's maps the way other people read faces—the coastline of a lake that had been drained and refilled twice, the mountain range with a monastery inked and then carefully scraped away, and there, in the corner behind the display case, the silver-point road running east into nothing. Her hand rose toward it and stopped short, hovering, as if the vellum were hot.

"You drew this from memory," she said.

"I drew it from grief. Memory was just the instrument."

"When I crossed into the Hollow as a child," August said, not turning around, "the road behind us went soft. Ilse—my mentor—said the valley eats the way back, so no one follows. I've dreamed about that road my whole life. In the dream it's always dusk, and it always ends in fog, and there's always something on the far side of the fog that knows my name." She turned. Her gray eyes had gone flinty. "If I give you my true name and you sell it—if one syllable of it reaches a ledger—I will find you, cartographer. I'm the last of an order that killed gods with grammar. Do you understand me?"

"Splendidly," Sable said. "And if you waste my roads or lead the Custodians to my door, I'll redraw you so thoroughly you'll struggle to remember your own face. We're agreed, then. Two dangerous women, mutually assured." She held out her hand.

August looked at it. Then, instead of shaking it, she stepped closer—close enough that Sable could smell woodsmoke and sword oil on her cloak—and spoke.

She spoke quietly, but the room heard it the way a struck bell hears itself. The name was three syllables and none of them were August, and when they left her mouth the lamps guttered though no door had opened, and the silver-point road on the shelf seemed to brighten by one degree, and Sable felt the name land in her memory with the specific weight of a thing that could never again be unknown. It settled there and stayed, warm and heavy, like a coal passed hand to hand.

"There," August said, stepping back, paler than before. "Now you're as dangerous as I am."

"Now we're equally damned," Sable corrected, already reaching for fresh vellum, because her hands needed work when they shook. "Which in my experience is the only solid foundation for a partnership." She pinned the sheet down and began, in strokes so light they were nearly breath, to draft the first road: out of the river town, north along the dry canal, to the crossing at Merrow's End where the erased country began. "We leave in three days. Bring the book. Bring the sword. Bring nothing with your name on it—which should be easy, since apparently you haven't in years."

August gathered her cloak. At the door she paused, hand on the latch, and looked back at the maps covering every wall—decades of impossible geography, a life's work of insisting on the unreal.

"Why keep drawing them?" she asked. "All these years. No one buys roads to nowhere."

Sable didn't look up from her pen.

"Because paper remembers," she said, "even when the world is made to forget. Someone has to keep the evidence." The pen whispered eastward, laying down a line no atlas would ever admit to, a thread of silver running toward the ash. "Someone has to keep the receipts."

When the door had closed behind her guest, Sable sat alone with the new map and the old silence, and found that the three-syllable name sat in her chest like a swallowed stone—dangerous, yes, and also, for the first time in eleven years, company.

Three days, she thought. One moon-turn down. Twelve to go.

Far to the east, in a field that officially contained nothing, something waited behind a door drawn only in margins—and tonight, for the first time, it had a route leading to it, and two women foolish enough to walk it.
