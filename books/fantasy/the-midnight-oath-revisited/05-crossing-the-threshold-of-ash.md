## 5. Crossing the Threshold of Ash

We left at the hour when even owls doubt themselves.

Sable had said midnight, but Sable's sense of time was a cartographer's sense—abstract, theoretical, concerned with positions rather than feelings. What she actually meant was *the dark hour*, and what I heard was *the last hour in which you could still change your mind*. So I packed by candlelight like a woman defusing something, folding two shirts into oilcloth, wrapping bread in linen, taking down the sword from the peg above my hearth where it had hung for nine years doing nothing but being heavy.

It was heavier tonight. I want to say that plainly, because everything afterward will sound like poetry, and this part wasn't. It was just weight. The sword had always weighed what swords weigh—call it four pounds of steel and leather, the kind of thing you stop noticing on your hip after a week. Tonight it weighed like a sleeping animal that had begun to dream.

"You'll want food for six days," Sable said from the doorway. She hadn't knocked. She never knocked; doors were, as far as she was concerned, lines on a map with opinions. "Seven if you're sentimental about pace."

"I'm not sentimental."

"You sharpened a sword that doesn't dull every morning for nine years." She let that sit between us, then stepped inside uninvited and looked at my packed bag, my banked fire, the prophecy book already wrapped against rain at the bottom. "You're sentimental about all the wrong things and none of the right ones. Good. That'll keep you alive longer than skill will."

I should have hated her then. There was still time to hate her properly. She had taken my name out of my mouth two nights before—"August Montrose," spoken back to me in her flat surveyor's voice, like coordinates—and it had felt like having a bone drawn out through my throat. In exchange she'd given me a route: not a map, maps were paper and paper could be seized, but a route recited aloud once and only once, forty-one turns and landmarks described in words so strange I'd had to make them into a song just to hold them.

*The stone that lies, the ford that dries, the ash that walks uphill...*

"Last chance," Sable said, and there was something almost gentle in how ugly she made her voice. "The Hollow is boring. Boring is the most expensive thing in the world and you've been rich your whole life without knowing it. Walk away from me, go back to bed, wake up tomorrow and sharpen your sword."

"The sword doesn't need sharpening."

"No," she agreed. "It needs something else now. Can't you hear it?"

I couldn't. Not yet. That came later, at the line, where everything came at once.

* * *

The Hollow at night is a bowl of held breath. No wind crosses it that hasn't first been worn smooth by the ridgeline; sound behaves itself; even the river runs like it's trying not to be noticed. I had grown up thinking this was peace. Walking out of it with Sable Vane a half-step ahead, I understood for the first time that it was acoustics—the valley was shaped like a hand cupped over a mouth, and we were the word it had swallowed.

She didn't take the road. Of course she didn't; roads are how the world finds you. She took a sheep track that became a deer track that became a suggestion, up through bracken silvered with frost, and I followed because the alternative was staying, and staying had just stopped being a thing I could do. The moon rode low behind us, one night past its twelfth turn. Thirteen more nights until the borders sealed. Thirteen nights, and somewhere ahead of us a door standing in a field of ash, waiting to be unlocked by an oath whose words had been scattered like a smashed mirror.

I had the first fragment already. The sword had started whispering three days ago, faintly, the way a house settles—single syllables surfacing while I chopped wood or washed dishes. *In the name of the* —and then nothing, like a sentence interrupted by a slammed door.

"Ilse," I said quietly, walking. Just to feel the shape of the name. My mentor had been dead eleven years. I had watched her die in a burning orchard with her sword still moving, and I had run, because running was the whole of my education, and I had never once let myself wonder whether she'd wanted me to. Some questions you keep locked in a room of yourself and feed only at night.

Ahead of me, Sable paused at a rise and held up one hand—not for silence, but the way a navigator signals landfall.

"There," she said.

There was nothing there. That was the point. The ridge ahead looked like every other stretch of the Hollow's rim: gorse, scree, a crooked hawthorn leaning east under old snow-load. But I felt it anyway, the way you feel a stair that isn't where your foot expected. A seam. A hem. The place where the world had been stitched shut.

"The boundary?" I asked, though I knew.

"What's left of it. Magic can't cross inward—that's the design, that's why your people picked this hole in the earth to hide in. Nothing enchanted gets in, which means nothing enchanted follows you in." Sable turned, and starlight caught the ink stains on her fingers, permanent-looking as bruises. "But it works both ways, girl. Nothing enchanted gets out either. Including you."

"Including my sword."

"Including whatever your sword has been muttering at you since the peddler handed you that book." She held out her hand, palm up, businesslike. "Once we cross, everything changes fast. Your blood wakes up. Your order's magic remembers you exist. And everything in the dark that hunts oath-bound blood gets your scent for the first time in nine years. So. Last chance, August Montrose."

Hearing my name in her mouth the second time was easier than the first. That frightened me more than the crossing did. Names are supposed to cost.

I put my hand in hers. Her grip was dry and strong and colder than the night had any right to make it.

"Don't let go," I said.

"That's not how this works," she said. "Nobody can do this holding your hand. But I'll be right behind you, and I'll tell you one true thing while you're in there, and you'll know it's true because it's the only thing I've ever given anyone free." A pause. "Ready?"

"No."

"Good. Ready means you think you understand. Go."

I went.

* * *

Crossing the boundary of the Hollow feels—I have searched eleven years of language for this and I keep coming back to the same impossible image—like drowning in reverse.

Drowning, they say, is peaceful at the end. Pressure first, then cold, then a soft blue letting-go. This was that entire sequence run backward out of my body. Pressure lifted off me first—a pressure I hadn't known I was carrying, the way you don't know you've been squinting until the light goes kind. Nine years of deliberate smallness peeled away in a single stride. Then warmth instead of cold, flooding up from the ground through my boots, my knees, my spine, a heat with texture to it, with grain, with *intent*—

Then the letting-go, but upward. Outward. Into color.

I have no better word. The Hollow is gray. Not visibly gray—its grass is green enough, its sky blue enough—but underneath, in some register below sight, everything in the valley is muted, the way a room is muted when someone has died in it. One step across the seam and the mute came off the world. The frost on the bracken burned silver. The stars weren't dots; they were *distances*, each one a specific depth of black with a wound of light in it. I could taste iron and juniper and the far-off mineral smell of snow on stone that was still miles away, and I could taste them separately, the way you'd once been able to pick out individual voices in a crowd.

And my bones woke up.

That's the honest phrase and I refuse to dress it. My bones woke up like a limb coming back from numbness—all at once, painfully, with pins and needles made of lightning. Every swordspeaker carried the craft in the marrow; Ilse told me that when I was small enough to sit on her knee, tapping my forearm. *It lives in the hard parts of you, little August. Soft things forget. Bone remembers.* For nine years those hard parts had been asleep in the magic-dead valley, dreaming nothing. Now the marrow lit along both arms like fuses, and the heat poured in and pooled, and I made a sound I'm not proud of, something between a sob and a laugh, and my knees did buckle, and Sable's hand was there between my shoulder blades exactly long enough to keep me upright and no longer.

"Breathe," she said. "Count. Don't chase it. Chasing it is how it takes the wheel."

I breathed. I counted. I got to nine.

On ten, the sword spoke.

Not whispered. Spoke. The blade, still wrapped in oilcloth across my back, produced sound the way ice produces cracks—sudden, structural, from inside. And it wasn't one voice. It was many, layered, a chorus pressed flat into steel: voices of dead men and dead women, some old, some young, one that sang, and beneath them all, closest to the surface, a voice I knew the way you know a scar.

*"In the name of the—"*

The phrase rose and broke off again, a wave against a wall. But this time it didn't stop cleanly. Fragments tumbled after it, loose coins from a torn pocket:

*"—the last light, the lit gate, the—"*

*"—sworn in marrow, sworn in breath—"*

*"—August."*

My own name. Out of the sword. In Ilse's voice.

I tore the oilcloth off. I don't remember deciding to; my hands decided, and I ratified it after. The blade came bare into the starlight and it was *glowing*—not metaphorically, not a trick of the moon. A thread of pale gold ran the length of the fuller like something molten trapped under glass, pulsing slow, pulse-slow, my pulse, matched to my heartbeat, and where the light touched the air it wrote faint characters that dissolved before I could read them. Oath-letters. The alphabet my order had written vows in, the one I'd learned as a child and forgotten as a survivor, and here it was, leaking out of my own weapon, spelling and respelling a sentence it couldn't finish.

"In the name of the last light," I said aloud, testing, and the gold flared, hungry, wanting more, reaching for the next word like a man reaching for a rope thrown too short.

"Stop." Sable's voice cracked like a whip. She had gone very pale, and she was looking at the sword the way sailors look at weather. "Don't finish fragments. You don't know what finishing does yet. You speak a whole oath into open air with no door to catch it and the oath looks around for something to bind. Do you understand? It binds *something*. Whatever's nearest. Sometimes that's the speaker."

I closed my mouth. The glow dimmed to embers, sulking.

For a long moment neither of us moved. The frost ticked as it formed. Somewhere downslope, water moved through rock with a sound like quiet speech, and it struck me that I was hearing it *clearly* now, hearing everything clearly, hearing the world at full volume for the first time since I was sixteen years old—and grief hit sideways through the wonder, because the last time I'd stood in full-volume world, Ilse had been alive beside me, correcting my stance, stealing bites of my dinner, telling me that power was a loan and character was the collateral.

"She's in there," I said. My voice came out wrecked. "Ilse. In the sword. I heard her."

Sable was quiet long enough that I turned to look at her. Starlight, ink-stained fingers, an expression I couldn't file anywhere.

"Ghosts in steel aren't unheard of," she said finally. Carefully. Each word set down like a coin she was checking for clipping. "Oath-bound orders bound more than promises. Sometimes people. Sometimes on purpose. Sometimes—" she hesitated, and I would replay that hesitation later, in worse hours, and ask myself why I hadn't marked it then, "—sometimes as mercy. A way to keep someone from ending entirely."

"Is that what this is? Mercy?"

"It's what this *might* be." She shouldered her pack. "There's a difference, and you're in no state to weigh it. Walk. We make the ford before the sky pales or we lose a day to the lying stone, and we don't have days. Thirteen moons, Montrose. Twelve now."

I wrapped the sword—not in oilcloth this time, but loosely, so the glow showed through, a lantern of dead voices riding my spine—and I walked.

And here is the thing I have never told anyone, the confession this whole account exists to carry: as we descended out of the hills toward the lowlands of a country that officially did not exist, terror and joy walked beside me like twin escorts, and they were *the same size*. I was afraid the way you're afraid standing at the lip of a great height, and I was exalted the same way. The power in my marrow was a fever, and I remembered suddenly—remembered with my whole body—that I had survived this fever once before, at sixteen, in a burning orchard, and that surviving it had cost me everything except the fever itself, which had merely lain dormant, waiting nine years in the hard parts of me for a border to soften.

Power doesn't leave people like me. It waits. That's what nobody tells you about hiding. You don't escape the thing you are; you just move somewhere it can't find you, and you call the waiting peace.

By the time the sky went gray-green in the east, the whispers had settled into a rhythm—one fragment every few hundred paces, patient, tidal:

*"...in the name of..."*

*"...sworn in marrow..."*

*"...come home, August..."*

Come home. The dead mentor's voice, warm as banked coals, saying *come home*, and my feet wanting to follow the sound, and my mind remembering, distantly, coldly, the shape of a lesson Ilse had taught me in another life: *the surest bait is comfort, little August. Anything that wants you will offer you rest.*

We crested the last ridge at dawn and looked down into the remains of Vessany—a country erased so thoroughly that the erasure itself had geography, gaps in the world where villages used to vote and children used to be named. Mist lay in the valleys like spilled milk. Far off, impossibly, I could see a smudge on the horizon that my new-waking eyes identified without being asked: ash. Miles of it. And somewhere in that ash, a door.

The sword pulsed against my spine.

"Twelve days," Sable said beside me, already sketching in the air with one ink-black finger, measuring distances only she could see. "Try not to fall in love with the view. Countries like this one bite."

Below us, in the mist, something answered her—a sound I would spend the next twelve days learning to dread.

A bell. Ringing in a tower where every bell had supposedly been melted.
