## 6. The Country With No Name

The border of Vessany did not announce itself. That was the first thing Sable noticed, walking out of the ash-field with August a half-step behind her: there was no line. No stone marker, no faded customs post, no scar in the earth where a wall had been pulled down and carted off for other people's houses. Countries that die loudly leave bones. Vessany had died quietly, and quiet deaths leave nothing but the absence of themselves.

"You're sure this is it?" August asked.

Sable didn't answer right away. She was reading the ground the way she read all ground—not for what it showed, but for what it declined to show. The road beneath their boots was old; she could tell by the way it had settled into the land like a vein settling into flesh. But every mile-marker they'd passed since dawn had been chiseled blank. Not broken. Blank. Someone had come along with hammer and patience and smoothed each face of granite until it said nothing at all.

"See those stones?" she said finally, nodding down the road. "They used to say *Vessany — 12 miles to Corrow's Ford*. Now they say nothing. A road with no name on its stones is a road someone paid to unname." She adjusted the strap of her map case. "We've been inside it since the river bend."

August looked at the blank stone for a long moment. Her hand had gone to the sword at her hip without her seeming to notice—the gesture of a woman touching a wound she'd forgotten was there. Sable had seen that gesture before, on survivors of other erasures, in other countries that no longer appeared on anyone's maps. It always looked the same: grief arriving late, still wearing its work clothes.

* * *

Corrow's Ford announced itself by smell before sight—woodsmoke, wet wool, and the particular sourness of beer kept too long in a cellar because the innkeeper couldn't afford to pour it out and couldn't afford to let it go flat. The village sat in a shallow bowl of hills, maybe forty roofs, a church with a leaning spire, and an inn whose sign had been turned face-down against the wall.

Sable clocked the sign first. She always clocked the signs.

"Face-down signs mean one of two things," she murmured as they came up the muddy lane. "Either the name's unlucky, or the name's illegal."

"Which is it here?"

"We'll find out over dinner."

Inside, the inn was warm and dim and half-full of farm laborers drinking slowly, the way men drink when the drinking is the only part of the day that belongs to them. Conversation dipped when the two women entered—Sable counted three heads turning, four—and rose again when they proved to be nobody worth fearing. She took the corner table with her back to the wall and ordered stew, bread, and whatever the cellar was proudest of.

The innkeeper was a broad woman in her sixties with flour worked so deep into the creases of her knuckles it looked like age itself. She brought the food herself, which meant she wanted a look at them, which meant she was either curious or worried. Sable laid a silver coin on the table—more than the meal was worth—and watched the woman's eyes do the arithmetic of greed versus caution.

"You've come far," the innkeeper said. Neutral words, testing waters.

"From the west," Sable said. "Over the fells."

"No one comes over the fells." But there was no heat in it. Just the flat recitation of a fact that had been true so long it had become furniture.

"People did once."

The innkeeper's gaze flicked—to the sword at August's hip, then away, fast, the way you look away from something on fire. "That's a fine blade," she said, too lightly. "Old pattern."

"It was my teacher's," August said.

Something moved behind the woman's face. Sable watched it happen: not recognition exactly, but the ghost of recognition, a door opening onto a room that had been bricked shut. The innkeeper set down the bread basket with exaggerated care, as if her hands needed a task to keep them honest.

"Eat well," she said. "Travel safe." And then, so low it barely cleared the distance between them, so low Sable almost lost it under the clatter of the taproom: "Don't say the word here. Whatever you carry, whatever you are—don't say it aloud in my house."

She left. August stared after her. "Say what word?"

Sable cut her bread and didn't look up. "You know what word."

* * *

They learned more from the walls than from the people. After the plates were cleared, Sable made a slow circuit of the taproom on the pretense of stretching her legs, and August followed, and together they read Corrow's Ford the way Sable read everything: by its absences.

There had been a mural behind the bar. You could see where it had been—a rectangle of unfaded plaster, cleaner than the wall around it, the shape of something scraped away. Beneath a century of smoke and scrubbing, faint as breath on glass, the outline remained: a raised arm holding something long and straight. A sword. Someone had painted swordspeakers on this wall once, proudly, large enough to be seen across the room, and someone else had scraped them off so thoroughly that only the hole they left behind could testify that they'd ever been there at all.

August stopped in front of it and went very still.

"There were others," she said quietly. It wasn't a question. "Before me. There must have been—I always knew I couldn't be the only one, I just—" She touched the clean rectangle of plaster with two fingers, gently, the way you touch a grave marker. "This was them. This whole wall was them."

"Look higher," Sable said.

Above the scraped patch, near the ceiling where the scrapers' tools hadn't bothered reaching, a fragment survived: a few inches of painted blue, the suggestion of a cloak, and—one small, defiant detail—a stitched sigil on the shoulder. A circle broken by a vertical line. Sable had seen it before, in a ledger she should never have seen, in an archive she should never have entered, and she watched August see it now for the first time and understand what she was looking at.

"That's mine," August whispered. "That's the mark on my sword. On my—" Her voice snagged. "I thought I dreamed it. I thought I'd invented it, because I'd never seen it anywhere else. Not on a coin, not on a building, not in any book. A hundred years and it doesn't exist anywhere except on me."

"It existed here," Sable said. "Until it was decided it hadn't."

* * *

The old man found them at dawn.

He was waiting outside the inn when they came down, a stooped figure in a shepherd's coat, hands folded like a man at a funeral he'd attended many times. He looked at August first, then at the sword, then at the sky, as if checking whether the authorities of heaven were watching.

"My grandmother served one of you," he said without preamble. His voice was dry as seed husks. "Before. When there were others."

August's breath caught audibly.

"She carried water for the garrison at Harlow Bell, back when the bells rang. She used to tell me stories when I was small. About how they spoke to their blades, and the blades answered. How they could hear a lie coming from three fields away." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, full of holes where memory had rotted. "Then the stories stopped being allowed. My father beat me once for repeating one. Not hard. Just enough. He said, *we don't say those things anymore, we don't even think them where thinking can be seen.*"

"What happened to your grandmother?" August asked.

The old man shrugged, and the shrug contained an entire massacre. "Same as everyone. And then worse than everyone. Because afterward, when they burned the records, her name was in them. So she wasn't killed, you understand. She was *unhappened*. There's no grave because there was never a person. My grandmother, who raised me, who taught me which mushrooms kill and which forgive—officially, she never drew breath." He looked at August with red-rimmed eyes that held no accusation, only a kind of exhausted wonder. "And here you come walking through Corrow's Ford wearing her stories on your hip."

"I'm sorry," August said, and Sable heard how inadequate it was, how the apology fell into the space between them like a coin down a well.

"Don't be sorry. Be exact." He dug in his coat pocket and produced something wrapped in oilcloth: a scrap of fabric, army-blue, with a broken-circle sigil stitched in thread gone brown with age. "She kept this hidden in a mattress for sixty years. Hidden from *what*, though? From whom? Everyone who would have cared is dead, and everyone who remembers has been told they remember wrong." He pressed it into August's hands. "I don't want it buried with me. Burying things is how they win."

August held the scrap like it might burn, like it might heal, both at once.

"They didn't just kill us," she said. Her voice had gone strange—flat and careful, the voice of someone walking on ice over deep water. "They killed us twice. Once with steel and once with ink. And the second time—" She stopped. Started again. "The second time is worse. Because the first time, at least, we would have known we were dying."

* * *

They walked north out of Corrow's Ford under a sky the color of dishwater, and Sable talked, because talking kept August from drowning in her own head. This was a service Sable provided; she did not advertise it.

"The purge had two phases," she said. "Most people only know about the first. Armies, burnings, the executions in the capital squares—that's the phase they teach, if they teach anything at all, which mostly they don't. But the second phase was longer and it was quieter and it was run by clerks." She said the word with the distaste most people reserve for vermin. "The Custodians. They weren't soldiers. They were archivists, registrars, tax assessors. Men and women with good penmanship and bad hearts. Their mandate was simple: make it so Vessany had never been. Not conquered—conquest leaves records, treaties, occupation stamps. *Erased.* Every document mentioning the country rewritten. Every map amended. Every family ledger, parish register, gravestone, songbook—corrected."

"Corrected," August repeated bitterly.

"It's the official term. I've read the style guides." She let that horror sit for a moment. "They had rules, August. Standards of practice. Section nine covered proper methods for altering gravestones without cracking them. There was an approved acid for dissolving ink that left the paper looking aged. They were craftsmen. They took pride."

Ahead, the road bent around a hillside terraced with long-abandoned orchards, trees grown wild and gnarled, fruit small and dark. A farmhouse stood among them, roof fallen in, walls standing. As they passed, Sable saw August slow, staring at the lintel above the door, where carved letters had been filled with some gray putty and smoothed over—but the carving ran deep, and the putty had shrunk, and the shapes showed through like bones under skin.

"*Vessany*," August read. "Someone lived here. Raised a family here. Carved the country's name over their own front door like a blessing. And someone came and filled it in."

"Not someone. A crew. It was contracted work. I've seen the invoices." Sable heard her own voice going flat and professional, the tone she used when feeling threatened, and made herself soften it. "That's what people don't understand about erasure. It isn't rage. Rage burns hot and leaves evidence. Erasure is bookkeeping. It's patient. It shows up on a Tuesday with ladders and putty."

They crested the hill and the land opened below them: a wide valley, a river, a market town with a slate roof-line, smoke rising from a hundred chimneys. Ordinary. Living. Except that Sable, who had spent her life reading roads, could see what was missing from the picture the way a musician hears a wrong note—by the shape of the silence around it.

"There should be monuments down there," she said. "Victory arches. Statues of whoever conquered this place. Conquerors always build those—it's the first thing they build, before they build the granaries. Look." She pointed with her chin. "Nothing. No arch, no column, no plaque. Do you know why?"

August shook her head slowly.

"Because you can't celebrate a victory over a country that never existed. The moment they raised a monument saying *here we defeated Vessany*, they'd have admitted Vessany had been real. So the conquest itself had to be erased. The war was unwritten. The dead were unwritten. Even the killers were unwritten—whole regiments struck from the rolls, because you can't have veterans of a war that never happened." Sable laughed, and there was no humor in it, only the sound a person makes when the absurdity gets big enough to hurt. "It's the most ambitious piece of editing ever attempted. A century of continuous labor. Generations of clerks. And it nearly worked."

"Nearly?"

"You exist," Sable said. "Standing on their road, wearing their sigil, carrying their blade. You're a typo, August Montrose. A spelling error a hundred years long. And somewhere, the people whose life's work is correctness know it, and it keeps them awake at night."

* * *

They slept that night in the ruined farmhouse among the wild orchards, because towns had become unsafe—word traveled faster than feet, and a woman with a swordspeaker's blade drew eyes like a wound draws flies. August sat by the small fire turning the blue fabric scrap over and over in her hands, and Sable pretended to study her maps and watched her instead.

"He called me a typo," August said at last.

"It's not an insult. In my trade, typos are the most important things on the page."

"How do you mean?"

"A perfect record tells you only what someone wanted you to know. An error tells you what they were trying to hide." Sable tapped the map spread across her knees—the route she'd drawn, the impossible route, the road that wasn't. "Every blank milestone between here and the coast, every scraped wall, every filled-in lintel—they're all errors now. Flaws in the correction. Each one is proof that something was removed, and the shape of the removal tells you the shape of the thing. Your whole country survives as negative space, August. And negative space can be mapped."

August looked up. In the firelight her face was young and old at once. "Is that what you're doing? Mapping the holes?"

"That's all any honest cartographer does. Everything else is flattery."

August almost smiled. Then the smile failed, and something harder came up underneath it. "My mentor died teaching me to hide. She burned our names, our histories, everything, so I could vanish. She gave her life for my invisibility. And now I'm walking through the corpse of my country picking up pieces of it like—" Her hands closed around the blue scrap. "Like a scavenger. Like a graverobber. What am I doing, Sable? Really?"

Sable considered lying. She was good at lying; it had kept her alive through years of working for people whose displeasure was fatal. But she'd spent enough of her life around falsified documents to develop a taste for the genuine article, and August Montrose, hunched over her fire with her stolen country in her fists, was the realest thing Sable had encountered in a decade.

"You're refusing to be filed," she said. "Somewhere south of here there's a ledger with your name already written in it—in the column reserved for things that end. Every step you take north is a mark against their arithmetic. That's what you're doing. Whether you finish the oath or not, whether the Door opens or not—you are being, right now, the one thing a Custodian cannot survive professionally."

"Which is?"

"Inconsistency." Sable rolled up her map. "Sleep. Tomorrow we cross the Sere, and the country gets stranger the deeper we go."

But sleep came slowly, and when it finally took August, Sable sat alone with the fire dying and did the thing she did every night and told no one about: she checked the horizon. North, where the road ran toward the bell country. East, where the moon was rising, fat and a little past full.

Four turns since the peddler's book. Nine remaining. Somewhere behind them, she was certain—had been certain for two days, in the itch between her shoulder blades, in the way birds went quiet along certain stretches of road—that the correction crews had noticed their typo walking. Clerks did not chase. Clerks did not need to. They simply opened the ledger, noted the deviation, and dispatched whatever the bureaucratic equivalent of a hunting pack was.

In the morning, August woke to find Sable already packed, staring north with an expression August had not seen on her before.

"What is it?"

"Smoke," Sable said. "Three columns of it, spaced evenly, moving parallel to our route. Nobody camps like that except people following a schedule." She swung her pack up. "Or a list."
