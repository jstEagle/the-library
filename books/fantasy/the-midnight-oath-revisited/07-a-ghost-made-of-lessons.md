## 7. A Ghost Made of Lessons

The whispers started three nights after we crossed the threshold of ash.

At first I thought it was the wind moving through the dead grass of Vessany, that long dry sibilance the land made when nothing lived close enough to interrupt it. Then I thought it was Sable, murmuring over her maps by the fire, because she did talk to her maps—low, coaxing, the way other people talked to horses. But on the third night, camped in the ribs of a burned-out granary with the fourth moon rising fat and pale over the broken hills, I heard my own name inside the whispering.

*August.*

Not shouted. Not even spoken, really. More like a word remembered than a word heard, arriving in the space behind my teeth rather than through my ears.

I sat up so fast my bedroll tangled around my ankles like a snare. Across the ashes of the fire, Sable slept sitting up, her back against a charred beam, her rolled maps clutched to her chest the way other people held children or weapons. She didn't stir. The whisper hadn't been for her.

My sword lay beside me where it had lain every night since we crossed the border—the plain, unremarkable blade I'd carried out of Vessany as a girl, the one thing besides myself that had survived the purge. For eleven years in the Hollow it had been silent. Steel and nothing else. I'd sharpened it out of habit, not need; it never dulled, but I liked the ritual of it, the small circle of care in a life built on hiding.

Now it was whispering. And it knew my name.

*August,* the steel said again, and this time there was a shape to it, a cadence, an accent that put the weight on the first syllable and clipped the second—the way it was said in the northern provinces, before the northern provinces were renamed. *You've let your footwork rot, girl. You cross a room like a farmer crossing mud.*

The voice went through me like cold water poured down the spine.

I knew that voice. I had spent six years of my childhood listening to it—over training yards and cook fires and long grey roads, always slightly annoyed, always precise, always certain that I could do better if I would only stop feeling things long enough to try. Ilse Marren. My mentor. The woman who died in the doorway at Carrow Fen with three Custodian bolts in her chest, buying me nine seconds to run.

Nine seconds. I had counted them every night for eleven years.

"Ilse," I said aloud, and my voice cracked on the second syllable, which she would have hated.

*Don't say it like a prayer,* the sword said. *Say it like a student. Sit up straight. You're slouching toward your own ghost, and ghosts notice these things.*

And then the air above the blade began to move.

It wasn't smoke, though it curled like smoke. It wasn't light, though it glowed faintly, the color of moonlight caught in shallow water. It gathered itself upward out of the steel in slow spirals, and it took a form, and the form was wrong in exactly one way that broke something open in my chest: it was young.

Ilse Marren had been fifty-three when she died, gray-streaked and heavy-shouldered, with hands scarred from forty years of teaching steel to speak. The figure standing over my sword was perhaps twenty-five. Smooth-faced. Bright-eyed. Wearing the training leathers of the order—not the faded ones Ilse had actually worn, but the ideal ones, the ones from the old woodcut portraits we weren't supposed to keep. It was Ilse as the order remembered her, not as she had aged. Ilse as a story instead of a woman.

"You're dead," I said. My mouth was dry. "I watched you die."

*Did you?* The ghost tilted its head, and the gesture was so perfectly, painfully Ilse that my eyes stung. *Watch closely, then. What did you see? Three bolts. A doorway. Blood. Yes. All true. Death is a door too, August, and some doors only swing partway shut when the oath is still unfinished.*

"The oath." I got to my feet slowly, keeping the bedroll between us like a fence, which was absurd—what fence stops a ghost?—but Ilse had taught me that posture mattered, that fear should be arranged rather than surrendered. "You're here about the oath."

*I'm here about you,* the ghost said. *The oath is merely the road. You crossed the border carrying a sword full of words and half a lifetime of pretending they weren't yours. Of course I woke up. You woke me. Every step into Vessany has been ringing me like a bell.* It smiled then, quick and crooked, and for a moment the young face looked less like the portrait and more like the woman—like Ilse laughing at her own joke in the mess tent at Harrow's Ford, before any of us knew what laughter would cost us. *Eleven years of silence, girl. Do you know what that's like? Being carried around by someone who won't answer when you talk?*

"Then you were awake in the Hollow." The thought landed cold. "All that time. You heard everything."

A pause. Small. Half a breath.

*The Hollow is quiet,* the ghost said. *Even for me. That valley swallows sound the way it swallows magic. I slept, mostly. Dreamed, maybe. It doesn't matter now.* It waved a translucent hand, and the gesture scattered its edges briefly into mist before they reformed. *What matters is the moon, and the number of turns left in it, and what you intend to do about either.*

"Sable says the borders seal on the thirteenth turn. Everyone bound by the old oath gets locked inside forever."

*Sable Vane talks too much and charges too little,* Ilse said crisply. *But yes. She reads the sky correctly for once. Thirteen turns, and Vessany closes like a fist.* The ghost began to walk—or to perform walking, its feet hovering a finger's width above the ash—and I found myself following without deciding to, the way I used to follow her across training yards. *Which means you have twelve turns to gather what you came for. The oath. The whole of it. Every word in its proper order.*

"I don't even have the beginning of it," I admitted. The confession came easier in the dark, to the dead, than it ever had to the living. "They cut it out of me along with everything else. I was eight, Ilse. Eight-year-olds remember songs and grudges. They don't remember oaths."

*No,* the ghost agreed softly. *But they remember teachers.*

It stopped at the granary's ruined doorway and looked out over the broken country—the fields gone to thistle, the roads grown over, the far-off silhouette of some village whose bell tower stood headless against the stars. When it spoke again, its voice had changed. Quieter. Older. The cadence of lessons.

*The oath was never meant to live in memory alone. Memory can be edited—we both know that better than anyone breathing. So the founders split it. Three words, three vessels. Places chosen because they couldn't be burned without the whole country burning with them.* It raised one finger. *The first word sleeps in the Belltower of the Unringed, at Saint Verre. The bells were melted down in the first year of the purge—all except their silence, which no clerk has figured out how to confiscate. Wake that silence, and the word wakes with it.*

A second finger. *The second lies in the River Liss, upstream where it runs backward through its own memory. The water remembers everyone who ever drowned in it, everyone who ever washed anything in it, everyone who ever swore on its banks. It will show you things you don't want to see. Look anyway. Take the word and don't drink the water, no matter how thirsty grief makes you.*

A third finger, and here the ghost hesitated—a flicker, a hitch, like a needle skipping in a song I almost knew.

*The third word...* It turned back to me, and its young face was unreadable in the way Ilse's face had never quite been; the real Ilse wore every emotion like weather. This one wore none. *The third is kept where the prophecy says the Door stands. The field of ash. South and west, past the salt flats. You'll know it when the ground stops pretending to be ground.*

"The field of ash from the book," I said. "The margin drawing. The door standing in all that grey."

*You've been reading,* the ghost said, and sounded pleased, and something in the pleasure sat strangely on the young face, like a coat tailored for someone else. *Good. Books are the one weapon the Custodians haven't learned to fear enough.*

Outside, the wind moved through the thistles, and somewhere far off a dog barked twice and fell silent, and I stood in a burned granary talking to a sword, and none of it felt impossible anymore—that was perhaps the most frightening part. Eleven years in a valley without magic had taught me the shape of ordinary disbelief, and this wasn't it. This was recognition. Some deep, buried part of me had known the steel was never just steel.

"Why now?" I asked.

The ghost looked at me.

"Eleven years I carried you across the border of the Hollow and back, and never a word. Now, the season I learn the borders are closing, you wake up with a complete itinerary. Three sites. Precise directions." I wrapped my arms around myself, more against the question than the cold. "Why now, Ilse?"

For a moment—one heartbeat, two—the firelight moved through the ghost's chest and I could see the granary wall behind it, and the stillness of it was absolute, a portrait's stillness.

*Because the border woke me,* it said finally. *Magic cannot cross into the Hollow, girl. I am made of magic now, such as I am. You didn't carry a ghost into exile—you carried a seed into winter. Nothing grows there. Nothing speaks. The moment you stepped out of that valley, you stepped into spring, and everything planted in you began to bloom at once.* It smiled, and the smile reached the eyes this time, and my suspicion loosened its grip despite itself, traitorous thing. *You want a conspiracy? The truth is duller. You left. That's all. You finally left, and I could finally reach you.*

"That's not dull," I said quietly. "That's eleven years of silence I could have ended by walking fifty feet past a valley line."

*Could you have?* Ilse's voice gentled into the tone she'd used for hard lessons—the ones delivered after bruises, the ones that stuck. *At fifteen? At twenty, with the Custodians' clerks combing every road out of Vessany for survivors? You stayed hidden because staying hidden kept you alive, and I taught you that lesson with my last breath. I'll not apologize for sleeping through the years it saved your life.*

It was a good answer. It was a perfect answer. Somewhere in the back of my mind, a very small voice observed that perfect answers are rare in this world, and rarer still from the dead—but the voice was tired, and the night was cold, and the face above the sword belonged to the person I had loved most in a life that had taught me to love carefully.

So I let it go. Mostly.

"Saint Verre," I said instead. "How far?"

*A day's walk south, if Sable's maps deserve their reputation. Less if you stop mourning and start marching.* The ghost drifted back toward the sword, its edges already thinning into the old faint glow. *We leave at first light. And August—*

"Yes?"

*Bring the blade yourself. Don't let the cartographer carry it. There are words in that steel that belong to no one else's hands.* A pause, and then, softer, in a register I hadn't heard since Carrow Fen: *It's good to see you, girl. You've gotten tall. Your mother would have been insufferable about it.*

And it was gone—poured back into the steel like water into a dry bed, leaving only the faintest warmth in the metal when I touched it, the first warmth the sword had given off in eleven years.

* * *

Sable was awake, of course. She had probably been awake the entire time; the woman slept like a cat in a house full of dogs, one ear always open. She watched me from across the dead fire with those mapmaker's eyes that measured everything twice.

"So," she said. "The sword talks."

"The sword talks."

"To you specifically, or to anyone with a grudge and a bedtime?"

I lowered myself back onto my bedroll and stared up through the granary's shattered roof at the wheeling stars. "To me specifically. It's Ilse. Or—it wears her. Younger than she was. Sharper than she ever let herself be in public." I turned my head to look at Sable. "She knows where the words are. All three of them. Sites, directions, warnings. A century after her death, and she has a route plan."

Sable considered this. She drew her knees up and rested her chin on them, and for a long moment the only sound was the wind worrying the thistles outside.

"When I mapped for the Custodians," she said at last, "they had a saying. *Nothing survives erasure except the eraser.*" Her eyes flicked to the sword at my side, glowing faintly now, warm as a banked coal. "Whatever's living in that steel, Montrose—it remembers being erased. Which means it remembers who held the pen."

"It was my teacher," I said. "She died buying me time to run."

"I believe you." Sable lay back and pulled her cloak over her shoulder. "Believing isn't the same as not counting, though. I count everything. It's how I'm still unrecorded." She closed her eyes. "Ask it sometime why a ghost needs directions. Ghosts, in my experience, go where they're sent."

I didn't sleep much after that. I lay watching the sword breathe its faint light into the dark, and I told myself the tightness in my chest was grief, and joy, and the vertigo of reunion—all the honest feelings, stacked in their proper piles.

Underneath the piles, quiet as a clerk, the counting went on.

First light came grey and slow. By then I had decided. Twelve turns of the moon. A belltower full of silence, a river running backward, a field of ash—and between me and each of them, whatever waited in a country that had tried to forget me so thoroughly it had nearly succeeded.

I buckled the sword at my hip. It whispered, just once, soft as a hand smoothing hair:

*Good girl. March.*

We marched.
