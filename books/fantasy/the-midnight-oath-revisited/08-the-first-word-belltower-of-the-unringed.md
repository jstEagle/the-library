## 8. The First Word: Belltower of the Unringed

The belltower rose out of the plain like a finger raised in permanent accusation.

They had been walking toward it since dawn—Sable with her rolled maps slung across her back like a quiver of arrows, Ilse a shimmer at the edge of August's vision that no one else seemed to notice, and August herself with the sword riding her hip in a way it never had in the Hollow. In the valley the blade had been an object. Out here, past the boundary, it was a presence. It had weight that shifted. It breathed, if steel could be said to breathe, and all day it had been breathing faster as they drew near the tower.

"That's it," Sable said, stopping at the crest of a low hill. She shaded her eyes against the late afternoon sun, though there was little need; nothing stood between them and the tower but brown grass gone silver at the tips and a scattering of stones that might once have been a road. "Belltower of the Unringed. I mapped it twenty years ago, back when mapping was still my trade." Her mouth did something complicated. "Even then, the locals wouldn't say its name. They'd point."

"People point at a lot of things in this country," August said.

"They point because naming things is how you get taken." Sable started down the hill without waiting for agreement. "Custodians' logic. A thing named is a thing that exists, a thing that exists is a thing that can be accused. The bells rang for Vessany, so the bells were melted. But nobody could figure out what to do about the tower itself. You can't melt a building."

"You could tear it down."

"You could," Sable agreed. "But tearing it down would be an admission that it mattered. So they left it. Erasure isn't destruction, Montrose. Destruction leaves ruins, and ruins leave stories. Erasure leaves *furniture*—things so ordinary nobody thinks to look at them twice."

August looked at the tower again. Up close, she supposed, it might have been furniture: a square stone shaft, maybe eighty feet tall, windowless except for the belfry at the top, where empty arches opened onto sky like mouths mid-sentence. There was no door. There should have been a door. Every tower needed a door, and yet the base of the structure was seamless stone, unbroken from ground to the first ledge, as if the builders had intended it to be climbed only by birds and regret.

Ilse drifted closer to her shoulder. Even after three days of walking together, August's chest still did the thing—the lurch, the ache, the impossible joy of seeing her mentor's outline drawn in light instead of memory. Ilse looked young here, younger than August had ever known her, the way she must have looked before the purge, before the Hollow, before the long slow work of raising a child who didn't exist on paper. Her ghost-light made the grass around her feet seem greener.

"There," Ilse said. She pointed—not at the tower, but at the shadow the tower cast across the field. Long in the late sun, it lay like a fallen tree, and where the shadow ended, the grass grew differently. Darker. Denser. As if the shade itself had been watered.

"The door's in the shadow," August said.

"The door was always in the shadow." Ilse smiled, and there was pride in it, and something under the pride that August couldn't name and didn't try to. "You're learning to read the country again. Good. That's the first word of every lesson I ever gave you, girl. Look where looking costs you something."

* * *

The shadow was cold in a way shadows aren't.

August felt it as she stepped into it—a drop in temperature that went past her skin and settled somewhere behind her ribs, like swallowing winter. The sword woke fully then, humming against her hip, and beneath the hum she heard the whisper again, the one that had been growing louder every day since they crossed the boundary: fragments, syllables, the shape of words spoken by voices long dead. *Vessel. Verity. Ve-*—and then always the same cliff-edge of silence.

"Careful," Sable called from outside the shadow-line. She had stopped at its edge the way a swimmer stops at cold water. "I'll keep watch. Custodians use roads, and roads run through plains. If you see me wave anything other than my left hand, run."

"Left hand?"

"I'm left-handed. Anyone impersonating me will forget." She said it lightly, but her eyes were doing their mapmaker's arithmetic, measuring distances to cover, angles of retreat. August was beginning to understand that Sable Vane survived the way August survived: by knowing exactly where everything was, including the exits.

She stepped deeper into the shadow.

And found the door.

It hadn't been visible from outside—it wasn't visible now, exactly—but her hand knew where it was the way her mouth knew where her teeth were. Stone gave way under her palm, not opening so much as *admitting*, and she walked into the tower's throat.

Inside was dark and round and full of sound that wasn't sound.

That was the only way she could describe it, standing in the belfry chamber with her head tipped back toward the open arches. The bells were gone. She could see the beams where they'd hung, the great oak yokes bolted to the walls, each one empty, each one still bearing the polished groove where rope had run for centuries. The Custodians had melted the bells—but they had not accounted for the fact that a belltower does not hold bells. A belltower holds *ringing*, the way a book holds a story even after you burn it, the way a grave holds a name even after you chisel it smooth.

The silence up there was load-bearing. It pressed down on her. And underneath the silence, so faint she had to stop breathing to hear it, was the residue of every note those bells had ever sung—weddings, funerals, alarms, the calling of hours, the ringing-out of a kingdom's whole history—and underneath *that*, almost too deep to find, was a single word, repeated like a heartbeat:

*Listen.*

August's hands were shaking. She noticed them distantly, the way you notice weather happening to someone else.

"What do I do?" she whispered.

"Ring it," Ilse said softly. She had followed her inside—or had been inside already; ghost-logic refused to stay consistent—and now she stood by the first empty yoke, her light making the darkness around her seem darker by contrast. "The word is in the silence, child. The bells are gone, but the ringing isn't. Ring what remains."

"Ringing silence. That's—" August laughed, short and disbelieving. "That's not possible."

"No," Ilse agreed pleasantly. "It isn't. Do it anyway. That's the second lesson. Everything worth doing is impossible right up until your blood decides otherwise."

* * *

She tried for two hours.

She tried striking the air where the bell had hung. She tried singing into the emptiness. She tried holding the sword up and letting it hum, which produced a vibration in her teeth and nothing else. She tried pressing her palms flat against the stone and listening so hard her ears ached, and got nothing but her own pulse and, far below, Sable pacing the perimeter of the shadow like a cat patrolling a fence line.

Twice she caught herself nearly giving up, and both times she heard Ilse's voice in her head—not the ghost, the memory: *Frustration is just information, girl. It tells you you're asking the question wrong.*

So she changed the question.

She stopped trying to make a sound and started trying to *hear* one. That was swordspeaking, really—that was the whole art, the thing the purge had tried to kill and the Hollow had taught her to starve instead of feed. Steel speaks. The world speaks. Everything that exists says its own name constantly, quietly, in a language underneath language. A swordspeaker doesn't shout. A swordspeaker *listens until the listening becomes speech*.

She closed her eyes. She let the silence press down on her. She stopped fighting it.

And the silence, feeling her stop resisting, leaned in close like a curious animal.

*There,* said something that was not a voice. *There you are. We know your blood. Your blood rang us.*

Her heart slammed. She kept her eyes shut. *I'm here to take back a word.*

*Take?* The silence moved around her, amused and enormous. *Words aren't taken, oath-child. Words are kept. We have kept yours a hundred years. No one came. Everyone forgot. You forgot.*

*I didn't.* Her own anger surprised her, rising hot and clean through the fear. *I remembered enough to walk here. I remembered enough to knock.*

A pause. The silence considered her.

*Then ring,* it said.

She understood then—finally, stupidly, simply—what she had to do. Not strike. Not sing. She reached into the place where the bell had been, into the hollow where a hundred years of silence sat like water sitting in a cup, and she took hold of it the way she'd take hold of a rope, and she pulled.

The silence rang.

There was no sound. Her eardrums reported nothing. But the ringing went through her like a lance of absolute quiet, and the tower answered—every empty beam, every seamless stone, every groove worn by a dead rope sang a note made of absence, and in the middle of that vast silent chord, clear as a coin dropped in a well, was the word.

*"Verity."*

August fell to her knees. The word was inside her now, seated behind her sternum like a swallowed coal, warm and alive and unmistakably *hers*. The first word of the oath. Recovered. Found. Won.

She laughed—actually laughed, kneeling in the dark of a murdered belltower, tears running freely down her face. "I've got it," she called, not caring who heard, not caring if the whole erased nation heard. "Ilse—I've got the first word!"

"Well done," Ilse said, and her voice was gentle and proud and very far away. "Now come down slowly, child. And look at your shadow before you do."

* * *

The sun had dropped low while she worked. When August stepped out of the tower's shadow into the last red light of evening, Sable was waiting, and Sable was staring at the ground beside her.

At her shadow.

"Sable?"

"Don't move." Sable circled her slowly, the way she'd circle an unfamiliar landmark she intended to chart. "Walk forward. Slowly. Toward me."

August took a step. Her body moved. Her shadow moved—

Half a beat later.

Not much. Anyone glancing would see nothing wrong. But August had spent fifteen years training her eyes to catch the flicker of a lie, and there was no mistaking it: her shadow lagged. It moved the way a delayed echo sounds, the way a memory replays. It arrived everywhere she went a half-second after she did, as if it had to think about following her. As if it were deciding.

"What did you do in there?" Sable asked. There was no accusation in it, which somehow made it worse. Just a cartographer noting that the terrain had changed.

"I rang the silence." August's voice came out thin. "The first word was in it. *Verity.* It's mine now—I can feel it, it's—" She pressed a hand to her sternum. Warm coal. Alive. "It's part of me."

"Mm." Sable looked at the lagging shadow a moment longer, then deliberately met her eyes. "In my old trade we had a rule: every mark you put on a map, the map puts a mark on you. You chart a road, the road knows your name. I used to think it was superstition." She rolled up her kit with sharp, economical motions. "Then I watched a colleague of mine draw a route into a place that didn't exist, and afterward he couldn't be seen by anyone who wasn't looking for him. He's still out there somewhere. Unfindable. Unremembered. The price of touching erased things, Montrose, is that erased things touch you back."

"It's the cost of the word," Ilse said, drifting to August's side, her light pale in the dying sun. "Every piece of the oath you recover makes you more of what you were always meant to be. More visible. More *findable*. That's not a flaw in the magic, child—it's the design. An oath is a public act. You cannot swear yourself in secret."

"Then whoever hunts oath-bound blood," Sable said quietly, "just got a clearer picture of where you are."

As if summoned by the sentence, the sword whispered at August's hip—one syllable, one fragment, *ve-*—and cut off. And far off across the darkening plain, so distant it might have been imagination, August saw lights. Three of them. Moving along a line that no road officially occupied, moving with the unhurried discipline of people consulting a ledger.

Moving, she realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the evening, *toward the tower*.

"They know," she said. "Ilse—they already know I'm here."

Ilse was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice was calm, warm, absolutely certain—the voice of every lesson August had ever survived by obeying.

"Then we don't go back the way we came. Trust me, child. I've gotten you out of worse than this."

August wanted—with a fierceness that frightened her—to believe her. And she did believe her. That was the trouble. She believed her completely, the way she had since she was six years old, and only later, much later, would she remember that the lights had appeared *immediately*, as if they had known precisely when and where to look—as if somewhere, patient as weather, someone had been counting the seconds since the silence rang.

She turned away from the tower, toward the dark, and followed her ghost.
