## 9. The Moon Counts Aloud

The moon was four nights old when it began to count.

Sable felt it first in her maps—the way the ink on her best route north had gone faintly gray, as though the paper were breathing through it. She sat by the fire in the ruined chapel where they'd made camp, holding the sheet up to the light, and watched a river she'd drawn from memory thin to a whisper of itself.

Ink did not fade in four days. Ink faded in decades, or never. But this was Vessany, and Vessany had been erased once already; the country remembered how it was done and was practicing on them.

"August," she said. "Come look at this."

August came out of the dark with her sword wrapped in sacking across her back, the way she carried it now that it had started talking—muffled, like a birdcage covered at night. Firelight caught the scar along her jaw and the wariness underneath it. Four days of walking through a dead country had taught Sable the shape of that wariness: August trusted nothing that stayed still too long, including campfires, including Sable.

"The map's fading," Sable said. "All of it. The roads are still there—" she pointed at the window, at the pale ribbon of the old king's road winding through black fields "—but the record of them is going."

August took the sheet. Turned it over, as if the truth might be printed on the back. "Like the villages," she said quietly. "Like the names."

"Yes." Sable fed a stick to the fire. "The erasure isn't finished. It's ongoing. We're walking through something being unwritten in real time, and we're standing on the page."

August looked at her for a long moment. Then: "How much farther?"

"To the river?" Sable consulted the ghost of her own drawing, the lines she could still see because she'd drawn them and memory is stubborn. "Two days. Maybe three if the roads keep stretching. They do that here now. I measured a mile yesterday that took an hour and a half."

"And after the river?"

"After the river, the belltower's shadow-side. After the tower, the field of ash." Sable hesitated. She had been hesitating for two days, which was unlike her; hesitation was a luxury for people whose pasts didn't have teeth. "And after the ash field, the Door."

August sat down across the fire from her, sword across her knees, and looked into the flames. The wrapped bundle gave one soft murmur, like someone dreaming aloud, and August pressed her palm flat against it without seeming to notice she'd done it. Sable noticed. Sable noticed everything; it was the trade she'd chosen instead of a country.

"You've gone quiet," August said. "Quieter. You've been quieter since the second night, and you were already quiet."

"I'm a cartographer. Quiet is the job."

"You talk to your maps. I've seen you argue with them."

"That's different. Maps hold their ground."

August almost smiled. Almost. Then the smile went wherever smiles went in her, and she said, plainly, the way she said everything: "Something's wrong. Tell me before I have to guess. I'm bad at guessing. Ilse used to say I solved problems by hitting them until they confessed."

Sable looked at her across the small fire, and the confession rose in her like floodwater against a door she'd been leaning on for days.

She might have held it longer. She wanted to hold it longer. But out in the dark beyond the chapel walls, something answered the question for her—a sound so ordinary it took her heartbeat a moment to understand it as terrible.

A bell. A small handbell, ringing twice, politely, in the middle of a country whose bells had all been melted down.

* * *

They put the fire out and moved through the chapel's broken nave to the east wall, where a window stood in its frame like a pulled tooth's socket. Sable pressed herself to the stone beside it. August came up on her other side, silent as snowfall, and Sable thought—not for the first time—that the girl had been built for exactly this and hated every part of herself that fit.

Three lights in the field below. Lanterns on long poles, carried high, the way clerks carry things they don't want to set down. And between the lights, figures in gray coats, moving in step, unhurried, patient as weather. At their head walked a tall figure with a ledger chained to its hip, and every few dozen paces the tall figure raised the handbell and rang it twice, and stopped, and listened, and wrote something down.

"Custodians," Sable breathed. It wasn't a word she meant to say aloud. It left her mouth like a splinter working loose.

August turned her head slowly. In the starlight her eyes were very still. "You know them."

"Everyone from Vessany knows them."

"No." August's voice didn't rise. It got flatter, which was worse. "Everyone from Vessany fears them. Knowing is different. You said the word like a rank. Like a colleague."

The tall figure below rang the bell again. Twice. And in the silence after, Sable heard what the bell was for: not alarm. Inventory. Each ring was followed by a pause in which the Custodians listened for anything that rang back—for oath-words, for swordspeech, for the hum of magic moving through blood. They weren't hunting. They were auditing.

"They'll find us," August said. "That bell will find us. What do we do?"

"We run now, while they're checking the far field, and we don't stop until—"

"How do you know their procedure?" August asked. Still flat. Still terribly calm. "Their pacing. Their listening gaps. Sable." Her hand had drifted, not to her sword but to Sable's wrist, and closed there, gentle and immovable. "How do you know when they write things down?"

And there it was. The door she'd been leaning against, swinging open onto everything behind it.

Sable could lie. Lying was a skill like mapping—you learned the terrain of a person, drew the route you wanted them to walk, shaded it attractively. She had done it professionally for years. For the Custodians, in fact.

Instead she said, "Because I mapped for them."

August's grip didn't tighten. That was the frightening thing. It simply stayed, exactly as it was, while the whole world rearranged itself around those five words.

"Eleven years ago," Sable went on, because once the door was open the flood does what floods do, "I was the finest road-surveyor in the capital of Vessany, which was a real city then, with a name, though I couldn't tell you what it was now because I helped take the name out. The purge didn't just kill swordspeakers, August. Killing is fast and leaves witnesses. The purge was *editing*. Someone had to erase the roads to the places where swordspeakers trained. The fords they crossed. The valleys that sheltered them. Geography remembers people longer than paper does, so geography had to go." She heard her own voice go dry and procedural, the voice she used for coordinates. "I drew the removals. I surveyed a village, and I wrote its roads into a ledger marked SUBTRACTION, and six weeks later travelers would walk through where it had been and feel nothing but a strange urge to look at the sky."

Below them, the lanterns paused. The tall figure wrote.

"When I understood what I was doing—I'm not going to pretend I didn't know for a long time, I knew, I liked the work, I liked being the best at it—when I finally understood, I tried to undo it. I drew a road back to a place I'd subtracted. A sanctuary. There were survivors there." Her throat closed around the next words and she made herself push them through anyway; August deserved them unsoftened. "The Custodians found the sanctuary because I led them to it. Not on purpose. My map was good. Good maps work for whoever holds them. Forty-one people. I can still draw every face. I fled the same night, and I've been exiled ever since—from my guild, my name, my country. I survive by being unrecorded. Same as you."

The fire pit smoked between them and the dark. Somewhere below, the bell rang twice more, closer to the chapel now, patient as arithmetic.

August hadn't let go of her wrist. August also hadn't spoken.

"Say something," Sable said. "Even if it's the end. Say it quickly. I hate suspense; it's bad surveying."

"You mapped forty-one people into a grave," August said at last, each word laid down like a stone.

"Yes."

"And you just told me instead of lying to me."

Sable blinked. "Yes."

"Why?"

"Because you asked. Because—" and here the flood took even the professional voice away, and what was left was just a tired woman in a cold chapel, "—because I have spent eleven years drawing routes for other people's escapes, and every single one of them ended where mine did, alone, with excellent maps. Because you are the first person in eleven years who has walked anywhere *with* me. And because if you learn what I am from a Custodian's ledger instead of my own mouth, then they win twice."

August looked at her for a long time. Out in the field, the lantern light swung nearer the chapel wall, throwing the shadows of the gray coats across the floor at their feet—three long fingers of darkness, reaching.

Then August did something Sable did not expect. She laughed. Once, low, no humor in it and some strange relief.

"Ilse," she said, "used to tell me that everyone who survives is guilty of something, and the only question worth asking is whether they're guilty *toward* you or *against* you. Everything else is bookkeeping." Her grip on Sable's wrist finally changed—loosened, slid, became briefly a squeeze, and released. "Forty-one people. You carry it. You told me the number without rounding it down. That's guilt *toward*, as far as I can measure it." A breath. "I've spent my life hiding in a valley because someone died teaching me to hide. I don't get to be the judge of anyone's sins tonight. Come on. We need a way past three clerks and a bell."

"They're not clerks," Sable said grimly. "Clerks is what they were. The ledger changed them. Write enough names out of existence and eventually you start believing you're holding the pen God lost."

"Then we need a way past three inquisitors."

"That I can help with." And despite everything—the bell, the lanterns, the ledger chained to a hip in the dark—Sable felt something in her chest unknot for the first time in eleven years. Chosen. Audited and chosen. "There's a drainage culvert under the chapel. Every chapel in old Vessany had one; the builders were obsessed with water rights. It won't be on any current map."

"Because you removed it?"

"Because I *drew* it," Sable said, and allowed herself the smallest of smiles. "Some things I hid inside the subtraction. A surveyor's revenge. Follow me."

* * *

The culvert was exactly where she'd penciled it a lifetime ago, choked with roots and standing water, and it stank of a century of rain with nowhere better to be. They crawled through it in the dark, August ahead because August insisted on going first toward danger—it was, Sable was learning, the only direction she trusted—and came out in a ditch on the far side of the hill with wet knees and the taste of old stone in their mouths.

Behind them, the handbell rang twice over the empty chapel, and then, after a pause, a third time. A different interval. Sable knew that interval. Her stomach dropped.

"What's the third ring mean?" August whispered.

"It means they found something." Sable pulled her along the ditch, low, fast. "A trace. Residue. Oath-blood leaves a mark on places, like heat on glass. They'll read the residue, and they'll know a swordspeaker passed through here within the last day, and they'll know roughly which way." She glanced back once. Far off, the three lanterns had begun to move—not randomly, not searching. In formation. A line abreast, sweeping the land like a brushstroke. "And they'll know roughly *who*, because there's only one swordspeaker left to be."

"They'll follow us to the Door."

"They'll follow us everywhere. That's the point of them. They aren't soldiers, August, you can't outrun a filing system. They don't sleep angry or march proud. They just... arrive, wherever the paperwork says you'll be." Sable scrambled up the ditch bank and pulled August up after her, and for a moment they crouched together in the open, under a sky salted with stars, the dead fields rolling away silver-black to every horizon. "Which means we have maybe a day's lead. Two if the roads keep stretching in our favor."

"Then we don't waste either of them." August unwrapped the sword from its sacking, and even in starlight Sable saw the steel had begun to hold a light that wasn't starlight—a faint, patient gleam, like something underwater turning over. The blade murmured. August tilted her head, listening the way she listened, and her face did something complicated.

"What did it say?" Sable asked.

"It's counting." August's voice had gone quiet and strange. "The moons. It says the moon counts aloud now. It says—" she stopped, and Sable watched her decide whether to finish the sentence, and finish it, "—it says we should reach the river before the fifth turn. It says the river remembers things. Things that don't fit."

"Things that don't fit," Sable repeated. "Fit into what?"

August wrapped the sword again, slowly, and looked north, toward water neither of them could see yet.

"I don't know," she said. "But the ghost knows the third word, Sable. And Ilse died before the third word was ever spoken."

The wind moved through the dead fields, carrying, very faintly, from miles behind them, the sound of a handbell ringing twice—and then, patiently, a third time.

Sable unrolled a fresh sheet of paper against her knee and began, in the dark, by touch, to draw the road to the river.

"* * *" — she thought absurdly, watching her pencil find the line — "*the moon counts aloud, and we are the numbers.*"

"Walk fast," she said aloud. "We're on a deadline written by someone who wanted us here."

And somewhere behind them, in the dark, the Custodians opened their ledger and began, in careful clerk's hands, to write the day's subtraction: *two travelers, one road, one river.*

The ink was already fading as they wrote it.
