## 10. The Second Word: River That Remembers

The river found us before we found it.

We had been walking since the fourth moon's first night—since the Custodians' torches swept the ridge behind us and Sable chose honesty over a clean lie, telling me she'd once drawn maps for the very clerks who hunted us. I was still carrying that confession in my chest like a swallowed stone. Not a betrayal, exactly. But not nothing.

"You'll want to take off your boots," Sable said, when the sound of water reached us through the birch trees.

I laughed. "It's autumn."

"It's that river." She stopped at the tree line and would go no farther, and I understood then why her voice was careful in the way voices get careful around graves. "The River That Remembers. It flows backward through memory—not upstream, understand, but *backward*. You don't wade into it. You wade into yourself."

I looked past her shoulder to where the water showed silver between the trunks. It didn't look like anything but water. It never does. That is the oldest trick in the world: catastrophe dressed as an ordinary Tuesday.

"The second word is in there," I said.

"The second word has always been in there. It was put there by someone who knew you'd come looking for it, which means it isn't hidden. It's *bait*." Sable crouched and began sketching in the dirt with a stick—the habit of a woman whose hands needed to be making maps even when there were none left to make. Her own maps had been blanking for days now, lines fading like breath off glass. "Ilse told you three sites?"

"She did."

"And she told you how to survive this one?"

I thought of Ilse's ghost, young-seeming and sharp-tongued, standing in the smoke of our campfire that never quite burned down to coals. *Don't drink*, she'd said. *Don't look away from what it shows you, and don't drink.* Three instructions, delivered in the clipped rhythm of every lesson she'd ever given me. I had accepted them without question, because accepting Ilse's instructions without question was the shape of my whole childhood.

"She gave me rules," I said.

"Good." Sable's eyes stayed on my face a moment too long, reading something there I hadn't written yet. "Then follow them exactly. And August—" She hesitated, and I watched her decide whether to say the rest. She was getting better at saying things. So was I. We were teaching each other, badly. "Whatever it shows you will be true. That's the cruelty of this place. Rivers don't lie. They just don't show everything."

*Don't show everything.*

Later I would turn those four words over so many times they wore smooth as river stone. But that morning I only nodded, pulled off my boots, and walked barefoot into the cold.

* * *

The water took me the way sleep takes a child mid-sentence.

One moment I was ankle-deep in October; the next, I was nine years old and the bells of Callowmere were ringing wrong.

Not unringed—that came later, that horror belonged to the tower we'd already visited—but wrong. Bells rung out of order, rung in panic, rung by hands that had never learned the patterns because the men who knew them were already dead on the cathedral steps. I stood in the market square of my childhood with flour on my fingers and my mother's voice somewhere behind me calling my name, except it wasn't *August*, it was the name I'd had before names became dangerous, and I couldn't hold onto it. The river kept it. The river keeps everything.

I tried to run. My legs were nine years old and short and they carried me anyway, through stalls overturned and loaves abandoned, through the smell of smoke that wasn't baking bread. I knew what day this was. Every part of me knew what day this was, and every part of me ran from knowing it at once, and the river held me in the running the way a fist holds a moth—not crushing, just refusing to let go.

*I can leave*, I told myself. *It's memory. It's water. I can open my eyes.*

But Sable had said whatever it shows will be true, and some traitorous scholar-part of me, the part Ilse had spent ten years sharpening, understood that truth was exactly why I'd been sent here. The word wasn't floating on the surface of the river. It was sunk to the bottom of the worst day of my life, and the only way down was through.

So I stopped running.

It felt like dying. It felt like the moment after you decide to stop drowning, which is either surrender or salvation depending entirely on what happens next. I planted my nine-year-old feet in the cobbles of the square and I made myself watch.

The Custodians came up the Processional Road in their grey coats, and here is the thing no one tells children who survive purges: the men doing it were bored. That is what the years had blurred out and the river restored in merciless detail. They were not shrieking devils. They checked lists. One of them had ink on his fingers and wiped his nose with the back of his wrist between entries. A clerk's gesture. A tired man's gesture. He crossed out names in a ledger the way a farmer crosses out seed that failed to sprout, and beside him other men did the wet work, and the wet work was almost incidental, an administrative footnote to the crossing-out.

Erasure first. Flesh second. I had remembered it backwards my whole life, because remembering it forwards meant understanding that the killing was never the point.

My mother found me in the square. In the true memory—in the one I'd carried like a splinter for fifteen years—she shoved me into the flour cellar and pressed her lips to my hair and said *don't make a sound, my heart, don't make a sound,* and the door closed, and that was the last of her I ever saw or let myself see.

The river gave me more door than I had ever allowed myself.

Because I was watching her hands this time. Watching the way survivors learn to watch, late and uselessly. Her hands were shaking, yes—but they shook *toward* the cellar door, not away from it. And before she closed it she glanced over her shoulder, once, quick, toward the alley mouth. Toward someone standing there. Someone whose face the memory refused to render, smearing it the way a dream smears a face, but whose silhouette I knew.

Grey coat. Ledger under one arm.

She hadn't been hiding me from the purge. Or not only. She had been waiting for it—watching the alley for the clerk who was coming to check the list, buying seconds with her body so the crossing-out could happen properly. And her last words to me, *don't make a sound*, had not been tenderness. They had been instruction. Compliance. A mother performing her small assigned role in the machinery, believing—I will believe this until I die, because the alternative is unbearable—that if the paperwork went smoothly, her daughter might be permitted to remain unrecorded rather than deleted.

Unrecorded. Like me. Like the Hollow. The whole architecture of my survival had been built that day, brick by brick, by people following orders I had never seen.

The cellar door closed. The dark came. And in the dark, in the flour-smelling silence where I had hidden while my country died, something moved against my ankle in the present tense—cold water, actual water, the river itself—and against my skin, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, the second word brushed me and slipped away.

* * *

I came up gasping on the far bank, soaked to the ribs though the water had never reached past my knees. Sable had a fire going. She didn't ask questions, which was its own kind of mercy, and she didn't offer comfort either, which was the other kind.

"I saw the purge," I said finally. My voice sounded dredged. "Really saw it. Not the version I've been carrying."

"That's the river."

"There were clerks. There were *ledgers*, Sable. They killed us second. First they—" My hands started shaking and I watched them do it with detached interest, as though they belonged to the girl in the flour cellar. "First they erased us. My mother died holding a door shut for a bureaucrat."

Sable fed a stick to the fire. When she spoke, her voice had the flat care of someone handling something sharp. "That's how it worked everywhere I mapped for them. The ledger comes first. Always. You can burn a country down in a night, but a country that's still *written down* grows back. Kill the record and it stays dead." A pause. "I drew the roads their clerks used. I told myself it was just geography. Geography doesn't care what travels on it." She looked up. "I'm telling you this because your face right now is the face I had for about six years. I want you to know it gets less loud. It doesn't get quiet. Just less loud."

Something in my chest loosened a fraction—an inch of give in a knot I'd been carrying since the ridge. Honesty traded for honesty. Maybe that was all trust ever was: two women showing each other their ledgers, page by page, and deciding the sums didn't cancel each other out.

"I have the word," I said. "Almost. It touched me and got away. It's still down there."

"Then you know what you have to do." Sable didn't pretend not to understand. "Go back under. Deeper."

* * *

The second descent was worse, because the second time you know.

The river took me to the cellar again, but deeper this time—past the door closing, into the weeks after. Into the flight. Ilse finding me, or me finding Ilse; even now the order of it was lost, two half-dead creatures stumbling into each other's orbit in the ruins of Callowmere's outskirts. She was forty-something then, grey coming in at her temples, a sword on her back that she carried the way priests carry relics nobody believes in anymore.

*Last swordspeaker*, she told me later, laughing at herself around a campfire much like Sable's. *Which makes you the last swordstudent, which makes the whole order your problem now, congratulations.*

In the river-memory I watched her teach me the first grip. Watched myself, seven years old and feral with grief, refuse to cry and refuse to eat and refuse everything except the sword, because the sword was the only thing in the world that hadn't been crossed out. I watched Ilse sit awake every night while I slept, watching the dark with an expression I had spent fifteen years misreading as vigilance.

Watching it now, with a clerk's attention—with the attention the purge itself had taught me, that records matter, that details matter—I read the expression differently.

It wasn't vigilance. It was patience.

No—it was *anticipation*. The look of a person keeping watch over something they expect to ripen. Ilse sat by our fire like a farmer sits by a field in spring, and I had mistaken it for love, and maybe it was love, but it was love of the kind that has a plan attached.

And then the death. The river took me there, because of course it did, because it keeps everything.

The pass above Vessany's western edge. Winter. The Custodians' outriders had tracked us for days, and Ilse sent me down the goat trail alone with the sword and the first word of the oath already taught, already buried in me like a seed. *Run, don't look back, remember what I gave you.* I ran. Of course I ran; I was twelve and obedient in the only direction obedience had left.

But the river let me stay this time. The river held me in the snow above the pass while the outriders closed, and it showed me Ilse turning to face them, and it showed me—

It showed me her mouth moving.

Words. Deliberate words, spoken clearly in the cold air, with the cadence of ritual, of recitation—and the river, which does not lie but does not show everything, gave me the shapes of her lips without the sounds. I read them the way I'd learned to read everything in that water: closely, greedily, hunting the detail that didn't fit.

Three syllables. Four. A phrase with the rhythm of an ending. And her hand, her sword-hand, pressed flat against the blade at her back in a gesture I knew—*I knew it*—as the gesture that closes an oath. The sealing sign. The sign a swordspeaker makes when binding a thing *into steel*.

The outriders reached her. The snow took the rest. I was never shown her death in full, not then, not now; the river, too, has editors.

I surfaced for the second time, and this time Sable caught my arms before I fell, and I was sobbing and laughing in the same ugly breath, because I had it—the word had come up with me, lodged behind my teeth like a river stone, the second word of the oath, *mine* now, recovered from the bottom of my own worst year—

—and underneath the triumph, quiet as the clerk's ink-stained fingers, the detail that didn't fit sat up and looked at me.

Ilse had died speaking an oath-phrase. Ilse had died with her hand in the sealing sign. Ilse had died *performing the binding herself*—which meant that in her final moments, alone in the snow with enemies closing in, the last swordspeaker of Vessany had chosen not to fight, not to flee, not even simply to die, but to work.

To finish a piece of magic.

And the ghost who wore her face, who had found us in the sword-whispers and mapped our road site by site, who gave me rules for rivers and warnings for towers—who had told me, in her sharp and loving voice, *the third word waits at the field of ash, little star, and I will be with you when you speak it*—

How did a dead woman know the words she'd never finished learning?

I opened my mouth to tell Sable. To say it out loud, to hear how it sounded in air instead of water: *she was casting something when she died; the ghost knows too much; the dates fit too neatly; I think we're being guided.*

Across the fire, Sable was watching me with those mapmaker's eyes that missed no territory, and beyond her, at the edge of the firelight where the dark began, the air shimmered faintly—young-seeming, sharp-tongued, patient as weather.

"August," Ilse said warmly. "You're shivering. Come away from that water. It shows you things that aren't real."

I closed my mouth. I smiled. I held the second word behind my teeth like a stone I was not yet ready to swallow, and I thought: *the river doesn't lie. It just doesn't show everything.*

Neither, apparently, did ghosts.
