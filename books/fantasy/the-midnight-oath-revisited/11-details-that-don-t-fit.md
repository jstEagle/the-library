## 11. Details That Don't Fit

August did not sleep.

She sat at the edge of the dying campfire with the second oath-word folded into her like a coal she'd swallowed, and she turned the memory over and over the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. The River That Remembers had given her back her childhood whole—every detail sharp as new-struck flint—and it was precisely in those details that something had gone wrong.

She closed her eyes and walked through it again, slowly this time, the way Ilse had taught her to read a room before entering it. *Look for what doesn't move,* the old lesson went. *The lie is always standing still.*

The night of the purge. She was nine. The sky above Vessany's western hills was the color of a bruise going green, and the bells of Corveth had stopped mid-toll—not silenced by order but drowned, as if the country itself had put its head underwater. Ilse had come for her through the smoke with her coat on inside out, which August remembered because even at nine she had thought it was funny, and Ilse had not laughed, and that was how she knew it was real.

They ran. The memory carried her down the goat path behind the schoolhouse, past the well with the crooked lip, into the birch wood where the ground went silver under a moon one turn short of full. And there—the part she had spent twenty years refusing to look at directly—Ilse stopped.

Custodians coming up the slope. Three of them, gray-robed, their faces blank as ledger paper. Ilse pushing August into the hollow beneath the fallen birch, pressing the sword into her hands, hilt-first, the way you hand someone something precious and breakable.

And then Ilse had spoken to her. Last words. August had kept them all these years like relics: *The oath has three words, little star. You know two already. Guard them until you're somewhere the world can't reach you, and then never speak them at all.* And then Ilse had stood up out of the hollow, drawn steel, and died there among the birches with her back to August so the child would not see her face.

August opened her eyes. The fire had burned down to a red eye of coals. Across the camp, Sable slept sitting up against her rolled maps, one hand still resting on the leather tube as if it might be stolen in the night. And beside Sable, close enough to touch the embers without flinching, sat Ilse—young-seeming, sharp-eyed, sharpening a stick with a knife she didn't need, because ghosts didn't need whittled sticks, and August had never once thought about that until now.

*You know two already.*

Two. Ilse had said *two*. On the last night of her life, with the third word unspent and unspoken, Ilse had told her child-self that she knew only two of the three.

So how did the ghost know all three?

---

She waited until the sky went the color of dishwater, until Sable woke and grumbled her way through cold tea and map-checking, until they were walking—because it was harder to have a dangerous conversation standing still than moving, another Ilse lesson, and August wondered bitterly how many of the lessons had been designed for moments exactly like this one.

They followed the old military road east through country that remembered being conquered. Burned farmsteads stood in fields of waist-high wheat that no one harvested. A shrine to some forgotten saint had been re-carved with the Custodians' seal, a closed book in an open eye, and someone had tried to chisel it off and failed.

Ilse walked at August's left shoulder, which was where she had always walked. That was the trouble with ghosts made of lessons: they came pre-arranged in all your habits.

"You barely touched the water when you pulled the word," Ilse said. "Most swordspeakers weep in that river. You knelt down like you were checking the depth."

"I wasn't crying," August said.

"No," Ilse agreed. "You were reading. I taught you that. Look for what doesn't move." A pause, light as a dropped leaf. "What did you find?"

There it was. Offered to her, straight out, before August had even decided to ask. It should have been reassuring—that Ilse saw everything, that nothing got past her mentor. Instead it felt like watching a card player turn over the ace before anyone had called for it.

"The night you died," August said. "In the birch wood."

"Ah." Ilse kept walking. Her boots made no sound; August's crunched on old cinder gravel. "That's a heavy thing to carry at breakfast."

"You told me I knew two words."

"I did."

"You said, *guard them until you're somewhere the world can't reach you, and then never speak them at all.*" August recited it flat, the way Sable read coordinates, stripping out everything that made it grief. "Two words. You counted them for me. You were very precise about numbers. You made me count my sword-drills in fives, you made me count my breaths in fours. You never rounded anything in your life."

They crested a low rise. Below them, the road bent around a dry fountain where a stone woman poured water forever into an empty basin. Ilse stopped beside it, looking down at the carved face as if it were an old colleague.

"So ask me," Ilse said quietly.

"How do you know the third word?"

The question landed between them and stayed there, the way a dropped sword stays on a floor. Sable, ten paces ahead, slowed without turning—she had heard, August realized, all of it, and was giving them the courtesy of distance while staying near enough to matter.

Ilse looked at the dry fountain a moment longer. Then she turned, and her ghost-face wore an expression August knew intimately: the patient, fond, faintly exasperated look of every lesson ever begun.

"Do you remember," Ilse said, "the winter you were eleven, and you found my field journal? The one with the cipher in it?"

"This isn't an answer."

"It's the beginning of one, which is more than most answers are. Do you remember?"

August remembered. Of course she remembered. She had picked the lock on Ilse's campaign chest with a hairpin and a week of patience, and found a journal written half in cipher, and decoded exactly one page before Ilse caught her. She had expected rage. Instead Ilse had sat down across from her, taken the journal gently out of her hands, and said: *What did you learn?*

"That some things are locked for a reason," August said now, the old answer, worn smooth by forty years of handling.

"And what did you actually learn?"

August's jaw tightened. "That you lock things you don't want found."

"That I lock things *until the person looking is ready to carry them*." Ilse spread her hands, and the morning light went through her fingers like water through a sieve. "Little star. You pulled two words out of bell-silence and backward water. You've crossed the boundary of the Hollow. You've done more in twelve moon-turns than I managed in a hundred years of being dead. If there were a lock on that third word, don't you think you've earned the key?"

"It's not a key I'm asking for. It's a source."

"A source." Ilse laughed—soft, genuine, hurt around the edges. "You want to know where a ghost buys her knowledge. Fine. Here is where: I am the sword's memory. I am every hour that blade was carried and every word spoken over it. Your oath passed across that steel the day you were bound to it—all three words, in the rite, in the old grammar. You weren't old enough to keep them, so I kept them for you. They live in me the way heat lives in a hearthstone long after the fire's out." She stepped closer. Her eyes were Ilse's eyes exactly, gray-green, quick, kind. "I know the third word the way you know the smell of bread baking. Not because anyone told me this morning. Because it's been in the walls of me since before you could write."

It was a good answer. It was a *complete* answer. Every piece fitted every other piece, no gaps, no rough edges, nothing left standing still.

And that, August thought, with a coldness rising in her like groundwater, was exactly the problem.

Because she had been in the birch wood. She had been nine years old and terrified and holding a sword too big for her, but she had *been there*, and the river had shown her the memory clean as glass—and in the memory, Ilse had said *two*. Not because she was protecting August. Because she was counting. Precise, number-loving, round-nothing Ilse, delivering her final inventory: two words in the child's keeping, one word still loose in the world, location unstated, disposition unknown.

If the rite had put all three words into the blade—if the hearthstone held everything—then the dying Ilse would have said *three*. Or she would have said nothing at all. But *two* was a specific thing to say. *Two* meant that at that moment, in that wood, Ilse herself had known only two.

Unless she had lied to a dying nine-year-old in her last breath.

Or unless—

August made herself stop. Made herself look away from the arithmetic, because on the other side of the equation stood the only person who had ever loved her without conditions, and love was arguing loudly, furiously, waving every year of the Hollow and every lesson and every night Ilse's voice had pulled her back from despair—and the evidence just stood there quietly, saying *two*, saying *two*, saying *two*.

"You're doing the thing," Ilse said gently. "Where your face goes still and your mind goes loud. I used to be able to hear it across a parade ground."

"I'm fine."

"You're not, but you will be. So let me give you today's lesson instead, since you've clearly decided I owe you tuition." Ilse leaned against the dry fountain, arms folded, entirely at ease in her own impossible existence. "Trust. Everyone thinks trust is a feeling. It isn't. It's a wager. You place it knowing you can't see the other side of the table, and you place it anyway, because the alternative is a life spent counting everyone's fingers. I have been dead for twenty years, August. Everything you have of me fits in a whisper and a length of steel. If I wanted to hurt you, I've had two decades and unlimited access to your sleeping mind. Instead I've spent them teaching you to stand straight." Her voice softened. "At some point, little star, the wager has to be placed. Not because the odds are good. Because you can't live at the table forever."

Sable had drifted back within earthing-shot, pretending to study the fountain. Her eyes met August's over Ilse's shoulder—one cartographer's flat, assessing glance, the look of a woman who trusted no one's odds and said so with her eyebrows. *Well?*

August looked at her mentor. Twenty years of voice in the dark. Twenty years of the only family she had left, and family was a wager, Ilse was right about that, family was always a wager—

But the river didn't wager. The river just remembered.

"I believe you," August said, and hated how much effort it took to make it sound true. "I believe you kept the words safe. Let's find the ash-field."

Ilse smiled, and the smile was warm, and warm things cast shadows, and August watched the shadow of her mentor fall across the empty fountain and thought, very quietly, in the locked room behind her own teeth:

*Then say the third word now. Say it here, on an open road, with witnesses. If it lives in you like heat in a hearthstone, say it, and prove the walls hold what you claim.*

Ilse said nothing. She fell into step at August's left shoulder, where she had always walked, and began pointing out edible nettles along the roadside as though the morning were an ordinary morning, and the third word stayed where it was—unspoken, unoffered, standing perfectly still.

They walked east. Behind them, in the dry basin of the fountain, the stone woman went on pouring her water into nothing, faithfully, forever, to no one.

And far ahead, past the bend of the road, past the hills that remembered being conquered, a column of dust rose against the morning—too regular for weather, too deliberate for beasts. Sable stopped, unrolled a corner of map, and went pale in a way August had learned to recognize.

"Custodians," Sable said. "Three days out. Moving like they know exactly where we'll be."

August looked at Ilse. Ilse was looking at the dust, and her ghost-face wore an expression August had never seen on it before.

Not fear.

*Recognition.*
