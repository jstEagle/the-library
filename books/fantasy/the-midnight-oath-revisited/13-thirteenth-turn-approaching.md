## 13. Thirteenth Turn Approaching

The twelfth moon rose over Vessany like a coin someone had flipped and forgotten to catch.

August felt it before she saw it—a tightening behind her sternum, the same sensation as a door closing in a house you'd thought empty. She stood at the window of the abandoned mill where they'd sheltered for two nights, watching the light of it pool silver on the broken cobblestones, and she thought: *one more turn. One more, and everything I am gets sealed inside a country that isn't supposed to exist.*

Behind her, Sable Vane was cursing at her maps.

Not ordinary cursing—Sable cursed ordinarily the way other people breathed, low and constant and almost musical—but the particular, rising, desperate cursing of a cartographer watching her life's work erase itself.

"Tell me what you see," Sable said without turning around.

August crossed the room. The maps were spread across the miller's old counting table, weighted at the corners with river stones and a rusted tin of buttons, and they were—wrong. Not torn, not stained. *Blanking.* The ink was fading from the edges inward, as though the paper were drinking it, and where the road to the ash-field should have run bold and certain there was now only a pale suggestion of a line, like a scar on skin that had mostly healed.

"The western route is gone," August said. "The one through the hollow hills."

"It's not gone." Sable pressed her palm flat against the parchment, hard, as if she could hold the ink down by force. "It can't be gone. I drew that road myself. I walked it twice before I put it down. Roads don't stop existing because—" She stopped. Her hand came away smudged gray. "Because the land decides to forget them."

August looked at the map again, and this time she saw what Sable didn't want to see: the blankness wasn't spreading randomly. It was spreading *toward* them. The routes nearest the Door Out were fading fastest, as if the world were closing its fingers around the one path left open, curling them shut one by one.

"How long do we have?" August asked.

"If we walk? Four days to the ash-field, maybe five." Sable rolled up the least-damaged map with hands that were almost steady. Almost. "If the roads keep lengthening while we walk them—"

"They're lengthening?"

"You haven't noticed?" Sable gave her a look stripped of all its usual irony. "The bridge at Marrowford took us half a morning to cross when I mapped it as a boy. Yesterday it took us most of the day. The distance hasn't changed, August. The *land* has. Every mile we walk toward that field, the ground quietly adds a little more ground between here and there. Like a book closing. You feel the pages pressing together?"

August did. She'd been feeling it for days without having words for it—the sense that the horizon was slightly farther away each evening than it had been that morning, that the sun was setting on villages that hadn't quite existed an hour before. She'd blamed exhaustion. Exhaustion was easier.

"One more moon," she said aloud, just to hear how small it sounded.

"One more moon," Sable agreed. "And then every oath-bound soul in Vessany is sealed in forever, and the Custodians get their perfect ledger, and the last swordspeaker becomes a footnote that no one will ever read." She slung her satchel over her shoulder. "So we walk tonight. While the moon is still counting and not yet finished."

August reached for her sword—the silent sword, Ilse's sword, which had spoken nothing but Ilse since the night the ghost first rose from its whispers—and felt, for the first time, something in the steel that wasn't Ilse. A coldness. A patience. As if the blade itself were waiting for the thirteenth turn along with everything else.

She strapped it on anyway.

It was the only piece of home she had left, and home, she was learning, could betray you and still be home.

* * *

They walked through the dead hours, and the dead hours walked back.

That was the only way August could describe it afterward. The road unspooled ahead of them in the moonlight—cobbled once, in some earlier century, now cracked and grassed-over—and with every mile it seemed to grow *longer*, not in any way she could measure but in the way of dreams, where the corridor always has another corner. Twice she looked back and saw the mill they'd left, impossibly, still visible behind them, small and pale as a milk tooth. The third time she looked back, it was closer than it should have been. The fourth time, it was gone entirely, and so was the memory of leaving it, and for three full strides she couldn't remember whether they'd ever had a roof over their heads at all.

"Sable," she said carefully. "What color was the mill door?"

"Blue." No hesitation. "Faded blue, peeling, with a horseshoe nailed above the lintel. Why?"

"Because I couldn't remember." August swallowed. "I stood under that door two nights ago and I couldn't remember it."

Sable stopped walking. In the moonlight her face was sharp and unreadable, a face made for margins, for the edges of things. "The forgetting starts with the land and works inward," she said quietly. "Roads first. Buildings. Then the details. Then—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to.

Then people.

August thought about the Custodians' ledger, about the column marked SEALED, about her own name already written there in a clerk's tidy hand. She had thought being erased meant dying. She understood now it was worse. Dying was an event; erasure was a *process*, slow and bureaucratic, the world unpicking your seams one thread at a time until even you couldn't say for certain what color your own door had been.

"Then we remember out loud," August said. "Everything. Constantly. If the land won't hold our details, we'll hold them for each other."

Sable raised an eyebrow. "You want to play the remembering game? Like children on a long march?"

"I want to play the remembering game," August said, "like two women who refuse to be paperwork."

Something moved through Sable's expression—too fast to name, too honest to be irony. "All right," she said. "The mill door is blue. Your sword is called—"

And there August stopped, because she didn't know. She had carried this blade for fifteen years, had slept with it, bled with it, hidden it inside a mattress in the Hollow wrapped in oilcloth, and she realized with a lurch that she had never once known its name—if it had ever had one—or if the name had been among the first things taken from her.

"Ilse never told me," she whispered.

"Or told you and it was taken," Sable said. "Keep walking. Tell me about the Hollow instead. What does the valley smell like in autumn?"

"Wet stone and woodsmoke. And apples—there's an orchard on the south slope, nobody plants it anymore, it just keeps growing. The apples are small and ugly and sweeter than anything you've ever tasted." August heard her own voice steadying as the details came, each one a plank laid over the void. "There's a woman, Marta, who sells honey at the crossroads market and cheats everyone cheerfully. There's a bell in the chapel tower that rings wrong, flat, and the priest insists it's fine, and everyone lets him insist because it's nicer than arguing."

"Good," Sable said. "More."

They traded the Hollow back and forth across ten dark miles, Sable offering her own inventory in return—the smell of her mother's ink shop, the sound of the harbor bells in a city whose name August watched her *not quite* say—and somewhere in that exchange August understood why Sable had demanded her true name as payment, weeks ago, at the start of all this. Not to own her. To *witness* her. Because a name spoken aloud is a nail driven into the world, and Sable had spent her whole life pulling nails out of herself, and knew exactly which ones mattered.

The sky was graying at the edges when August caught sight of her reflection in a flooded wheel-rut—and stopped so abruptly that Sable walked three paces on before noticing.

Her reflection was late.

Not absent. Not distorted. *Late.* She raised her hand and the woman in the water raised hers half a heartbeat afterward, the delay just long enough to notice, just short enough to doubt. When August leaned closer, the reflected face gazed back with an expression August wasn't making yet—and wouldn't make until a moment later, when her own mouth caught up to it, arriving at fear the way one arrives at a destination already printed on a ticket.

"August." Sable's voice, careful. "What is it?"

"My shadow's been lagging since the belltower." Her own voice sounded distant to her, a half-second behind her thoughts. "Since the first word. Now my reflection's doing it too." She looked up. "Every word I take makes me harder for the world to hold. Isn't it? That's what's happening. I'm becoming too heavy for the present tense."

Sable crouched beside the rut, studying the water. Her reflection arrived promptly, crisply, obediently. Only August's lagged.

"The oath is old magic," Sable said slowly. "Older than the erasure, maybe older than the borders. Words like that don't sit in a person lightly. They anchor you to the moment they were forged in. The more of it you carry, the further back you're pulled—and the slower the rest of you catches up." She met August's eyes. "By the thirteenth word—by the *last* word—you might arrive entirely in the past. Standing at the Door Out in body, but living a hundred years ago. Sealed in yesterday forever."

"That would suit the ledger just fine," August said bitterly. "SEALED. Filed. Done."

"So we hurry," Sable said simply, "and hope the race is close."

But as they walked on into the gray dawn, August kept glancing at puddles, at the polished boss of her sword's scabbard, at the dark of her own cupped palms—and every time, the woman looking back at her arrived a little later, and looked a little less like her, and smiled sometimes when she had not yet thought to smile.

* * *

Ilse appeared at noon, on the ridge above the last village, and August noticed immediately that the ghost cast no lagging reflection in anything, because the ghost cast no reflection at all.

"You look terrible," Ilse announced, folding her arms the way she used to in the training yard, back when August was nine and terrified and grateful. "Both of you. Have you eaten? Don't answer, I know you haven't. There's a farmstead half a mile east, the family's long scattered, the cellar might still have something in jars."

"We're not stopping," August said.

"You're stopping long enough to eat, or you'll collapse by tomorrow and I'll have to listen to Vane complain about carrying you." Ilse's ghost-smile flickered. "Twelfth turn tonight, girl. One more after that. Nervous?"

"No," August lied.

"Liar," Ilse said fondly. "Good. Fear sharpens. Now—" and she began, briskly, to outline the final approach to the ash-field: which ridgelines to trust, which streambeds had been rerouted by the land's forgetting, where the Door would stand relative to the lightning-split oak that appeared in no map because it predated maps.

August listened. It was what she had always done; Ilse spoke and August listened, the way iron listens to the magnet, and it was only afterward—walking down into the valley with Sable, the ghost pacing them silently—that the question surfaced, the one that had been growing teeth in the back of her mind since the river, since the memory that showed Ilse dying *before* the final word was ever spoken.

"Ilse," she said casually. Too casually; she felt Sable glance sideways. "How do you know the way to the Door?"

The ghost's pacing didn't falter. But it *almost* did—a hitch no larger than a half-second, the exact width of August's lagging reflection.

"I walked this country for forty years," Ilse said smoothly. "Before the purge. Before you were born. There's hardly a road in Vessany I haven't worn grooves into."

"You died in the north," August said. "At Fennmark. We fled south. You never came this far down with me."

"A friend's stories, then. A fellow swordspeaker's."

"What was her name?"

Silence. The wind moved the dead grass on the ridge, hissing like a quill on parchment.

"You ask a lot of questions lately," Ilse said at last, and her tone was still warm, still the training-yard tone, but underneath it August heard something else—the faintest strain, a voice stretched over a frame that didn't quite fit it. "Trust your teacher, girl. It's the last lesson I'll ever give you."

*Trust your teacher.* How many times had she heard that? How many times had it saved her? And how strange, now, that the lesson arrived precisely whenever the questions got close to something.

August said nothing. She kept walking, down toward the ash-field, toward the Door, toward whatever waited in the dark beyond it. Beside her, Sable's hand rested near the knife at her belt—not threateningly, just *presently*, the way a witness keeps her hands where they can be seen.

Above them the sun climbed thin and pale, and the shadows of the three of them—two women and whatever Ilse was—stretched long across the grass, pointing like compass needles at the place where the last page waited to be turned.

One more moon, August thought. One more turn, and the book shuts.

Unless she learned, in time, who was holding the pen.
