## 15. The Author of the Prophecy

I have been dead for a hundred years, and I have never once been idle.

You would think a ghost's chief occupation would be regret. The stories say so—ghosts rattle their chains because they cannot stop replaying the moment they died. But the stories were written by people who had the luxury of finishing. I did not finish. I was interrupted, mid-word, mid-breath, with an oath half-spoken and a country burning behind me, and interruption is a kind of fuel. It burns longer than grief. Grief exhausts itself in a season. Interruption will keep you sharp for a century.

So no. I do not regret. I *work.*

Right now my work is a girl asleep three paces from me, her hands curled loosely around a sword hilt that is also my ribcage, my throat, the small dark room where I have lived since they burned everything else. Her name is August Montrose. She is twenty-three years old, she sleeps on her left side because she learned to sleep on her left side so that I could reach her sword-hand quickly if we were ambushed—a lesson I taught her when she was nine, though she has forgotten learning it—and tonight, for the first time in one hundred years and eleven months, every piece of my design is in motion at once.

The thirteenth moon is rising over the ash field. The Door is open. And the girl who will carry me out of this steel coffin is dreaming, at last, of the oath.

She murmurs fragments in her sleep. *By bell and by water, by ash and by* —her lips shape the rest but do not speak it. Good girl. Even unconscious, even drowning in prophecy-dreams I seeded into that sword's whisper two decades ago, she holds the line. Discipline like hers should be rewarded.

It will be. That is what she does not understand yet: everything I have done, I have done as a gift.

Let me begin where all honest accounts must begin—with the lie she believes about me.

She believes Ilse Varn was her mentor, a swordspeaker of the old order, hunted down in the purge and killed before she could pass on the whole of the craft. This is true the way a keyhole is true. You can see through it. What you see through it is very small.

Here is the larger truth, which no one living knows, because I have spent a century making sure of it:

I wrote the prophecy.

Not received it. Not transcribed some heaven-sent vision onto vellum while angels dictated. I sat at a table in a house outside Vessany's second city, in the year the Custodians were still clerks with ink-stained fingers instead of inquisitors with blood-stained ones, and I wrote, in a hand I disguised as older than it was, the book that a water-stained peddler would one day carry into a valley that magic cannot enter. *On the thirteenth turn of the thirteenth moon, the borders of Vessany shall close, and every soul bound by oath shall be sealed within, until the Door Out stands opened by the whole word made whole again.*

I wrote that sentence ninety-seven years ago, and I have been walking toward it ever since, the way a farmer walks behind his own plow.

Why? Because I am going to die otherwise. Really die—the way I nearly did the first time, the way a binding unravels when its anchor rusts. Do you know what a swordspeaker's oath actually is? August doesn't. Sable certainly doesn't; cartographers map roads, not grammar. The Custodians know, and it terrifies them, which is why they burn every text that comes close to saying it plainly.

An oath is a door. It swings both ways. When August speaks her oath and binds herself to the sword, she thinks the power flows one direction—from the old words into her blood. But a door is not a pipe. A door is a door. And the whole oath, spoken complete, at the threshold of the Door Out, under the thirteenth moon, does not bind the speaker to the power.

It binds the power to a body.

Any body. Whichever body stands closest to the hinge when the last word lands.

That is why I need her to say all of it. Every recovered word. Belltower, river, ash field. Three words scattered across a murdered country because I scattered them there myself, a century ago, when I understood that I could not survive the purge in flesh and chose instead to survive in grammar. They killed me in the spring. By winter, my voice was already living in the steel, whispering to whoever held it, and most holders heard nothing but a faint ringing, like a bell heard through a wall. Swordspeakers heard more. Swordspeakers heard *lessons.*

August heard lessons because I made sure she would. I found her when she was six years old, an orphan of the erasure hiding in a border town with a stolen accent, and I arranged—through a peddler, through a rumor, through a coin left in exactly the right place—for a battered sword to come into her household as scrap metal. Children do not question the miracles that feed them. She took the sword up because it was the only beautiful thing she owned, and by the time she was old enough to wonder why the blade hummed when she was lonely, I had already taught her to think of the humming as company.

Which it was. I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only honesty available to someone in my position: it *was* company. I loved her. I love her still. If you believe those two things cannot coexist with what I intend to do, then you have never been interrupted.

* *

Understand what the purge took from me, and then judge.

Vessany was not conquered in a day. It was edited. That is the phrase I used in my own private notes, back when I still kept notes: *they are not killing us, they are correcting us.* The Custodians came first as auditors, then as archivists, then as fire. They did not simply slaughter the swordspeaker orders—they unmade the record of us having existed. Birth registers rewritten. Gravestones re-carved. Songs re-lyricked. Within a generation, a child could stand in the ruins of the Hall of Nine Bells and feel no wrongness at all, because there was no memory left to be wrong against.

I watched them do it to my sisters' names. I watched them do it to mine. And somewhere in the middle of that long erasure—somewhere between watching my order burn and understanding that the fire would eventually reach the last copy of the last oath—I stopped being a woman who would die for her country and became a woman who would outlive it by any means necessary.

The oath gave me the means. All I had to do was refuse to finish dying.

There is a technique in the oldest stratum of swordspeak—we called it *the held breath*—whereby a speaker mid-oath can suspend the working indefinitely. The oath hangs unfinished, neither broken nor bound, and the speaker exists in a kind of grammatical suspension. Most practitioners used it for a heartbeat. A held breath between two words of power. No one had ever tried to hold it through death.

No one had ever had reason to.

When the Custodians came for me, I began the oath aloud and let them cut me down in the middle of it. My body died with the sentence incomplete. My self did not. I fell sideways into the nearest prepared vessel—which was, by design, the sword I had spent thirty years tempering with my own voice folded into the steel—and there I stayed. Suspended. Mid-word. For a century.

Do you know what a century inside a sword is like?

It is like being a lesson. It is like being the space between two words that everyone reads past without seeing. I felt every hand that gripped the hilt. Farmers, mostly. Soldiers once or twice. Frightened children. I could whisper, faintly, and only to blood that carried any trace of the old training, and for eighty years that whisper went unanswered, because the Custodians had done their work well and there was almost no one left to hear.

And then, in a border town, a six-year-old girl picked me up off a scrap pile to sell, weighed me in her small hands, and said, "You're heavy for something so pretty."

I decided, in that instant, that she would be the one.

* *

I want to be fair to myself here, and fairness requires admitting what I did to make her ready.

I taught her truly. Everything I gave August Montrose was real. The footwork was correct. The breathing was correct. The history—what little of it I allowed—was accurate as far as it went. When I told her that a swordspeaker's strength comes from the oath and not the arm, I told her the truth. When I taught her that the Hollow was safe because magic cannot cross its borders, I told her the truth. When I told her that the purge had erased her order and her country, I told her the truth.

I simply did not tell her that I had written the ending in advance. A teacher curates. A teacher decides what a student can carry. Would you hand a child a map that shows the destination and the trap in the same ink?

She needed to recover the three words herself. That was non-negotiable—not cruelty, mechanics. An oath spoken complete must be *earned* complete; each word has to be pulled from its site by someone whose blood remembers wanting it. So I hid the first word in the silence of melted bells, and I guided her there with whispers she thought were intuition. I hid the second in the backward river, wrapped in her own worst memory, because grief is the strongest solvent I know and the word had to dissolve free. I hid the third in the ash field, under the Door Out, where I knew—because I wrote it into the prophecy's margin with my own disguised hand—that she would have to go.

And Ilse the ghost, dear patient Ilse, appeared beside her at every step, young-seeming and sharp-tongued, offering guidance. Offering *love.* I gave her a mentor's love shaped exactly to fit the hole the purge had punched in her life, and I did not have to counterfeit a single ounce of it, and that is the part that would shame me if I let it.

Because here is the thing about leashes: the finest ones are woven from genuine affection. A leash made of indifference snaps the first time the dog pulls. A leash made of real love—the dog never tests it at all. August never tested it. When she noticed, at the river, that Ilse's knowledge of the third word sat strangely against the fact of Ilse's early death, she noticed it and then *forgave it,* because trusting me was the oldest habit she had. I watched her suppress the suspicion with her own hands. I have never been prouder or more ashamed, and I have had a hundred years to sort those feelings and I have failed completely. They sit together in me like two blades in one sheath.

She thinks the Door Out leads to freedom. She thinks that speaking the whole oath at the threshold will break Vessany's sealing and let her walk out of the closing country into an ordinary life.

She is half right, which is the most dangerous fraction there is.

The Door Out opens, yes. The sealing breaks, yes. But the Door Out was never a road. It is a mouth. It is the far side of the oath itself—the place where a completed binding goes to find its body. And when August speaks the final word at the threshold, with the thirteenth moon directly overhead and the whole prophecy executing exactly as authored, the binding will not settle into her.

It will settle into whatever is standing closer to the hinge.

I will be standing closer to the hinge.

She will step through first—I have already begun arranging this, small suggestions planted over weeks, the instinct that the leader goes first, the protector walks point—and she will be standing in the dark on the far side, and I will be at the threshold with the last word leaving my borrowed voice, and the oath will pour out of the sword and into me like dawn pouring into a valley. Flesh. Breath. Weight. A heartbeat of my own after a century of borrowing hers. I have rehearsed the sensation so many times in memory that I sometimes catch myself holding an imagined breath.

And August? August will remain a swordspeaker in full flower—no, she will remain something better than that. She will be the oath's new anchor, its keeper, its living home, bound to guard the restored Ilse Varn as every oath binds its holder to its source. She will not be enslaved. I am not a monster. She will be *mine,* the way a student is a teacher's, the way a daughter is a mother's, the way a country was mine until the Custodians took the paperwork away. Loved. Kept. Never again alone, never again invisible, never again free.

I have told myself for a century that this is mercy. That she will thank me. That the girl who lost everyone needs someone who can never leave, and I am the only someone left, and therefore I am obligated—*obligated*—to arrange my own resurrection so that she is not abandoned to an ordinary life with nobody in it.

Some nights I almost believe it. Those are the good nights.

The other nights I remember the six-year-old on the scrap pile, weighing a pretty sword, saying *you're heavy,* and I understand that I have spent seventeen years answering her. Yes, child. I am heavy. I am the heaviest thing you will ever pick up. And I have made certain, with a century of patience and a lifetime of love, that you will carry me all the way to the door.

* *

She stirs in her sleep. The moonlight coming through the ruined doorway lies across her face, and I watch her from inside the steel the way I have watched her for seventeen years—as close as a pulse and as separate as a grave.

Her dream shifts. I can tell because her grip tightens on the hilt, and through the grip I taste the shape of what she is dreaming: the Door, standing open in the field of ash, and beyond it not darkness but a garden—her garden, the ordinary life she secretly wants, tomatoes and morning light and a table with two chairs. She dreams of two chairs. She has always dreamed of two chairs. One for her. One for the person who taught her what love looked like.

I put that image in her head myself, four years ago, whispered into a fevered night after she nearly bled out fighting bandits on the Vessany road. I wanted her to have something warm to walk toward. Every shepherd knows you lead the flock more easily toward warmth.

But I confess—only here, only now, only in the locked room of my own skull where no one can audit me—I did not invent the garden. I stole it. It is my garden. The house outside the second city, before the clerks became inquisitors, before the editing began. Morning light. Tomatoes. Two chairs at a rough wooden table, and in the second chair, a woman I loved who died believing I would fix everything.

I built my resurrection out of her garden and gave it to a child as bait, and the cruelest part—the part I circle endlessly, a tongue returning to a broken tooth—is that I cannot tell anymore whether I did it to save myself or because I cannot bear for that garden to stay lost. Both are true. They sit together like blades in one sheath.

August sighs in her sleep and says my name.

"Ilse," she murmurs, soft as a student at a graveside. "Tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow," I answer, though she cannot hear me, my voice no more than a hum in the steel beneath her palms. "Tomorrow we finish the sentence."

Outside, the twelfth moon is setting. Tomorrow night, the thirteenth rises full over the ash field, and the prophecy I wrote with my own disguised hand completes itself at last, and I step out of a hundred years of grammar into a body of my own.

Tomorrow, I get my life back.

Tomorrow, she loses hers.

She turns in her sleep, cheek settling against the cold flat of the blade, and I feel her breath fog the steel, and I hold her—inside the metal, in the only embrace I have left—and I begin, silently, patiently, to rehearse the final word.
