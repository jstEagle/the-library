## 21. Visitors Who Remember

The first stranger came on a morning of thin gold light, when the frost still held the garden in its silver teeth and I was on my knees in the dirt arguing with a row of onions.

I heard her before I saw her—boots on the road, unhurried but not easy either, the walk of someone who had been checking over her shoulder for so long it had become a way of breathing. I stayed low among the onion rows and listened. The Hollow had been quiet for two seasons. Quiet enough that I had almost stopped listening at all.

She stopped at the edge of my fence, where the gate hung open the way it always hung now. We had never bothered with latches since the borders broke. Nothing needed keeping out. Nothing needed keeping in.

"You're her," the woman said.

I straightened slowly, dirt on my knees, an onion in one hand with its pale roots trailing like something drowned. She was perhaps fifty, gray shot through dark hair, a travel-stained coat that had once been fine—Vessani cut, the high collar, the silver thread gone dull at the seams. Her eyes did the thing I remembered from before, the thing every survivor's eyes did: they moved past me first, scanning the valley walls, counting exits, before they ever settled on my face.

"I'm someone," I said.

"The woman who broke the oath." She said it like a prayer she wasn't sure was permitted anymore. "They tell it different ways. In some of them you're ten feet tall."

"Most stories get me wrong," I said. "Usually they make me shorter."

Something cracked in her face then—not grief exactly, more the place where grief had been packed down for so long it had turned to stone, and now a hairline fracture let a little warmth through. "My grandmother," she said. "Her name was Maren Ostell. She was a swordspeaker's daughter. When they came to rewrite the parish rolls, she hid the family book inside a loaf of bread and carried it through the checkpoint in a market basket." Her hand went to her breast pocket, an old gesture, protective of something long since moved elsewhere. "I have the book. It has names in it. For sixty years I thought I was the only person alive who knew those names meant anything."

I set down the onion. Wiped my hands on my apron, which is the kind of thing you do when your hands need something ordinary to do.

"Come in," I said. "I'll put water on. You can tell me all of them."

* * *

That was how it began, though I didn't know yet that it had begun. That's the way of most beginnings—they arrive looking like a single traveler asking for bread.

Maren Ostell's granddaughter—I never learned whether Ostell was her name too or a borrowed one; people who survive erasure are careful about names, and I never pressed—stayed three days. She read me every name in her grandmother's book, forty-one names, and I wrote each one down as she spoke it, in my own hand, in the ledger I kept now beside the door where the sword used to hang. When she finished, she looked at the page and wept without sound, the way people cry when they've had decades of practice at doing it quietly.

"They're back somewhere now," she said. "On paper. In ink. They exist again."

"They existed the whole time," I said. "It's the world that's catching up."

She left on the fourth morning, walking east toward the coast, carrying copies of the names to scatter along her way like seed. And I stood at my gate watching her go, and I felt something settle into my chest that I didn't have a word for yet. Later I would call it weight. Later still I'd understand it was ballast—the thing that keeps a ship upright in weather.

* * *

They came in twos after that. Then fives. Then, by the turning of the tenth moon, in carts.

Word travels strangely through a country that spent a century being unmade. It doesn't move along roads so much as seep along old affections—a cousin here, a childhood friend there, a letter written in a cipher only the erased ever learned. Someone told someone that the valley where magic couldn't follow had opened its mouth. Someone told someone that the last swordspeaker had broken the hundred-year oath and lived, and that the sealing magic had come apart like wet paper, and that in a green hollow between mountains there was a woman keeping a ledger of names and signing her own beneath them.

August Montrose. Signed. Visible. The thought still made my skin prickle some mornings, like wearing clothes that fit after years of wearing nothing at all.

Sable arrived before the carts did, of course—Sable always arrived before whatever she was arriving for, because Sable believed preparation was a moral virtue and lateness a form of lying. She took one look at the stream of strangers coming down the mountain pass, took one look at my small house with its one hearth, and made a sound like a kettle deciding to boil.

"You're going to feed all of them?" she said.

"I'm going to try."

"With what? Your legendary stores of—" she gestured at my garden, which even in autumn was generous, "—onions?"

"People survived worse with less."

"That's not an argument, that's an indictment." But she was already unrolling a fresh sheet of vellum across my kitchen table, already sharpening her pen, because this was how Sable Vane loved things: she mapped them. Within a week she had drawn the valley with new structures sketched in—longhouse here, well there, terraced gardens on the south slope where the sun pooled longest. Within a month, half of it was built. The survivors built fast. People who have watched their homes edited out of existence develop strong opinions about putting roofs up quickly.

I cooked. I wrote. I listened.

That was the work, I came to see. Not the sword-work—the other kind, the kind no order ever trained me for. Every arrival brought a story, and every story had been carried alone too long, and most of them just needed somewhere to be set down. An old man named Corvin who had been a bell-founder's apprentice in Vessany's capital and had spent fifty years unable to explain why the sound of bells made him flinch. A pair of sisters who had inherited their mother's memory of a street that no map admitted to, and who could both, unprompted, draw it perfectly—the crooked lantern post, the blue door, the gutter where the cats slept. A boy of nineteen whose grandfather had died reciting fragments of a sword-oath he didn't understand, syllables passed down like contraband, and who asked me if the words meant anything and I said yes and watched his whole face change.

At night I added them to the ledger. Names, places, the small facts that erasure had starved: trades, birthdays, the names of streets, the names of dead children. Some nights the ink blurred and I told myself it was the damp.

Some nights it wasn't.

* * *

The child came on a day of hard blue sky, near the end of the eleventh moon's turn, with the thirteenth approaching the way winter approaches—not suddenly, but unmistakably.

She arrived in the company of an aunt, or a woman presented as an aunt, one of those arrangements the erased make when bloodlines get tangled in survival. The girl was maybe nine. Small and wiry, with a scowl that seemed borrowed from someone much larger, and she walked through my gate like she owned the deed to it.

She looked at the house. She looked at Sable's maps drying on the line. She looked at me kneeling in the garden, and then—and this is the part I will keep until I die—she looked at the sword.

It hung by the door now, silent, retired. I'd oiled it out of habit rather than need. It hadn't whispered since the Door collapsed, since Ilse scattered into honest light, since the oath broke and took my power with it like a tide going out and never coming back. The blade was just steel now. Good steel. Dead steel.

The girl walked straight to it and stood before it the way people stand before altars.

"That's a real one," she said.

"It is."

"My gran talked about them." She didn't look at me. She was studying the blade with the fierce concentration of the young, as if she could interrogate it by staring. "Before they took her words away. She said there was a loud way of talking to steel. She said her mother knew it, and her grandmother, all the way back, and then it stopped." The scowl deepened. "Why did it stop?"

"Because people made it stop," I said. "Bad people, a long time ago."

"And you know it. The loud way."

"I knew it," I said carefully. "It's gone from me now."

That got her to turn. She looked at me with the flat, merciless skepticism only children and cats can produce. "Gone how? Things don't go gone. My gran said everything that was true is still true somewhere. It just needs somebody to say it right."

I opened my mouth to explain—to give her the gentle, adult version, the version about sacrifice and broken grammar and doors that close forever—and then I stopped. Because standing there in the dirt with onion soil under my nails, I heard myself about to lie to a child for her own good, and I recognized the technique. I'd been raised on it. Ilse had called it protection. Ilse had called it many things.

Ilse had also taught me that a swordspeaker's first duty was truth spoken plainly, even when it cut.

"Here's what happened," I said, and sat back on my heels so we were eye to eye. "There was an oath. A very old one, bound into blood, and it was used against us—it was a leash disguised as a gift. To break it, I had to speak it wrong on purpose, break its grammar, put my own name where the ritual demanded another's. It worked. Everyone the oath held went free." I spread my hands. "And the speaking-power went with it. The sword doesn't talk to me anymore. Maybe it doesn't talk to anyone. Maybe it's finished."

The girl considered this with the gravity of a judge.

"But you remember it," she said. "How it worked. What the words were."

"Yes."

"And how to hold it." She mimicked a grip, awkward, thumb wrong, but the shape of the intent was there, unmistakable, and something in my chest turned over like soil. "And how to stand. And listen. All the parts around the words."

"All the parts around the words," I agreed.

"Then it's not gone." She said it with terrible simplicity, the logic of a guillotine. "It's just waiting for somebody new. That's how my gran said it works with true things. They wait."

Behind her, at the fence, her aunt had gone very still, watching me with an expression I knew intimately—the expression of someone hoping desperately for a gift and bracing not to receive it.

I looked at the sword. Dead steel, silent steel, hanging in the slant light of late autumn. I looked at the girl's hands, making their clumsy hungry grip around air.

"I can't teach you the loud way," I said, and her face began to fall, and I kept talking before it could finish falling. "Not because it's lost. Because it isn't mine to hand over whole. What I can do is teach you everything around it—the stance, the listening, the history, the truth-telling, the names of everyone who carried it before you. And then, when you're older, if the steel wants to talk to you, it'll find you fluent."

The scowl returned, but changed—tempered now, banked like a fire put to coals for the night.

"When do we start?" she said.

"Tomorrow. Dawn. Bring your aunt, and bring your patience, because the first lesson is standing still for an hour, and nobody likes it."

"I'll like it."

"You won't."

"I'll like it anyway," she said, which is, I have since decided, the entire secret of teaching, delivered by a nine-year-old in a doorway.

Her name was Tess. Of course it was. Of course the child who walked into my life demanding the future was named Tess, short for Contessa, which her gran had chosen because it sounded like a name no clerk would bother to erase.

* * *

That evening Sable found me at the practice yard behind the longhouse—a flat stretch of ground I'd cleared without quite admitting why, raked smooth, with wooden staves leaning against the fence. Tess had gone home to bed. The stars were out in their cold multitudes. Sable leaned on the fence and watched me run through forms I no longer had any business running through, slow, empty-handed, muscle memory talking to itself in the dark.

"You're going to do it," she said. "You're going to rebuild it."

"I'm going to teach children how to stand still," I said. "What grows out of that isn't up to me."

"That's a beautiful dodge. Very mentor-shaped of you." She came through the gate and picked up a stave, weighing it. "You know what I've noticed, mapping all these arrivals? They don't come for safety. The roads are safe everywhere now; the Custodians are ash in the wind, their ledger burned blank. They come here. To this valley. Do you know why?"

"Because it's defensible," I said. "Old habits."

"No." Sable set the point of the stave in the dirt, planted like a standard. "Because you're here, and you're writing it down, and you sign your name. They're not fleeing to a place, August. They're fleeing toward a witness. There's a difference, and the difference is going to be the whole rest of your life."

I lowered my hands. The stave felt heavy in the cold.

"The thirteenth moon finishes in six days," I said.

"I know."

"The night the prophecy was dated. The night the borders were supposed to seal forever. Except we broke it, so instead it's just—" I searched for the word. "A date. An anniversary of a door that never closed."

"Is that what you think it'll be?" Sable asked gently, which is how she asked her sharpest questions.

I looked north, past the valley rim, to where the field of ash lay under the stars—the place where the Door had stood, where Ilse had scattered into honest, wordless light. The land there had begun, impossibly, to green. Grass through cinders. Life insisting.

But six nights from now, on the last night of the thirteenth moon, the old book in my house—the peddler's water-stained book, the prophecy Ilse wrote with a century of patience—would reach its final inscribed date. Prophecies were paperwork, Sable had learned that in the Custodians' ledger. Paperwork gets executed. Or paperwork gets amended.

And lately, on quiet nights, I had begun to notice something I hadn't mentioned to anyone.

Out past the valley's edge, far off across the dark country, where no bell should be able to ring—

something was ringing.

Once each night. Always once. Closer than the night before.

Sable followed my gaze, and I saw her hear it too, faint as a pulse in a wrist.

"Six days," she said again, and this time neither of us pretended it was only a date.
