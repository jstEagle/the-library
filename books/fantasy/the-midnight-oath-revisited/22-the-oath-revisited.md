## 22. The Oath, Revisited

The thirteenth moon rose the color of a fresh scar, and August Montrose climbed the eastern ridge of the Hollow to meet it.

She had not hurried. That was the strangest part—the whole year had been a lesson in not hurrying. For twenty years she had lived like a held breath, and now, walking the sheep path with her boots scuffing loose stone and her coat collar turned up against the autumn wind, she breathed the way ordinary people breathed: without counting.

The valley lay below her in the last blue light. Smoke from supper fires stood straight up in the still air. Somewhere down there, Tam Weller's dog was barking at nothing, the way he always did when the moon came up full. The garden she'd planted where the peddler's stall used to stand was a dark rectangle of turned earth and sleeping winter greens, and beside it hung the sword—she could just make out its shape on the peg outside her door, silent as a horseshoe, silent as it had been since the night an oath broke and took everything else with it.

She had expected to grieve that silence longer than she did.

What she had not expected was to find, under the grief, something like gratitude—as if the power had been a language she'd spoken fluently but never chosen, and losing it had left her, at last, with her own voice.

The ridge path narrowed. She followed it to the place she'd chosen weeks ago: a shelf of granite jutting over the valley's edge, close enough to the boundary that she could feel it—or rather, feel that she couldn't feel it. The Hollow's old refusal was gone now, dissolved along with the sealing magic, along with the ledger, along with Ilse. What remained was simply land. Hills on hills, going out into the dark like any other country's dark.

She sat down on the stone, folded her hands around a cup of tea gone lukewarm, and waited for the moon to clear the far peaks.

* * *

It had taken the survivors most of a year to arrive, trickling in along roads Sable drew and redrew as memory returned to them. They came alone or in families, hollow-eyed, carrying whatever they'd managed to keep through the erasure: a christening spoon, a lock of hair whose owner they couldn't name, a song they only knew the chorus of. The first ones had stared at August as if she were a story they'd been told as children and had stopped believing in.

"You broke it," a one-armed ferryman from the river country had said, gripping her hand too hard. "A hundred years of us, sealed up like flies in amber, and you broke it."

"I mispronounced it," August had said, and he'd laughed until he wept.

They didn't all stay. Some passed through the valley like water through a channel, bound for cities that had forgotten them and would have to be reminded. But some stayed. There were eleven new roofs on the south slope now. There was a school that met in Sable's map room three mornings a week, where children learned letters alongside the shapes of countries that no one would ever again be permitted to unmake. There was a bell.

That had been the ferryman's idea, and Sable had drawn its foundry into being with the particular ferocity she reserved for things that should never have been possible to destroy. It wasn't a swordspeaker relic. It wasn't magic at all, as far as anyone could tell—it was bronze and rope and stubbornness, cast from the melted-down ledgers' iron bindings, raised in a tower of fieldstone on the western rise. They called it the Unringed Bell for a year, because no one dared ring it; ringing felt like claiming something none of them had earned yet.

Tonight, August thought, watching the moon lift free of the mountains, might be the night someone finally did.

But first she had a vow to say. Her own this time. She'd been drafting it in her head since midsummer the way she'd once drafted sword-forms—testing each phrase for balance, cutting what showed off, keeping only what would hold weight.

* * *

The moon cleared the last peak and poured itself across the valley floor, silvering the smoke, silvering the garden, silvering the silent sword on its peg. From up here it looked like a country at peace, which was, after a century, finally accurate—and it looked small, which was also accurate, and August let herself hold both truths at once without flinching. Ilse had taught her many things. The hardest one had come too late: that love could be a leash, and that the hand holding the leash could tremble with real tenderness even while it pulled.

She still dreamed of Ilse sometimes. Not the ghost, and not the thing behind the ghost's face, but the woman as she must have been a hundred years ago—young and furious and afraid, writing prophecy by candlelight because she could see no other way to survive. In the dreams, August didn't forgive her. She just watched her work, the way you watch a storm from inside a strong house, and understood that some people break the world trying to keep one person safe in it.

In the dreams, the sword whispered. In the waking world, it hung silent, and August had decided that this, too, was a kind of mercy. Words spoken into steel lasted forever. Words spoken into paper could be argued with, corrected, revised. She preferred paper now. Paper let you be wrong on purpose.

She finished the tea. Set the cup down on the granite with a small ceramic click that sounded enormous in the quiet. Stood.

Below her, at the valley's edge where the boundary had once refused all magic entry, a few lanterns had gathered—word traveled fast on nights like this, and half the valley knew what tonight was. She could pick out Sable's tall silhouette, the cartographer's roll of blank vellum slung over her shoulder as always, ready for whatever the morning demanded. And smaller, wriggling, holding somebody's hand: the girl. Merrin, six years old, born after the breaking, who had asked in the flat fearless way children ask whether swordspeakers could teach her "the loud way of talking to steel."

August had said maybe. Tonight she would say something better than maybe.

She planted her feet the way she'd been taught—not the oath-stance, not anymore, just a stance, a body on a hill deciding to mean what it said. The wind came up the valley and moved through her hair, and she spoke into it, quietly, in a voice pitched not to carry to the lanterns but to carry exactly as far as it needed to:

"I am August Montrose. I was born in Vessany, which existed, and I remember it."

The words went out into the dark and did not glow, did not bind, did not seize anything anywhere. That was the whole point. She kept going.

"I vow to witness. What I see, I will see truly, and what I see truly, I will not pretend away. I vow to record—in my own hand, signed with my own name, so that no one after me has to learn themselves from a stranger's book. I vow to stay reachable. I will live where people can find me. I will answer when I'm called. I will not seal myself anywhere, not for safety, not for grief, not for love."

Her throat tightened on that last word, and she let it tighten. Love had nearly eaten her alive, worn as a dead woman's face. But refusing love outright would have been letting Ilse win too—would have meant the leash still worked, just pulled in the opposite direction. Reachable, then. Open-handed. Terrified and doing it anyway.

"I will not be invisible," August said, "even when it would be easier. This I choose freely, on the last night of the thirteenth moon, in my own words, binding no one but me."

She bowed her head. The wind moved. Nothing answered, and the nothing was perfect—a vow with no teeth in it except hers, no hunger in it except the ordinary human kind.

Down among the lanterns, someone began to applaud, and then everyone did, and August laughed despite herself, wiping her eyes with her sleeve, feeling the heat of being seen wash over her like weather she'd finally stopped hiding from.

And then—far off, across the western rise, from the tower they'd built out of broken bindings—

a bell rang.

Once.

Not the melted bells of the Belltower of the Unringed. Not any bell from the erased country. This was their own bell, their own bronze, rung by hands unknown—perhaps the ferryman, perhaps Merrin's mother, perhaps Sable herself, who had a flair for timing that bordered on the prophetic and would deny it to her grave. The note rolled down over the valley, over the gardens and the new roofs and the sleeping schoolroom, over the silent sword on its peg, and up the ridge to where August stood, and it did not command anything. It just said: *here. We are here.*

August stood very still while the sound faded, and found that she was crying and smiling at once, and that both felt like arriving somewhere rather than leaving.

Then a small shape detached itself from the lantern-light below and started up the sheep path toward her—small, determined, breathing hard, a girl of six climbing a mountain at night because she had decided the moment was right and children are wise that way.

"Merrin," August said, crouching to catch her before she tripped. "It's past your bedtime."

"The bell woke everybody up," Merrin said breathlessly, entirely unrepentant. Then she looked up at August with the moon full on her face, and asked the question she'd been saving all year: "Is tonight the night? Will you teach me?"

August looked past her, down at the valley—her valley, no longer a hiding place, just a home—and at the lanterns, and at Sable already unrolling fresh vellum by lantern-light, beginning the long patient work of mapping a country that got to name itself. She looked at the silent sword waiting on its peg, and understood suddenly that silence was not an ending. Silence was a page.

"Tomorrow," August said. "First light. Bring the wooden practice blade—I'll show you how to stand."

Merrin whooped loud enough to startle the dog two valleys over, grabbed August's hand, and towed her down the path toward the light, toward the bell's fading sweetness, toward the ordinary, miraculous, witnessed day waiting on the other side of the moon.

Behind them, the thirteenth moon set over the Hollow for the last time, and the valley, unsealed and unafraid, slept soundly beneath it.

**THE END**
