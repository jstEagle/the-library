## 1. The Keeper's Ledger

The first frost of the season had come in the night, silvering the thornbushes along the gatehouse wall and turning the puddles in the courtyard to thin panes of glass that cracked like knucklebones under Bram Lockridge's boots. She stood a moment at the door with her breath steaming around her, listening to the mountain the way her father had taught her to listen before she could read: not for sound, but for the absence of it. Thornfell was never truly silent. There was always the wind working through the pass above, the groan of ice forming in the cistern, and beneath it all — so deep that on most days she could convince herself she imagined it — a slow pulse from the stone itself, like a heart buried in the roots of the world.

This morning the pulse felt steady. That was good. That was, in fact, the whole of her life distilled into a single sentence: *the pulse is steady, and I am here to see that it stays so.*

Bram pulled her cloak tight and crossed the yard to the stable, where the mule, Barley, regarded her with the flat indifference of an animal who had long ago concluded that his keeper was slightly mad. She did not blame him for the conclusion. Twenty years alone beneath a sealed mountain pass, keeping a ledger no one would ever read, guarding a door no one was permitted to open — madness was the charitable interpretation. The uncharitable one, whispered in the valley taverns when they thought she couldn't hear, was that the Lockridges were cursed, touched by whatever slept behind the Hollow Gate, and that Bram was simply the last of a line too stubborn to die out.

She saddled Barley anyway. It was audit day.

* * *

The Hollow Gate was not one door but three, though only the outermost could be seen without a lantern. From the valley road it appeared as nothing at all — a seam in the mountainside, hidden behind a curtain of ivy and scree, marked by two pillars of black granite that travelers' tales called the Teeth. Between them ran a passage forty feet high, cut so precisely by hands three centuries dead that a knife blade could not slip between the blocks. At its end stood the First Door: iron-banded oak, older than any kingdom now claiming the land on either side of it, carved over every inch with script that no living tongue spoke.

Behind the First Door lay the Warding Hall, and behind that the Second Door, and behind *that* — sealed since before her grandfather's grandfather drew breath — the Third, which the old records called only the Throat. No Lockridge in living memory had seen what lay beyond it. That was rather the point.

Bram's morning ritual had not changed since she was nine years old, standing on a stool to reach the lock because her father's hands were already failing. She walked the passage. She checked the wards — seven iron seals set into the walls, each one cold to the touch, each one tested with the back of her fingers the way you test a child's forehead for fever. She read the script aloud, or rather she made the sounds that the script had taught her, phonemes passed down keeper to keeper like a stolen inheritance, words whose meanings had been lost even before the Sundering War ended. Her father had called them the Watchwords. *You don't need to know what they mean,* he'd told her, when she'd asked. *You need to know that they're true.*

She said them now, all seven, her voice echoing down the empty hall until the stones seemed to murmur them back at her, half a heartbeat late, as if the mountain were learning her language the way she had once learned its.

Then she lit the lamp in the record room, took down her grandfather's ledger, and began the audit.

* * *

The ledger was a thing of beauty and terror, as all honest ledgers are. Aldous Lockridge — no relation, thank the saints, to the knight whose name she would come to curse; her grandfather had been Aldous the Elder, and the coincidence of names was one more small wrongness in a family history full of them — had kept it in a hand so fine it looked printed. Every seal inspected, every ward renewed, every crack in the mortar charted and dated across fifty years of winters. And in the back pages, in a section headed *Keys and Custody*, the tally marks.

There should have been four.

Bram knew this the way she knew her own heartbeat, because she had counted those marks every autumn for twenty years, and every autumn there had been four, and every autumn she had told herself the same comforting story: one key for the Crown's warden in the south, one for the temple archive at Coldford, one held by the Lockridges themselves, hanging on the hook beside her father's old coat where it had hung since before she was born. And the fourth — the fourth was the mystery her grandfather had never explained, the mark entered in a year she'd been too young to ask about, annotated only with a symbol she'd never found in any cipher book: a circle bisected by a line, like an eye closed by a scar.

Four marks. Four keys. One of them lost, or hidden, or waiting, and her grandfather dead these thirty years with the answer folded into his silence.

She turned to the page this morning expecting four.

There were five.

The new mark sat below the others in ink that was not her grandfather's iron-gall brown but something darker, almost black, and the penmanship was wrong — not finer, not coarser, just *other*, the way a stranger's handwriting in your own journal is other. The date beside it was three days past. Three days ago Bram had been in this room herself, oiling the hinges of the record chest, and the page had borne four marks. She would have sworn it on her father's grave. She *did*, silently, swear it on her father's grave.

She sat down slowly on the stool and did not touch the page for a long moment. The lamp flame leaned in a draft she couldn't locate. Somewhere above, wind worried at the scree slopes, and beneath everything, steady as ever, the mountain's buried pulse went on.

Then she looked closer, and the dread that had been gathering in her chest condensed into something colder and more specific. Below the fifth mark, in that stranger's hand, someone had written a single word in the old script — the script on the Gate itself, the script she could pronounce but not read. She copied it onto a scrap of paper, letter by careful letter, the way her father had taught her to copy inscriptions: *shape first, meaning never.* She compared it against her rubbing-book, the collection of tracings she'd made of every inscription in the passage, hunting for a match.

She found it on the lintel above the Second Door. A word that appeared, by its position and its repetitions elsewhere, to be a name. Or a title. Or perhaps — the thought arrived unwelcome and stayed — a warning.

* * *

She finished the audit because routine is a rope and you hold it tighter precisely when the ground starts moving. She walked the full circuit of the passage twice. She tested all seven wards again, pressed her palm flat against the First Door and held it there until the iron's chill seeped through her skin into the bone, listening with her whole body for anything — a tremor, a warmth, a whisper of air where there should be none.

Nothing. The Gate was shut. The Gate, as far as any instrument of sense she possessed could tell her, had never been anything but shut.

But a ledger does not lie about arithmetic, and five is not four, and ink does not dry itself onto a page in a locked room in a sealed passage at the roof of the world unless a hand carried it there. Which meant a hand had been here. Three days ago. Or the means of a hand — a key, a token, some authority her grandfather had recorded and never explained — had passed out of the dark and into someone's keeping, and that someone had come to the Gate in the night, entered where only she could enter, written their mark in the book of her family's duty, and gone again without troubling the dust on the floor.

Or without troubling much of it. She found, on her second circuit, a single print in the grit near the Warding Hall threshold. Small. Narrow. The size of a boot worn by someone either young or slight, and gone before the draft could feather its edges away entirely.

Bram stood over that print for a long time, and the competent part of her mind — the part that had survived twenty winters here, that knew every smuggler track through the high passes better than the smugglers themselves, that could field-dress a wound, mend a roof, and hold off three armed men with a quarterstaff and the geography of her own doorstep — that part began, methodically, to make lists.

Who could enter? Holders of a key. There were, per the ledger, four keys besides her own, one of them unaccounted for since before her birth. Who would enter and leave a mark? Someone who wanted the Gate's custodianship acknowledged — claimed, contested, or announced. Someone who knew the ledger mattered. Someone who knew the *rituals* mattered, because the word they'd written was a word from the Door-lintel, a word no thief or scholar would choose at random.

And who, in all the valleys north and south, even knew the old script well enough to write it?

No one. That had been the answer her whole life. The gate-builders were dust; their tongue survived only in stone and in the garbled Watchwords keepers mouthed without understanding. The war that broke the kingdoms had broken the language too, deliberately, the way you break a sword.

So the answer was no one, and yet the mark was there, and the mark was fresh, and the print by the threshold belonged to someone small enough to be a child.

Bram banked the lamp, locked the record room, and stood a while in the dark of the Warding Hall with her hand resting on the Second Door's iron bands, feeling for the pulse. Steady. Steady. But her own heart was not steady, and she noticed, with the detached clarity that solitude grants, that for the first time in twenty years the two rhythms felt like they might fall out of step — hers quickening toward fear, the mountain's keeping its ancient, indifferent time.

* * *

She rode down to the valley that afternoon, because there was one more thing a competent keeper does when the ledger turns strange: she checks the roads.

The smuggler roads were hers in the way that wild things are yours — not owned, but known, mapped in her memory ridge by ridge. Wren Halloway's caravans had run the high passes for a decade now, threading between the kingdom checkpoints through cuts so narrow the mules had to be unloaded and led by hand. Bram could have closed those roads. A word to the garrison at Coldford, a few fallen trees, a watched ford, and Wren Halloway's enterprise would starve by midwinter. She never had. Her father hadn't either, nor his mother before him. The arrangement was older than any of them and never spoken of aloud: the smugglers kept the valleys fed in the hard winters, kept the garrisons honest by giving them something to pretend to chase, and in return the Lockridges kept their lanterns burning on the ridge lines and their eyes turned diplomatically upward at the sky.

It was, Bram understood now, standing in the taproom of the Drowned Stag while sleet ticked against the shutters, a kind of oath too. Everything up here was. The mountains did not recognize the laws of kingdoms; they recognized only what was promised, and to whom, and whether it was kept.

Wren was not at the Stag — her people said she was wintering at the Coldford end of the passes this year, provisioning early, buying grain in quantities that made the miller's eyebrows climb. Famine prices, someone muttered. Smart woman. Or frightened woman. With smugglers the two wore the same face.

Bram asked her questions carefully, wrapped in the small talk that was the local currency. Strangers on the high trails? No, nothing but Wren's own runners. Anyone asking after the Gate? A shrug; nobody asked after the Gate; the Gate was a story mothers used to frighten children off the scree. Anyone new in the valleys at all, anyone at all?

The innkeeper's wife paused over her tankards. *Now that you say it,* she said, *there was a tinker came through last week, heading north. Quiet fellow. Had a little girl with him.*

*A daughter?*

*Didn't talk, that one. The man neither, much. Paid in old coin.* She said it the way valley folk said such things — lightly, superstitiously, already turning back to her work — but the words landed in Bram like stones dropped down a well, falling and falling toward something deep.

Old coin. A silent child. North, toward Thornfell, three days ago.

Three days ago, when a hand had written a fifth mark in her grandfather's ledger in the dead tongue of the gate-builders.

Bram finished her cider, paid with exact coin, and did not let her face do anything at all. Outside, the sleet had thickened toward snow, the first real snow of the long winter, the kind that closed the high passes and turned Thornfell into an island of stone. By tomorrow the roads would be white silence. Whatever had come to her Gate was coming ahead of the weather, or had already arrived, or was somewhere on the mountain right now, small and silent and carrying a key that should not exist.

She rode home in the falling dark, and the snow came down around her like a curtain being drawn across the world, and behind her the valley lights went out one by one, and ahead the black mass of Thornfell rose against the last grey of the sky with its hidden door and its five marks and its steady, patient, buried heart.

At the gatehouse she lit the lamps, fed Barley, barred the door. Then she took down her grandfather's ledger one more time, opened it to the page with the five tallies, and set it on the table beneath the lamp where she could see it from her chair.

She would watch it tonight. She would watch it every night, if she had to, the way you watch a wound for signs of fever.

Somewhere out in the storm, something was walking toward her door. She could not have said how she knew. But the mountain's pulse had changed — not faster, not slower, just *attentive*, the way a sleeping dog's breathing changes when someone comes up the path — and Bram Lockridge had spent twenty years learning to trust the mountain over her own eyes.

Near midnight, over the wind, she heard a knock.

Small. Faint. Almost polite. The kind of knock a child makes when they have knocked on many doors and been turned away from most of them.

Bram sat very still in the lamplight, her hand closing around the poker, her heart hammering out of rhythm with the stone, and listened to the snow fall on the roof of the only home she had ever known, while outside, in the dark, something waited to be let in.
