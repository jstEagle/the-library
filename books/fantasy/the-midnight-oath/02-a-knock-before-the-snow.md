## 2. A Knock Before the Snow

Bram did not open the door at once.

Twenty years of keeping the Hollow Gate had taught her that the mountains rewarded patience and punished haste, and whatever stood on her threshold in the first snow of winter was either a miracle or a mistake. Both kinds, in her experience, were best met with a poker in one hand and a lamp in the other.

The knock came again. Small. Faint. The sound of a fist no larger than a sparrow's body striking oak three times, then waiting — with the terrible courtesy of someone who has learned that knocking too hard gets you shouted at, and not knocking at all gets you left to die.

Bram rose from her chair by the fire. Her joints announced themselves as they always did after a day spent climbing the switchback path to the Gate's face, checking the seal-stones, chalking the survey marks she would compare against last month's chalk tomorrow, and every month until she died or the mountain did. She took down the poker. She took up the storm lantern from its hook by the door and lit it from the hearth with a spill of pine kindling, and the flame steadied, and she made herself breathe once, twice, before she lifted the bar.

The wind hit her like a wall of knives. Snow swirled into the entryway in a white gasp, melting instantly on the warm flagstones, and beyond it — huddled against the doorframe as though she had been trying to press herself into the wood for warmth — stood a child.

A girl, Bram saw. Eight years old, perhaps nine, though it was hard to tell because the child was so small, so thin, wrapped in a cloak that might once have been fine wool and was now stiff with ice. Frost rimed her lashes. Her lips were the color of slate. Her hands, bare, were curled against her chest as if she had been holding something there for a very long time and could no longer feel what it was.

She looked up at Bram, and her eyes were dark and enormous and utterly calm, which frightened Bram more than weeping would have. Children who had been turned away from many doors learned a particular stillness. This child had it in her bones.

Then the girl opened her mouth, and spoke.

Bram had guarded the Hollow Gate for twenty years. She had grown up beneath it, running through the gatehouse corridors while her grandfather recited the ledger entries aloud to teach her numbers: *tally of flour, tally of salt, tally of wax, tally of iron.* She knew every word of common speech the valley spoke and most of the words the high passes spoke, and she knew — better than anyone alive, she had believed — the language cut into the Gate itself. The old tongue of the gate-builders. The dead speech. She had spent whole winters copying those inscriptions into the margins of her grandfather's ledger, sounding out the angular syllables, learning them the way you learn the shape of clouds: by heart, without meaning.

The girl was speaking that language. Fluently. As easily as Bram breathed. The syllables came out of her small frozen mouth rounded and certain, each one landing in the air between them like stones dropped into a well — *and Bram knew them.* Not all of them, not cleanly, but she knew them. *Vesh-kara. Ilum-veth.* Words she had traced a hundred times with her fingertip on the great bronze doors, wondering what they meant, and here they were, alive, coming from a living throat, and the world tilted sideways in the lamplight.

"Stop," Bram said uselessly, in common. "I can't— slow down—"

The girl stopped. She looked at Bram with an expression that was not quite frustration and not quite despair — the look of someone who has spoken to many people in the only tongue she has and watched understanding die in their faces, one face after another, all the way across the mountains.

And then her gaze fell on the ledger, lying open on the table just inside the door where Bram had left it that afternoon, and something changed in the child's face. Recognition. Hope. A desperate, drowning hope.

She stepped forward — Bram tensed, poker half-raised — and instead of entering she held out her hands.

They were shaking now, whether from cold or effort Bram couldn't say. The fingers uncurled slowly, stiffly, the way frost-stiffened things move, and revealed what she had been clutching against her chest.

An iron token.

It was the size of a coin but heavier-looking, old workmanship, the metal gone black with age except where wear had polished it bright. It had been broken clean through the middle — not snapped, Bram thought dully, *broken*, deliberately, in the old way, the way you broke a thing when you wanted its power ended or its bond severed — and the two halves had been bound back together with fine copper wire, wound tight by careful, loving, desperate hands.

Stamped into the iron, spanning both halves across the mended break, was a crest.

Bram knew that crest the way she knew her own heartbeat. A mountain split by a keyway. The Lockridge mark. Her family's mark, pressed into the gatehouse ledgers for four generations, branded into the beams overhead, hammered into the hinge-plates of the very door this child stood shivering outside of.

But it wasn't right. That was the thing that made the cold walk up Bram's spine and settle between her shoulders. The crest was almost right — the same mountain, the same keyway — but the proportions were older, the lines cruder, the stamp struck by a different hand in a different century. It was the Lockridge crest the way a river is the shape of its own dry bed. It was the crest *before* her crest. The original. The thing her family's mark had been copied from.

Bram had seen that original exactly once, in her life. She had been eleven years old, and her grandfather had opened the iron strongbox beneath the floorboards — the box he never opened, the box whose key he wore on a cord around his neck and slept with under his pillow — and shown her, for less time than it takes to draw breath, a broken iron token bound with copper wire, before locking it away again and saying, in a voice she had never heard him use before or since:

*Some things are kept so that they are never used.*

Now the token was in a dying child's hands on her doorstep, and her grandfather had been ten years in the churchyard at Thornfell, and there was no one left to ask.

* * *

Bram got the girl inside the way you get a half-frozen creature inside: quickly, and without asking permission. She stripped off the ruined cloak, wrapped the child in the good blanket from the third-floor chest — the one that smelled of cedar because it was only ever taken out for guests who never came — and sat her in the chair closest to the hearth with a bowl of barley broth that had been simmering since noon.

The girl ate the way she had knocked: carefully, gratefully, as though food were a thing you thanked even when it burned your mouth. Bram watched her over the rim of her own bowl and tried to make the pieces fit together, and they would not fit, and the not-fitting rang in her head like a cracked bell.

A child alone on the pass. In the first snow. On the exact night the long winter began — and Bram had known it would be a long winter; she had read it in the way the ravens flew low and the way the ache in her hip sang and the way the Gate itself, when she'd laid her palm against the seal-stone that morning, had seemed almost warm.

That was another thing she would not think about yet. The stone had been warm.

The girl finished the broth and looked at Bram and spoke again — slower this time, deliberately, pointing at herself with one small chapped finger.

"*Ilum-neth,*" she said, and touched her own chest. "*Ilum-neth.*"

Her name. Or the word for *I*. Or both. In the old tongue they might have been the same thing.

"Ilumeneth," Bram repeated, clumsily, and the girl's whole face changed — lit from within, briefly, like a window at dusk — before the wariness settled back over it, because the child was clearly accustomed to hope being a brief and expensive commodity.

Then the girl pointed at the ledger.

Bram frowned. "What?"

The girl slid off the chair, crossed to the table on unsteady legs, and put her finger on the open page — on the column of tallies, on the margin where Bram had copied, years ago, in her younger and more obsessive hand, the inscription from the Gate's western face. The syllables she had sounded out through a dozen winters. The dead words she knew by shape and not by meaning.

The girl read them.

Not stumbled through them. *Read* them, her finger moving under the angular script, her voice low and sure, and as she read, Bram felt the hair rise on her arms, because the child was not pronouncing the syllables the way Bram pronounced them — flatly, decoratively, like a charm. She was speaking them the way you speak a sentence you understand. There were pauses in the right places. Weight on the right words. And underneath the sounds there was something else, some rhythm in the stones of the gatehouse themselves, a faint answering hum in the floor that Bram told herself she imagined, and then told herself she didn't, and then stopped telling herself anything at all.

When the girl finished, she looked up at Bram and said one more word. A short one. Pointing at the inscription, then at herself, then out — toward the dark, toward the pass, toward the direction a person would come from if they had crossed the high valley in the snow.

Bram didn't know the word. But she knew, with the sick certainty of a keeper who has spent her life reading signs, what the sentence meant.

*I am from the place these words come from. I am what these words belong to.*

* * *

Later — much later, when the girl had finally gone down into sleep in the bed beneath the eaves, curled so tightly into herself that she seemed to be trying to take up no space at all, even in sleep, even in safety — Bram sat at her table with the broken token in front of her and the ledger open beside it, and did not touch either one.

The lamp burned low. Outside, the snow came down thick and silent, sealing the pass, sealing the valley, sealing the gatehouse into its long white isolation. By morning the road down to Thornfell would be gone. By tomorrow night the high trail east would be gone. Whatever had sent this child up the mountain had done it knowing the snow would close behind her like a trap, or had not known, or had not cared, or had been burning behind her worse than any weather.

Bram turned the token over with one finger. The wire binding caught the lamplight. Two halves of one whole, mended by hand. Someone had loved this object enough to repair it and carried it far enough to wear the polish smooth, and then — Bram looked at the girl sleeping upstairs, and made herself finish the thought — someone had pressed it into a child's hands and pointed her up a frozen mountain toward a crest on a door.

Her grandfather's voice, twenty years dead, said in her memory: *Some things are kept so that they are never used.* And beneath it, unbidden, colder: the memory of the strongbox, and how her grandfather had looked standing over it — not solemn, as she had always remembered, but afraid.

She should go down to Thornfell when the weather broke. Send word. Tell the magistrates there was a child on the pass speaking the builders' tongue, carrying a broken oath-token with her family's stolen — her family's —

With her family's crest.

Bram sat very still. The fire ticked. The wind worried the eaves. And she understood, in the way you understand a thing gradually and then all at once, that she could not send word down to Thornfell, because the law of Thornfell had a name for children born speaking the dead tongue of the Gate, and the name was written in the charter her grandfather had helped draft, and the name was *abomination*, and the penalty was fire — a small clean fire, a lawful fire, in the yard behind the courthouse, with the magistrates watching and the ledger signed afterward.

She had copied that clause herself, once, into the fair copy of the charter. She had been proud of her neat hand.

Upstairs, faintly, through the ceiling, the child murmured in her sleep — two syllables of the old tongue, soft, questioning, like someone asking a mother a question and expecting an answer.

The floor hummed. Very slightly. The way it had hummed when she read aloud.

Bram looked down at the broken token, at the crest that was older than her crest, at the wire-mended break running through the middle of it like a scar, and felt wonder curdle slowly, quietly, into fear.

Because there was a line in the ledger — her grandfather's hand, the last page of his last audit, thirty-one years ago, which she had read a thousand times and never understood — that read simply:

*Tally of keys: one.*

And Bram had spent her whole life believing there was only ever the one key, the keeper's key, hanging on its hook by the inner door.

Tonight, for the first time, she found herself wondering what her grandfather had counted, and what he had left out — and what, exactly, had come knocking at her door wearing her family's oldest face.
