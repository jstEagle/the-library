## 4. Riders in Grey

The men below are grey like the sky before snow.

She knows this because she has learned to name colors by what they are near. Red is fire. Brown is the door that would not open anymore. Grey is the color of the last morning, and now it is the color of the riders, and she does not think this is an accident. Nothing is an accident. Mother said that once, or maybe she said *nothing is owed to you*, and those sound almost the same in the language of stones, which is why the old words are dangerous and beautiful at once — they can mean two true things and you cannot always choose which one you have said.

The girl lies on her belly on the loft floor of the gatehouse, where a gap between two boards lets her see down into the valley road. She has been lying there since before light. Her knees ache. She does not move them. Moving makes noise, and noise makes her visible, and being visible is the thing that killed everyone whose faces she can still almost see.

*Almost see.* That is a strange phrase for a child to own. She turns it over while she watches. She can almost see her mother's hands. She can exactly remember how the iron token bit cold into her palms. The hands themselves are gone, worn away by remembering, like a coin rubbed smooth.

Below, four horses. Four men. Their cloaks move even when the wind doesn't.

* * *

There is a word for *watch without being seen* and it is one of the first words her mother gave her, back when words were gifts instead of weapons. It has three sounds, and the middle sound is like water going over a stone lip, and when you say it right, your whole body understands that it should become smaller. Smaller than small. Small as a held breath.

She says the word inside herself now, over and over, a little wheel turning.

The tall man dismounts at the miller's fence. The miller comes out with his hands open, the way people do when they want to be seen as harmless, and she has noticed that grown-ups show their empty hands a great deal, as if hands could lie. Hands can lie. Hers had lied all through the forest, holding nothing, pretending they were not shaking.

The tall man holds up something flat. He shows it to the miller, and even from here, even across all that white distance, the girl knows its shape. A circle with a line through it, and under the line three marks like teeth.

It is her face. Not her face — but the shape of someone who looks like her, drawn badly, the way strangers draw children: too-big head, no history in the eyes. Under the drawing, words she cannot read because they are written in the common letters of the valley, the clumsy square ones, not the flowing script that lives in stone.

But she does not need to read them. She has heard the word they use for her twice already on the road, once through a tavern wall and once from a man who did not know she was under his own wagon.

*The little speaker.*

They say it carefully, the way you say the names of diseases.

* * *

Fire first. Always fire first when the remembering comes.

That is how memory works in her, she has decided — it is not orderly like the ledgers she saw once in her father's study, lines marching straight as soldiers. Memory is fire. It starts wherever it wants. It eats whatever is nearest. You do not get to choose what burns.

So: fire. Then smoke, which tasted the way iron smells. Then her mother's voice doing something terrible — staying level. Staying level was the terrible thing. When mothers scream, children know to run. But her mother did not scream. Her mother pressed the token into her hands so hard the edges cut, and said one word against her ear, fast and soft, and the word meant *run*, except it also meant *live*, except it also meant — and this part took her years of nights to understand — *I am sorry*.

One word. Three truths. That is why the old tongue was made for stone and not for people. People break under it.

Then the window. Then the cold. Then the sound behind her that she has never once let herself finish hearing.

* * *

Below, the miller shakes his head. The tall man keeps showing him the paper, patient as winter. Patience frightens her more than shouting. Shouting means a man is losing; patience means he believes he will win.

Another rider walks his horse along the fence line, looking down at the ground. Looking at tracks. She remembers the tracker from the pass, the one who crouched at the stream edge and lifted a wet leaf and looked at the underside of it for a long time, and after that they had gone the wrong way around the mountain, the long way, the way that ate five days she did not have.

Small as a held breath. The little wheel turns.

The miller's wife comes out and stands beside her husband, and she is holding their smallest boy by the collar of his coat, holding him close against her leg, and the girl understands this completely, the way she understands few things about grown-ups. The woman is not holding the boy back. She is holding him *in*. Making him small. Teaching him the word without saying it.

The tall man finally rolls up the paper. He says something to the miller that makes the miller go stiff, then nod, then bow a little, which is worse than anything, because bows are what people give to power, and the man in grey has not earned a bow, he has only taken the shape of one.

Then all four heads turn at once toward the mountain. Toward Thornfell. Toward her.

She stops breathing entirely, which is the final size of the watching-word, the size past small, the size of stone itself.

They look for a long moment. Then the tall man mounts, and the four of them ride on north along the frozen road, unhurried, patient, certain, and the grey of their cloaks swallows them into the grey of the day until there is only the miller standing at his fence with his family gathered around him like a small fire they are trying to shield with their bodies.

The girl breathes again. Air tastes like iron. Everything tastes like iron lately. Maybe, she thinks, that is what fear is made of. Or maybe it is the token, which she has carried against her skin for so long that its metal has entered her blood and made her partly a key. Mother, she thinks, I am becoming a thing that opens doors. Is that what you wanted? Was that the third meaning? There were only supposed to be three.

* * *

In the afternoon the keeper feeds her bread and honey and does not ask questions, which she loves him for, though she does not know yet that this is love, she only knows it as warmth in the chest that makes her eyes leak, which is inconvenient and embarrassing and happens more every day.

He is a big man, the keeper, with hands like the roots of trees, and he moves slowly, as if the world might startle. She has decided he is safe. This decision took her four days, which for her is a very short time, and she made it because of three proofs.

First: he did not touch her when she arrived half-frozen at his door, even though touching would have been faster and easier. He wrapped a blanket around her shoulders without letting his hands land anywhere. Only people who understand brokenness are careful like that.

Second: he reads the ledger at night by candlelight, moving his lips, checking things twice, and once she watched him find a mistake — one tally mark too many, she heard his breath catch — and he sat with the mistake for a long time instead of erasing it, because he believed numbers told the truth and deserved to be listened to. People who listen to numbers will listen to a mute girl. This seemed like sound reasoning.

Third: he talks to the Gate. She heard him through the wall, low and formal, apologizing to it for the weather. You do not apologize to a wall unless the wall is alive, and if the wall is alive, and the man tends it, then the man is a kind of shepherd, and she is — she has begun to suspect — a kind of lamb. Or a kind of key. Lambs and keys both get carried to places they did not choose.

She eats the bread. Honey sticks her fingers together. For a moment the world is only honey and warmth, and then the loft window goes grey with cloud-shadow, and she remembers the riders, and the honey turns to iron on her tongue.

* * *

At night she dreams in the old way, the forge-priest way, which her mother said was a gift and which the girl has come to understand is simply a door left unlatched.

She dreams of the Gate from the inside.

This is the part she has never told anyone, not the keeper, not anyone, because telling it would make it real and because some doors, once opened, cannot be shut again — she knows this better than most children, she knows this in her bones and in her blood and in the cut-scars on her palms.

Inside, the Gate is not a gate. It is a throat. It goes down farther than mountains should go, and the walls are carved with ten thousand names, and the names are not decoration. The names are *holding*. Each one is a hand. Each one belongs to someone who gave their heartbeat to the stone so the stone would stay shut, and the girl can feel all of them sleeping, layered like rings in a tree, generation under generation, keepers folded into keepers, and beneath all of them, oldest and deepest, a hollow place shaped exactly like a person, waiting.

Waiting for what?

Waiting, the dream-voice says, which sounds like her mother and like the mountain and like her own mouth all at once, for someone with the right blood to fill it.

Or someone willing.

Those are not the same thing. The dream is very clear about that. Blood fills the hollow whether you agree or not. Willingness fills it singing.

She wakes with the token clenched in her fist so hard the edges have printed their pattern into her skin — circle, line, three teeth — and she uncurls her fingers one at a time and looks at the mark and thinks: my mother wore this same print into her palm. And her mother's mother. All the way down to the first forger, whoever she was, whose name is somewhere on those walls, whose hand is one of the hands.

We are a family of hinges, the girl thinks. Hinges don't get to decide which side of the door they're on.

Then she feels ashamed of the thought, because it is a bitter thought and her mother did not die so her daughter could grow bitter in a warm loft with honey on her breath. Her mother died so her daughter could grow *free*. Those are different words. In the old tongue they are almost the same word, and the girl is tired, so tired, of almost-the-same.

* * *

On the fifth morning the riders come back.

Not four this time. Nine. And they do not stop at the miller's fence. They ride up the switchback road toward the gatehouse itself, slow, deliberate, their breath steaming like small ghosts escaping them one at a time, and the girl watches from the loft gap with her heart doing something painful and enormous in her chest.

The tall man rides first. She has learned his face now, the way prey learns faces — the pale eyes, the scar through one eyebrow, the mouth that never does anything. As he passes below her window he lifts his head, just slightly, and sniffs the air, actually sniffs it, like a hound, and for one horrible instant his gaze crosses the gap in the boards where she lies.

She is stone. She is the Gate. She is small as a held breath and smaller, she is the space inside the held breath, she is —

He looks away. The column rides on. Hooves ring on the frozen road, nine iron hearts counting themselves out.

The girl stays in the loft long after they have passed, listening to the silence they leave behind, which is not a real silence but the kind that leans, the kind that waits. Below her, faintly, she hears the keeper moving through the gatehouse — the heavy tread, the clink of the kettle, the ordinary music of a careful man beginning a careful morning.

He does not know yet. He is making tea while wolves climb his hill.

The girl presses the broken token to her lips. Its cold bites her mouth and she welcomes it, because cold is honest, and because the token is the last piece of her mother that exists outside her own skull, and because — she understands this suddenly, wholly, the way the forge-priests must have understood things — everything is about to change. The riders are not passing through. They are arriving. The question they carry on their paper has found its answer, and the answer is lying on her belly above a stove-warm room in the house of the last Lockridge, wearing his family's crest on a stranger's iron.

She whispers the watching-word one last time, not to hide, but to steady herself. Three sounds. Water over a stone lip.

Then she gets up, knees aching, heart hammering its small stubborn drum, and goes down the ladder to warn him — the keeper, the shepherd, the man who apologizes to walls — because the grey riders are coming up his road, and they are asking after a little speaker,

and she has just remembered, with a lurch like the mountain shuddering, that she spoke aloud in her sleep last night, loud enough that the dream-words echoed off the stone walls of the gatehouse,

and stone, she knows better than anyone alive, always answers.
