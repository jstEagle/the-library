## 8. Ambush at Coldford Bridge

The river under Coldford Bridge had frozen so hard it sang.

Bram heard it first as they came down through the pines — a long, low note like a struck bell, the ice expanding in the deep cold, groaning against its own weight. She had grown up with that sound. Every winter of her twenty years on Thornfell had included some version of it: the mountain clearing its throat, the water beneath remembering it was still water. But tonight, riding in the dark among Wren Halloway's wagons, the singing sounded less like a song and more like a warning.

"Quiet," Wren said ahead of her, and her voice carried down the column without being raised — a smuggler's trick, pitched to travel. "Lamps out. We walk them across."

They stopped at the tree line and the caravan went dark around Bram, one lantern after another pinched out until only the snow-glow remained, that strange blue half-light that a heavy sky gives back to a white world. The bridge was a black seam across the river's white expanse, old stone, older than any kingdom that claimed it now, its parapets furred with frost. On the far bank the road climbed toward the southern passes, and beyond those passes lay the lowlands, and beyond the lowlands, safety — or something she was learning not to call by comfortable names.

Bram sat her horse and looked at that bridge and felt the wrongness settle over her like the cold itself.

She could not have said what it was, exactly. The snow on the far approach lay smooth, but smoothed — no wind-drifts, no animal tracks stitching across it. The pine boughs above the far road held their snow in perfect, unbroken shelves, which meant nothing had shaken them, which meant nothing had passed beneath them recently, which meant someone wanted it to look exactly this way. Twenty years alone with a ledger and a gate had taught her what untouched things looked like. This was not untouched. This was arranged.

"Wren," she said quietly.

"I see it." The smuggler queen stood at the head of the column, small and grey in her fur-lined cloak, chewing something slowly, her eyes narrowed at the crossing. "Been seeing it for an hour. Question is whether they're behind us or ahead of us."

"They'll be both," Bram said. "That's how Vane would do it. He doesn't chase. He waits where you must go."

Wren spat into the snow. "You've known the man a day."

"I've known his kind my whole life." Her whole life was perhaps too generous a phrase for what her life had been — ledgers and oil-lamps and the long patient silence of stone — but she had read enough of her grandfather's correspondence, before she burned most of it, to know the Order of the Rekindled Oath the way you know a disease by its symptoms. Courtesy first. Certainty always. And when certainty met obstacle, steel.

The girl stirred against Bram's back.

She had ridden bundled in Bram's cloak since the caves, three days ago, when Wren's people had folded them into the middle of the caravan like contraband — which, Bram thought grimly, was precisely what they were. The child weighed almost nothing. When she slept she made a small sound in her throat, not quite a word, and Bram had learned to hate that sound because it reminded her of the Gate itself: something speaking a language almost hers, almost understandable, and never once saying anything she wanted to hear.

Now the girl was awake. Bram felt her go rigid, felt the small hands close on the front of her coat.

*Hessa,* the girl breathed. *Hessa vael.*

Grey men. Bram didn't know how she knew it — she didn't, not properly, not the way the child knew words that made stones listen — but the meaning arrived in her anyway, the way meanings from the Gate inscriptions had begun arriving these past weeks, unbidden, unwelcome. Grey men. Riders in grey.

"Yes," Bram murmured. "I know."

"You know what?" Wren had turned her horse alongside. In the blue light her face was all angles and suspicion, forty years of mountain winters carved into it. "Because if the little speaker has opinions, I'd dearly love to hear them before I commit my livelihood to a frozen bridge."

"She says there are grey riders ahead."

"There are grey riders everywhere, love. It's winter. Half the realm wears grey because dye costs coin." But Wren's hand had gone to her belt knife as she said it, and her eyes never left the bridge.

Below them, the river sang its one long note.

* * *

They crossed in the end because there was nowhere else to cross. That was the whole truth of Coldford Bridge and Wren said so aloud, flatly, the way she said everything: "It's the only span between here and Ravenmere that will hold a wagon. So we hold our breath and we pay whatever toll is waiting on the other side, and we count ourselves lucky if the toll is coin."

Bram walked her horse. Everyone walked. The wagons rolled slow and silent, wheels wrapped in sacking, and the column strung itself out thin across the ice — twelve wagons, thirty-odd souls, Wren's crew in their mismatched greys and browns, and somewhere near the middle, a girl who did not need a key because she was the key.

Halfway across, the far bank lit with torches.

Not many. Six, seven, set along the road in a line, and silhouetted figures standing motionless beside them, cloaked, unhurried. They had been there all along, Bram understood, watching the column pick its way onto the ice, waiting until retreat would cost more than advance. Behind her, up the slope they had just descended, horns sounded — two notes, clean and cold, answered by two more further off. Both banks. As she'd said. As anyone who knew Ser Aldous Vane would have known.

"Stand your wagons," Wren called back down the column, and her voice was still level, but Bram could hear the arithmetic running underneath it, fast and merciless. "Nobody draws. Nobody runs. We're honest traders caught on a bad road, and honest traders talk."

"Honest traders," one of Bram's neighbors muttered, "don't carry false-bottomed crates of northern furs past the tariff stations," but he reined in all the same.

They came down onto the ice to meet the torches. Bram counted the riders as they approached — fourteen, maybe sixteen, moving with the terrible economy of men who drilled together every day of their lives, and at their center a figure she recognized even before the torchlight found his face. Ser Aldous Vane rode the way he spoke: upright, courteous, absolutely certain. His grey cloak bore no sigil. His sword stayed sheathed. He drew rein ten feet from Wren's lead wagon and inclined his head as though they had met at a market fair rather than a trap.

"Mistress Halloway." Pleasant. Almost warm. "And Mistress Lockridge. How good. I had hoped to spare you the discomfort of a chase."

"You've spared me nothing," Bram said. "There's a girl in this caravan and she goes free, or every knight you brought dies on this ice, and I promise you the ice will help me."

It was the sort of thing a heroine in a ballad said. She heard it leave her own mouth and winced inwardly, because Vane only smiled — a small, genuine, sorrowful smile — and Wren made a sound beside her that might have been a laugh or might have been a prayer.

"Mistress Lockridge," Vane said gently, "you are a keeper of accounts. You have spent your life counting things that cannot lie: barrels, candles, marks in ledgers. So let me give you numbers. Fourteen knights on this bridge. Thirty-two more within a horn's call. Two hundred at Coldford garrison by morning. Against that, one aging smuggler whose crew will scatter the moment their queen stops paying them, and one woman who has never — forgive me, but the ledger does not lie — drawn a blade in anger in her entire life." He spread his empty hands. "Give me the child. She is a ward of the Crown, stolen property, and my charter authorizes her recovery. You lose nothing but a burden. The realm loses nothing but a danger. Everyone on this ice goes home to their supper."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the law takes her from you, and takes your freedom besides, and I will grieve it sincerely." He said it without heat, without cruelty, and that was the worst of him, Bram thought — that he had built a fortress of pure procedure around whatever fire burned in him, and no arrow she owned could find the man inside it.

The girl's grip tightened on her coat. Through the wool Bram felt the child trembling, and felt something else too — a vibration, faint and rhythmic, rising up through the ice beneath them, up through the horse, into her own bones. The river's long note had changed pitch. Lowered. Deepened. As if something far below had turned over in its sleep.

The girl heard it. Bram saw her tilt her head, the way she tilted it at the Gate, listening.

*Don't,* Bram thought fiercely, absurdly, as if the child could hear thoughts. *Whatever word you're reaching for — don't. Not here. Not with all of them watching.*

Vane's lieutenant moved forward with a coil of rope, and that was the moment the arithmetic broke.

It broke from the south bank first — a shout, then a scream, then a wagon-load of Wren's crew coming off the treeline in a wedge, torches streaming, driving two riderless horses straight through the knights' left flank. A feint, a diversion, something Wren had seeded hours ago and never told Bram about; later Bram would understand that smugglers planned ambushes the way rivers planned floods, patiently and everywhere at once. But understanding came later. At the time there was only noise, and torchlight wheeling wildly across the ice, and Vane's careful geometry dissolving into shouting knots of men and horses.

"Burn it!" Wren roared — not at the bridge, at the *river*, at the far bank, and Bram saw then what the smuggler had already seen: the ice below the bridge was thick, thick as a castle wall, but the ice at the banks, where current ran fast and shallow, was young ice, days old, a finger's thickness. Two of Wren's people were already out on it with sledges, swinging at the shelf-edge where river met land, and the ice was cracking in long white veins like a window breaking in slow motion.

"No," someone screamed. "No, the wagons—"

"Leave the wagons! Leave everything! *Off the bridge!*"

What followed, Bram's memory kept forever in fragments, the way bad dreams keep things. The crack of the southern shelf going. Horses screaming. A knight dragging another from the water by his cloak, both of them vanishing into steam-white spray. Wren's crew abandoning cargo they had killed to protect, chests and crates sliding on the tilting deck-planks of the world. And through all of it, the girl pressed against Bram's back making no sound at all, utterly silent, utterly awake, her small face turned toward the breaking ice with an expression Bram could not bear to look at — recognition, almost. As if she had seen a river die before.

Bram herself fought. She would remember that too, and be ashamed of it forever: that when a grey-cloaked knight cut through the panic toward the middle of the column — toward *her*, toward the bundle on her saddle — she drew the keeper's short sword that had hung on her wall for twenty years without ever leaving it, and swung it like a woman chopping wood, badly, wide, telegraphed, and the knight took it on his vambrace almost lazily and hit her forearm with his gauntleted fist hard enough to numb her to the shoulder. The sword spun away onto the ice. He raised his blade for the second stroke, the finishing one, unhurried —

— and the ice took his footing from under him. He staggered, cursed, caught himself, and in that heartbeat Wren's knife-hand appeared from nowhere and opened the man's cheek to the bone, and then they were both dragging Bram's horse forward through the smoke and the shouting while behind them the southern span of the Coldford Bridge cracked, groaned, and dropped a wagon-sized bite of itself into the black singing water.

* * *

They came off the north end of the bridge at a dead gallop, or as near to a gallop as a lathered horse carrying two could manage, and plunged up the north road with the burning bridge-light strobing orange against the snow. Behind them, Vane's remaining knights pulled their own from the freezing shallows. No pursuit. Not yet. The river had bought them the night, at a price Wren would spend the next week cursing.

Two miles on, at a fork where the road split toward Ravenmere, a single rider stepped out of the trees ahead of them and did not draw.

Bram knew him. He was the knight who had nearly taken her head — she knew him by the bandage already bound across his cheek, dark with fresh blood, and by the way he held his sword point-down like a man leaning on a shovel rather than a weapon. Older than the others. Tired in the way veterans are tired. He looked at her for a long moment, and then at the crest stitched on her saddle-roll — the interlocked gate-and-key of House Lockridge, which she had carried without thinking, the way she carried her own name — and something moved through his face that she would spend many sleepless nights afterward trying to name. Grief, perhaps. Or recognition. Or the particular shame of a man meeting a ghost.

"Keeper," he said. His voice was rough, ordinary, human. "I served at Thornfell. Before you. Under your grandfather."

The words landed strangely, like coins dropped down a well.

"He kept the Gate shut," the knight said. "All his life, they say. All his life, they *say*." He let that sit between them in the cold. Then he straightened in the saddle. "Ser Aldous will come again, and next time not on a bridge of his choosing. Go to the old chapel at Ashvale. Burned these fifteen years, but the crypt below still keeps what the Order wants forgotten. Your grandfather's letters are there. Read them, Keeper, and then decide whose side of this war you've actually been fighting." He turned his horse away, then paused. "I should have taken your head back there. Understand that. The only reason you're breathing is that crest — and that it won't keep you alive twice."

He rode off into the trees, and the dark closed behind him like water.

Wren came up alongside, leading a spare horse, her face streaked with soot and other people's blood. She looked at the place where the knight had been, then at Bram, then at the sleeping-or-feigning-sleeping bundle at Bram's back.

"Ashvale," she said flatly. "Of course. Straight toward the garrison road. Naturally." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Tell me why, Keeper. Tell me why every road out of these mountains bends toward the one thing hunting us."

Bram looked down at her numb arm, at the sword lying somewhere behind her on the ice of a broken bridge, at the crest on her saddle-roll that had spared her life for reasons she did not yet understand and already feared.

"Because my grandfather," she said slowly, "was not the man I buried."

Ahead, the road climbed toward Ashvale, and the snow began again, soft and indifferent, filling the tracks of everyone who had ever passed that way — the guilty and the hunted alike — as though none of it had ever happened at all.
