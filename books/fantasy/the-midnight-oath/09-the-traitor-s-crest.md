## 9. The Traitor's Crest

The knight's words followed Bram down the mountain like a second shadow.

*Ask your chapel what your grandfather buried there.*

She had not slept. She had sat by the gatehouse fire with the girl asleep against her shoulder and turned the sentence over until it wore a groove in her mind, the way water wears stone — slowly, patiently, without malice, until there was no bottom left to stand on. The knight at Coldford Bridge had held her life in his gauntleted fist for three full heartbeats. He had seen the Lockridge crest on her sword-belt, and instead of taking it he had leaned close, snow melting on his visor, and spoken as though reciting something learned by rote.

Bram had told herself on the ride home that it was a trick. A wedge driven between keeper and Gate, the oldest tactic in any siege. Vane wanted her doubting; doubt was cheaper than ladders.

But she had not told the girl. That was the tell, and she knew it. If she truly believed the words were nothing, she would have laughed them off over supper, made a joke of grey knights and their riddles. Instead she had banked the fire early, tucked the blanket to the child's chin, and lain awake listening to the wind worry at the shutters like an old dog at a bone.

In the morning she saddled the mule and rode down out of Thornfell alone.

* * *

The chapel of Saint Aldhelm-of-the-Snows stood two hours south of the pass, in a fold of the valley where the pines grew thick enough to swallow sound. It had been a wayshrine once, before the Sundering War — so the story went — a place where pilgrims walking between the kingdoms could warm themselves and hear mass in either tongue. Then the war came, and after the war the Gate was sealed, and after the sealing the chapel burned. Everyone knew that much. Lightning, said some. Reivers, said others. The fire was older than Bram's grandfather, older than her father; it was simply part of the landscape, like the scree fields and the frozen waterfall, one more ruin the mountain kept.

What no one explained was why it had never been rebuilt. There were forty souls in the valley who walked past it every market day. There was timber to spare, and masons, and a priest in Coldford who complained each winter about the length of his parish rounds. But the blackened walls stood, and the roofless nave filled with snow and emptied with spring, and no one lifted a hammer. Bram had ridden past it a hundred times and never wondered why.

Today she wondered.

She tied the mule among the pines and walked the last stretch on foot, boots crunching through crusted drifts. The chapel rose out of the white like the ribs of some drowned leviathan — granite walls furred with frost, window arches empty and staring, the great beam of the roof ridge lying across the nave where it had fallen a century ago and been left to rot into black lace. Ivy had died on the east wall in a long brown curtain. Everything smelled of cold ash and stone, the particular smell of places where fire had happened and been forgotten.

The knight had said *the archive*. Bram had spent half the night puzzling over the word. Chapels kept vestries, reliquaries, maybe a chest of parish records if they were grand. An archive implied intention. It implied someone had chosen to store things here rather than carry them home.

She found the entrance to the undercroft behind the collapsed altar — a door of iron-strapped oak, scorched nearly to charcoal but still seated in its frame, still locked. The Lockridge key opened it.

That was the first thing. Before she saw a single document, before she read a single word, that was the thing that stopped her breath: her family's key, the key her grandfather had pressed into her father's hand and her father into hers with the same solemn words each time — *for the Gate and nothing else* — fit this lock as naturally as it fit the gatehouse. The tumblers turned with a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat.

Her grandfather had locked this door himself.

* * *

The stairs went down into darkness that smelled of dry paper and mouse droppings and, faintly, underneath everything, of old smoke that had never quite finished leaving. Bram lit the lantern she'd brought and held it high.

Shelves. Rows of them, cut into the living rock of the undercroft wall, running back farther than the light reached. And on the shelves — ledgers. Dozens of them. Bundles of letters tied with waxed cord. Scroll cases of cedar. A strongbox with its lid thrown open long ago and left empty. Dust lay over everything like a second snowfall, undisturbed, thick enough to show that no foot had come down these stairs in twenty years.

Not since her grandfather's death.

Bram stood at the bottom of the steps for a long moment, listening to her own breathing and the small sounds of the lantern's flame, and understood that she was standing inside an answer to a question she had never thought to ask. Keepers did not keep archives. Keepers kept *the ledger* — one ledger, passed hand to hand, the tally of the Gate. That was the whole of the tradition. One book, one key, one duty.

Unless the one book was meant to hide the many.

She took the nearest ledger off the shelf and blew the dust from it. The cover was plain calf-skin, unmarked. The first page bore a date sixty years before her birth and a heading in a careful clerical hand:

*Records of the Office of the Oath-Forgers, removed for preservation.*

Oath-forgers. The words rang a bell so distant she almost missed it — a phrase from the litany every keeper learned, the formal name of the office that had *made* the Gate's binding, back when the sealing was new. The forge-priests. She had always assumed the title was honorific, ancient, extinct. Like saying *the kings of the old dynasty.* You said the words; you did not expect to find their laundry lists.

But here were their records. Here, in a burned chapel beneath a valley her family had watched for generations. Removed for preservation — by whom? By whose authority?

She turned pages. Inventories. Correspondence. Rosters of names with titles beside them — *Keeper of the Word, Keeper of the Stone, Keeper of the Blood* — and her eye snagged on that last one and would not let go. Keeper of the Blood. The phrase was familiar in a way that made her skin prickle, because it was *almost* her own title. Her grandfather had been called Keeper of the Gate. Her father too. She was the third of that line. But the ledger used the older phrase in parentheses, as a gloss, the way a scribe might note that a village's new name had replaced its old one:

*Warden of the Seal (formerly: Keeper of the Blood) — see annex, folio 12.*

Annex, folio 12. Bram set the ledger down with hands that had begun, very slightly, to shake, and went looking along the shelves for anything marked annex. She found it halfway down the third row: a slim bundle, oilcloth-wrapped, the knot tied in the particular double hitch her grandfather had used on everything — harness, parcels, the gatehouse woodpile. She had untied that hitch ten thousand times without ever wondering where he'd learned it or why he favored it so.

Inside the oilcloth was a single folio of loose pages, and a letter.

She read the letter first. It was brief. It was dated eight days after the feast of the Sundering's end — the traditional date of the Gate's closing, the holy day her family had observed every year of her life with fasting and prayer. The hand was elegant, confident, the hand of a man accustomed to writing things that others obeyed.

*To the Warden-Designate of Thornfell Pass,*

*The matter is concluded as agreed. The three keepers of the old office are dead, and their records are secured against misuse. The child is accounted for. No bloodline remains through which the Words can be lawfully spoken, and thus no rival claim to the Seal can ever arise. What we do, we do for peace. Let no future generation judge us harshly for the price of it.*

*I have taken the liberty of drafting the oath your house will swear. It binds you to the Gate and to silence in equal measure. Sign it, teach it to your sons, and the Crown will confirm your wardenship in perpetuity.*

*Burn this letter. I know you will not — you Lockridges keep everything — but burn it nonetheless.*

*— V.*

Bram read it four times. The lantern flame hissed. Somewhere above her, wind moved through the roofless nave with a sound like slow applause.

*The three keepers of the old office are dead.* Not died. Were dead — passive voice, the grammar of deeds done by other hands. *Their records are secured against misuse.* Secured — here, under lock, under her family's key. *No bloodline remains through which the Words can be lawfully spoken.*

And then, this morning, a girl had stood in Bram's kitchen speaking those very Words to the stones of the Gate, and the stones had answered.

*The child is accounted for.*

Bram's legs carried her to the shelf bench without her deciding to move. She sat down hard on cold stone and stared at the letter until the letters blurred. Eight days after the Sundering's end. Her grandfather had signed the Lockridge oath nine days later. She had seen the original once, as a girl, displayed in the gatehouse on the feast day — vellum and ribbon and her grandfather's steady signature, and she had thought, even then, even at seven years old, how strange it was that the holiest document her family owned had been *drafted by someone else.*

Now she knew who. And the initial — V. — reached up out of sixty years and closed around her throat, because Ser Aldous Vane had introduced himself at her gate with the same courtesy, the same confidence, the same assumption that the world would arrange itself around his purposes. Whether it was the same man — impossible, surely, sixty years — or a son, an heir, an order that simply renewed itself generation after generation like a snake shedding skins, she could not yet say. But the hand felt the same. The certainty felt the same.

She opened the annex pages with fingers gone clumsy.

They were rosters. Death records, written in the same clerical script as the first ledger, each entry short and final. Three names. Three dates within a single week, sixty years past. Beside each name, a cause: *fever*, wrote one. Another said *accident upon the heights*. The third said nothing at all — just a dash, a blank where a lie should have been, as if whoever recorded it had run out of inventions by the third killing.

And below the names, in a different hand — her grandfather's hand, the hand she knew from twenty years of ledger entries, from labels on preserves, from the single letter he had once written her when she was away at school — a note, added later, cramped into the margin as if the writer could not leave the page alone and could not bear to write more:

*They trusted me. That is all I will set down. They trusted me, and I showed the riders where the forge-fire burned lowest. God has given me fifty years to repent and I have not managed it. If you are reading this, you are a Lockridge, and you have inherited a door I built out of other people's bones. I am sorry. I chose the valley over the truth. Every winter, when the snow comes, I choose it again.*

Below that, one more line, smaller, almost illegible:

*The girl is not accounted for. Pray she stays lost.*

* * *

Bram did not know how long she sat in the dark under the ruined chapel. The lantern burned down twice; she trimmed it twice without remembering doing it. The cold crept up through her boots and her cloak and settled into the marrow of her, and she let it, because the alternative was feeling what was actually happening inside her, and she could not afford that yet — not here, not with miles of mountain between her and the gatehouse and the sleeping child who was, according to everything in this room, supposed to be dead.

Every story she had ever been told about herself rearranged itself around the letter and the roster and the marginal note, the way a valley rearranges around a rockslide.

She had believed her family were guardians. Faithful, austere, unthanked — a line of watchmen who had accepted a hard duty so that two kingdoms could sleep. She had believed the oath was sacred, that the words her grandfather taught her (*the Gate is shut, the Gate is ours, the Gate is God's*) descended in an unbroken chain from the heroes of the Sundering. She had believed the forge-priests were a legend, a golden age, a beautiful story about people so powerful and so wise they had ended a war with a word.

They had not ended it. They had *built the mechanism* — the Words, the seal, the whole architecture of the binding — and then her grandfather had helped murder them for it. Had handed them to riders in the night. Had stolen their office, their records, their authority, and dressed the theft in liturgy. The Lockridge oath was not a covenant. It was a fence around stolen ground, and every keeper who had sworn it — her grandfather, her father, Bram herself, kneeling on the frost-hard flagstones of the gatehouse at fourteen years old while her father laid the sword-belt across her shoulders — every one of them had sworn allegiance to a crime.

*What we do, we do for peace.*

She thought of Vane at her table, courteous and cold, telling her the child was property of the Crown's charter, telling her refusal was treason. He had known. Of course he had known — his order's founder had written the letter, drafted the oath, arranged the murders. The Rekindled Oath. The name turned over in her mind and showed its underside: not rekindling the old oath, but *reclaiming* it — finishing what the founders had started, tying off the last loose thread, accounting for the last unaccounted child. Sixty years, and they had never stopped hunting the bloodline her grandfather swore was extinguished.

Because it wasn't. Because a mother had run, and hidden, and borne a daughter, and taught her the Words in secret, and died for it with a broken iron token pressed into small hands.

Bram looked down at her own hands, chapped and lantern-lit, and saw them for what they were: the hands of a thief's heir. Twenty years she had kept the Gate. Twenty years of solitary, faithful, prideful service — and the faithfulness itself had been the theft's best disguise. No one guards a stolen thing more fiercely than the one who inherits it, because to open the door is to ask what's inside.

She thought of the girl saying the Words to the stones, and the stones answering. She thought of the extra tally mark in her grandfather's ledger — the mark she had puzzled over in the autumn audit, the evidence of a key she didn't hold. Now she understood it. The oath-forgers' line had held keys of its own, and one of them had survived, and somewhere in sixty years of silence her grandfather had known it and marked it in his own book, unable to destroy the record and unable to act on it. *Pray she stays lost.*

God had not granted that prayer. And now the last heir of the murdered line was asleep in the murderer's bed, trusting the murderer's granddaughter, wearing the murderer's blanket against the murderer's winter.

Bram stood up. Her knees cracked in the cold like green wood in a fire. She wrapped the annex back in its oilcloth, retied her grandfather's double hitch with hands that no longer shook — that had gone past shaking into something harder and colder — and took the bundle, and the letter, and the first ledger, and climbed the stairs out of the dark.

Above ground the day had turned to gold and iron, low sun through the pines, the snowfields blazing. She loaded the mule in silence. As she swung up into the saddle, she looked back once at the blackened chapel, at its empty windows full of evening light, and understood at last why it had never been rebuilt. Her grandfather had not left it standing out of neglect. He had left it standing because it was *his* ruin — the true shrine of the Lockridge oath, the temple of the crime, and burning it again or building over it would have been, in whatever account book he kept between himself and God, one sin too many.

She rode north, toward Thornfell, toward the child, faster than the ice-safe pace, because there was a confession waiting at the top of the mountain that had waited sixty years already and could not wait another night.

And behind her, far down the southern road, dust moved where no caravan should be — a column of riders in grey, making for the pass, making for the Gate, carrying in their leader's saddlebag a charter signed by a hand that had written, sixty years ago: *burn this letter. I know you will not.*

Vane was coming to collect what his founder had stolen. And Bram was riding to give it back.
