## 11. Wren's Price

Wren Halloway had learned long ago that the worst bargains were the ones that made sense.

She stood at the mouth of the smuggler cave above Coldford, watching the snow come down in fat, silent flakes that erased the world a handspan at a time. Below her, in the blue half-dark of the cavern, Bram Lockridge slept against the wall with her sword across her knees even in sleep, and the girl — the little speaker, the mute-turned-speaking child whose words could crack a mountain open like an egg — was curled against Bram's side with the broken iron token clutched to her chest the way other children held dolls.

Two days out from Thornfell. One day from the bridge at Coldford, if the weather held. Three days beyond it to Wren's own territory, where her caravans wintered in the deep-cut passes of the Serrated Edge, where two hundred mouths waited on her judgment the way sailors wait on wind.

And in her coat pocket, folded twice and worn soft as cloth from handling, lay a piece of paper that would feed every one of those mouths through the famine winter.

Wren took it out again. She had taken it out forty times in two days. She knew what it said by heart anyway, because she'd written it herself, in her own neat smuggling-hand, six weeks before Bram Lockridge had ever knocked on her door.

*A route map. The high roads through Thornfell. And passage beneath the Hollow Gate.*

Signed with her mark. Witnessed by a man in grey.

---

It had seemed simple, then. Everything seemed simple in autumn, before the snow closed the world and hunger started doing arithmetic on your behalf.

Ser Aldous Vane had come to her not with soldiers but with ledgers. That was how she should have known he was dangerous; men who came with swords wanted something you could refuse. Men who came with ledgers wanted something you'd already lost. He'd sat in her counting-house at Marrow Ford, drinking none of the wine she offered him, and laid out his papers one by one like cards: grain prices in the southern provinces. The Crown's granary tallies for the mountain districts. A list — a long, patient list — of villages in the high country that would eat bark-bread by midwinter or starve outright.

"I'm told you move more goods through these mountains than the King's own customs men know exist," Vane had said. His voice never rose and never fell. It was a voice like a frozen river: smooth all the way down, and something moving under the ice. "I'm told your people are good people. I'm told they're hungry."

"We manage," Wren had said, which was true, which was also a lie, which was the only answer there ever was.

"Manage." He'd turned a page. "The village of Ashen Mill has eleven children under the age of six. I have their names here." And he did. He had *names*. "By spring, if nothing changes, I will have fewer names than I do now."

"You want me to believe you care about high-country children."

"I care about the Gate," Vane said, and this was the moment Wren understood she was talking to a man who had already decided everything, and was merely arranging the furniture. "The Gate stays shut, and the highlands stay poor, and the poor starve politely while the lowland lords buy up their land at famine prices. Open the pass, and trade flows north and south again, and the mountains live. I don't need your conscience, Mistress Halloway. Only your roads."

"And when your order gets its war? Because that's what the Rekindled Oath wants, isn't it? Everyone knows it. You want the old borders back and the old fires lit."

Something had moved behind Vane's eyes then — the first and only time — some grief-shaped thing surfacing and sinking again. "War is coming regardless," he'd said quietly. "I intend to be standing where I can choose its shape. Your roads will let me do that. Your people will eat. Those are my offers. I don't make second ones."

He hadn't. He'd left the contract on her table and walked out into the autumn rain without looking back, and Wren Halloway had sat alone with her lamp and her accounts and done the thing that made sense.

She'd signed it.

*Two hundred mouths,* she thought now, in the freezing dark of the cave, turning the paper over though there was nothing on the reverse. *Ashen Mill's eleven children. The Serrated Edge crews. Old Marta with her bad lungs. The twins born last spring who've never once been warm.* Against them: one keeper with tired eyes and no family left to speak of, and a child of eight who had watched her mother burn and run barefoot through a kingdom to reach a door she'd never seen.

The arithmetic was clean. The arithmetic was damning. The arithmetic was exactly the kind Ser Vane would appreciate.

That was the trouble with making sense.

---

"Can't sleep either?"

Bram's voice, low, from the shadows. Wren hadn't heard her wake, which annoyed her; twenty years of keeping a lonely gate had given the woman ears like a fox.

"Sleep's for people with clean books," Wren said, and crossed the cavern to sit on a stone near the fire-pit, close enough for courtesy, far enough for honesty.

Bram studied her. Firelight moved on her face, carving the lines deeper, and Wren found herself thinking that Bram Lockridge looked like a woman who had spent two decades being slowly written by wind and solitude, every year another stroke of the chisel. There were worse things to be carved by. Wren's face had been written by ledgers and lies, and it showed.

"The girl asked me today," Bram said, "in her tongue, whether you were friend or wolf. I didn't know how to answer her."

"What did you say?"

"I said you hadn't decided yet."

Wren laughed — a short, sharp sound that the cave swallowed whole. "Your gate-keeping hasn't dulled your eyes, Keeper."

"They're not my eyes. They're my grandfather's, and his father's before him. I just wear them." Bram fed a stick to the fire. "You've looked at me sideways for two days. You keep touching your left pocket like a man checking a wound. And tonight you sat where you could see both exits. So either you're planning to rob us, or you've sold us, and you're too honest for the first and too unhappy for the second."

For a long moment Wren said nothing. The snow hissed faintly against the cave mouth. Somewhere in the dark, one of her outriders coughed in his sleep, and the girl stirred and murmured something in the dead language of the forge-priests — soft, questioning, a child asking the night if it meant her harm.

"I signed a paper in autumn," Wren said finally. "Before I ever met either of you. A route charter. My high roads, drawn plain, and the ways under the Gate besides. Sold to Ser Aldous Vane of the Rekindled Oath." She drew the paper out and let it fall open between them, and watched Bram read it by firelight, and watched the woman's face do what faces do when a floor gives way.

"When?"

"Six weeks past."

"Why?" Bram's voice wasn't angry. That was somehow worse. It was the voice of someone setting a bone — necessary, careful, and hurting the whole time.

Wren told her. All of it: Vane in her counting-house, the ledgers, the names of eleven children, the granary numbers, the famine arithmetic laid out like a surgeon's chart. She told it flatly, without ornament, because ornament would have been a kind of lying, and whatever else Wren Halloway was, she had never lied about numbers.

"So that's the price of my head start," Bram said at last. "Two hundred bellies."

"That's the price of everything, Keeper. You'd know that better than most. You've been paying it since before you could walk — your family's whole life bought with one sealed door." She nodded toward the sleeping girl. "Difference is, your bill came due twenty years ago and mine came due Tuesday."

"And what happens to her when Vane collects? Have you seen his knights work? Have you heard what happened to her family?" Bram leaned forward, firelight catching the anger rising in her now, slow and enormous, like water finding its level. "They burned a house with people in it, Wren. To get one child. What do you imagine they'll leave of your roads, your crews, your precious hungry villages, once they've used you and opened the pass and put armies through it? Men like Vane don't honor contracts. They honor leverage. The moment you stop being useful—"

"I know how men like him end," Wren snapped. "I've buried friends who learned it slower than I did."

"Then why?"

And there it was. The real question, sitting between them in the firelight like a third traveler.

Wren looked at her hands. Smuggler's hands, scarred at the knuckles, steady as bedrock, that had carried grain through blockades and babies through blizzards and once, years ago, had pulled a drowned boy out of the Whitemere current and beaten the water out of him with sheer refusal. Hands that had always known exactly what they were worth and what they cost.

"Because I've watched people starve," she said. "Not in songs. In fact. I was eight — same age as her, maybe — the winter the river froze early and the relief barges couldn't get through. I watched my mother give her share to my brothers until there was no mother anymore. Just a shape in the blankets that got smaller every week." She turned the contract over in her fingers. "So when a man puts eleven children's names in front of me and says *you can save them*, I can't unsee the names. That's his genius, understand. He doesn't threaten you. He hands you the ledger and lets your own heart write the terms."

Bram was quiet a long time. When she spoke, her voice had gentled into something almost like recognition. "And now there are different names in front of you."

"Now there are different names." Wren almost smiled. "One of them can't be more than eight winters old and speaks to stones. The other one fights like a farmer swinging a scythe — no offense meant — and took a sword-wound across the ribs for a child she met four days ago. And the two of them together are carrying something that two kingdoms will burn each other to possess."

She stood, abruptly, because sitting still had become unbearable. She walked to the cave mouth and stood in the falling snow, letting it settle cold on her shoulders, feeling it erase her outline the way it was erasing the road below, the bridge at Coldford, the whole treacherous beautiful world.

Here was the truth she'd been circling for two days: she could not save everyone. No one could. Every choice fed someone and starved someone else, and the only sin was pretending otherwise. Her whole life had been built on that honesty — take the toll, pay the toll, count true, sleep sound.

But the girl had asked whether she was friend or wolf. And Bram had answered honestly: *she hasn't decided yet.*

Deciding, Wren thought, was for people who believed the ledger balanced. She had stopped believing that somewhere around the age of eight, crouched by her mother's shrinking shape. What she believed instead was this: that when the sums refused to come out clean, you chose the debt you could live inside. Not the debt that hurt least — the debt you could *carry*. The ones that got heavier were the ones that hollowed you.

Vane's contract would feed two hundred people through the winter and deliver a child to the fire. That debt would get lighter every day, because that was how debts paid in blood worked — the blood dried, the memory thinned, the ledgers balanced, and by spring you'd barely feel the weight at all. That was the trap of it. That was why men like Vane hunted for people like her. Not because they were weak. Because they were *practical*, and practicality was a knife that cut the hand that held it so gradually you never bled until you were empty.

But a night's head start — a burned bridge, a false trail, a lie told well enough to buy thirty hours — that debt would grow heavier every single day of her life. She would feel it in her shoulders every winter, every famine, every time a child in Ashen Mill went to bed hungry. She would carry it until it broke her back or buried her.

Wren Halloway had always chosen the debts that got heavier.

God help her, but she had.

---

She came back into the firelight and crouched by Bram, and put the contract down between them.

Then she took out her flint and steel, and set the corner of it alight.

Bram caught her wrist. "Wren. That's your proof of—"

"It's my proof of nothing," Wren said. "It's a paper. Papers burn. What I signed in my heart in autumn — that takes longer, and it needs different fire." She shook free of Bram's grip and held the page until the flames climbed it, until Vane's neat terms curled black and rose in sparks toward the cave roof, until there was nothing left but ash settling on stone. "There. Now I'm a traitor to the Rekindled Oath instead of to my own conscience, and between those two treasons, Keeper, I know which grave I'd rather lie in."

"Wren—" Bram's voice cracked on the name, and Wren saw, for the first time, the keeper of twenty solitary years genuinely at a loss.

"Don't thank me yet. Listen, because I'll say this once and then we're both going to pretend I'm still hard-hearted." She kept her voice low and level, businesslike, because that was the only way she could say it at all. "I can't come with you. If my crew learns I've burned a Vane contract, half of them will follow me off a cliff and the other half will tie me up and sell me back to him — and they'd be right to, because two hundred lives hang on my judgment and I've just spent it. So I'm going to do the next best thing and hate myself for it."

"What's the next best thing?"

"I'm giving you one night." Wren held up a single finger. "At dawn, I send my fastest rider down the valley road with word that you went east over the Shepherd's Stair. It's a lie, and Vane's scouts will waste half a day proving it's a lie, and that half-day is yours. But understand what I'm buying you, Keeper: one night. Not safety. Not escape. One night's lead through country he knows better than you do, toward a bridge I'm supposed to hold for him at Coldford." Her jaw tightened. "A bridge I am now going to have to lose, convincingly, in front of his own knights, without getting my people killed or hanged for it. Do you see the price tag on this gift? Because I do. I've read it twice."

"I see it," Bram said quietly. "Why, Wren? Truly."

Wren glanced back at the sleeping child, small and iron-tokened and impossibly heavy with everything she carried, breathing the slow breaths of someone who had finally, briefly, found a warm place in a cold world.

"Because she asked if I was friend or wolf," Wren said, "and I'm tired of being the answer nobody wants to hear. And because — curse it all — I've spent my whole life moving other people's treasures through the dark and never once asking where they truly belonged. This once, I'd like to deliver something to its right home."

She stood, and clapped the ash from her palms, and became again in a single motion the brisk, flinty caravan-mistress that two hundred people trusted with their lives.

"One night, Keeper. Wake your girl. We break camp within the hour — I want you across the high shoulder before moonrise, and I want my outriders gone ahead to make noise on the eastern trails. And Bram—" Wren paused, and for a moment the merchant's mask slipped, and underneath was something rawer and older and afraid. "When you stand at Coldford Bridge tomorrow, look for the red lantern on the western pier. If it's burning, my people have cleared your path south. If it's not burning—"

"Then what?"

"Then Vane got there first," Wren said, "and you'd best pray the ice holds, because nothing else will."

Far away, down the valley, muffled by snow and distance, a horn sounded — three notes, repeated. Wren went very still.

She knew that signal. Every soul on her roads knew it.

Riders on the southern approach. Grey cloaks. Moving faster than any honest traveler moved in weather like this.

"He didn't wait for dawn," Wren breathed. "He never does. Move, Keeper. *Now.*"
