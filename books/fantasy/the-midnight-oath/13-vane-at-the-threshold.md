## 13. Vane at the Threshold

The mountain did not want them, and Ser Aldous Vane respected it for that.

He had climbed a great many hard places in his life — the sea-cliffs of Vell with the tide coming in behind him, the walls of Karrow Keep on a ladder of rope and hooks while arrows sang past his ears — but he had never climbed anything that so plainly wished him dead. Thornfell's smuggler roads were not roads in any honest sense. They were scars in the rock, half-collapsed stairways cut by hands long gone to dust, and the wind came down through them like a blade drawn slow across a whetstone. It found every gap in a man's armor. It whispered through the visor slits. By the third day of the descent, two of his knights had frost-blackened fingers and a third had begun to pray aloud, which Vane permitted because prayer was discipline wearing different clothes.

Twelve knights had come out of the south. Ten still walked. The mountain was collecting its toll, patient as any tax-collector, and Vane paid it without complaint because he understood what it was asking. *Prove you are serious.* The Sundering War had been won by men who paid such tolls gladly. It would be refought by the same kind, or not at all.

"Ser." Corven, his lieutenant, dropped back beside him on the ledge, boots crunching on ice that had never known sunlight. "The boy's tracker says the caravan turned off the high road two miles back. Toward the caves."

"Toward the Gate," Vane said.

"The old passages, then. Under it." Corven's breath plumed white between them. "Ser — do we know what they mean to do down there? The girl. The Lockridge woman. If they mean to open it—"

"Then we have arrived precisely in time," Vane said, "and if they mean to close it more tightly than before, we have arrived precisely in time for that as well. Either way, Corven, our work is below. Walk."

Corven walked. He was a good soldier, Corven — obedient, unimaginative, devout in the way of men who needed their faith handed to them in a book with rules. Vane envied him sometimes, in the way one envies a man who has never been burned.

Because Vane had been burned. That was the whole of his truth, the small black seed from which the Order of the Rekindled Oath had grown, and he carried it with him always, the way other men carried a saint's fingerbone or a mother's lock of hair. He carried it in the place where his heart should have been lighter.

* * *

It had happened eleven years ago, in the spring of the year the kings signed their precious Accord, and it had taken less than an hour.

Vane had been a hedge knight then, twenty-six years old, sworn to no order, famous for nothing except a tourney win at Coldford and a stubbornness his father called *honesty*. He had been home on leave — that was the word the garrison used, *leave*, as though war were a service you could step away from like a man stepping out of church. He had been home at Ashen Mill, the little fortified farmstead in the border valley, with his wife Maren grinding barley in the yard and his daughter Ilsabet, four years old, chasing geese with a willow switch, and his son Aldric — named for no one; they had wanted him to have a name that belonged to himself alone — asleep in the cradle by the hearth because he was teething and had screamed all night.

The raiders came over the pass at midmorning, which was the insult of it. Not in the dark, like men ashamed. In broad daylight, down the very road that the closed Gate above them was supposed to make safe.

That was the thing everyone forgot, later, when the tribunal wrote its report and the clerks filed it away under *border incident, resolved*: the Hollow Gate had already been sealed for eighty years by then. The Lockridges had kept their vigil. The pass beneath Thornfell had been shut so long that maps marked it with a little sign meaning *impassable*, and the kingdoms had congratulated themselves on their wisdom, and the garrison at Ashen Mill had been quietly reduced because why garrison a valley whose gate was shut?

But mountains have more than one door, and seals only seal what they touch. The raiders came down the western scree — thirty of them, maybe forty, hungry men with northern steel and southern grievances — and they took the mill in forty minutes.

Vane had fought. He wanted that recorded somewhere in the ledger of his own soul: *he fought*. He killed six men in the yard with a hay-fork and then a sword off a corpse, and when they set the granary alight he went into the smoke after his son and came out with his arms full of nothing and his lungs full of fire. Maren died at the well, Ilsabet among the geese. He found Aldric last, in the cradle, untouched by flame, simply gone — and he never learned whether it was smoke or cruelty, and the not-knowing was the wound inside the wound, the splinter too deep for any surgeon.

And here is what he told no one, what he had carried silent for eleven years like a stone in the boot: when it was over, kneeling in the ash with his family cooling around him, he had looked up at Thornfell's grey shoulders against the sky and thought, with perfect clarity, *the Gate is shut, and my children are dead, and the shutting saved nothing.*

The sealed pass had not protected them. It had only made the kingdoms lazy. It had let the south disarm the border garrisons and let the north pretend its grievances were forgotten and let everyone sleep behind a lock that guarded one door of a house with a hundred windows. Peace, he understood then, was not a wall. Peace was a debt, and debts unpaid do not vanish — they gather interest in the dark until the day they come due all at once, in fire, at midmorning, while the geese scatter.

So when the Rekindled Oath had found him — a broken hedge knight drinking himself toward oblivion in a Coldford gutter — and offered him purpose instead of wine, he had taken it the way a drowning man takes a rope, without examining where the rope was anchored.

Let the Gate open. Let the war come again. At least a war was *honest*. At least in a war everyone could see the blade coming, and garrisons stood their proper strength, and fathers were not left alive to kneel in ash and do arithmetic about doors and windows. He did not love slaughter. People who said that about him had never watched him eat dinner — quietly, neatly, saying grace. But he had concluded, with the cold patience of a man who had done nothing for eleven years but think, that grief was the only teacher both kingdoms respected, and that they had grown ill-mannered students in the long peace, and that the lesson would need to be delivered again.

He would deliver it. And afterward there would be a harder, truer peace, one built on memory instead of forgetting, and no child would ever again die at midmorning because her father's king believed a locked door was the same thing as safety.

That was his creed. It fit in a sentence. Most true things did.

* * *

They reached the threshold an hour before dusk.

It opened out of the rock without warning — a doorway in the mountainside, tall as a cathedral's mouth, framed in stone so old the chisel-marks had weathered to silk. Beyond it the passage descended into blue darkness, and from somewhere far below came a sound that made all ten knights stop as one man: a low, vast resonance, like a bell struck underwater, like the mountain clearing its throat.

Corven made a sign against ill fortune. Two of the younger knights did not quite manage to hide that their hands shook.

"Hold here," Vane said. "Torches at the mouth. If anyone comes up those stairs who is not us, they do not pass." He drew off one gauntlet and flexed his bare fingers against the cold. "I go down alone."

"Ser—" Corven began.

"You have your orders." Vane smiled, and it was the smile that unsettled people most about him, because it was genuinely warm. "This part is not soldier's work. This part is conversation."

He took a single torch and went down.

The stairs spiraled into the mountain's heart, and the walls changed as he descended. The rough-cut stone of the upper passage gave way to worked stone, and the worked stone gave way to carving — ranks of figures in relief, marching around the walls in procession, and Vane slowed his steps despite himself. He knew these figures. Every child in both kingdoms knew them from woodcuts: the forge-priests of the Sundering, the oath-makers, hooded and hollow-eyed, bearing chains and keys. Here they marched not in fear but in ceremony, and between their rows ran lines of script in the dead tongue, and Vane — who had studied, who had spent eleven years studying everything the Order's archives held about this place — read enough of it to feel the hair rise on his neck.

*We bind, so that none need bleed. We remember, so that none need forget.*

Propaganda, he told himself. Carved propaganda. Every victor chisels his virtue into the walls. The forge-priests had bound the Gate with blood and called it mercy, and the Lockridges had kept their little vigil for eighty years and called it duty, and meanwhile the borders bled anyway, quietly, unglamorously, in places like Ashen Mill where no chroniclers came.

Still, he walked faster after that.

* * *

He heard them before he saw them — voices echoing up the spiral, distorted by stone into something ghostlike. A woman's voice, low and urgent. And beneath it, threading through like water under ice, a sound he recognized from the reports and had never truly believed until now: the child, speaking syllables that were not speech in any tongue of men, and the mountain *answering*.

The resonance deepened as he descended. He felt it in his teeth. His torch-flame leaned sideways, as if bowing.

Vane stopped on the last turning, composed himself — squared his shoulders, sheathed the anger where he kept it, folded the grief away behind the courtesy the way a man folds away a letter he cannot bear to reread — and stepped into the light of their torches.

They were in a vaulted chamber before a wall of black stone shot through with veins of dull iron — the Gate itself, seen from beneath, vast beyond sense. Before it stood Bram Lockridge with a sword in her hand and the child behind her, and Vane noted, with a professional's eye, how badly the woman held the blade. She had keeper's hands, ledger-keeper's hands. Whatever fight she'd given his knights at the bridge, it had cost her, and she knew it.

"Ser Aldous Vane," he said, and inclined his head with genuine respect. "You'll forgive the intrusion. The mountain's front door was closed to me."

Bram Lockridge shifted, putting herself more fully between him and the girl, and her voice came out steady, which cost her something; he could see it costing. "You're a long way from your charter, ser."

"I am exactly where my charter points." He planted the torch in an ancient bracket, deliberately unhurried, letting the silence work. "Lady Lockridge. Or should I say former lady — though I suspect the Crown hasn't gotten around to the paperwork yet. I'm going to speak plainly, because you strike me as a woman worn thin by lies lately, and plainness is a kindness."

"Is it."

"It is." He spread his hands, empty. "Here is what you believe: that you defend a child from a monster. I know. I've read the ballads they'll write about tonight either way. But consider — truly consider — the position you stand in. That child is the last heir of the line that forged the Gate's oath. Her blood can open what your grandfather's signature merely padlocked. Do you imagine the Crown will let that walk free? Do you imagine *either* kingdom will?" He shook his head slowly. "There is no version of her life, Lady Lockridge, in which some power does not eventually come down this mountain for her. The Order is merely first. And unlike the others, we offer her something no charter ever will."

"And what's that?"

"Purpose." He let the word ring. "She was born to be a key. Keys belong in locks, and locks belong to someone. Give her to the Order, and she will be raised not as property — whatever the law papers say — but as what she is: the heir of the forge-priests, the living oath. When the Gate opens — and it will open, madam, whether by her hand or by sappers and fire, the question is only which — better it opens by right than by violence. Better she stands at the threshold of a new age than bleeds in a cellar while men argue over her corpse."

The child made a small sound behind Bram — not words, just a breath — and Vane saw the woman's grip tighten on the sword.

"You talk about her like a hinge," Bram said.

"I talk about her like history," Vane answered. "History uses us all as hinges, Lady Lockridge. I lost a wife and three children to the peace you're defending — yes, three; the babe Maren carried never drew breath, though the tribunal didn't count him — at Ashen Mill, in the spring of the Accord, down the road your grandfather's sealed Gate was supposed to keep clean. So do not lecture me on what closing costs." His voice stayed level. He was proud of that, always proud of that. "I have done the arithmetic you refuse to do. Eighty years of false quiet, purchased with one family's vigil and another family's blood, and the interest comes due regardless. I am here to see the debt paid honestly, once, in the open, where both kingdoms must finally look at it."

For a moment — and he saw it, saw it land, saw the doubt move behind her eyes like a fish under ice — she wavered. Good. She was intelligent. Intelligent people were the only ones worth persuading.

Then she said, quietly: "My grandfather helped murder her family. I've read the proof. Your Order's archives hold it too, doesn't it — the burned chapel records? You knew what the Lockridge oath rested on, and you came to me offering *partnership*?"

Ah. There it was.

"Yes," Vane said simply. "I did. Because your grandfather's crime changes nothing about the arithmetic, Lady Lockridge — it only proves my point. Everything touching this Gate is built on blood already. The forge-priests bled to close it. Your family bled to steal the keeping of it. My family bled because of what the closing failed to prevent. The only question left is whether the next blood is spent building something or spilled for nothing." He took one step forward, and she raised the sword, and he stopped, palms open. "Come with me. You and the girl both. Stand beside me at the opening, and I swear on my children's names — Aldric, Ilsabet, Maren — that no hand of the Order touches her. She will be honored. *You* will be honored. Or stay, fight, lose — and be remembered, if you're remembered at all, as the last keeper of a lie."

The chamber was silent except for the torch and the deep, slow breathing of the mountain. Far below them, faint as a heartbeat heard through a floor, the Gate resonated — waiting, Vane thought, the way all old things wait, for someone foolish or desperate enough to answer.

Bram Lockridge looked at him for a long moment. And when she spoke, her voice was almost gentle, which was somehow worse than hatred.

"You buried your family," she said, "and dug yourself a grave to live in with them. I'm sorry for them, ser. Truly. But I won't help you make more ashes for other fathers to kneel in."

Vane exhaled slowly. Something in his chest — the small black seed, the old splinter — turned over, the way it always did at moments like this, whispering its familiar poison: *they never understand until the fire.*

"So be it," he said, and stepped back, and his voice hardened into command as he turned toward the stair. "Knights of the Rekindled Oath! Down, in order, blades drawn!"

Above, boots began to thunder on the spiral stairs.

Bram lifted her sword. Behind her, the child pressed both small hands flat against the black stone of the Gate — and the mountain's slow heartbeat quickened.
