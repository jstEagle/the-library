## 15. The Girl Who Speaks for Kings

The altar-stone was warm.

That was the first wrong thing. Everything else in the deep places of the mountain had been cold — the passages where their breath hung like ghosts, the ice caves with their blue throats, the black water dripping somewhere in the dark, patient as a counting heart. Cold had been the mountain's whole voice for days. But when they set her hands upon the stone, it was warm as a sleeping animal, and she snatched them back before the grey knight could press them down again.

*Do not be afraid,* her mother had said, in the old tongue, the tongue that lived only in her mouth now and in the stones. *Fear is a door. Do not open it.*

But her mother had also said *run*, and pressed the iron token into her palms still hot from the burning house, and run was all she had done since. Run through snow. Run into the gatehouse where the tall woman with the sad eyes gave her soup and did not understand her words. Run down smuggler roads and ice caves and under-halls until running brought her here, to this round chamber lit by a hundred candles the knights had carried down themselves, arranged in rings around a stone that drank the light and gave back none of it.

She looked for Bram and found her kneeling between two knights, wrists bound with grey cord, blood at her temple from where they had struck her. The tall woman was not crying. That mattered. She did not know why it mattered, but it did, the way some things matter without reasons — like the way the mountain hummed under her bare feet, or how the candle flames all leaned toward her no matter which way the draught came.

Ser Vane stood at the altar's edge. He had taken off his gauntlets, and his bare hands were folded behind his back, and he watched her the way men watch weather they have decided to walk into anyway.

"You are the last," he said. He spoke slowly, carefully, as if to someone very young or very far away. "The last of the forge-priests' line. Do you know what your family made?"

She said nothing. Her mother had taught her that too: *when the grey men come, be stone.* But the stones here were not stone the way other stones were stone. The altar heard her silence and answered it, a low note rising through the floor, through her knees, through the cage of her ribs, and Ser Vane's mouth tightened because he had heard it too.

"She understands us," one of the younger knights whispered. "I told you. I saw her at the gatehouse. She understands."

"Then she can choose," Vane said. "Child." He crouched then, which surprised her; great lords did not crouch, her mother had said, unless they wanted something. His face up close was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. There were lines around his eyes like cracks in river ice. "There is a word that opens this Gate. Your family sealed it shut, but the sealing runs both ways — what a priest's blood closes, a priest's blood opens. Speak it, and the pass will never close again. No more winters of starving valleys. No more villages burned on one side while armies sit safe on the other." His voice went quiet, and something raw moved beneath it, an old wound shifting under bandages. "I lost my wife and daughters to men who came through a border the kingdoms swore was guarded. It wasn't. Words on parchment kept them safe, not walls, not oaths. I want the truth of open roads instead of the lie of locked ones. Speak the word, little speaker, and no one here will harm you or the keeper. You have my oath on it."

Her mother's voice again, from a night so far away it felt borrowed: *An oath is a door too, little bird. Ask who built it, and who must live inside.*

She looked past him at the altar. Now that she was close, now that the stone's warmth crept up through the cold air to find her face, she could feel what slept inside it — vast and slow and sorrowful, like a bear in winter denning, like her grandmother's breathing in the last days. The Sundering War was not a story to this stone. It was a memory it could not put down.

And there were the two words.

They rose in her without being called, the way her own name rose. The opening word sat behind her teeth like a coal — she could taste it, could feel how it would go out of her and into the stone and the stone would *open*, and she understood suddenly, wholly, what that would mean: not just a pass, but every buried thing beneath it waking, the war's old anger rising through both kingdoms like floodwater. And underneath the coal, deeper, colder, the closing word — the word that would end it forever, that would make the seal absolute and final, that would burn out the last of the forge-priests' blood in the speaking. Her mother had known it. Had carried it unspoken all her life like a stone in the shoe. *If you must choose, little bird, choose the ending. Better our line ends than the world does.*

Open and avenge. Close and die. Those were the two doors, and everyone standing in this candlelit dark — Vane with his cracked-ice eyes, the knights with their hands on their swords, even Bram, who had fought for her, bled for her, knelt bound for her — everyone believed she stood between only those two.

She thought about Bram kneeling in the cord. About the woman's hands, rough from ledger ink and gate-iron, giving her soup, learning her words one by one, writing them down in a book with careful strokes like the words were treasures instead of curses. About the word of mending in the ice caves, when she had touched the broken token to Bram's scarred palm and the girl inside herself — the small frightened animal self — had gone suddenly quiet, because mending was a thing she could do, not only a thing done to her.

Everyone thought she was a key.

Keys did not choose. Keys hung on hooks and waited in pockets and got turned by other people's hands. All winter she had been passed hand to hand like a key — her mother's hands, the snow, Bram's door, Wren's caravan, Vane's knights — and every hand believed it knew what lock she fit.

But her mother had left her more than the token. On the last night, with the fire coming and her voice already breaking, she had held the girl's face and spoken fast and low, the way people speak when they are spending their last coins:

*"They will tell you there are two words. The word of opening and the word of closing. They will fight over you like dogs over a bone, each certain they know what you are for. Listen to me. Our line did not forge two words. We forged three. The first two are for kings and priests — power, power, always power. But the third—" and the fire had roared then, and her mother had coughed smoke, and gripped her harder— "the third is for witnesses. It was the first word ever carved, and it will be the last. When the time comes, do not open. Do not shut. Make them see. Promise me. Promise me, little bird—"*

She had promised. She had promised with the roof falling in orange above them, and she had run, and she had kept the promise folded small inside her all these cold months like an ember banked in ash.

Now the ember stirred.

It was not a word she had ever spoken aloud. It was older than the opening and the closing; she could feel that in her bones the way she felt the mountain's hum. It had no power to command. That was its secret, the thing her mother had tried to say with her last breath: the other two words *made* the world obey. The third word only asked the world to *look*. And nothing — not stone, not knight, not king — could refuse to look. Not truly. Not once the word was spoken.

Ser Vane was still watching her. "You hesitate," he said, and there was almost gentleness in it. "That is wisdom. But hesitation has a price, child, and others pay it. The keeper, for one." He glanced at Bram, kneeling in the cord. "Speak the opening word, and she walks free beside you. Refuse, and—" he did not finish it. He did not need to. The younger knight's hand moved to his sword hilt, and the sound of leather and steel was very loud in the round room.

Bram lifted her head. Blood had dried in a line down her temple, and her eyes found the girl's across the candle-rings, and Bram shook her head — small, barely a movement, *don't*, don't give them anything, don't spend yourself for me — and the girl looked at her and thought: *she thinks I am a key too. Even she does. Even kind Bram, who wrote my words in her book, thinks there are only two doors.*

She stepped forward. The nearest knight flinched; Vane raised a hand and the ring of grey did not close around her. Barefoot on the warm stone floor, candle flames bowing toward her like courtiers, she walked to the altar and laid both palms flat on it, and the warmth climbed her arms like sunlight remembered, and the whole chamber sighed — a long, settling breath, the sound of a house recognizing its owner after a hundred years.

"What is she doing?" someone whispered.

"The stone knows her," Vane breathed. "Look — the carvings —"

He was right. Around the altar's rim, old inscriptions she had never seen before were rising to the surface of the stone like fish coming up through dark water, glowing faintly, letters in her mother's tongue arranging themselves in circles. And she read them, because reading them was easy, because they had been waiting for her the way the gatehouse door had waited, the way everything her family had made waited patiently down the long years for the last small hands to arrive.

*Here stands the speaker,* said the stones. *Let the speaker choose: the Word of Opening, the Word of Closing, or the First Word, which is neither.*

Vane read the glow on her face and took a half-step forward. "What does it say?"

She turned to look at him — at the crack-lines around his eyes, at the grief he wore like armor worn so long he'd forgotten it wasn't skin — and she pitied him, and the pity frightened her more than the swords did, because pity was the beginning of the third word. You could not witness what you would not pity.

"There are three words," she said.

She had not spoken the common tongue before. Not properly. A few stolen words from Bram's patient mouth, half-formed. The syllables came out strange and thick, shaped wrong, but every soul in the chamber went absolutely still, because a child who had been mute in their language for a whole winter had just spoken.

"There are three," she said again, steadier, tasting each word like new fruit. "You fight over two. You never asked about the third."

Vane's face changed — hope and fear crossing it together like weather. "Child. Whatever the stone offers you, think. Power like yours should serve the living, not sleep under mountains. Open the road. Give the dead kingdoms honesty at last—"

"You want the Gate open," she said, "because you are sad."

The words fell into the silence like a stone into a well. Behind Vane, a knight made a shocked sound. Bram, kneeling, closed her eyes briefly — the girl could not tell if it was despair or prayer.

"I am not sad," Vane said quietly, dangerously. "I am *ruined*. There is a difference."

"Yes," she agreed, because her mother had taught her that too: *agree with the wolf until you know where the trees are.* "My mother was ruined too. In the fire. So I understand." She lifted her chin. "But my mother said the ruin is not a door. Sadness is not a door. Only the third word is a true door, and the third word belongs to no one. Not to kings. Not to priests. Not even to me."

"Then speak it," Vane said, and his composure cracked at last, and she saw beneath it not a villain but a drowning man who had swum twenty years toward what he believed was shore. "Whatever it is. Speak it, and let us all be free of this mountain."

"No," she said gently. "Not yet. First you must be *willing to see*." She looked around the ring of grey faces, the candle flames, the kneeling shape of Bram Lockridge with blood in her hair. "All of you. That is what the word asks. Nothing more. Nothing less. And it cannot be unsaid, and you cannot look away, and most people—" her mother's voice moved through her own, ancient and small and certain— "most people would rather start a war than see truly."

She drew a breath, and the mountain drew it with her.

Far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of rock, a horn sounded — once, twice, a third time. Then another horn, farther, from the southern side of the pass. The knights looked upward. Somewhere in the world above, armies were waking; the girl felt them through the soles of her feet like distant thunder deciding whether to become a storm.

"They are coming," the young knight said hoarsely. "Both hosts. My lord — the Gate alarms —"

"Then we have minutes, not hours," Vane said, and turned back to her, and his voice dropped to something almost tender, almost pleading. "Little speaker. Last daughter of the forge. Before the world comes down on us — open it, close it, or speak your third word. Choose *now*."

She looked at the altar. At the circling script, patient as starlight. At Bram, whose slow heartbeat she could somehow feel through the stone now, steady as a drum in another room. At the candle flames, all leaning toward her like a congregation bending toward its priest.

Her mother had said *run*, and she had run. Her mother had said *choose the ending*, and she had carried that weight a thousand miles. But her mother had said, last of all, *neither* — and the neither was not emptiness. The neither was a door her family had built for exactly this night, a hundred years before she was born, because the forge-priests had known, even then, that someday a child would stand here with wolves on every side and two terrible words in her mouth.

She opened her mouth. The mountain leaned in.

And the candles went out — every flame in the chamber at once, snuffed by no wind — and in the sudden dark, the stone beneath her palms began, softly, terribly, to shine.
