## 16. War Above the Pass

The horn sounded at dusk, and Wren Halloway heard it from three miles south of Thornfell, carried down the frozen valley on a wind that smelled of iron and coming snow.

She had heard horns before. She had heard them at Marrow Field, where her father died holding a wagon line against tax soldiers; she had heard them in the famine year, when the granaries closed and the roads filled with men who had nothing left to lose. But this horn was different. This horn came from under the earth.

It rose out of the mountain itself, out of the sealed throat of the Hollow Gate, and it did not fade so much as *stop* — cut off mid-note as if some vast hand had closed around it — and in the silence afterward every bird on the valley floor went up at once, a black cloth thrown across the grey sky.

"That's not possible," said Doss beside her, his breath steaming. "That gate's been shut since before my grandfather's grandfather."

"Things that are shut can open." Wren turned her horse south, toward the road, toward the long column of torchlight already crawling up from the lowlands like a lit fuse. "And things that are sold," she added quietly, to no one, "can be bought back."

She had sold them. That was the wound she carried up the switchbacks with her, sharper than the cold: she had taken Ser Aldous Vane's gold and given him the route through the ice caves, the timing of the caravan, the names of every guide she trusted. Her people would eat this winter. That had been the whole of the arithmetic, simple and clean, the kind of sums a smuggler queen could do half-asleep.

Then she had sat by the fire with the girl asleep against Bram Lockridge's shoulder, and watched the child's fingers move in her dreams — tracing shapes in the air, over and over, shapes Wren now understood were letters in a dead tongue — and the arithmetic had stopped being simple.

Below her, in the dark under the Gate, those fingers might be moving again right now.

"Queen's word," Doss said. It wasn't a question. It was the old formula, the one that meant *tell us what we're dying for*.

Wren looked north once, at the mass of Thornfell black against the last red light, at the thin thread of smoke rising from the gatehouse chimney. Then she looked at her crew — forty riders, six wagons, the best of what remained of her after Coldford Bridge — and gave them the only order she had left that was worth anything.

"Turn the wagons across the road at the narrows," she said. "All of them. And light everything we brought to burn."

* * *

In the southern kingdom, in the war-hall at Coldford Keep, Lord-Commander Edmun Rask stood before a map older than he was and listened to a boy describe a sound.

"The ground shook," the boy said. He was a shepherd, snow-crusted, half-dead from the climb down out of the high passes, and he kept his fists clenched at his sides as though afraid someone would take the story away from him if he loosened his grip. "Not like an avalanche. Like a *door*. And then the horn — under the ground, my lord, under it — and the ice on the lake cracked all in lines, all at once, like a spiderweb."

Rask had spent thirty years commanding the southern marches, and in thirty years the Hollow Gate had been a footnote, a boundary stone, a place where maps met and agreed to stop. His father had served the same frontier. His grandfather had helped build the treaty walls that made the pass irrelevant. War was something that happened elsewhere, among kingdoms with softer borders and shorter memories.

But he knew the old songs too. Everyone knew the songs, the way everyone knows the shape of a scar they've never felt. And the songs said that when the Gate sang, the Sundering had begun.

"How many men can you raise before the passes close?" he asked his marshal.

"With the famine levies? Four thousand in nine days. Six if we strip the river garrisons and pray."

"And the northerners?"

"They'll have heard the same horn, my lord. They'll be counting the same numbers."

Rask put his finger on the map, on the narrow inked defile beneath Thornfell, and felt the weight of everything upstream of that small mark — the harvests already failing, the grain riots in the river cities, the Crown's need for a victory more than for peace. The Gate opening was not a disaster. He understood, with the weary clarity of a man who had watched courts for decades, that the Gate opening was an *opportunity*, and that somewhere in a warm palace a council of men who had never seen the pass was already drafting the proclamation that would make four thousand farmers into avengers.

"They will call it a liberation," he said. "Whatever happens up there, whatever is actually true, they will call it a liberation, and they will send us to do it." He straightened. "Double the watch on the pass roads. If anyone comes down out of Thornfell — soldier, smuggler, child — I want them alive and talking before the army arrives. After the army arrives, it won't matter what anyone says."

The marshal hesitated. "You believe the shepherd?"

Rask looked at the boy a long moment — at the frostbitten hands, at the terror still living behind his eyes like a coal that wouldn't go out.

"I believe the mountain," he said. "The rest is politics."

* * *

On the northern side, the horn reached the garrison at Thornfell village as a shiver in the wine cups.

Sergeant Maren Cole had just sat down to her supper when the table jumped, spilling broth across her maps, and she was in the yard before her second thought arrived, watching men pour out of barracks in various states of dress while the captain shouted for lanterns. Above them, the mountain glowed.

Not firelight. Not moonlight. A seam of pale blue light ran down the rock face where no light should be — a crack, thin as a knife blade, high on the cliff above the Hollow Gate, pulsing slow and steady like something breathing.

"It's the alarm-stones," said old Pell, the garrison's relic-keeper, arriving breathless with a scroll case clutched to his chest like a grandchild. He had spent forty years cataloguing inscriptions nobody read. Now every man in the yard wanted to know what he'd found. "The forge-priests set them into the rock when the Gate was sealed. I always took them for markers. Grave-markers." His voice cracked. "They're not grave-markers, Captain. They're bells. The whole mountain's a bell, and someone's struck it."

"Struck it how?" the captain demanded.

Pell looked up at the seam of light, and Maren saw the answer arrive in his face before he said it, saw the old man understand something terrible about his life's work — that the dead tongue he'd transcribed so lovingly was not history but *instruction*, waiting all these years for a mouth.

"A word," Pell said. "Someone spoke a word."

By midnight, riders were on every road. By dawn, beacons burned on the high crags, and the message leapt from tower to tower down the length of the northern valleys: THE GATE STIRS. TO ARMS. TO THORNFELL.

Maren rode with the vanguard, and what she saw on the second day stayed with her longer than any battle ever would: the roads filling. Not with soldiers first — with everyone. Farmers walking beside their sons who'd been issued pikes that morning. Priests hauling wagons of relics toward a mountain none of their scriptures mentioned. Refugees going the other way, and then, shamefully, refugees turning around to follow the army, because where four thousand men marched there was food, and the famine had stripped even fear of its priority.

Nobody asked why they were marching. The horn had asked for them. The mountain had spoken, and two kingdoms that had spent a generation pretending to be neighbors remembered in a single week that they were enemies, and came running to confirm it.

On the third night, camped within sight of Thornfell's southern crags, Maren climbed a ridge alone and looked down at the pass road, and saw a sight she would tell her grandchildren about and never fully explain: fire, laid neat as a fence, across the whole width of the valley road.

Burning wagons. Dozens of them, chained wheel to wheel, blazing in the windless cold like a wall built out of orange light. And along the line, small figures moving — women and men in mismatched leathers, unhurried, feeding the flames, arranging the barricade the way a housewife sets a table.

"Smugglers," her lieutenant said, arriving beside her. "Blocking the road? Smugglers live off roads. It's like a fish drowning itself on purpose."

Maren said nothing. But she thought about what it cost to burn your own livelihood, and who you'd have to love to pay it, and she decided — privately, without orders, in the way sergeants decide such things — that when her company reached that wall of fire, they would find some reason to be slow.

* * *

Ser Aldous Vane stood in the antechamber beneath the Gate and felt the war come for him through the stone.

He could feel it in his boots first — the tremor, rhythmic now, the mountain's pulse gone quick and uneven, a heart in arrhythmia. Then in the air: a pressure change, a taste like struck flint. Then, distantly, through fifty feet of rock, the sounds of the world rearranging itself — hooves, horns, the deep subsonic groan of avalanches loosed on distant slopes by the shaking. Two armies converging on a single frozen pass. Everything he had worked twenty years to summon.

And it meant nothing. He understood that now, standing in the blue-lit dark with the girl's small footprints leading away down the oath-corridor ahead of him, past the place where Bram Lockridge lay disarmed between two of his knights, past everything he had planned and everything he hadn't.

He had wanted the Gate opened so the kingdoms would have to be honest — so the old grief would have to be counted, named, paid. He had told himself that war was simply truth with a banner attached. But standing here, feeling the mountain shudder with something that was not war at all but *witnessing*, Vane felt for the first time the possibility he had refused for twenty years: that truth might not need armies. That the thing he'd lost might be honored by remembrance instead of vengeance, and that he had dragged two nations toward a graveyard to avoid kneeling at one.

"Ser?" The knight at his elbow — young Ferren, who had lost brothers at the border raid too, whose loyalty was the purest thing in Vane's order — waited for orders that Vane found he could not shape.

Above them, muffled by stone, a new sound rolled down the valley: cheering. Thousands of voices, arriving, exultant, certain. Men who believed they had come to witness a miracle, or win a war, or liberate a pass, and who did not know yet that they had come to stand trial.

"Form ranks at the threshold," Vane said finally. "No one ascends to the altar-chamber. No one harms the girl. Whatever comes down that corridor—" he looked back once, at Bram Lockridge's bloodied face, at the fury and pity in it, "—we let it speak first."

"You've gone soft, ser," Ferren said, not unkindly. "The war's here. It's what we wanted."

Vane touched the worn locket at his throat — inside it, a lock of hair, a name, a date, the whole architecture of his grief — and answered the only honest way left to him.

"No," he said. "It's what we lost."

* * *

Deep beneath them all, in a corridor where the walls had begun to glow like the inside of a lamp, Wren Halloway's bargain burned on the surface of the world, six wagons' worth of it, and the smoke drifted up through cracks in the mountain until even here, in the realm of the forge-priests, the air tasted faintly of pitch and cinder.

Bram Lockridge knelt where the knights had left her, wrists bound, and listened to the mountain breathe.

She had spent her life believing the Gate was silent. Twenty years of audits and ledgers and solitary suppers, twenty years of guarding a door that never spoke — and now the whole massif above her hummed like a struck bell, and the girl's voice echoed up from the altar-chamber ahead, speaking syllables Bram couldn't parse but could suddenly, terribly, *feel*: each one landing in her chest like a footstep on a bridge.

The war was coming. Both armies, both kingdoms, thousands of lives marching on a decision being made right now by an eight-year-old girl with a broken token and a dead language on her tongue.

Bram pulled at the bindings until her wrists bled. The nearest knight — the young one, Ferren, the one whose eyes kept flicking toward the altar-chamber as though something in there frightened him more than disobedience did — watched her struggle and did not stop her, and did not help her, and did not look away.

"Hurry," he said at last, very quietly, to no one in particular. "Whatever you're going to do."

Far above, the fires on the southern road roared against the falling snow, and the armies came on anyway, because wars, once summoned, obey no one — not smugglers, not keepers, not kings — and the mountain held its breath, and waited for the third word.
