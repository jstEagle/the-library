## 19. Aftermath of the Long Winter

The thaw came late that year, and it came mean.

Wren Halloway stood at the mouth of Coldford Ravine and watched the ice go out of the river in great groaning plates, grinding against one another like old men arguing. Below her feet, the southern road was a churned mess of mud and ash — ash from her own wagons, burned three months past to buy an hour for a woman and a child she had sold once and then, in some fit of conscience she still didn't fully understand, saved twice.

Three of her people were buried up on the ridge under cairns of river stone because the ground hadn't been soft enough to dig until last week. She'd waited for it. She'd hated waiting for it.

"You're going to stand there until your boots rot," said Doss behind her, and she didn't turn around. Doss had been with her eleven years and knew better than to expect courtesy before midday after a funeral.

"They can wait," Wren said. "They've gotten good at it."

She meant the dead. She also meant the armies, which was the joke she wasn't going to say out loud, because the dead were owed more than a bitter laugh.

* * *

The war had come to Thornfell the way weather comes — slowly, then all at once, then gone again leaving everything rearranged. Two kingdoms' worth of mustered men had crawled toward the pass through the worst winter anyone living could name, and they had arrived to find the mountain shut. Not broken, not opened, not sundered. Shut. Frozen so deep and so clean that the scouts who climbed close enough to touch the Gate came back pale and would not speak of it, except to say that the stone was warm, which stone has no business being in a Thornfell February.

There had been a girl. There had been a knight in grey who walked down from the pass alone with his sword wrapped in his own cloak, surrendered his order's banner to the first tribunal officer he found, and asked — politely, they said, politely — to be tried for what he'd done rather than praised for what he'd stopped.

Wren had not seen any of this. What she had seen was the inside of a command tent on the southern road, where she'd sat across from a colonel with frostbitten ears and lied about roads, tolls, and cargo for four hours while her crew waited in the cold with their hands visible and their knives not.

"Your wagons blocked a military road," the colonel had said.

"Burning wagons block roads," Wren agreed. "That's generally why one burns them."

"You impeded the movement of His Majesty's forces."

"I fed forty families in Harrowdale through the famine," she said. "You can put whichever of those two things you like in your report."

He had looked at her a long time with eyes red-rimmed from wind and bad sleep, and then he'd done something she hadn't expected: he'd laughed. One short, exhausted bark of a laugh, the kind men make when the alternative is weeping in front of a stranger.

"Everyone hates this winter," he said. "Go home, Halloway. If I ever see you again it'll be too soon."

That was how the war ended for Wren Halloway: with a colonel's tired permission to leave, and no glory attached anywhere.

* * *

They buried Marta first, because she was oldest and because Doss insisted rank mattered even underground. Then the twins — no, not twins, Wren corrected herself every time; Ferro and Anselm were brothers born two years apart who happened to die the same day, and flattening them into "the twins" was the kind of lie grief tells to save itself effort. Then young Pip, sixteen, who had signed onto the company in autumn for one winter's wages and never saw spring.

Wren spoke over each grave. She kept the words short because long speeches were for priests and liars, and she was neither, whatever the Crown's clerks might eventually decide. For Pip she said: "He drove well. He complained less than Doss." Doss, standing at the graveside with his hat off and his bald head pink in the cold, had grunted, and that had been the eulogy, and it had been enough.

Afterward the company drank in the longhouse at Hollow Bridge, which was not really a bridge anymore since the knights burned it and not really hollow since the smugglers had roofed it over years ago, and someone started singing, and someone else started crying, and both sounds went on into the small hours and neither was wrong.

Wren sat by the window with a cup she wasn't drinking and watched the road south, where the mud was already filling with cart tracks again. Merchants. Already. The world had spent a winter staring into the mouth of hell and here it came back with flour and salt cod, asking after tolls.

"You did right," Doss said, dropping onto the bench beside her. "For once."

"Don't ruin it."

"You sold them to Vane." He said it without heat, just setting the fact on the table between them the way he set down ledgers. "Then you burned half our rolling stock to un-sell them. The crews want to know which Wren they have."

"The one who pays," Wren said. "It's always been the same one."

"And if the paying ever stops?"

She turned her cup in her fingers. Through the window the last light was going off the mountains, and the highest peak — the one with the wound in its side that folk had begun calling the Sleeping Mouth — caught the sun a moment, warm-colored, almost kind.

"Then they'll bury me too," she said, "and complain about my ledger."

Doss snorted. But he stayed, and after a while he took the cup from her hand and drank it himself, which among Wren's people was as close to absolution as the faith offered.

* * *

The tribunal summons came in April, delivered by a courier who rode like a man being paid by the mile and talked like a man being paid by the word.

"—required to appear before the Commission of Inquiry sitting at Thornfell town, there to give account of your movements during the months of—"

"I know what months," Wren said. "I lived in them."

"—and further to surrender any documents pertaining to the passage known as the Hollow Road, per the Crown's interest in—"

"There are no documents."

The courier smiled the smile of a man who has heard that before. "The Commission anticipated as much, madam. They ask instead that you bring your memory."

Wren looked at the sealed paper, at the wax stamped with the double crown of the two kingdoms, at the neat clerk's hand listing the charges she could already recite: unauthorized obstruction, suspected smuggling, harboring persons unknown, association with the fugitive formerly styled Ser Aldous Vane's quarry—

And there it was, the word that made her read the whole thing twice: *fugitive*. Not the girl. The other name at the bottom of the list, the one that stopped her breath.

**Bram Lockridge.**

Charged with treason, theft of a royal ward, desecration of the Hollow Gate. Trial to commence upon conclusion of the Vane inquiry.

So the keeper had made it down the mountain. Alive, which was more than Wren had bet on that night at the ice caves, when she'd handed over the last of her honest maps and told Bram which passages flooded and which ones breathed. *One night's head start*, she'd promised, and then given two, and then stood in the snow watching the dark swallow them and understood she had just chosen a side without ever picking one.

"They'll hang her," Doss said later, reading over her shoulder. "Keeper or no. Treason's treason."

"She sealed the Gate," Wren said. "Half the kingdom owes her their not-being-dead."

"Crowns don't count that way. Crowns count what's written down."

Wren folded the summons along its crease, carefully, the way she handled anything that might yet be useful. "Then somebody had best get some writing done."

Doss looked at her. She looked at the window, at the road, at the mountains going blue with evening, and said nothing more, because saying nothing more was a skill she'd practiced her whole life and tonight it felt almost like virtue.

But underneath the silence, working like water under ice, was the thing she had sworn to tell no one: what she'd seen below, that night she went down the smuggler roads herself to fetch what was left of her bargain. The mural. The bones. The altar-stone with its shallow bowl worn smooth by centuries of offered lives — and the fresh crack running through it, healed over with new crystal, like a wound that had decided, finally, to close.

And standing in the dark before it, the sound she would carry to her grave: a heartbeat. Not hers. Slower than hers. Deeper. Coming out of the mountain itself, patient as bedrock, keeping time with nothing above ground.

Bram Lockridge's heart, or what was left of it, beating somewhere in the stone — and the stone beating gently back.

No one had asked Wren what happened below the Gate. No one had asked, and she had volunteered nothing, and the tribunal's clerks could write *no knowledge* in her file until the ink ran dry. Some things weren't hers to sell, even to kings. Especially to kings.

But now they wanted her memory brought to Thornfell town like cargo, weighed and taxed, and they wanted it in the same room where they meant to try the woman whose heart the mountain was holding.

Wren finished her drink at last and set the cup down with a click.

"Doss," she said. "Saddle the fast mare."

"We going to answer a summons?"

"No," Wren said. "We're going to arrive early, learn everything, and decide afterward whether I'm a witness or a weapon." She pulled her coat off its peg and settled it on her shoulders, and outside the longhouse the spring rain began, soft and late and smelling of mud and growing things. "There's a difference, and it matters to exactly one person on that road, and she doesn't know it yet."

Doss grinned despite himself. "And who's that?"

Wren stepped out into the rain and let it hit her face, cold and clean, the first honest weather in a year.

"The keeper," she said. "Obviously."
