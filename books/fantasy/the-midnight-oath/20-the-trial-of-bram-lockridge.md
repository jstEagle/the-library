## 20. The Trial of Bram Lockridge

They brought her down from Thornfell in a wagon with barred windows, as though the mountains themselves might be an accomplice.

Bram watched the pass fall away behind her through those iron slats — the white shoulders of the peaks, the dark seam of the Hollow Gate, invisible at this distance but present in her chest all the same. She could feel it now the way one feels a heartbeat, which was, she supposed, precisely what it had become. Her heart beat slower than it used to. The physicians who examined her on the journey south had frowned over that, pressed their fingers to her wrist and counted, exchanged glances. *Slowed, but steady,* one had written in his ledger. *Like a clock wound to some other hour.*

She almost laughed when she heard him say it. A clock wound to another hour. If only he knew.

The trial was held in the great hall at Carrowmere, under a ceiling painted with the ascension of King Alderic III, all gold leaf and trumpeting angels, though half the angels' faces had been repainted sometime in the last century and now wore expressions more suited to a funeral. Bram stood on a platform of bare oak — no chair, no cushion; condemned prisoners were not permitted comfort until verdict was given — and listened while three days of charges were read against her in voices trained to carry to the back of the hall.

*Treason.* For refusing lawful custody of a ward of the Crown's charter.

*Theft of a royal ward.* As if Wren Halloway's caravan had been a purse cut from her belt, as if the girl had been carried off rather than carried toward safety.

*Desecration of the Gate.* This was the word they'd chosen for what she'd done below, and Bram thought it a strange mercy of language that they had no better one. There was no word for offering your life to a mountain. The old forge-priests would have had a word. The girl might have one still. But the Crown's clerks wrote in ink and law, and law had no shelf for the third word of the oath-builders.

The presiding magistrate was Lord Chancellor Edmun Corvane, a narrow man with ink-stained fingers and a voice like dry paper turning. He was not cruel. That surprised her. She had expected cruelty — had braced for it through every mile of the southern road — but Corvane simply read, and asked, and listened, and occasionally glanced at the gallery where the Rekindled Oath's remaining knights sat in their grey cloaks like a stain of weather that would not lift. Vane himself was not there. Word had come north weeks ago: Ser Aldous Vane had surrendered himself at the border garrison, unarmed, and requested tribunal before he would permit his order to make a martyr's relic of him. What he had said at that tribunal, Bram did not know. What she knew was that his knights sat in the gallery now without a commander, and that they watched her the way men watch a fire they cannot decide whether to warm their hands at or put out.

"Keeper Lockridge." Corvane set down the charge sheet on the third day and folded his hands. "You have heard the charges read. The court has heard your testimony, such as it was. You claim you acted under an older obligation than the charter."

"I acted under the original obligation," Bram said. Her voice sounded strange to her in this hall — too quiet, worn thin by winter. "The oath my family took was never written into the Crown's books because it predates the Crown's books."

"The oath," Corvane said, "which your family took in circumstances the court has recently found... instructive."

There it was. The burned chapel archive had traveled south ahead of her, or its contents had, copied by clerks with quick pens and no understanding of what they copied. The whole hall knew now what Bram had learned in the ashes: that Aldous Lockridge, her grandfather, Keeper of the Hollow Gate, had ridden down from the pass twenty years ago with six men and come back alone, and that the six men had been forge-priests of the line of Oathwrights, murdered in a chapel that burned conveniently behind them. That the Lockridge authority to keep the Gate sealed was stolen authority, bought with blood and buried under a keeper's careful ledger.

"You understand," Corvane said, "that this complicates matters considerably. Your family's claim to guardianship rests on a crime. And yet the Gate itself — the stone, the seal, whatever occurred beneath it last winter — appears to have accepted your act." He looked down at his papers, and for just a moment the dry-paper voice went quieter. "The court has received testimony from His Majesty's surveyors. The Gate is frozen solid, Keeper. Sealed in ice a fathom thick, in the middle of summer, in defiance of every natural law we can write down. Whatever you did down there, the mountain has ratified it. Which places this court in the position of either condemning an act the mountain has endorsed, or admitting that our law ends at the tree line."

A murmur moved through the hall like wind through wheat.

"I don't ask the court to endorse anything," Bram said. "I ask it to weigh. You have your law. It says the child belonged to the Crown. I say the child belonged to herself, and to a promise older than the Crown — a promise made by the people your law's own charter dispossessed when it claimed the Gate. You want to know if I committed treason. Ask instead whether a law built on a murdered family's silence can call anything else treason."

Corvane's fingers tightened on the edge of his lectern. Above them, the repainted angels looked down with their funeral faces.

"We will hear," he said, "the witness."

* * *

They had kept the girl away from the first three days. Bram understood why — a child's testimony could be dismissed too easily, or exploited too thoroughly — but the waiting had been its own kind of sentence. Now the side doors opened and the hall turned as one body, and Bram's heart — slow, mountain-slow — lurched against its strange rhythm.

She had changed. Of course she had. Six months in Wren Halloway's company, fed and clothed and taught, had filled out her face and steadied her walk. She wore plain grey wool, unadorned, her dark hair braided close, and she climbed the witness steps without looking at anyone except — once, briefly, at Bram. It was enough. In that glance was everything the winter had held: the ice caves, the token pressed to her hand, the word of mending, the altar-stone, the moment when Bram's blood had gone into the mountain and the girl's tears had gone onto Bram's face and neither of them had known yet whether it would be enough.

"What is your name?" Corvane asked.

The hall leaned in. Everyone knew the story by now — the mute child, the dead tongue, the little speaker the grey riders hunted. Everyone wanted to hear what came out of her mouth.

"Nessa," the girl said. Clear. Unhurried. The common tongue sat in her mouth now like a garment she had learned to wear well, though the accent of the high valleys still curved around certain vowels. "Nessa Oathwright."

Not the name the Crown's charter gave her. Not the designation from the custody writ. Bram saw the clerks scramble, saw Corvane let it stand.

"Nessa Oathwright. You are the last of the line that forged the Hollow Gate's original binding. Is that correct?"

"Yes."

"And you were, according to the charter, a ward of the Crown following the death of your kin."

"Yes." Nessa's small hands folded in front of her. "After the people the Crown sent killed them."

The murmur this time was louder. Corvane raised one hand and it died away.

"You do not mince words, child."

"Wasting words got my family killed," Nessa said. "I learned to speak late and I mean to use it properly."

Someone in the gallery — one of the grey knights — made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Bram kept her eyes forward.

Corvane consulted his papers. "Tell the court what happened at the gatehouse last winter. In your own words. Begin wherever you wish."

Nessa looked out at the hall. Bram watched her take her time — watch her choose, deliberately, the way her mother must once have chosen words in the dark with fire coming up the valley.

"Bram Lockridge opened her door to me when she didn't know what I was," Nessa said. "That's the beginning and most of the middle. The law says I was stolen. I walked to her door myself, through the snow, because my mother told me before she died that if everything else burned, the Gate would still be there, and the Gate's keeper owed my family a debt they didn't know they owed. I didn't know what I was walking into. Neither did she. She found out her grandfather was a murderer, that her whole life's duty was built on a crime, that helping me would cost her everything the law could take — and she helped me anyway. Not because the law told her to. Because something older than the law told her to, and she listened."

"And the Gate," Corvane said carefully. "What occurred beneath the Gate. You were present."

"I was present." Something flickered across Nessa's face — the memory of the altar-stone, of Vane's knights closing in, of the third word rising in her throat. "Ser Aldous Vane wanted me to speak the opening word. My mother's ghost, if ghosts listen, wanted — I don't know what my mother's ghost wanted. Maybe revenge. The old tongue gives me two words for the Gate. One opens it. One closes it forever, and takes the speaker's life into the stone. Those are the two words everyone knows. The two words the war was fought over." She paused. "There is a third."

The hall had gone utterly silent. Even the clerks' pens had stopped.

"I spoke the third word," Nessa said. "It means *witness*. It doesn't open the Gate and it doesn't shut it. It shows. For a few minutes, everyone standing in that chamber saw the Sundering War exactly as it happened — not the songs, not the banners, not the heroes. Just people dying and grieving and lying about both afterward. Ser Vane saw it. His knights saw it. Some of them broke. Some of them didn't, and moved to kill me so my dying breath would force the opening word out of me — that's how the old binding works, or so they believed." Her voice did not waver, but her hands had gone white where they gripped each other. "And Bram Lockridge broke free of four armed knights with her hands bound, and stood between me and their blades, and spoke the closing word herself. She gave her life to the stone. Or most of it. The mountain took what it took and left the rest. You've seen the surveyors' reports. The Gate froze solid in midsummer. That wasn't my doing. That wasn't the old oath working as designed. That was her, choosing, when she had every reason and every legal right to let us all burn."

"She confessed to desecration of the Gate," Corvane said. "Unauthorized alteration of a sealed royal structure—"

"It isn't royal." For the first time, Nessa's voice cracked like young ice. "That's the whole point. It was never royal. The Crown drew a circle around a mountain it doesn't understand and called the mountain its property, and when my family wouldn't sign the charter, the Crown sent men to erase us and handed our authority to whoever would take it. Bram Lockridge is the only person in this kingdom who has ever treated that place honestly. Punish her, and you'll be telling every keeper who comes after her that honesty is treason." She stopped. Drew breath. When she spoke again it was quiet, and it carried anyway. "I'm eight years old. I was supposed to be a key. Instead I'm a witness — that's the word, that's the whole word, *witness* — and I am telling you what I witnessed. Weigh it however you have to. But know that I'll be here long after everyone in this room is dust, and the mountain will remember what you decided."

* * *

They deliberated for two days.

Bram spent them in the holding cell below the hall, listening to the drip of water in the dark and feeling her heartbeat keep its foreign time. She slept more than she meant to. The mountain pulled at her even here, a hundred miles south — gentle tugs, like a sleeping animal shifting against a rope. On the second night she dreamed of the oath-chamber, of the mural with its keeper giving herself to the stone, and in the dream the painted figure turned its head, and it had her face, and it was smiling.

On the third morning they brought her up.

The hall was fuller than before. Word had spread, and Carrowmere's gentry had crowded the galleries, and above them, in the screened box reserved for the Crown, sat a silhouette that made the whole room hold its breath: the king himself had come to hear the verdict, or at least to be seen hearing it.

Lord Chancellor Corvane rose with the judgment in his hands. Bram noticed his fingers were stained with fresh ink, as though he had been writing until the last possible moment — revising, weighing, arguing with himself on paper.

"Bram Lockridge," he said. "Keeper of the Hollow Gate. The court has weighed three charges against you. Treason. Theft of a royal ward. Desecration of a sealed structure of the Crown."

He paused. Somewhere in the gallery a grey cloak shifted.

"On the charge of treason: the court finds that your refusal of Ser Vane's custody demand occurred under circumstances in which the custody itself was legally compromised — the charter authorizing the Rekindled Oath having been granted on evidence the order suppressed regarding the manner of the Oathwrights' deaths. Custody procured by fraud is not custody. Treason requires a realm to betray. You defended the realm's oldest border against a man who would have opened it. The charge is struck."

A sound went through the hall — relief from some benches, fury from others.

"On the charge of theft of a royal ward: the court notes the testimony of the ward herself, given freely and at length, that she came to you of her own will; and further notes that the Crown's claim upon her person derived from the same tainted charter. The charge is struck. Further, the court recommends — and the Crown concurs — that the ward Nessa Oathwright be recognized henceforth as a freeholder of the realm, in her own right and name, with all protections pertaining."

Bram's eyes found Nessa in the crowd. The girl was very still, the way she had been at the altar-stone, absorbing the blow of a thing finally going right.

"On the final charge—" Corvane's voice slowed, and Bram understood that this was where he had worn through his ink. "Desecration of the Hollow Gate. Here the court finds itself in honest difficulty. By written law, what you did beneath that mountain was unauthorized, unlawful, and irreversible. By every report this crown possesses, it was also the sole reason two armies did not meet above the pass in the worst winter in living memory, and the sole reason the Gate stands sealed today. Law condemns you. Consequence absolves you. The court cannot hold both."

He set the paper down. When he spoke again, he spoke plainly, the dry-paper voice stripped to something almost human.

"Therefore: the office of Keeper of the Hollow Gate is dissolved. The Lockridge line's custodianship — purchased in blood, however honorably maintained since — is ended by decree. You are stripped of office, of title, and of the wages and protections pertaining to it. And having removed from you the office whose authority you exceeded..." He met her eyes, and there was something in his face that was not quite apology and not far from it. "...the court finds there remains no office against which your actions may be charged. The charges are dismissed. You are free to go."

The hall erupted — half cheers, half outrage, a clerk somewhere protesting loudly that the logic was circular and being shushed by three colleagues at once. Stripped of office, freed of charges: punished and pardoned in the same breath, justice administered with a shrug. It was, Bram thought, exactly the kind of verdict the written law would produce when it collided with something older than itself. Imperfect. Evasive. Honest, in its way.

As the guards escorted her down from the platform, she passed the gallery where the grey knights sat. One of them — the one who had let her go at Coldford Bridge, the one with the warning that had led her to the burned chapel — caught her eye. He did not salute. He inclined his head, a few degrees, no more, and looked away.

Outside the hall, the spring air of Carrowmere smelled of horses and thawing river. Nessa was waiting at the foot of the steps with Wren Halloway looming behind her like a well-dressed thundercloud, and the girl ran to Bram and stopped short at the last moment — remembering, perhaps, that they were in public, that things had names now, that the world was watching — and then hugged her anyway, hard, regardless.

"They took your job," Nessa said into her coat.

"They took the job," Bram corrected. "The Gate's still mine. That was never theirs to give."

Wren snorted. "Sentimental mountain-worshippers, the pair of you. Come on. My wagons leave for the north road at dawn, and I've got a company to feed, a famine to outrun, and a freeholder of the realm who owes me a season's worth of table manners." She clapped Bram on the shoulder with a hand like a fence post. "You're welcome to ride along, ex-Keeper. Long as you understand the fare."

"Fare?"

"A story," Wren said, grinning. "The real one. About what happened under that mountain. Told proper, around a fire, to a crew that's earned it. No songs. No heroes." Her grin softened into something rarer. "Just grief, and what people chose to do about it."

Bram looked north, toward the hills beyond the city walls, toward the distant white line of the peaks where her heart's new rhythm lived. The office was gone. The duty remained. Somewhere up there, the mountain was keeping her time, patient as stone, waiting to see what she would build from what was left.

"All right," she said. "One story. But it starts with a knock before the snow."

Nessa slipped her small hand into Bram's as they walked toward the wagons, and behind them the great hall's doors swung shut on the sound of clerks still arguing about the law — arguing, Bram suspected, for a generation or more, exactly as the frozen Gate intended.
