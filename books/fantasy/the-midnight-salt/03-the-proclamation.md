## 3. The Proclamation

The criers came at the change of the watch, when the fog off the Tarn was thickest and most of Corrow was still pretending to be asleep.

Wren heard the first bell from her attic room above the dyehouse, where she rented a bed and half a window for eight coppers a week. She knew that bell. It wasn't the fire bell or the tide bell or the bell they rang when a barge jammed the weir. It was the proclamation bell, and it hadn't been rung in her lifetime.

She lay still in the dark and listened to her own heart doing something stupid against her ribs.

*Not yet,* she told it. *We don't know anything yet.*

But she'd known for weeks. You could smell a purge coming the way sailors smelled weather — in the small changes first, the ones you could explain away. The chandler on Rope Walk had stopped selling lamp oil to certain houses. The magistrate's clerk had started asking for names at the ferry landings, cheerful about it, writing them down like he was taking orders for bread. Old Sefa at the prayer-house had taken down the charms hanging over her door, all eleven of them, and hung up a crown seal instead, and when Wren asked her why, Sefa had looked at her with wet eyes and said, *Girl, I sell candles. That's all I am now.*

That's what fear did to this city. It didn't roar. It tidied.

The second bell rang, and then the third, and then the criers began, their voices carrying flat and strange through the fog, each one reading from the same paper with the same official cadence, so that the words arrived at Wren's window slightly out of step with one another, overlapping, like a choir singing a lie in rounds.

*"By order of His Majesty the King: be it known that within the realm there dwell persons gifted with unlawful arts—"*

Wren got up. She didn't light the candle. She went to the half-window and put her forehead against the cold glass and listened to all of it.

*"—persons who traffic in souls, shadows, futures, fortunes, and other matters reserved unto God and Crown alone. Such persons are hereby declared enemies of the realm. Any who shelter them, aid them, trade with them, or fail to report them shall share in their punishment—"*

There it was. The word she'd been waiting for without letting herself wait for it. *Punishment.* Not arrest. Not questioning. She'd read enough old broadsheets folded away in Odell's back room — she'd spent one glorious, guilty afternoon three years ago reading twenty years of guild correspondence while Juniper catalogued doorknobs or whatever it was he did down there — to know what the crown meant by punishment, and it wasn't a cell and it wasn't a fine.

*"—the hunt begins at dawn. God keep the King."*

Below her, the street began to make noise. Shutters opening. Voices. Someone laughing, high and wrong. Somewhere a dog going mad.

Wren stood at the window until her forehead went numb against the glass, and then she did the thing she was best at in the whole world, the thing that had kept her alive and fed and invisible since she was nineteen years old.

She made a list.

*Burn the letters. Take nothing with his name on it. Nothing with mine. Cash out the loom-work, not the bank — banks ask questions. Buy passage as far north as coin allows, walk the rest. Get to the Saltgate before they get past the marshes.*

And underneath, in smaller letters, the item she wrote last because writing it made everything else real:

*Get to Juniper.*

She'd been sending him money for twelve years through channels she pretended were clean. She'd sent him a scarf every midwinter and a letter every quarter and never once, not once, had she said the true thing, which was: *they will come for you someday, little brother, because of me, and when they come I will be there.*

Well. They were coming.

* * *

Mistress Halvard ran the dyehouse the way a ship's captain runs a ship in a storm — loudly, competently, and with total indifference to anyone's feelings but the wool's. By full light Wren was downstairs among the vats with her sleeves pushed up, mordanting a batch of fleece in alum the color of weak tea, and Mistress Halvard was shouting at everyone about steam pressure and also, pointedly, at no one in particular:

"They took the Vell family."

The vats went quiet. Steam hissed. Somebody's stirring rod stopped moving.

"Which Vells?" asked Pieter, the journeyman, though everyone knew which Vells. There was only one Vell family on Tanner's Lane.

"All of them," said Mistress Halvard. "At dawn. Soldiers at the door before the fish carts came through. Old Vell argued — you remember how he argues, like the man is constitutionally incapable of losing — and one of the soldiers hit him with the flat of a sword. In front of his wife. Then they marched the whole household down to the quay like a funeral procession, except the dead man was walking." She snapped her fingers at the vat. "Stir."

Nobody stirred. Everybody looked at Wren.

It took her a heartbeat to understand why, and another heartbeat after that to understand what the looking meant, and by then it was too late to pretend she hadn't understood. The Vells' daughter, Annet, was Wren's friend. Had been, since Wren came to Corrow with nothing but a fake surname and a talent for arithmetic. Annet taught her card games; Wren tutored Annet's brothers in sums. They drank cheap wine on the roof in summer and complained about men and rent.

And Annet, once, two years ago, drunk on that same cheap wine, had shown Wren a trick — held a needle in her closed fist, sang three notes under her breath, opened her hand, and the needle was threaded. A nothing-gift. A parlor trick. A thread through an eye.

"You knew them," Mistress Halvard said, not unkindly. Which was worse than unkindly. Unkindly, Wren could have fought.

"I knew Annet," Wren said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that later, in the way you're proud of things you did while drowning. "We played cards."

"The soldiers asked the neighbors if anyone on the lane kept company with the family." Mistress Halvard picked up Wren's stirring rod herself and handed it back to her, handle first, like returning a weapon. "Mrs. Ostler said no. Mrs. Ostler said it very fast."

The dye lot was ruined. Everyone knew it; nobody said it. Alum batch gone gray-brown, hours of work, a week's wages. Wren stirred it anyway, because stopping would have meant thinking, and thinking would have meant seeing old Vell hit with the flat of a sword, in front of his wife, for the crime of raising a daughter who could thread a needle with a song.

At noon she climbed to the roof and sat with her back against the chimney where the sun hit warmest, and she let herself think it.

They'd taken the Vells because of Annet. But the soldiers hadn't known about Annet. Somebody had told them. And the somebody had been paid, or frightened, or both, and either way the arithmetic was simple: in Corrow, starting today, knowing a gifted person was worth more than loving one.

Which meant every friend she'd made in six years was now a door someone might open for coin.

*Get to Juniper.*

She went down and gave notice. Two weeks' wages owed; she asked for half, in coin, today. Mistress Halvard counted it out without asking questions, which was its own kind of message, and pressed three extra coppers into Wren's palm and closed Wren's fingers over them and said, quietly, so the vat-room couldn't hear:

"I don't know what you are. I've known since your second week, when you balanced my books and found the error in the excise tally that saved me forty crowns, and no girl finds that error by counting. I don't want to know. Go north. Don't write."

* * *

The burning took longer than she expected, and it hurt more.

She'd thought of herself as careful. Twelve years careful, six of them in this room, and she'd built her life like she built a ledger — everything entered twice, nothing left loose. But carelessness isn't the same as emptiness, and when she pulled up the floorboard behind the trunk and lifted out the tin box, it was heavier than any box of ash should need to be.

Her mother's recipe book, with the marginalia in the old Brightwater hand. Four years of Juniper's letters, every one of them written in that careful slanted script, full of ledgers and door hinges and the names of objects he'd listened to that week — *the gate key remembers being cast, it says the smith sang while he worked* — and never once complaining, never once asking her to come home, and every single one signed *your brother, J.* Because even in letters, even to her, he'd hidden himself inside an initial.

One letter from Odell, formal and dry, thanking her for her continued remittances to *the maintenance of the archive*, which was the code phrase they'd agreed on a decade ago and which had always made her feel like a conspirator, because she was one.

And at the bottom of the box, wrapped in oilcloth, the thing she'd sworn she'd burn first and reached for last.

The locket.

Silver, worn smooth, small enough to hide in a fist. Inside it, a curl of paper no bigger than a thumbnail, folded to the size of a fingernail, covered edge to edge in handwriting so fine it looked like stitching. She'd carried it since she was fourteen. She had never once read it. She had tried, exactly once, the winter she came home — came *back*, rather — and her eyes had slid off the ink the way rain slides off oiled cloth. Whatever was written there, it didn't want to be read. Or she didn't want to have read it. After that winter, she'd stopped trusting her own memory of which.

She remembered almost nothing of that winter, actually. That was the joke of it, the private joke she'd never told anyone: everyone assumed she'd fled Corrow's plague year like everyone else, and the truth was she couldn't account for four months of her own life, and she'd learned to stop reaching for them the way you learn to stop touching a bruise.

She sat on the floor with the tin box between her knees and the fire going in the grate — a proper fire, wasteful, the kind she never allowed herself — and she fed the letters in one at a time, watching each page catch and curl and go to lace. Juniper's careful voice, going up the chimney. *The gate key remembers being cast.* Gone. *Odell says I balance the ledgers better than he ever did.* Gone. Twelve years of a brother's love, converted to heat and smoke in under an hour, because that was what the world had decided a life had to be now: something you burned ahead of the soldiers.

The recipe book she saved for last, and when she went to feed it in, her hands shook so hard she nearly dropped it in the coals. She held it instead. Held it and breathed and finally, deliberately, tore out only the pages with the Brightwater hand in the margins, and burned those, and put the rest of the book — plain recipes, harmless, her mother's plum cake and her father's fish stew — back in the box. Some ashes you owe the dead. Not all of them.

Then the locket. She stood at the hearth with it in her palm, chain dangling, and she thought: *burn it, it's the one thing that ties you to whatever you did that winter, it's the one thing that can't be explained.*

She thought about the signature she sometimes dreamed of. Silver ink. A hand signing something, and her standing beside it, and the strange lightness afterward, like having set down luggage she couldn't remember picking up.

She put the locket around her neck and tucked it under her shirt, against her skin, where it had lived for twelve years.

"Some things," she said out loud, to the emptying room, "you don't get to make me burn."

* * *

She left at dusk, in the crowd, wearing her plainest dress and carrying a market basket as if she were coming back.

The city had changed texture in one day. She felt it the moment she stepped onto Tanner's Lane: people weren't talking to each other anymore. They were performing at each other. Every conversation she passed had a listener in it that wasn't part of it. At the well, two women discussed turnips with the intensity of scholars defending a thesis while their eyes flicked to every passing stranger. A boy of maybe ten stood on a corner reciting the proclamation to anyone who'd stand still, and a man pressed a copper into his hand and walked away quickly, and Wren understood that the boy wasn't collecting coins, he was collecting *names* — because children hear everything, and children can be asked questions, and children repeat what they're told to repeat.

At the ferry landing, the magistrate's cheerful clerk had a table set up and a queue of travelers, and he was asking each one where they were bound and for what purpose, and writing it down, and stamping a chit, and smiling, smiling, smiling.

Wren turned around and walked to the coal wharf instead, where the bargemen didn't use tables or stamps or smiles, and paid a coal-heaver named Doss triple fare to put her aboard the *Kindly Gull*, northbound with the evening tide, no manifest, no chit. Doss took the coin and looked at her for a long moment with river-gray eyes.

"You're the fourth tonight," he said. "Going north."

"That should worry you."

"It does." He spat over the side. "It also pays my winter. Hold still."

He smeared coal dust across her cheekbones with a thumb rough as a brick, took her market basket and replaced it with a sack of actual coal to carry, and suddenly she wasn't a fleeing woman, she was a laborer heading to a night shift, and laborers were invisible. Laborers were furniture. She'd spent six years learning how money moved invisibly through Corrow; it turned out people moved the same way, if you dressed them right.

From the stern of the *Kindly Gull*, as Corrow shrank behind her into a smudge of lamplight and fog, Wren allowed herself exactly ninety seconds of grief — for the city, for Annet somewhere in a hold below the quay, for the tin box of ash in her room that some other girl would find someday and puzzle over.

Then the ninety seconds ended, because grief was a luxury with a body count now.

North. Three days to the marsh road, then two more across the fens to the hills, and then the pass, and then the valley where the Saltgate sat in its cliff like a secret kept so long it had become a fact of geology. Juniper was there. Juniper, who wrote letters about doorknobs because he thought that was what a normal person loved, who had never in his life suspected — she was almost sure he'd never suspected — what his safety had cost.

She touched the locket through her shirt. Cold metal, warming slowly against her skin.

Whatever she'd traded that winter, whatever silver-inked name was stitched inside this little silver case, it had bought him twelve quiet years among the ledgers. The crown was done selling quiet. If the price came due again — if it came due in blood, in soul, in whatever currency the court beneath the mountain dealt in — then she'd pay it again, and pay it forward this time, with her eyes open.

The bargeman called softly that the tide was turning, and the *Kindly Gull* leaned into the dark water, and Corrow's lights slid away astern like coins dropped down a well.

Behind her, the city that forgot its friends. Ahead of her, the marshes, the hills, the gate.

Ahead of her, her brother, who had no idea what was coming — and no idea, she thought with a sharp twist of guilt she refused to examine yet, what he was.

She faced north and watched the water run.
