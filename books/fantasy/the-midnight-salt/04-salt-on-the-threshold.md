## 4. Salt on the Threshold

Odell woke me before dawn by dropping a sack of salt on my chest.

"Get up," she said. "We're doing the closing rite."

I sat up in my cot in the gatekeeper's alcove, salt spilling down my nightshirt, and looked at her. She was already dressed—full dress, which was wrong for a Tuesday, or whatever day it was; the guildhouse didn't much believe in days of the week, but I kept a ledger of them anyway, and this one was a workday if ever there was one. Odell wore her formal coat, the one with the brass clasps and the salt-white cuffs, the one she'd worn to exactly two funerals and one coronation.

"It's not solstice," I said. "It's not a full moon. It's not even—" I checked the small clock on my shelf. "It's four in the morning."

"The rite doesn't keep a calendar," Odell said. "Neither do I. Up. Bring your hands and nothing else."

The Guild of Impossible Things kept many doors. Most of them were lies—doors that opened onto cupboards, doors that opened onto other doors, doors that opened onto nothing at all but did so with tremendous conviction. The Saltgate was the only true one. It sat at the bottom of the guildhouse's oldest stair, behind three locked doors and a curtain that had been washed so many times it was more memory than cloth, and it was made of a stone no quarry would claim: grey-white, faintly warm, veined with something that glittered when you weren't looking directly at it.

I had walked past it nearly every day for twelve years. I had touched it perhaps a dozen times, always briefly, the way you touch a sleeping animal. The Saltgate remembered things too—I could hear it if I pressed my palm flat and held still—but its memories were old and deep and cold, like listening to the sea through a wall, and I had never liked it the way I liked doorknobs and teacups and other honest objects with short, simple lives.

Odell was already at the bottom of the stair, setting out bowls.

"You've watched me close it twice," she said. "Both times you pretended to be counting inventory."

"I was counting inventory."

"You were listening to the hinges." She didn't look up from her arranging. "That's not an accusation. That's why we're here." She straightened. "Tonight you close it. I watch."

My stomach did something complicated. "Why tonight?"

"Because I said so."

"Odell."

"Because," she said, with the enormous patience of a woman who had answered ten thousand of my questions over twelve years and intended to answer perhaps nine thousand more, "the wards are thinning, and I want your hands to know what to do before your head has time to argue. Hands learn faster than heads. Heads talk themselves out of things."

"What wards? Thinning how?"

She picked up the first bowl and pushed it into my arms. "Salt from the eastern shore. Lay the circle. Start where the stone is warmest."

That was how Odell taught: by refusing, redirecting, making the lesson happen sideways while you were busy objecting. I knelt at the threshold and laid my palm against the Saltgate to find its warmth, and it noticed me immediately—the way it always did, a slow attention rising through the stone like heat through floorboards—and I heard, distantly, that sound like the sea, and beneath the sea-sound something else tonight. A thinner note. A note like ice creaking on a pond.

*Later,* I told the gate, and it let go of me, unoffended. Gates are patient. It's their whole nature.

I poured the circle. The salt ran white and fine through my fingers, and where it landed on the flagstones it seemed to land *twice*—once as grain, once as light, a second ghost-pouring just slightly offset from the real one. The wards, then. The wards were the ghost-circle, the older pour that lived underneath everything in this house, and I could see it now because—

Because it was fading. The ghost-light guttered like a candle in a draft, and where it failed, the salt I poured sat alone and ordinary on plain stone, guarding nothing.

"Odell," I said. "The under-circle is dying."

"I know."

"How long has it been dying?"

"Since before you could hear it." She came and stood beside me, looking down at my salt circle with her hands folded behind her back, a woman inspecting a fence she suspected wouldn't hold. "Wards aren't walls, boy. They're promises. Someone made them a long time ago, and every promise needs someone who remembers the terms. The last person who remembered all the terms died eleven years ago, and I have been remembering as much as I can ever since, and my memory"—she tapped her temple—"is a leaky bucket."

Eleven years ago. The year I arrived at the guildhouse, half-frozen, nameless except for the name Wren had shouted after me down the road until the wind took it. I had never asked what happened to the guildhouse's previous keeper, or the keeper before Odell. There was a shape in my understanding of my own history, the way there's a shape where a tooth used to be, and I had learned not to press my tongue against it.

"Whose promise was it?" I asked. "The original wards."

"Someone's." She crouched, took a pinch of salt from my circle, and flung it at the gate. It should have scattered. Instead it hung in the air for a heartbeat—a full heartbeat, grains suspended like flies in amber—and then fell in a perfect ring around the threshold, as if the gate itself had said *no, stay.*

"Now," Odell said. "The words. You've heard them twice. Say them back."

"They're not words. They're—" I struggled. When Odell performed the closing rite, the sounds she made weren't language, exactly. They were more like the sounds objects make when they remember: the creak, the chime, the sigh of a hinge. She spoke the gate's own memory back to it. "They're the gate remembering itself. How do I say a thing I can't remember?"

"That," Odell said, "is the entire lesson."

* * *

It took me four hours. It took me four hours because I kept trying to *say* the rite, and saying it was wrong, and Odell kept drinking tea at the top of the stair and offering no help beyond the occasional devastating silence.

Finally, somewhere past sunrise, I stopped trying to speak and started trying to listen.

I put both palms flat against the Saltgate. Its attention rose to meet me, that slow deep cold, and I did what I had spent twelve years learning to do with doorknobs and lockets and coins: I went quiet inside, quieter than quiet, until there was nothing of me left but the listening. And the gate's memory opened like a well.

There was a maker. Of course there was a maker—everything honest has one—but the Saltgate's maker was not a person with hands and a workshop. The Saltgate's maker was a bargain. I felt it in the stone the way I'd once felt a wedding ring remember vows: not the event, but the *shape* of the event, the commitment compressed into matter. Two sides. One door. Something agreed to be shut on one side, and something agreed to stand watch on the other, forever, and the forever was the part that hummed in the stone like a struck bell that never quite stops ringing.

And the sound the bell made—the sound of *forever, watched*—that was the rite. Not words. The note underneath the words.

I sang it. Or I didn't sing it; my throat moved and what came out wasn't air. The salt circle flared white. The ghost-wards flickered—one breath, two—and for those two breaths the whole guildhouse above us groaned like a ship settling, every door in the building swinging an inch open and shut, and somewhere upstairs a hundred impossible objects rang out in chorus, alarmed, awake.

Then it passed. The ghost-wards dimmed again, guttering back toward dark, but they had flickered. They had answered.

Odell set down her teacup very slowly.

"Well," she said.

"What did I do?"

"You did the rite." She came down the stairs, and I saw—for the first time since I'd known her—that her hands were shaking. Not much. Just enough. "Twelve years I've been propping up those wards with memory and stubbornness, and you walk down here half-asleep and give them a heartbeat of true strength." She stopped in front of me. "Where did that come from?"

"The gate told me."

"The gate doesn't tell anyone anything. I've stood where you're standing for forty years, and the gate has never told me a blessed thing." Her eyes were sharp and strange. "Juniper. What did it feel like?"

I considered lying. I had gotten very good at lying about my gift—not because anyone at the guild would have hurt me for it, but because gifts attract hunters, and hunters attract grief, and I had promised myself at eight years old, standing in a burnt doorway, that I would never again be the reason someone I loved got taken.

But Odell was looking at me with forty years of gatekeeping in her face, and lying to her felt like salting a well.

"Like it knew me," I said. "Not my gift. *Me.* Like it had been waiting."

Odell was quiet for a long moment. Then she laughed—one short, unhappy bark—and turned away to gather the bowls.

"Just in case," she said.

"What?"

"The rite. Why tonight, why now, why I finally taught you. You've been asking. That's the answer. Just in case." She stacked a bowl into another bowl with more force than crockery deserved. "A keeper who knows the closing rite is a keeper who can be replaced by anyone who can't. A keeper who knows it can hold the door alone. I want your hands to know. Whatever happens next, I want your hands to know."

"Odell, what happens next?"

She paused at the foot of the stairs, her back to me, and I watched her decide something. I watched her decide *against* it.

"Rest," she said. "You've earned it. We'll run the rite again tomorrow night, and the night after, until your hands know it the way they know how to lie. Then—" She stopped herself again. I could actually see her swallow the rest of the sentence, physically, like a pill going down.

"Then what?"

"Then we'll see," she said, and climbed the stairs, and the curtain fell closed behind her, and I was alone with the gate.

* * *

I should have slept. Instead I did what I always did when the world grew teeth: I went to work.

The morning's inventory waited on my desk—a crate recovered from a dead trader's estate in the marsh country, contents uncatalogued. I unpacked it piece by piece, laying each item on the felt and pressing two fingers to it, listening. A candlestick that remembered being stolen (twice). A mirror that remembered nothing, which was somehow worse than any memory it could have had. A wooden duck, badly carved, that remembered being loved so fiercely by a child that the love had soaked into the grain like stain. I catalogued it all in my neat hand, and the work settled me the way it always did, and by afternoon I had almost convinced myself the morning had been a drill. An old woman's caution. Just in case.

Then I went to oil the front door's hinges.

Every keeper before me had oiled those hinges on a schedule, and I had inherited the schedule along with the desk and the alcove and the key ring. It was a small ritual. The front door of the guildhouse was a good honest oak door with a brass knob, and the knob was my favorite thing in the entire building, because it remembered its maker.

Most objects only remembered fragments—a hand, a smell, a moment. But this knob had been cast by a man named Aldous Ferren, who had talked to his work while he worked, and he had worked slowly, and so the knob remembered him almost whole: a big man with burn-scarred forearms who whistled flat and believed that a door should welcome people home. Whenever the world got heavy, I would wrap my hand around that knob and listen to Aldous Ferren whistle, and feel the warmth of a forge, and remember that things could be made gently, on purpose, for strangers not yet born.

I put my hand on the knob.

Fire.

Not the forge. The knob couldn't mistake a forge for anything else—it knew fire the way I knew my own name, the friendly roar of it, the orange light. This was different. This was fire remembered from the outside: heat slamming against the door like a fist, wood screaming, smoke rolling black across the ceiling of some room, and through it all Aldous Ferren's knob staying cool in someone's grip as a hand turned it, desperately, and the door held shut, held shut, held—

I ripped my hand off the brass so fast I knocked the oilcan off the shelf. It clattered and rolled. My heart was going like a hammer.

Doorknobs don't remember things that haven't happened.

That was the rule. Twelve years of listening had taught me the rules: objects remember their pasts, vividly and completely and uselessly. They don't remember futures. They can't. Memory runs backward like water runs downhill, and in twelve years I had never once—not once—heard an object hold a memory of something yet to come.

But the knob was still warm under my hand's absence, and the memory was still there, sitting in the brass like a splinter: *fire at the door.* Not a forge-fire. A burning. And the door holding shut from the inside while someone on the other side wanted in—or someone on the inside wanted out—and the smoke, the terrible black smoke, and beneath the crackle of flame, faintly, a sound I recognized with a lurch:

Boots. Many boots, moving together, in step.

I stood in the entryway with my heart slamming and forced myself to breathe. Options. Odell would want options.

One: I had imagined it. I was tired; I'd been up since four; exhaustion did strange things to the hearing. Except I had never once hallucinated a memory in twelve years, and this hadn't sounded like a mishearing. It had sounded like a fact.

Two: the knob remembered a fire from long ago—some siege, some old catastrophe before the guildhouse was the guildhouse. Except the knob remembered its *maker*, and Aldous Ferren had died sixty years back, and the fire in the memory had been seen from the inside of a hallway I was standing in. The wainscoting in the memory matched the wainscoting at my elbow. The smoke had rolled across a ceiling I could look up and touch.

Three.

I didn't like option three. Option three was that the rules were wrong, or that I was wrong about the rules, and that somewhere ahead of us—soon, close, close enough that the brass had begun to sweat it—there was a fire coming for this door.

I walked the house. Every room. Every object with ears, which was all of them. The candlesticks remembered nothing new. The mirrors remembered their usual nothing. The great hall's chandelier hummed its decades of parties. Nothing burned anywhere in the guildhouse's memory except in that one brass knob, holding its splinter of future like a secret it was ashamed of.

In the kitchen, the kettle remembered being boiled, which was normal. In the pantry, the salt crock remembered the sea, which was normal. In the stairwell, the banister remembered ten thousand tired hands, which was normal.

And at the top of the old stair, behind the third locked door, the curtain stirred in a draft that had no source, and from below—from the bottom of the stair, from the threshold—I heard the Saltgate.

Not the sea-sound. Not the bell-hum of *forever, watched.*

A note like ice creaking on a pond. Louder than yesterday. And underneath it, so faint I might have invented it, something that raised every hair on my arms:

Knocking.

I stood at the top of that stair for a long time with my hand pressed flat against the locked door, listening to a gate knock on the inside of the world, and I thought about Odell's shaking hands, and her dress coat worn on a workday, and *just in case,* and the boots in step behind the smoke.

Then I went to find her, and she wasn't in her study, and she wasn't in the kitchen, and she wasn't in the archive.

She was in the yard, standing in the blue evening light, looking north toward the city, where—far off, too far to see clearly, but unmistakable to anyone who knew what they were looking at—a smudge of smoke rose against the darkening sky.

It wasn't coming from Corrow.

It was coming from the coast road. From the direction of the ferry landing. From the direction travelers came from, when they were coming to us.

From the direction of my sister.

"Odell," I said. "What is that smoke?"

She didn't answer for a long moment. When she finally spoke, her voice was flat and quiet, the voice of a woman reading terms she'd hoped never to sign.

"Go pack the ledgers, Juniper," she said. "The oldest one first."
