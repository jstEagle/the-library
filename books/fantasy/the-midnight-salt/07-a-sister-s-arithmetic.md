## 7. A Sister's Arithmetic

The cottage door was locked, which was the first wrong thing.

Wren stood in the lane with her hood up and her heart going like a trapped bird, because their mother had never owned a lock in her life. Brightwaters left doors open for travelers and cats and weather. Brightwater doors were a promise: *nothing here worth stealing but bread and kindness, and you're welcome to both.* The lock on this door was new iron, good iron, the kind that cost more than the door it hung in.

Someone had cared about what was inside.

She put her hand flat against the wood and told herself she was checking for warmth, for signs of life, for anything except the truth, which was that she was afraid of her own childhood home. Snow ticked down through the bare alders. Somewhere behind her, back along the road to Corrow, a dog barked twice and quit, as if even the dogs had decided not to notice her today.

*You burned the letters,* she reminded herself. *You walked out of the city past criers shouting the crown's bounty on the gifted, and you did it in daylight, because running looks worse than walking. You can open a door.*

The lock turned to a hairpin from her sleeve — another thing she'd learned that winter, that winter she didn't talk about — and the door swung inward on darkness and dust and a smell like cold ash.

Wren counted to three, out loud, in the old way, and went in.

* * *

The main room was exactly as they'd left it eleven years ago, and that was the second wrong thing.

Their mother's kettle still sat on its trivet. Juniper's slate still leaned against the hearthstone, chalk ghost-faint with the shapes of letters he'd practiced when he was six, before anyone knew what he was, before he'd learned to be quiet the way furniture is quiet. A cup sat on the table with a ring dried inside it. Wren picked it up and set it down again without deciding to. Her hands were doing things while the rest of her stood very still and did arithmetic.

Eleven years. No one had lived here. But no one had let it rot either. The roof didn't leak — she could see the fresh lashing on the ridge-poles where reeds had been replaced within the last few years. The floor had been swept. Recently. The dust lay thin and even over everything like a sheet laid over furniture in a house where the owner means to come back.

And under the thin dust, everywhere, the older marks. That was the third wrong thing, and it took her a moment to see it properly, because grief kept fogging the glass.

Chair legs dragged. Drawers pulled out and shoved back crooked. The seam of the mattress in the loft room split and restitched by someone in a hurry, with the wrong thread. A loose floorboard by the hearth pried up and pressed back down — she knew it was loose because she'd hidden her first stolen apples under it at age nine, and when she knelt and worked it up now, the cut edges were pale and new against the dark of the old wood.

Someone had gone through every inch of this cottage looking for something.

Looking for someone.

She sat back on her heels in the middle of the swept floor, and the cold came up through her skirts, and she made herself do the sums plainly, the way she did everything, because plainness was the only courage she'd ever had.

The searching had happened years ago. Not last month, not since the Proclamation — these marks were old, healed over, grown into the house like scars into skin. Someone had torn her childhood apart looking for Juniper, and then they had stopped, and then someone else — or the same one, patient as rot — had begun keeping the place. Maintaining it. Keeping the kettle on the trivet and the roof tight, keeping it *ready*, the way you keep a trap ready.

Keeping it ready for him to come home.

*They've always known,* she thought. *All these years I thought we'd buried it. I thought if we were careful enough, small enough, invisible enough—*

But that wasn't right either, and she flinched away from the thought before it finished forming, because there was a shape underneath it she'd spent eleven years not looking at, a shape with her own handwriting on it.

* * *

Here is what Wren Oyelaran-Brightwater told people, when anyone asked, which almost no one did:

When she was fourteen, the winter after their mother died, she went to stay with cousins near Tarnmouth. She came home in spring. She grew two inches that year and stopped eating eggs, and everyone said grief does strange things to a girl, and that was the whole story.

Here is what actually happened:

There were no cousins near Tarnmouth.

There was a woman who came to the cottage on a night of hard rain, a woman in traveling grays with a face like a closed book, and their mother — sick already, coughing blood into her kerchief when she thought the children couldn't hear — had gotten up off her deathbed to bar the door and argue with the woman in low, furious voices. Wren had listened through the floorboards with her ear against the cold planks, and the words she caught were these: *the list, the purge, the boy's ears, they'll hear of it, they'll come for the boy.*

And their mother saying: *over my body.*

And the woman saying, mild as milk: *that can be arranged, or we can discuss terms.*

Terms. Their mother had died nine days later, and Wren had gone away for a winter, and whatever was discussed in those nine days — whatever their mother signed or begged or bargained — Wren had been part of the payment. She remembered fragments the way you remember a fever: silver ink on vellum, so fine and bright it seemed lit from inside. A room that smelled of stone and candle smoke and something sweeter underneath, something like flowers rotting in honey. Her own hand shaking as she wrote her name, and a voice — a woman's voice, courteous as a hostess, patient as winter — explaining the arithmetic.

That was the word the voice used. Arithmetic. *It is only arithmetic, child. One small soul, lightly held, against one boy left entirely alone and entirely unnoticed. Weigh them.*

Wren had weighed them. She'd have weighed them a thousand times over. There had never been any other sum.

She'd come home in spring, and she'd been lighter. Not thinner — lighter, the way a purse is lighter. Something of hers stayed below the mountain, in a vault or a bottle or a ledger, drawing interest. And in exchange, Juniper had grown up unheard-of. Unlisted. A boy no hunter's list had ever found room for, in a kingdom that burned the gifted out of barns and hayricks every few years like rats.

He'd never known. He thought his invisibility was his own gift working — and maybe some of it was; he'd always been able to make himself small in rooms — but the crown's lists, the hunters' rolls, the parish tallies of the marked? Those were paper, and paper answered to contracts, and the contract was hers.

*They searched the cottage anyway,* she thought now, kneeling in the dust. *Years ago. Before Mother even died, or just after. They searched it, and they found—*

Found what?

She looked around the room with new eyes. If the searchers had been hunting Juniper, they'd have found nothing but a dead woman's kettles and a boy's old slates. Unless they'd found the thing she was suddenly, horribly sure they'd found: proof that a bargain existed. Evidence of the debt. Because whoever searched hadn't destroyed the cottage, hadn't taken it, hadn't moved in. They'd *kept* it. You keep what you expect to collect against.

Midwinter was coming. She felt it in her teeth the way you feel a storm coming, a pressure behind the eyes, and she didn't know why midwinter mattered, only that lately the word arrived in her head uninvited, wearing frost, like a bill slipped under a door.

*One small soul, lightly held.*

Lightly held. That was what the voice had promised. Held, though. Held means held.

* * *

She made herself useful, because usefulness was how she kept the fear from spreading. She built a fire in the hearth — the chimney drew clean, of course it drew clean, they maintained the flue too, the thorough bastards — and while the kindling caught she went through the cottage the way the searchers must have, but gently, apologizing to her mother's things under her breath.

In the loft, the mattress. In the loft, under the mattress, tucked into the rope webbing where their mother had hidden the rent money all her life: a packet of papers wrapped in oilcloth, and the oilcloth was dusty but the knot had been untied and retied, more than once, by hands that weren't theirs.

Wren carried it down to the firelight like it might bite.

Inside: their mother's marriage lines. Deed to the cottage, such as it was. And beneath those, folded small and many times, a letter in her mother's hand, addressed in a wobbling, medicine-blurred script: *For my daughter. Burn this unread, if you are wise. Read it if you are her.*

"You know me," Wren whispered, and unfolded it.

*My Wren,* it began. *If you are reading this, then either I am dead and you are home, or you have gone ahead and done the thing we spoke of, in which case I am sorry beyond all the sorry there is, and proud beyond all the proud, and do not tell your brother, ever, on any account, not while I live and not after.*

*The woman's name is Vess. Do not speak it aloud more than once. She holds your name now and will hold it kindly if you are sensible, but she is not kind, she is fair, and learn the difference young because it will save your life. What you carry below is not taken from you, only kept. Kept things can be reclaimed. That is all I will write, because paper is read by firelight in other houses than this one.*

*Your brother is the reason. Let him believe whatever lets him sleep. A boy who knows himself bought and paid for grows either cruel or broken, and he is neither, he is my Juniper, and he will grow up to be a keeper of accounts and an honest man, and he will never once suspect that his honesty was purchased. Forgive me. Forgive yourself sooner than I managed.*

*I love you past the edges of the world. Count well, my girl.*

No signature. Just a small inked bird in the corner, the wren, their namesake, drawn the way she used to draw it on the backs of envelopes.

Wren read it three times by the light of a fire in her mother's hearth, and then she did the thing she was best at in all the world, the thing that had kept her alive in the city and would keep her alive on this road: she folded the arithmetic away into the back room of her mind, banked it like a fire for the night, and got up to pack.

Because the letter changed nothing about what she had to do. It only told her the price had been real all along, and that the collector had never lost sight of the collateral, and that midwinter — that word again, that pressure behind her eyes — was closer than she'd let herself count.

* * *

She was rolling her blanket when she heard the gate.

Not the latch — the gate hinge, out front, the one that sang a flat note unless you oiled it, and nobody had oiled it in eleven years. Wren went still with the blanket half-rolled, one hand drifting to the knife at her belt, the other snuffing the lamp with two fingers.

Footsteps in the snow. Two sets. Unhurried. People who had every right to be where they were going, or who wanted very much to sound that way.

"—swear the smoke," a man's voice said. "Chimney draws clean, so somebody's lit a fire tonight."

"Or the house is haunted," said a second voice, younger, and laughed without much in it. "Crown pays the same either way, far as I'm concerned."

Wren's mouth went dry. Hunters. Not the mob kind from Corrow with their torches and their songs — these sounded bored and professional, which was infinitely worse. And they weren't following rumor. They were following smoke, in the middle of nowhere, to a cottage that shouldn't exist on any map worth burning.

Unless the cottage was on a different kind of map. Unless it had been on it for years, kept ready, watched, waiting for exactly this: a Brightwater coming home.

*Count well, my girl.*

She counted. Back window: barred, recently, and that answered the question of whether the keeping was friendly. Front: two men between her and the lane. Loft: no exit, and she wouldn't fit anyway anymore, she'd grown those two inches the year she came back lighter.

Which left the floorboard. Her apple-board. The gap beneath ran back under the hearthstone into the old cold-air crawl their father had dug for storing roots, and it came out — she prayed it still came out — behind the alder thicket above the goat pen.

Wren pried the board up, silent as water, and lowered herself into the dark while the front door handle, that new good iron, began very quietly to turn.

Above her, muffled by floorboards, a man's voice said, pleasantly, "Miss? Crown business. Best open up."

And beneath her feet, in the cold earth-smell of the crawl, Wren began to move toward the mountains — toward Corrow, toward the undercity, toward wherever her brother was — doing the only sum that had ever mattered.

One brother. Everything else.

The arithmetic was simple. Getting out alive was the hard part.
