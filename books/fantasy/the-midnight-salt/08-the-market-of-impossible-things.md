## 8. The Market of Impossible Things

Corrow at night smelled like a lie told by a city that believed itself. Above ground: lamp-oil and roasting chestnuts and the clean river smell the aldermen paid to have pumped through the squares. Below: tallow smoke, wet stone, and something older underneath both, a sourness like coins held too long in a sweating hand.

Juniper had been down here two days and had learned three things.

First: the undercity did not forget you if you let it remember. He'd spent twelve years perfecting his trick of stillness—the gift pressed flat, the mind quiet, the whole self folded small enough to slip past attention—but down here, stillness marked him worse than noise. The beggars by the sluice gates watched him pass with the flat patience of people who knew what it meant when someone tried not to be seen.

Second: everyone down here was selling something they couldn't afford to lose.

Third: the guild was not dead. It was only hiding, the way he was hiding, and it hid better.

He found the first sign on the second night, chalked inside the arch of a drain where no honest person would look: a salt circle, broken at the top, with a single vertical line drawn through it. The old trade-mark. Odell had taught him to read it when he was nine—*that means goods moving quiet, Juniper. Means someone's trading what shouldn't be traded, and doing it badly enough to leave marks.*

*And when we see it?*

*We note it in the ledger,* Odell had said, *and we do nothing, because the Saltgate keeps its own door clean. But you remember the mark. A keeper who doesn't know where the dirty doors are is a keeper guarding one room in a burning house.*

Juniper stood in the drain-arch until his breath stopped fogging, then followed the mark.

It led him down. Everything down here led down; Corrow had been built on top of an older Corrow, which had been built on top of a drowned village nobody mentioned, and the stairs between the layers were narrow and slick and lit by lamps that burned a greenish color because cheap oil stretched with spirits burned green. His boots found water in the low places. Somewhere behind a wall, machinery turned over and over without accomplishing anything, the way machinery does when the men who tend it have stopped believing in it.

The marks came more often now. Chalk on brick. Scratched into a barrel stave. Once, stitched in grey thread along the hem of a coat hanging abandoned on a nail—a message for anyone desperate enough to be checking hems.

At the bottom of a stair that had no business existing—it went down through a cellar that belonged to a chandler's shop that had burned out years ago, according to the scorch marks and the smell—he came to a door.

It was a good door. That was the first thing he noticed, before the wards or the watchfulness or the man beside it. Oak, iron-banded, hung true on its hinges. Someone had loved this door once, or at least respected it.

Juniper put his palm flat against the wood, the way he'd done ten thousand times with the Saltgate, and listened.

The door remembered being made. They always did. Doors were vain that way—they held their making close, the way beautiful people hold their youth. This one remembered a joiner with sawdust in his beard, a workshop above a tannery, a wife who brought him beer at noon and complained about the smell. Ordinary memories. Warm ones.

But under the warmth, the door remembered something else: hands knocking in patterns. *Salt-salt-shadow. Salt-salt-second-chance.* Knocked so many times the wood had worn smooth at the spots, the way a church step wears hollow from knees.

The knock-code. The guild's oldest.

Juniper stepped back and looked at the man beside the door properly for the first time. Big, in the way of men whose size was the cheapest tool they owned. Wool cap pulled low. Hands like ham hocks, scarred across every knuckle.

"You're leaking," the man said.

"I'm sorry?"

"Leaking." He tapped his own temple. "You've got your gift shoved down so tight I can hear it creaking from here. Like a barrel with a bad stave. Nobody down here stands next to a door looking like they're praying unless they're listening." He tilted his head. "You're one of Odell's."

The name landed in Juniper's chest like a dropped anchor. "You knew him?"

"Knew of him. Knew *of* plenty of Saltgate keepers." The man's eyes were small and brown and entirely unfooled. "None of them ever came down here. That was the arrangement. We stay dirty so they can stay clean." He spat, with feeling. "Then somebody burns their house down and suddenly the clean ones come knocking."

"My name is Juniper Brightwater. I need passage."

"Everybody needs passage. Passage is the one thing down here that's never free." He jerked his chin at the door. "You know the knock?"

"Salt-salt-shadow," Juniper said. "But I'm not here to trade. I'm here to find what's left of the guild."

That got him a longer look. "Boy," the man said, almost gently, "you're standing in it."

* * *

Inside was not what he expected, which was itself a lesson. He had imagined—without admitting he was imagining it—a smaller version of the guildhouse. Desks. Ledgers. The careful lamplight of people doing sacred work badly.

Instead there was a tavern.

Or something shaped like a tavern, sunk into the bones of the drowned village, all low ceilings and candle-grease and a long counter scarred by decades of elbows. Maybe forty people packed into it, and every single one of them, Juniper understood within three heartbeats, was gifted. Not loudly—not fire from fingertips or prophecy shouted over the dice. Quietly, the way most gifts actually lived. A woman dealing cards whose hands never lost their warmth no matter how cold the room. Two men arguing over a map while the map subtly rearranged its rivers to favor each man's argument in turn. A child asleep on a bench, and the candle nearest her burning steady as a held breath.

A market. Not of things. Of impossibilities.

"Drink?" asked the big man, who had introduced himself during the walk in as Cutter, which Juniper suspected was a job description rather than a name.

"No, thank you."

"They'll think you're a hunter, refusing a drink."

Juniper took the drink. It was cider, sharp and cloudy, and it tasted like apples that had grown somewhere with bad soil and good intentions. Around them the room's noise continued, but he felt the texture of it change—conversations tilting, attention sliding toward him and away again, the whole room performing unconcern so badly it was nearly a shout.

An empty chair waited at the corner table. It had been waiting, he realized, since he walked in.

He sat.

The woman already there was old in the specific way of people who had chosen each year deliberately. Silver hair cropped short. A shawl that might once have been fine. Her eyes were the pale grey of winter light on ice, and they went through him like a gimlet through soft wood.

"Brightwater," she said. Not a question.

"How do you know my name?"

"Because names are stock-in-trade down here, boy, and yours has been circulating since the guildhouse ash was still warm." She pushed a second cup toward him. "Maren. I kept the eastern book, back when there were books to keep. Sit. Drink. Tell me what you traded to get down my stair, and don't lie, because the candles will tell me if you do."

"The candles?"

She nodded at the nearest one. Its flame leaned toward Juniper very slightly, like a dog scenting meat.

He set down the cup. "Nothing. Yet. Your doorman stopped me before I could make an offer."

"Mm. And what were you going to offer?"

"A memory," Juniper said. "One of Odell's. I have—" His voice caught, and he hated it, and he kept going anyway. "I have a great many of those, and I am discovering I cannot carry them all where I'm going."

Maren was quiet for a moment. Around her, the candle flame stood perfectly straight, attentive.

"Which memory?" she asked.

"The last lesson he gave me." He hadn't known he was going to say it until it was out. "The closing rite. He taught it to me four days before the hunters came, and he wouldn't say why, and he looked—" *like a man paying off a debt*, he thought, "—tired. Tired in a way I'd never seen. I keep replaying it. It's eating the other memories around it. Better it should feed someone who can use it."

"And what do you want for it? You said passage. Passage to where?"

"The mountain."

The room did not go silent. That would have been too obvious, too theatrical. Instead the silence arrived the way weather arrives—gradually, and then all at once, conversations thinning out at their edges until the only sound was the child on the bench breathing and the candles burning their steady, listening flames.

Maren laughed. It was not a kind laugh, but it wasn't cruel either; it was the laugh of someone watching a young person walk into exactly the trap she'd once walked into.

"The mountain," she repeated. "Of course the mountain. They always want the mountain."

"*They?* Who else has wanted it?"

"Two others this season. Both gifted. Both running. Both asking after the Undercourt like it's a place you buy a ticket to." She leaned forward, and up close her eyes had flecks in them, tiny movements, as though something small lived in each iris and swam. "Sit still, Brightwater, and listen, because I'm going to tell you things your Odell should have told you years ago and didn't, and you can hate him for it later the way I do."

"He's dead," Juniper said quietly. "They killed him holding the door."

Something moved behind Maren's face—grief, old and practiced, the grief of a woman who had buried too many colleagues to let it arrive fresh anymore. "I know," she said. "We heard. The whole undercity heard. Hunters don't burn a guildhouse quietly; fire makes its own announcements." She touched the table with two fingers, briefly, the way you'd touch a grave-marker. "So. The dead teach faster than the living. Listen.

"You know what the guild trades. Souls, shadows, second chances. You've written the entries—you wrote half the eastern entries yourself, I'd wager, in that pretty invisible hand of yours. What the ledger doesn't show, because Odell kept it out of even the keepers' heads where he could, is *where the goods go.*"

"Buyers. People who want—"

"People who want, yes. But a trade needs two ends, Brightwater, and the guild was never more than the middleman. Every soul sold above ground travels *down*. Every shadow pawned, every chance swapped, every bargain struck at a crossroads at midnight—all of it clears through the court beneath the mountain. The guild doesn't own the market. The guild is the market's *front*. Has been since before Corrow had walls."

Juniper's hands had gone cold around the cup. "The Saltgate—"

"—was built to manage the flow. Built by your family, if the oldest stories are true, though the oldest stories cost more than I pay to hear them." Maren waved a hand. "The point is this. You want to go to the mountain. Fine. Everyone wants something from the mountain eventually; that's why it's rich and the rest of us are down here drinking bad cider. But you don't walk in the front door of the Undercourt, and you don't climb the pilgrim stair, and you don't hire Brannoc."

"Why not Brannoc?"

"Because Brannoc sells to whoever's buying, and right now the buyers are witch-hunters with crown coin and a list of names." Her smile showed teeth but no warmth. "Including, I'm told, yours."

The candle flame nearest Juniper bent toward him again. He realized distantly that it had been leaning since he sat down—that all the candles in the room were leaning slightly, the way grass leans toward a thing walking through it.

"What do you mean, a list?"

"I mean the hunt isn't a purge, boy. Purges are for cleaning. This is a *collection*. They're not killing the gifted, mostly. They're gathering them. Ask yourself why the crown wants live witches, and ask yourself why the answer rhymes with 'mountain,' and then ask yourself why a Saltgate keeper—one of the invisible ones, the ones nobody's supposed to know exist—is on the list at all."

The cider sat untouched in Juniper's stomach like a stone. He thought of Wren. He thought of the locket she wore and would not take off. He thought of the torn page in the old ledger, edges burned, humming with grief, and Odell's face when Juniper had asked about it.

"There's a phrase," Maren said, softer now. "You'll hear it below, if you get below. The brokers use it. *The debt at midwinter.* Say it to yourself a few times so it doesn't frighten you fresh when you hear it proper."

"What debt?"

"If I knew, I'd be rich instead of old." She spread her hands. "But I know this much, from thirty years of keeping books: debts don't come due on their own. Something was borrowed. Something large. And midwinter is when the collector calls, and collectors below don't send letters."

Juniper set the cup down. His hands were steady. He was surprised by that.

"I'll pay for passage," he said. "The memory I offered. Odell's last lesson."

"That memory's worth more than passage," Maren said. "Passage plus a question answered, truthfully, by anyone in this room you choose."

"Then passage plus this: how do I get into the Undercourt without walking in the front door?"

Maren studied him for a long time. The candles leaned. Somewhere in the dark behind the counter, a bottle clinked against a glass, and Juniper heard—faintly, the way you hear rain coming—the bottle remembering being full of better wine.

"There's a woman named Sef," Maren said finally. "Runs the tide-gate at the drowned harbor, the one under the old chandler's row. Twice a month the Undercourt sends up a barge for collections—souls, shadows, whatever's owed. Sef can put a body in an empty collection crate, if the body's small and the bribe is right." Her mouth twisted. "The bribe is never right. It's always terrible. That's how you know the price is honest."

"I'll manage."

"You'll manage," Maren agreed, without belief. Then she reached across the table and caught his wrist—her grip stronger than it looked—and turned his hand over, palm up, studying it as though reading lines no palmist had ever charted. Whatever she saw there made her go still.

"Gatekeeper's callus," she murmured. "On a Brightwater. Well." She released him and sat back. "That explains the list."

"It explains the list," Juniper said, "how?"

But Maren only shook her head, and the candles all straightened at once, like soldiers standing down, and the room's noise came flooding back in—dice and argument and the child laughing in her sleep—as though a held breath had finally been let go.

"Ask your sister," Maren said. "If you have a sister. If she's still alive. Ask her what she did the winter she was seventeen, and then come back and I'll sell you the rest of the story."

The floor seemed to tilt. "What winter—"

"Go," Maren said, not unkindly. "Sef opens the gate at moonset. And Brightwater—" she raised her cup to him, and her swimming-flecked eyes were, for one unguarded moment, full of pity, "—whatever you traded to be born invisible, somebody down below remembers the receipt."

* * *

He found Sef by the smell of salt and the sound of black water lapping at stone that had been a street once. She was younger than he expected, missing an arm from the elbow, and she took his offer—the memory, sealed in a stoppered vial Maren supplied, humming faintly with Odell's tired voice saying *just in case, Juniper, learn it just in case*—without ceremony, weighing it in her palm like a plum.

"Crate's small," she said. "Barge leaves at moonset. If you sneeze, they'll hear you, and what hears you won't be human." She paused, vial halfway to her pocket. "You're going down to settle something."

"Yes."

"Everyone says that." She looked at the black water. "Nobody comes back settled."

Juniper stood at the edge of the drowned street while she made her arrangements, and below him the harbor water lay still and deep and patient, and far beneath it—so far he could not truly hear it, only feel it, the way you feel a storm over the horizon—he sensed the mountain. Listening. Counting. Keeping books of its own.

*Midwinter,* he thought. *The debt at midwinter.*

And underneath the fear, quieter and worse, Wren's face at seventeen, waving goodbye from a doorway, saying *back by spring, Junny, I promise*—

—and coming home lighter.
