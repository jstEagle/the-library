## 9. Reunion, Unevenly

The cottage at the edge of the salt marsh had one door, one window, and a chimney that remembered every fire ever lit in it. Juniper heard them all as he waited on the doorstep—thirty years of peat smoke and fish stew and, underneath it all, two children laughing. The house remembered his childhood better than he did.

He'd been standing there for an hour. He could hear Wren inside. He could hear her breathing, her boots pacing the same four steps over and over, her heartbeat doing something arrhythmic that he recognized from the ledgers—the rhythm of someone about to open a door they'd been dreading for twelve years.

He knocked anyway. It seemed politer than listening his way through the wall.

The door opened before his knuckles fell the second time.

For a moment neither of them moved. Twelve years is a long time to be a stranger to your own face; hers was there in his, the same sharp jaw, the same marsh-grey eyes, aged differently because she'd lived hers above ground where the sun could get at it. Her hair had gone copper at the temples. She looked like their mother. That undid him more than anything.

"You look terrible," Wren said.

"I've been sleeping in ditches."

"You always looked terrible in ditches." And then she hit him—not a blow, just a collision, arms around him hard enough to crack a rib, her face buried in his shoulder, and Juniper stood in the doorway of the house that remembered everything and let himself be held by the only person left who'd known him before the ledgers, before Odell, before the Saltgate swallowed him whole and called it employment.

* * *

Inside, she fed him because that was what Wren did with feelings too large for words. Fish stew, bread gone stale but toasted, tea brewed strong enough to stand a spoon in. She sat across the table from him and watched him eat with an intensity that would have embarrassed anyone who wasn't used to being invisible.

"The hunters came through Corrow," Juniper said. "I took the marsh road."

"Everyone takes the marsh road now. Half of it's underwater and the other half's forgotten itself, so it suits everyone fine." She refilled his bowl without asking. "Tell me what happened at the guildhouse. All of it."

So he told her. Odell teaching him the closing rite with hands that shook. The wards thinning like ice on a spring pond. The doorknob remembering fire the night before the fire came. He told her about the hunters' iron lanterns and the way the guildhouse screamed when it burned—a building with that much memory inside it screams, did you know that—and he told her about Odell holding the door, and how the last thing Odell said was *go*, not *save me*, not *be brave*, just *go*, the way you'd tell a dog to leave a room where something bad was happening.

Wren didn't cry. Her hands went still around her cup, and stayed still, and that was worse.

"He knew," she said quietly. "Odell knew they were coming."

"He prepared me. He wouldn't explain why. I thought—I thought it was age, or caution, or—"

"It wasn't caution." She said it flatly, and then caught herself, and made her voice lighter, which fooled no one, least of all him. "Eat your stew."

"Wren."

"Eat."

He ate. But while he ate he listened, because that was what he did instead of eating sometimes, and the cottage was full of voices. The table remembered their father sanding its grain. The kettle remembered being bought secondhand with coins counted twice. And across the table, close enough to touch, Wren's locket—

The locket was speaking.

It was a small thing, tarnished silver, hanging against her collarbone where it had hung since she was fifteen. He'd never listened to it before. You didn't listen to family's things; there were courtesies even among objects. But grief had cracked something open in him since Corrow, since the market under the city where he'd traded a memory of Odell's laugh for passage north, and now the locket's voice slid into him unbidden:

*Silver ink. A signature pressed into vellum, the pen scratching like something alive. Cold air rising out of the ground. A voice like a ledger closing—*and the name, the signature, written in silver ink that glowed faintly, wetly, like moonlight poured into letters—*

Juniper set down his spoon.

"What?" Wren said. Too fast. "What is it?"

"Your locket," he said. "When did you—" But he stopped, because the memory kept unspooling, and it wasn't silver ink he was hearing now but a date, a season, snow on a threshold, and Wren much younger, standing in a doorway that breathed cold, saying words in a language the locket didn't understand but stored faithfully, syllable by syllable, the way lockets store everything they're present for.

The winter. The winter she'd gone away.

"Wren," he said carefully, "the winter I was eleven. When you went to Aunt Sable's in Harrowmere."

Her face did three things in one second—blankness, calculation, guilt—and landed on a smile so badly assembled it would have failed inspection at any market stall in Corrow.

"That winter. Nothing happened that winter. It was boring. Harrowmere is boring. That's why nobody talks about it."

"Harrowmere is famous for its glassworks."

"Well. Boring glass."

Juniper looked at his sister, whom he loved more than the Saltgate, more than the ledgers, more than the gift that had shaped every hour of his life, and felt the distance between them open like a page torn out. Because she was lying. She was lying badly, which meant she was lying desperately, which meant whatever she was lying about was heavy enough to bend her spine.

And her locket kept whispering. *Silver ink. Silver ink.*

* * *

Later, by candlelight, she asked about his gift—or tried to.

"They say the gifted can hear things. Objects. Is that true? What do they say?"

Juniper turned his cup slowly on the table. "You've known since I was nine. I told you myself. I heard Mother's ring crying after she died, and you put your hand over my mouth and said never, ever tell anyone."

"I remember." A pause. "Do you still hear it? The ring?"

"No. I sold my memories of her years ago, mostly. What's left doesn't speak anymore." He watched her flinch. Yes—there it was again, the flinch, quick as a bird startled off a wire. Every time he mentioned the gift directly, she flinched. Not disgust. Fear. Fear with a shape he almost recognized. "Wren. Why did you ask me to stop using it, back then? You never said why. Just that if anyone found out, they'd take me."

"People were right to be afraid. Look what happened to Odell. Look what happened to the guildhouse."

"That's new fear. This is old fear. You've been afraid of my hearing since before the hunts, before the crown, before any of it." He leaned forward. "Since the winter you came back lighter."

"Lighter?"

"Aunt Sable wrote to Father. She said you'd come home thinner. I heard Father read the letter aloud to Mother. *Thinner,* she wrote. But that's not what the letter said. I heard the paper under the ink, and the paper said *lighter.* Like something had been taken out of you and the writing couldn't find a polite word for it."

The silence stretched so long the candle guttered and recovered.

"You were a child," Wren said finally. "Children hear things wrong."

"I don't hear wrong. That's rather the whole problem with me."

She stood up abruptly and began stacking dishes that didn't need stacking, moving them from the table to the basin with the fierce economy of someone who needed her hands occupied or they'd start shaking. "You're exhausted. You've walked half the country and slept in ditches and watched your mentor burn. Sleep. We'll talk tomorrow."

"We'll talk tomorrow," Juniper agreed, "about everything."

"Everything," she said, in the tone of someone promising a payment they intended to default on.

* * *

But Juniper had spent twelve years balancing ledgers, and he knew a hidden column when he saw one. So he lay awake in the narrow bed of his childhood—he could feel the mattress remembering his boyhood weight, lighter then, lighter than this—and he listened to the house settle around him, and through the thin wall he listened to Wren sit up past midnight in the main room.

He heard her take off the locket. Heard the chain whisper across wool. Heard her hold it, and hold it, and once, barely, half-swallowed: "Not yet. Not yet. He can't—" and then nothing, because she'd bitten the sentence in half.

*He can't.* He can't what? Hear? Know?

The locket, resting now on the table beside her elbow, murmured on regardless, faithful and oblivious. Silver ink. A signature. A cold draft rising from somewhere beneath the world, and a voice like a ledger closing, and Wren—fifteen-year-old Wren, or seventeen-year-old Wren, some version of her from the vanished winter—saying, in a steady voice that must have cost her everything: *"Then it's done. Take it. But he lives. He lives, and no one finds him, ever."*

Juniper lay in the dark with his heart going like a fist on a door.

*No one finds him, ever.*

The hunters had come for the guild. The hunters had burned twelve years of records and a man who held doors. And somewhere under all of it, threaded through it like silver ink through vellum, was a bargain his sister had made in a winter he'd never questioned, buying something with a currency he hadn't known she possessed.

He got up quietly and stood in the doorway of the main room. Wren sat with her back to him, shoulders rigid, the locket cupped in both hands like a coal she was trying to keep alight.

"I can hear it," he said softly. "The locket. I'm sorry. I've never listened to your things before tonight, and I wish to God I hadn't started."

She didn't turn around. "How much?"

"Enough to know the winter wasn't boring."

"Juniper—"

"There was a contract. There was a signature in silver ink. And there was a price, and the price bought—" His voice caught. "Bought *me.* Didn't it."

For a long moment the only sound was the candle burning and the house holding its breath around them, every joist and nail and door-hinge listening the way he listened, waiting to see what would be said next and whether it would be true.

Wren turned around. Her eyes were dry, which frightened him more than tears would have. Tears meant the truth was still ahead of her. Dry eyes meant she'd already lived it, alone, for twelve years, carrying it the way he carried the ledger—close, constant, unspeakable.

"Sit down," she said.

"Wren—"

"*Sit down,* Juniper." She set the locket on the table between them, open, facing him. Inside, he saw for the first time, there was no portrait—only a scrap of vellum, blank to any ordinary eye. But it wasn't blank. It was humming. Faintly, sweetly, terribly, it hummed with a signature in silver ink, and beneath the signature, so small he had to lean close to catch it, a single line of contract-language that the locket had memorized along with everything else:

*Payable at midwinter.*

"What happens at midwinter?" Juniper asked.

Wren closed her hand over the locket, and the humming stopped, and outside, far off across the frozen marshes, something howled that was not a wolf.
