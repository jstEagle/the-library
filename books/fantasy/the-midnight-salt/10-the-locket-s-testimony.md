## 10. The Locket's Testimony

Wren slept in the loft of the cooper's abandoned workshop like someone who had learned to sleep anywhere and trust nothing: on her side, boots on, one hand curled around the cold iron poker she'd pulled from the hearth before lying down.

Juniper sat below in the dark with her locket in his palm.

He had not taken it. He wanted to be clear about that, even if there was no one to be clear to. She had left it out—hung it over a nail by her bedroll the way their mother used to hang hers, a habit so old it predated memory—and he had simply stood looking at it too long, the way you stand at the edge of a well. Then his fingers had done what his fingers always did. They had reached for the thing that remembers.

The locket was brass, cheap brass gone green at the hinge, oval as a robin's egg. Inside, when you opened it, there should have been two portraits. There were none. Just a shallow hollow on each side, worn smooth, holding nothing but shadow and the faint ghost of glue.

But objects don't keep only what they're shown. Objects keep everything that happens near them, the way river stones keep the shape of the current long after the water has gone somewhere else. And this locket had been held against Wren Brightwater's heart for eleven years, through things Juniper could not begin to guess at.

He knew he shouldn't. He heard Odell's voice in his head—the old man's voice, which was strange now, because Odell was ash and a locked door and a key in Juniper's pocket, and dead men shouldn't still have opinions—saying, *Some doors you knock on, boy, and some you leave shut because what knocks back is worse.*

But Wren had flinched from him tonight. Wren, who used to let him hold her hand and read the memories of every stone they walked past, narrating the road like a bedtime story. Wren had looked at his gift the way people look at a wound, and she had lied to him about the winter she disappeared, badly, the way she lied about eating the last honey biscuit when she was nine—chin up, eyes steady, entirely unconvincing.

And her locket had hummed.

Not loudly. Not even really a sound. More a pressure behind his ears, a taste at the back of his throat, the way an object full to bursting announces itself to him. Most things murmured. A doorknob remembered its maker's chapped hands; a coin remembered three pockets and one desperate gamble; a spoon remembered soup, mostly, endlessly, soup. But some few things *hummed*, dense with what they carried, and he had spent twelve years learning to walk past humming things without touching them, because once you pressed your gift into something that full, you didn't get to stop listening until it finished.

The locket was the fullest object he had ever held.

Outside, Corrow's undercity dripped its endless drip. Somewhere above, the witch-hunters were burning names off doors. Somewhere far away, a guildhouse that had been his whole world for twelve years was a black scar in the salt flats, and Odell was under it, and the ledger was gone.

Juniper closed his hand around the locket and opened himself.

* * *

It came all at once, the way floods come.

Cold. That was first. Cold like the underside of a mountain, cold that had never met the sun, and beneath the cold a vast slow breathing that wasn't air moving but something older than air deciding whether to bother. The locket remembered being warm—it remembered skin, Wren's skin, the pulse at her throat hammering against the brass—but the warmth was thin, a candle in a cavern.

Then light. Bottled light. Hundreds of small glass vessels hanging from chains in a darkness so complete the lights seemed to float free, each one glowing a different color, and the locket remembered what they were because Wren had been told, standing there, shaking, while something in a silver gown explained it with the courtesy of a banker:

*Souls,* the queen said. Or rather, the locket remembered the queen saying it, in a voice like water finding its level. *Each one priced honestly. We are very honest here, child. It is our single virtue and our entire economy.*

Wren's hands. Juniper saw them from the locket's vantage point, hung at her collarbone, and they were trembling, and they were also—this was the part that made his chest seize—*steady at the fingertips*. The way her hands got when she had decided something. When she'd set the cottage fire herself at fourteen because the fever had the sheep and there was nothing else to be done. Decided and grieving and going forward anyway.

A table of black stone. A document laid upon it, longer than any letter, written in ink that caught the bottled light and threw it back wrong—not reflecting the colors but drinking them, silver-dark, hungry ink. A pen beside it, white as bone.

*Read it,* the queen said. *All of it. I insist. People who sign what they haven't read make poor neighbors, and I intend for us to be neighbors a very long time.*

And Wren read. The locket couldn't hold every word—it kept the shapes of sentences more than the sentences themselves—but it kept the ones she read twice, and it kept the ones she read aloud in a whisper, testing them, the way you test ice.

*...in consideration of services rendered and services to be rendered...*

*...the protection of one (1) individual, designated herein as the Brightwater boy, to extend to all agents of crown and church, all hunters commissioned or otherwise...*

*...until such time as the debt is called...*

There it was. His name—or almost his name, reduced to *the Brightwater boy*, as if he were a parcel. Every hunter who had passed their village in the old purge years and never once glanced at the two children in the cottage doorway. Every checkpoint he'd ever slipped through without papers. Every night the guildhouse wards had stood thick and easy around him while other gifted children ran. It hadn't been luck. It hadn't been his invisibility, his carefulness, his twelve years of keeping his head down and his gift folded up small.

It had been bought. He had been bought.

And the price—

The locket held the price the way it held everything: completely, mercilessly. Wren had read the clause aloud, both times, her voice going flatter on the second reading.

*...the undersigned conveys to Her Majesty Queen Vess of the Undercourt, in perpetuity upon collection, the whole of her soul, defined per court custom as the animating essence, the accumulated self, and all rights of continuation thereafter...*

Her soul. Not a memory, not a shadow, not a talent. The whole of her. To be collected at midwinter—some midwinter; the contract had been patient—and until then she would carry the locket, which was not a keepsake but a lien. The queen had said so, kindly, pressing the little brass oval into Wren's palm and closing her fingers over it:

*A token. You'll find it grows warm when we think of you. And we will think of you, child. We are very attentive creditors.*

Juniper realized his cheeks were wet. He was sitting in a dark workshop in Corrow's undercity, holding his sister's locket, crying without any memory of having started, and the locket wasn't finished.

Because signatures remember.

That was the thing about documents—he'd catalogued enough trades at the Saltgate to know. Paper forgot fast, but the moment of signing lived in whatever touched the page, and the pen had touched the page, and the pen had been bone-white and borrowed, and the locket had been pressed to Wren's lips afterward like a charm against nightmares, and so the locket held the signature itself.

Silver ink. He watched it happen inside the memory: Wren's hand descending, the nib touching black stone, and the name flowing out of her in silver—*Wren Brightwater*, in her own handwriting, the same confident slant she used on shopping lists—and the ink drinking the bottled light as it dried, and something in the deep breathing of the mountain changing rhythm. Satisfied. A ledger entry completed.

Eleven years ago. The winter she went away. The winter she came back lighter, and thinner, and quieter, and neither of them talked about it because she said she'd been visiting Mother's cousin in Lowmarsh and Juniper was fifteen and self-absorbed and believed her.

She had been underground. She had been in this cathedral of bottled souls, seventeen years old, selling everything she was so that the hunters would look at her brother and see nothing worth looking at.

* * *

He came back to himself in stages. First the cold of the workshop floor through his coat. Then the taste of silver ink that wasn't there, coating his teeth. Then the sound of his own breathing, too loud, and the terrible ordinary fact of the dripping undercity, the dark rafters, the sleeping loft above him where his sister breathed with one hand around an iron poker.

The locket lay open in his palm, warm. Warmer than body-warm. The queen had told the truth about that, at least: it grew warm when they thought of her. Which meant, he understood with a lurch, that it was warm now because they were thinking of her now. Because midwinter was close. Because the debt was coming due, and somewhere beneath the mountain, an honest queen was reviewing her accounts.

He thought about setting the locket back on its nail. He thought about going up to the loft, waking Wren, and asking her—gently, without accusation, giving her every chance to lie again. He imagined how her face would move. He couldn't do it. He couldn't watch her decide whether to keep lying to him. Some doors you knock on, and some you leave shut because what knocks back is worse.

Instead he did what he had always done with unbearable knowledge: he wrote it. No ledger anymore—that was ash in the salt flats—but he found a carpenter's pencil stub in the workshop shavings and a board with a planed face, and he wrote, in the shorthand of guild clerks, the terms as the locket had given them. Soul conveyed. Collection at midwinter. Protection purchased: one Brightwater boy.

He stared at the last line a long time.

*One Brightwater boy.*

Every hunt he'd ever evaded. Every close call he'd ever thanked his own cleverness for surviving—the patrol outside Corrow when he was nineteen, the informer in the market town, the whole long careful architecture of his hidden life. None of it had been his doing. He had been a debt provision. A clause. Someone's collateral arrangement, kept safe the way you keep a valuable item in a vault, and the vault just happened to be his own caution, and he had been proud of it.

Twelve years at the Saltgate, recording other people's impossible trades—souls, shadows, second chances—and he had never once suspected that the largest trade of his life had been made about him, without him, by a girl of seventeen who then came home and made him dinner and never said a word.

And Odell. Odell, who had taken him in, trained him, taught him the gate rites, given him the iron key. Had Odell known? The torn page from the oldest ledger rose in his mind, its burned edges humming with grief, and Odell's face when asked about it—shutting like a door. *Some histories are load-bearing,* the old man had said once. *You pull them out and see what falls.* What else had been load-bearing? How much of Juniper's whole life was a structure built over a bargain?

The locket pulsed faintly against his palm. Warm. Warmer than before.

They were thinking of her harder now.

Midwinter. He counted days the way he'd once balanced columns: nine, maybe ten, depending on the roads, and the roads above were full of hunters, and the hunters were hunting him—except they weren't, not really, they were hunting the gift in him, and the gift was the only currency he had that might buy his sister back, if souls could be traded at all, if debts could be paid by anyone but the debtor.

There was a market in this city. Brannoc had said so—the smuggler's message scratched under a bridge stone, the guild remnant's sign, the phrase that had been following him for days now like a dog: *the debt at midwinter.* He'd thought it meant something general. Something proverbial. Now the words rearranged themselves into a date, a contract, a collection notice with his family's name on it.

Above him, the loft creaked. Wren turned in her sleep, and the poker clinked softly against the floorboards, and Juniper blew out a breath he felt in his whole chest, wiped his face with his sleeve, and put the locket back on its nail exactly where it had hung.

Then he stood in the dark and made himself say it plainly, silently, the way you enter a figure in a ledger so it can't hide:

My sister sold her soul for me. In ten days they've come to collect. And I am the only person in the world who can't ask her why without watching her break.

The locket glowed on its nail, faint as a coal under ash.

Warm, and getting warmer.
