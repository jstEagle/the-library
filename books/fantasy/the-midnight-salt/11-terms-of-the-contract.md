## 11. Terms of the Contract

Wren had lied to her brother for twelve years, and she had gotten good at it in the way that people get good at anything they do out of love: thoroughly, and with a growing sickness underneath.

She was not good enough for the locket.

She'd seen his face when he set it on the table between them—the inn's back room, one candle, snow ticking against the shutters like something patient asking to be let in. He hadn't shouted. That was the worst part. Juniper never shouted. He just looked at her with those grey eyes that had spent a lifetime listening to objects instead of people, and he said, "It remembers silver ink. It remembers your hand shaking."

"Wren." His voice was quiet. "Tell me what you sold."

The candle guttered. Somewhere below them, the innkeeper's dog turned in its sleep, and Wren heard its collar clink against the floorboards, and she thought absurdly, *he can hear that too*, before she understood that no—he couldn't hear it now, because he wasn't listening, because every part of him was aimed at her like a lamp.

She could lie again. She opened her mouth to do it, and what came out instead was the truth, because apparently after twelve years her body had simply decided it was done.

"I sold my soul."

The words didn't sound like hers. They sounded like something read off a page, which was fitting, because that was essentially what they were.

Juniper didn't move. The candle flame stood very straight, as if it too wanted to hear this properly.

"To whom?" he said.

"The court beneath the mountain." She wrapped her hands around her cup of cider gone cold hours ago, just to have something to hold that wasn't herself. "Queen Vess. The soul-broker. You've heard the name—everyone has, in the way you hear a name and don't believe it's real."

"Everyone sells things at that market," Juniper said slowly. "Memories. Shadows. Years off the end of a life. I traded a memory of Odell three days ago just to find you." His voice caught slightly on Odell's name—grief still raw, still bleeding—and then steadied. "But souls are different. Souls aren't for sale."

"No," Wren agreed. "They're for *rent*."

* * *

She told it in order, because disorder would have been mercy and she did not want mercy.

"You were nine," she said. "I was thirteen. Do you remember the winter of the grey fever?"

He frowned. "You went away. To Aunt Sable's, in the lowlands. You came back in spring."

"Aunt Sable doesn't exist." She said it flatly, watching it land. "There was no aunt. There was a man who came through Corrow buying children—not their bodies, not their labor. Their gifts. The old purge, Juni. Before the witch-hunters wore crowns' colors, when they worked freelance, and the crown paid bounties by the head. They took gifted children and—" Her stomach turned over. She made herself keep going. "They didn't kill them. Killing is fast and wastes the goods. They sold them south, over the mountains, to buyers who liked them alive."

Juniper had gone very pale in the candlelight. "I don't remember any of that."

"No," Wren said. "You don't. Because I bought that too."

* * *

Here is what happened, as she laid it out for him piece by piece, like a body on a table:

In the winter Wren was thirteen, men came to Corrow with a list. On the list, among forty other names, was *Brightwater, J., age 9*. A neighbor—a woman named Maren Cobb whose son played with Juniper at the tannery—had reported that the Brightwater boy talked to hinges. Talked to locks. Made dead lamps flicker awake when he walked past and asked them nicely.

Their mother was already dead a year. Their father drank and worked and noticed nothing, which was, Wren understood even then, its own kind of protection and its own kind of abandonment. So when the men came, there was no one to stand in the doorway but a thirteen-year-old girl.

"They were going to take you that night," she said. "I saw them marking our door. Chalk. Three strokes and a circle—you know the mark, you've seen it on burned houses since. And I ran. Not away. *Toward.* Everyone runs from the mountain, Juni. I ran to it, because Maren Cobb got drunk once and told my mother about a door under the world, and a queen down there who buys anything, absolutely anything, if the price is honest."

"And she bought—" Juniper started.

"She didn't buy anything." Wren laughed, and hated the sound of it. "That's the thing nobody understands about Vess. She's not a buyer. Buyers take and leave you poorer. She's a *broker*. She trades. Fair dealing—that's her religion, her law, her whole nature. She won't cheat you. She'll just make sure the price is exactly, precisely, mathematically what the thing is worth, and then she'll hold you to it until the stars burn out."

"What did you trade?"

"My soul." She said it again because the second time was easier than the first and she needed it to get easier or she wasn't going to finish. "On deposit. Held in trust. For a term."

"A term."

"Twelve years." She met his eyes. "It comes due at midwinter. Eleven days from now."

* * *

For a long moment the only sound was the snow against the shutters, and the candle eating its wick, and Juniper breathing.

"What did she give you," he said finally, "for a soul? What's worth that?"

"You." The word came out harder than she meant it to. "Your safety. Your freedom. Your whole unremarked, invisible, ordinary life—I bought you twelve years of being nobody, Juni. Don't you understand what that cost me to arrange? You think your gift hid itself? You think a boy who talks to doorknobs survives a purge by luck? Every hunter who ever came within a mile of you forgot why he'd come. Every list your name ever landed on lost the page. The chalk mark on our door washed off in rain that fell for three days straight while the sky stayed clear everywhere else in Corrow. That was the contract working. That was my soul, spent a little at a time, keeping you safe."

"That's not how it works," Juniper said. "Souls aren't currency you spend down. When you sell—"

"When you *deposit*," she corrected, and heard Queen Vess's own precise cadence come out of her mouth, and flinched from it, "the soul stays yours. It stays *in* you. It just isn't yours to keep. At the end of the term, the holder collects. I walk into the Undercourt at midwinter and I don't walk out. My body will probably live another fifty years. It just won't have a Wren in it."

The candle flame bent sideways, though there was no draft. Juniper was gripping the edge of the table so hard the wood creaked, and Wren realized distantly that he was hearing it creak, hearing what the table remembered of every argument and grief ever leaned across it, and she almost told him to stop listening, and then understood: he wasn't doing it on purpose. Things spoke louder around strong feeling. The whole room was probably shouting at him.

"Eleven days," he said.

"Ten and a half. Midwinter dawn."

"Why didn't you tell me?" And here his voice finally cracked, and she saw twelve years of it hit him all at once—the invisibility she'd arranged, the loneliness he'd built his whole careful life inside, the ledgers and the hidden gift and the gate that opened only for him because she'd paid for a small, safe, sealed existence with everything she had. "Twelve years, Wren. You let me think I was just—lucky. Quiet. Uninteresting. I built my entire self around being uninteresting, and it was a *purchase*. I was a thing someone owned the debt on and I never knew."

"I know." Her throat closed. "I know. I'm sorry. I'm not going to tell you I did it for your own good, because everyone says that and it's always a lie people tell to make their cowardice sound like courage. I did it because I was thirteen and terrified and I loved you more than I loved myself, and I would do it again tomorrow, and that's the truth, and I'm not proud of any part of it except the loving-you part."

Juniper put his head in his hands.

* * *

She let him sit with it. That was another thing she'd learned in twelve years of secret-keeping: you cannot rush someone through the room where the bad news lives. You can only sit in it with them.

When he looked up, his eyes were red but dry.

"The market," he said. "The phrase I kept hearing in Corrow's undercity. *The debt at midwinter.* People said it like weather. Like something coming regardless."

"It's known," Wren admitted. "Vess's collections are famous. She doesn't hide them—hiding would be dishonest, and she can't be dishonest, it would break her. Every midwinter she collects what's owed, and the city above pretends not to notice, and the guild used to record every collection in its ledgers." Something moved across his face. "What?"

"Odell had a ledger with a torn page," he said slowly. "Burned edges. It hummed with grief so loud I could barely stand near it. I thought it was some trade gone wrong decades ago."

"Or a collection," Wren said. "Or a contract written and regretted." She shook her head. "It doesn't matter now."

"It might." But he set it aside; she watched him physically put it down, the way their mother used to set down worries too heavy for the hour. "Can it be undone? Can you buy it back? Pay it off?"

"With what?" She spread her hands. "I've spent twelve years as a seamstress in Corrow, Juni. I have four crowns and a locket. Soul-debts don't work like money anyway. Vess holds mine because a soul is the one commodity that's always worth exactly what the holder values it at—which means the price only goes up the longer you live, and the more you're loved." Her voice thinned. "And you're very easy to love. I priced myself high without meaning to."

"So we run."

"From Vess?" Wren actually smiled, and it hurt. "You can't run from an honest creditor, brother. She knows where my soul is at all times—it's inside me. Distance means nothing. Borders mean nothing. The hunters, yes, we can outrun hunters. We did tonight. But midwinter comes whether we're in a palace or a ditch. The contract executes at dawn on midwinter day, wherever I am, whoever's holding my hand." She reached across the table, hesitated, and took his hand anyway. It was cold. Hers was colder.

"Then we go to her," Juniper said. "Before midwinter. We go down there and we renegotiate."

Wren stared at him. "You want to walk into the Undercourt. Voluntarily. Into the hall of the creature who holds my soul, eleven days before she collects it."

"Yes."

"That is the single worst idea you've ever had, and I once watched you eat a tidepool."

"I'm serious, Wren."

"I know you're serious. That's what frightens me." She pulled her hand back and pressed both palms against her eyes until she saw orange ghosts. When she lowered them, she made herself say the rest of it, the part she'd been circling all night, the part that had kept her silent through two provinces and three bad inns.

"There's something else you need to understand about Vess before you decide we're walking into her house. She doesn't trap people. Trapping is crude. What she does is worse: she *waits*. She puts the terms in front of you fair and square, and then she lets you build your own cage out of the only materials you have left. Everyone who deals with her thinks they've found the loophole. Nobody finds the loophole. There is no loophole. There's just the deal, and what you're willing to become to escape it." She looked at him across the candle. "So whatever you're planning—whatever clever gatekeeper thing is forming behind those eyes right now—you look me in the face and promise me you won't pay my debt with something worse than a soul."

Juniper held her gaze for a long, long moment.

And Wren, who had lied expertly for twelve years, recognized a withheld truth when she saw one, even in a face she loved.

"You already know what you're going to offer her," she said quietly.

Outside, far off down the frozen road, a bell began to ring—not the inn's bell, not the village bell, something deeper, older, a sound that seemed to come up through the ground rather than down from a tower. Once. Twice. Ten times.

Midwinter was counting down.

Juniper rose, took the locket gently from the table, and closed Wren's fingers around it.

"Pack your bag," he said. "We ride at first light. There's a smuggler in the high passes who owes the guild a favor—and I need to ask him the way down."

"The way down where?"

Juniper looked at her, and for the first time since the burning of the guildhouse, since Odell's death, since the flight and the fear and the snow, her brother smiled—and there was nothing comforting in it at all.

"To the back door of the world," he said.
