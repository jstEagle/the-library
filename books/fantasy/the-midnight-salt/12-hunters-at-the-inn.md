## 12. Hunters at the Inn

The snow started an hour before they reached the inn, and by the time the sign swung into view—*The Drowned Lamb*, its painted sheep drowning in actual weather—it had buried the road ankle-deep and shown no interest in stopping.

"We should keep moving," Wren said.

"We should not," Juniper said. "You're shivering so hard your teeth are keeping time."

"I'm *warm*."

"You're lying. I can hear it." He nodded at her coat pocket, where the locket sat. "Everything you lie to me comes out flat. Like a coin dropped on wood."

Wren pulled her scarf higher and said nothing, which was also flat, also wooden, and Juniper had learned in three days of shared roads and separate silences that this was how his sister apologized without apologizing. She was still angry that he'd pressed the locket for its testimony. She was still carrying the contract like a stone sewn into her chest. And she was still, stubbornly, walking toward the mountain with him instead of away from it, which was the only apology he needed.

The Drowned Lamb was the only building for miles with smoke coming out of it, which meant it was the only building for miles with people in it, which meant—Juniper realized too late, hand already on the door—that it was exactly the kind of place hunters would warm themselves.

He heard it before he saw it. He always did.

Iron remembers fire. Leather remembers the hand that broke it. And a witch-hunter's badge, stamped tin on a wool coat, remembers every door it has been carried through—and the Drowned Lamb's door remembered four such coats passing through it that afternoon.

"Wren." His voice went very quiet. "Back door?"

She'd learned to read him now, at least the urgent registers. Her eyes swept the common room through the frost-furred window: low beams, a peat fire, a dozen travelers, and near the hearth, four figures in grey traveling cloaks whose boots were too good for their clothes.

"Too late," she murmured. "One of them just looked up."

* * *

Inside, the inn was warm in the way of places that have given up pretending to be anything but cold underneath—the fire was real but small, the ale was real but thin, and everyone spoke in the hushed register of people sharing warmth with strangers and resenting it slightly.

Juniper counted the hunters without looking at them. Four. Two by the fire, one leaning against the stair rail, one at the back table with a map spread out and a stub of charcoal pencil, marking something. Marking *roads*. The map's parchment remembered being unrolled in a cold command tent, remembered a voice saying *the girl travels toward the mountain*, and Juniper pulled his attention off it before it could tell him more, because listening to a hunter's map in a room full of hunters felt like shouting in a library.

They took seats at the far end of the room, backs to the wall, old habit. A serving woman came; Wren ordered stew and bread for two in a voice pitched lower than her usual, and Juniper watched the hunter at the map tilt his head, very slightly, like a dog catching a scent on wind.

"They don't know us yet," Wren said under the clatter of bowls. "Not our faces. Vane's lot work from descriptions. Gifted are supposed to look—" she gestured vaguely at herself, "—marked. Mad-eyed. We just look tired and poor."

"You look tired and poor beautifully."

"Don't flirt while we might die."

"It's when we might die that flirting matters most."

But his hands weren't steady under the table, because the room's objects were talking all at once and most of them were saying versions of the same thing. The poker by the fire remembered being heated until it glowed—not for the peat, but held over a man's palm while other men asked questions. The floorboard under the back table remembered boots pacing all night. The cellar hatch remembered a woman being lowered through it, bound, while someone above said, *the crown pays double for ones who can listen.*

"Juniper." Wren's hand covered his. "Your face is doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you go somewhere else and come back looking like you've seen a ghost. What did you hear?"

"The poker," he said. "It's been used for questioning."

Wren's jaw set. "Then we eat fast and leave through the kitchen."

They ate fast. The stew was actually good, mutton and turnip and too much pepper, and there was a strange, stolen sweetness in sitting across from his sister eating a hot meal, both of them alive, both of them free, neither of them yet knowing how short the freedom would run. For eleven minutes, Juniper let himself pretend they were just siblings on a bad winter road, and that the worst thing waiting at the end of it was a debt that could be argued down.

Then the front door banged open and the snow came in wearing a fifth grey cloak, and everything got worse.

* * *

Captain Ilsa Vane stamped the snow off her boots with the efficiency of a woman who considered weather a personal failing of the landscape. She was younger than Juniper had imagined from the way the objects feared her—a hard, plain face, hair cropped short, a sword worn like a tool rather than a trophy. But it was what she carried in her left hand that made the whole room's memory lurch:

A ledger page. Torn. Burned at the edges.

Juniper knew that paper. He'd spent twelve years binding ledgers for the guild; he knew the rag-weight of the stock, the particular iron-gall ink, the way Odell's hand slanted leftward on the descenders. This was a page from *his* archive. From the oldest book. The same torn page—or its sibling—that had hummed with grief in his hands a lifetime ago, back when the guildhouse still stood.

Vane crossed to the back table and laid the page over the map. "Roads out of Corrow," she said, to the room at large, in a voice calibrated to carry. "Anyone come through the pass since midday?"

Murmurs. Headshakes. The innkeeper said two travelers, brother and sister, come in ahead of the storm.

Juniper felt Wren go still beside him the way water goes still before it freezes.

Vane looked up. Not at them—across the whole room, unhurried, letting her gaze do the work a search would do worse. When it passed over Juniper, it snagged. Just for a heartbeat. The way a fisherman feels a nibble and knows better than to strike.

"Brother and sister," Vane repeated. "Names?"

The innkeeper shrugged. Didn't ask. Country manners.

"Mm." Vane rolled the burned page closed around her finger. Then, casually, terribly: "There's a bounty posted. Gifted souls, taken quiet. The crown prefers them breathing, but—" she smiled with about a tenth of her face, "—accidents happen on mountain roads. If anyone's sheltering such a person, now's the hour to find your conscience."

Nobody moved. Nobody breathed loud.

And then the poker by the fire, which remembered pain, decided to share it.

That's the trouble with Juniper's gift, when it ran loose: strong memories bled. Twelve years of practice had taught him walls, filters, a keeper's discipline—but fear cracked discipline, and the poker's memory of glowing iron and screaming men poured off it like heat, and the nearest hunter flinched from the hearth as though it had bitten him, and looked straight at Juniper, because Juniper was the only face in the room doing anything at all.

"There," the hunter said. "The tall one. He's *listening*."

* * *

What happened next happened in the space between heartbeats, and afterward Juniper would only be able to assemble it in pieces, like a dropped plate.

Vane moved first—she was fast, faster than a woman her size had any right to be, and her hand was on her sword before the accusation finished leaving her hunter's mouth. "Take them breathing," she snapped, and four hunters came off the walls.

Wren threw the table.

Not pushed it—threw it, with a shove that had something behind it, something Juniper had never seen her use, and the heavy oak went across the room like a thrown barrel and caught two hunters at the knees. Bowls went everywhere. The serving woman screamed. The innkeeper, with the pragmatism of his trade, dove behind his own bar.

"Kitchen!" Wren shouted, and ran, and Juniper ran, and that was when the room's objects—all of them, at once—rose up in his hearing like a tide.

Because he stopped suppressing. There was no time for walls. And the Drowned Lamb, in that moment, told him *everything*: the loose board by the pantry, the knife block with one blade missing, the cellar hatch with its bolt rusted to half-strength, the back door whose hinges had been screaming for oil since autumn, the stable yard where a sledge stood loaded with peat. The whole inn became a map drawn in memory, and Juniper read it at a sprint.

"Left at the barrels—the hinge door screams but the bolt's weak—the sledge in the yard, take the sledge—"

"How can you possibly know that—"

"I'm listening!"

They burst into the kitchen trailing flour-smell and shouts. Behind them, Vane's voice cut through the chaos, level as a ledger line: *"Seal the back. They'll run for the horses."*

But they weren't running for the horses. Juniper hit the back door with his shoulder, and it screamed exactly as promised, and the yard was a white blur of falling snow and one loaded peat-sledge standing by the fence, runner-tracks already cut, ready to be dragged somewhere.

"Harness?" Wren gasped.

"No time. Push."

They put their shoulders to the sledge together, and it groaned, and didn't move, and behind them the kitchen door banged open and a hunter came through with his sword already clear, and Juniper did something he had never once done in twelve years of careful invisibility.

He answered the door.

Not the kitchen door—the *memory* in it. The kitchen door remembered thirty years of opening and closing, of hinges oiled and neglected, of a carpenter who had hung it crooked and been ashamed of it ever after. Juniper reached into that memory the way Odell had taught him to reach into a lock, turned the door's own recollection of its crookedness *against* it, and the door slammed shut in the hunter's face with the force of a grudge thirty years old. The hunter rebounded. Something heavy fell against it from the other side.

"What," Wren said, staring, "was THAT."

"Later! PUSH!"

This time the sledge moved. Snow is generous to runners; the track was already cut; and they got it to the top of the inn's gentle slope just as Vane came around the corner of the building through the whiteout, sword out, alone, having sent her hunters to cover doors that no longer mattered.

For a moment, captain and fugitives regarded each other across ten feet of falling snow.

Vane's eyes went from Wren to Juniper and stayed on Juniper, and there was something in her face that wasn't hatred. Curiosity, maybe. Recognition, even—the look of a woman reading a page she'd been hunting for a long time.

"Brightwater," she said.

Juniper's blood went cold. That name hadn't been spoken aloud in years. He'd buried it under *keeper*, under *clerk*, under *nobody*.

"You know my name."

"I know lots of names." Vane lowered her sword, which was somehow more frightening than raising it. "Yours is worth more than most. Run along home, Brightwater. You're expected."

Then Wren shoved the sledge, and it tipped over the crest of the slope, and they rode it—actually *rode* it, two grown adults on a peat-sledge, careening down a hillside in a blizzard, spinning, laughing or sobbing or both—until the slope flattened and the snow swallowed them and the inn vanished behind the white.

* * *

They walked the rest of the night, dragging the empty sledge because abandoning it felt wasteful and riding it felt impossible, and somewhere around the third mile Wren finally asked.

"The door. You made it slam. How?"

Juniper blew out a breath that froze in front of him. "Everything keeps its memories. Doors, locks, knives. Usually I just... listen. Tonight I talked back. Made the door remember being hung wrong so hard it corrected itself." He shook his head. "Odell called it 'addressing the object.' Said it was the deep end of the gift. Said most listeners drown trying."

"And you just—did it. In a kitchen. While running."

"Fear's a good teacher."

Wren walked a few steps in silence. Snow filled their footprints behind them almost as fast as they made them, which was either comforting or ominous depending on your mood, and Juniper couldn't decide which mood he was in.

"You've been hiding it," she said at last. "All these years. Hiding *you*. Keeping it small."

"Keeping it safe. There's a difference."

"Is there? Because tonight you used it twice in ten minutes, and the world didn't end. You ended." She stopped walking and faced him, and snow gathered on her shoulders and eyelashes, and beneath the frost her expression was fierce and frightened in equal measure. "Juniper. They know your name. Whatever protection being invisible bought you—spent it tonight. All of it. Which means the only thing left between you and the crown is speed, luck, and whatever else you've been rationing for twelve years." Her voice dropped. "Stop rationing."

He wanted to argue. He opened his mouth to argue. But the locket at her throat had gone warm against her skin—he could feel it from here, humming, the way it always hummed when the truth got close—and the truth was that she was right, and that some part of him had known it since the guildhouse burned, since Odell died holding a door for a gate that opened for one man only.

"Fine," he said. "No more rationing."

"Good." She started walking again. "Because I think we're being followed."

He'd heard it too—had heard it for the last mile and told himself it was the wind. But wind doesn't remember. And somewhere back along their own trail, faint and patient, something metal was remembering being drawn from a scabbard.

Behind them, far off through the white, a single horn sounded. Not a hunt-horn. A *signal*-horn—one note, long and flat, calling to another horn somewhere south of them, which answered, and then another, east.

They weren't chasing. They were *corralling*—passing his name hand to hand across the dark, folding the net shut.

Wren's hand found his in the snow. Her fingers were freezing. Her grip was not.

"Off the road," she said. "Now. Toward the high rocks."

And Juniper, listening past her to the horns laying their invisible fence across the night, heard something worse than pursuit in the pattern of them: they weren't trying to catch them anymore.

They were herding them somewhere specific.

Somewhere the crown had chosen.
