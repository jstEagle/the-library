## 13. The Descent Road

The man who called himself Brannoc was waiting for them at the third milestone past Corrow, which was the first thing Juniper didn't trust about him. Nobody waited at milestones in winter unless they were paid to, and nobody was paid to wait for two fugitives unless they already knew they were coming.

He was sitting on the stone like it was a bench he'd owned for years, a broad-shouldered man wrapped in a fisherman's oilskin gone gray with salt, though the sea was eighty miles east. His beard was the color of wet iron, his hands were the size of dinner plates, and around his neck he wore a guild token on a leather cord—the old sigil of the Impossible Things, a door standing open onto darkness. Except his had been defaced. Someone had taken a chisel to the door, striking it shut.

"You're the Brightwater boy," Brannoc said. Not a question.

"I'm nobody's boy," Juniper said. "And I don't know you."

"No. But I know your master's key." He nodded at Juniper's coat pocket, where Odell's iron key sat against Juniper's hip like a second heartbeat. "Odell Quillon carried that for thirty years. He used to say if anyone ever came to him wearing my face, he'd know the world was ending." A pause. "World ending?"

Juniper said nothing. Wren, beside him, shifted her weight from foot to foot, breath steaming. They'd been walking since before dawn, and the marsh road behind them had already begun to forget their passage—Juniper could feel it, the mud smoothing over their prints, the reeds standing back up where Wren had pushed through them. His gift, suppressed down to an ember, doing its grim work. The road forgot them. Everything would forget them, eventually, if he kept it up long enough. That was the trick of it. That was also the cost.

"Odell's dead," Juniper said finally.

Brannoc looked at him for a long moment. Then he stood, slow as a drawbridge coming up, and took off the ruined token, turning it over in his enormous fingers.

"They burned the guildhouse four days ago," he said. "I heard it in Corrow. Smugglers hear everything; it's how we stay alive. And then three days ago, word comes down the undercity pipes that the Saltgate keeper is running south with a girl, asking after passage to the mountain." He smiled without warmth. "You've been loud, Brightwater. For a man whose whole talent is being unheard."

"We asked quietly."

"Everything's loud when hunters are paying for whispers." He hung the token back around his neck. "So. You want the Descent Road. The mountain's back door. There are maybe six people alive who can find it in winter, and five of them are dead or bought. Which leaves me."

"Why?" Wren asked. She'd been quiet all morning, but her voice came out sharp now, honed by weeks of fear into something almost aggressive. "Why help us? What do you get?"

Brannoc looked at her with something like approval. "Good. Ask that. Always ask that." He turned back to Juniper. "I'll tell you what I told Odell once, when he found me selling guild secrets to keep my crew fed: I'm exiled, not faithless. The guild made me. Taught me locks and ledgers and how to carry a thing across a border so no one remembers carrying it. The crown burned my house. I'd like to see the crown choke on what it burned." He shrugged, a landslide in miniature. "Also, I get paid."

"There it is," Juniper said.

"There it is," Brannoc agreed pleasantly. "Passage to the back door of the Undercourt, safe from hunters, safe from the things that live between here and there. My price is simple: I name it later."

Wren laughed—a short, ugly sound. "Later. No. We pay upfront or not at all."

"You can't pay upfront," Brannoc said mildly. "Because you don't know yet what this trip costs. Neither do I, exactly. But I know enough to know you couldn't cover it now." He held up one vast hand before Wren could speak again. "Here's the honest shape of it, girl. Your brother is going to the Undercourt to bargain with Queen Vess, yes? Everyone who goes to Vess comes out lighter than they went in. Sometimes it's coin. Sometimes it's memory. Once, in my smuggling days, I carried a man back up the Descent Road who'd sold his shadow, and let me tell you what it looks like when a person walks without one—the snow doesn't mark him. Dogs won't look at him. His own mother walked past him in the street." His eyes settled on Juniper, heavy and unreadable. "Whatever you pay her, you'll need a guide on the way back too. Half-dead men make poor navigators. So my price gets named after I see what's left of you. Fair's fair."

"That's not fair," Wren said. "That's extortion with extra steps."

"It's smuggling," Brannoc said. "Same thing."

* * *

They walked.

The marsh gave way to moorland by afternoon, the ground rising in long black swells under a sky the color of dishwater. Snow began—not falling so much as arriving, sideways, in grains fine as salt. Juniper kept his gift banked to almost nothing, and the world responded by treating him like weather: a cart driver passed within arm's reach and never turned his head; a kestrel stooped on a vole six feet away without startling. It should have felt like safety. Mostly it felt like practicing.

But he couldn't help himself, sometimes. Little touches. A mile marker remembered being carved by a stonemason who sang while she worked and hated her apprentice. A fencepost remembered thirty years of sheep leaning on it, patient as furniture. A rusted gate remembered hands, hundreds of hands, and underneath the rust, faintly, the smell of the forge where it was born. Small memories. Cheap ones. He rationed himself the way a starving man rations bread, telling himself each taste was nothing, knowing it wasn't.

Brannoc watched him do it once, outside an abandoned tollhouse, and said nothing, but his silence had a texture to it. Calculation. Juniper had spent twelve years reading silences in a building full of people trading pieces of themselves, and this one read like a merchant appraising stock.

They made camp that night in the lee of the tollhouse, out of the wind. Brannoc built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped palm and produced food from nowhere—hard cheese, black bread, a strip of dried pork apiece, all of it tasting faintly of pipe smoke.

"How do you know about the Undercourt?" Juniper asked, when the eating was done. "Really. Smuggling runs below the mountain—that's guild work, isn't it? Old work."

"Older than you think." Brannoc fed a twig to the fire. "Your guild didn't invent impossible trades, Brightwater. It just wrote them down. Before the Saltgate there were other doors, other brokers. Vess's court is the oldest. The guild used to run caravans down the Descent Road—souls going one way, miracles coming back. Contracts, cures, second chances, all of it priced in the only currency that holds its value." He tapped his own chest. "Then some generation of your family decided the trade was rotten and shut the front door. Didn't shut the back one, though. Never does. There's always a back door. I ran goods through it for nine years before the guild caught me."

"Caught you how?"

"Warden caught me. Odell." Something moved behind the man's face, quick and buried, like a fish under ice. "He could have handed me to the crown. Instead he handed me exile and took my token." He touched the chiseled door at his throat. "Said I could earn it back someday. Said the guild always needed a man who knew the roads that don't want finding." He was quiet a moment. "Now the guild's ash and Odell's ash and here I am, earning it back for a boy who wasn't even born when I lost it."

"What did you smuggle?" Wren asked. "Down there."

Brannoc looked at the fire.

"People," he said. "Out, mostly. Debtors who wanted one more chance. One or two going in—you'd be amazed what people will sell when the famine's bad enough. Whole villages, nearly." He shook his head slowly. "I stopped asking what happened to them after delivery. That's the first rule of smuggling and the last rule of decency, and I broke the decent one every day for nine years." He glanced up, and for a second the merchant's appraisal was gone and something older and tireder looked out. "Don't go down there thinking Vess is a monster you can outfight, girl. She's not a monster. Monsters take. Vess *trades*. Every deal fair on its face, every scale balanced to the grain—and somehow she ends up owning everything, and everyone still thanks her for the courtesy. That's the trap. You don't beat her by refusing to bargain. You beat her—or you don't—by understanding the bargain better than she expects you to."

"And you understand it?" Juniper asked.

"I understand roads," Brannoc said. "Roads and weights. What a thing weighs going in and what's left coming out." He lay back against his pack, hat over his eyes. "Sleep. We cross the high fells tomorrow. After that, no more fires, no more roads. Just the mountain and the ways under it."

* * *

Juniper didn't sleep. Or barely.

Near midnight he gave up pretending, wrapped his coat tighter, and stepped out of the tollhouse doorway into the blue-black cold. The snow had stopped. The sky had cleared hard, and the stars were out in their terrible multitudes, and away north—a smudge darker than the dark—the mountain stood against them.

His sister's soul was inside it.

He tried to imagine the shape of the thing he was walking toward and failed. Twelve years of ledger entries had taught him the grammar of impossible trades—he could recite the standard clauses in his sleep, the weight tables, the substitution rules, the dawn clauses and mercy clauses that the guild had fought to write into the old contracts—but grammar wasn't meaning. Somewhere beneath that mountain was a contract with Wren's signature on it, signed in whatever ink souls sign with, and somewhere in his chest was the growing certainty that he'd been living on borrowed time his entire life without ever seeing the loan.

*She sold herself to buy my safety.* He still couldn't hold the thought for more than a few seconds at a stretch. It slid out of his grip like wet rope. She'd been—what, sixteen? Seventeen? Young enough to be light after that winter, young enough to come home smiling and let him believe it was just a job in the lowlands. And all those years he'd been downstairs at the Saltgate listening to doorknobs remember their makers, safe and invisible and *content*, and the price of all that contentment had been ticking over in the dark beneath the world, accruing interest, waiting for midwinter.

The snow crunched behind him. Brannoc, moving quiet for a big man.

"Can't sleep either?" the smuggler said. He came and stood beside Juniper, looking north at the mountain. For a while neither spoke. Then Brannoc said, conversationally, "Your gift. The hearing-things. How far down does it go?"

Juniper went still. "Why?"

"Professional curiosity." Brannoc's voice was mild, but Juniper heard the carefulness under it, the way a floor sounds careful when it's about to give way. "Guild records say the Brightwater line keeps gates. Gatekeeping's a hearing kind of work, isn't it? Listening to locks. Knowing what a door wants." He turned his head, and the starlight caught the ruined token at his throat. "A man who can hear what objects remember could hear what a *gate* remembers. If the stories are true."

"The stories are mostly wrong," Juniper said, which was true, and also not the denial Brannoc deserved.

"Mm." Brannoc nodded slowly, accepting it the way a man accepts a coin he intends to weigh later. He clapped Juniper on the shoulder—a blow gentle by his standards, staggering by anyone else's—and headed back toward the fire. At the doorway he paused.

"For what it's worth, keeper," he said, "when I name my price—I'll name it to you first. Not to the hunters, not to the crown. To you. Whatever else I am, I'm guild. We settle our debts among ourselves." A pause, and then, softer, almost to himself: "It's the only law left worth keeping."

He went inside. Juniper stayed out a while longer, holding Odell's key through his coat, feeling its iron remember fire and a hand on a door and *hold, hold, hold*—

—and above the fells, the mountain waited, patient as a creditor.
