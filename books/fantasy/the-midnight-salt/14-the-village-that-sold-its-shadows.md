## 14. The Village That Sold Its Shadows

The road to the mountain ran through a valley where the snow lay gray instead of white.

Wren noticed it first as a wrongness in the light—the way noon came down on the fields and seemed to lose its nerve halfway. She had been walking behind Juniper for three days now, close enough to catch him if he stumbled, far enough that his gift couldn't reach her uninvited. He'd learned to walk like that too. Like two people sharing a room in a house with thin walls, careful about which side of them touched.

"Look," she said, and pointed.

He looked. She watched him do the thing he did—head tilting slightly, eyes going soft and unfocused, listening to whatever the world remembered. His face changed. Whatever he heard out there in those gray fields, it made him stop walking.

"What is it?"

"A fencepost," he said quietly. "It remembers being a tree, and it remembers being cut, and it remembers—" He stopped. Started again. "It remembers a man selling something. Something he was wearing. The post doesn't have a word for it."

Wren felt the cold go through her coat before the wind found it.

Ahead, down in the valley's throat, sat a village.

* * *

They came down into it at midafternoon, because the alternative was another night in the open with hunters somewhere behind them, and because Wren told herself they were only passing through. That was all. Passing through.

The village was called Harrow Fen, according to a sign whose letters someone had once painted over and then, apparently, lost interest in finishing. The houses were stone, squat, well-built. Chimneys smoked. There were people in the lanes—an old woman beating a rug, two children chasing a dog, a man splitting wood with the easy rhythm of long practice—and every one of them cast no shadow.

That was the thing Wren's eyes kept trying to refuse. The sun stood low and hard over the western ridge, the kind of light that throws everything long and sharp across the frozen ground. And the ground stayed clean. No shadows from the fences. No shadows from the woodpile. No shadows from the people, who moved through their own brightness like figures cut out of a page and pasted onto nothing.

The old woman with the rug saw them first. She didn't wave or call out. She just stopped beating the rug and stood there, straightening slowly, watching them come down the road with an expression Wren would spend years afterward trying to name. It wasn't hostility. It wasn't fear.

It was recognition. The look you give someone arriving late to a funeral.

"They can see us," Juniper murmured. "I mean—they're *seeing* us. Not through us." He said it strangely, as if visibility were a currency he'd forgotten he carried.

"Everyone can see us. That's how seeing works."

"Not for me, usually." He flexed his gloved hands inside his pockets. "And not for anyone carrying what we're carrying. I'd expected—" He shook his head. "Never mind. Let's find the inn. We need food, and Brannoc said the back-door trail starts past this valley."

Brannoc walked ahead of them both, and Wren caught the smuggler glancing at the shadowless villagers with something that wasn't surprise either. That frightened her more than the missing shadows did. Brannoc had known. Brannoc had brought them here on purpose, or at least without any wish to spare them.

* * *

The inn was called the Lamplighter, which Wren thought was either a very good joke or a very bad one. Inside it was warm and dim and smelled of onions and tallow, and there were perhaps a dozen villagers drinking quietly, and not one lamp in the place was lit.

"Why bother," said the innkeeper, when Juniper asked. She was a broad woman gone gray early, with forearms like a blacksmith's and eyes the exact color of dishwater. "Light doesn't hold here. You'll see."

She fed them barley stew and dark bread and charged fairly, and she answered questions the way people answer questions when they've answered them so many times the answers have worn smooth as river stones. Yes, everyone in Harrow Fen had sold their shadow. No, not by choice, exactly. Yes, it had been worth it, at the time. No, they didn't talk about it much anymore.

"It was the famine year," she said. She wiped the same clean spot on the bar while she talked, over and over. "Eleven years back. The blight took the barley twice running, and the river silted, and the crown sent tax collectors anyway, because crowns do. Come midwinter we were eating bark and boiling hides. Then the peddler came."

Juniper's spoon had stopped moving. Wren kept hers going, deliberately, because she wanted to seem bored, and boredom was armor.

"What kind of peddler?" she asked.

"The polite kind." The innkeeper almost smiled. "Fine coat, fine manners. Set up his little table in the square and unpacked scales, real silver ones, and he says, good folk, I understand your situation, and I'm prepared to be generous. A shadow for a sack of grain. One shadow buys enough to feed a family till spring. Two shadows buys seed grain besides. Three—" she shrugged her heavy shoulders— "three buys whatever you want forever. Or so he said."

"And people believed him," Wren said.

"People were starving." For the first time the innkeeper looked up, and her dishwater eyes had iron filings in them. "You believe anything when you're starving. That's not foolishness. That's arithmetic."

Wren thought of a winter twelve years ago. Thought of a girl who went away for one winter and came back lighter. She put her spoon down after all.

* * *

They learned the rest in pieces, the way you learn anything in a village that has decided to tell the truth because lying costs more effort than it used to.

A shadow, it turned out, was not a trifle. It was not the flat dark shape on the ground; that was only its coat, its outer garment. What the peddler bought was the shadow itself, the thing underneath—the doubling self, the second Wren that walks when Wren walks, that grieves when Wren grieves, that carries half of every weight so the body can carry the other half. Sell it, and you got grain. And you also got, the peddler had promised, freedom from fear, because a shadow holds your dread the way a cup holds water, and without it you simply don't feel things the same.

"That's the part nobody mentions at first," said a woodcutter who'd joined them near the fire, unasked. He was perhaps forty and looked sixty. "You think you're selling your dark half. Everyone thinks that. But there isn't a dark half. There's just you, and the part of you that stands beside you and takes some of the load. Sell it and you feel lighter. That's true! First week, everyone's walking around like they're young again. Not hungry. Not scared. Not anything." He turned his mug in circles on the table. "Then spring comes. The blight breaks, the river clears, the crown forgives the taxes because even crowns get embarrassed sometimes. And you plant your fields, and you eat your meals, and you sit with your children, and none of it lands. None of it arrives. It's like eating with a numb tongue. Like embracing someone through a wall."

His wife was in the corner of the room, darning. She didn't look up. Neither of them had looked at each other once since Wren had entered, and Wren understood suddenly that this wasn't coldness between them. It was worse. It was the absence of any reason to look.

"You can buy it back," Juniper said. It wasn't quite a question.

"You can try." The woodcutter laughed, and the laugh was the most terrible sound Wren had heard in years, because it was completely empty, a bell with no clapper. "Peddler said so himself. Buyback clause, he called it, very official, wrote it all down in his ledger. Price of return: the shadow's current value, plus interest, plus the fee of the transaction. And the shadow appreciates, see. Every year you live without it, it grows more precious. More of your life has pooled behind the glass. By year five, buying back what you sold costs more than any of us will earn in twenty lifetimes. That's the joke. That's always the joke." He drank. "He never lied to us. Not once. Everything he said was true. He just never said the truest thing, which was that we couldn't afford our own selves back."

Wren's hands had gone cold in her lap. Across the table, Juniper was looking at her—not at the woodcutter, at her—with an expression she recognized and hated, because it meant his gift had reached toward her without permission, reading the memory rising off her skin like heat off a stove. Twelve years ago. One winter away. Came back lighter.

She stood up too fast. "Air," she said. "I need air."

"Wren—"

"I need *air*, Juniper."

* * *

Outside, the light was failing, and the village was filling up with a darkness that belonged to no one.

That was the thing about a shadowless place at dusk, she discovered. Everywhere else, evening comes gradually, shadows stretching and merging until the whole world eases into night. Here there was no such easing. Day simply ended, like a door shutting, and the darkness that followed was total and ownerless, a darkness that had been evicted from every person in the village and had nowhere left to go except everywhere.

She stood in the square, in that orphaned dark, and hugged herself, and tried to breathe.

There was a fountain in the square, dry and cracked. Beside it stood a notice board, and nailed to the board, weathered but legible, was a paper covered in dense handwriting. Wren read it by the last gray light.

It was a contract. Or rather, a copy of a contract—one copy per household, posted publicly, the terms of sale laid out in language so clean and courteous it made her teeth ache. *Whereas the undersigned party does convey unto the Broker all right, title, and interest in the shadow entire... Whereas the Broker shall furnish sustenance sufficient... Whereas repurchase shall be available upon payment of assessed value plus accrued appreciation...*

At the bottom of the page, in a column, were the signatures. Dozens of them. Every adult in the village had signed, and the signatures were strange—some confident, some shaky, one that was clearly just an X pressed hard enough to tear the paper, one that trailed off into a long ink stroke as though the hand writing it had fallen asleep or given up halfway through.

Near the bottom, third from last, was a signature in silver ink.

Silver. Not gray, not faded—silver, still faintly gleaming after eleven years, the way metal gleams and ink doesn't. Wren leaned close. The name was *Maren Odo*, and beneath the name, in smaller script, was a note in the same silver hand: *paid in full — three shadows — family provision secured.*

Three shadows. Maren Odo had sold three—hers, and presumably her husband's, and one more. A child's? Could you sell a child's shadow? Could you sell it *for* them?

Wren stood in the square with her heart hammering and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly why the silver ink had snagged her. Because she knew silver ink. She knew the particular glide of a silver-nibbed pen across vellum, knew it from the inside of her own memory, from a night twelve years ago that she had folded up small and buried deep and told herself was a dream, a fever, a story she'd invented—

The locket.

Her hand went to her collarbone. The locket was there, under her shirt, where it always was. Warm. Warmer than her skin, which made no sense, unless warmth could leak out of a memory.

"Wren."

Juniper's voice, quiet, behind her. He came and stood beside her in the ownerless dark, and for once he didn't tilt his head, didn't listen, didn't reach. He just stood next to her, the way you stand next to someone at a grave.

"We shouldn't have come here," she said.

"Brannoc chose the road."

"Brannoc chose the road, and we followed it, and we're following it still—all the way down under the mountain to sign ourselves into another ledger." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it. "Look at this place, Juniper. Look at what surviving costs. They got through the famine. Every single one of them is alive, and fed, and warm, and they are *nothing*. They're shells with chores. That man's wife doesn't look at him. The children chase dogs because a dog is the only thing that feels like anything. And the debt never closes. It just appreciates. Year after year, it gets more expensive to be yourself."

"I know."

"You don't know. You hear objects remember. You don't know what it is to owe." She turned to face him, and the words were coming up out of her like blood from a reopened cut, twelve years of pressure behind them. "I know what it is to owe. I know the exact weight of it. I know what I signed and what it cost and what's coming due at midwinter, and standing here, in this village, I finally understand what 'forever' looks like on the far end of a bargain. It looks like a woman darning socks who never once looks up at her husband. It looks like silver ink that never fades. If I turn around—if we both turn around and walk away from the mountain right now—I die at midwinter, quietly, politely, in accordance with my contract, and you live, and maybe that's the trade working as intended. Maybe that's the best price available. This village is what happens when people take the best price available."

Juniper was silent for a long moment. In the dark she could barely see him, and she realized that for once the dark and she were alike—both of them holding something no one could see.

"There's a third option," he said finally.

"There's always a third option. That's what the peddlers say."

"No." He stepped closer. "This one's mine. I found a page, Wren. Back at the Saltgate, before the burning—a torn page from the oldest ledger, edges scorched, humming so loud with grief I could hardly sleep near it. Odell wouldn't speak of it. But I've been thinking about it ever since, and I keep coming back to one thing: the guild recorded impossible trades for four hundred years, and someone tore out a page about the Brightwaters. Our name. Our history. Somebody didn't want us to read it—which means it matters." His voice dropped. "Whatever grandmother started, whatever's written on that page, I think the answer to your contract is in the mountain. Not in running from it."

Wren looked back at the inn. Through its window she could see the villagers inside, lamplit from within by nothing at all, sitting in their purchased safety, their appreciated debts. An old man raised his mug to someone across the table, a gesture so perfectly performed and so utterly hollow that it looked like puppetry.

She thought: *that could be me in six months. That could be me in sixty years.* Alive. Fed. Shadowless.

She thought: *or I could be dead, honestly dead, and Juniper free.*

She thought: *or.*

"Show me the back door," she said.

* * *

They left before dawn, and the whole village turned out to watch them go.

Not to stop them. Just to watch. They lined the road in their gray morning light, shadowless and silent, and as Wren passed among them the innkeeper pressed a parcel of bread into her hands and held her wrist a moment longer than necessary.

"Wherever you're going," the woman said, "whatever you're about to sign—read the clause about buyback. Read it twice. Make them say the number out loud." Her dishwater eyes held Wren's. "Make them say the number out loud before your hand touches the pen."

Behind her, at the edge of the crowd, Brannoc was waiting with the horses, and Brannoc was smiling, and Wren thought: *he knows what's under the mountain. He's known since the ferry. And he's still smiling.*

The road climbed north, toward the black shoulder of the world, and the last thing Wren saw of Harrow Fen was its people waving—not with joy, not with sorrow, but with the flat courtesy of people who have forgotten what waving is for—and above them, thrown against the brightening sky by the rising sun, one shadow that did not belong to any of them.

Hers.

She rode on, and did not look back again.
