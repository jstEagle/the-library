## 17. The Court Beneath

**Juniper**

The stair stopped being a stair somewhere around the four-hundredth step and became something closer to a throat.

Juniper felt it in his teeth first—a low hum, like the doorknob at the Saltgate but a thousand times larger, as if the whole mountain were an object that remembered being made. And what it remembered was not stone. Stone was just the skin of it. Underneath, all the way down, the mountain remembered being *promised to*.

"Talk to me," Wren said behind him. Her voice came out flattened by the dark. Brannoc walked ahead with a lantern that burned greenish and gave off no heat.

"It remembers contracts," Juniper said. "The rock does. Every foot of it."

Brannoc glanced back, the light carving his face into planes. "That's the toll road for you. Everything down here's been sold at least once." He said it lightly, the way men say things they've decided not to feel. "Keep your hands on the wall. The wall keeps count."

"The wall keeps—"

"Don't touch it if you don't want to know."

Juniper kept his hand exactly where it was. Twelve years of listening to objects remember had taught him one iron law: you never flinch from a memory. Flinching is how you end up like the hunters, burning things rather than hearing them. So he pressed his palm flat against the stone and let it speak.

It spoke in ledgers. Not words—*entries*. He felt them sliding past under his fingers like pages turning: names given, names taken, years sold in lots, a childhood traded for a harvest, a harvest traded back for the childhood when the buyer tired of it. The mountain was a warehouse of fine print, and every inch of its walls had witnessed a signing.

"Odell would have loved this," he murmured, and then hated himself, because Odell had died holding a door so that Juniper could walk into places exactly like this one.

Wren's hand found his elbow. She didn't say anything. She'd gotten good at not saying anything about Odell, about the guildhouse, about the smoke they'd both seen hanging over Corrow's heights two nights ago. Grief, between the two of them, had become a thing they carried silently on opposite ends of a pole, like water carriers who'd forgotten where the well was.

The stair opened without warning.

One moment there was rock, close and breathing; the next, the space simply *arrived*, vast as a held breath let out. Brannoc snuffed the lantern with two fingers—"No need now"—and Juniper understood why.

The Undercourt lit itself.

Light came from everywhere and nowhere honest. It hung in the air in slow-drifting constellations, thousands upon thousands of small warm glows, each one sealed inside glass—not lamps, he realized with a lurch, not candles. *Bottles.* Long-necked bottles, round-bellied bottles, bottles shaped like tears and eggs and hearts, and each one held a soft gold radiance that pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like breathing.

"Oh," Wren said, very quietly. It wasn't a word of wonder. It was the sound a person makes when they've understood something their body understood first.

"They're souls," Juniper said.

"Aye." Brannoc didn't look up. He was studying the floor instead, which Juniper now saw was not floor but *page*—an unbroken expanse of pale vellum-smooth surface stretching toward a distant dais, inscribed edge to edge with script so fine it looked like frost. "Court calls them 'the reserves.' Don't point. Don't stare. And whatever you hear in your head down here—" he tapped his own temple, "—remember whose house you're standing in."

But Juniper couldn't stop hearing. That was the whole ruinous architecture of him. The bottles sang—not with sound, with *memory*. Each soul remembered being someone. A woman who'd traded hers to cure her son's fever. A soldier who'd spent his on a victory his king had already decided against. A child's—small and bright and recent, and Juniper's stomach turned over so hard he had to stop walking.

He thought of the ledger at the Saltgate, the trades he'd recorded in his neat invisible hand. *One shadow, wintering rights, second chances (partial).* He had always told himself the guild dealt in small sorrows honestly brokered. He had believed, in the way you believe things that let you sleep, that the impossible things were mostly harmless.

Every bottle overhead was a line item. Every pulse of light was somebody's *yes*, extracted fair and square according to terms.

Awe curdled in him like milk left in sun.

"Wren." His voice came out wrong. "Look up. Really look."

"I'm looking."

"No—I mean *listen*." He caught her wrist and pressed it against his own chest, as if she could borrow the hearing through him. "That one, third rank, the blue-tinged glass. She was a midwife. She sold her soul for a license to practice medicine because the college wouldn't take women, and the court honored the trade, and she delivered four hundred babies before she died, and now she's *lighting a hallway*."

Wren went still. Then, slowly, she looked up—and Juniper watched his sister, who had lied to him for twelve years with a straight face, come apart a little at the seams. Not with pity. With recognition.

"That's why I hate this place," she whispered.

"You've *been* here?"

She didn't answer. Above them, ten thousand bottled souls breathed gold, and the vellum floor bore their names in script too fine to read, and somewhere ahead a throne waited with a queen who signed in silver ink.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren had promised herself she wouldn't look up. Looking up was how they got you. Down here, beauty was the bait and the hook was the contract, and she had signed one at seventeen with ink that had crawled off the page and up her arm and settled somewhere behind her sternum, where it still itched on cold nights.

She looked up anyway. Because Juniper had asked, and because his hand was shaking against hers, and because some stubborn buried part of her had spent twelve years wanting someone else to see what she'd seen in this room at seventeen, alone, terrified, trading away the only thing she owned that mattered.

So she saw it. All of it.

The cathedral of contracts rose in tiers, and every tier was shelves, and every shelf was bottles, and the light they gave was genuinely beautiful—that was the obscenity of it. It fell across the vellum floor like late afternoon through honey. It made the great pillars glow amber. If you didn't know, you'd think you'd wandered into a church built by people who worshipped warmth itself. Pilgrims would weep here. Wren had wept here.

And every single source of that holy light was a person.

Her eyes found the blue-tinged bottle Juniper meant—the midwife—and stayed there longer than was safe. Four hundred babies. Wren did the arithmetic she always did, the arithmetic that had brought her to this mountain in the first place: *four hundred lives weighed against one.* She'd made the same trade, smaller scale. One brother against one soul. At the time it hadn't even felt like a decision. It had felt like breathing out.

Now, standing under the accumulated weight of ten thousand such breathings-out, she felt sick with it. Not remorse, exactly—she'd have done it again, would do it again tomorrow—but the *scale* of it. This whole glittering economy of desperation. Somewhere above them the crown burned guildhouses over gifts like Juniper's, while down here the court ran a bank on the same currency, and nobody called either one evil. They called it law.

"Move," Brannoc said softly. "Procession coming."

Wren dropped her eyes. Along the far aisle, a column of figures approached—courtiers, she guessed, though they moved strangely, smoothly, like things that had practiced walking. Their clothes were beautiful and slightly wrong, colors that shifted when you weren't looking directly at them. Each one wore a small glass vessel at the throat, empty, polished, waiting.

Jewelry as advertisement. *Yours could hang here.*

One of them turned its head as it passed. Not its body—just the head, further than a neck should go, and beneath a lovely powdered face the eyes were patient as an accountant's. It smiled at Wren specifically. It knew her. Of course it did. Her name was written on the floor somewhere under their feet, in script fine as frost, with eleven weeks left on the term.

She smiled back. Twelve years of practice made it flawless. Inside, she was seventeen again, and the silver pen was moving, and the queen's voice was saying *such a reasonable girl.*

Juniper's grip on her wrist had gone tight enough to hurt.

"Not here," she breathed. "Whatever you're about to say. Not here. They can hear intent, Juniper. I'm serious. They *trade* in it."

His jaw worked. He swallowed whatever awe-shaped anger had been building in him, and she watched him do it, and hated that she'd taught him nothing better, because she'd never dared teach him anything at all.

* * *

**Juniper**

The dais at the heart of the court was carved from a single block of black glass, and on it sat a desk.

Not a throne. A *desk*—vast, ancient, scarred with centuries of pen-strokes, littered with contracts fanned like a hand of cards. Behind it stood a woman Juniper understood, with a lurch, to be Queen Vess, though she wore no crown unless you counted the arrangement of bottles suspended above her head: seven of them, larger than the rest, arranged in a circlet that rotated slowly, shedding gold light down onto her papers.

Seven souls, worn as regalia. He wanted to burn the whole room.

He didn't. Instead he did what Odell had trained into him across a decade of quiet nights: he *catalogued*. Exits—three visible, all choked with courtiers. Guards—none obvious, which meant they were everywhere or unnecessary. Objects—he reached out with the gift and listened to the desk.

The desk screamed.

There was no other word for it. Beneath the hum of the mountain, beneath the breathing of the bottles, the desk remembered every contract ever signed upon it, and it remembered them *all at once*, a chorus of yeses and betrayals and small print and weeping, layered so deep that Juniper staggered and put a hand on Wren's shoulder to steady himself.

"Easy," she murmured, and steered him behind a pillar, out of the direct line of the dais. "First time down here, everyone listens too hard. Pace yourself."

"How do you know that?" he asked. Too sharp. But the question had been waiting in him since the stair, since the locket, since the burned page: *how does my sister know the etiquette of hell?*

Wren looked at him. In the gold light her face was open in a way it almost never was, and for a moment he thought—foolishly, hopelessly—that she might finally tell him everything, right here, under the eyes of ten thousand witnesses.

Then a bell rang.

It wasn't a sound so much as a *conclusion*—a single tone that passed through the cathedral and left silence behind it like a wake. Every courtier stopped. Every bottle's glow steadied. Even the vellum floor seemed to hold its breath, and Juniper felt the fine print beneath his boots shift, rearranging, like ants rerouting around a dropped crumb.

Queen Vess set down her pen.

"Visitors," she said, and her voice carried without effort to every corner of the vastness, gentle as a hostess greeting guests at a door. "How rare. How *wonderful*." She stepped around the desk, and the seven-souled crown tilted its light to follow her. "Brannoc. Still alive. I'd have bet against it."

"Queens shouldn't gamble," Brannoc said. "Bad example."

"And yet here you are, gambling anyway. Bringing me something I've wanted for a very long time." Her gaze slid past him—to Wren, and lingered, warm and terrible, the look of a creditor greeting collateral. Then it moved to Juniper.

It stopped moving.

For three full seconds the Broker Queen of the Undercourt said nothing at all, and in that silence Juniper heard, faint and clear as a bell struck in another room, the sound of her interest re-pricing itself upward. He felt the whole cathedral lean in—the bottles, the floor, the patient-eyed courtiers—all of them suddenly aware of him the way he had spent his entire life making sure no one ever was.

"There you are," Vess said softly. "I wondered when the gatekeeper's blood would walk in my door."

Beside him, Wren's breath caught. Ahead, Brannoc took one careful step backward, a man distancing himself from a debt. And high above, in the blue-tinged glass, the midwife's soul flickered—once, twice—like a candle guttering in a draft, as if even the dead were trying to warn him.

Juniper lifted his chin, and did the only thing a keeper of the Saltgate knew how to do in the presence of an account come due.

He prepared to negotiate.
