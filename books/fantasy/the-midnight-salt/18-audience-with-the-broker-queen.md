## 18. Audience with the Broker Queen

The doors to the throne room were made of teeth.

Not metaphorically—Juniper checked, because he had learned in three days beneath the mountain that the Undercourt punished assumptions. Actual teeth, thousands of them, set in rows of ivory lattice, and when he laid his palm against one to steady himself he heard it remember chewing. Not food. Something older. He pulled his hand back and did not touch them again.

"State your names," said the herald, a tall figure whose face was a smooth blank oval, like wax waiting to be useful.

"Wren Brightwater," Wren said, her voice steadier than Juniper felt. "And my brother, Juniper."

Something moved through the hall at his name. A rustle, almost—like paper turning pages all at once. The bottled souls lining the walls in their thousand glass vessels flickered, every flame leaning toward him for half a heartbeat before righting itself. Brannoc, walking behind them with the easy slouch of a man among friends, went very still.

*They know your name here,* Juniper thought. *Of course they do. This is where it was spent.*

He kept his eyes forward. The hall stretched away from them longer than the mountain should have allowed, its ceiling lost in dark where chandeliers hung upside down like roots, each one blazing with bottled light. The floor was polished black stone, and Juniper could see reflections in it that didn't match the people standing above them. His reflection was looking slightly off to the left, at something he couldn't see.

Don't look down, Odell would have said. Or rather: look down once, learn what's there, then never again. That had been the guild way. Knowledge was a tool; obsession was a debt.

Juniper looked down once. His reflection smiled at something. He looked up and walked on.

* * *

Queen Vess sat on a throne of stacked contracts.

That was the only way Juniper could describe it—tens of thousands of documents, folded and pressed and fused into a seat, their edges fanned out like feathers, their ink still faintly wet after however many centuries. She was smaller than he'd expected, a narrow woman in grey silk with hair the color of unpolished silver, and she might have been someone's grandmother if not for her eyes, which were the exact amber of sealing wax held to a flame.

"You've brought guests," she said, and her voice was warm. That was the worst part. It was genuinely, sincerely warm, the way a hearth is warm while the house around it burns. "Brannoc. You're late."

"The mountain roads were bad," Brannoc said.

"The mountain roads are always bad. It's why I pay you." She turned that warmth on Wren, and Juniper watched his sister stand under it the way you'd stand under falling rock. "Wren Brightwater. Twelve years since your signature crossed my desk. You've kept well."

"I've kept," Wren said.

Vess laughed—a real laugh, quick and bright, gone as fast as it came. "Honesty. Rare, above ground." Her gaze slid to Juniper, and he felt it the way he felt the wards at the Saltgate: a pressure, a weighing. "And this is the famous brother. Juniper. Do you know how many contracts mention you? Neither do I—I'd have to count, and I own a great many counting houses. Sit. Both of you. This needn't be unpleasant."

There were chairs. Of course there were chairs—comfortable ones, upholstered in something soft and grey, placed exactly where guests would want them, which meant they'd been placed by someone who knew precisely what guests wanted. Juniper sat because refusing comfort in the Undercourt would be its own kind of signature. Wren sat beside him. Brannoc drifted to the wall and became furniture himself.

"I'll be direct," Vess said, folding her hands. "You've come about the contract. I admire that. Most debtors spend their audience hours pretending they've come to discuss the weather. You—" she nodded at Wren—"came down a mountain in winter. That's arithmetic. I respect arithmetic."

She lifted one hand. Somewhere behind the throne, paper whispered.

A document came forward through the air—not carried, simply *arriving*, drifting down like a leaf and settling into her palm. Juniper heard it before it landed. That was the trouble with his gift; he couldn't not listen. Objects remembered, and this object remembered everything.

It remembered a girl of seventeen, cold and frightened, in a room like this one. It remembered the weight of the pen. It remembered the smell of the ink—silver, Juniper realized, the same silver that hummed in Wren's locket—and it remembered the girl's hand shaking so badly the first attempt at her name had to be blotted and begun again. And underneath all of it, threaded through every line like a spine:

*For the safety of Juniper Brightwater. For his passing unseen. For his name to slip the hunter's eye, the crown's list, the burning year.*

Twelve years of hiding, purchased in advance. Every road that forgot him, every witch-hunter who looked straight through him at some more interesting prey—it hadn't been luck or talent. It had been bought. With his sister's soul. Paid out slowly, like an annuity, and now the account stood empty and midwinter was four days away.

Juniper discovered his hands were shaking. Wren's hand found his knee and pressed, once: *not now.*

"So," Vess said. "You've read it. Good. I find contracts read aloud are theater, and contracts read silently are understood." She let the page rest on her knee. "Now. The terms are simple, and I want you to hear me say that I mean them fairly. Fair dealing is the whole of my law. It is why this court has stood ten thousand years while empires above us rotted. I honor every agreement to the letter, and the letter is generous."

"A soul of equal weight," Wren said flatly. "By midwinter."

"A soul of equal weight, delivered freely, by midwinter dawn." Vess inclined her head. "Or the contract executes, and you remain. Forever, yes, though I dislike that word—forever implies no further negotiation, and there is always further negotiation. But effectively forever."

"And what counts as equal?" Juniper asked.

His voice came out rougher than he intended. Vess regarded him with something that looked almost like pleasure.

"Ah. There's the question. Weight isn't size, gatekeeper. A soul's weight is what it cost, and what it was worth, and what it would have become." She ticked points off on pale fingers. "A murderer's soul weighs little—it was already spent. A child's weighs enormously—not for innocence, that's sentiment, but for potential. Your sister sold me a young woman's soul at the height of its becoming, plus interest accrued across twelve years of deferral." She smiled, and the smile had too much understanding in it. "So no, you cannot fetch me a beggar from the snow and call the account settled. The scales are not sentimental. They are, however, honest. Bring me a soul of true equal weight, freely given, and Wren Brightwater walks out of my court owing nothing to anyone."

"And if we don't?"

"Then at dawn on midwinter day, she stays. Her body will climb back up your mountain, because I don't deal in bodies—bodies are clutter—but everything else, everything that accumulates in a person over a lifetime, remains here. On deposit." Vess said it the way a banker says *on deposit*, which was to say: without cruelty, without kindness, as simply the shape of the world. "You may visit. Souls in residence receive visitors. It's encouraged. Loneliness devalues the asset."

Juniper's stomach turned. *Devalues the asset.* Wren, laughing at him across a childhood kitchen; Wren teaching him to whistle through a blade of grass; Wren lying badly about a winter and flinching from his gift—all of it filed, shelved, accruing interest in a glass bottle on a wall.

"You're offering us a fair deal," he said carefully, "in a place where fairness is the trap."

Vess tilted her head. "Is it a trap to tell a drowning man the water's depth?"

"It is if you're the one who dug the lake."

For a moment—just a moment—the hearth-warmth banked, and Juniper saw what was underneath: something old and patient and appraising, a creature that had watched ten thousand families do this arithmetic and knew every wrong answer by heart. Then the warmth returned, seamless, and she spread her hands.

"You mistake my role. I dug nothing. The lake was here when I found it. I merely built the stairs." She rose, and the contract-throne rustled like a field of wheat. "I'll tell you what I tell every debtor. I am not your enemy. I am your counterparty. Your enemies are time, and appetite, and the things people want that they cannot afford. I simply keep the books." She glanced at Wren. "Your brother has a talent for books, I'm told. Perhaps he can appreciate the distinction."

"How do you know about his—" Wren started.

"My dear, everyone down here knows about the Brightwaters. We have long memories. It's practically our trade." Vess began to walk, slow, along the front of the dais, her footsteps silent on stone that reflected her perfectly and matched her not at all. "Which brings me to a courtesy. Because I honor fair dealing, I'll tell you what the fair options are. Most brokers wouldn't. Consider it a discount on honesty."

She held up one finger. "One: pay. Find a soul of equal weight. Difficult, ugly, possible. People manage it. I have a wing of this court full of souls traded by parents, lovers, children—one memorable fellow paid with his own reflection, which is a story for another evening. The point is: the door exists."

A second finger. "Two: default. Dawn comes, she stays, life goes on. I won't pretend it's painless, but it's clean, and clean matters. Half the misery in my archive comes from debtors who tried cleverness instead of cleanliness."

A third finger, and here she paused, and the amber of her eyes caught the bottled light. "Three. Negotiate. Which brings me to something I suspect you already carry, Juniper Brightwater, because Brannoc has a loose tongue and I have excellent hearing."

Juniper felt Brannoc shift against the wall. Felt, more than saw, Wren go rigid beside him.

"The founding ledger," Vess said. "Or pieces of it. Pages your guild tore out and scattered a hundred years ago when they decided they'd rather forget what they built. One of those pages came through my market last month. I bought it. I've read it. And it mentions—" she smiled again, and this smile was different, narrower, hungrier— "a clause. An old clause. Older than me, which is saying something. A clause about substitution. About what a keeper's name weighs."

The hall was very quiet. Even the bottled flames seemed to lean in.

"I haven't offered you anything yet," Vess said softly. "I want that on the record. I have produced your sister's contract, stated her terms, and extended a courtesy regarding information you'd have learned eventually. Everything else—" she opened her hands, empty, the gesture of a merchant displaying honest scales— "is yours to bring to me. Come to my desk before midwinter dawn with a proposal, and I will weigh it fairly. That is a promise, and my promises are load-bearing."

*She wants the ledger,* Juniper thought. *No—she wants what's in it. She wants me to walk into her office and put it on her scale.* Every instinct Odell had ever taught him rang like a struck bell: *the fairest dealer in any market is the one selling the scales.*

"We'll consider our options," he said.

"Consider them quickly." Vess resumed her seat, and the warmth closed over the hunger like water over a dropped stone. "Midwinter is four days out. Time runs faster below, but deadlines above hold firm—the contract is signed in your world's ink, under your world's sky. Dawn on midwinter morning finds me... inflexible." She folded her hands. "Was there anything else? Visitors are usually crying by now. I'm rather disappointed."

Wren stood. "One thing."

"Yes?"

"When you weighed my soul, twelve years ago. When you priced it against his safety." Wren's voice didn't shake, and Juniper loved her fiercely for it. "What did it come to?"

Vess considered her for a long moment, and for the first time something almost gentle moved behind the amber.

"More than you knew," she said. "Less than you feared. Which is true of nearly everything." She waved a hand toward the doors of teeth. "We're done. Brannoc will see you to the guest quarters—they're comfortable, and the locks work from the inside, which is more courtesy than most courts extend. Rest. Eat. Do your arithmetic."

* * *

The doors of teeth swung shut behind them, and the sound they made was the sound of a book closing.

They walked in silence through corridors lit by dimmer bottles, past alcoves where indentured souls worked at desks without desks, writing contracts on air. Wren's jaw was set. Brannoc whistled tunelessly, which Juniper now understood was a performance, and a poor one.

At the first branching of the halls, when Brannoc paused to consult a door that remembered directions, Juniper stepped close and spoke low.

"She told us everything we needed to hear and nothing she didn't want us to know. What did she promise you for the founding ledger, Brannoc? Before we met. What was the price going to be named later?"

Brannoc's whistle died. He looked at Juniper with something that wasn't quite guilt and wasn't quite apology—something more like a trader caught between two markets.

"Funny thing about the queen," he said. "She told me to deliver you alive and unharmed, and to make sure you heard about that clause. Word for word, near enough. She *wants* you negotiating." He shrugged, palms open. "I just carry freight, Brightwater. What she wants you for—that's between her and you."

He walked on. Wren caught Juniper's arm.

"Whatever you're thinking," she whispered, "she named the clause out loud so we'd run to it. So we'd think there's a door marked escape and walk through it holding the one thing she actually wants."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you've got that look. The ledger look. Odell used to say you get it when you find a page that hums."

Juniper didn't answer. Because the truth was he'd been listening this whole time—to the doors, the floors, the walls of the corridor, all of it remembering, all of it whispering—and underneath the queen's warm arithmetic and Brannoc's careful shrug, one memory kept surfacing from the stone itself, old and buried and repeated like a warning carved where only objects would read it:

*A Brightwater came down before. A Brightwater negotiated before. And the last time a keeper's name touched the queen's scale, the inner gate nearly opened.*

Four days. And somewhere in this mountain, a desk was waiting for him, and a clause, and a price that had been named once before—with consequences the stone still flinched to remember.
