## 20. Brannoc's Price

Brannoc made his tea the same way every night: three pinches of black leaf, a splash of something from a flask he never explained, and water boiled over a fire that gave no smoke. He'd been doing it for six days now, on the long climb toward the mountain's back door, and Juniper had watched him do it every time without ever once seeing where the flask came from or went.

That should have been the first warning. A man who could make things vanish was not a man you wanted to owe.

They were camped in the throat of a ravine called, according to Brannoc, the Widow's Gullet—narrow enough that the stars showed only as a ragged seam overhead, cold enough that Wren had stopped complaining about it somewhere around noon and gone silent instead, which was worse. She sat with her back against the ravine wall, her locket in her palm, turning it over and over. Counting days. Four until midwinter. Juniper didn't need his gift to know what she was doing; he could read the arithmetic in her shoulders.

He was checking the ledger—the guild's ledger, the one he'd carried out of the burning Saltgate—when Brannoc settled down across the fire from him with his tin cup and said, conversationally, "You've been listening to my knife."

Juniper's hand stilled on the page.

"For two days," Brannoc went on. "Every time I sharpen it. You get that look. Like you're eavesdropping on a conversation you're not part of." He blew across his tea. "So. What does it say?"

"It remembers the forge it was made at," Juniper said carefully. "And the man who quenched it. And—" he hesitated, because this part felt like handling something sharp-edged even secondhand, "—the first thing it cut."

"Which was?"

"A rope. Holding a man over a gorge." Juniper met his eyes. "The knife's sorry about it."

Something moved behind Brannoc's face—not quite surprise, not quite amusement. "Sorry," he repeated. "The knife is sorry."

"That's how objects are. They don't have opinions so much as regrets." Juniper closed the ledger. "Why? Does that bother you?"

"No." Brannoc sipped his tea. "It confirms something, is all. I wanted to be sure before we got any closer to the door."

Juniper felt it then, the way you feel a floorboard give under your weight a half-second before it cracks. The ravine seemed colder. Wren had gone still across the fire, her locket hanging forgotten from its chain, her eyes moving between the two men with the wariness of someone who had grown up knowing when a conversation turned into an ambush.

"Sure of what?" Juniper asked.

Brannoc set his cup down on a flat stone with great care, the way a merchant sets down scales before weighing something valuable. "Your price," he said. "I told you I'd name it when the time came. The time has come."

"You said after we reached the stair."

"I said later. This is later. We're close enough now that I'd rather settle accounts while everyone can still walk away." He smiled, and it was the first genuinely honest smile Juniper had seen from him—a small, tired, almost apologetic thing, which somehow made everything that followed worse. "Here's what I want, Brightwater. Your gift."

* * *

For a moment Juniper simply didn't understand. It was such a blunt word for it. *Gift.* Not the hearing—that was only the surface of it, the trick he'd spent twelve years hiding—but everything underneath. The gatekeeping blood. The thing in him that had answered the sealed lock on the mountain's back door like a voice answering its own echo. The inheritance that had slept in his family for generations and woken, lately, like a banked coal breathed on.

"My gift," he said. "You want me to give up hearing what objects remember."

"I want what's under that." Brannoc leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight carving his face into planes and shadow. "I've been in the smuggling trade thirty years. I've run salt past customs wards, run souls past church censors, run one living ghost past the entire garrison of Corrow. And in thirty years I have never once seen a lock answer a man's blood. Locks don't do that. Doors don't do that. Unless there's something in the blood that doors were built to obey." He tapped the stone beside him. "That's not a party trick. That's an office. An inheritance. Maybe the last one in the realm, now Odell's ash. And there are people who will pay very, very well to own it."

"People," Juniper said. "Meaning the hunters."

"Meaning Captain Vane specifically." Brannoc said it without flinching, which took a particular kind of courage or a particular kind of emptiness. "She's been buying from me since before your guildhouse burned. Names, routes, ledgers' worth of little truths. She pays in gold and she pays on time and she has never once asked me to betray anyone who hadn't already made themselves my enemy." He spread his hands. "Until now."

"So you'll sell us."

"I'll sell the *gift*," Brannoc corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is that you can live without it. I've thought about this. Believe me, I haven't slept much thinking about it. But here's my arithmetic: Vane gets what she actually wants—which isn't you, it's the key you carry in your veins—and you and your sister walk down that stair tomorrow morning with my blessing and my silence. Everyone eats. Nobody burns."

Wren stood up. She did it slowly, deliberately, the way their mother used to stand up when the neighbors said something unforgivable at market—giving everyone present time to reconsider. "You let us trust you," she said. "Six days. Six days of your road and your fires and your miserable tea, and you let us believe you were helping us."

"I am helping you."

"You're selling us."

"Selling *him*," Brannoc said. "To a buyer who doesn't even know yet that the goods exist. That's the offer, girl. Take it or leave it, but don't stand there pretending I ever pretended to be a good man. I told you the price would come due. Prices always come due. Ask your brother—he balances ledgers for a living. He knows nothing in this world stays free."

"He can't sell it," Wren said. "Can you? Can you just—it's not a coat. You can't take it off and hand it over."

"Actually," Juniper said quietly, "she might be able to."

Both of them looked at him.

He hadn't known he was going to say it until it was out. But it was true, or true-adjacent, and lying to himself about it wouldn't help. He'd been feeling it since the back door of the world—the way the gatekeeping blood answered locks, the way it *wanted* to be used, the way it pooled toward any threshold like water finding a low place. Things that wanted to move could be moved. He'd seen Odell do stranger things with less.

"The Undercourt trades in exactly this kind of thing," he said. "Souls. Shadows. Second chances. If a gift lives in the blood—and it does, it's an inheritance, it passed down through my family like eye color—then it has weight. Weight can be priced. Price can be paid." He looked at Brannoc. "Queen Vess could take it out of me. Or I could trade it away myself, cleanly, if I knew the rite."

"Then we have a deal," Brannoc said.

"We don't have anything." Juniper stood too, and found he was steadier than he expected. Fear did that sometimes; it froze you into a shape that held. "Because here's what you've missed, smuggler, in all your careful arithmetic. You think the hunters want my gift so they can open doors. Open *a* door. One specific door, under one specific mountain, for one specific client wearing a crown." He let that sit. "And you think handing them the key buys you safety. But keys get used. And whatever that crown wants through the inner gate—whatever's been sealed down there since before the Guild of Impossible Things had a name—I promise you the payment for opening it won't stop at gold."

Brannoc's smile had faded entirely.

"You've been down there," Juniper pressed. "Or near it. You knew the back door existed, which means someone told you, which means you've heard the stories. What did they say happens to the villages above the old gates?"

Silence. The fire cracked. Somewhere far up the ravine, wind moved through stone like breath through teeth.

"They empty out," Brannoc said at last, grudgingly. "People leave. Or stop being people."

"And you want to put a key to that lock and take coin for it."

"I want to *live*," Brannoc snapped, and for one unguarded moment there was something raw underneath the smuggler's calm—something old and frightened. "Thirty years, Brightwater. Do you know what I've lost to this trade? Fingers. Friends. A wife who waited nine winters and then sensibly stopped. I'm fifty-one years old and every debt I've ever dodged is circling, and along comes one clean sale—one sale that sets me up beyond the border, past the reach of every broker and hunter and creditor in this rotten kingdom—and you want me to feel bad about it?" He laughed, short and ugly. "Feel bad on your own time."

"Then why tell us?" Wren demanded. "If you were going to sell him out anyway, why give us the chance to run?"

Brannoc looked at her. Then at Juniper. And Juniper, watching him with every sense he owned stretched wide—not just the hearing but the older, deeper thing, the gatekeeper's instinct for thresholds and crossings and the exact moment a person decides to step through one—saw it happen. Saw the man arrive at his own crossroads in real time.

"Because I wanted you to hear it from me first," Brannoc said. "Which is either honor or vanity, and I've given up trying to tell the difference." He rubbed his face with both hands, and when they came down he looked ten years older. "All right. Counter-offer. Since you're both so fond of arithmetic."

"I'm listening," Juniper said.

"You go down tomorrow. You deal with your queen and your sister's soul and whatever else is waiting under that rock. And if you come back up alive—if you come back up *at all*—you give me the gift yourself. Freely. By whatever rite does it cleanly. No hunters involved, no Vane, no crown. Just you, me, one transaction, done proper." He stuck out his hand, callused palm up, over the fire. "Because you're right about one thing. I don't trust the hunters to use a key responsibly. But I trust *me*. And a gatekeeping gift in the hands of a man whose whole life is doors and passages and getting things through unmolested—that's not a betrayal, Brightwater. That's a retirement plan with a conscience."

Juniper stared at the offered hand.

It was, he had to admit, a good bargain. That was the trap of it. Brannoc had learned his trade well: the worst offers always wore the clothes of reasonable ones. Sell the gift to Vane and it became a weapon aimed at the last seal in the world. Refuse outright and Brannoc sold him anyway, tonight, by signal-fire or courier hawk, and Vane's hunters came down the Gullet before dawn with iron and oil. But accept the counter-offer—

Accept it, and he walked into the Undercourt already half-sold. Already planning his own dismemberment. How do you negotiate with a soul-broker queen when you've promised the best part of yourself to another buyer? How do you weigh a soul against a name against a gift when your own scale keeps tipping?

And underneath all of it, quieter and more terrible: some traitorous part of him *wanted* to say yes. Wanted it the way a tired man wants to set down a heavy load. Twelve years of hiding. Twelve years of flinching from every doorknob and hinge and wedding ring that had something to say. The gift had never once let him rest. There was a version of himself, small and exhausted and shameful, that heard *you can live without it* and felt not horror but relief.

Wren's hand closed over his wrist. Not gripping. Just anchoring. Her eyes said: *whatever you choose, choose it awake.*

Juniper looked at Brannoc's palm, at the old burn scars crossing it like a map of everywhere he'd ever been.

"One amendment," Juniper said.

Brannoc's eyebrow rose.

"After the Undercourt—after Wren walks free—I'll consider your offer honestly. Fairly. On my word, which Odell taught me is worth more than a signed contract because it can't be forged." He didn't take the hand yet. "But I keep the gift until then. All of it. Because tomorrow I'm going to need every drop of my grandmother's blood to get past Queen Vess, and if you take even a taste of it tonight, you're not buying a retirement—you're spending my sister's life to fund it."

The silence stretched. Firelight. Wind. Wren breathing beside him, four days left, each one audible.

"And if you die down there?" Brannoc asked. "What do I get then?"

"The ledger," Juniper said. "The founding one. I've seen fragments of what's written in it—what my family did, what we built, what we traded to build it. It's worth more than my gift, Brannoc. It's worth more than *ten* gifts. It's the deed to everything the Undercourt thinks it owns." He let that land, watching the smuggler's eyes flicker with avarice and suspicion in equal measure. "My life against a document. Those are your odds. Take them or sell me to Vane tonight and explain to her why the key she bought opens nothing anymore, because dead men's blood answers no locks I've ever heard."

Another silence, longer. Brannoc studied him the way he'd probably studied a hundred cargoes across a hundred borders—looking for the flaw, the false bottom, the thing sewn into the lining.

Then he laughed. Once, short, real.

"Odell," he said, shaking his head. "That old woman always did hire people too clever to rob." He withdrew the hand, drained his tea, and stood. "Amendment accepted. You keep your gift till you're back above ground with your sister breathing free air. Then we settle. My word on it."

"Your word," Juniper echoed. "Which is worth—"

"—more than a contract, yes, yes." Brannoc was already turning toward his bedroll, waving dismissal like a man closing shop. "Sleep, Brightwater. Tomorrow you meet the queen. You'll want your wits about you. She'll offer you something reasonable within the first five minutes, and the reasonable ones are the traps that catch."

He lay down with his back to the fire, and within moments his breathing slowed into the practiced rhythm of a man who had slept in dangerous places for thirty years and could summon sleep like a tool.

Juniper stayed by the flames a while longer, the ledger open on his knees though he wasn't reading it. Across the fire, Wren had picked up her locket again. Turning it. Counting.

"Four days," she said softly.

"Three and a half."

"Do you believe him? About wanting it from you instead of the hunters?"

Juniper listened—not to her, but to the camp around them, the way he'd learned to listen to rooms. To Brannoc's knife, wrapped in its roll at the smuggler's hip: remembering the forge, remembering the gorge, and now, faintly, newly, remembering something else. A resolve, folded into steel the way a letter is folded into a pocket. Not malice. Not quite.

*Patience,* the knife remembered its owner deciding. *Let him open the doors first.*

"Yes," Juniper lied, and closed the ledger, and wondered what else in the founding archive below might tell him what his grandmother had really traded—and whether there was a clause anywhere in it that could save him from the bargain he'd just struck.

Above the ravine, the ragged seam of stars was beginning to pale. Midwinter was coming down the mountain to meet them.
