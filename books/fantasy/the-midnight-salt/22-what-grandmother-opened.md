## 22. What Grandmother Opened

Wren had never been good at sitting still. Twelve years of running a bakery counter in Corrow had taught her hands to work while her mind wandered—kneading, folding, counting change—and so she did the same now with the torn page, turning it over and over beneath the lamp in Brannoc's smuggler's cabin while Juniper slept three feet away, exhausted past dreaming.

The page was older than anything she'd ever touched. That was the first thing. Not old like Grandmother's Bible, whose leather had cracked and flaked; old like stone, like the mountain itself, as if paper had simply agreed to be paper for a while and might change its mind. The edges were burned to lace. The ink had gone brown as dried blood, and half the words had faded into suggestions.

But not all of them.

She'd copied out what she could read onto the back of a flour invoice she'd carried from Corrow like a talisman, and now she smoothed it flat on her knee and read it again, for the dozenth time, letting the fragments arrange and rearrange themselves.

*...the Brightwater keeper shall hold the Saltgate against all comers...*

That part was easy. Guild boilerplate. Every ledger Juniper had ever described to her had language like that—*shall hold, shall record, shall witness*. She'd heard him recite it in his sleep once, when they were children sharing a bed in the cottage, before the winter she went away.

*...in exchange for which the founding family surrenders...*

The next three words were burned away entirely. Wren found herself pressing her thumb against the scorch mark as if she could feel the missing syllables through her skin. The page hummed against her fingers—not sound exactly, more like standing too close to a beehive inside a wall. Grief, Juniper had called it, when he'd first pulled this page from the oldest ledger back at the guildhouse, before the fire, before everything. He'd said objects remembered, and this one remembered something it didn't want to.

She was starting to understand why.

*...surrenders [burned] of every Brightwater born thereafter, unto the seventh generation and beyond, that the gate may be fed what it requires: [burned] [burned] remembrance...*

"Remembrance," she whispered. Her breath fogged the cold air of the cabin. Outside, somewhere far above them, wind moved across the mountain's shoulders, and the whole structure groaned like a ship.

Every Brightwater born thereafter.

Her hand wasn't shaking. She noticed that with a strange detachment, the way you notice the sky is the wrong color. Her hand was perfectly steady, and that felt like the most frightening thing in the room, steadier than it had any right to be, as if some part of her had already known and had been waiting years for the rest of her to catch up.

* * *

Juniper woke near dawn, or what passed for dawn down here where no light reached—the hour when Brannoc's guttering lamps burned lowest and the dark pressed hardest against the windows. He woke the way he always did, all at once, silent, his eyes finding her before his body finished sitting up. Even now, even after everything, that instinct made her chest ache. Twelve years apart and he still came awake ready to protect someone who'd spent those twelve years protecting him without his knowledge.

"You didn't sleep," he said.

"I slept."

"Liar." He rubbed his face. His hair stuck up on one side, gray coming in early at the temples, and for one absurd moment he looked nine years old again, caught sneaking biscuits. Then his gaze dropped to the papers spread around her, the copied fragments, the original page itself lying face-down where she'd finally covered it because looking at it made her teeth ache. "You've been reading it."

"I couldn't stop reading it." She gathered the fragments and handed them over, watched his eyes move, watched the color leave his face in stages, like a tide going out. "There's more. Keep going."

He kept going. She watched him reach the line about surrendering, watch his lips move through the burned-away words as if he could hear them the way he heard everything else—doorknobs and lockets and pages humming their grief—and then he stopped moving altogether.

"Wren." His voice came out strange. Flat. "This says we *made* the gate."

"I know."

"Not kept it. Not inherited it. Made it." He looked up at her, and she saw the thought land in him the way it had landed in her hours ago, heavy and irreversible. "The Saltgate. The guild. All of it. It's ours."

"Ours the way a debt is yours," Wren said. "Read the third fragment again. The bottom one."

He read it. She watched him read it, watched the exact moment he understood, because his hand rose slowly to his mouth and stayed there.

*...the founding family surrenders [burned] of every Brightwater born thereafter... that the gate may be fed what it requires... for the thing behind the inner gate does not sleep, and remembers, and would rise if any door it touched were left unremembered by its makers...*

"It eats memory," Juniper said. "The gate. Or—" He stopped. Started again. "We traded our memories to make it. To seal something. And every Brightwater since has been paying the toll without knowing there was a toll."

"That's why I can't remember Mother's voice." The words came out of Wren calm and terrible, and she let them. "I've thought about it my whole life, Jun. Everyone says grief blurs things, but it doesn't, not really—you lose the small stuff, not the whole sound of someone. I can't remember her voice at all. Or Father's laugh. Or Grandmother's face, only her hands. I used to think I was broken."

"You're not broken." He said it fast, fierce, reaching for her hand.

"No," she agreed. "I'm *paid*. We're paid. We're a family that's been paying taxes for two hundred years to a bank we didn't know existed." She laughed, and hated the sound of it, thin and wrong in the little cabin. "And you know what's worse? Read the last line. The one that isn't burned."

He read it aloud, quietly, the way you read a eulogy.

*...and should the line of Brightwater fail, or flee, or forget its charge, the seal shall thin, and the court below shall know freedom first among all doors.*

"The purge," Juniper said. "Years ago. When they hunted the gifted the first time—that's why you sold your soul, to hide me from it. But the hunters weren't just hunting gifted people, were they. They were hunting us specifically. Because if the Brightwaters die out—"

"The seal thins," Wren finished. "I know. I did the arithmetic a long time ago, Jun. I just didn't have the numbers until tonight." She took the page from him gently, folded it along its old creases, and tucked it inside her shirt against her heart like the locket she no longer wore—it was downstairs in the Undercourt's evidence vault, along with her soul, and thinking about that could wait, had to wait, because if she thought about it now she would come apart and there was no time to come apart. "Our family isn't a family. We're a lock. And somebody up top has decided locks are heresy."

* * *

Brannoc found them there an hour later, neither of them sleeping, the lamp burned down to a thumbnail of flame. The old smuggler filled the doorway the way weather fills a horizon, and his eyes went from the scattered papers to their faces and narrowed.

"Well," he said. "You've read it, then."

Wren went very still. "You knew."

"I suspected." Brannoc lowered himself into the chair by the door, joints cracking like green wood. "Been running cargo through these tunnels thirty years. You learn the shape of things. Learn that the Undercourt buys memories by the barrel, and most of 'em come from the same few bloodlines up north. Learn that the guild's ledgers always listed a Brightwater as keeper, generation after generation, and nobody ever asked why one family held the job for two centuries like it was a curse instead of a post." He spat, accurately, into the corner spittoon. "Learned enough to guess."

"Guess," Juniper repeated. There was an edge in his voice Wren had rarely heard—her gentle brother, her invisible brother, sharpening. "You guided us down here. You watched my blood open a lock that's been sealed since before the guild had a name. You suspected, and you said nothing."

"I said I'd guide you for a price to be named later," Brannoc said, unbothered. "That price just got clearer, is all. But it hasn't changed." He leaned forward, lamplight carving his face into ridges. "Here's what I'll give you free, since you look like folk who need it: whatever your grandmother opened—or built, or buried—it's still behind that inner gate. Vess wants it open. Has wanted it open longer than I've been alive. And the crown wants the Saltgate, which is the same door seen from the other side of the world. Everybody wants what your family locked away, boy. Nobody wants to know *why* it's locked." He stood, bones crackling. "Now you know why. Sleep on it. Dawn comes early in a place with no sun."

He left. The door boomed shut. In the silence, Wren realized her hands had started shaking only now that it was safe for them to, and she pressed them flat against her knees.

"Juniper." Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "If the seal needs Brightwaters—if it's been *fed* by us, all this time—then what happens to it when we're both down here? What happens to it when Vess takes my soul at dawn?"

He didn't answer right away. He was looking at the folded page against her chest, and his expression was one she'd never seen on him before: her careful, hidden, quiet brother wearing the face of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish.

"I don't think it's an accident," he said slowly, "that Vess set your collection date for midwinter. I don't think it's an accident that the crown launched its hunt the same season. I think everyone who wants these doors open has been waiting for one thing." He met her eyes. "For the last two Brightwaters to walk down here on their own feet."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere deep beneath them—beneath the cabin, beneath the stair, beneath the contract cathedral with its bottled souls—something vast shifted in its sleep, or seemed to, and the mountain groaned, and Wren felt the page against her heart hum its grief one note louder.

Above them, dawn was coming. Below them, everything else was.
