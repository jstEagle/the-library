## 23. Midwinter Eve Above

The snow came down on Corrow like a judgment nobody had ordered, and Ilsa Vane stood at the window of the Crown's Rest inn and watched it bury the city's sins one rooftop at a time.

Behind her, the common room roared. Midwinter Eve, and half the garrison was drunk on spiced wine, singing the old songs about the long night and the sun that always came back. Vane did not sing. She had stopped believing in things that always came back around her fourteenth winter, when the fever took her mother and the priests took the house and the crown took everything else and called it charity.

She turned a coin over in her fingers. Silver, minted with the face of a king who had been dead eleven years. Old coin still passed; new coin was worse.

"Captain." Sergeant Dree loomed up beside her, breath fogging the glass. "Dispatch rider's here. From the capital."

"At midwinter eve?"

"He wouldn't wait. Said it was sealed with the privy mark."

Vane looked at the envelope in Dree's hand. Black wax, pressed with the crowned key — not the seal of the Witch-Hunters' Office, which was a torch crossed with a sword, plain and honest about its business. The crowned key belonged to the Office of the Royal Household. The office that officially did not exist.

"Anyone see him arrive?"

"Just me."

"Burn his horse's shoes when he leaves. Both sets." She took the letter. "And Dree — if anyone asks what the rider brought, it was the usual. Lists. Encouragement to greater zeal."

Dree nodded and melted back into the noise of the room, and Vane carried the letter upstairs to the room she'd paid for out of her own purse so no one would know which bed she slept in.

She broke the wax with her thumbnail.

*Captain Ilsa Vane,*

*Your campaign proceeds with His Majesty's full confidence. Your zeal is noted. Your discretion is assumed.*

*Amendment to standing orders, effective immediately: regarding the fugitive designated Juniper Brightwater.*

*He is not to be killed. He is not to be harmed beyond necessity of capture. He is not to be interrogated by any officer below your own rank. He is to be delivered, alive and whole, to the undercroft gate of the royal residence in Corrow, where he will be received by persons authorized to receive him.*

*The woman, Wren Brightwater, remains subject to standing orders. The smuggler Brannoc remains subject to standing orders. The Brightwater man alone is exempt.*

*You will note that this exemption extends retroactively. Any report of injury previously inflicted upon him should be amended for accuracy.*

*Burn this letter after reading. Burn it thoroughly.*

*There is no signature. There was never a signature. You received no letter.*

Vane read it three times.

Then she sat down on the edge of the narrow bed and laughed, once, without any humor in it at all, because she had been a witch-hunter for nineteen years and she knew exactly what a letter like that meant.

It meant the hunt had never been a hunt.

* * *

She thought about the guildhouse fire.

She had been there, at Ashes Before Dawn, as they said now in the taverns, though nobody who said it had been inside. She had watched Odell the keeper die holding his own front door, an old man with iron keys on his belt and nothing in his hands but the door itself, and she had written *resisted arrest, casualties unavoidable* in her report because that was what reports were for.

But she remembered something else from that night. Something she had not put in any report.

When they brought Juniper Brightwater's trail to her — the ledger pages, the witness accounts, the burned remnants of the guild's records — there had been a moment, sorting through the ash, when one of her corporals held up a scrap of vellum and said, *Captain, this one's warm.* Hours after the fire. A scrap of paper, warm as a living hand, humming almost, if paper could hum. She had taken it herself and felt it: something moving underneath the surface, like water under ice, like a voice too far away to make out words.

She had reported it as residual enchantment. That was the correct term. It went in the file and the file went to the Office and the Office filed it somewhere dark and that was the end of it.

Now she wondered who had read that file. Who had pulled the thread.

Because here was the thing Vane understood, the thing nineteen years taught you if you were paying attention instead of just zealous: the crown did not care about witches. The crown cared about *things*. Objects. Levers. The crown was a machine for finding levers, and every few years it discovered a new kind of lever growing somewhere in its kingdom, and then men like her were sent out with swords and fire to dig the lever up before anyone else got their hands on it.

Except this time they didn't want it dug up. They wanted it *delivered*. Alive and whole. Retroactive exemption for prior injuries.

That wasn't the language of extermination. That was the language of acquisition.

*Bring him to the undercroft gate,* the letter said.

Undercroft. Gate.

Vane got up and walked to the window again. Below, the city glowed with festival fires, little orange eyes in the snow, and beyond the rooftops, far to the north, the mountain stood against the stars with its crown of cloud, the mountain everyone knew and no one visited, the mountain with the sealed door in its roots that appeared in exactly zero official documents and in every nursery story ever told.

Everyone knew what was under the mountain. Nobody said it above a whisper. And the crown, which officially believed in nothing but law and order and the divine right of kings, had a private office whose seal was a *key*, sending letters that didn't exist to captains who would deny receiving them.

She thought about the phrase the informants kept using. The phrase that had come up in Corrow's undercity, in the market where impossible things changed hands: *the debt at midwinter*. Her people had heard it secondhand, thirdhand, garbled. Debt. Midwinter. The Brightwaters.

Tonight was Midwinter Eve.

* * *

"You're not drinking," Dree said, appearing at her door an hour later with two cups of the garrison's terrible mulled wine, because it was Midwinter Eve and even sergeants observed the forms.

"I'm working."

"It's midwinter. Even the witches take the night off."

"The debt collects at midwinter," Vane said, and watched Dree's face go carefully blank, the way faces did when you touched something people only discussed in whispers. "You've heard it too. In the market talk. Everyone down there says it like they're afraid the walls are listening."

"They say the court beneath the mountain keeps accounts," Dree said slowly, setting down the cups. "My gran used to tell it different. She said the Brightwater family *made* the account. Made the book it's all written in." He shrugged, embarrassed. "Nursery stories."

"Nursery stories don't get people burned out of their houses, Sergeant." Vane took the cup anyway, mostly to have something to hold. "Tell me about the gate."

"What gate?"

"The one under the mountain. The one your gran told stories about."

Dree shifted his weight. Nineteen years, and Vane could read him like a drill manual. He knew something. Everyone from the northern provinces knew something; it was bred into them along with the accent and the habit of locking doors against weather that hadn't arrived yet.

"My gran said there were two doors," he said finally. "One for the court, where the bargains happen. And one behind it, older, that the first keepers shut with something worth more than keys." He looked at her. "She said the family that shut it gave up their name to do it. All their names, forever. That's why you can't find a Brightwater in any record older than sixty years, Captain. I've looked. We've all looked. It's like the family invented itself out of thin air."

"And now the crown wants the last Brightwater delivered to a gate."

"To a *door*, Captain. Not a gate." Dree's voice had gone quiet. "Gates open both ways. Doors are meant to stay shut." He paused. "Why does the crown want him? Why not kill him like the others?"

Vane looked at the cold hearth, at the letter already curling in the flames she'd fed it ten minutes ago, black-edged and obedient.

"Because someone above us has decided he's more useful open than dead," she said. "And whoever that is, they're paying for my obedience with orders I'm not allowed to question, signed by an office that doesn't exist, on the holiest night of the year." She drank the terrible wine. "Which means either they think we're stupid, or they think we're loyal, and I haven't decided which insult I prefer."

* * *

She spent the rest of the evening doing what she did best, which was arithmetic.

Two fugitives, last seen fleeing south toward the mountain roads with a smuggler named Brannoc, a man with a price list instead of a conscience. Three days ahead of her riders at most, slowed by snow. One pass through the village of Hollowmere — the shadow-village, the one that made even hardened troopers sign superstitious charms before riding through — and then the high road to the mountain's back stairs, if the stories were true, and Vane had learned the hard way that the stories were always true and always worse than advertised.

Her orders said bring Juniper Brightwater alive. Her orders said nothing about what happened when she found him standing in front of a door the crown wanted opened, next to a sister whose soul was collateral on a debt collecting at dawn, in the middle of a negotiation between a broker queen and a family that had traded away its own memory.

Her orders assumed she'd be on time.

They also assumed she'd be willing.

Vane sat with that word for a long time. *Willing*. She had been willing for nineteen years. Willing to burn the guildhouse. Willing to write *casualties unavoidable* over Odell's body. Willing to believe that zeal was the same thing as service, right up until the moment a letter with no signature told her that the service had a client she'd never been allowed to see, and the client wanted a live gatekeeper the way a man wants a live key — undamaged, unexamined, and ignorant of what it unlocks.

Somewhere out in the dark, past the festival fires, past the snow, a young man who could hear what objects remember was walking toward a mountain with his sister's soul in his pocket. And somewhere above him, behind a door with a crowned key on it, someone was waiting to use him.

She thought about the warm scrap of vellum. About how it had hummed. About how, holding it, for half a heartbeat, she had heard — or thought she heard, which amounted to the same thing — a voice saying a name she didn't recognize, patient as water, like something that had been waiting a very long time to be asked.

She had reported it as residual enchantment.

She had not slept properly since.

* * *

At midnight, the bells rang in the old year and the bells rang in the debt, and Vane stood on the inn steps in the falling snow and gave her orders.

"We ride at first light. Full company, sledges for the pass, chains for prisoners." She let her gaze move across the assembled troopers, forty faces orange-lit by the festival lanterns, young men and women who believed they were hunting witches. "Targets are the Brightwater siblings and the smuggler Brannoc. Capture priority on the male. Questions?"

A young corporal raised a hand. "Alive, Captain? Standard interrogation protocols?"

"Alive," Vane said. "Unharmed. He's worth more to the crown breathing."

She did not say *to whom in the crown*. She did not say *delivered to a door*. She did not say *I have orders inside my orders, and the inner set frightens me more than anything we've burned in nineteen years*.

The company cheered, because it was midwinter and they were young and they still believed the hunt was simple.

Vane went back inside, sat down, and took out a fresh sheet of paper. Not a report. Something else. A list, private, in her own shorthand, of everything she knew and everything she suspected and everything she had chosen not to look at directly for nineteen years.

At the bottom of the list, after a long while, she wrote a single line:

*Find out who holds the key before the key finds out it's being used.*

Then she folded the list into her breast pocket, over her heart, where she kept the other things she couldn't burn, and looked north through the frosted glass toward the mountain, invisible now behind the snow but as permanent in her mind as an unpaid bill.

First light. Forty riders. One pass through Hollowmere, where the shadows had all been sold and the empty people would watch her column go by with eyes like unlit lamps.

And beyond it, at the bottom of the world, a brother and a sister running out of night.

"Midwinter," Vane said to the empty room, and reached for her sword belt. "Let's see who's been keeping the books."
