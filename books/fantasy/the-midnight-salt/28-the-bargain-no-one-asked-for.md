## 28. The Bargain No One Asked For

They put Wren in a cell of salt-veined stone, deep enough in the mountain that the dark had a texture to it, and left her alone with the sound of her own heart.

She had expected chains. Witch-hunters were supposed to use cold iron and blessed rope, the way the proclamations promised. Instead Captain Ilsa Vane's people had simply walked her down a stair that went on too long, opened a door that wasn't locked because it didn't need to be, and said, *Wait here.* As if she were a parcel. As if there were nowhere in the world she could run that they couldn't follow.

Maybe there wasn't. The Undercourt was Vane's now, or would be by dawn. The battle in the contract cathedral had ended with hunters among the bottled souls, their lantern-light making the whole terrible place glow like a festival, and Juniper running—she'd seen him go, ledger clutched to his chest like a child, toward the inner gate with three hunters shouting behind him.

That had been an hour ago. Maybe two. Time moved strangely down here, pooling in the corners.

Wren sat on the stone bench built into the wall and did the thing she was best at, which was arithmetic.

Juniper was somewhere below her with a book full of ghosts and a door he should never open. Queen Vess had her soul under contract, collectible at dawn—which was coming, she could feel it in her teeth, a thin bright ache like biting foil. And Ilsa Vane had her, Wren Brightwater, in custody, which meant Wren was not a prisoner so much as a piece on a board, and every piece on a board could be spent.

*Spent,* she thought. *There's a word I know.*

* * *

The door opened without warning. No knock—the hunters didn't seem to believe in them—but Vane came in alone this time, which was itself information. She carried no lantern; the corridor outside gave enough light to see her by, a tall shape in grey wool with frost still melting on her shoulders from the world above. She looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"You're awake," Vane said.

"I've been doing sums."

"Most people pray."

"I'm better at sums." Wren stood. Her legs ached from the stair, but standing mattered. She refused to have this conversation sitting down, looking up. "How much trouble are you in, Captain?"

Vane's expression didn't change, but something behind it did—a flicker, quickly banked. "You're presuming."

"I'm good at reading ledgers," Wren said. "And you're one. Your orders say bring the gifted in alive, don't they? But your orders tonight said take the girl alive and let the boy run. You didn't chase him properly. I watched you. You had him at the third archway and you called your people off."

"He had the founding ledger."

"And you let him keep it." Wren took one step closer. The cell was small enough that it felt almost intimate, two women and all that salt-dark between them. "Someone above you wants that book more than they want my brother dead. Someone who told you not to kill him at all. Am I close?"

Vane was silent long enough that Wren heard the mountain settle, a groan of stone far overhead, like an old man turning over in bed.

"You're a dangerous woman," Vane said finally.

"No. I'm an honest one. It's rarer and worse." Wren folded her arms. "Here's what I know about you, Captain. You burned the guildhouse in Corrow. You killed Odell Marsh, an old man holding a door. You've done your duty for years and slept anyway, because the gifted were strangers to you, abstractions, sins with faces. And then you came down here, into this court, and you saw what's behind the inner gate."

That landed. She watched it land—Vane's jaw tightened, a small muscle jumping beneath the skin.

"You saw it," Wren pressed. "Through whatever crack there was, whatever leaked out when we opened the door a hand's width. You felt it look at you. Didn't you."

"Don't," Vane said quietly.

"It's still looking, isn't it? You can't stop feeling it. That's why you're down here talking to me instead of sleeping before dawn. Because you know now that everything above—the crown, the hunt, the commissions, the burnings—it's all just people pretending there isn't a door." Wren let that sit. Then, softly: "I grew up next to someone who made the same discovery. He kept a gate his whole life and no one ever knew his name. He died for a door, Captain. My mentor. So don't tell me I'm dangerous. Tell me what you're going to do at dawn."

* * *

What Vane did was nothing. What she did was stand there with frost water dripping off her cloak onto old stone, making a small irregular ticking sound, and Wren understood suddenly that this was the moment—not some dramatic turn, not a key handed through bars, just a tired woman deciding whether to lie.

"At dawn," Vane said at last, "the queen executes her contract. The girl's soul transfers to the Undercourt's keeping. Standard collection."

"And my brother?"

"The crown's orders are explicit. The gatekeeper is to be taken alive and delivered." A pause. "Delivered where is not my concern."

"But you're wondering."

"I don't wonder," Vane said. "I execute orders. It's why I'm still alive and most captains aren't."

"Then execute this one." Wren reached up and unclasped the locket.

It was warm. It was always warm—she'd stopped noticing years ago, the way you stop noticing your own pulse—and now, holding it out on her palm in the salt-dark, she felt it hum faintly against her skin, like a held breath. Inside it was the one thread of silver ink, the signature-memory, the small impossible fact of her brother that she had carried since she was eleven years old.

"My soul is already sold," Wren said. "It's spoken for. But a soul freely offered—" she had spent the night learning the court's grammar, listening through the walls, picking up the law the way you pick up a language in a port town— "a soul freely offered weighs more than a soul collected. Every broker knows it. Even your witch-fearers know it; it's half your theology. The gift given outweighs the gift taken."

"That's court-law," Vane said. "Not mine."

"It could be. You're standing in the court." Wren turned her hand over, letting the locket swing. "Here's the trade, Captain. I walk to that ritual at dawn and I give myself. Not taken. Given—willingly, gladly, with witnesses. In exchange, my brother walks out of this mountain untouched. Not captured. Not 'delivered.' Untouched, by your order, in writing if you write such things. And you get to be the captain who closed a contract cleanly instead of the one who fed another soul to whatever's behind that door."

Vane stared at her. "You'd do that."

"I did it once already," Wren said. "When I was eleven. Nobody witnessed it then. It bought twelve years and nobody ever thanked me, including me." Her voice caught, and she hated it, and she pushed through. "So yes. This time I want terms. This time I want it written down. This time I want someone to watch."

For a moment—one long drip of frost water—something moved behind Vane's eyes that might have been pity, or might have been recognition. A woman who had spent her life executing other people's bargains, being asked to make one of her own.

"The queen holds your contract," Vane said. "Not me. I can't release what I don't hold."

"I'm not asking you to release anything. I'm asking you to witness a new offer and carry it to her. A substitution, freely made. She's bound by her own law—you said so yourself, all of you keep saying it. Court-law. Let court-law save my brother."

"And if she refuses? If the crown's claim outranks mine?" Vane's voice was flat again, professional, but slower now, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "If the gatekeeper is worth more than your soul, then your sacrifice buys nothing and I've wasted the last hour of my life negotiating with a girl in a hole."

"Then I'll have lost nothing I wasn't losing anyway," Wren said. "But you'll have lost something. You'll have chosen to hand a willing soul to a collection and a keeper to a crown, and you'll feel it, the way you feel that thing behind the door feeling you. Every day. Forever." She stepped back, letting the locket rest against her breastbone again. "I don't think you want that. I think you came down here hoping I'd talk you out of it. Well. Consider yourself talked out of it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, muffled by fathoms of rock, came a sound like a bell struck underwater—dawn preparations, maybe, or the court waking, or just the mountain dreaming. Vane looked at the locket for a long moment.

"What is that?" she asked. "The metal. It doesn't look guild-made."

"It's a memory," Wren said. "The only one I have that nobody can take."

Something crossed Vane's face—fast, quickly gone—that looked almost like envy.

"I'll carry your offer," she said at last. "To the queen. That's all I promise. If Vess refuses, the contract stands and my orders stand, and you'll wish you'd never learned my name."

"I already wish that," Wren said. "But thank you."

At the door, Vane paused. She didn't turn around. When she spoke, her voice was low enough that the salt seemed to drink it.

"When we took the guildhouse in Corrow," she said, "there was a man holding the door. Old. He didn't fight. He just stood there and said, *The gate opens for those who need it, and closes for those who fear it.* Then the fire took him." She was quiet a moment. "I've thought about that sentence every day since. I don't know why I'm telling you."

"Because doors matter," Wren said. "And so does who's standing in front of them."

Vane left. The door stayed unlocked behind her—Wren noticed, and filed it away, and wondered if it was mercy or trap.

* * *

Alone again, Wren pressed her forehead against the cool stone wall and let herself shake, just for a minute, the way you let a dam shudder before you shore it up.

Because here was the truth under the trade: she hadn't offered Vane her soul to save Juniper. Or rather—she had, of course she had, that was the surface, that was true. But underneath, in the place where arithmetic became honesty, there was something else. Twelve years ago she'd made this bargain in secret, in the dark, alone, and it had cost her a winter and a weight and a self she'd never gotten back, and she'd carried it like a stone ever since, and some stubborn buried part of her had wanted—needed—to do it *again*, but this time in the light, with witnesses, with terms, with a name attached. To prove it hadn't been nothing. To make the first sacrifice mean something by repeating it on purpose.

*This time I want someone to watch.*

Well. Now someone had watched. And it hadn't made the stone lighter. It had made it heavier, somehow, and clearer: this was who she was. The sister who paid. The Brightwater who traded. It ran in the blood like salt runs in seawater, and fighting it was like fighting the tide.

But Juniper—he'd found another way, hadn't he? He'd gone down to that archive and come back with a plan that didn't require anyone's soul, only a name. *A name of equal weight.* He'd stood in front of the worst door in the world and refused to open it, and he'd found a clause, a loophole, a legalism dressed up as heroism, and she loved him for it and feared for him because she knew—if she knew anything—what names weighed.

He would pay with himself. He was already paying. And she had just tried to pre-empt him with the oldest coin the family owned.

*We're going to ruin each other saving each other,* she thought, and laughed, a short wet sound in the dark, and wiped her face with the heel of her hand.

Then she straightened, because there was work to do. If Vane carried the offer, Vess would weigh it—Wren had seen the queen weigh things, seen that terrible patient attention—and while she weighed it, there would be a gap. A moment of consideration, of interpretation, of lawyers' pause. And gaps were doors, and doors were the family business.

Wren began, methodically, to take stock. The door unlocked. The corridor beyond, patrolled lightly—hunters unsettled, spooked by the deep places, wanting to be anywhere else. The stair she'd been brought down. The cathedral above, where the ritual circle would be chalked by now, where dawn would find them all.

And somewhere in it, Juniper, with a ledger full of ancestors and a closing rite in his head and a price on it that made her sick to think about.

*Hold on,* she told him silently, to the dark, to the mountain, to wherever he was. *Don't you dare pay before I do.*

Far above—or far below; down here the directions blurred—a bell rang once, thin and clear, and the mountain began counting down to dawn.
