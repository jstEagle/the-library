## 35. Above Ground, Unnamed

The first thing I learned about being no one was that the mountain did not care.

Snow came down on me the same as it would have come down on anybody. Wind found the gaps in my coat and filled them with cold. My boots left prints in the drifts—real prints, deep ones, the kind a tracker could follow for miles if he thought to look. The world still registered me. It was only people who didn't.

I sat against a granite shoulder halfway down the mountainside and watched the hunting party pass below me.

There were nine of them, torches guttering in the wind, moving in that tight practiced wedge Captain Vane favored. I recognized her by her stride even at this distance—the long impatient swing of a woman who had been lied to by geography all day. They were following the trail of footprints I'd left coming up out of the Undercourt's back door, and they were following it badly, because the snow was filling my tracks faster than their lanterns could find them.

But that wasn't why they passed me. One of them—a young hunter with a crossbow slung across his back—walked within ten feet of where I sat. He looked straight through me. Not past me. *Through* me, the way you look through a window at a view you've stopped seeing. His eyes were the flat gray of a sky deciding whether to snow, and there was nothing behind them when they touched me. No flicker of curiosity. No animal prickle of *someone is here*. I might have been a boulder with opinions.

He stepped around the rock I leaned against without breaking stride, and kept walking, and was gone into the white.

My hands were shaking. I told myself it was the cold.

*You're free,* I thought. That was the word I kept reaching for. Free. Twelve years pressed small inside the Saltgate, invisible by discipline, invisible by habit, invisible by the careful architecture of being unremarkable—and now invisibility wasn't something I performed anymore. It was something I *was*. No ledger held my name. No memory held my face. Odell was dead and couldn't remember teaching me; Wren was somewhere below or above—I didn't know which, I couldn't hold the shape of her longer than a breath—and she couldn't remember having a brother. Even the doorknob, if some other keeper was listening to it now, would remember its maker before it remembered me.

Free. The word sat in my chest like a stone in a stream, and the water went around it, and nothing moved.

I took the locket out from under my shirt.

It was warm. That was the miracle of it, the one thread I had pulled through the closing rite: a locket full of remembered silver ink, holding the ghost of my own signature, the last place in creation where *Juniper Brightwater* existed as anything more than a body breathing on a cold hillside. When I opened it, the little hinge remembering its maker's thumb, I could almost hear the scratch of a pen. When I closed it, I could almost hear my own name.

Almost. The almost was the whole of my inheritance now.

* * *

I walked down off the mountain because staying still felt too much like agreeing to disappear.

That was the thing nobody warns you about erasure. It isn't quiet. Quiet would have been a mercy. Instead every hour rang with the absence, the way an empty room rings after the furniture's gone. I'd catch myself reaching for a name to say to the dark—*good morning, door*; *mind the third stair, it remembers falling*—and the dark wouldn't answer, because the dark had never heard of me either. Objects remembered everyone but me now. I'd press my palm against a fencepost on the road below the mountain, hungry for the hum of it, for the long slow memory of rain and nails and someone leaning tired against the wood—and the fencepost gave me everything, the whole wet history of itself, except any moment anyone had ever spent beside *me*. I had become the one thing in the world objects wouldn't keep.

A gatekeeper who can't be remembered. A keeper with no gate. I laughed at that once, alone on a frozen road, and the sound frightened me, because it echoed and the echo belonged to somebody else.

On the third day I reached the low country, and the low country taught me how deep the trade had gone.

There was an inn at a crossroads where the marsh met the hills—I knew it, we'd hidden there once, Wren and I, the night the hunters burned the guildhouse. I stood outside it in the sleet and watched the light in the windows and understood that I could walk in, order a meal, sleep in a bed, and leave coin on the table, and no soul under that roof would know a guest had come. The innkeeper would find the coin and invent a story to explain it. The bed would warm for no one. I could live like that. I could live entirely like that—stealing warmth from rooms that would never know they'd shared it—and the realization made me lean against the inn's outer wall and breathe until my lungs agreed to work again.

Instead I listened to the inn.

Old buildings talk if you ask them right, and this one had been standing since before the first purge, and it remembered things the way old soldiers remember campaigns: selectively, and with flinches. It remembered the night Wren and I had hidden in its attic—remembered her heartbeat going like a drum, remembered my hand flat against its beams saying *hold together, hold together, please.* It remembered that. It remembered the plea. It just couldn't attach it to anyone. There was a hole in the inn's memory shaped exactly like a man, and the edges of the hole were worn smooth, as though the building kept touching them, puzzled.

"You remember her," I whispered to the wall. "Tell me about her."

The wall obliged. Warmth of her, fear of her, the salt-smell of her cloak, the locket clicking against her collarbone as she shivered. And then—this was the part that undid me—the inn remembered what she'd said, there in the attic with the hunters' torchlight sliding under the door. She'd said, very quietly, to no one visible:

"He'll come back for me. He always comes back. You'll see. He's the only person in the world who never forgets anything, and he won't forget me."

She'd said it to the dark, to steady herself. She'd said it believing it.

I slid down the wall into the sleet and sat there like a man whose legs had been repossessed. Because she'd been wrong, hadn't she. I hadn't forgotten her—I couldn't, she was the last thing I had—but she had forgotten *me*, clean, completely, and whatever came looking for her out of the mountains would be a stranger wearing her brother's face. The locket might anchor a thread. One thread. Against a queen's law and a whole world's smooth forgetting.

Unless.

I turned that word over all night, camped in a haybarn whose timbers remembered forty good harvests and no Juniper. *Unless.* Unless the erasure worked both ways. Unless the thing that made hunters walk past me also made Wren's heart snag on me like cloth on a nail, some animal recognition deeper than memory. Unless love kept a copy the ledger couldn't reach.

Or unless I went and found her, and stood in front of her, and let the thread do its work.

* * *

It took me four days to understand that being unnamed had rules, and another three days to learn how to use them.

Rule one: doors don't argue with me. A locked door doesn't know it should stay shut against a man who doesn't exist, so locks open for my hand like flowers opening for weather. I tried not to enjoy this. I mostly failed.

Rule two: dogs are honest. Every dog I met went mad—hackles, barking, the full sermon—which meant dogs could see me fine. Animals don't consult ledgers. A shepherd's collie followed me for half a mile outside Corrow, pressing against my leg, whining, as though it were trying to give back something the world had taken. I fed it my last sausage and told it I was nobody, and it wagged, which was the kindest lie anyone offered me that week.

Rule three—the important one—the hunters can't hunt me anymore, but they can hunt *evidence*, and evidence remembers me perfectly. Footprints. Coin. A bed warmed. A door unlatched. Vane was thorough; Vane had looked through a crack in the inner gate and seen the thing the Brightwaters died keeping shut, and a woman who has seen that does not stop looking for the man who closed it. So wherever I went, I left traces, and Vane collected them, and somewhere in her reports a pattern was growing that pointed at a ghost moving south along the river road.

Toward Corrow. Toward the burned guildhouse. Toward wherever Wren had gone to ground.

I caught up with Vane's patrol on the sixth day, quite by accident, because they'd set their camp in a hollow I meant to sleep in. I lay in the bracken above them and listened, the way I used to listen to doorknobs—patiently, letting the memory come.

They were arguing about me. Or rather, about the absence of me.

"—no name on any roster," a sergeant was saying, turning a paper in his gloved hands. "No bounty posted. Captain, we've got a description and nothing else. You can't hang a reward on a man who officially doesn't exist."

"Then we post the description without the name." Vane sat apart from the fire, cleaning a blade that had no business being cleaned. Up close she looked worse than the mountain—hollowed, sleepless, the crack in the inner gate still living somewhere behind her eyes. "Tall. Dark-haired. Listens to walls. Walks like he's carrying a ledger. Anyone matching, report, don't engage." She snapped the whetstone down. "And if anyone asks why the crown wants an unnamed man brought in alive, you will tell them you don't know, and you will be telling the truth."

"And if we find him?"

Vane was quiet a moment. The fire cracked. Somewhere in the dark, a dog started up at nothing—at me—and was silenced.

"Then we find out whether a man can be arrested twice," she said. "Once by the law, and once by whatever he traded to get away from it."

They didn't find me. They never would, not lying ten feet below them in the bracken. But as I backed away down the slope I felt the shape of the trap she was building, and it was a clever one: she couldn't see me, so she would make the whole country into a net of descriptions, and descriptions travel faster than ghosts.

Which meant I had to move faster than descriptions. Which meant Corrow. Which meant finding Wren before Vane's paper ghost did—and doing it as a man who could stand in a crowded market and not be seen, could speak to a stranger and not be heard, could knock on his sister's door and watch her look through him the way the young hunter had looked through me on the mountainside.

I took the locket out one more time, there on the river road with the ice coming down the water in gray plates. Opened it. The silver-ink memory stirred, and for one heartbeat I felt my own name arrive in me like warmth arriving in cold hands—not *Juniper Brightwater*, the full weight of it, but the shape of it, the outline, the way a glove holds the shape of the hand that wore it.

"Hold on," I told it. "Both of us. Hold on."

Then I put it away, and turned south, and walked toward the city where nobody would ever remember I'd been born—except, God willing, the one person whose forgetting had a seam in it.

Behind me, far up the valley, a dog began to bark again. It sounded, if I let myself believe it, less like a warning than like a guide.

I let myself believe it. It was the first thing in six days that felt like breathing instead of drowning.
