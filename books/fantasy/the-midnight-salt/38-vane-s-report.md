## 38. Vane's Report

The report took Ilsa Vane four days to write, and she burned three drafts before she got it right.

Not right in the way her superiors would have meant it. Right the way a surgeon means it when he decides where to cut and where to leave the knife in. The report had to be true enough to survive scrutiny and hollow enough to protect what mattered, and finding the line between those two things was the hardest work of her career—harder than the descent, harder than the dark, harder than standing in that cathedral of bottled light while the mountain itself screamed through a gap no wider than a hand.

She sat in the garrison office at Corrow with a candle guttering beside her and wrote:

*Report of Captain Ilsa Vane, Witch-Hunter First Rank, on the operation beneath the Grayfell massif, midwinter.*

*Commissioned by writ of the Crown to apprehend gifted persons and secure artifacts of the so-called Guild of Impossible Things. Operation commenced with the burning of the guildhouse at Saltgate (see prior report). Subjects traced to the undercity of Corrow, thence to the mountain's back stair, guided—per intelligence received—an unnamed local smuggler.*

That much was true. Brannoc had sold them out for coin and spite, and Vane had paid him through a fence in Corrow without ever learning his real name. She suspected he hadn't learned hers either, which suited them both. There was a professional courtesy between people who sold other people.

*Subjects included one Wren Brightwater, taken into custody during the engagement in the contract cathedral. One male subject, presumed the sibling, fled deeper into the complex during the fighting and was not recovered.*

Presumed. Not recovered. Both words chosen like blades from an armory.

There had been a male subject. She had seen him—seen him clearly, twice, in the worst moments of her life. Once across the floor of the cathedral, holding a book against his chest like a man carrying a child out of a fire. And once afterward, on the mountainside, in the snow, when her squad had walked past him as though he were weather.

That was the part she could not put in any report, because there were no words in the Crown's approved lexicon for it. The man had stood ten feet from Sergeant Halloway. Halloway had looked directly at him. And then Halloway's eyes had slid off him like rain off waxed canvas, and the sergeant had said, *Clear this slope, Captain? Nothing here,* and Vane had said nothing, because she had felt it too—the smooth, total absence where a person should have been. Not invisibility. Invisibility would have been easier. This was worse: he had been *there*, fully there, present to every sense, and every sense had agreed, unanimously, instantly, to forget.

She had caught his eye. That was the thing that haunted her. For half a heartbeat, before the forgetting closed over like water, their eyes had met, and he had not looked afraid of her. He had looked at her the way you look at someone you have already forgiven for something they haven't done yet.

Then he was gone—not gone, *unremembered*—and she had stood in the snow with forty men and no prisoner and a report due.

*The female subject, Wren Brightwater, was released from custody under circumstances the undersigned does not fully understand,* she wrote, and stopped.

That was the second thing she couldn't say.

Wren Brightwater had offered Vane her soul. Freely. Openly, in front of her own soldiers, in a cell lit by a lamp that burned without oil. *Take it. Let my brother go untouched.* And Vane—who had taken souls before, who had signed the requisitions, who had delivered eleven gifted persons to the crown's assessors over six years and never once asked what happened to them after the wagon doors closed—Vane had hesitated.

Because she had already seen, by then, what a soul looked like when it was handled. She'd seen the shelves in the Undercourt. Rows upon rows of glass bottles, each holding a soft gold light that moved the way breathing moves, and each bottle labeled in silver ink, and the labels did not say names—they said *weights*. Twelve ounces of a miller. A pound and a half of a schoolteacher. She had understood, standing there, that somewhere in her own ledgers back at the garrison, in her own neat hand, she had written entries that ended up on shelves like that, and she had called it justice.

And then the girl had offered herself up like a coin, and Vane had heard herself say, *No,* before she knew she was going to say it.

She still didn't know why. She told herself it was tactics—a free soul freely given might be cursed, might be bait, might collapse the whole negotiation. But that was a lie she kept in the same drawer as the other one, the one about the mountainside, the one about the man nobody could remember.

*Released under circumstances the undersigned does not fully understand. No further trace of either subject has been found. The subterranean complex remains sealed. Recommend no further operations below the Grayfell.*

Recommend no further operations. Her superiors would choke on that line. They would want the archive. They wanted the archive very badly—she had read the sealed annex attached to her original orders, the one that wasn't about witch-hunting at all, the one that used words like *asset* and *access* and *the gate*. Someone above her rank had known the gate existed. Someone had wanted it opened, and had been willing to spend a captain and forty soldiers to get a key.

The key was ash now. Or stone. Or whatever a gate became when a keeper closed it with his own name. She had felt it close—felt it the way you feel a door slam in a house you're standing in, a pressure through the bones. And in the half-second before it sealed forever, something had looked out through the crack, and it had looked at *her*.

That was the third thing she couldn't write.

She had seen what lived behind the inner gate. Not seen-seen; her mind refused the shape of it even now, smoothing it over like a thumb smearing wet ink. But she had seen enough. Enough to know that Queen Vess—elegant, reasonable, courteous Queen Vess with her contracts and her scales and her soft terrible voice—was not the monster of that place. Vess was the *keeper* of the monster. Vess was the dam holding back something that made soul-brokers look like honest tradeswomen, and she had spent centuries doing it, and the first thing that thing had done when it glimpsed Vane through the crack was offer her everything she had ever wanted.

It had known what she wanted. That was the worst of it. It had reached into her the way you reach into your own pocket, and it had shown her her father, alive, unmaimed, the accident undone—and it had asked, politely, so politely, only that she open the door a little wider.

She had stepped back. She would like to tell herself it was discipline. It wasn't. It was terror, pure and animal, the terror of a woman realizing the thing offering her a gift could see all the way down into her and had found the bottom of her and found it shallow.

Her father had died nine years ago. The grief was old and worn smooth, a stone she carried in her coat pocket. But for one moment under the mountain she had held him again, whole and laughing, and she had wanted it so badly her knees had nearly buckled—and some cold vast patience had measured that wanting, weighed it, and smiled.

No. She couldn't write that. If she wrote that, some assessor would file it, and some minister would read it, and someone clever would start wondering whether the thing behind the gate could be bargained with from the *other* side. Whether the crown could hold what Vess held. Whether the crown should try.

Over her dead body. Which, if she filed the truth, it likely would be.

So she finished the report the way you finish a wound: clean, closed, and lying about the depth.

*Summary of findings: The guild's operations beneath the Grayfell constituted a containment measure of considerable antiquity. Its destruction or exploitation is assessed as gravely dangerous to the realm. The gate has been permanently sealed by parties unknown at significant cost. The undersigned recommends the site be struck from all records, marked as unstable ground on survey maps, and left undisturbed. The undersigned further recommends that all surviving documentation regarding the Saltgate guildhouse be destroyed, as its contents invite exactly the ambitions that the seal exists to frustrate.*

*Casualties: fourteen soldiers, listed by name in appendix. The undersigned requests commendations be entered posthumously and quietly.*

*Residual anomalies: none of note.*

None of note. The man on the mountainside. The girl walking free. The way her best tracker, Corporal Fen, had frowned at the snow for a full minute and said, *Captain, I keep feeling like we left someone up here,* and she had said, *We didn't,* and watched the feeling die in his face because she ordered it to.

She signed it. Vane. The ink dried gray in the candlelight, the color of old ice.

* * *

She delivered it personally. That was unusual—a courier could have carried it—but she wanted to see the face of whoever received it, wanted to watch the words pass through them and come out the other side as orders.

The receiving officer at the Ministry of Inquiry was a thin man named Corvess with ink-stained cuffs and the patient expression of someone who reads other people's sins for a living. He read her report standing, quickly, the way powerful men read things they've already decided about.

"The gate is sealed," he said. Not a question.

"Permanently, by every test I know."

"By whom?"

"Parties unknown." She let the phrase sit there, flat and impenetrable. "Whatever opened it spent itself doing it. There was nothing left to arrest. Nothing left to *remember*." She chose the word carefully and watched it sail past him, unnoticed, which was the whole point.

Corvess set the report down and laced his fingers. "Captain. My instructions from above were explicit. The gate was to be secured for the Crown."

"It can't be. I'm telling you it can't be. What was behind it—" She stopped. Steadied. "Minister, I am telling you as a soldier: there are places the Crown should want and cannot have. This is one. I would stake my commission on it."

Something flickered across Corvess's face—disappointment, yes, but underneath it, briefly, something that looked almost like relief. As if he'd been hoping someone would give him a reason not to follow his orders.

"You understand," he said, "that a recommendation like yours will end your advancement."

"I understand that I'm recommending it anyway."

"And your commission?"

She had rehearsed this part for four days too. But rehearsal, she found, meant nothing. The words came out plain, unwrapped, the way true things do when you finally stop dressing them.

"I'm resigning it. Effective on your acceptance of the report."

Corvess studied her for a long moment. Outside his window, Corrow went on being Corrow: carts, cries, a bell tolling noon across roofs furred with old snow. Somewhere in that city a girl with a restored soul was looking for a brother no one else could remember, and somewhere a man who had traded his name for her life was walking unseen past the hunters sent to find him, and none of it would ever appear in a ministry file.

"Your report will be accepted," Corvess said finally. "The site will be struck from the surveys. Your resignation—" he paused, and for just a moment the ink-stained clerk looked tired, human, older than his years, "—is also accepted, Captain. With regret, which I am instructed not to express, so please disregard that I have."

She almost smiled. "Disregarded, Minister."

"One question before you go." He tapped the report with one finger. "Fourteen dead, a gate sealed, a queen of the deep left unexamined—and you recommend we forget the whole business. You're not a woman who forgets easily. I've read your earlier filings. You remember *everything*. So tell me plainly: what did you see down there that makes forgetting the merciful option?"

Vane stood very still.

She thought of the bottles on their shelves. She thought of Wren Brightwater's face when she'd offered her soul—calm, certain, the calm of someone who had done this arithmetic before and trusted the answer. She thought of a man in the snow whom forty trained eyes had agreed not to see, and the forgiveness in his look, and the terrible gentle weight of being forgiven by someone whose name she would never know.

She thought of the crack, and what had looked out of it wearing her father's face, and how it had smiled with his mouth.

"I saw what every bargain costs," she said. "All of them. Even the fair ones. Even the ones we call justice." She picked up her cap from the chair. "Forget the gate, Minister. Keep the mercy."

She left the report on his desk, and her commission with it, and walked out of the Ministry of Inquiry into the noise and stink and ordinary blessed light of Corrow, carrying nothing but her sidearm, her father's grief-stone, and one memory she would take to her grave: that somewhere in the world there was a man no record could hold, no hunter could find, and no empire could spend—and that he had looked at her, once, like she was worth saving too.

Behind her, in the ministry archives, a clerk shelved her report between a grain tariff dispute and a complaint about bridge tolls, which was exactly where she had designed it to disappear.

And far below the Grayfell, in a court of contracts, a queen in silver ink paused over her ledger, noted the absence of one name she had expected to collect, and began—quietly, patiently—to wonder who had taught a witch-hunter to lie.
