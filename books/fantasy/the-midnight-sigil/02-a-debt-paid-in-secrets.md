## 2. A Debt Paid in Secrets

The ink was still wet.

Gideon had checked three times in the hour since finding it, tilting the ledger toward the archive's single lamp, touching the corner of a page and drawing back a fingertip stained black. A century old, the ledger. The archivist's catalog said so in a clerk's tidy hand: *Prophetic register, recovered from the Vaelmoor borderlands, year 1147.* And yet the letters of Gideon's own name gleamed as freshly as if written that morning — *Gideon Winterbourne, copyist, second desk by the north window* — and beneath it, in the same impossible wetness, the words *will run*.

They had not run yet. They were still sitting at the second desk by the north window with a stolen book open on their knees and their heart going like a trapped bird against their ribs.

*Will run,* the ledger insisted. And below that: *with the smuggler who owes them.*

Gideon read the line again and felt the floor tilt gently under the chair. There was only one smuggler who owed them anything. Mara Quill, who two winters ago had come to Gideon at midnight with a temple seal forged badly enough to hang her, and whom Gideon had quietly corrected before dawn — one stroke on the *Q*, a flourish on the date, small mercies of ink that had bought Mara her life and Gideon a debt they'd never expected to collect.

No one knew about that night. No one but Gideon, Mara, and whatever hand had filled this ledger a hundred years ago and somehow known anyway.

Down the corridor, boots. Not the soft slippers of the day scribes — boots, several pairs, moving with the unhurried certainty of men who had never once been told *no*. Gideon closed the ledger and slid it beneath a stack of confiscation manifests just as the door swung open.

Three of them. Temple enforcers wore grey, not white — grey cloaks, grey gloves, faces kept smooth as river stones. The one in front was broad through the shoulders, with a captain's pin at his collar and eyes the color of slate after rain.

"Copyist." He didn't look at Gideon so much as through them. "Captain Doss. We're here for a text logged into your care this evening."

"The prophetic register," Gideon said. Their voice came out level, which surprised them. "Vaelmoor provenance. It's on my desk for transcription."

"It's on no one's desk. It was withdrawn an hour ago." Doss extended a gloved hand, palm up. "You'll have handled it. You'll give it back now, and tomorrow you'll remember an unremarkable evening, and there will be no further conversation."

There it was — the shape of the trap laid out plainly, almost kindly. Remember nothing. Be nothing. The temple erased kingdoms; erasing a copyist would be a morning's light work.

Gideon thought of the wet ink. *Will run.* If the prophecy had already been written, then every second they sat still was a second spent arguing with a book that did not lose arguments.

"I put it in the secure cabinet," Gideon said, standing. "Let me fetch the key."

Doss's gaze flicked — just once, just briefly — toward the stack of manifests where the ledger's spine showed its cracked leather like a wound. Gideon saw him see it. Saw him decide, some cold arithmetic behind the slate eyes, that a copyist would not dare.

That decision lasted exactly as long as it took Gideon to knock the lamp off the desk.

Oil spread fire across the reading table in a bright sheet, and the archive erupted — Doss shouting, the other two stamping at flames, a century of dry paper suddenly the most interesting thing in the room. Gideon took the ledger, took the manifest stack too because burning evidence needed company, and went out the window over the north desk, down the drainpipe they'd shinnied a dozen times to sneak cigarettes, into the dark of the temple's outer court with smoke rising behind them like a signal to everyone in the city that something had gone very wrong.

*Will run,* said the ledger, zipped inside their coat against their hammering heart.

Fine. Gideon Winterbourne ran.

* * *

The Saltrow docks at midnight smelled of tar, dead fish, and other people's crimes. Gideon moved through it hooded, hugging the wall where lamplight couldn't find them, checking twice behind every corner. Twice, nothing. The third time, a figure detached itself from a warehouse shadow and fell into step beside them, and Gideon's whole body clenched before the voice came.

"You look terrible," said Mara Quill.

She was shorter than Gideon remembered, or maybe the years had simply built her up — wiry, quick-eyed, a knife at her hip worn with the casualness of jewelry. She looked Gideon over once, head to boots, and her mouth curved without warmth. "Temple coat under the hood. Smoke in your hair. And you're carrying a book like it's a baby." Her eyes sharpened. "What did you do?"

"I need to get out of the city."

"That's not an answer."

"Mara." Gideon pulled back the hood. In the sodium wash of the harbor lamps their face must have shown what they felt, because Mara's smirk faded into something more careful. "They're coming for me. Enforcers. Captain Doss himself. I took a book I wasn't supposed to see, and now either I'm dead or I'm forgotten, and I don't much like either option."

"Everyone in this city is already forgotten," Mara said. "It's practically the civic religion. Why come to me?"

"You know why."

"I owe you a debt," Mara agreed pleasantly. "I've been enjoying owing it. Debts are comfortable. You can carry them around, feel virtuous." She jerked her chin at the ledger-shaped bulge in Gideon's coat. "And that thing is going to make collecting it expensive."

"Then name a price."

"Oh, I will." Mara glanced out at the black water, at the shapes of ships riding at anchor, and when she looked back there was something in her face Gideon had never seen there before — a kind of wary respect, the look a gambler gives a card they didn't know was in the deck. "First tell me what's in the book that's worth dying for. Because I've smuggled relics, poisons, and a cardinal's mistress, and none of it was worth dying for."

Gideon hesitated. Then they opened the ledger to the page and held it where the lamplight could reach.

Mara read. Her lips moved slightly. Gideon watched her go still in a way that people only went still when the world rearranged itself — watched her finger hover over the line *the smuggler who owes them*, then rise to Gideon's face, then back down.

"That's your name," she said.

"Yes."

"And mine. Without my name even being—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "This book is a hundred years old?"

"The catalog says so."

"The catalog," Mara repeated. She laughed once, short and sharp, with no humor in it anywhere. Then she snapped the ledger shut and handed it back, and her whole manner changed — the lounging smuggler gone, and underneath her someone quicker, harder, someone who had grown up in border towns where maps had holes in them. "Vaelmoor," she said. "My grandmother used to sing songs about Vaelmoor and swear she'd never sung them if you asked. Everyone from the Thornfell side does. Songs about a country that isn't there."

"You believe the book?"

"I believe the temple sent a captain and two greys to retrieve it at midnight instead of sending a clerk in daylight." Mara was already walking, fast, and Gideon had to lengthen their stride to keep up. "Ink doesn't lie about wetness. Whoever wrote that wrote it knowing you'd read it tonight. That means either a miracle or a trap, and I've made better money betting on traps than miracles. Either way, we move now, because in about ten minutes those enforcers figure out whose window you went out of, and in twenty they'll lock the gates."

"Where are we going?"

"Thornfell Pass. It's how my family always moved cargo nobody official wanted to exist." She shot Gideon a sideways look. "Fitting, since that seems to be your area of expertise now."

* * *

They cut through the fish-market while the stalls slept under canvas, past the customs house where a bored watchman didn't look up, and reached the north postern as the bells above began to stir — not alarm yet, but the pre-alarm murmur of a temple waking to a loss. Mara spoke three words to a gate guard whose debts she apparently also owned, and the small door in the great gate opened just wide enough for two bodies and one impossible book.

Outside the walls, the road climbed into hills black under a scatter of stars, and the city's glow fell away behind them like something sinking. Gideon walked until the gate was out of sight, until the air smelled of pine instead of tar, and only then let themselves stop shaking.

Mara pretended not to notice, which Gideon appreciated, or perhaps she genuinely hadn't seen. With Mara it was impossible to know.

"They'll follow," Gideon said finally. "Doss struck me as patient. Patient men are worse than angry ones."

"Doss." Mara tried the name like tasting wine. "Grey cloak, slate eyes, never smiles?"

"You know him?"

"I know *of* her." Mara smiled thinly at Gideon's expression. "Captain Ilsa Doss. Burned a village's records once so thoroughly they had to invent new ancestors. If she's hunting you, copyist, we don't stop to sleep in any bed with a roof."

"She said *he*—" Gideon stopped. Realized the hood and the dark had lied to them, and that they'd been so afraid they'd never noticed. Something about it steadied them, absurdly. Even their terror had been imprecise. "A hundred-year-old book knows my name and I can't even correctly identify the person trying to kill me."

"Welcome to my profession." Mara paused where the trail split — one branch dropping toward the river valley and honest roads, the other climbing into a dark so dense it seemed solid. She stood looking at the upward path the way sailors look at weather.

"If we take the pass," Gideon said, "what's at the end of it?"

Mara was quiet a moment. "Nobody knows," she said. "That's rather the point. The maps go blank up there. Officially there's nothing between Thornfell ridge and the lowland farms. Unofficially—" she patted the ledger through Gideon's coat, "—unofficially there's a country that bargained itself out of existence, and a book that thinks you belong to it."

The wind came down the pass and smelled of stone and old snow, and somewhere far above them something shifted — a rockfall, or wings, or nothing at all.

"So," Mara said. "Do we climb?"

Gideon thought of the archive burning behind them, of Doss's glove held out palm-up, of the wet ink drying slowly against their chest as they walked. Fear was still there — fear would probably always be there now — but somewhere in the last mile it had changed states, the way water changes at freezing, hardening into something with edges. Something you could hold.

"We climb," Gideon said.

Mara grinned, sharp as her knife, and started up into the dark.
