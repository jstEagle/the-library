## 3. Where the Maps Go Blank

The map lied to me before the valley did.

I'd stolen it fair and square off a caravan master in Duskwater — a surveyor's chart, one of the good ones, inked on waxed linen so it wouldn't rot in the rain. It showed every road from the temple city to the Thornfell highlands, every ford, every tollhouse, every place a person with cargo of dubious provenance might slip between the world's fingers. I'd trusted it through three smuggling runs. It had never once failed me.

So when it stopped showing anything at all, I took it personally.

"Your map's dead," Gideon said, peering over my shoulder as our horse picked its way down a track of loose shale. They still had the ledger strapped against their chest under that ridiculous temple coat, both arms wrapped around it like a mother with a colicky infant. Two days of hard riding and they hadn't let go of it once, not to eat, not to sleep. "Or you're holding it upside down."

"I don't hold maps upside down." I said it with more bite than the sentence deserved. "I hold *people* upside down when they suggest otherwise."

The valley opened below us the way a held breath opens — all at once and too late to stop. Green going gold at the seams, a river threading through it like spilled mercury, and roads. Roads everywhere, branching and braiding, more roads than any valley needed, as if the land itself had grown anxious and couldn't stop pacing.

None of them were on my map.

Worse than none of them: where the valley should have been, my map showed blank linen. Not unexplored territory — surveyors hatch their ignorance honestly, little scribbled clouds and *here be marshland*. This was nothing. Smooth, empty, finished nothing, as if whoever drew the chart had run out of world right there and thought nobody would notice.

"Surveyors don't miss valleys," I said. "Valleys this size have tax collectors. Tollhouses. At least one inn with watered ale and a landlord who talks too much." I turned the linen over, checking the back, as if the valley might have slipped through a seam. "This whole country has been walked end to end twice over. Somebody looked at this and wrote *nothing here*."

"Somebody was told to," Gideon said quietly.

That was the thing about them I'd been learning, these two days on the road. They said quiet things in the tone other people used for shouting.

* * *

We followed the river road because the river, at least, behaved like a river. Water runs downhill even in cursed country; it was the only honest thing left.

By midday I'd stopped trusting the road behind us. I kept checking landmarks — that split boulder shaped like a sleeping dog, the lightning-blasted alder — and they kept being *ahead* of us. Same boulder. Same alder. Twice we passed the same leaning fence post with the same red rag tied to it, fraying in the same wind.

"We're looping," I said finally, reining in. "I've run this trick myself, you know. False switchbacks to shake pursuit. Except I build those, and I bill for it."

Gideon slid off the horse and stood very still in the middle of the road, head tilted, listening to something I couldn't hear. The ledger's presence under their coat seemed to warm the air around them; I'd started being able to tell when its pages turned on their own, because Gideon's whole body went taut as a bowstring.

"It's not looping," they said. "It's *refusing*. The road doesn't want us to leave. Or—" they frowned, correcting themselves the way they corrected everything, precisely, painfully, "—the road doesn't exist until we're standing on it. Behind us it forgets."

"That's not how roads work."

"Neither is a prophecy written in wet ink a hundred years ago, Mara. We left 'how things work' back at the archive, along with my life."

I hated that they were right, and I hated more that they were getting comfortable being right about impossible things. People who get comfortable around the impossible start making deals with it. I've watched it happen. It always ends with someone owing someone else something terrible.

A village appeared around the next bend.

I say *appeared* because that is genuinely what happened. One moment there was a curve of road between two hills, and the next there were slate roofs and smoke rising from chimneys and a well with buckets and geese complaining in a yard — a whole village, complete, settled, *occupied*, as though it had been sitting there since the founding of the world and we were the latecomers.

The people came out to look at us. That's normal enough for a small village. What wasn't normal was what they said.

"You're not here," an old woman told me, quite pleasantly, when I asked after provisions. She had flour on her hands and kindness in her face and she said it the way you'd remark on weather. "Nothing here, dear. You'll want to move along."

"I'm *standing in your yard*," I pointed out.

"Are you?" She looked at her hands. Flour. Geese. The ordinary miracle of bread. "How strange. Nothing here. There's never been anything here. Move along now, before you're missed."

Every soul in that village gave me the same answer in different words, and underneath the words, the same flinch. Not fear of us. Fear of *naming*. I know that flinch. Smugglers live by it — the moment you write down what you carry, the moment you speak the route aloud, you've made it real enough for someone to take from you.

These people had made themselves unreal on purpose. A hundred years of practice in it, from the look of their roofs and the depth of their wells.

Gideon bought bread anyway — or tried to. The old woman pressed a loaf into their hands and refused coin, refusing it the way you'd refuse a hot coal. "Money says where you've been," she whispered, urgent suddenly, gripping Gideon's wrist. "Don't leave marks. Don't leave names. *Especially yours.*"

Then she shut her door, and the village began, gently and completely, to forget we had ever been in it. Smoke still rose, but the chimney it rose from was already becoming somewhere else.

* * *

We camped that night in the hollow of a ruined shrine, because ruins at least admitted to having existed.

Gideon unwrapped the ledger by firelight, and I saw immediately that something had changed. I'm no scholar, but I've handled enough forged documents to know when paper is telling the truth, and this paper was *moving*. Not fluttering. The words themselves were rearranging, slow as sediment, like the page was a pond settling after a stone.

"It's rewriting," Gideon said. Their voice had gone thin. "It's done this before — small things. Corrections. But look."

Look I did. By firelight, in the ledger's old script, a new passage was surfacing line by line, the way bruises surface:

*The courier crosses the blank on the third day. She does not believe. Belief is not required of her. Only passage.*

"Courier," I repeated. "That's not me. I'm a guide. Guides get paid. Couriers are the ones who get hanged when the package turns out to be illegal."

"What do you think you're carrying?"

"The client. The client carries his own trouble."

But Gideon wasn't listening anymore, because the page kept writing. More lines surfaced, and I watched my traveling companion go pale by degrees, firelight doing half the work and horror the rest:

*She will be offered her debt forgiven. She must count what forgiveness costs.*

*I am sorry for what the border will ask of her. I was sorry when I wrote it. I am sorrier still that it is true.*

Gideon closed the book. Gently. The way you close a door on a sickroom.

"It knows you," they said.

"It's a book. Books don't know anybody." But my mouth had gone dry, because a hundred years ago somebody had sat down with wet ink and written *me* — written my debts, my choices, my price — and apologized for it. Written me the way you write a character. And I have strong opinions about being a character in someone else's story. Most of my opinions involve knives.

"Sleep," I said. "Tomorrow we find out how deep the blank goes."

* * *

The answer was: all the way.

Dawn came up wrong. Not missing — I'd seen sunless dawns in the fen country, gray light with nowhere to land — but *wrong*, the light arriving already tired, as if it had traveled farther than dawn should. And across the valley floor, where last night there had been fields and a winding road south, there was now a wall.

Not stone. Not hedge. A wall made of *absence*, if absence can have texture. My eyes kept sliding off it, trying to file it as fog, as distance, as anything at all. The ledger grew warm against Gideon's chest — I could see it, actually see a faint glow through the cloth like coals banked under ash.

"The border," Gideon breathed. "The ledger said the door only opens where Vaelmoor's borders stood. This is it. The edge of the erased country."

"And we're walking in." I checked my knives out of habit. All present. Comforting in a way that felt increasingly quaint, like praying. "Tell me again why the enforcers haven't caught us. Two days, Gideon. Temple hounds don't lose a scent in open country."

"They haven't caught us," Gideon said slowly, "because maybe we're already somewhere they can't follow. Maybe the blank protects itself." They looked at me, and there was something new in their face — not the archivist's fear anymore, something older and hungrier, the look of someone reading their own name in an old book and finding it fits. "Or maybe it isn't protecting us. Maybe it's *collecting* us."

I didn't like that word. I liked it less when we crossed the wall.

There was no moment of passing. No shiver, no threshold chill. One step there was tired dawn and the valley behind us; the next step the light was clean and gold and *old*, old the way honey is old, and the air smelled of cut wheat though no field stood within sight. Behind us, when I turned, the wall was gone. Ahead, the road ran straight as a plumb line into a country that my map insisted did not exist, toward hills crowned with ruins that had no business being ruins — walls too fine, arches too tall, the bones of a kingdom wearing its grave clothes.

And far off, at the center of it all, on a hill where a single border stone caught the morning light, something glinted. A door, standing free and upright in the open country, with nothing built around it. Waiting the way things wait when they've been waiting a hundred years and expect to wait a hundred more.

"There it is," Gideon whispered.

Behind us — impossibly, terribly — hoofbeats. I spun. Down the road we'd just come, at the vanished edge of the blank, riders had appeared out of thin air. Dark coats. Temple grey.

They hadn't lost our trail after all.

They'd simply been waiting for the door to open to us first.
