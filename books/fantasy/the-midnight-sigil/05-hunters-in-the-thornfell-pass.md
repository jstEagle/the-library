## 5. Hunters in the Thornfell Pass

Mara Quill had spent eleven years learning which silences in Thornfell were safe ones, and the silence that fell over the pass on the third morning was not one of them.

She stopped at the bend where the trail narrowed between two walls of black granite, and she did it casually — a stretch, a glance back at Gideon, a hand resting easy near her belt knife. Behind them, the switchbacks they'd climbed since dawn dropped away into fog. Ahead, the trail climbed toward the high notch that would spit them out onto Vaelmoor's forgotten plateau.

Nothing moved. That was the problem. Thornfell was never quiet in the mornings. There should have been rock doves clattering off the ledges, marmots whistling from the scree, wind worrying through the pines below the treeline. Instead there was nothing, and the nothing had a texture to it, a held-breath quality she'd felt only twice before — once outside a magistrate's door the night before her father was taken, and once in the hold of a ship when the crew stopped singing because someone had spotted sails on the horizon.

"Gideon." She kept her voice low. "How fast can you run uphill?"

Gideon looked up from the ledger. They'd been walking with it cradled against their chest like a sleeping child for three days now, ever since the pages had started rewriting themselves. "Depends what's chasing me."

"That's the spirit."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, a fire no bigger than two cupped hands, and Mara laid out the geography of their trouble on a flat stone with pebbles and pinecones.

"Temple enforcers are behind us. Doss's company — I saw their lanterns at the ford yesterday, six riders moving like people who know exactly where they're going. That means someone's told them the route." She nudged a pebble southward. "Which is its own kind of bad, because I didn't tell anyone the route."

"The ledger told them," Gideon said quietly.

"Don't say things like that while I'm eating."

"It's true. The ink moves, Mara. It's been moving since we crossed into the valley. If it can write itself forward, maybe it can be read backward. Maybe Doss has a reader who knows how."

"Fine. Enforcers behind us." She pushed a pinecone to the north side of the stone. "And here's the part I've been chewing on all day. We're not being followed by just one set of hunters anymore. This afternoon, on the ridge above the loop-village — you remember, the one where the old woman swore up and down there was no village?"

"I remember."

"There were cairns on the ridge. Fresh ones. Stacked stones don't happen by accident out here, Gideon. Somebody's marking a trail for people who come after. And those people aren't temple. Temple marks their trails with paint and prayer-scraps. Cairns mean mountain folk. Exiles. People who learned this country before it was erased and never forgot the paths."

Gideon went very still. In the firelight, Mara watched them work it through — the same way they'd worked through everything since the archive, methodically, like copying out a difficult passage. "The remnant," they said. "The hermit said some of Vaelmoor's people refused the bargain. Refused to be erased. They've been living in the mountains for a hundred years, waiting for—"

"Waiting for an heir to show up holding a book that opens doors." Mara fed a twig to the fire. "Congratulations. You're not just running from the law anymore. You're the most wanted person in a country that doesn't exist, wanted by two different sets of people for two different reasons, and both of them think you belong to them."

"And which do you think I belong to?"

The question hung there. Mara looked at Gideon across the tiny fire — this archivist who couldn't lie without blushing, who had called in a debt Mara had spent nine years hoping would never come due, who was carrying around a book that was slowly eating its own pages and rewriting them into something else. Nine years ago, Mara had been seventeen and bleeding out in a Thornfell ditch with a smuggler's brand on her wrist and a dead partner on the road behind her, and Gideon Winterbourne had knelt down in the mud, torn up their own coat for bandages, and lied to a temple patrol with a straight face about a fever outbreak upstream. Lied to temple. For her. A stranger.

So no, she wasn't going to answer that question honestly, which was that Gideon belonged to whoever they chose, and Mara's job was to make sure they lived long enough to choose well.

"I think you belong to yourself," she said. "That's rarer than you'd think. Sleep. I'll take first watch."

* * *

The ambush came at dawn, in the narrows below the Shepherd's Teeth, where the pass pinched down to a corridor barely wide enough for two abreast and the walls rose sheer on either side.

Mara smelled them before she heard them — woodsmoke and wet wool, mountain smells, wrong smells for a place this high and cold. She grabbed Gideon's collar and hauled them both into a crease in the rock as the first crossbow bolt buried itself in the pine trunk where Gideon's head had been.

"Down! Down, stay down—" Six of them, dropping from the ledges like they'd been grown there. Grey-cloaked, faces wrapped, moving with the terrible economy of people who had trained on these cliffs their whole lives. Not temple. The remnant.

"Winterbourne!" The voice came from everywhere, bounced off the granite until it seemed to come from inside her own skull. "We don't want your blood on our stones. Give us the ledger and walk away!"

"Walk away where?" Gideon shouted back, pressed flat against the rock beside her, breath ragged. "There's nowhere that isn't yours, apparently!"

"Give us the book!" A pause. "Or we take it and the archivist too!"

"They're going to take us either way," Mara muttered. She counted bolts — two more thudding into the pines, keeping them pinned. Below and behind, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that turned her stomach: hoofbeats. Iron-shod, disciplined, climbing the same trail. "Oh, that's perfect. That's just perfect. Gideon, we are about to be in the middle of something extremely unpleasant, so I need you to listen to me very carefully—"

The remnant came down the walls then, ropes and handholds, closing fast, and the enforcers rounded the switchback below at almost the same moment, and the pass became a throat swallowing both of them.

What happened next, Mara would spend a long time trying to describe afterward, and never manage.

A bolt meant for Gideon's chest — loosed by a grey-cloak at ten feet, dead certain, unmissable — stopped. It did not deflect or shatter. It stopped, mid-air, three feet from Gideon's heart, and hung there trembling like a thing embarrassed to exist. Then it drifted, slowly, gently, to the ground, and lay there like a fallen leaf.

Every fighter in the narrows froze.

Gideon stood in the center of it, the ledger open in their hands, and Mara saw that they hadn't opened it — it had opened itself, pages riffling in wind that wasn't blowing, and the ink was rising off the paper. Not metaphorically. Rising, lifting from the parchment in fine dark threads, hanging in the air around Gideon like a swarm of patient insects, and where the threads touched stone, words appeared — old words, carved-looking, glowing faintly along the granite walls:

**THE PASS REMEMBERS THE SHEPHERDS. THE SHEPHERDS BARGAINED. THE BARGAIN HOLDS ALL WHO WALK HERE.**

The grey-cloaks fell back. One of them — a broad-shouldered woman with a scarred jaw — made a sign against her chest, an old sign, a sign Mara had seen once scratched into a border stone and never understood until this moment.

"An heir," the woman breathed. "The ledger chose. It *chose.*"

"Chose nothing!" That was Doss, hauling herself into the narrows at the head of her enforcers, sword drawn, face grey with exhaustion and fury. "Put it down, archivist. You have no idea what you're holding."

"You're right," Gideon said, and their voice carried strangely in the stone, doubled somehow, as if the pass itself were repeating them a half-beat behind. "I don't. But I'm starting to understand that neither does anyone else — including whatever wrote this." The threads of ink circled them slowly. "Everyone wants the book. Nobody wants to ask what it wants."

"We're asking it to restore a kingdom," the scarred woman said.

"You're asking it to put you back on top of one." Gideon closed the ledger, and the ink sank home, and the words died off the stone, and the ordinary silence rushed back in. "I've read what Vaelmoor was. I've read who held the land and who worked it. Opening that door for *you* isn't justice. It's a restoration."

For a heartbeat, Mara thought the woman might order her people forward anyway. Crossbows were still levelled; enforcers still had blades out; the narrows held perhaps thirty armed people and one unarmed archivist standing between every grudge in the mountains.

Then the scarred woman gave a short, sharp gesture, and the remnant melted upward into the rock like water soaking into earth, gone in seconds, leaving only fresh cairns and the crossbow bolts in the pines.

Doss stared at Gideon across the empty narrows. Her sword arm lowered, slowly, an inch, then another. She looked older than she had at the archive, worn down to something raw.

"Captain?" one of her enforcers prompted.

Doss didn't answer him. She was looking at the place on the wall where the words had been, and her lips were moving slightly, silently, as if reading something written there long ago — or remembering something sung to her, once, in a kitchen that officially had never existed.

"Mara," Gideon whispered. "Why does the temple captain look like she's about to cry?"

Mara watched Doss sheathe her sword — slowly, deliberately, a choice made visible — and felt the whole shape of the hunt tilt under her feet.

"Because," Mara said, "I think we just found out whose grandmother sang the lullabies."
