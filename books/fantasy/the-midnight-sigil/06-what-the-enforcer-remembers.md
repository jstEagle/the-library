## 6. What the Enforcer Remembers

The order came at dusk, written in High Prelate Corvane's own hand, and it was shorter than a grocery list.

*Burn the ledger. Burn the copyist. Bring me ash and I will believe you.*

Captain Ilsette Doss read it twice, standing in the doorway of the enforcers' barracks while the last light went copper on the temple's spires. Around her, her squad buckled greaves and checked crossbow strings with the easy chatter of men who thought they were going to catch a thief. They did not know yet what the order actually said. She had not read it aloud.

Burn the copyist.

Not *capture.* Not *question.* The temple had words for mercy and words for procedure, and Corvane had used neither.

"You want us to take her alive, Captain?" asked Brannoc, shrugging into his coat. "Quill's got friends in Thornfell who'll fight for her. Might be easier to—"

"Alive," Doss said. "Both of them."

Brannoc looked at her a moment too long, then nodded and went back to his straps. Doss folded the order into her breast pocket, where it lay against her heart like a stone she had swallowed.

They rode out through the west gate as the lamps were being lit, six riders moving quiet along the pilgrim road toward Thornfell Pass. Doss had been an enforcer for nineteen years. She had dragged debtors from their beds and heretics from their pulpits and once, in a flooded river town, pulled a drowning child out of the water only to arrest him for the theft that had made him run. She knew the shape of her work. She had never once looked directly at it.

Tonight she looked.

---

They made the pass by moonrise, and the mountains closed around them like a fist. Thornfell was old country — older than the temple, older than the roads — and the locals said the thorn hedges there had been planted on purpose, generations ago, to keep something in or keep something out. No one could remember which.

Doss remembered something else about this country. Something she had never told anyone.

Her grandmother had sung to her. That was the whole of it, when you stripped it down. An old woman in a smoky kitchen in the docklands, rocking a child who could not sleep, singing songs in a language that was not the language of any hymnbook Doss would ever be handed. The melodies were strange — they climbed where temple music fell, and fell where it climbed, and they always ended on a note that felt unfinished, like a door left open on purpose.

*"What are those songs, Gran?"*

And her grandmother had gone very still, the way animals go still, and said: *"Songs from nowhere, little bird. Sing them and forget them. Never sing them twice in front of the same person."*

Doss had been seven. She had sung them anyway, all over the docklands, until a temple deacon heard her outside the fish market and took her by the wrist hard enough to bruise, and asked — kindly, gently, terribly — where she had learned such things. Her grandmother moved them within the week. Two months later the old woman was dead of a fever, and Doss was in the temple's care, and the songs were locked away in the one archive that never burned: the body of a careful child.

She still hummed them sometimes. In the bath. On night watch. She had never once let anyone hear.

Now she rode through the pass where the songs had been born, following two fugitives so that she could burn them, and the melody kept rising in her throat like water into a lung.

---

They found the ambush site an hour before dawn — not their ambush. Someone else's.

A rival camp in the rocks above the trail, cold fires, boot prints everywhere, and the unmistakable sign of a faction that wanted the fugitives alive and unspoiled. Doss crouched over the ashes and read the ground the way other people read letters.

"They've gone ahead," Brannoc said. "Whoever they are."

"Vaelmoor," said Sergeant Ilex quietly, and spat. "Remnant sympathizers. You hear the stories."

There were no stories. That was the point of Vaelmoor. There were no maps, no records, no laws mentioning it; there was only a hole in the world shaped like a kingdom, and people like Ilex filling the hole with rumors. Doss stood up slowly. Somewhere below them, down a trail that wound toward a valley no map acknowledged, Gideon Winterbourne and Mara Quill were walking into that hole carrying a book full of wet ink.

"Burn order stands?" Brannoc asked.

Doss looked east, where the sky was beginning to gray. She thought about the phrase *burn the copyist*, in Corvane's neat, unhurried hand. She thought about how a hundred years ago, some other captain had probably received some other order with those same four words in it, and obeyed, and that was why there were no stories.

"Stands," she said. And hated the taste of it.

---

They caught the fugitives at midmorning, in a hollow where the trail dipped between walls of thorn, and everything happened at once.

The remnant faction came out of the rocks first — eight or nine figures in travel-stained grey, moving with the coordination of people who had drilled in secret for years. Crossbows. A net weighted with iron. They wanted Winterbourne breathing.

Doss's squad came off the ridge behind them, and the hollow became a knot of shouting and smoke. She saw Mara Quill put a knife through a crossbow's string and drag Winterbourne bodily behind a boulder. She saw a remnant man level his weapon at Quill's back, and she fired first — a warning shot that cracked off stone a hand's width from his ear, because even now, even here, some part of her refused to simply kill.

Then the ledger happened.

She saw it clearly, though she would struggle later to describe it plainly. Winterbourne had the book open against the boulder, one hand flat on the page, and the ink was moving — she could see it moving from thirty yards, black lines crawling like ants — and when the remnant captain hurled a burning brand toward their cover, the fire *stopped*. Hung in the air. Curved, impossibly, like water finding a channel, and streamed back along its own path into the rocks. As if the page had rewritten the direction of the flame. As if the words had decided what was true.

In the ringing silence that followed, Winterbourne looked up — directly at Doss, across the whole smoking hollow — and their eyes met.

That was when the song rose in her throat and would not be swallowed.

It was the climbing one, the one her grandmother sang when storms rolled in off the harbor, the one that ended on the unfinished note. It came out of her before she chose it, thin and clear over the wreckage of the skirmish, and she watched Winterbourne's face change — watched disbelief crack across it, then recognition, then something rawer than either.

Because Winterbourne mouthed the words. The actual words. The ones in the language that was not in any hymnbook. The ones Doss herself had never known the meaning of.

The remnant faction broke off and melted into the rocks, dragging their wounded. Her own squad regrouped around her, breathing hard, looking to her for orders, and Doss stood in the middle of the hollow with a dead fire smoldering at her feet and a lullaby dying on her lips and understood, finally, what she had been humming to herself her entire life.

It was a warning. It had always been a warning. *Sing them and forget them* — no. *Remember them.* Her grandmother had garbled it on purpose, or grief had garbled it, but the song itself had been waiting a hundred years for someone to carry it back to the country it belonged to.

"Captain," Brannoc said. "Winterbourne and Quill. They're right there. Do we take them?"

Doss looked at her squad. Six faces. Nineteen years of shared bread and shared blood. She thought about giving the order — *take them* — and letting the machinery do what machinery did, and standing afterward in Corvane's office saying *ash, High Prelate, as ordered*, and spending the rest of her life unable to hum three notes without tasting it.

"No," she said.

Brannoc blinked. "Captain?"

"I said no." She pulled Corvane's order from her breast pocket, held it up so they could all see the seal, and tore it in half. Then in half again. "You are going to ride back to the temple, and you are going to tell the High Prelate that the fugitives crossed into unmapped country and could not be followed. Every word of that is true." She met Brannoc's eyes, then Ilex's, then the others'. "Or you can report what I'm about to do, and we can find out which of us Corvane believes. Choose."

The silence stretched. Somewhere upslope, wind moved through the thorns with a sound almost like singing.

Ilex laughed — short, disbelieving — and turned his horse toward home. One by one, the others followed. Brannoc lingered last, and Doss braced herself, but he only said, quietly: "My mother used to hum something like that too. I never asked her where from." Then he rode away, and she was alone in the hollow with the torn order drifting down among the thorns.

---

She found them half a mile on, where the trail dropped toward the blank place in the maps. Quill heard her first — of course she did — and had a knife out before Doss was within twenty yards, putting herself between the blade and the archivist with the bandaged hand.

"Easy," Doss said, and stopped, and raised empty hands. "I tore the order. My squad's gone home to lie for me, gods help them. I'm not here to take you."

Winterbourne stepped out from behind Quill, slow, watchful. Up close they looked younger than their reputation and older than their years, and their eyes went straight past her face to her mouth, as if the song were still hanging in the air there.

"That song," Winterbourne said. "Where did you learn it?"

"My grandmother." Doss's voice came out rougher than she intended. "Docklands. She called them songs from nowhere. I've been singing them my whole life without knowing there was anywhere for them to come home to." She swallowed. "Until I saw your book rewrite a fire."

"It rewrites more than fire," Winterbourne said. "Why are you helping us? Corvane sent you to destroy the ledger. To destroy *me*."

"Yes." Doss held their gaze. "And a hundred years ago, someone like me destroyed whatever came before it, because someone like Corvane gave the order, and everyone like me obeyed. I've spent nineteen years being the hand that obeys." She looked past them, down into the valley where the maps went blank, and the climbing melody stirred again under her ribs, patient as water. "I'm done being the hand. Take me with you as far as the border, if you'll have me. I know these songs better than anyone left alive — and I think, down there, you're going to need someone who knows what they're *for*."

Quill snorted softly. "An enforcer. Wonderful. Next you'll tell me the High Prelate knits."

But Winterbourne was studying her with an expression she recognized from mirrors — the look of a person whose certainty has just come apart in their hands, and who is deciding, right now, whether to grieve it or build with the pieces.

"The songs," Winterbourne said quietly. "Do you know how they end?"

"No," Doss said. "None of them end. They all stop on the open note."

Winterbourne glanced down at the ledger under their arm, and its pages rustled though there was no wind, turning themselves, eager as a dog hearing its name.

"Then walk with us," they said, "and we'll find out together."
