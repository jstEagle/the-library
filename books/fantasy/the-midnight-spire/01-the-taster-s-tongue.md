## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The pheasant was a trap.

I knew it the way I knew most things — with my tongue, before my mind caught up. Oscar Voss, royal food-taster to the House of Aldmere, stood at the sideboard in the prince's private dining room with a silver spoon going cold in my hand, and every nerve from throat to stomach rang like a struck bell.

Bitterness first. Green and resinous, the ghost of hemlock folded into the glaze of the cherry sauce — done well, I'll grant whoever did it. Cooked hemlock loses its edge if you're clever with heat, and someone had been very clever. A lesser taster might have missed it entirely, or caught it too late, which is the same thing in this trade.

But under the hemlock there was something else. Something that didn't taste like poison at all.

It tasted like a place.

That's not how poisons are supposed to work. Poison is chemistry; it has weight, temperature, an accent you learn after enough years of swallowing small deaths for a living. This had none of those things. It had geography. When it touched my tongue I smelled rain on stone I'd never stood on, heard — not imagined, *heard* — a market bell with a cracked note, felt the specific damp cold of a harbor town at dusk. It was as if someone had distilled a country and dripped three drops of it into the sauce.

My hands were steady. Twenty years of this work buys you steady hands even when your spine is screaming.

"Is it good?" asked Prince Aldous, who was nineteen, hungry, and constitutionally incapable of waiting.

"It's excellent, Your Highness," I said, and set the plate down in front of him, and smiled the smile they pay me for.

Then I turned back to the sideboard, lifted the second serving — the one no one would eat now, because the protocol was already in motion, the footmen drifting toward their stations, the physician's steward noting the time of my tasting in his little book — and I ate another bite myself, deliberately, while no one watched.

Because here is the thing about being Oscar Voss: I don't just detect poison. I keep accounts of it.

And what was sitting in that sauce was something I had recorded. With my own hand. In ink I mixed myself.

* * *

Let me explain how a woman ends up eating poison twice on purpose on an ordinary Tuesday.

By daylight I belong to the crown. I am a fixture of the palace the way the caryatids are fixtures — useful, ornamental, assumed to be furniture. The court thinks of me as a curiosity, half-servant and half-miracle: the woman who cannot be poisoned. They've tested me over the years. Early on, when I was new and ambitious, I let them test me. Arsenic in the wine. Wolfsbane in the honey. One memorable evening when the late king's brother insisted on watching me drink a cup of distilled nightshade neat, to settle a bet. I drank it. I described its flavor to him — sweet at first, then a numbness like snow falling on your tongue — and he laughed and paid up and never liked me again.

What the court doesn't know is where the immunity came from. They assume I was born lucky, or blessed by some minor saint of digestion. The truth involves a guild they've never heard of, a cellar beneath a chandler's shop in the Tallow Quarter, and a ledger bound in grey leather that lists, among other assets: *one memory of the sea, traded by a fisherman who could no longer bear the sight of it; four hours of yesterday, sold by a widow to escape a grief; the last known ember of dragon-fire, unpriced.*

The Guild of Impossible Things. We deal in what can't be owned any other way. And eleven years ago, the guild bought me my tongue — or rather, paid off my tongue's debts, dish by dish, poison by poison, until my body became a ledger of its own, every immunity entered against my name.

In exchange, I keep their books. Every trade, every price, every impossible thing that passes through our cellar — recorded in my hand, sealed with my wax, balanced nightly the way other people balance prayers.

So yes. When I tasted something in the prince's dinner that I recognized from a page I had written, I did what any sensible bookkeeper does upon finding an entry she doesn't remember making.

I went home to check the arithmetic.

* * *

The walk from the palace to the Tallow Quarter takes forty minutes if you take the honest roads, and I took them, because a food-taster who sneers at honest roads starts looking like a woman with secrets, and the whole art of my life is looking like nothing at all. Grey dress, grey cloak, the flat competent face of a servant whose feet hurt. Inside that face, behind it, underneath it: the flavor of the pheasant replaying on my tongue like a tune I couldn't stop humming.

Hemlock, laced with Vellmark ash.

No. That wasn't right. I didn't have the word yet. But the shape of the recognition was already forming, the way a bruise surfaces before the skin shows color. Somewhere in the grey leather ledger — the oldest one, the one I'd inherited from my predecessor along with her eyestrain and her nightmares — there was an entry describing exactly what I'd tasted. I remembered transcribing it because the description had unsettled me even then, secondhand: a poison agent so old it predated the guild's own taxonomy, catalogued not by its chemistry but by its *effect*, which was said to be less a killing than a *returning*. The victim doesn't die of it. The victim remembers everything that was taken from them, all at once, and dies of the weight.

The guild had priced it once. The entry ended with a figure I'd always found absurd, because you cannot put a number on something like that — but then, that's the whole business, isn't it? Putting numbers on things that should be beyond price. That's what we do. That's what I do, in my neat columns, in my careful hand, and I have told myself for eleven years that keeping the accounts is different from making them.

The chandler's shop smelled, as always, of tallow and candle-smoke and, faintly, underneath, of paper and dust and old cold iron. Maren was minding the counter, pretending to trim wicks.

"You look terrible," he said.

"I ate poison."

"Twice?"

"How did you—"

"You've got your ledger face on." He nodded past me, toward the cellar door. "Corvane's been asking after you since noon. He asked twice whether you'd come in tonight, which from Corvane is practically a love letter." Maren lowered his voice. "Osc. He's been in the archive. Alone."

Something cold walked up my back and settled between my shoulder blades.

"The archive is under seal," I said.

"I know what it's under." Maren picked up his wick-trimmers and looked at them as though they were fascinating. "I'm just telling you what I saw. Go down. Check your books."

* * *

The cellar archive of the Guild of Impossible Things is not large. Visitors expect vaults, cathedral shadows, chained tomes. What they'd find instead is a low brick room with shelves of grey ledgers, jars of preserved light on the high racks, and a smell like the inside of a locked drawer. Order is the whole point. Impossible things are dangerous precisely because they resist order, so we impose it ruthlessly: dated, priced, indexed, sealed. My predecessor built the system. I maintain it. On the worst nights, when the trades have gone badly and someone upstairs is weeping over the receipt for a childhood, I tell myself the ordering is a kindness. That a thing entered honestly into a ledger is a thing witnessed, and witness is the poor man's monument.

Tonight the room felt wrong before I'd crossed the threshold.

Small things. My chair angled a degree off true. The dust on shelf nine disturbed in a long swipe, the swipe of a sleeve, not a cloth. And the oldest ledger — the grey one, the one with the page I needed — sitting out of sequence. Not on its shelf. On the reading table. Closed, but closed carelessly, its clasp not fully seated, and when I opened it I found the ribbon marker resting on a page I hadn't left it on.

Someone had been reading my books.

I checked the seals first, the way you check your purse in a crowd. Wax intact. Wax intact. Wax intact — except. Except the seal on the oldest ledger had been melted and re-set, expertly, with wax of the identical shade, and only a person who had made that particular seal ten thousand times — a person, in other words, like me — would ever have noticed the difference. The re-poured wax sat a hair too smooth. No thumbprint. Mine always carried my thumbprint; I'd thought it vanity. Apparently it was security.

I sat down. I turned to the entry.

*Item: sovereign's bane (so styled), provenance unknown, acquired [date illegible — water damage]. Effect: total restoration of excised memory to the subject. Not a toxin in the ordinary sense. Administered, the subject recalls all that forgetting was purchased to bury — and per the seller, dies of it. Price: see attached. Note of caution from previous Keeper: this item should never have entered inventory. Recommend destruction. Recommendation overruled by G.M.* 

G.M. Guildmaster.

And below, in the margin, in ink I did not recognize — newer ink, browner, someone else's hand:

*Possible application. Ask V. whether the taster's records corroborate.*

Ask V. Ask Voss. Whether my records corroborate.

I read it four times. The room got very quiet around the words, the way rooms do when they're deciding whether to become a different kind of room.

Someone had used my ledger as a reference. Someone had written a note in my book — in my sealed book, in my sealed archive — consulting me without my knowledge, treating me not as the keeper of these accounts but as a resource within them. An asset. Which is, I suppose, what I am. I've known that for eleven years. Knowing it and having it written in the margin of your own handwriting are different experiences, the way hearing about a war and tasting hemlock are different experiences.

And the entry itself. *Sovereign's bane.* The flavor came back to me across forty minutes of city streets and a locked cellar door, exact as a signature: hemlock carrying a cargo of somewhere else. Rain on stone. A cracked market bell. Harbor damp.

I had recorded this poison years ago as a curiosity. Tonight it had been served to the crown prince of Aldmere in cherry sauce, and the only reason the heir to the throne was still breathing was that a woman who keeps impossible books had swallowed his portion first.

Unless — and here the dread stopped being abstract and put its cold hand around my throat — unless the poison hadn't been meant for the prince at all. Unless the prince was the test, and the true target was the palate that would recognize the flavor. Unless someone wanted to know, urgently, secretly, whether Oscar Voss had read a certain page.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above me, the cellar door opened, and footsteps started down the stairs — unhurried, measured, the tread of a man who owns every stair he climbs.

Corvane. Come to ask me about my evening.

I closed the ledger on the margin note, laid my palm flat on the cover, and arranged my face into the flat competent blank of a servant whose feet hurt, while behind it every column in my body refused to balance.
