## 2. Ledgers of Impossible Things

The cellar under the Guild of Impossible Things was the only place in the world where Oscar Voss felt entirely honest.

Not because she told the truth here — she lied constantly, professionally, everywhere — but because down here, among the shelves and the cold stone and the smell of ink and tallow, everything was written down. Every lie had a line item. Every theft had a column. The archive did not care who you were; it cared only that your numbers balanced. In a life spent tasting other people's intentions and swallowing what they hid, there was a profound comfort in arithmetic.

She came down after midnight, as always, carrying the day's receipts in a leather satchel against her ribs: three memories of first snowfall, traded from a dying woman in the Saltward district for two more winters than her lungs should have allowed her; half a bottled sunset, decanted and split between two brothers who could not agree on which of them their mother had loved more; and a single ember of dragon-fire, sold by no one, bought by no one, moved only because the vault it slept in was being repainted and the fire objected to the smell of fresh plaster.

Oscar sat at her desk — a slab of ship's timber scarred with candle-wax and older scripts — and opened the master ledger.

The ledger itself was an impossible thing, though a modest one as impossible things went. It had been traded into the guild's keeping ninety years ago from a monastery that no longer existed, and it recorded whatever was written in it in whatever language its keeper could read. When Oscar wrote *hemlock, 4 drams* in her own hand, the ledger understood. When she'd been a girl of nineteen, barely able to spell *arsenic*, the ledger had understood that too. It was patient. It was precise. It was the closest thing to God she believed in.

She dipped her pen and began.

*Received: 3 memories, winter/first-snow, quality high, donor terminal.*
*Paid out: 2 years, lifespan, unsecured.*
*Balance: owed to donor's estate, 1 memory, any season.*

Her pen scratched. The candle guttered and steadied. Somewhere overhead, the guildhouse settled on its foundations like an old dog turning over.

This was the work nobody saw. Upstairs, the guild dealt in wonder — clients came through the blue door on Thieve's Row and gasped at jars of last light, at vials of the smell of a house you grew up in, at small tin boxes that held the sound of someone's laughter from before they died. Down here, Oscar made sure the wonder added up. A memory given without payment was a debt the universe remembered. A sunset bottled twice was a sunset stolen from somewhere real. The guild's whole trade rested on the ledger staying true, and the ledger stayed true because Oscar Voss checked it the way a taster checks soup: thoroughly, cynically, with her whole attention, expecting poison in every spoonful.

Halfway through the sunset account, she stopped.

The numbers were wrong.

Not dramatically. That was the thing that made her blood go cold and quiet rather than hot — it was off by almost nothing. Half a sunset received, half a sunset paid out, and yet the entry for the decanting showed a loss of three drops. Three drops of a bottled evening. She ran the column again. Ran it a third time, lips moving, the way she'd been taught by the old archivist whose name she still didn't know whether to trust.

Three drops. Someone had taken three drops out of a jar of captured dusk, and they had entered it in the ledger — entered it *neatly*, in a fair imitation of her hand — but not in her hand.

She knew her own writing the way she knew her own tongue. She knew the slight drag her nib made on the downstroke when her wrist was tired, the way her sevens never quite crossed, how she pressed harder at the start of a line and let the ink feather at the end. This entry dragged in all the right places. But the sevens were crossed. And whoever had written it had begun each line softly and finished hard, the exact reverse of her habit.

It was her handwriting worn like a borrowed coat.

Oscar sat back. The candle flame leaned and righted itself. Above her, faintly, she heard footsteps crossing the floor of the counting room — the particular uneven gait she would have known anywhere, one boot heel struck harder than the other, a man who had learned to walk loudly so no one would ever hear him coming quietly.

Corvane.

* * *

He came down the stairs with a lamp he didn't need — the cellar lamps burned without oil, another modest impossibility the guild kept the way a rich man keeps a clock that costs more than a house — and set it on the corner of her desk without asking, which was his way of saying this was now his desk too.

"Working late," he said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't praise. Corvane was sixty if he was anything, lean as a folded letter, with grey in his beard arranged so precisely it looked painted on. He wore the guildmaster's ring on his thumb instead of his signet finger, which everyone assumed was eccentricity and Oscar had long suspected was so he could feel it against the wood of every door he pushed open. "I'm told the prince dined well tonight."

"The prince dined adequately," Oscar said. "The cook is still drowning the quail in honey because he thinks sweetness hides ambition."

"And did it?"

"Honey hides nothing from me." She kept her eyes on the ledger. "You didn't come down here to discuss quail."

"No." He drew the stool from beneath the second desk — the empty one, the one kept for the archivist before her — and sat, unhurried, arranging his coat tails with the care of a man seating himself at his own trial. "Tell me about the fourth course."

"There was nothing wrong with the fourth course."

"I didn't ask if anything was wrong with it. I asked you to tell me about it."

Oscar laid down her pen. She made herself do it slowly, setting it parallel to the ledger's edge, because her fingers wanted to grip it like a knife and Corvane missed nothing, least of all what a person's hands confessed.

"Fourth course," she said. "Braised eel, river-caught, wine sauce. I tasted it. It was clean."

"It was clean," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "And the fifth?"

"The fifth course hadn't been served. I was dismissed before—"

"You tasted the fifth course, Oscar." His voice stayed warm. Warmth was the most frightening register he owned. "In the pantry corridor, off the serving tray, before it went up. You stood behind the door where the page can't see you, because you always stand behind the door, because you've never once trusted a palace servant farther than you could throw the tray." He folded his hands. "Hemlock. And something else. Tell me what else."

The cellar was very quiet. The impossible lamps hummed at the edge of hearing, a sound like a held note.

"How do you know what I tasted in a pantry corridor?"

"I know everything that happens under my roof."

"That isn't an answer, guildmaster."

"No," he said, and smiled, and let it sit there between them like a coin on a table neither of them would pick up.

Oscar looked at him. Twenty-two years she had belonged to this guild — recruited at seventeen, when she'd survived a poisoning meant for her father and spat the residue into a cup and said, hoarsely, *it was henbane cut with something cheaper*, and a stranger in a good coat had bought her dinner and asked her to read a page aloud. Twenty-two years of ledgers and loyalty and the slow accumulation of a body that could hold things no body should hold. She knew every tone Corvane had. She had catalogued him the way she catalogued everything: by effect, by dose, by what he did to her blood.

Tonight he was dosing her with patience, and patience from Corvane meant he wanted something.

"The hemlock was ordinary," she said. "Court-grade. Bought through the same apothecary channel the last three attempts came from — the crown will find the supplier inside a week and hang him, and the supplier won't be the buyer, and everyone will be satisfied except the person who paid." She paused. "But the hemlock was carrying something. An old preparation. Bitter root, mineral undertone, and underneath it—" she searched for the word she'd reached for at the tray, the word that had made her stomach drop before her mind caught up, "—a signature. A method of binding the poison so it wakes slowly. I've seen it once before."

"Where?"

"In our own records." She turned the ledger toward him, to the oldest section, the pages she'd transcribed herself in her first year, entries copied from ledgers older still. "Page forty-one. An account from before my time. A dish prepared for a border delegation, eighty years ago. The poisoner used the same binding. The entry calls it *the slow key*. It says the preparation came from—" she watched his face as she said it, watching for the flicker, the tell, the drop of sweat that would taste of guilt, "—a country that no longer appears on any map."

Corvane's face did nothing at all. That was the flicker. Men flinched; Corvane went still, the way water goes still over something deep.

"Old entries are old stories," he said. "Half of them are fables the guild tells itself to feel ancient. What matters is the present account. Who has access to the crown prince's kitchen who also knows a preparation from page forty-one?"

"That," Oscar said, "is what I intend to find out."

"Alone?"

"I found it alone. I'll trace it alone."

"You'll do nothing of the kind." He rose, smoothing his coat, and the warmth drained out of his voice so smoothly she might have imagined it. "The guild does not involve itself in succession politics. We trade in things people want, Oscar, and dead princes want nothing. If the crown asks, we are merchants of curiosities and nothing more. You tasted a dish. You found nothing. Am I understood?"

"You're understood." She met his eyes. "Whether I obey is a separate question, and we both know it, or you wouldn't have come down here yourself instead of sending Maren."

Something crossed his face at Maren's name — quickly, gone, filed away. He tapped the ledger once with one finger, right beside the entry with the crossed sevens, and she felt the tap go through the table into her sternum.

"Keep your books balanced, taster," he said. "That's all I've ever asked of you. Balance is safety. Imbalance is how kingdoms burn." He took his lamp and went up the stairs, one heel striking harder than the other, and left her alone with the humming lamps and the cold.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She worked.

That was the discipline of it, the comfort of it: when the world went crooked, you straightened the columns. Oscar pulled the day's remaining accounts toward her and began the full audit, the deep one, the one she did quarterly and had done early tonight without deciding to. Memory receipts. Sunset inventories. The candle-vault, the rain-jar, the shelf of unlabeled boxes that even she was not permitted to open. Line by line, page by page, her pen moving in the dark like a blind woman's fingers reading a face.

She found the second forgery an hour later. Then the third.

Small losses. Always small — a thimble of dream, a pinch of salt from a jar of someone's grief, four days subtracted from a purchased year. Never enough to notice unless you were her. Never enough to matter to anyone who didn't understand that the ledger's whole holiness lay in exactness, that three drops of stolen dusk was not a petty theft but a crack in the load-bearing wall of everything.

And every forged entry was in her handwriting, and none of them were hers.

By the third hour she had stopped auditing and started hunting. She pulled the master ledger into her lap and turned to the frontispiece, where the guild's oldest law was inscribed in a script so old the monastery that invented it had been forgotten: *What is written truly cannot be unwritten falsely.* Beneath it, in newer ink, the operating rule every keeper swore to: the ledger answered only to its keeper's seal, pressed in wax, renewed at each change of hands. Her seal. Her wax. Her thumbprint in the cooling red, done on the night she took the desk, done every solstice since.

She turned to the seal page.

The wax was intact. The impression was hers. The thumbprint ridge-lines matched her own thumb, which she pressed against it now, heart tapping, to be certain.

Intact. Perfect. Untouched.

Which meant one of exactly two things. Either the forger had access to her seal while it was warm — and the seal lived in a locked drawer in her own rooms, and she slept badly and lightly and would wake to a mouse breathing in the walls — or the forger needed no seal at all, because the forger had some other key to the ledger, some older permission, some line of credit opened before she was born and never closed.

Or — and this thought arrived last and sat down heaviest — the ledger itself had decided to let someone else write in it. Ledgers of impossible things were, after all, impossible things. They had preferences. They had, occasionally, loyalties.

Oscar closed the book and pressed both palms flat against its cover and sat in the humming dark, and felt, for the first time in twenty-two years, the specific vertigo of a woman standing on a floor she had always trusted and hearing, beneath her feet, a hollow sound.

Someone was reading her ledgers. Someone had been reading them for months — the forgeries went back at least to midsummer — walking through her accounts wearing her hand, taking small sips of the guild's inventory, and leaving everything balanced just enough that only she would ever catch it. Which meant they weren't stealing to sell. You don't skim three drops of dusk for profit. You skim to test. To learn. To find out whether anyone is watching closely enough to see.

They were testing her. And tonight, upstairs, Corvane had known what she tasted in a corridor she'd stood in alone.

She blew out her candle, though the lamps didn't dim, and gathered the satchel, and climbed the stairs into the grey hour before dawn. Through the counting room's narrow window, the sky over the rooftops had gone the color of wet slate. Rain was coming — a real storm, by the weight of the air, the kind that made the river climb its banks and knocked branches down across Thieve's Row and drove sensible people indoors behind bolted doors.

At the top of the cellar stair she paused, hand on the rail, and listened to the wind rising over the city like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Somewhere out in that weather, someone who wrote with her hand was awake too.

The first thunder broke as she reached her rooms, and beneath it — faint, absurd, impossible — she thought she heard knocking at the guild's blue door.
