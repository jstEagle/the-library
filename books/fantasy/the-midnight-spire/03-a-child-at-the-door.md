## 3. A Child at the Door

The storm came up the river valley like something with a grudge. I heard it before I saw it — a long low grinding in the dark, the sound the sky makes when it has decided against you — and by the time I'd shuttered the archive windows and banked the ledger-room lamps, rain was hitting the guildhouse roof hard enough to make conversation impossible.

Which suited me fine. I had accounts to balance.

The cellar archive of the Guild of Impossible Things is not a grand place. People imagine vaults of gold and thrones of bone; what we actually have is forty feet of damp stone under a chandler's shop, shelves of ledgers bound in everything from calfskin to dragon-hide, and a smell like old candles and older secrets. I had worked there eleven years, first as Corvane's clerk, then as his keeper of records, and in that time I had learned one rule above all others: *the ledger is the truth*. Not the trade itself, not the trader's word, not even the memory as it was lived. The ink. Ink doesn't flatter. Ink doesn't grieve.

I was three lines into reconciling a sale of bottled sunset — someone in the Marches had paid a scandalous price for the last evening of a summer that no longer existed anywhere else — when the knocking started.

Three strikes. A pause. Three more. Then, absurdly, small and thin beneath the storm: a child's fist, hammering on a door built to withstand siege engines and worse.

I went up the stairs two at a time with a lamp in one hand and a knife in my boot, because eleven years in this trade teaches you that the strange things always arrive at the worst hours, dressed in the most disarming shapes.

* * *

The guild door is oak, iron-banded, older than the street it stands on. It has no keyhole that locks and no handle that turns for anyone the house does not want inside. I have seen grown men — inquisitors, kings' men, one very persistent archmage — stand before it and knock until their knuckles split while the door regarded them with all the warmth of a cliff face.

It opened before I reached it.

Not wide. Just enough. And through the gap, into the lamplight, stepped a girl.

Eight years old, perhaps. Small for it, even. Soaked to the skin in a way that made no sense, because the covered porch would have kept the rain off anyone standing where she stood. Her hair was dark and cut badly, hacked short with something duller than a knife, and her eyes were the color of shallow water over pale stone — that particular grey-green you see in northern rivers, if you ever get that far north, which almost nobody does anymore.

She looked at me. She looked at the lamp. She looked past both of us, down the corridor toward the cellar stairs, as though she knew exactly where they led.

Then she said something.

It wasn't any language I knew, and I knew a great many. The crown requires its tasters to be fluent in six tongues and passable in four more, because poisoners are a cosmopolitan lot. This was none of them. It had a sound like water going over stones — rounded, unhurried, with stresses that fell where no grammar I owned expected them. Four syllables, maybe five. She said it again when I didn't answer, patient, the way you speak to someone you assume is merely slow.

And the floor went out from under me.

Because I *knew* it. Not the meaning — I didn't have the meaning yet — but the shape of it, the rhythm of it, the way a musician knows a tune he's only ever seen written down. I had seen those rhythms before. In my own handwriting.

"Wait," I said. "Wait here. Don't— just wait."

She waited. Of course she waited. She had knocked on a door that opens for no one, and it had opened, and whatever patience had carried her through the storm to us, she had plenty of it left.

I took the stairs back down to the archive three at a time, lamp guttering, heart doing something unprofessional against my ribs.

* * *

My oldest ledger sits on the bottom shelf, wrapped in oilcloth, and I am the only person who has touched it in eleven years. It was already in the archive when I arrived — Corvane told me it had come with the founding collection, that it predated even him, which is a sentence that should terrify anyone who knows how long Corvane has been guildmaster. Its pages are vellum gone soft as cloth. The early entries aren't mine. They're in an antique merchant's hand, cramped and fussy, recording trades so old they read like myth: *one winter, traded entire, for a promise kept*; *the name of a river that has since changed its course, sold back to the river*.

And then, near the middle, a page I had copied out myself in my first year, working from loose folios whose origin I never asked about. Words in the antique script. Beneath each line, a phonetic rendering in Common — someone's attempt, decades ago, to trap a spoken language on paper the way you'd pin a moth.

I found the page by touch in the dark, because of course I knew where it was. I held the lamp close. I read the phonetics aloud, clumsy, mangling them.

The sounds were hers.

Not similar. Hers. The same four syllables, the same fall and rise, the same unhurried water-over-stones cadence, preserved in my ledger like a fly in amber, waiting however many years it took for the mouth that spoke it to come knocking.

I stood in the cold of the archive with the lamp shaking in my hand and felt wonder go through me like a current — real wonder, the kind I hadn't felt since I was a child myself, the kind the guild usually beat out of you by your second year. A language out of a dead page, alive. Standing in our doorway. Dripping on our stones.

Then the second feeling arrived, right on schedule, because I am who I am.

Guilt.

I don't know how else to describe it. Nothing on that page was a crime. Loose folios, an old transcription, work done before my time. But I have kept this guild's accounts for eleven years, and I know what most of our inventory actually is. Bottled sunsets are the pretty ones. We also hold last words, homelands, faces of the dead, the smell of a kitchen that burned down with everyone in it. Every entry in every ledger is something somebody lost, priced and filed. I had never once thought of the oldest ledger's anonymous language as somebody's loss. It had been an entry. Curious. Unclassifiable. Filed.

Now its owner was eight years old and alone in a storm, and I had helped file her.

I wrapped the ledger in its oilcloth, took the lamp, and went back up.

* * *

She hadn't moved. Rain still fell behind her in silver sheets, but she herself was dry now, or nearly — I noticed it without understanding it, and decided, in the great tradition of guild clerks everywhere, not to understand it until morning.

"You'd better come in," I said, uselessly, in Common.

She tilted her head. Then she pointed at herself and said the word again — the four-syllable phrase — and this time I understood it wasn't a greeting. It was a name. Or names. Or possibly a whole sentence that meant something I wouldn't learn for weeks, though at the time I wrote it down later simply as: *this is what I am called*.

"I'm Oscar," I said, touching my chest.

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she reached out and touched my forearm — lightly, deliberately, the way you'd touch a book you weren't sure was safe to open — and said something new. Two syllables, falling. Whatever it meant, she gave it to me like a gift.

Then she walked past me into the guildhouse as if she had lived here all her life, which, in a sense I could not yet imagine, she may have.

* * *

Here is what I want to record about that night, because the nights after blurred badly and this one deserves its own clean page.

I fed her. Bread, cheese, dried apples from the chandler's stock next door. She ate like someone who had learned not to trust the next meal, quickly and completely, watching me the whole time. When she finished she folded her hands and looked at me with an expression I have since learned to translate without needing the language: *your turn. Explain yourself.*

I couldn't. So instead I did what I do. I got paper.

I drew. Badly — I'm a taster and a tally-keeper, not an artist. But I drew a tower, tall and crooked, and beside it a smaller building, and I pointed at the floor beneath our feet, at the walls around us, at the whole stubborn improbable house, and I raised my eyebrows.

She understood instantly. Children always understand drawing faster than adults; adults have been embarrassed out of it.

She took the charcoal from my hand. And she did not draw our guildhouse.

She drew a building — I recognized it immediately, the proportions, the deep-set door, the narrow high windows — and it was our guildhouse, exactly, except older. Rougher. With a carved lintel over the door bearing letters in the water-over-stones script, and around the base of the tower, other buildings, a whole street of them, drawn with the confidence of someone sketching her own street. Because it plainly was her own street.

Then she tapped the drawing, looked me full in the face, and spoke.

Five syllables this time. I didn't need the translation. Some things cross any language: the tone of a person naming their home.

Except the home was here. Under my feet. Our cellar, our shelves, our locked door — hers, once, named in a tongue that predated the kingdom that now claimed the ground it stood on.

I sat back. My hands were not steady. I am a woman whose hands do not shake over hemlock, and they were shaking over charcoal.

Because I remembered — suddenly, horribly clearly — a line in the founding folios, the antiquarian's fussy hand, a marginal note I'd skimmed a hundred times and never once parsed: a notation that the property was acquired *in exchange for the prior name*, surrendered willingly, price undisclosed. I'd assumed it was guild whimsy. Houses change hands; houses change names. That's all a name is.

That is not all a name is. I know that now. I sat across from a soaked eight-year-old in a house that had forgotten what it used to be called, and I knew it in my teeth.

"What did it used to be?" I whispered, to her, to myself, to nobody. "What was this place called?"

She answered. Of course she answered. She said it slowly, kindly, twice, the way you offer something precious to a stranger you hope will hold it carefully.

And I wrote it down. Phonetically, in the margin of my working notes, in my own neat clerk's hand — the same hand that had copied her language into the oldest ledger years ago without knowing what it cost anyone.

The letters looked wrong under my charcoal. Like a stolen coat.

* * *

Near midnight, Corvane came down.

I heard him on the stairs — he has a particular tread, unhurried, weight slightly forward, the walk of a man who has never once entered a room he didn't intend to own — and I had perhaps four seconds to decide what to do with the drawings, the notes, the sleeping child curled in the chair by the cold hearth with my own cloak over her.

I hid nothing. Later I would understand this was instinct, not wisdom; later still I'd understand it was neither. That night, I simply didn't move fast enough, and Corvane stood in the doorway with lamplight behind him and took in the scene with those grey eyes that have never once shown surprise.

"A guest," he said.

"She came to the door," I said. "In the storm."

"They often do." He crossed the room and stood over the girl, looking down at her the way he looks at inventory — and I want to be fair to him here, because fairness will matter later, when everything is unfair. There was no cruelty in his face. There was appraisal, and beneath the appraisal, something that might, in a better man, have been tenderness. He looked at her the way a man looks at the last of anything.

"Do you know what she is, Oscar?"

"No," I said. Which was true. I knew only what she had drawn, and what my ledger held, and the shape of a guilt I hadn't yet found words for.

Corvane nodded slowly. "Neither do I," he said. "But the door opened for her."

He let that sit. The guild door opens for no one. He and I both knew what it meant, and neither of us said it, and the unsaid thing filled the room like smoke.

"Keep her warm," Corvane said finally, turning for the stairs. "Feed her. And tomorrow — tomorrow we find out what she's carrying, and what it's worth."

*Worth.* Even then, half-asleep in the chair, the girl's eyes opened. Just a slit. Just enough.

I have spent my life tasting other people's intentions in food. That night, for the first time, I tasted one plain, in the air, in a single ordinary word — and it was bitter, faintly, the way almonds are bitter, the way the beginning of a thing is bitter before you can name it.

The child closed her eyes again. Outside, the storm was moving off downriver, taking its thunder with it, and the guildhouse stood silent around us, keeping whatever it keeps.

I sat up with my notes until dawn. On the oldest ledger's page, beneath the transcriptions in dead men's hands, I added a line of my own:

*A speaker lives. The language was never ours to file.*

Underneath it, after a long while, I added the name she'd given me, and the name she'd given the house, side by side, in the script I could not yet read.

By morning I intended to show them to Corvane.

By morning, I had already begun to suspect I shouldn't.
