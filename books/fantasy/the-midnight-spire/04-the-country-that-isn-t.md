## 4. The Country That Isn't

The ledger did not want to be read.

Oscar had felt this before — the stubbornness of certain pages, the way old ink could seem to pull away from the eye like water from oiled cloth. Guild paper sometimes did that. Paper that had been traded for, or paid in, or written under conditions no honest scribe would recognize as writing. But she had never felt it from her *own* hand before.

She sat alone in her narrow room above the guildhouse kitchen, one candle lit, the door barred with a chair she'd wedged beneath the knob. Downstairs, the storm still worked at the shutters. The child — Ashka, though Oscar didn't yet know that was her name — slept in the cellar archive, wrapped in Maren's spare coat, guarded by two guild members who were either protecting her or holding her, and Corvane had been very careful not to say which.

The page lay open on Oscar's desk. Page one of the oldest ledger she kept. The ledger she had inherited, eleven years ago, from a dead man named Osket Brandt, whose handwriting hers had slowly grown to resemble, because for three years she had copied his entries letter by letter until the two scripts became one.

The entry was dated in no calendar she knew. It was numbered in no system she used. And it was written in the same language the girl had spoken through the storm-dark doorway, syllables like river stones turning over, older than the kingdom's founding, older than the word *kingdom* itself.

Except — and here Oscar's stomach turned over slow and cold — except it wasn't entirely that language. It was that language rendered in the guild's cipher. Her cipher. The cipher of balances and debits and prices paid.

Someone had translated Vellmark into arithmetic.

*Vellmark.* She didn't know where the word came from. It rose out of the page the way heat rises off a stone at dusk, unasked for, undeniable. She said it once aloud, quietly, and the candle flame leaned toward her as if listening.

The entry ran long. Longer than any entry she'd ever recorded. She worked through it the way she worked through everything — methodically, line by line, tasting each phrase for what it concealed — and by the third hour she understood enough that she had to set the pen down and press both hands flat against the desk to keep them from shaking.

It was an inventory.

Not of goods. Not of memories, not of bottled sunsets, not of the last embers of dragon-fire. An inventory of a *country*. Every parish. Every market town. Every bridge and bell tower and breed of sheep. Each item listed with its weight — not in stone or ounces but in something the cipher called *holding*, a unit Oscar had seen used only for memories too large to trade whole, memories that had to be divided into shares.

And beside each item, a price.

And beside each price, a buyer's mark.

There were dozens of marks. Some she recognized from the guild's own rolls — merchant houses, minor nobles, a monastery that had purchased, apparently, the memory of a river's original course. Others were seals she had never seen, though their geometry carried the unmistakable weight of royal warrant. Crown work. Treaty work.

At the bottom of the page, beneath the final line item — *the language entire: one holder, living; see attached* — someone had written a single sentence in plain trade-tongue, in handwriting that was not Osket Brandt's:

*Erasure complete upon payment of balance. Holder to remain as index. Do not restore. Do not remember. The country is closed.*

*The country is closed.* Like a shop sign hung in a window. Like a ledger balanced and shelved.

Oscar looked at her own hands. Taster's hands. Poison-scarred, calloused at the fingertips from years of dipping silver spoons into other people's dinners. Hands that had recorded, faithfully and without question, every transaction the guild had asked her to record.

Had she recorded *this*?

No. This page predated her by decades. But the system was hers now. The cipher was hers. The discipline of turning unbearable things into neat columns of figures — she had maintained it, refined it, taught it to two apprentices. Somewhere in the vault below her feet, if this entry existed, then others did too. Payment schedules. Delivery confirmations. The quiet, orderly paperwork of a nation's disappearance.

And somewhere in those pages, perhaps, was her own signature, appended to some later maintenance of the account. A yearly audit. A confirmation that the index remained living and the country remained closed.

She thought about all the entries she'd made without understanding. The strange ones. The ones Corvane had brought her personally, late at night, saying only, *Record it, Voss, don't read it.* She had assumed shame — guild business that embarrassed even the guild. Now she wondered if it was simpler than shame. Maybe he just hadn't wanted her to do what she was doing right now.

Maybe he hadn't wanted her to find the country.

* * *

The child woke near dawn, and Oscar went down to her.

The archive at night was a place of soft breathing — thousands of ledgers exhaling their faint smell of dust and tallow and, underneath, something sweeter, the scent memory leaves behind when it is stored properly. The girl sat up in her nest of coat and blanket when Oscar descended the stairs, and her eyes found Oscar immediately, unafraid, as if she'd been waiting.

"You read it," the girl said. In trade-tongue, clear as a bell. Last night she had spoken only the old language, the one that had come off the page like water. Now her words were ordinary, a little formal, the accent of someone who had learned speech from books rather than streets. "I felt it. When you said the name."

"Vellmark," Oscar said.

The girl flinched — a small, involuntary tightening, the way people flinch at the name of a dead parent — and then, deliberately, unflinched. "Yes," she said. "That's me. I mean—" she pressed a small hand flat against her chest, "—that's where I am. All of it. In here."

"How much?"

"All of it." She said it simply, without pride. "Every road. Every song. My grandmother's hands making cheese. The way the harbor smelled in autumn. The tax rolls. The jokes." Something moved across her face, grief and amusement tangled together. "You wouldn't believe the jokes."

"What's your name? Yours, not the country's."

"Ashka." Then, after a pause, with the precision of someone reciting something learned: "Ashka Vell-Emmerich, daughter of the scriptorium at Harn, which stood where there is now a hill with nothing on it."

Oscar sat down on the floor across from her, because standing suddenly felt wrong, too tall, too far away. "Who gave you to the guild?"

"No one gave me." Ashka drew her knees up. "They always say it wrong, the traders. They say *sold*, or *given*, or *found*. Nobody *chose*, so nobody writes *chose*." She looked at Oscar with eyes that were eight years old and also, somehow, considerably older. "My teachers chose for me first, and then I chose too. When the treaty men came and started buying — they bought so cheaply at first, Oscar, people sold a wedding day for a horse, a father's face for a debt cleared — my teachers understood what was happening before anyone else. You can't sell a country piece by piece unless somebody keeps the whole of it somewhere. So they needed a vessel. A living index. Someone young enough to hold sixty years more, someone trained in the deep script so nothing would decay."

"They picked you."

"There were three candidates. The other two were older." Ashka shrugged, a child's shrug, and it broke Oscar's heart precisely because it was a child's shrug. "I volunteered. That's the part that isn't in your ledger anywhere. I *wanted* to. I thought I was going to save us." Her voice caught, steadied. "I thought someone would come back for me."

Oscar's throat was tight. "How long have you been an asset on our books?"

"I don't count time the way you do. Time outside is..." She waved a hand vaguely toward the stairs, toward the world. "Thin. But the last person who came to take readings wore a different style of collar than the one who came before him. So. A while."

*A while.* Years, probably. Possibly most of her life, moved between safehouses, read from like a reference volume, stored between uses. And Oscar had been keeper of the ledger for eleven of those years without ever once asking what *holder, living* meant. She had seen the notation a dozen times. She had balanced against it. She had treated a human being as a line item and gone home at night feeling virtuous about the tidiness of her columns.

"I'm sorry," Oscar said. It was inadequate and they both knew it.

Ashka studied her with that unnerving directness. "You're the recorder."

"Yes."

"Then you can do something better than sorry." She reached out and took Oscar's hand — her grip surprisingly strong — and turned it palm-up, the way a fortune-teller might. "You can write me down *true*. Everything they paid to make forgotten, you can put it back in ink. Ink doesn't forget unless someone burns it."

"The guild burns things," Oscar said quietly. "We're very good at it."

"Then we should hurry, shouldn't we?"

* * *

Corvane summoned her at midmorning, and the summons itself was a message: not a note slipped under her door, but a servant, publicly sent, so the whole house would know the guildmaster wanted the archivist. Corvane believed in the theater of ordinary procedure. He believed that crimes committed in daylight looked like bookkeeping, and bookkeeping, done correctly, looked like virtue.

He received her in his study, which smelled of cedar and cold tea, and which contained, as always, the great wall of small drawers — his personal collection of impossible things too valuable or too dangerous for the general archive. He was feeding crumbs to none of the birds that weren't there; he simply liked to stand by the window with a plate of bread as if he were, a gesture Oscar had long ago stopped finding eccentric and started finding obscene.

"The child," he said, without preamble. "What did you learn from the page?"

Enough to hang us all, she thought. Enough to hang me twice.

"She's an index," Oscar said. "A living record of Vellmark."

Something crossed Corvane's face — quick, quickly mastered. Not surprise. Confirmation. "Yes," he said. "I suspected as much when Halvard's office began asking questions last spring. Accounts that old don't surface on their own. Something is calling them forward." He turned from the window. "You understand what she is, then. What she's worth."

"I understand what she *is*, Guildmaster."

"Do you?" He set down the plate. For a moment the affable mask thinned, and Oscar saw the man beneath it, the one who had brokered the sale of a river's memory and called it mercy, because the alternative to selling a thing is watching it destroyed for free. "Vellmark died thirty years ago, Oscar. Not by fire, not by sword. By *treaty*. Three crowns signed, and the signatures were expensive, and the forgetting was the consideration that made the peace hold. If she stands in a public square and speaks that language, if one farmer remembers his grandmother's lullaby, if one mapmaker starts wondering why the border runs where it runs — treaties unravel. Wars start. Do you know how many people have died since in wars that would not have happened if Vellmark had stayed closed?" He let the number sit unspoken between them, enormous. "Her protection is not sentiment. It is statecraft."

"And keeping her in a cellar for thirty years — is that statecraft too?"

"It's accounting." He said it without apology. "You of all people shouldn't flinch from the term. We keep her because she must be kept. Because losing her means losing the last copy of anything, forever, and the guild does not deal in forever-lost. Even the crown understands that. It's why they haven't killed her. Why they *can't* kill her. Destroying the index doesn't destroy the truth, it just makes the truth unfindable, and unfindable truths have a way of resurfacing at the worst possible moment." He smiled, thin and reasonable. "So. She stays in the vault. Warded, fed, comfortable. For her protection."

"For her protection," Oscar repeated.

"You may visit her. You're her keeper of record; it's fitting." He returned to his window, dismissal implicit in his shoulders, and then, just as Oscar reached the door: "One more thing, Voss. Your oldest ledger. Page one. I'll want it moved to the inner vault today. Under seal. There are parties who pay very well for exactly the kind of information that page contains, and I'd rather it not walk out of this house in anyone's pocket."

"Whose pockets are you worried about, Guildmaster?"

He didn't turn around. "Everyone's," he said pleasantly. "Including yours. Especially yours, if you keep reading entries marked above your clearance."

* * *

She moved the ledger. Of course she moved the ledger. She was a guildswoman, and obedience was the mortar of the guild, and she carried page one down into the inner vault with steady hands and laid it in its designated drawer among the other sealed accounts of things that should never have been purchasable.

Then, alone in the dark of the stacks, she did the thing she had been not-doing since dawn.

She opened her working ledger — the current one, the one she kept on her body, the one no order could seize without cutting it out of her coat — and she turned to a fresh page, and she wrote, in trade-cipher, the only rebellion available to a woman built of records:

*Account 4471-V. Holder: living. Status: mislabeled.*

Because that was the crack she'd found, the hairline fracture running through all of Corvane's beautiful arithmetic. The ledger called Ashka an *asset*. Assets could be sold, leased, spent. But an index wasn't an asset. An index was a *witness*. And witnesses, unlike assets, could testify — and testimony, once given, couldn't be bought back, burned, or balanced against.

Somewhere in the palace, Dame Halvard was already asking her pointed questions about tasters and archives. Somewhere in the treaty offices, buyers' seals were stirring. And somewhere in a cellar below her feet, a girl who had volunteered to save her country was waiting for someone to write her down true.

Oscar clicked the pen shut. Above her, muffled by floors of stone and paper, the guildhouse went about its morning — footsteps, the kettle, the ordinary commerce of extraordinary theft.

She had eleven years of clean columns behind her, and she had just, knowingly, entered her first dishonest figure.

It would not be the last.
