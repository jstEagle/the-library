## 5. What the Crown Pays For

Dame Halvard had been an inquisitor for nineteen years, and in nineteen years she had learned that the truth rarely announced itself. It seeped. It came up through floorboards like damp, through the cracks between a steward's sentences, through the way a kitchen hand's eyes flicked toward a door before he lied about it.

The poison in the prince's dinner was seeping into her inquiry the same way.

She stood in the palace kitchens at half past six in the morning, before the fires were fully lit, watching the undercook scrub a copper pot that was already clean. The boy had been at it since she arrived. He would be at it until his knuckles bled if she let him, because Dame Halvard of the Office of Inquiry had asked to see the pot, and no one in this building wanted to be the one who had touched it last.

"Tell me again," she said pleasantly, "about the taster."

The head cook, a broad woman named Sela with flour to her elbows and sweat on her lip despite the cold morning, wiped her hands on her apron for the fourth time. "Oscar Voss. Been with us six years. Never missed a day. Tastes everything the high table eats, same as always."

"Same as always," Halvard repeated. She turned the phrase over like a coin she suspected of being clipped. "And did she taste the hemlock?"

The kitchen went very quiet. Even the boy stopped scrubbing.

"She tasted it," Sela said carefully. "She spat it out. Warned us. Saved His Highness's life, near enough."

"Mm." Halvard walked the length of the prep table, trailing one gloved finger along the wood. Someone had scrubbed this too — recently, and thoroughly. Too thoroughly. Kitchens were never clean; kitchens only pretended to be. A surface scoured to this pitch meant someone had been told to scour it, and people were only ever told to scour things when there had been something to find. "Hemlock in the venison sauce. And yet the sauce itself has vanished. Not set aside as evidence. Not sealed by the guard. Vanished."

"The physician took—"

"The physician's office confirms nothing was delivered to them." She smiled at Sela, and made the smile warm, because warmth disarmed people faster than cold ever had. "I'm not here to punish anyone, Mistress Sela. I'm here because someone tried to kill the crown prince in your kitchen, and either you know who, or you know who doesn't want me finding out. Both are useful to me. Only one of them is safe."

Sela's jaw tightened. Nineteen years had taught Halvard that jaw-tightening preceded confession more often than tears did.

"There was a man," Sela said finally. "Came Tuesday with a delivery of saffron from the Marcher coast. We didn't order saffron. But the seal looked right, and Voss inspected it like she always does, and—" She stopped. Her eyes did the thing Halvard had come to expect: a flick toward the door, quick as a fish turning. "She tasted the saffron straight off. Said it was fine. Said it was *very* fine."

"Very fine."

"Her words." Sela lowered her voice. "Look, Dame. Voss is the best taster the crown's ever had. Everyone knows it. She can tell you what farm a pig was raised on from one spoon of the broth. But that day she tasted the saffron and went white as whey, and then she said it was fine anyway, and we served it, and three hours later the prince is vomiting blood and the guards are kicking our doors in. You tell me what that means."

It means, Halvard thought, that your taster recognized something she'd hoped never to taste twice. It means she made a choice in the space of a swallow. It means she is either the most remarkable traitor in the kingdom or the most remarkable witness, and in my experience the two wear the same coat.

What she said was: "Thank you. You've been helpful. The crown remembers its friends."

Sela did not look comforted. Good. Comforted people talked too much; frightened people talked *carefully*, which was where the truth hid.

* * *

The Office of Inquiry occupied four rooms above the Chancery, and by law its records were public to any sworn officer of the crown. By practice, three shelves of those records were locked behind a door that existed in no floor plan, and Halvard held the only key that officially did not exist.

She unlocked it now, alone, and pulled the file on royal food-tasters.

There were eleven tasters serving the palace across its halls and households. Nine had clean histories: appointed, sworn, paid, unremarkable. One had died of old age in the reign of the previous king. And the eleventh —

Oscar Voss. Appointed six years ago on a recommendation Halvard had chased down a year back and found to originate nowhere. No family listed. No prior residence. A woman who appeared, fully formed, at the palace gates with a tongue worth more than the rest of the kitchen staff combined and a past thinner than paper.

Halvard had flagged the file then and been instructed, in writing, by the Chancellor's office, to *stand down*. The instruction had used the word *stand down* twice, which was once more than such letters ever needed, and had carried a faint smell of the cellar about it — not literally, though she'd checked the paper for scent-marks twice, but in some quality of phrasing. Certain phrases were guild phrases. She knew them the way a customs officer knows counterfeit coin: not by any single feature, but by the weight.

Someone had bought Oscar Voss her position. Or bought her silence within it. Or both.

And now someone had tried to poison the prince with hemlock cut with something older — the physicians' report sat open on her desk, and its language kept circling back to *unknown compound, possibly botanical, possibly not* — and the taster who should have caught it had tasted it, gone pale, and called it fine.

Halvard poured herself tea and considered the shape of the problem the way she considered all problems: as an account.

Every crime was a ledger. Debits and credits. Who gained, who lost, who paid, and in what currency. Most men paid in coin. Some paid in blood. The Guild of Impossible Things — and she did not put that name in any written record, ever — traded in other denominations entirely. Memories. Places. Names. She had spent four years assembling proof of their existence and nine months watching each piece of proof quietly stop being true: witnesses forgetting mid-interview, documents reverting to blank pages, one informant waking with twenty years of marriage missing from behind his eyes and a wife he no longer recognized weeping in his doorway.

You could not arrest a guild that could unwrite your arrest. You could not even reliably *remember* arresting it.

So Halvard had done the arithmetic every honest officer eventually faced, and she had done what the crown actually paid her for. Not justice. The crown had courts for justice, and the courts handled theft and murder and treason among people who agreed to remain legible. What the crown paid *her* for was the other kind of case. The kind where justice, applied honestly, cost the realm more than injustice did. Where a treaty rested on a lie, and the lie rested on a grave, and the grave rested on nothing anyone could afford to dig up.

Menace wearing a lawful face — that was her uniform, and she wore it well, and she had long ago stopped asking herself whether the face underneath was still hers.

But.

She turned the saffron question over. A taster who recognized the poison and said nothing. A guild signature in the compound, if the physicians' "possibly not botanical" was what Halvard suspected it was. And beneath it all, the thing that had actually brought her down to the kitchens at dawn rather than sending a clerk:

Three weeks ago, a sealed manifest from the Office of Provisioning had listed a delivery to the Underchancery cellars. Wine, cheese, lamp oil. Ordinary. Except the requisition number belonged to a department that had been dissolved forty years ago, and the receiving clerk's signature matched, stroke for stroke, the hand on a document Halvard had found in her father's effects after his death — a deed for a house in a town whose name she had never been able to find on any map, though she had looked for thirty years, first as a grieving daughter and lately as something closer to obsession.

Vellmark. The word surfaced in her mind unbidden, the way it always did, and she pressed it down, the way she always did. It meant nothing. It was a sound her father had murmured in his sleep. Towns changed names. Provinces were redrawn. Maps were political instruments.

She wrote *Voss* on a fresh sheet, and under it: *saffron, Tuesday. Hemlock, Friday. Recognized both?*

Then she gathered her gloves, because there was one more place to ask questions, and it was not a place one arrived looking rumpled.

* * *

The Guildhall of the Chancery Ward was not, on any register, a guildhall. It was recorded as a counting-house, and it counted things, and the things it counted were none of the crown's business, and everyone involved understood this arrangement perfectly, which was why it had lasted two hundred years.

Dame Halvard arrived at noon with two clerks she trusted and a warrant so broadly worded it was functionally decorative, and she was received in the front room by a factor named Ostrec who poured wine nobody drank and answered questions nobody believed.

"We supply the palace with certain provisions," Ostrec said. "Spices. Preserved fruits. Our saffron is the finest in the Marches."

"I didn't mention saffron."

Ostrec paused for exactly the length of time a careful man needed to decide whether to look caught. "Didn't you? Forgive me. Trade talk."

"You sent a delivery Tuesday to the palace kitchens. Unordered. Sealed correctly. Your mark on the wrapping." She laid the wax impression on the table between them. It matched the guildhouse seal the way her father's deed had matched the clerk's signature — perfectly, impossibly, in a way that suggested the seal was less a stamp than a *signature*, alive enough to vary.

Ostrec examined it without touching it. "Counterfeits circulate."

"They do. Here's what interests me, Factor." She leaned forward, and let the lawful face do its work — the patience of it, the terrible courtesy. "The taster who cleared that delivery is named Oscar Voss. Six years ago she appears at the palace with no history and a recommendation from nowhere. My office asked questions. We were told to stand down. The letter bore certain phrasings that my predecessors catalogued as characteristic of a particular correspondence style. So here is my question, and please consider how much easier both our lives become depending on your answer: does your counting-house have an account open in the name of Oscar Voss?"

For a moment — half a breath — something moved behind Ostrec's eyes that Halvard had seen only twice before in her career. Not fear. Recognition. The look of a man realizing that the person across the table knows more than she should, and trying to work out *how*, and failing, and being afraid of exactly that failure.

"Dame Halvard," he said, and now his voice had changed, dropped its merchant smoothness for something older and quieter, "may I speak plainly?"

"That's what warrants are for."

"What the crown pays this office for is continuity. Treaties hold. Prices hold. Kings succeed kings without the streets learning what was traded to make it so. You serve the same client we do, whatever your writ says." He refilled her untouched wine, unhurried. "If one of our accounts concerns you, take it up through channels. You know the channels."

"I know the channels," Halvard agreed. "I also know that Tuesday's saffron and Friday's hemlock came through the same door, and that somewhere between them stands a taster who said *fine* when she meant *run*." She rose. "Tell whoever holds your ledgers that the Office of Inquiry will be asking again. And tell them—" she paused at the door, and allowed herself, for once, a sentence that was entirely hers, "—that I remember more than they think I do."

Ostrec's teacup, halfway to his mouth, stopped.

It was a small motion. A fractional hesitation. But Halvard had spent nineteen years reading rooms, and that cup told her everything: they knew what she might remember. They had known for years. Perhaps they were the reason she remembered so little of her childhood, her mother's face, the town with the house and the deed — perhaps those absences were purchases, transactions settled before she was old enough to be shown the bill.

Or perhaps her father had simply been a quiet man from an obscure town, and grief made maps of every blank.

She did not know. That was the unbearable part. She walked out into the noon light with her clerks falling in behind her and did not know, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

That evening, in her office above the Chancery, Halvard laid out what she had on the long table: the kitchen testimony, the physicians' report, the seal impression, Voss's thin file, the stand-down letter with its cellar-smelling prose.

Nine facts. Maybe ten, counting the teacup.

Against them, the machinery she served hummed along its grooves. Tomorrow she would draft her interim report, and the report would say what reports to the Chancellor always said: investigation ongoing, no conclusive findings, palace security measures strengthened. It would buy her another month. It would also, she understood with perfect clarity, land on Corvane's desk — or whatever the guildmaster called himself in the Chancery's address books — before it landed anywhere else, because the channels ran both directions and she had always known they did.

She served the guild. She had told herself for years that she served it the way a dam serves a river: containing it, shaping it, standing between its weight and the valley. That the alternative to her managed complicity was an ungoverned trade in human memory run by men with fewer scruples and worse handwriting.

Tonight, for the first time in a long while, the justification felt less like architecture and more like alibi.

Because of the saffron. Because a taster had tasted death and called it fine, which meant she had recognized it, which meant she had *known*, which meant somewhere in the palace's long chain of sealed doors and scrubbed pots there was a person who had chosen — for one swallow's worth of a moment — not to protect the prince but to warn someone else. Warn whom? Of what?

Halvard blew out her lamps and stood in the dark, and let herself say the word she had been pressing down for thirty years.

"Vellmark."

The word sat in the dark room and refused to mean anything, the way it always did, and she felt — distinctly, absurdly, like a child — that the darkness listened.

In the morning, she decided, she would pull the file on the dissolved department with the borrowed requisition numbers. In the afternoon, she would request an audience with the taster herself, official, documented, impossible to intercept through channels.

Dame Halvard banked her fire and went home through streets slick with rain, and behind her the locked shelf of records waited in the dark, patient as a grave, holding among its folders one thin file marked with a name that no map remembered and a date of birth that, if anyone had ever thought to check it against the archives of a country that officially did not exist, would have placed her birth — to the week, to the village — inside Vellmark.

She did not check. Not that night.

But she dreamed of a kitchen she had never stood in, and a woman's voice saying *taste this, little one, and tell me what country it is,* and woke with the taste of saffron in her mouth and no memory of eating anything at all.
