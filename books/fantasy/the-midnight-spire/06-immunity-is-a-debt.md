## 6. Immunity Is a Debt

I didn't sleep after Corvane left the archive.

That was nothing new. I hadn't slept properly in nine years — not since my first season as royal taster, when I learned that sleep is just another way of letting your guard down and that a taster's guard never comes down, not even in dreams. What was new was the shape of my waking. Before tonight it had been the ordinary insomnia of a careful woman. Now it had edges. Now it had arithmetic in it.

Someone had read my ledgers without my seal.

I sat at my kitchen table with the lamp turned low and did what I always did when the world refused to make sense: I counted things. Three locks on my door. Two bolts. One window, painted shut since before I moved in. The knife under the loose brick by the hearth. The second knife in the bread box, because assassins rarely check the bread box. My hands were steady. They are always steady. That steadiness used to feel like an achievement. Tonight it felt like something someone else had put inside me.

Because here is the thing I could not stop turning over, the way you turn over a coin to check if both faces match: hemlock should not have frightened me.

Hemlock and I are old acquaintances. It came at me twice in my first year at the palace — once in a pheasant glaze, once in a honeyed custard that a disgruntled sous-chef thought no one would trace to him. Both times I felt it arrive, catalogued it, and sent it on its way, because that is what my body does. That is what my body *is*. A ledger of poisons, every page balanced. Conium maculatum: known, entered, dismissed.

But the poison in the prince's soup had not been alone. Something older had been folded into it like a letter slipped into an envelope, something with a signature I recognized the way you recognize handwriting on an envelope you yourself addressed. And I knew — I knew with the cold certainty of the archivist's instinct — exactly which page of which ledger that signature lived on. Page forty-one of the black-bound volume, third shelf from the left, behind the bottled sunsets. I had recorded it myself, four years ago, transcribing a guild acquisition: a poison recovered from a country that no longer existed. Vellmark. I had written its properties in my own hand. I had written, in the margin, in my own shorthand: *no antidote known; no immunity possible.*

And then I had tasted it in the crown prince's dinner.

No one should have been able to use that page. The black-bound volumes don't leave the cellar. The cellar doesn't open for anyone but me and Corvane. Which meant either the guild had sold it — which meant Corvane had sold it — or someone had walked into my archive through a wall I didn't know was a door.

Either way, the message was the same: the order I had built my life around, the beautiful balanced books, the comfort of knowing where everything was kept —

someone else had been keeping count of me.

* * *

The guildhouse doesn't have hours, exactly. It has moods. In the morning it smells of ink and cold stone; at dusk the stairwells fill with the ghost of whatever was last traded down them, so that you might climb past a staircase that remembers being a river, or a landing that remembers a wedding. I came up from the cellar archives near dawn with two of the black-bound volumes under my arm, and the house smelled of rain and hot metal, and I knew before I reached Corvane's study that he had been awake all night too.

He was standing at his window with his back to the door, watching the city go grey. Guildmaster Corvane is not an imposing man. He is small and neat and silvering at the temples, with the kind of face that makes strangers tell him their troubles, and he has spent thirty years making sure that face works on everyone including himself. When I set the volumes on his desk he didn't turn around.

"You found it," he said.

"I found page forty-one missing from the working copy." I laid the true black-bound volume beside it. "The master copy is intact. So whoever took the recipe copied it out by hand, which means they needed time, light, and privacy in my cellar." I let that sit. "My cellar, Corvane."

"Your cellar," he agreed mildly, and finally turned. "Which raises an interesting question of ownership, doesn't it? Because the ledgers are yours. The archive is yours. But the inventory—" He tapped the desk once, gently, like a man tapping a melon to test its ripeness. "The inventory was never yours, Oscar."

"The inventory is what I keep."

"It's what you *kept*." He came around the desk and sat, folding his small neat hands. "Sit down."

I didn't. Nine years of standing at a prince's shoulder while he eats has made sitting optional for me.

Corvane sighed the sigh of a patient man about to be patient. "Do you remember your hiring?"

"Of course."

"Describe it."

"A woman collapsed outside the palace gates during the autumn tasting trials. Poisoned — foxglove, badly administered. I carried her water, held her head. When she woke she looked at me and said I had the right kind of tongue. You interviewed me three days later in a room above a chandler's shop. You gave me wine with arsenic in it and watched whether my mouth blistered." I paused. "It didn't."

"No," Corvane said softly. "It didn't. Do you know why?"

Something in the room changed temperature. The guildhouses do that — they are old buildings holding impossible things, and sometimes the impossible leaks into the plaster. But this felt less like the house and more like the floor dropping out from under a very long fall I'd been having all night.

"Because I'm rare," I said. "You told me. One in ten thousand. A natural tolerance."

"I told you what was useful." Corvane opened a drawer — his drawers always open smoothly; everything Corvane owns opens smoothly — and drew out a slim folio tied with green string. Green, in guild accounting, means *encumbered*: an asset spoken for. "Oscar Voss. Age nineteen. Foxglove poisoning, self-administered—"

"That's a lie. She was poisoned by her employer."

"—self-administered, per the intake record," Corvane went on, unbothered, "presenting the rarest thing this guild ever handles: a body that survives poison naturally. Except it wasn't natural, Oscar. It was purchased. By us. Six years earlier."

The lamp flame leaned sideways though there was no draft.

"We found her in a border town where we were buying up memories ahead of a treaty renegotiation. Dying woman, no family, one asset worth having. She couldn't sell us her tolerance outright — tolerance isn't portable, it lives in the blood, it can't be decanted. But it can be *transferred over time*. Dish by dish. Year by year. A slow siphon." He untied the green string with two fingers. "So we bought her future instead. Her tolerance, accruing to us, payable to whoever we designated. She signed because dying people sign anything. We designated you."

The words went into me one at a time, like courses at a tasting. Each one distinct. Each one worse than the last.

"Transferred how?" My voice came out flat. Taster's voice. Serving voice.

"The same way all our transfers work. Exposure. Every meal you've eaten these nine years — every trial dish, every deliberate dose, every 'accidental' contamination we arranged and you absorbed — wasn't testing you, Oscar. It was *building* you. We weren't finding out what your body could survive. We were teaching it. Borrowing her resistance, plate by plate, and installing it in yours." He slid the folio across the desk. "You're not one in ten thousand. You're an account. And accounts accrue interest."

I looked down at the folio. Intake signature: a shaking hand, the letters leaning like drunks. *All that is in me that resists, to the Guild of Impossible Things, against future consideration.* Below it, the designation line, added later in Corvane's tidy script: *Payable to: O. VOSS.*

Nine years. Every dish. Every careful, professional swallow in front of the prince, every night I'd gone home and logged what my body had survived, proud of myself, proud of my rare and faithful tongue—

"You built me," I said. "Like a vault. Like a strongroom. You put the gold in me and waited for the day you'd need to move it."

Corvane had the grace not to smile, which is more than most traders manage. "I prefer 'reserve.' A reserve is a responsible thing. Every institution keeps one. And the guild has come to a moment when the reserve must be drawn."

"There it is." I heard my own laugh, and hated it — high, thin, nothing like mine. "There's the collection notice. Four years I've balanced your books, Corvane. Four years I've asked you, twice, what happens when the guild decides to collect on me, and twice you told me immunity was a gift. *A gift*, you said. Given freely."

"And I believed it, when I said it." For a moment — just a moment — something tired surfaced in his face, some old man underneath the trader, drowning fast. Then it was gone, and the accountancy returned. "But the child changed the arithmetic. Ashka. You've read the ledgers; you know what she is. A living index of Vellmark. Every door, every key, every sealed name of that country, walking around in an eight-year-old girl. There are buyers who would give the guild its next hundred years for access to her. The crown among them — though the crown wants her erased, and there are others who want her *opened*." His fingers steepled. "Opening her requires keys we do not hold. The doors of Vellmark don't answer to gold or force. They answer to payment in kind. And the only currency those doors accept—"

"Is immunity," I said.

"Is a body that has survived everything," Corvane said. "Poison is only the beginning of what Vellmark sealed its doors against. Their scholars were thorough. Their thresholds are lethal in ways we haven't catalogued. But a woman whose blood has been built, decade by decade, into a fortress—" he spread his hands, apologetic, reasonable, monstrous, "—a woman like that might walk through doors no one has opened in two hundred years. Your tolerance isn't just an asset, Oscar. It's a skeleton key. And the guild intends to spend it."

"Spend it *how*?" I said. "Say the words. Say what collection looks like. Because I've seen what happens to assets the guild draws down. I've recorded it often enough."

Corvane met my eyes, and I will say this for him: he didn't flinch, and he didn't lie.

"Tolerance can be transferred out as well as in," he said. "Dish by dish. Threshold by threshold. Each door Ashka needs opened takes payment, and each payment takes a measure of what's in your blood and puts it through the lock instead. You'll survive the first few — probably. The middle ones will hurt. And somewhere past the halfway mark you'll be what the dying woman was when we found her: a body with nothing left in it that resists, standing in a world full of poisons." He folded his hands. "We'll protect you as far as we're able. You have my word."

"Your word." I picked up the folio with the dead woman's drunken signature on it. "Here's her word, too. Look how much it protected her."

"Oscar—"

"How long?" I cut him off, because if he said my name gently one more time I was going to put the folio through his teeth. "How long have you known you'd collect? Since the prince was poisoned? Since the child arrived?"

Since longer than that. I saw the answer before he chose to give it, in the way his eyes went briefly to the window, to the grey city, to anything but me. He had known for years. Possibly since the day he hired me. Possibly since the day he signed the designation line — *payable to O. Voss* — and understood that he was not giving me a gift. He was addressing an envelope.

"The girl is in the vault," Corvane said. "For her protection, as ordered. You'll continue as her keeper — you're good with her, and she trusts you, and trust is also an asset. The first threshold is a week away. Rest while you can."

I gathered the black-bound volumes. My hands were steady. They are always steady; I know now that the steadiness itself was installed, poured into me one arsenic-laced cup at a time by a dying stranger's borrowed courage, and even that was never mine.

At the door I stopped. Not to plead. To ask the archivist's question, the one I ask every ledger, every entry, every balance:

"Her name. The woman. The one who actually paid."

Corvane looked genuinely surprised that I wanted it. Then he told me. Sela Voss. No relation — Voss was common as rain in the border towns. Or maybe relation; the guild's records wouldn't say, because the guild had never thought to ask.

Sela Voss. I carried the name upstairs through the smelling-of-rain-and-hot-metal corridors, past a staircase remembering a river, and stood a long moment outside the vault door where Ashka slept, listening to the small sounds of a living index dreaming in a language older than kingdoms.

Then I did the only thing a ledger-keeper does with a fraudulent entry.

I began, silently, methodically, to plan how to strike it from the book.

* * *

They came for the ledgers at noon.

Not all of them — just the black-bound volumes, the poison acquisitions, carried out of my cellar by two clerks with a signed requisition in Corvane's hand: *for consolidation prior to threshold negotiations.* Perfectly lawful, within guild law. Perfectly theft, within any other kind. I stood aside and watched my nine years of careful work carried up the stairs on a tray like dishes from a table, and I smiled at the clerks, and thanked them, and noted — the way I note everything, the way I was built to note everything — which clerk pocketed the requisition rather than filing it, and which one glanced back at the cellar door twice, as if he already regretted what he was part of.

Everyone in this house is an asset. Corvane taught me that this morning. He shouldn't be surprised when I start keeping his books by his own rules.

In the vault, Ashka was awake, sitting cross-legged on the cot they'd given her, drawing characters in the dust on the floor with one finger. The pre-kingdom script. She looked up when I came in, and her face did what it always does when it sees me — brightened, quick and total, the way faces only do when they haven't yet learned what adults do to each other.

"You look sad," she said, in her strange half-accented court-tongue.

"I'm balancing an account," I said.

She considered that with the seriousness of a child who is herself an entry in a ledger she never agreed to. Then she held out the finger she'd been writing with, showing me the character traced in dust: a shape like a door, like a keyhole, like a mouth about to speak.

"This one costs," she warned me.

Everything costs, little index. I sat down beside her in the dust, and I began to learn what I would have to pay.
