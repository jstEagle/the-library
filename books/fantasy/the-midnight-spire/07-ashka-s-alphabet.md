## 7. Ashka's Alphabet

The vault smelled of cold iron and older paper.

Oscar had kept ledgers in that room for nine years — she knew every shelf by its creak, every drawer by the resistance of its wax seal. But tonight the vault was different. Tonight Corvane had locked them both inside it, turned the key twice, and told Ashka to teach.

"Protection," he'd said, with a face that believed itself.

Oscar sat on an overturned crate across from the girl, a candle between them, a blank ledger open on her knees. The good paper. The kind she saved for accounts she never wanted anyone else to read.

"All right," she said. "Teach me your alphabet."

Ashka studied her the way Oscar had seen merchants study coin they suspected was clipped. "You understand what it costs."

"I understand you've said that three times."

"Because you keep not listening." The girl folded her hands in her lap like a small, patient magistrate. "The letters aren't marks. They're the country. Every character is a piece of Vellmark wearing ink so it can survive. When I write one, nothing happens — I'm already made of it. When *you* write one, the letter needs somewhere to live. It takes room from you."

"Room from me how?"

"You'll see." Ashka dipped the quill and drew a single character on the crate lid between them: a shape like a door leaning against a wall, with a small hook at its base. "This is *vel*. It means home, but only the home you leave. Not the house. The leaving."

Oscar looked at it. It looked back, which should have been her first warning.

"And if I write it?"

"Then you'll know what it means. Truly know. And knowing costs."

* * *

**Ashka**

The woman wrote the character wrong twice, then right.

Ashka watched her do it — tongue pressed to teeth, the way scholars did, as if the hand needed the mouth's permission. And when the stroke finally closed, she saw the cost arrive.

It came quietly. That was always the worst part. No scream, no flinch. Just the woman's eyes going somewhere else for a moment, and coming back emptier by exactly one thing.

"What did you lose?" Ashka asked. She hated asking. She asked anyway, because Mother had taught her: *the price must be named or it isn't paid, it's stolen.*

Oscar blinked slowly. "A kitchen," she said. "There was — there was a kitchen, I think. Someone singing. I can't—" She stopped. Tested the edges of the absence the way a tongue tests a missing tooth. "My mother sang while she cooked. I don't remember the song anymore. Or the kitchen. Or—" A pause, and something behind her face rearranged itself around the hole. "I remember she existed. I just can't see her anywhere."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be exact." Oscar straightened. "How many characters are in this alphabet of yours?"

"Thirty-one."

"How many words does Vellmark need to exist again?"

Ashka looked down at her hands. "All of them."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to shield it.

"Then we'd better be efficient," Oscar said, and reached for the quill.

* * *

**Oscar**

By the third hour, Oscar had learned seven characters and lost seven rooms of her childhood.

She built a list in what remained of herself, the way she balanced any account: *vel*, home-left-behind. *Sarn*, the sound rain makes on stone roofs. *Ith*, bread shared with a stranger. Each one written, each one known, each one paid for.

The market where her father sold his fishhooks — gone. She could feel the gap where it had been, smooth-sided, like a socket. The smell of tallow candles in winter — gone, though she still knew intellectually that tallow smelled of something, and that once she'd known what. Her brother's laugh. She had a brother. She was almost certain she had a brother. His name started with a sound she could no longer make.

"That one," Ashka said gently, when Oscar's pen hovered over the eighth character. "*Dren*. It means debt that love forgives. You don't have to—"

"Write it."

"It's your brother, isn't it. Whatever you're trading for it."

Oscar set the nib down. "Every letter buys a word that keeps a dead country alive. My childhood against theirs. That's arithmetic even Corvane would respect."

"No." For the first time all night, Ashka sounded angry — a small, fierce, eight-year-old fury. "That's *his* arithmetic. He counts people because counting makes spending them feel clean. I'm not asking you to spend yourself. I'm telling you what things cost so you can *choose*. Those are different trades. If you can't tell them apart, you're already his."

Oscar put the pen down.

They sat in silence. Somewhere above them, the guildhouse settled and groaned, beams contracting in the cold, and Oscar thought: *I used to know why that sound comforted me.*

"Ashka. Why didn't it cost you? When you write?"

"Because I'm not writing." The girl uncurled her fingers, palm up, and Oscar saw — really saw, for the first time — the faint lines running through her skin like watermarks in paper. Characters. Hundreds of them, fine as veins. "The scholars couldn't save the books. The crown was buying up every archive, every memory-broker, every guild that touched remembering. So my mother and the others did the only trade left. They wrote the whole country into a person who hadn't finished becoming anyone yet." She closed her fist. "I volunteered. Remember that, whatever you learn about me. Nobody made me."

"I believe you."

"Do you? Your guild helped erase us. Your ledgers recorded the payment."

Oscar felt the old sick lurch, the one from the night she'd found her own handwriting on the erasure contracts. "Yes. And I keep those ledgers. Which means some part of me has been carrying your country around for years without knowing it." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Maybe that's why the letters take my memories so easily. Maybe I owe Vellmark from the start."

Ashka considered this with terrible seriousness. "Debts transfer," she said. "That's *dren*. Write it."

This time, Oscar wrote it.

And this time, watching the cost come, she braced for another room of her childhood to empty out —

— but what left instead was smaller, stranger: the taste of the prince's poisoned soup. Not the fear of it. Just the flavor itself, hemlock threaded with that older, darker note. She reached for the memory of it and found the shelf bare, a card in its place reading, in her own bookkeeper's hand: *paid to dren.*

"The letters choose," Ashka said quietly, watching her face. "I told you. They're not marks. They take what fits."

"Why would Vellmark want my memory of a poison?"

"Because," Ashka said, and leaned closer, and lowered her voice as if the vault walls had ears, which, Oscar reflected, they might, "your poison wasn't ours. That taste you lost — the old note underneath the hemlock — I know it. It's in my bones. It's *sennet*. The burning-word. The thing they used to erase the records of us." Her eyes were very wide and very old. "Someone is poisoning your prince with the weapon that killed my country. And now the only person who remembers what it tastes like is standing in this room holding a quill."

The candle went out.

Neither of them had moved.

In the dark, Oscar heard Ashka breathing, quick and shallow, and heard — beneath it, beneath everything — the faintest scrape of a key turning in the vault door.

Not unlocking. Testing.

Someone was checking the lock from the outside.

* * *

**Ashka**

Ashka counted the footsteps the way Mother had taught her: heel first, weight forward, a man in a hurry pretending not to be.

Two sets. One heavy, pausing longest at the door. One light, staying back, near the stairs.

"They're listening," she whispered.

"I know."

"Corvane doesn't test his own locks. He trusts iron more than he trusts people."

"I know that too." In the dark, Oscar's voice had changed — lower, stripped of the warmth she wore like a servant's uniform. This was the other Oscar, the ledger-keeper, the one who lived half her life in cellars. "Which means he suspects someone else wants in. Or wants us out."

Ashka pulled her knees to her chest. Around her, under her skin, thirty-one characters and ten thousand words slept, waiting. Sometimes at night she could feel them shift, restless, homesick. *We are here,* they murmured. *We are here. Say us.*

"Oscar," she whispered. "The last character I was going to teach you tonight. Before the candle died."

"Mm?"

"*Hal.*" Ashka traced it on her own knee, feeling it warm under her fingertip. "It means witness. The one who sees and tells. It's the most dangerous word we had. The scholars argued about whether to include it in the index at all, because a witness can't be bought, only silenced — and silencing a witness is expensive. Empires hate witnesses."

"And you want me to write it."

"I want you to decide to write it. Knowing what it will take." Ashka reached out and found the woman's hand in the dark — found it scarred, steady, colder than it should be. "Whatever's left of your childhood after tonight. *Hal* will ask for the best part of it. Witnesses pay the most."

Outside the door, the footsteps withdrew, unhurried now, satisfied. Up the stairs. Gone.

Oscar's hand turned over and squeezed hers, brief and hard.

"In the morning," Oscar said. "Light the candle. Bring the ledger. And Ashka — if I start forgetting you, if I look at you like a stranger — you tell me who you are. Every time. However many times it takes. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Good." A breath. "Because tomorrow we start spelling out the truth, and I intend to be alive to read it."

From far below the vault — deeper than the vault, deeper than anything the guild admitted to owning — came a sound like a held breath finally beginning to end.

A fire, somewhere, learning how to die.
