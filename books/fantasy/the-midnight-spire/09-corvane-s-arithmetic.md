## 9. Corvane's Arithmetic

Corvane had always believed that the worst conversations were best held over food.

It was a habit from his father — a customs clerk in a river town that no longer appeared on maps, a man who could tell you exactly what a smuggled cargo cost in bribes, in blood, and in the slow rot of a town that learned to look away. His father had served the difficult truths at dinner, portioned out between courses, so that by the time the plates were cleared there was nothing left to do but nod and carry on. *The world runs on arithmetic, boy,* his father used to say, sharpening the knife for the next day's meat. *And most of it is division.*

So Corvane set the table himself. Two chairs. A white cloth gone soft with age. A roast fowl from a bird that had never existed anywhere but in a bottled summer the guild had sold three times over, which meant it tasted of someone's remembered childhood and was therefore better than any honest chicken could hope to be. Bread still warm. Wine the color of garnets. And beside Oscar's plate, where a napkin might have gone, he placed her own ledger — the oldest one, the Vellmark index, opened to the page she had spent four days deciphering while his hunters combed the ash-fields for a runaway taster and an eight-year-old country.

He did not lock the door. That was part of the meal.

* * *

She came down the cellar stairs at the hour he'd specified, because of course she did. That was the thing about Oscar Voss that Corvane had understood from the very first week, eleven years ago, when he'd found her in the palace kitchens writing poison-signatures in a cipher of her own invention: she came when called. Not out of obedience. Out of bookkeeping. She kept accounts even with the people who wronged her, and you could not close an account by hiding from it.

She stood in the doorway a long moment, taking inventory — as he'd known she would. The two settings. The unlocked door. The ledger lying open like an accusation neither of them had made yet.

"You knew I'd come back through the first door," she said.

"I paid for that door. It opens for what I tell it to open for." He gestured to the chair across from him. "Sit. Eat. You've been living on stolen bread and fear for four days, and fear ruins the palate."

She sat, but didn't touch the food. Her hands stayed flat on the table, and he watched her read the room the way she read a dish — looking for what was hidden underneath. She'd gotten thinner since the vault. There was a new scar along her jaw he hadn't seen before, and her left hand trembled slightly until she pressed it flat. The alphabet was costing her. Good. That meant Ashka was teaching her, which meant the child trusted her, which meant everything Corvane needed was still within reach.

"You've fed me poisoned meals before," she said. "Is this one?"

"No."

"That's almost disappointing."

"It would be wasted on you now anyway." He poured the wine. "Half your tolerances are already spent, aren't they? I can see it in how you hold your hands. The guild's ledgers say you paid out ironweed tolerance at the threshold of the first door. And something else. Something older." He let that sit between them like a third guest. "You're paying passage in the only coin we ever truly accepted from you, Oscar. I taught the guild that trade, so believe me when I say I know its price better than anyone alive."

"Then why am I here?"

"Because you're an accountant," Corvane said. "And accountants deserve to see the full column before they sign."

* * *

He talked while she watched, and he did not soften any of it.

"The treaty structure of the northern kingdoms rests on eleven documents," he began. "You know some of them. The Concord at Greywater. The Salt Oaths. The Dowry of Ashes, which is my personal favorite — a treaty named for what everyone agreed to pretend was just weather." He turned his glass slowly by the stem. "Every one of those documents contains clauses that only work if Vellmark does not exist. Border settlements calibrated against a frontier that officially belongs to no one. Reparations distributed among survivors who officially number fewer than forty. A shared crown treasury whose founding capital was, in fact, seized from a nation we erased. Pull one thread — one clause, one name spoken aloud in the right court — and the thread pulls another, and another, until the whole northern web comes apart."

"And if it comes apart?" Oscar said quietly. "People remember what they lost. Kingdoms renegotiate. It's happened before—"

"It has happened before," Corvane said, "exactly twice, and both times it took a generation of war to settle the new arithmetic." He leaned forward. "I have read those wars, Oscar. Not the songs about them — the grain requisitions. The plague ledgers that follow armies. The orphanage rolls. When the Salt Oaths nearly unraveled sixty years ago, forty thousand people died in eighteen months, and not one of them was a king or a guildmaster. They were farmers whose fields became roads. Fishermen whose harbors became anchorages for troop ships. Children who died of things children stop dying of when the granaries are full and the borders are quiet." He spread his hands. "Vellmark's restoration doesn't resurrect forty thousand dead Vellmarkers, Oscar. It just adds a new forty thousand to the pile — different names, same ledgers. The dead country stays dead either way. The only question is whether the living join it."

Oscar was silent for a long moment. Then: "You rehearsed this."

"I have given this speech before," Corvane admitted. "To kings. To inquisitors. To myself, more than once, at hours of the night when the arithmetic wouldn't sit still." He smiled, and it cost him something to make it reach his eyes. "It gets easier. That's not a confession; it's a warning. Everything gets easier. That's how you know the trade is working."

"And the child?"

"Is an index. A library with legs." He said it flatly, deliberately, watching her flinch. "I know you've started thinking of her as something else. Four days in a borrowed inn will do that — you learn how she takes her tea, you braid her hair, you start believing you rescued her instead of acquiring her. But she was made into an index before you met her, Oscar. Made by her own people, by scholars who chose her, who wrote their whole nation into her bones because they wanted it to survive. Do you know what that makes her? It makes her the single most valuable object on this continent. And objects in circulation keep people safe. Objects locked in one pair of hands start wars."

"She's not an object."

"No," Corvane said softly. "But she can be traded like one, and that distinction has never once stopped a transaction in the history of the world. Ask me how I know. Ask my father, who sold the town he lived in, parcel by parcel, and died wealthy and hated and correct."

* * *

She ate then. He'd expected that too — hunger wins arguments eventually — and he watched her take the first bite of the impossible fowl and saw her face change as it landed. Somewhere behind her eyes, a memory that wasn't hers surfaced: a kitchen with copper pots, a woman singing off-key, bread burned at one end. Someone's childhood, sold three times, ending up on a traitor's table in a cellar beneath a city that had helped erase a nation.

"That's the other thing I want you to understand," Corvane said gently. "Everything we trade does this. Every memory we broker, every vanished place we keep in a bottle, every ember of dragon-fire we ration out — all of it ends up making someone's life gentler. A widow buys back one afternoon with her husband. A king buys the courage to sign a peace he'd otherwise refuse. A frightened girl on a cold road eats a meal that tastes like being loved." He turned his palm up toward her plate. "This is what the guild actually is, Oscar. We are the reason the world's losses don't all land at once. We spread the grief out. Thin. Manageable. Priced. And yes—" he raised a hand before she could speak, "—yes, we profited from Vellmark's erasure. The guild took the commission. I signed the receipt. My signature is on the instrument that unmade a country, and I have never once pretended otherwise, not even to myself, not even at three in the morning with the ledgers balanced and the cellar dark."

Oscar looked up sharply. In eleven years she had never heard him say that aloud.

"But here is the question I need you to answer honestly, because you're the only person I trust to do the sums without flinching." He pulled a paper from inside his coat — a single sheet, dense with figures in his own hand — and laid it beside her ledger. "If Vellmark comes back, count the wars. Count the treaties that collapse. Count the crowns that fall and the succession crises that follow and the famine years that follow those. Then count what comes back: one small nation of ghosts, restored to a continent that has rearranged itself around its absence. One nation, Oscar. Against the stability of eleven kingdoms and the lives of however many hundreds of thousands live ordinary Tuesdays inside that stability." He tapped the sheet once. "Do the division. Tell me what you get."

"I get a number," Oscar said. Her voice was very steady, and that steadiness told him more than shouting would have. "I get the same number you get. I'm good at arithmetic, Corvane — you trained me. And the number says leave the country dead." She pushed the sheet back across the table. "But numbers are what we taste the poison in. Somebody has to be the taster. Somebody has to swallow it and say *this is wrong*, even when it looks like dinner, even when it smells like mercy, even when the king himself seasoned it. That was my job at the palace and it's my job now. Your arithmetic isn't false. It's just laced. And I know what laced things look like, because I have been tasting them since I was nineteen years old."

Corvane sat back. For a moment — a real moment, one he would keep in a private ledger of his own — he felt something very close to pride, and very close to grief, and could not have told you which was larger.

"You sound like her," he said. "Like the woman who wrote the first entry in the first ledger this guild ever kept. Before me. Before any of us decided that grief was a commodity." He drained his wine. "I hoped you'd say something else tonight. I genuinely did. Because the alternative costs me things I had planned to keep."

"The alternative being?"

Corvane rose, crossed to the cellar door, and opened it wider — not to let her out, but to admit the man waiting beyond it. Maren stepped in, wet to the shoulders from the storm above, and would not meet Oscar's eyes, and that was when she understood the shape of the evening at last: the meal had been the negotiation, and the negotiation was over.

"Halvard knows where you're keeping the girl," Corvane said quietly. "She'll be at the inn by dawn. I can't stop her — she outranks our arrangement now, and besides, I'm not certain I should. So here is the last thing I will offer you freely, Oscar Voss, because you earned it before you betrayed it: walk out that door, go to the palace, turn yourself in, and give Halvard the child and the ledgers. She'll bury your guild-work like she promised, you'll die old in a kitchen somewhere, and the world keeps its Tuesday." He held her gaze. "Or run. Keep running. Pay doors with pieces of yourself until there's nothing left to pay with. But understand that from this moment forward, every door that opens for you opens onto my loss, and I collect my debts, Oscar. Ask anyone. Ask my father."

She stood. She picked up her ledger, closed it, and tucked it under her arm like a shield.

"For what it's worth," she said at the door, "your roast was excellent. Whoever's childhood that was, they were loved."

Then she was gone up the stairs, and Maren shifted his weight and said, "Guildmaster — she'll run," and Corvane looked down at the untouched second setting, at the wine going bitter in the glass, and thought of his father's knife whispering against the whetstone, and answered:

"Yes. And now we learn what she spends."
