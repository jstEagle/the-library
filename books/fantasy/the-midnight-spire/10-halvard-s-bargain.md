## 10. Halvard's Bargain

Dame Halvard had interrogated forty-one people in her career, and she had learned long ago that the truth never lived in their answers. It lived in what they did with their hands while answering.

The taster — Voss, the records said, Oscar Voss, entered into palace service eleven years ago, commendations for discretion, one reprimand for hoarding — was doing something interesting with her hands. She kept them flat on the table, palms down, like a woman pinning a map against wind. Like a woman holding a country still.

"You were seen," Halvard said, "leaving the guildhouse on Ropemaker's Row. Night of the storm. Carrying a child."

"I carry deliveries for my cousin. A fishmonger."

"Your cousin has no license to trade."

"He has no license to be called my cousin, either, and yet."

Halvard let the silence do its work. She was good at silence. It was the only weapon she owned that never needed sharpening. The interview room in the White Tower was built for it — stone that drank sound, a single high window, a table bolted so neither party could turn it into anything but a table.

Voss did not fidget. That was the first mark against her. Innocent people fidgeted; guilt sat still and counted its exits. But Voss wasn't counting exits either. Her eyes had gone somewhere else entirely — somewhere Halvard couldn't follow — and come back.

"The child is sick," Voss said at last. "She needs a physician, not a tribunal."

"The child is a document."

Something moved behind Voss's face, quick as a fish under ice. *So you know,* the movement said. *You know exactly what she is.*

Halvard had read the treaty. All three copies of it, including the one in the crown vault that officially did not exist, because it had been burned in a fire that officially happened, sealed by a flame that officially was weather. The Treaty of Quiet Accord, Year 214 of the Founding. Two pages of trade concessions and border adjustments, and between them, folded in like a knife in bread: *the nation of Vellmark, having ceased by consent of all parties to exist, shall not be named, mapped, mourned, or remembered.*

*By consent of all parties.* She had signed it herself once — or her predecessor had, with her seal, before her time, when she was a girl of six living in a fishing town whose name she could never quite hold onto. There was a gap in her childhood the shape of a coastline. She had stopped asking about gaps years ago. Asking about gaps got people reassigned. Reassigned people did not get to keep their rank, their rooms, or their memories of why they'd asked.

"Here is what happens now," Halvard said, and laid it out plainly, because she believed in plainness the way other women believed in saints. "You will be detained pending inquiry. Your quarters searched. Your correspondence read. If the child is found in your keeping, you'll be charged with theft of crown-adjacent property — and yes, before you object, she *is* property, legally speaking. The guild registered her as an asset nine days ago. Signed by Corvane himself." She watched the word land. "You didn't know your own guildmaster sold her to us."

Voss's palms pressed flatter against the table. "No."

"No, you didn't know, or no, you won't accept it?"

"Both."

*There it is.* Halvard stood, gathered her papers, and paused at the door — not turning back, just pausing, letting the pause be an offer.

"Corvane wants the girl delivered quietly and the ledgers buried where they've always been buried. I want the same thing, Voss, but I'm the only one in this building offering to bury them *with you alive.* Think about which of us is actually on your side."

She left the room and did not look at Voss's face, because she had already seen everything in it she needed, and because there was a smaller thing in it she did not want to see — a flicker of pity, aimed at her, from the woman across the table. As if Voss knew something about Dame Halvard that Dame Halvard had paid good money not to remember.

In the corridor, her adjutant fell into step. "The cell?"

"Keep her comfortable. And send word to Ropemaker's Row — if a child passes through those doors again, I want her taken gently and brought to me. Not to Corvane. To *me.*"

"And if the guild objects?"

"The guild," Halvard said, "serves the crown."

The adjutant wrote it down. Halvard almost laughed. Almost. Somewhere beneath her breastbone, in the place where the gap in her childhood ached on cold nights like an old break, something stirred and turned over and refused to settle.

*The nation of Vellmark, having ceased by consent of all parties to exist.*

She had been six years old. Six-year-olds did not consent to anything except bedtime.

She walked faster, and did not ask herself why.

* * *

They gave Oscar a cell with a window, which meant they wanted something from her. Cells without windows were for people who'd already told you everything.

She sat on the cot and took inventory, the way she took inventory every night of her life. Body: intact. Tongue: hers, still carrying eleven years of purchased immunities — nightshade, arsenic, salts of lead, four varieties of serpent venom, and the older things with no common names, the ones she'd recorded in ledger seven and never spoken aloud. Freedom: none. Options:

One. Give them Ashka. Deliver the child to Halvard, hand over the ledgers, walk away clean, spend the rest of her life tasting princes' dinners and never sleeping well again. The guild would call it prudence. Ashka would call it — no. Ashka wouldn't call it anything. Children who were erased countries didn't get to be bitter; they got to be *filed.*

Two. Refuse. And then what? Rot here while Corvane negotiated her body away dish by dish, trading her tolerances to whoever paid for keys to doors that no longer existed. She had seen the entry in her own ledger, in handwriting almost like hers: *Asset VOSS-O. Collateralized. Tolerance schedule attached.* Eleven years of dinners, each one a payment she hadn't known she was making, each immunity a lien against a debt she'd never signed for.

Three.

Three was the window.

It was narrow — a slit, really, more arrow-loop than window, cut for archers in some earlier century when the Tower expected wars instead of paperwork. A slim person could get a shoulder through it. A slim person who had spent eleven years swallowing poisons and learning, dish by dish, the exact weight of what a body could survive — such a person knew things about edges and squeezes and the arithmetic of nearly-fatal that no climber's manual contained.

Oscar also knew the guards' rotation, because she'd tasted for the garrison commander for six years and he talked with his mouth full. She knew the wall-walk changed hands at the second bell. She knew the ivy on the north face was real ivy, planted by a sentimental mason, and that sentimental masons planted things that held weight.

And she knew — this was the part that made her laugh, alone, in a cell, at midnight — that the drain grate in the corner of the yard below was guildwork. Old guildwork, pre-Corvane, a borrowed space stitched into the city's guts decades ago: a grate that opened onto a stairwell that existed on no surveyor's map because it existed on *three*, disagreeing with each other. Maren had shown her once, drunk on festival wine, swearing her to secrecy. *Emergency egress,* he'd called it. *For when the books need to run.*

The books were running.

She went through the window at the second bell, when the wall-walk changed hands and the new sentry was always thirty seconds late because old Wenna stopped to argue with the gate-sergeant about dice. The ivy held. Her palms bled. She hung above the yard for a moment, arms shaking, looking up at the lit window of the interview room where Halvard had offered her a clean life at the price of a child, and she thought: *she meant it, too. That's the horror. She'd have kept her word.*

That was the worst kind of bargain — the kind that was sincere.

The grate gave with a grunt of old hinges that had waited twenty years for this. Below it, stairs went down into dark that smelled of river and candlewax, and Oscar dropped through, pulled the grate shut above her, and stood in the guild's oldest secret with blood on her hands and her heart going like a drum at a hanging.

She should have felt free. She felt *hunted* — properly hunted, finally, no more pretending otherwise. By morning every inquisitor, every guild hunter, every man Corvane could buy would have her face. She had traded a salary for a price on her head, a warm bed for a smuggler's warren, and the only thing she'd bought with all of it was a promise she'd made to an eight-year-old in a cellar full of impossible accounts: *I will not let them file you.*

Down the disagreeing stairwell, then, taking the middle version of the steps because it was the one that smelled least of floodwater. At the bottom, scratched into the plaster where only runners would look, someone had left a message in chalk — fresh, days old, in Maren's cramped hand:

*Vault 3 emptied. Girl gone before they came. R. knows where. Trust the drain, not the door. — M*

Oscar read it twice. *Girl gone before they came.* Ashka had run — or been moved — ahead of the inquisitors. And *R. knows where.* R. Rosethorn, the archivist, ancient, half-blind, loyal to nothing but catalogues. Or — her stomach tightened — *R* could be anyone. Chalk messages in escape tunnels were how traps introduced themselves.

But Maren had signed it. Maren, who owed her for the winter he'd nearly died of gray fever and she'd sat up with him for nine nights running broth past his lips, testing every spoonful before he swallowed, because even friends' kitchens weren't safe in their line of work. He wouldn't sign a lie. He might sign a mistake, but not a lie.

She pressed her bleeding palm flat against the chalk, an oath without words, and started walking.

Behind her, far above, a bell began to ring — not the second bell, not any honest hour. The alarm. They'd found the empty cell, the bent bars of ivy, the grate.

By dawn, Oscar Voss would be a fugitive with a bounty and no guild, no crown, no name worth saying aloud. Ahead of her lay the dark of the borrowed stairwell, an archivist who might or might not still be trusted, and a missing child who was the last living memory of a murdered country.

Oscar put her shoulder to the darkness and went to find her.

Somewhere in the walls, faint as a coin dropped in another room, she heard footsteps matching hers — patient, unhurried, one flight over, keeping pace.

Not hunters, she told herself. Guild spaces didn't echo.

Guild spaces *remembered.*
