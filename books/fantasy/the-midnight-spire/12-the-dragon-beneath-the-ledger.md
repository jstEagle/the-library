## 12. The Dragon Beneath the Ledger

The staircase down did not want us.

I had walked the guild's borrowed spaces long enough to know their moods — the polite reluctance of a corridor asked to be somewhere else, the sulky resistance of a room that had been moved too often. But the stair beneath the counting-house was different. It descended the way a throat swallows: reluctantly, and with every intention of bringing us back up.

Ashka felt it too. Her small hand tightened around mine, and she said something in the old tongue — that language older than any kingdom, the one I could now read a little and speak not at all. I had learned enough in the vault above to catch the shape of it.

*"It remembers being burned,"* she'd said once, of a candle. Now she said it of the stairs.

"The fire's below," I said. "Not here."

She looked at me with that terrible patience children have, the patience of people who have not yet learned to lie to themselves. "It's all one fire," she said, in trade-tongue this time. "You know that. You keep the ledgers."

I kept the ledgers. That was the whole of my sin, if you wanted to be precise about it, and precision was the only comfort I had left. Somewhere behind us — above us, through walls that were also other walls — Corvane was gathering hunters, and Dame Halvard was gathering warrants, and both of them were gathering me. We had perhaps a night. Perhaps less. The borrowed rooms were closing behind us one by one as the guild sealed its network, and the only way out of Vellmark's ghost-country ran straight down through the guild's most guarded secret.

So we went down.

* * *

The last flight of stairs ended at a door made of ledger-covers.

I mean that literally. Someone — Corvane, or Corvane's predecessor, or some archivist with more devotion than sense — had bound hundreds of old account books flat and stitched them into a single surface, spines out, gilt titles facing inward like a wall of secrets turned to face itself. I recognized some of the bindings. Guild stock takes from before my time. Ledgers older than the current dynasty, maybe older than the crown it served.

Ashka put her palm flat against the spines and read them the way she read everything: quickly, hungrily, as if afraid the words would be taken back.

"This one is mine," she said softly. "This one is my mother's name. It's written on a spine." She looked up at me. "Why would her name be on a spine?"

"Because names are inventory," I said, and hated how easily it came. "That's what the guild does with them. It buys, it sells, it binds." I drew the key Corvane had given me three years ago, when he still trusted me, when trusting me had been part of the purchase. "The debt comes due tonight either way. Let's see what we bought."

The lock took the key with a sound like a swallowed word.

Beyond the door there was no room. There was a *hold* — a space dug out of the world's basement, so far down that the stone sweated warmth instead of cold, and so old that the architecture had stopped pretending to be human. The ceiling arched away into darkness. The floor was smooth glass-black rock, and across it, in patterns like frozen lightning, ran veins of gold that pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like breathing.

And in the center of it, in a cradle of iron and chain and prayer-marks hammered into the metal by hands that had hoped iron meant anything to what they caged —

The dragon-fire.

* * *

You have seen a hearth. You have seen a forge. You have perhaps seen a wildfire from a safe hillside, and thought yourself acquainted with flame.

Forget all of it.

What burned in that cradle was the size of a fist and it filled the entire hold the way a king fills a throne room — not by bulk but by presence. It did not flicker. Flickering is for fires that are uncertain. This fire *knew*. It burned a color I have no word for except to say it was the color of the moment just after you wake from a dream in which someone you loved was still alive — gold shot through with a red so deep it was nearly grief. And though it sat no bigger than a heart, the light of it touched every wall of that vast dark hold at once, evenly, the way truth touches a room.

The heat was not heat exactly. It was *attention*. Standing at the threshold I felt the way a document feels under a magistrate's eye.

"Oh," Ashka breathed. She stepped past me before I could catch her, walking across the veined black rock toward the cradle, and the veins of gold brightened under her bare feet, waking as she passed over them, one after another, a path lighting up like recognition.

"Ashka—" I caught her shoulder. "Slowly. Please."

"It knows me," she said. Not wonder now. Something rawer. "Oscar. It *knows* me. It's been waiting so long."

I should have understood then. I tell myself I should have. But I was looking at the chains.

They were beautiful, which is the guild's signature on anything. Silver links, each engraved with a clause. I leaned close and read them, because reading contracts is what I am for, and my stomach turned over with the slow deliberate roll of a ship taking water.

Every link was an oath of silence. Not a binding on the fire — a binding *through* it. Each chain-link held a sealed record, and the seal was the fire itself. I traced the engravings with a fingertip and read clause after clause, and my blood went colder with each one.

*Item: the Treaty of the Grey March, ratified in ash. Sealed.*

*Item: the census rolls of Vellmark, forty-one thousand names. Sealed.*

*Item: the confession of the signatories who sold the borders. Sealed.*

*Item: the map of Vellmark's capital as it stood on the morning of the burning. Sealed.*

*Item: the true text of Article Nine. Sealed.*

Article Nine. The clause everyone in the palace pretended not to remember, the clause Halvard's questions kept circling like a hawk over a hedgerow. I had transcribed a copy of its public version into the guild's ledgers years ago and noticed even then that the ink of the original had been burned out of the archive — not removed, *burned*, with a precision no lamp-oil achieves.

Now I stood in front of the lamp.

"That's what you are," I whispered to the fire. "You're not an asset. You're a *lock*."

"And locks rust," said Ashka.

* * *

We circled the cradle together, and I made myself look properly at what the guild called its master asset, and I saw what I had been refusing to see since the first vein of gold pulsed under Ashka's feet.

The fire was dying.

Not dramatically. Nothing about it was dramatic. It was simply *smaller* than the hold expected — the light reached the walls, but thinly now, the way an old man's voice reaches the back of a hall. The gold veins in the floor pulsed slower than they should have. Between one pulse and the next there was a gap, a small darkness, a held breath — and the gaps were getting longer. I timed six pulses against my heartbeat, the way I time everything, because timing is the taster's whole craft, and the intervals stretched like taffy.

"How long?" Ashka asked.

I did the arithmetic I did not want to do. "A day," I said. "Two, if it fights. It doesn't look like it's fighting."

"It isn't." She pressed both hands against the lowest chain, against the clause sealing the census rolls, and her voice cracked down the middle. "It's tired. It burned a whole country's name out of the world, do you understand? Every record, every map, every memory anyone agreed to sell. It did the work of forgetting for forty thousand people, because we *asked it to*." She looked at me, and in the dying gold light her face was very old and very eight years old at once. "We asked it to. My own scholars brought it here. They fed it our archives and said *make us forgettable so some of us might live*. And it has been holding all of that — every name, every border, every crime — inside itself ever since, alone, in the dark, so that no one would have to know what was done to us."

The hold was silent except for the pulse. Pulse. Gap. Pulse.

I thought of the cellar archive upstairs, of the neat rows of bottled sunsets and traded memories, of Corvane's voice explaining, patiently, always patiently, that the guild trades in stability. I had balanced those accounts for nine years. I had believed — I had *chosen* to believe — that the deepest vault held treasure.

It held the bill.

"If it dies," I said slowly, working it forward clause by clause the way you work a poison through a body, "the seals die with it. Everything it burned — every treaty, every confession, every name — comes back. All at once. Into every archive where the burn-marks sit waiting."

"Not just archives." Ashka's eyes were wide. "Into *people*. The scholars didn't only feed it paper, Oscar. They fed it the forgetting itself. Every person alive who took the crown's coin to forget Vellmark — the fire holds their forgetting too. When it goes out, everyone remembers at once. Everyone who signed. Everyone who sold. Everyone who watched the borders close." She swallowed. "Dame Halvard's kind of people."

"Dame Halvard," I said, and the name tasted of hemlock.

Because that was the thing I had been circling since the prince's dinner, since the taste of hemlock laced with something older than any poison I knew — older, I realized now, standing in the presence of the thing itself, because it wasn't a poison at all. It was dragon-fire, distilled, diluted, threaded through hemlock like a wire through wax. A message. Or a trigger. Someone had put a fragment of *this* — of the master asset, the guild's own lock — into the crown prince's food, and I had recognized its signature from a page in my own ledger, and I had spent every hour since running without once asking the question underneath:

Who could get dragon-fire out of this vault?

Only the guildmaster. Only Corvane.

Unless the vault was already leaking. Unless the lock was failing in more ways than one, and fragments of the fire were slipping out along its own cracks, finding their way into the world, tasting of endings.

Pulse. Gap. The gaps longer now. I counted again without wanting to.

Two days. Maybe one.

* * *

Ashka knelt by the cradle and began to speak.

Not to me. To the fire, in her own tongue, low and steady, the way you speak to something that has been alone too long to trust company. I couldn't follow the words, but I didn't need to. I am a professional listener; listening is half my trade, and the half no one pays for. She was telling it who she was. She was telling it that the country it had helped erase had left one living index behind, one girl who carried forty-one thousand names in her bones, and that she had come down into the dark not to spend it but to say *thank you*, and *I'm sorry*, and *you can rest soon*.

The fire leaned toward her.

There is no other word. The flame tilted, the way wheat tilts toward sun, the way a dog tilts its head at a beloved voice. The gold veins in the floor blazed from her knees outward in rings, and for one moment the whole hold lit up like dawn through amber, and I saw — I swear I saw — shapes in the light. Crowds. Rooftops. A market square with awnings snapping in wind that hadn't blown for two hundred years. Market-day sounds, faint as memory, fading as I strained toward them.

Then it guttered, and the shapes went out, and the gaps between pulses stretched again, and Ashka knelt there with her forehead almost touching the chains, crying without any sound at all, which is the worst kind of crying, the kind that has given up on being witnessed.

I put my hand on her back. I had nothing else to offer. I am a ledger-keeper; I deal in what can be counted. And what could be counted, right then, was this: one dying fire. One child carrying a nation. One woman whose body was a book of debts she'd never signed.

And outside these walls, closing in: Corvane, who had known about the guttering for longer than I had — who must have known, because Corvane knew everything the ledgers knew, including the pages I'd never shown him — and who would not come down here to mourn.

He would come down here to *arithmetic*.

A dying asset. A hard deadline. If the fire dies uncontrolled, the seals break all at once and every treaty built on Vellmark's erasure burns with them, and kingdoms shake, and thousands die in the unraveling — his argument, ready-made, waiting. Unless. Unless someone controlled the ending. Unless someone opened the seals *in order*, chose what came back and what stayed forgotten, and sold that choice to whoever could pay for stability.

The final door out of Vellmark needed a price paid in immunity. Corvane had told me that much, smiling, before everything broke. He needed my tolerance — dish by dish, purchased against my will across nine years of royal dinners — and he needed it *before the fire finished dying*, because the doors of the erased country answered to the same flame that sealed its records.

Everything ran through the fire. The doors. The seals. The clock.

And the clock was almost run out.

* * *

"We can't stay," I said. My voice sounded strange in the huge dark, flattened by all that listening stone. "If Corvane reaches the vault before we're through the last door—"

"He won't hurt the fire." Ashka didn't lift her head. "He can't. It's the collateral on everything the guild owns. Even Corvane wouldn't—"

Even Corvane wouldn't. I thought about Corvane's face when he'd told me my immunity was collateral. I thought about the hemlock in the prince's dish, and who besides the guildmaster could walk down a throat of stairs that swallowed strangers.

"Ashka." I turned her gently by the shoulders until she faced me. In the pulse-light her tears were gold. "Listen to me carefully. What happens to the seals if someone feeds the fire?"

Her brow furrowed. "Feeds it what?"

"Memories. Names. Anything. It's been starving, yes? Running on nothing for two hundred years. What if someone gave it fuel?"

She stared at me. Then she looked at the fire. Then at me again, and I watched the understanding arrive in her face the way frost arrives across a window — fast, total, and terrible.

"You'd feed it me," she said. "My memories. I'm the index. I'm forty-one thousand names. I'm the richest meal in the world." Her chin came up, furious and frightened and eight years old. "That's what Corvane wants. That's why he wants the doors. He wants to pour me into the flame and choose what Vellmark gets to be. He'd make it last forever — a country kept barely alive, gutted, tradable, a *product*—"

"I know," I said.

"And you're asking because you're wondering if it would work," she said, quieter now, and worse than the fury. "You're wondering if there's a version where we feed it just enough. Just some of me. Just the parts nobody needs."

I was. God help me, I was. Because I had done that arithmetic my whole life — the taster's arithmetic, the ledger-keeper's arithmetic, the arithmetic that keeps everyone at the table alive by deciding which dishes are expendable — and I did not know yet whether I could stop doing it.

The fire pulsed. The gap afterward stretched so long I counted four of my own heartbeats inside it.

"No," I said. "No versions. No parts. Nobody gets fed to anything. Not you, not me, not one single name in your keeping. We find another way through the last door, or we don't go through at all."

Ashka searched my face for the lie, found none, and nodded once — a small, formal nod, like a scholar accepting a colleague's sworn statement. Then, because she was eight, she threw her arms around my neck and held on hard enough to hurt.

Over her shoulder, the gold veins in the floor pulsed again.

And in the gap — the long dark gap, longer than any before — I heard, distinctly, from everywhere and nowhere in that vast hold, a sound I knew better than my own name.

A knock. Three knocks. Polite. Patient.

Coming up through the stairs behind us.

Corvane had a key to this vault. Corvane had always had a key.

But Corvane did not knock.
