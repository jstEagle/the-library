## 13. Midpoint: The Price of Passage

The door out of Vellmark was smaller than I expected.

That sounds like nothing, but it isn't. Everything else in that country's ghost had been built to a scale that made you feel invented — the ash-field where the capital had stood went on past the horizon in every direction, and the market-day sounds still caught in the air there were loud enough some mornings to wake us, vendors crying prices for bread that had been ash for three hundred years. A country that size should have had a door like a cathedral.

Instead it was set into a low hillside of grey grit, no bigger than the pantry door back at the palace where I'd spent eleven years tasting other people's meals so a boy who whined about parsnips could live. Oak gone silver with age. An iron ring handle. And above it, carved in the script Ashka had taught me one letter at a time while each letter took a piece of my childhood:

*Only what is given freely may pass.*

Ashka stood beside me, small hand buried in mine, her breath fogging even though the air in this place didn't quite believe in weather. Behind us, half a mile back toward the drowned market, Maren's blood was drying on the stones of an inn that existed on three different nights, and Corvane's hunters were methodically working through the borrowed rooms looking for us, and somewhere above all of it Dame Halvard was asking questions in a lawful voice that frightened me more than any hunter's blade.

And in front of me, between me and everything — survival, sanctuary, the chance to get this child somewhere the crown couldn't reach — the door wanted paying.

"It's not coin," Ashka said. She'd said it before. She said it again because she could see I was hoping she'd been wrong the first time. "I told you. It's not coin, Oscar."

"I know what it is." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "I just needed to hear the wind for a minute."

"There isn't any wind here."

"No," I agreed. "There never is. That's rather the point."

I sat down on the cold grit with my back against the silvered oak and took out the ledger.

Not the guild ledger. Those were ash now, or worse than ash — they were Corvane's again, every page of them, every account of bottled sunsets and traded childhoods and the last embers of dragon-fire balanced in my neat clerk's hand. I'd kept those books for nine years because order was the only prayer I knew how to say. Let him have his order.

This was my own book. Cheap paper, sewn binding, bought from a stationer in the Lower City whose name I could no longer remember, because I had spent that name's memory buying our way across the Stair-River two nights ago. On its first pages, in a script older than any kingdom, were the letters Ashka had taught me. Each character I'd written had cost me a memory of my childhood, and I had written them anyway, because the truth was the only currency Vellmark respected.

The rest of the book was blank.

"This is what I have left," I told Ashka. "What I am. What's in my blood."

She climbed into my lap without asking, the way children do when they've decided a person belongs to them, and looked at the empty pages with the grave attention she gave everything. Eight years old, or eight hundred — she'd been both since I met her, and I had stopped trying to make the arithmetic behave.

"The scholars of Vellmark wrote themselves into me," she said. "So they couldn't be erased. Every one of them gave something. A language. A recipe. The way their mothers braided hair. It hurt them too." She tilted her head back to look at me. "You don't have to. There are other doors. Worse ones. Doors that take other things."

"Worse things than my own life?"

"Things you'd miss more."

I thought about that honestly, because she deserved honesty and not heroics. Heroics are just another kind of performance, and I had been performing for the crown long enough.

But the thing I would miss most was sitting in my lap, smelling of woodsmoke and the strange clean cold of a country that existed only as a wound in the world, and so the arithmetic finished itself before I could pretend otherwise.

"Then we start with the dishes," I said.

* * *

Here is what you must understand about what I was giving away.

A food-taster does not simply survive poison. Anyone can survive poison once, if they're lucky, or die of it once, if they're not. What I had — what the guild built into me dish by dish over twenty years, and what I had believed, right up until the night I read my own contract in Corvane's vault, was simply my body's stubborn gift — was a *ledger*. Every poison I had ever taken lived in me as a tolerance, a paid account, a balance carried forward. Hemlock, arsenic, the seven salts of the southern marshes, widow's-tears, king's-grief, the blue mold that grows only on the coffins of executed traitors. Twenty years of small deliberate deaths, each one survived, each one banked.

And the crown knew it. That was the joke at the center of my life. They thought they owned my tongue. Corvane had mortgaged it.

Now the door wanted it back.

We started at dawn, which is a courtesy the ash-country extended out of habit, the sun rising pale and apologetic over a horizon with nothing on it. I had carried with me, wrapped in oilcloth, the last of the guild stores we'd taken on the run: a taster's kit, the old kind, the kind they stopped issuing after they realized men like me made the whole profession obsolete. Twelve vials. Twelve poisons, each one keyed to a tolerance in my blood, and the door's mechanism — Ashka showed me, tracing the carved letters with her finger until they warmed — would take each tolerance as I surrendered it.

"You drink," she said. "You almost die. You remember what surviving felt like. The door takes the remembering. Then you can't do it again."

"And then the next one kills me outright, if the door doesn't finish the job first."

"Yes." She said it the way she said everything, without cruelty and without flinching, because she was an index of a dead nation and grief had burned the flinching out of her centuries before she was born. "So we go slow. We go in the right order. I know the order." She tapped the first page of my ledger. "Vellmark knew about paying in the body. We were good at it. We had practice."

The first vial was hemlock.

Of course it was. It always came back to the prince's dinner, to that night eleven days and one lifetime ago when I'd tasted hemlock laced with something older than any poison in the crown's codex, and recognized the signature from a page I myself had recorded — the first crack in the comfortable lie of my double life. I uncorked the vial. It smelled green, the way spring smells if spring wanted to kill you.

"Hold my hand," I said.

"I'm holding your hand."

"Good. If I stop squeezing, tell me a word. Any word. In the old language."

"What kind of word?"

"A good one."

She thought about it seriously, lips moving. "*Sennaveh,*" she said finally. "It means the last warm loaf of the day. The one the baker sells cheap because the day is done and he wants to go home. There's no word for it in the king's tongue."

"That's because the king's tongue has never wanted to go home," I said, and drank.

Hemlock, when you've been drinking it in measured doses for two decades, tastes like a mistake you've learned to live with — numbness climbing the throat, the world going soft at the edges, the heart forgetting its tempo and then, grudgingly, finding it again. I knew every step of that dance. I rode it out with my back against the door and my hand crushing Ashka's small fingers and my eyes fixed on the pale sun, and when the numbness receded I felt it *leave* — physically leave, a coolness draining out through my spine and into the oak behind me, and the door drank it, and somewhere in the hillside a lock turned.

I opened my eyes. The world was sharper than it had been in twenty years. Sharper, and thinner. Like a page with the margins cut away.

One account closed. Eleven to go.

* * *

By the fourth vial I understood why the door worked this way, and why Vellmark had built it so.

It wasn't cruelty. It was bookkeeping — real bookkeeping, honest bookkeeping, the kind Corvane had never practiced despite his ledgers and his seals and his cellar full of other people's lives. A price that costs nothing is not a price; it's a transfer. Corvane moved memories between people like coin and called it trade because the ledgers balanced. But the door out of a murdered country demanded that the one leaving feel exactly what they were spending. Not the poison itself. The *surviving*. Every tolerance I surrendered, I surrendered twice — once in the flesh and once in memory, watching myself survive, knowing I would never survive it again.

Between doses I wrote. That was the ritual Ashka insisted on, and she was right, she was always right about the shape of things. After each payment I opened the ledger and wrote the poison's name in the pre-kingdom script, and each character took another piece of my childhood, and I let it go, and the strangest part — the part I will put down plainly, because someone should — is that it didn't feel like loss while it was happening. It felt like lightening. Like setting down bags I'd forgotten I was carrying.

It was only later, reaching for something — my mother's voice, the smell of the harbor in the city where I was born, the name of the boy who taught me to swim — that I found the shelves bare, and understood what I'd been spending all along.

"Sennaveh," Ashka reminded me, when the fifth vial — widow's-tears, sweet and rotten, like fruit at a funeral — left me shaking and blind for an hour. "Think of the bread. Think of the baker going home."

"There's no bread," I whispered. "There's no baker. This country is ash."

"But the word is true," she said. "Words can be true about things that aren't here anymore. That's the whole reason I exist."

So I held the word in front of me like a lamp and waited for my eyes to come back.

* * *

Nine vials. The sun crawled across the empty sky and began to lean westward, and I had aged a decade in a day, and I was laughing.

I want to be clear that it wasn't madness. Or if it was, it was the useful kind — the clarity that comes when every comfortable layer has been stripped off and you can see the plain bones of a choice. Because here is what I saw, lying on the cold grit with Ashka's arms around me and three vials left and my heart stuttering through rhythms no physician would have certified as living:

Corvane would have sold this door. He'd have found a buyer — a duke, a foreign crown, a collector of impossible things — and priced the passage out of Vellmark by the head, and the erased would have stayed erased, and the buyers would have gotten out, and the books would have balanced.

Halvard would have sealed it. Lawfully, cleanly, with warrants and wax, and called the sealing justice, because the alternative meant admitting what the law had helped bury.

The guild had built its stability on this silence, and thousands might indeed suffer if the treaties unravelled — Corvane wasn't entirely wrong, that was the horror of him, he was *arithmetic* right. But arithmetic has never once in history mourned anybody.

Somebody had to choose the mourning. Somebody had to walk out of this country having paid, visibly, in their own body, so that the price of the truth could never again be described as free.

If that somebody was a royal food-taster with a mortgaged tongue and a cheap notebook, then so be it. Somebody had to taste it. That had always been my job. I was, at last, doing it honestly.

"Two more after this one," Ashka said, holding up the eleventh vial. King's-grief, the worst one, the slow one. "Oscar. When it's gone — when all of it's gone — you'll be an ordinary woman. Any kitchen accident. Any vindictive cook. Any plate the crown sends you, ever again, and you'll die like anyone dies." Her voice cracked, just slightly, the index of a nation sounding suddenly like what she also was: a child who did not want me dead. "You could stop. You could stay here instead. The country would keep you. It keeps everyone it loves."

For one breath — I'll admit it, I'm putting it down because someone should — I considered it. Stay in the ash with the fading market-cries and the ghosts of bakers going home. Be kept. Be loved by a dead nation rather than hunted by a living one.

Then I heard it, faint across the plain: voices. Human voices, disciplined, spreading out in a search line. Halvard's inquisitors had found the borrowed rooms. Or hunters. Or both.

No. Not staying. There was a child beside me who had volunteered, at eight years old, to become a book so her people couldn't be deleted — and books are meant to be *read*, not shelved in ash. Somewhere outside this country there were archives to open, names to speak aloud, a guild to drag into the light. I had spent twenty years tasting death so that liars could eat safely.

I was not going to die in the dark for them too.

"Give me the vial," I said, "and hold my hand, and tell me about the bread."

* * *

King's-grief does not kill quickly, which is its mercy and its cruelty both. It takes the strength first, then the warmth, then the wanting, and leaves you lucid at the bottom of yourself, watching. I had banked a tolerance against it twelve years ago, in a cellar under the palace, with Corvane timing my pulse and taking notes.

I drank it and paid it out and felt twelve years of surviving drain through my spine into the silvered oak, and heard, distantly, another lock turn, and lay on the grit with Ashka's arms around me while the last vial — plain arsenic, almost homey after the rest, the workman's poison — waited in her hands.

"One more," she whispered.

"One more," I agreed. My lips barely moved. "Open the ledger first. Write the entry. All twelve. Finish the account."

She wrote, slowly, tongue between her teeth, in the beautiful old characters, and I watched her do it and felt almost nothing of the cost anymore — there was so little childhood left to take. Then she corked the last vial, raised it to my mouth —

— and stopped.

Her head turned toward the horizon. West. Where the voices had been.

"Oscar," she said. "Someone's coming. Someone alone. Walking straight for us, and none of the country is hiding her."

Nothing hid travelers in Vellmark unless Vellmark wished it. I knew that much by now.

"Describe her," I managed.

"Armored. But she's carrying the armor's helmet under her arm like she forgot why she put it on. And she's crying." Ashka frowned, reading something in the approaching figure that I couldn't see. "She's crying like a person who's just remembered something."

I tried to lift my head. The sky swam. Through the blur, a single figure resolved against the pale sun — and I knew the walk before I knew anything else, that lawful, measured stride, though it had never once, in all the months of her hunting us, wavered like this.

Dame Halvard. Unarmored in spirit if not in steel, weeping, walking out of the ash toward a dying taster and a child made of a dead country — and calling out, in a voice I had only heard issue warrants, a single word.

It was not a word from the king's tongue.
