## 14. What Halvard Remembers

Dame Halvard did not sleep in the palace. She slept above the interrogation rooms, because that was where the truth lived, and she had learned long ago that a person is shaped by what they sleep near. The cells below her bed breathed all night — a settling of stone, a drip of water, occasionally the small animal sound of someone weeping into a straw pallet. She found it restful. It was honest noise. The palace itself was full of lies so old they had fossilized into architecture.

She woke before dawn on the fourth day of hunting the taster, and lay still, and listened to the building confess.

Oscar Voss had been gone three days. Three days since she'd refused the bargain — refused it cleanly, without tears or bargaining, which Halvard respected and also knew was the most dangerous thing a cornered person could do. A woman who wouldn't trade was a woman who intended to win.

The trail should have been cold by now. It wasn't, because Voss had done something careless in her flight: she'd left a ledger page behind. Not by accident. Halvard had examined it under three kinds of light until midnight, and there was no accidental way to leave a single page from a bound volume — the stitching had been cut with a razor, deliberately, precisely, one page's worth. Voss had wanted it found.

The page was written in two hands. One was Voss's neat clerk-script, recording quantities and prices. The other was older, looping, almost decorative — a script the crown's own archivists could not read, though they pretended otherwise. At the top of the page, where a merchant would write the name of the goods, someone had written a word in that older hand, and beneath it, in Voss's script: *Vellmark — index, living — see vault seal nine.*

Halvard had read that line forty times. She had not yet written it into her report.

That was the crack, though she didn't name it as one. Dame Halvard did not have cracks. She had procedures, and a procedure for everything, including this: when an investigation touches the investigator, escalate to the Crown Inquisitor-General and recuse oneself. She had performed the procedure twice in nineteen years of service. Both times it had been the right thing, and both times it had cost her something she'd decided not to count.

This time she sat on the edge of her narrow bed with the ledger page in her hands and did not move toward the escalation forms.

*Vellmark.*

She knew the word. Everyone knew the word, which was to say everyone knew nothing about it, because knowing nothing about Vellmark was itself a kind of knowledge every citizen carried like a vaccination scar. There had been a treaty. There had been a border adjustment. There had been, somewhere in the deep past of the kingdom, a place whose name now appeared only in the phrase *the Vellmark Accords*, and if you asked what those accords settled, you got a look of patient pity, as if you'd asked who your grandmother was. Of course you knew. Everyone knew. The absence had edges, and the edges were smooth from handling.

Halvard dressed in the dark, strapped her knife where her rank permitted it and her conscience permitted it, which were different places, and went down into the archive levels instead of up to the morning briefing.

* * *

The Royal Archive kept its true depths below the water table, which was either very prudent or very telling, depending on how much rain you thought a secret needed to drown in. Halvard had clearance for the third sublevel. She used it, and then used a corridor she wasn't cleared for, because the corridor was empty at this hour and the lock on it was guild-work — she recognized the make, a mechanism that opened for anyone carrying the right kind of patience rather than the right kind of key.

Inside was a room the size of a chapel, shelved floor to ceiling, and cold in the way places are cold when they've been forgotten by their owners but not by their contents.

She took down files at random and understood within minutes that none of them were random. They were all the same file. Treaty annexes. Border surveys. Compensation ledgers. And running through them, again and again, in the margins, in that looping pre-kingdom script, annotations in a language the archivists catalogued as *obsolete regional dialect, untranslatable* — which was a lie, because no dialect gets its own sealed sublevel and a lock made by the Guild of Impossible Things.

Halvard had interrogated liars for nineteen years. She knew the anatomy of a lie the way a surgeon knows the anatomy of a body: where it was thickest, where it hid, where it could be cut open without killing the thing around it. The kingdom's lie about Vellmark was fat and well-fed and had been fed by generations of careful people, and she had been one of them.

She sat down on the archive floor with her back against the shelves and did the thing she trained others never to do. She stopped looking for what confirmed the case and started looking for what didn't.

It took her until noon. What didn't confirm the case was a compensation ledger, ninety years old, listing payments made by the Office of Forgetting — which officially did not exist, which officially had never existed, and whose expenditures were therefore recorded, with magnificent bureaucratic cowardice, under *miscellaneous civic improvements*. The payments were for memories. Purchased memories. The column headers said so plainly, in the flat language of accounting: *Item acquired. Item verified. Item surrendered voluntarily. Price paid.*

And there was a second list. Not payments. Births.

A registry of children born in a district whose name had been struck through with a single ruling line — the kind of strike that isn't deletion but burial, the record kept precisely so it can be denied. Dozens of names. Dates. And next to each name, in a later hand, a notation: *placed*, followed by a year and a household. Fostered out. Scattered across the kingdom like seeds from a crushed pod, each child planted in a family that had been paid to raise a stranger and told never to ask about the stranger's first four years.

Halvard ran her finger down the registry with the detachment she used for casualty lists, and then her finger stopped.

Her own name was on it.

Not Halvard — her mother's name had been Halvard, acquired, a soldier's widow in a river town who'd taken coin for a child she told her neighbors was a niece. Before that, in the older ink, half worn: *Sera.* Sera Vell. Born in the district whose name was buried. Age at acquisition: four years and two months. Item verified. Surrendered — and here the word *voluntarily* had been written, and then scratched out, and then rewritten, and the ink of the last version was fresher than the paper, which meant someone had come back to this line decades after the fact to make the paperwork agree with the law.

Four years old. She had a memory of being four years old. It was a small memory, one of the few she kept from childhood, and she had always treasured it precisely because it was strange: standing in a kitchen that was not her mother's kitchen, watching steam rise off something cooking, while a woman sang a song in words Halvard didn't know, and the song was sad, and little Sera had understood — with the absolute, unreasoning certainty of a small child — that the song was about a door closing.

Her whole life she had assumed the memory was a dream. Dreams are permitted. She had even, in the vanity of her twenties, taken a certain pleasure in it: *I dreamed in a language I never learned; how strange and poetic I am.* She had built a career on the principle that everything could be traced to a source, and she had held one unexamined mystery at the center of herself like a pearl in a closed fist, and called the ache of holding it character.

The woman in the kitchen had not been singing about a door. She had been singing about a country.

* * *

Halvard came up out of the archive into afternoon light and stood in the courtyard letting it hit her face, because she needed proof of the ordinary world. Carters shouting. Pigeons. The smell of bread from the bakery lane. All of it real, all of it sitting on top of the same buried district the way grass sits on a grave.

Here was the thing about interrogation — the thing she taught, the thing she believed. You cannot break a person with questions. You can only show them their own story until they see the seams. Every confession she had ever won had been, in the end, a person recognizing the shape of what had been done to them.

So she sat down across from herself, in the empty courtyard, and began the interview.

*Dame Halvard. Where were you born?*

In the river town. My mother was a soldier's widow.

*And before the river town?*

Before doesn't exist. Records begin—

*Records begin,* she answered herself, *where someone decided they would begin.* Four years and two months. Someone had decided. Someone had signed. Somewhere there was a document with her infant name on it and a price in the adjacent column, and she had spent her life serving the office that kept that document filed under *civic improvements*.

Nineteen years. She counted her cases the way a gambler counts losses. The weaver who "confessed" to smuggling and could never say what he'd smuggled. The scholar who broke down weeping over a book he swore he'd never owned, insisting he'd loved it, insisting he'd lost it, insisting on grief for a thing that per official record he'd never touched. The old woman in the provinces who had begged the inquisitors, over and over, to tell her why she woke every night mourning a city she couldn't name. Halvard had marked that last one *hysteria* and moved on. The woman hadn't been hysterical. She'd been homesick for a country that had been stolen out of her head while she slept, sold by the pound, and she was the only witness left who knew anything was missing, because everyone else's missing had been purchased too.

Every case that had ever bothered her — the ones she'd stayed up over, the ones that didn't close clean — shared a seam. Grief without object. Knowledge without source. People haunted by absences with edges.

They weren't suspects. They were survivors. Her investigation had been walking through a population of amnesiacs asking who stole their memories, and the answer was the crown she served, and the buyer was the guild she hunted, and the merchandise had included her.

She thought about Oscar Voss then, and had to stand very still.

Three days ago she had offered the taster safety in exchange for the child and the ledgers, and Voss had looked at her with something Halvard had taken for insolence. It hadn't been insolence. It had been recognition. *You don't know what you're selling,* that look had said. Halvard had walked away from that table certain she was the lawful one, the anchored one, the one with nothing hidden in her ledger.

Her ledger had a buried district in it. Her ledger had a singing woman in a kitchen. Her ledger had been compiled by other people and priced in coin.

* * *

By evening she had made her decision, and the making of it felt less like courage than like arithmetic finally balancing.

She went back down past the third sublevel, past the guild-lock, to the deepest shelf, and took the registry with her — the whole registry, all the buried names — wrapped in her cloak. If she was going to commit treason, she intended to commit it thoroughly, with documentation. Nineteen years of procedure had to be good for something.

At the archive door, a page was waiting for her, breathless, holding a message in the Crown's black wax. Orders. The hunt for Voss and the child was escalating; the Inquisitor-General wanted results within the week; and appended, in a clerk's indifferent hand, a note that the Guild of Impossible Things had offered its full cooperation, through its guildmaster, Corvane, who wished particularly to assist in the recovery of "guild property currently held by the fugitive."

Guild property.

Halvard stood in the corridor reading those two words and understanding, at last, exactly what the child was. An index. A living country folded into a girl of eight. And Corvane — the man whose cooperation the crown accepted the way a body accepts a graft — wanted her back in his vaults, priced and shelved.

The message asked for her reply. She looked at the waiting page, at his young face already learning to keep his eyes down, already learning the smooth edges of things, already one buried name away from being any of the people on that registry.

"Dame?" he said.

"Tell them," Halvard heard herself say, "the investigation has changed direction."

He waited for more. She gave him nothing, because she had just remembered — with a clarity that hurt worse than any truth she'd ever extracted — the rest of the song from the kitchen. The part after the door closes. Her mother's voice had carried it, in a language the kingdom had drowned, and even at four years old, even stripped and scattered and renamed, some grain of her had kept the tune:

*What they burn they must first write down. What they bury keeps growing. Come home.*

Somewhere out there, a fugitive food-taster was running with a child who spoke the dead country's tongue. Halvard put the registry inside her coat, over her heart, where she kept the things she was no longer willing to lose, and went to find them — no longer as a hunter.

As kin.
