## 15. The Guild Turns

The notice went up in the borrowed rooms before we did.

We learned about it the way you learn about weather — by the change in the air. Ashka and I had spent two nights in the inn that exists on three different Tuesdays, and on the third morning the innkeeper, a woman with flour to her elbows and guild ink under her fingernails, stopped smiling at us. She set down our bread without meeting my eyes. She left the door to the cellar open when she would normally have locked it, and through the gap I could see the glint of a posted paper, and I didn't need to read it to know whose face was on it.

Mine.

"Thief of Guild Assets," the bill read, because Corvane had always been precise even in his cruelties. "Oscar Voss, former Ledger-Keeper, Third Rank. Wanted for the theft of one (1) living index, one (1) keyring of defunct doors, and sundry memories not her own. Reward: ten thousand crowns, or equivalent trade. The Guild does not forget what it is owed."

Equivalent trade. That was the phrase that made my hands go cold around the bread. Ten thousand crowns was a lot of money. But the guild dealt in more than money, and Corvane knew exactly what his hunters would prefer to be paid in — years off their debts, memories they'd sold and regretted, immunity for someone they loved. He wasn't buying my capture. He was buying *willingness*, which is cheaper and lasts longer.

"He put your face on walls," Ashka said. She was eight years old and could read three dead scripts, but she still sounded her way through common letters like any child. "Your face looks angry."

"It's an old sketch. From my induction." I touched the charcoal likeness, the younger me with her chin up, proud of her new rank, proud of her ledger hand, proud of belonging to something. It seemed like a portrait of a different person. Perhaps it was. "I was very pleased with myself that day."

"Why?"

"Because I thought keeping accounts meant keeping things safe."

Ashka considered this with the terrible honesty of children. "It doesn't?"

"Sometimes it means keeping things *kept*," I said. "There's a difference, and I've spent a month learning it."

We left through the inn's Tuesday door, because Tuesdays were quietest, and stepped out into a stairwell that was also a river — the guild's oldest borrowed room, water flowing upward along steps worn smooth by feet that had never technically been there. Ashka loved it. She would crouch at each landing and trail her fingers in the current, and once she'd told me the water tasted like the harbor at Vellmark's capital, and then she'd cried, and then she'd eaten half a loaf of bread and been fine, because she was eight, and grief moved through her the way weather moves over a field: total, brief, leaving everything green behind it.

I envied her that. My grief had learned to live indoors.

We were four landings up when the hunters found us.

* * *

They came from above and below, which is how guild people hunt — never from one direction, because every direction in the borrowed rooms can be argued with. There were five of them. I knew four by name.

Dessa Kell, who'd taught me to hold poison on my tongue like a coin held under it, flat and patient. The Voss brothers, who ran the archive's cold shelves and between them remembered every debt ever forgiven, which made them dangerous in arguments. And old Tam Wicke, seventy if he was a day, who had traded away his own childhood so thoroughly that he no longer remembered being small and therefore believed no one else should get to keep theirs either.

The fifth was Maren.

Maren Solde, who had sat across from me at the long table for eleven years, who had helped me carry Ashka out of the vault the night we fled, who had stood in the doorway afterward and lied to Corvane's face with such fluency that I'd believed him capable of anything except this.

"Maren," I said. The river-stair gurgled upward around our ankles. Above us, Dessa blocked the top landing. Below, the Vosses and Tam. Maren stood level with me, close enough to touch, holding a guild net of woven hours — throw it over someone and they lived the same minute over and over until it was cut away.

"Oscar." His voice was gentle. It always had been. Even now, hunting me, he couldn't find a cruelty in himself to use as a tool. "Don't make this hard."

"You could just not do it."

"I took an oath."

"You took a *job*, Maren. The oath was Corvane's invention. You watched him write it."

Something flickered across his face — not agreement, nothing so useful, but recognition. He had watched too. We all had. That was the guild's great trick: it never asked anyone to believe a lie. It only asked them to sign.

"The girl comes back to the vault," he said. "You come back to the ledgers. Nobody gets hurt. That's the deal he's offering and I'm telling you it's real, Oscar, I heard him make it. He wants this quiet."

"And after?"

Maren didn't answer, and his not-answering answered.

Ashka's hand found mine. Small, warm, gripping hard enough to hurt. I looked down at her and she looked up at me, and she did not say *run*, because she had learned already that running in the borrowed rooms is a language the rooms themselves speak better than you do. Instead she said, in the old tongue of Vellmark, one word.

I have since tried to translate it honestly, and the closest I can come is: *the door remembers you.*

"Dessa," I said loudly. "You taught me that hemlock hides best in fat. You taught me that the first duty of a taster is to the truth of a dish. Was any of that true, or was it training?"

Dessa Kell looked at me across four flights of ascending water, and her jaw worked. "It was true," she said finally. "That's why I'm here. Truth has a price, girl, and somebody pays it, and better it's you than—"

"Than whom?" I asked. "Name one person safer than an eight-year-old in a vault."

Silence, except the river.

Tam Wicke shifted his weight, and I saw him begin the small hand-motion that would loose the net from Maren's grip, and I understood that we had perhaps three seconds and no good options, and so I did the thing I am actually best at, the thing the crown paid me for and the guild trained me for and eleven years of poisons had perfected in me.

I read the room like a dish.

Dessa wanted to be let off the hook. The Vosses wanted their debts settled and hated Corvane for making them collect instead. Tam wanted, simply and horribly, to matter. And Maren — Maren wanted permission. He had wanted permission the night he lied for us. He was standing there with a weapon made of repeated minutes hoping, praying, that someone would give him a reason not to use it.

So I gave him one.

"Maren," I said. "The last time we talked, before all this, you told me about your sister. The one you don't remember. You told me you'd sold the memory when you were young and stupid and you've spent thirty years feeling the shape of the hole. You said—" I made my voice soft, because this part mattered, "—you said the worst thing wasn't losing her. It was that somewhere in a vault, some stranger could buy her back and know her better than you did."

His eyes closed.

"That's what Corvane is doing to her," I said. "To all of them. To a whole country. He's got a child in a cage who *is* everyone he sold, and he calls her assets."

"Oscar." Barely a breath.

"Throw the net or don't."

The seconds stretched. Tam Wicke's fingers twitched toward the signal. The river climbed its stairs, patient, going nowhere, like everything the guild had ever kept.

And Maren Solde turned the net in his hands, and threw it — upward, past me, into the dark of the stairwell's upper reaches, where it caught on nothing, unspooled its hoarded minutes into empty air, and was spent.

"Go," he said.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and I want to record it accurately, because accuracy is the only prayer I have left.

Dessa shouted — but at Tam, not at us, something about *not on the stairs, you fool, the current carries*. The Voss brothers moved to block the lower landing and then, with a synchrony that must have taken thirty years of shared shelving to perfect, discovered simultaneously that their boots were stuck. Tam lunged for Ashka and met Dessa's arm across his chest, and whether she meant it as help or merely as housekeeping I will never know, and I have decided it doesn't matter.

Maren grabbed my shoulder. "The Tuesday inn is burned," he said quickly. "He posted watchers in every version of it. Take the Thursday door — the one behind the wine racks, the inn that exists on three different *Thursdays*. Nobody likes Thursdays. Nobody watches them." He pressed something into my hand, small and cold: a keyring, six keys, each one shaped like a word I couldn't pronounce. "Defunct doors. The ones the ledger says were lost. They weren't. I lost them. On purpose. Years ago."

"Maren—"

"Don't." His voice cracked clean through. "Don't thank me, don't forgive me, just *go*, because in about forty seconds I'm going to have to explain that net, and I need you gone before I decide what kind of lie to tell."

Ashka stepped forward — past me, past caution — and took his hand in both of hers, and said something in the old tongue, short and formal, the cadence of it like a benediction. Maren stared at her. "What did she say?"

"She said you're owed," I translated, though translation isn't really the right word; the language doesn't translate so much as *settle*, the way a debt settles. "She said the country keeps books too. She said you're marked down as paid."

Maren Solde, who had sold his sister and his childhood and his courage one installment at a time, sat down on a stair in an impossible river and put his face in his hands.

We went.

* * *

The Thursday inn smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet. Three hearths burned in the same room, each lit on a different Thursday, and the innkeeper — or the innkeepers, plural across their Thursdays, though they agreed on policy — took one look at Ashka and gave us the back room without being asked, and I understood that word travels even between days.

For one hour, we were almost warm.

Ashka slept against my side, and I sat with Maren's keys in my palm and did arithmetic I hated. Six defunct doors. A price on my head that would rise, because Corvane did not tolerate failure and his hunters would come back harder and hungrier. A dragon-fire guttering beneath the deepest vault with every sealed memory of Vellmark packed around it like kindling. A girl asleep on my shoulder who was worth more alive to the guild than any ten thousand crowns, and who trusted me absolutely, which was the most dangerous thing either of us owned.

I had spent my whole life reading dishes for hidden things. Now every kindness tasted faintly of hemlock. The innkeeper's mercy. Dessa's arm. Maren's keys. All of it might be true, and all of it might be bait, and the awful skill of my profession was that I could no longer tell the difference between loyalty and poison, because they sat on the tongue the same way — heavy, sweet-edged, waiting to see what you'd do.

Then the knock came at the back-room door. Three raps. Paused. Two more.

Guild knock. Archive rank.

I stood, put Ashka behind me, and opened the door with a knife I did not remember drawing.

Maren stood in the corridor, bleeding from a cut above his eye, his shirt torn at the collar where someone had taken hold of him. Behind him, at the end of the hall, the front door of the inn stood open onto ordinary night, and beyond it, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of many boots and one voice calling measurements — Dame Halvard's voice, level as a surveyor's, ordering her inquisitors to seal the street.

"They traced the net," Maren said. "Spent minutes leave a residue. I didn't think — I threw it blind, I didn't think they could read where it had been." He laughed, wet and short. "Eleven years keeping ledgers and I forgot that everything leaves an entry."

"There's another way out," I said. "Tell me there's another way out."

"The wine cellar. But Oscar — the Thursday door opens onto the river-stair, and the river-stair is crawling with the Vosses by now, and Halvard's people are coming through the front, and—" He stopped. He looked at Ashka, awake now, watching him with those eyes that had seen a country die. He looked at the knife in my hand.

"Take the keys," he said. "All of them. And listen — the sixth key. The one like a hook with a bar through it. That's not a defunct door. That's the vault below the vault. Where they keep the fire."

My blood went to ice water. "How do you have that?"

"Because I was the one who carried it out of Corvane's office the night you ran, and I told myself I'd stolen it to sell later, and I've been carrying it around for weeks pretending that was true." He pushed past me into the room, knelt, and pressed the sixth key into Ashka's hands, closing her small fingers over it the way you'd close a hand over a coal you meant to keep alight. "You know where it goes," he said to her. Not a question. An index knows its own index.

Boots on the stairs now. The innkeepers of three Thursdays shouting in unison that the establishment was closed, had always been closed, would be closed on every Thursday forever.

"Maren," I said. "Come with us."

"And lead them straight to the fire? No." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying. "Someone has to answer the door. Someone respectable. Someone they'll believe when he says the thief went east through the flooded market." He straightened his torn collar like a man dressing for court. "Go, Oscar. Write it down somewhere. What I did. Put it in a ledger. I want it *entered*."

The door burst inward.

What happened next, I will set down exactly, because he asked to be entered and I am the only keeper left who can enter him. Maren Solde stepped between the inquisitors and the back window, and he talked — gods, how he talked, eleven years of guild diplomacy pouring out of him, apologies and jurisdiction and *Dame Halvard, surely we can settle this like professionals* — and his voice followed us out the window and down into the flooded market and across the rooftops while Ashka clung to my back and the sixth key dug its shape into both our palms.

The last thing I heard, before the city swallowed it, was Halvard's voice, cutting through Maren's patter like a blade through broth:

"Where are the keys, Master Solde?"

And then Maren, laughing, saying the bravest stupid thing I have ever heard anyone say:

"What keys? I lose everything. Ask anyone. Ask my *sister*."

We ran east, the wrong way, toward the flooded market and the first of six doors, and behind us I heard the sound of Maren Solde being arrested to the accompaniment of his own laughter, and ahead of us, somewhere in the drowned dark, a lock waited that had not been turned in a hundred years — and beneath it, beneath everything, a fire going out.
