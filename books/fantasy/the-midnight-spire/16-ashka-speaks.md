## 16. Ashka Speaks

The borrowed room was small — a scholar's cell that existed somewhere between a Tuesday and a Wednesday, with a window that looked out on weather no season had claimed. Rain fell upward past the glass. Ashka sat cross-legged on the narrow bed, watching it, and for the first time since the guild door had opened to her, she was alone by choice.

Oscar was two floors down, arguing with Maren's cousin about safe passage. Halvard's hounds — not dogs but debts, sniffing out unpaid obligations — circled the inn's three different nights. They had hours, maybe. Not many.

Ashka pressed her palm flat against the cold glass and spoke aloud in her own language, quietly, just to hear it.

*Vellmark. Vellmark. Vellmark.*

Her name for herself, in her own tongue: *Ashka vel-Dornen*, which meant *the one who remembers aloud*. Everyone in Vellmark had a name like that. Her mother had been *Sera vel-Illen*, the one who mends what winter breaks. The names were promises. You grew into them or you spent your life apologizing.

She was tired of being an index. She was tired of being a thing looked up.

The door opened without a knock — in this room, doors did what they liked — and Oscar came in carrying bread and a candle stub, her face gray with exhaustion, her hands still trembling from the last payment she'd made at some threshold or other. She'd paid away the taste of plums, Ashka had noticed. Oscar hadn't eaten a plum in front of her since.

"You should sleep," Oscar said.

"So should you."

"I don't dream anymore. It saves time." Oscar set the bread down and lowered herself onto the floor across from the bed, back against the wall, like a soldier. "What were you saying? At the window."

"Nothing. Counting."

"In your own tongue?"

Ashka nodded.

"I've been meaning to ask," Oscar said carefully. "The scholars. In Vellmark. When they built you into the index — how old were you?"

And there it was. The question everyone had been circling for weeks, the one Corvane had never asked because he didn't care, the one Halvard couldn't ask because the answer would break something in her. Even Oscar had handled her gently, as if the answer could only be one thing: *taken. stolen. made.*

Ashka looked at her hands. Eight years old in body. Something much older folded inside her, page after page of a country pressed flat and stored behind her ribs.

"I wasn't taken," she said.

Oscar went very still.

"I volunteered." She said it in trade-tongue first, then again in her own language, where the word for *volunteer* also meant *to step forward when the line is drawn*, and there was honor in it, and weight, and no shame at all. "I want to tell it properly. Will you write it down? Not in the ledger. Just — write. The way I taught you. It costs you, I know. But some things should be written twice."

Oscar took out her stylus without a word. Her hand shook. Ashka knew what each character would take from her — a morning, a smell, a voice — and hated asking, and asked anyway, because that was the whole point of everything that was about to be said.

"The erasure didn't come suddenly," Ashka began. "It came like winter comes. First the maps changed at the edges. Then the treaties. Then the money — crown coin started appearing in our markets, and with it came men who bought things no market should sell. Years. Names. The color of our sky in autumn. My grandmother sold her memory of her wedding and didn't know she'd been married until my grandfather showed her the ring he still wore, and even then she only believed him halfway."

She watched Oscar write. Each character cost her something; Ashka could see the small flinches, the way her eyes went distant and came back emptier. She hated it. She kept talking.

"Our scholars saw it coming twenty years off. Master Ellun-Vai — she held the chair of Unbinding at the University of Dorne — she stood up at the convocation and said: *They will not burn us. Burning leaves ash, and ash can be read. They will do something cleaner. They will make us forgettable.* And she was right. That was the horror of it. She was right, and knowing didn't save anyone, and she died anyway, but first—"

Ashka's throat closed. She breathed through it the way her mother had taught her: *in for the length of a name, out for the length of a grief.*

"First she designed the index. A living book. Not paper — paper burns, paper can be bought, paper can be misfiled by people like your guildmaster. A child, raised inside the whole of the country. Every genealogy, every song, every border-stone and recipe and grave. Taught to hold it all so that if Vellmark was erased, Vellmark would still exist — walking around, breathing, waiting for someone to ask."

"They chose you," Oscar said quietly.

"They *asked*. That's different." Ashka lifted her chin. "There were four of us in the final selection. I remember all their names; that's part of what I carry. There was Tam, who wanted it desperately and wept when he wasn't chosen, and who died in the erasure holding his little sister's hand, and I say his name so he isn't only a fact in me. There was Ilse, who refused, which was also brave, and lived, and doesn't remember living. There was a boy called Fen whose family fled east before the end, and I have carried his goodbye for eight years and never found anyone to give it to."

"And you?"

"I was seven." Ashka smiled, and knew it must look strange on a child's face — too old, too certain. "Master Ellun-Vai sat across from me and explained everything. The cost. That I would become a country instead of a girl. That I might never be asked about, that I might wander forgotten forever, that the ones who erased us would hunt me if they ever guessed I existed. She said, *You may refuse, and no one will think less of you, and I will think more of you.*"

"What did you say?"

"I asked her one question. I asked: *If I hold the country, does the country get to keep me?*" Ashka laughed softly. "She cried. Grown woman, greatest scholar of her age, and she cried in front of a seven-year-old, and said, *Yes. That is exactly the bargain.* So I stepped forward. I put my hand on the Index Stone and let them pour a nation into me, and it hurt the way birth hurts, I imagine, and when it was done I was both of us — Ashka, and Vellmark — and I have never once regretted it. Not in the vault. Not in the storm. Not now."

Oscar had stopped writing. Her stylus hovered. "Everyone assumed—"

"Everyone assumes the erased were passive." Ashka's voice hardened, and the rain outside the window froze mid-fall, every drop hanging like beads on a string, because in this room the weather listened too. "That we waited to be saved, or mourned, or traded. Your guild put me in a vault *for my protection* — protection from what? From being useful? Corvane looks at me and sees an asset that appreciates. Halvard looks at me and sees evidence. Even you—" she gentled, seeing Oscar's face, "even you look at me sometimes like I'm a wound that needs tending. But I am not what happened *to* Vellmark. I am what Vellmark *did about it*. Do you understand the difference? One is a crime. The other is a choice."

Oscar set the stylus down. "Say it again. All of it. I want to get this right."

So Ashka told her the rest, and this time she told it standing, the way her people told important things, feet planted, hands open. She told her about the night the erasure came — a soundless wave rolling out of the capital at moonset, and how she'd been smuggled out days earlier through the marsh gate with a wet-nurse who forgot her own name before they reached the border, and how Ashka had walked alone after that, an eight-year-old index with no one left to index *for*. She told her how she'd known, somehow, in the way the knowledge inside her knew things, that the Guild of Impossible Things had sold the forgetting — that Corvane's predecessors had brokered the treaty's silence clause — and how she had gone to the guild anyway, deliberately, through a storm, because the ledger-keeper there was famous for keeping honest accounts, and honest accounts cut both ways.

"I came to *you*," Ashka said. "Not to shelter. To settle scores. You keep ledgers, Oscar Voss. Well. So do I. Mine has every entry yours does — and mine records who signed."

The hanging rain began to fall again, upward, as though ashamed.

Oscar stared at her. "You knew about the guild. Before the door."

"I knew everything before the door. I'm a country, Oscar. Countries notice their neighbors." Ashka sat back down, suddenly very tired, the way she always was after speaking at full weight. "I chose you too, if you want the truth of it. Out of everyone in that building, your handwriting in the old ledgers was the only one that recorded prices *and* names. Everyone else wrote what things cost. You wrote who paid. I read your ledgers before you ever saw me — through the door, in the storm. Doors talk to me. Everything that holds a record talks to me."

"That's why the guildhouse answered to its old name."

"That's why the whole city leans toward you when you walk past, and you've never noticed. Things that remember are drawn to the person who writes memory down honestly." Ashka reached over and closed Oscar's fingers around the stylus. "You thought you were protecting me. All this time on the run, paying your immunity away dish by dish. But I picked *you*, ledger-keeper. I've been picking you since the storm."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere below, the inn's third night ended and its second began, and the sounds of a market that had happened forty years ago drifted up through the floorboards.

Then Oscar did something Ashka didn't expect. She turned the stylus around, offered it handle-first, and said:

"Write your own chapter, then. In your own hand. What should it say?"

Ashka took it. The wood was warm from Oscar's grip. She thought of Master Ellun-Vai crying. Of Tam and his sister. Of Fen's goodbye still undelivered. Of her mother's name, which meant *the one who mends what winter breaks*, and how winter had broken everything except this: a child with a stylus, alive, choosing.

She wrote, in characters older than any kingdom, and felt the words settle into the world like stones into a riverbed:

*I, Ashka vel-Dornen, was not lost. I was sent.*

Below them, a floorboard creaked that belonged to none of the inn's three nights.

Then the fourth stair — the one that existed on no night at all — spoke with Dame Halvard's voice, ragged and cracked: "*Vellmark.* That's — that word, I know that word. Why do I know that word?"

Ashka and Oscar looked at each other.

"She remembers," Ashka breathed. "The fire's dying fast enough now that the seals are slipping. Halvard's memories are coming home." She was already moving toward the impossible stair, heart hammering with something that was not fear — it was closer to dawn. "Come on. We need to reach her before Corvane does. Because if Halvard remembers she was born here—"

"—then she knows exactly where the dragon-fire vault is," Oscar finished grimly. "And so does everyone she's ever told."

The fourth stair groaned under their feet, opening downward into dark.
