## 19. The Last Dish

The door out of Vellmark was a kitchen door.

Of course it was. Oscar should have known. Every door in this country had led somewhere domestic — a pantry, a hearth, a scullery where someone had once scrubbed pots and hummed to herself while the world outside went about the business of existing. Vellmark had been erased from treaties and tax rolls and the memory of every crown that bordered it, but it had not been erased from its own kitchens. The ash-field remembered bread. The dead streets remembered water boiling.

The door stood alone in the ruin of what had been a market square, set into a wall that supported nothing — no house behind it, no house before it. Just a wall, standing out of the ash like the last tooth in an old jaw, and in it a door with a worn brass latch and a step worn hollow by two hundred years of feet that had carried baskets and buckets and children on hips.

Ashka put her small hand flat against the wood and said something in the old language. The word came out soft, round at the edges.

"What did you call it?" Oscar asked.

"The way home." Ashka looked up at her, and in the grey light of the guttering country she looked older than eight, the way children look older when they have been carrying something too heavy for too long. "It's locked. It's been locked since they wrote us out. And locks like this one don't take keys."

Oscar already knew what they took. She had known since the vault beneath the guildhouse, since Corvane's ledger with her own name entered in it as an asset: *Voss, O. — immunity, cumulative, 412 dishes. Value appreciating.*

"It takes a taster," Oscar said.

* * *

They sat down to eat in the shadow of the door, because that was the shape of the toll. Not blood. Not memory, not this time. The door out of Vellmark wanted the one thing Oscar had spent eleven years building in her body, dish by dish, dose by dose, until poison slid through her like rain through sand.

Her immunity. All of it. Paid out as a meal.

Ashka had found the table — of course there was a table; Vellmark's ghosts were hospitable even now — dragged from a collapsed doorway and set before the door, and on it they laid out the final course. It was not food. It was the guild's arithmetic made edible: twelve small dishes, each one holding a single immunity, each one keyed to a poison Oscar's body had learned to shrug off one terrible evening at a time. Wolfsbane. Arsenic in honey. The pale blue powder the southern kingdoms called widow's mercy. A dozen more whose names existed only in the guild's ledgers, written in Corvane's neat hand, priced and owned and waiting.

"How do we know it works?" Oscar asked, turning one dish in her fingers. It held nothing visible. It didn't need to. She could smell it — or rather she could smell the absence, the hole in the air where a poison would be, which was worse.

"Ashka knows," Maren said quietly.

He had come with them as far as the market square and no further. He was leaning against the broken wall, arms crossed, one eye still swollen shut from the hunter's blow he'd taken in the borrowed rooms so that they could run. He had paid for their escape with half his face and all of his standing in the guild, and he stood now in the country his employers had erased, looking like a man who had finally read the fine print on his own life.

"Ashka," Oscar said. "Tell me."

The girl climbed onto the bench beside her. Up close, in this light, Oscar could see the script moving under her skin again — faint, silver-grey, like veins seen through ice. The living index. Two hundred years of a nation written into a child who hadn't been born when any of it happened, who had volunteered anyway.

"When they closed the doors," Ashka said, "they paid them. That's how the guild does it. Everything is paid for. Nobody steals a whole country — they buy it, piece by piece, from people who are too tired or too scared or too hungry to say no. And the doors took payment the same way everything else did." She touched the door's brass latch without opening it. "This one was paid with a taster's tongue. Someone gave up knowing what was safe to eat. So the way home stopped being safe to eat. You have to pay it back the same coin."

"And if I don't survive the meal?"

"Then the door stays locked," Ashka said simply, "and I stay here, and eventually the dragon-fire goes out and I go with it, and Vellmark finishes dying properly instead of halfway. Which is what everyone wanted anyway. Except me. Except us."

Maren shifted against the wall. "There's another option," he said. "There's always another option. We go back through the borrowed rooms, find Corvane, put a knife in—"

"No." Oscar said it before she'd decided to. "No knives. No more trading. This whole country died because everyone kept making deals." She set the first dish down in front of her and looked at it the way she'd looked at four hundred plates before crowned heads: calmly, professionally, as though her life were not the stake but merely the service.

Besides. She had been paying since the vault. Eleven immunities gone already, spent on doors across three impossible rooms, and each one had taken something with it. When she'd surrendered her tolerance for wolfsbane, she'd lost the memory of her mother's stew — the actual taste of it, gone like a page torn out. When she'd paid the arsenic debt, she'd lost the taste of the wedding wine she'd stolen a sip of at seventeen. Her childhood was becoming a menu with items struck through. What remained after tonight, she could not guess.

But she knew what she was. She had known since the hemlock in the prince's soup, since the signature she recognized from her own ledger pages. She was a woman who tasted things other people couldn't afford to. That was all. That had always been enough.

"All right," she said. "Let's eat."

* * *

She ate the way she'd been trained: small sip first, wait, watch her own hands for tremor. Then the rest.

The first dish was wolfsbane's ghost, and it hit her like a fist inside her chest. Not the poison — the *absence* of armor. Eleven years of tolerance unwinding in her bloodstream, and her body remembering, all at once, what aconite actually did. Her lips went numb. Her heart stumbled and then ran. She gripped the edge of the table and breathed through it, and Ashka's small hand covered hers, and Maren swore softly in three dialects, and she did not die.

The second was arsenic, and it burned a cold line down her throat and settled behind her eyes like winter. The third made her bleed a little from the gums, which she noted with detached professional interest, the way she'd note an unfamiliar spice. The fourth, fifth, sixth — she lost count around the eighth, because by then the world had narrowed to the table and the next dish and the sound of Ashka counting under her breath in the old language, numbers, just numbers, keeping time for her like a nurse counting a fever.

Somewhere past the tenth, her vision went silver at the edges and she understood, distantly, clearly, that she was dying. Not quickly. But certainly. The immunity had been the only wall between her body and every poison the crowns and guilds had ever brewed, and she had eaten the wall.

"Last one," Ashka whispered.

Oscar looked at the twelfth dish. It was different from the others — she could tell even through the fog, even with her senses collapsing. The others had been debts. This one was a question. It smelled like the space between words. It smelled like the page in her oldest ledger, the one transcribed in a script no kingdom used, the one she'd copied without understanding, years ago, for a client whose name she'd never known.

"What is it?" she managed.

Ashka's face crumpled and reassembled itself into something fierce. "It's the original payment," she said. "What the door was fed the day they closed it. It's the poison of forgetting, and it's been sitting in the lock for two hundred years, and the only thing that beats it—" Her voice caught. "You know what beats it. You've known since the vault. Since you started writing my letters."

Oscar had. God help her, she had.

Every character of Ashka's alphabet she'd learned had cost her a memory — that was chapter seven's cruel arithmetic, the vault lessons, the candle burning down both ends. But memories weren't all the writing had taken. Something else had been happening, something neither of them had named: each character she'd written had also *written itself into her*. The script lived in her hand now, in the muscles of her forearm, in the way she thought about beginnings and endings. She was the only living person outside Ashka who could write Vellmark's tongue, and she had written it in her own blood-chemistry, letter by letter, without knowing she was composing anything at all.

She was not just a taster anymore. She was a text.

"Write," Ashka said. "As it goes down. Write your name in the old letters and keep writing and don't stop, no matter what it tastes like."

Oscar picked up the knife beside the dishes — the plain kitchen knife some dead Vellmarker had left in a drawer two centuries ago — and pressed its point to the back of her own left hand, and drank the twelfth dish.

* * *

The forgetting-poison tasted like nothing.

That was the horror of it. Every poison she had ever catalogued announced itself — bitter, sweet, metallic, the almond ghost of cyanide, the hay-and-honey rot of hemlock. This one tasted of absence, of a clean plate, of a mouth rinsed and ready. It tasted like the moment before a memory starts. And she felt it move through her with the perfect confidence of something that has done this work for two hundred years, feeling along her mind for the seams of her, ready to unpick her entire self the way the guild had unpicked a nation.

She began to write.

The knife moved in the old characters, dragging lines across the back of her hand, and she spoke them aloud as she cut because Ashka had taught her that Vellmark's script was meant to be voiced — that writing silent was how the erasure had worked in the first place, ink without breath, record without witness.

"*I am Oscar Voss.*"

The poison reached for her name and found it defended.

"*I was born in Saltmarsh Row, third house from the tide-gate.*" The poison pulled at that — and staggered, because the words were anchored twice, once in her and once in the script, and the script belonged to a country that had spent two hundred years refusing, in its own grammar, to be forgotten.

"*I have tasted four hundred and thirteen dishes.*" Her handwriting wobbled. Blood ran off her knuckles onto the table and the drops, she saw dimly, were forming characters of their own, curling into the grain of the wood like seeds into soil. "*I kept accounts for thieves. I am sorry. I kept accounts, and the accounts are open now, and every entry will be read aloud.*"

The poison surged. She felt it find the loose thread of her — some small memory, the smell of the guild cellar, the exact weight of a ledger in her hands — and begin pulling, and she wrote faster, cutting the letters deeper, saying them louder, Ashka's voice rising with hers now, chanting the alphabet in counterpoint, the girl's silver script blazing under her skin like dawn under ice:

"*I choose to remember. I choose it unpaid. I choose it untraded. Let it be worth nothing and mine.*"

The door clicked.

Not opened — *clicked*, the sound of a lock recognizing its key, and the key had never been the immunity at all. The immunity had been the fee, the old debt settled, the ledger balanced. The key was a literate tongue. The key was a woman who had learned a dead country's alphabet at the cost of her childhood and could now speak it in flesh and blood and knife-cut skin.

The forgetting-poison broke over her like a wave over a seawall and found no purchase anywhere, because there was no blank page left in her to write on. She had filled herself in. Every line.

She came back to herself on her knees in the ash, the knife still in her hand, the back of her left hand a raw map of silver-edged cuts that were already healing wrong — healing *into* letters, scar tissue settling into permanent script. Ashka was holding her shoulders. Maren was holding Ashka. Somewhere far away her heart was beating fast and clean, unprotected for the first time in eleven years, and every poison in the world was suddenly, permanently, a matter of luck.

And she had never felt safer in her life.

Because safety, she understood now, had never been the immunity. The immunity was just a price. What kept you alive was what was written on you, and by whom, and whether you'd agreed to it.

"Can you stand?" Maren asked hoarsely.

"I can stand." She got up. The door waited, brass latch dull in the grey light, and beyond it — she could feel it now, the way you feel weather coming — beyond it was the world that had forgotten this place, the world of crowns and inquisitors and Halvard, who was somewhere out there discovering that she had been born on the wrong side of her own warrant.

Ashka touched the latch. Looked up at Oscar. "Once we're through, we can't come back this way. And the fire—" Her voice dropped. "The fire doesn't have long. You know that."

Oscar knew. The dragon-fire in the deep vault, the flame that had sealed the erasure, guttering toward death with every hour — and when it died, everything it had sealed would come unsealed at once, every purchased forgetting refunded in a single catastrophic instant. Corvane wanted to control that moment. Halvard feared it. The whole machinery of two kingdoms was grinding toward a single trembling ember.

But first there was a door, and a child, and a step worn hollow by two hundred years of absent feet.

Oscar Voss, royal food-taster, guild accountant, fugitive, text, lifted the latch — and heard, from far across the ash-field, from the direction of the deep vault, a sound she had no ledger-entry for: a vast indrawn breath, like a fire deciding, at the very bottom of its last strength, whether to flare or to die.

She opened the door.
