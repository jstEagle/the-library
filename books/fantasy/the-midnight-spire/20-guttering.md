## 20. Guttering

The ember was the size of a sparrow's heart.

That was the first thing Oscar thought, kneeling on the cold stone of Vellmark's deepest vault, watching the last dragon-fire gutter in its iron dish. All this — the ledgers, the vaults, the treaties, the centuries of careful forgetting — and it had come down to something you could cup in two hands. Something that trembled when she breathed near it.

Ashka knelt beside her. The girl had stopped shivering an hour ago, which worried Oscar more than the shivering had. Cold was a complaint. Cold meant you still expected the world to be kind.

"It's smaller," Ashka said. Not a question. She had been counting all night, the way she counted everything — doors, stairs, the fading sounds of market-day carried on air that should have been empty. "It was bigger when we came down."

"Fire eats what it's given." Oscar kept her voice steady. Taster's discipline. You never let your face tell the prince his soup is death; you never let your face tell a child her country is dying twice. "And it hasn't been given anything in four hundred years."

"That's not true." Ashka turned to look at her, and in the ember-light her eyes were the color of the old ledger pages, that amber that might have been gold once or might only have been old. "It's been eating me my whole life. Slowly. A memory at a time. That's why I'm the index and not just the girl who carries it." She held up one small hand, fingers spread. "The fire keeps Vellmark sealed. Sealing takes fuel. I am the fuel."

Oscar had known this. She had read it in the ledger in Corvane's hand, three days and one lifetime ago: *The index sustains the seal; the seal sustains the asset.* She had read it as arithmetic then. Hearing it from Ashka's mouth, in that language older than kingdoms, translated into words a child would choose — *I am the fuel* — was a different thing entirely.

"How much longer?" Oscar asked.

Ashka considered. She always considered; she had the patience of someone who had been waiting four hundred years to be understood. "If nothing feeds it? Three days. Maybe four. Then it goes out anyway, and everything it sealed comes loose at once." She looked up. "Every treaty. Every bought forgetting. Every name. All of it, unsealed in a single hour, with no one ready for it."

"And if we feed it?"

"Then Vellmark stays alive." Ashka's voice did something careful there, the way voices do when they are carrying something fragile across a long distance. "Not restored. Alive the way a thing in a jar is alive. Kept. Owned. Tradable." Her chin lifted. "You'd have to feed it me. My memories. The ones I got back when Corvane died — the scholars' names, my mother's kitchen, the word for the space between the door and the wall. It would eat those, and keep eating, and I'd go back to being a book instead of a girl."

*The guild trades in stability,* Corvane had said, standing in the ash-field with the wind taking his voice apart, and he had almost been right. He had been right the way poison is food, if you define food loosely enough.

* * *

They sat with it for a long time. There was no hurry, which was its own cruelty; the fire gave them exactly enough time to understand what they were choosing.

Oscar found herself cataloguing, because cataloguing was what she did, the way other people prayed. The vault around them was Vellmark's treasury once, before the erasure, and the guild had never bothered to disguise it. Shelves of sealed jars along the walls — memories brokered from the dying nation, bottled sunsets over its harbors, a grandmother's recipe for honey-bread, the sound of a specific bell on a specific morning. Dust on every jar except the path Corvane had walked, which was worn smooth by greed.

And in the center, the iron dish. The dragon-fire.

It had been magnificent once. She could see the scorch-fan on the ceiling above it, black petals radiating out twenty feet in every direction, the mark of a flame that had needed a cathedral to hold it. The fire that had burned the records of Vellmark's existence — every map, every census, every treaty-page that admitted the country had ever been — while the crown paid and the guild brokered and no one, anywhere, was allowed to say the name aloud.

Now it was a sparrow's heart. Now it flickered when Ashka moved too fast, hungry and afraid, and Oscar hated herself for feeling sorry for it.

"You could put it out now," Ashka said quietly. "Your hands are closer than mine."

"I know where they are, yes."

"You're thinking about it. I can see your mouth doing the thing it does when you're tasting something you haven't decided about yet."

Oscar laughed despite herself — a short, cracked sound in the dark. "My mouth has opinions. It's the most honest part of me. It's spent thirty years being lied to by everything else." She looked at her own hands, palms up in the ember-light. Hands that had tasted hemlock and survived. Hands that had paid passage through impossible doors, dish by dish, immunity bleeding out of her like warmth from an open window, until the poisons of the world stood at her threshold again with their hats in their hands.

She had maybe a season left, unprotected. Halvard's people were still hunting them. Maren was dead or worse. And here, in the bottom of everything, sat the choice the whole rotten architecture of her life had been building toward.

Feed the fire, and Ashka went back to being an index. A jar. An asset on a ledger with Oscar's own handwriting in the margins.

Let it die, and the truth came loose all at once — unmanaged, unmourned, unprepared-for. Wars had started over less than a border redrawn honestly. And Vellmark itself would stay dead either way; the fire's dying wouldn't raise one stone of its capital, wouldn't refill one harbor. It would only stop the pretending.

"Tell me again," Oscar said, "what happens if it dies slowly instead. If we don't decide. If we just leave."

"Then it starves over three days, and the unsealing happens anyway, but ragged. Some things break free clean. Some things come loose wrong — half-memories, grief without names attached. People wake up crying and don't know why." Ashka hugged her knees. "My mother said that's the worst way. She was a scholar. She used to argue about it with the others, before they decided about me." A pause. "She voted against me becoming the index. She lost."

*The erased were not passive victims.* Ashka had said that in the ash-field, standing over the ghost of the marketplace, and Oscar had believed her. Believing it now, in the dark, with the fire between them like a question — belief cost more.

* * *

"Ashka."

"Mm."

"When you volunteered — you were eight. Same age you are now."

"Seven," Ashka said. "I lied upward. Everyone does, in a crisis." She unfolded slightly, turning so the ember lit half her face. "You want to know if I regret it."

"I want to know what you'd choose now. Not seven-year-old Ashka. Not the index. You."

The girl was quiet for long enough that the fire's crackle became a sound with texture, a tiny dry voice saying the same syllable over and over, *please, please, please*, or maybe *feed me*, or maybe nothing at all — fire didn't mean things. Oscar had learned that the hard way, tasting for a court that wanted meaning in everything.

"If you'd asked me a month ago," Ashka said finally, "in the vault, when you first came down the stairs with your lamp — I'd have said keep it burning. Because keeping it burning meant Vellmark wasn't finished. As long as the fire lived, the country lived, somewhere, somehow, even in a jar. Even owned." Her jaw set, and for a moment she looked every year of four hundred. "But I've watched you since then. You pay for things. Everything costs you, and you pay anyway, and you write it down honestly in the ledger after. That's the opposite of the guild. The guild makes other people pay and hides the bill." She pointed a small finger at the ember. "That thing isn't Vellmark. It's the lock on Vellmark. And I am tired of being a lock."

"So we let it die."

"I think—" Ashka stopped. Started again, slower, the way she did when translating from her first language. "I think a country that can only survive by eating a child was already dead, and the fire is just the part of it that won't admit it. I think my mother knew that, and voted for me anyway because she couldn't bear to be the one to say it out loud. I think—" and here her voice finally broke, small and furious, "—I think I want my kitchen back. The real one. Not the memory of it. And that's impossible, so I'll take the second-best thing, which is everyone remembering it was real."

Oscar reached out and pulled the girl against her side. Ashka came stiffly, then not stiffly at all, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the fire said *please, please, please*, and the dust settled through the ember-light like snow that had taken four hundred years to fall.

* * *

But knowing the answer and speaking it were different countries, and there was a border to cross between them.

Because Oscar Voss had spent her whole life inside ledgers, and she knew what a debt looked like from both sides of the page. If the fire died unmanaged, the truth would land on thousands of people who hadn't chosen it. Halvard, waking to a childhood she'd never known was stolen. Border towns whose charters dissolved overnight. Children — other children, ordinary children — learning their grandfathers had signed the forgetting into law.

Corvane's arithmetic had been cold, but it hadn't been empty. That was the trap of him. He had been wrong the way a load-bearing wall is wrong: catastrophically, and only if you removed it without building anything underneath.

"We can't just snuff it," Oscar said at last. "We owe the truth a better entrance than that."

"No," Ashka agreed. "But we can't feed it forever, either. Forever means me."

"Then we do neither. We let it die on our terms, not its hunger's." Oscar stood, knees cracking, and looked down at the ember with the appraising eye she usually saved for suspect soups. "How long to move it? Can it be moved?"

"Everything in the guild can be moved. That's the whole point of the guild." Ashka scrambled up beside her. "Why?"

"Because it doesn't die down here, alone, in the dark, where the only witnesses are us and the dust." Oscar was pacing now, the old ledger-keeper's rhythm, thought turning into motion. "It dies in the open. In the ash-field, where Vellmark stood. With names read over it. With Halvard there, if we can get her — and we can, because she's half of Vellmark herself whether she knows it yet or not. With the defectors. With anyone Maren's people can pull together in two days." She turned to Ashka. "Let it gutter in front of witnesses. Let the last thing it does be give the country back instead of sell it."

Ashka stared at her. Then she smiled — the full, unguarded version, the one that made her look seven instead of ancient — and it was like a lamp coming up in a cellar.

"You're going to make its death an entry in the new ledger."

"The first public one. Witnessed. Dated. No price column." Oscar felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight since chapter one of her life, some knot she'd assumed was structural. "A record instead of a trade. That's the whole idea, isn't it? That's what you've been teaching me in that alphabet of yours, one childhood memory per letter."

"Not one anymore." Ashka took her hand. "You've paid enough. From here, we both write for free."

The ember chose that moment to flare — a single pulse, brighter, almost the old gold — and both of them flinched toward it, protective, absurd, mourning a thing that was still alive.

"It heard us," Ashka whispered.

"Fire doesn't hear."

"This one does. It was a dragon once, or the end of one, or the promise of one. Things that were promises stay listening." Ashka tilted her head, listening to something Oscar couldn't catch. Then her face changed. "Someone's coming down the stair. Two someones. One of them limps."

Oscar's hand went to the knife she'd stopped needing and started carrying anyway. "Friend?"

"Limping friend or limping enemy," Ashka said. "In Vellmark's language, those are the same word until they choose."

The footsteps grew louder. And out of the dark of the stairwell, leaning hard on a broken spear-staff, bloodied at the temple but grinning like a man who had cheated at cards and meant to do it again, came Maren.

"They told me you were dead," Oscar said.

"They tell everyone that. It's cheaper than a funeral." He lowered himself onto a shelf of bottled memories, dislodging four hundred years of dust, and nodded at the ember with something close to reverence. "Halvard's with me. She's slow — she stopped at the top of the stairs and cried for ten minutes, which she'd deny under torture. She wants to see it happen." His grin faded into something steadier, older. "Two days, Oscar. I scattered the word through every borrowed room I could still open. People are coming. Scholars' grandchildren. Charter-towns. A few guild defectors with guilty pockets. They're coming to watch the fire die and hear the names."

Ashka squeezed Oscar's hand hard enough to hurt, which was how she said important things.

Above them, muffled by stone, faint as market-day sounds over an empty field, they could hear the first arrivals gathering — boots on ash, voices finding each other in the dark, the beginning of a crowd, the beginning of a witness.

The ember pulsed again. Smaller now. Braver, somehow.

"Tomorrow," Ashka said, to Oscar, to Maren, to the fire, to everyone who had ever been erased and everyone who had done the erasing. "At dawn. We read the names."
