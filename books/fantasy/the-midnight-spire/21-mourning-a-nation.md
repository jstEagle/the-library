## 21. Mourning a Nation

The fire did not go out all at once.

That was the cruelty of it — or the mercy; Oscar could never decide which. It guttered the way a candle gutters, leaning toward its own death and then straightening, as if remembering some older promise. The dragon-fire in the deep vault had burned for three hundred years on a diet of sealed truths, and now that the seals were broken, it had nothing left to eat but itself.

They stood in a ring around the brazier: Oscar, Ashka, Maren with his bandaged hands, Halvard in armor she no longer had any right to, and two dozen guild defectors who had chosen, in the space of one terrible week, to stop being traders of the impossible and start being witnesses.

"Someone should say something," Maren said.

"No one knows what to say," said Oscar. "That's rather the point."

Ashka stepped forward. She was nine years old and four thousand years old, depending on how you counted, and she held her small hands out over the flame the way you'd cup water to drink from a spring.

"It doesn't want to die," she said. "But wanting isn't living. I learned that in Vellmark." She looked up at Oscar. "You have to tell it. It's your ledger now."

Oscar's throat closed. She thought of every dish she'd ever tasted, every poison catalogued in her body like coins in a purse. She thought of hemlock laced with something older than any poison she knew — the signature from her own recorded page. She thought of immunity spent dish by dish, memory by memory, until the woman standing here was mostly scar tissue and borrowed words.

She knelt beside the brazier.

"You were paid for," she told the fire. "Everything you burned was paid for by people who wanted the world smaller. You kept their secrets warm. And now we're going to let you go cold, because a secret kept is not the same as a truth honored."

The flame bowed low — almost out — then rose one final time, tall and blue-white, and in its heart they all saw it: Vellmark. The spires of its capital before the ash. The market-day crowds. A harbor full of ships whose names existed nowhere else. It lasted three seconds. Then it collapsed into an ember, and the ember into grey, and the grey into nothing at all.

The vault was dark. No one moved.

Then Ashka began to speak — not the pre-kingdom tongue this time, but plain trade-speech, the language of the kingdom that had swallowed her country. She spoke the first name aloud.

"Solveig Ansdottir, lamplighter of the Copper Quarter."

And Maren answered, the way you answer a call in the dark: "Remembered."

"Halvard Ericsdottir, ferryman's daughter, born in Vellmark before the forgetting."

From the darkness, Dame Halvard's voice cracked like ice on a pond. "Remembered."

They went through the night. Names from ledgers opened for the first time in three centuries. Names Ashka carried in her blood. Four hundred and eleven names before dawn came down through the vault grates, and when the last one faded, no one had slept, and everyone had wept at least once, and the grief had changed shape — from a locked thing into an open one.

* * *

The crown did not take it well.

Of course it didn't. Truths about erased nations are never convenient, and this one sat at the center of six treaties, two royal marriages, and a border whose very existence depended on Vellmark having never been. Within a month of the ledgers going public — Maren's doing, carried out in broad daylight on the steps of the old guildhouse while Corvane's remaining loyalists watched in impotent fury — the palace sent summons.

Halvard went alone.

Oscar waited outside the audience chamber, in the corridor where she'd once tasted princes' dinners without anyone knowing her name, and tried not to imagine what was happening behind the doors. She heard fragments. Halvard's voice, level as a surveyor's: *I was born there. My memories were purchased. I have testimony, documents, and a name.* The chancellor's voice, smooth as oil: *Dame Halvard, surely you understand the instability—*

"I understand," Halvard said, loud enough to carry through oak, "that stability purchased with forgetting is just forgetting wearing better clothes. I am done selling it."

She walked out past Oscar without breaking stride, stripped of her office in eleven minutes, and smiled for the first time since Oscar had known her.

"Well," she said. "Now I'm nobody. What a relief."

"You'll be arrested within the day."

"Probably." She rolled her shoulders like a woman setting down a heavy pack. "But they'll have to arrest me publicly, which means acknowledging the question exists. That's how it starts, taster. Not with restoration. With acknowledgment. Everything else takes generations, and we won't live to see it, and that's fine." Her eyes, which had always been weapons, were tired now and strangely gentle. "My mother named me for a city that officially never existed. Someone should write that down somewhere permanent."

"It's already in the ledger," Oscar said. "Number four hundred and twelve. I wrote it the morning after."

Halvard stopped walking. For a moment the inquisitor — the ex-inquisitor — pressed the back of her hand to her mouth and stood very still in the palace corridor.

"Thank you," she managed.

"Don't thank me. Testify. They can arrest you on the steps; make sure there's a crowd."

There was. There was a crowd so large that the arrest became a spectacle, the spectacle became a scandal, and the scandal became the first crack in the official history of the realm in three hundred years. Halvard spoke from the scaffold with her hands bound, and half the crowd wept, and the other half argued, and arguing was enough. Arguing meant the name Vellmark was in ten thousand mouths by nightfall, and mouths are the oldest archives there are.

* * *

The guildhouse changed too.

It was strange, watching it happen — stranger than the trial, stranger than the fire. The cellar archive where Oscar had balanced accounts of traded memories and bottled sunsets was emptied shelf by shelf, and the shelves themselves were carried up into the light. Maren organized the work with his ruined hands and his stubborn grin, cataloguing everything, sealing nothing.

"The Guild of Impossible Things," he announced to the assembled former members, "is hereby dissolved. In its place: the Archive of Impossible Things. Same inventory. Different rules. Nothing here gets bought. Nothing here gets sold. Anyone may read. No one may remove."

An old broker in the back — one of Corvane's generation — raised a tremulous hand. "And when someone wants to trade? When the hunger comes? It always comes, boy. It came for Corvane. It will come for us."

Maren didn't flinch. "Then we remember what happened to Corvane. He died full of everything he'd ever bought and owned by none of it. That's the business model now. We keep the stories. We don't keep them locked."

Corvane's death was still spoken of in whispers. Consumed by every memory he'd ever brokered — thousands of lives pouring through him at once, all the stolen sunsets and first kisses and vanished places reclaiming themselves in a single terrible instant. They'd found nothing left of him but a chair and a faint smell of rain over dry ground, which the archivists agreed, after long debate, to record as *evidence of Vellmark's coastal climate, circa the erasure.*

Even his ending had become a document.

* * *

Ashka taught on the steps.

It started with three children from the neighborhood who thought the strange symbols chalked on the guildhouse stones were a game. Then nine children. Then adults came, pretending they were only passing by, lingering at the edge of hearing while Ashka sounded out the pre-kingdom alphabet in her clear, unembarrassed voice.

"This character," she'd say, drawing a looping mark like a bird's footprint, "means *the place where a river remembers being a lake*. Your language doesn't have a word for it. That's why you need mine."

Oscar supervised from the doorway, ostensibly keeping order, actually memorizing. Every character still cost her — that debt didn't vanish with the fire; the memories she'd paid were simply gone, childhood afternoons traded away forever — but writing the script no longer took anything new. Ashka had remade her in the threshold, letter by letter, and what remained of Oscar Voss belonged to herself.

Some evenings, after the lessons, they walked together through the ash-field where Vellmark's capital had stood. The market-day sounds were gone now, fully faded, the ghost finally quiet. But wildflowers had begun to come up through the ash — real ones, ordinary ones, daisies and vetch, growing where no seed had been planted — and Halvard, released on technicality after her testimony turned public sympathy into political cover, swore she remembered the fields being yellow with them.

"You can't restore a country," Oscar said once, watching Ashka run laughing through the flowers with the local children chasing her. "We let the fire die for nothing?"

"We let it die so it wouldn't be for sale," Halvard said. "Those aren't the same thing. Restoration would've been another trade — a nation as an asset, bought by whoever paid most. What we have instead is worse and better. We have grief. We have names. We have a nine-year-old teaching dead letters on a doorstep." She nodded toward the children. "Look at her. She's not restoring anything. She's *continuing*. That's the only immortality there ever was."

* * *

On the first anniversary of the guttering, they held no ceremony.

Instead, the Archive opened its doors at dawn and did not close them until midnight, and people came all day — scholars, skeptics, the merely curious, and, quietly, in ones and twos, people who lingered too long over certain pages and left with wet eyes and set jaws. Descendants of the displaced. Families who'd inherited houses built on land that had a different name once. One old woman stood before the ledger of purchased memories for two hours, then asked, very softly, if her grandmother's laugh might be among them.

Maren found it for her. Entry 3,091. He couldn't return it — what was traded was traded — but he read the description aloud to her, and the old woman listened the way you listen at a graveside, and thanked him, and left upright.

That evening, Oscar climbed to the roof of the old guildhouse with Ashka, and they ate a simple supper of bread, cheese, and late plums, and Oscar tasted every bite with the unprotected tongue of an ordinary mortal woman, and nothing in it was poisoned, and nothing in it was hidden, and it was, she thought, the finest meal of her life.

"Do you miss it?" Ashka asked. "Being able to taste lies?"

"No," Oscar said, and found it was true. "I miss my mother's kitchen. I traded that away learning your alphabet. But missing things is allowed now. That's what we bought with all of it — permission to grieve instead of permission to forget."

Ashka leaned her head against Oscar's shoulder. Below them, lamplighters were moving through the streets — and one of them paused beneath a lamp-post, checked a small book against the flame, and made a mark, and Oscar thought: *Solveig Ansdottir, lamplighter of the Copper Quarter. Remembered.*

"Oscar," Ashka said sleepily. "Tomorrow, can we start the advanced class? There are characters I haven't taught anyone yet. Old ones. From before even me."

Oscar frowned. "Before you? The index was supposed to hold everything."

"That's what I thought too." Ashka's voice had gone quiet and strange, awake now despite the hour. "But I've been reading the deepest ledger — the one the fire couldn't finish burning. And there are pages in it, Oscar. Pages written in a hand I don't recognize, describing places even Vellmark forgot. Places the fire was never meant to seal." She looked up, and her eyes reflected the lamplight like small flames of their own. "*Someone else was erased first.*"

Oscar set down her plum. Somewhere below, a lamp flickered and steadied, and the night went on being ordinary, which suddenly felt like the thinnest possible skin stretched over something vast.

"First lesson tomorrow, then," she said. "Bring the pages."

And far across the city, in the Archive's deepest shelf, a book that had been silent for centuries turned its own page.
