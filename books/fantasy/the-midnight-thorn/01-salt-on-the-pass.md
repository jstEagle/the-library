## 1. Salt on the Pass

The wind came off the high crags smelling of iron and old snow, and Ivo Kavanagh tasted it and knew they would have weather by morning. Not the soft grey drizzle of the lowlands — something with teeth. He crouched at the lip of the pass, pressed his bare palm flat against the stone, and let himself listen.

The rock said nothing useful. Stone rarely did. It remembered only weight and cold, centuries of it, the patient memory of a mountain that had never once been in a hurry. But beneath that deep slow drone he felt the faint tremor of water moving where no water should be — the melt-channels shifting under the trail ahead, the way they did before a thaw-quake. Three days, maybe four.

He rose, brushed the frost from his knees, and turned back down the switchbacks to where the caravan waited in the blue hour before dawn.

Nine mules. Fourteen souls. Forty chests of salt packed in oiled canvas, because salt was cheaper to smuggle than sin and worth nearly as much on the far side of Thornfell. And one of those fourteen souls, he was now almost certain, had sold them all to the Crown's men waiting at Greywater Ford.

"Trail holds," he told Denna Marsh, the caravan mistress, a broad woman with a face like a slammed door. "But we take the low fork past the Widow's Teeth, not the ridge road."

"The ridge is faster."

"The ridge is dead by Thursday." He nodded up toward the peaks, where the stars were being eaten by cloud. "Thaw-quake coming. The melt-channels under the ridge road are already running. You'd feel it if you put your hand to the ground."

Denna looked at him the way she always did — half suspicion, half hunger for the certainty she was paying him for. Seven years he had guided caravans through these mountains, and seven years she and every other master of the trade had learned not to ask how the quiet man in the grey cloak knew what the mountains intended. They called it luck, when they spoke of it at all. Luck was safe. Luck could be bought.

"Low fork, then." She spat into the scree. "Wake the others. We move within the hour."

* * *

They moved, and the mountain closed around them like a hand.

Ivo walked point, as he always did, close enough to the lead mule to smell its sour breath, far enough ahead that his lantern threw shadows instead of casting them. The trail wound between walls of thorn-pine so dense and black that even starlight gave them up as lost, and the only sounds were the click of hooves on stone, the creak of harness, and the long slow respiration of Thornfell itself — a sound he had stopped hearing years ago, the way a man stops hearing his own heartbeat.

His god had been silent for seven years.

That was the thought that came to him on every trail, in every hour before dawn, unbidden and unwelcome as an old scar aching before rain. Veyra, the Quiet One, the god of thresholds and crossings, whose voice had once come to him soft as breath against the nape of his neck: *This way, my own. Step where I step.* He had been nineteen, newly sworn, kneeling in a shrine hardly bigger than a coffin, and the voice had filled the little stone room like water fills a cup.

Then, one morning seven years ago, nothing. No voice. No presence. Just Ivo, alone with a gift that had kept growing anyway, like a fire left burning in an empty house.

He had wondered — in the honest hours, the ones nobody else saw — whether the silence was not abandonment but sentence. Whether the gift itself was the punishment. Because that was the truth he carried up and down these mountains, wrapped in oilcloth like the salt: he could touch a thing and read its memories. A knife remembered its killings. A door remembered who had knocked. A locket remembered everything its wearer had ever whispered to it.

And every time he used the gift, he heard, faint and far away, something that was almost weeping.

"Kavanagh." One of the drovers, a lean Corvess man called Pell, had drawn level with him. "You want me to spell you at point?"

"No."

"You've been walking since midnight."

"I know the ground better than sleep knows me." Ivo glanced sideways. Pell had the look of a man rehearsing a question, turning it over like a coin he wasn't sure was real. "Ask it or don't."

Pell chewed on it a moment longer. "There's talk in the camp. Back at Thornhollow, before we set out. Criers in the city squares. They're saying the crown's declared a hunt." His voice dropped, though there was nothing ahead of them but mules and pines. "For gifted folk. All of them. Seizure on sight, examination, whatever that means. They say the king signed it himself."

The trail seemed to tilt, very slightly, though Ivo knew it was only the world tilting inside him.

"Talk," he said.

"Criers don't lie. They just shout." Pell studied him with the frank, incurious stare of a man who had decided Ivo Kavanagh was exactly as boring as he appeared. "Anyway. Thought you'd want to know. You've got a look about you sometimes, like a man listening for something."

"I'm listening for thaw-water," Ivo said. "Go back to your mule."

But his heart had begun, quietly, to hammer, and did not stop for the next three miles.

* * *

They made camp at noon in the shadow of the Widow's Teeth, three granite spires rising out of the treeline like fingers frozen mid-reach. Denna set watches, posted the salt-guards, and got a cookfire going small and smokeless in a hollow of stone. Fourteen people ate hard bread and salt pork in a silence that had edges to it.

Ivo did not eat. He sat apart, sharpening a knife he did not need sharpened, and watched.

It was habit more than suspicion, at first. He had known one of them was wrong since the ford at Greywater, two days back — had felt it in the way the camp sat, the way eyes moved. Someone had been too interested in the route. Someone had asked, casually, over supper, whether they'd cross at Greywater Ford or push for the bridge at Marrowgate. And this morning, while the others broke camp, someone had knelt by the fire-ring a moment too long, doing nothing, touching nothing —

Touching.

He closed his eyes. *Don't*, said the part of him that still hoped Veyra's silence meant the gift would someday starve. *Not yet. Sleep on it.*

But the gift was not a thing he could leave sleeping, any more than he could leave his own heartbeat sleeping. And besides — fourteen lives, forty chests of salt, and Crown soldiers at the ford. If the hunt had truly been declared, then betrayal here was not theft. It was a gallows, delivered early.

After the midday meal, the caravan scattered to rest. Blankets, pipes, muttered cards. And Ivo walked the camp slowly, a man stretching his legs, until he came to the drover Tam Wicker — young, cheerful Tam, who sang off-key on the climbs and shared his tobacco too freely — sitting against a pine with a bit of bread in one hand and, around his neck, on a leather cord, a small silver locket.

Tam wore it always. Everyone knew that. Everyone also knew it held a portrait of a girl in Corvess he meant to marry, and everyone had laughed about it kindly enough, once.

Ivo sat down beside him. "Bit of bread to spare?"

"Always, for the man who keeps us off the ridges." Tam tore the loaf, easy and warm, and handed it over. And Ivo took it with his bare right hand, and as their fingers brushed, he let his gift reach — gently, the way you dip a ladle into another man's well — into the locket at Tam's throat.

Metal remembers. Silver best of all; it drinks light and keeps it.

He fell into the locket's memory the way a man falls into cold water.

*A girl laughing in a doorway, flour on her hands. Tam's own hands opening the clasp. Warmth. Then — later, much later, lamplight and a different room — Tam holding the open locket over a table and speaking to it in a whisper, because he could not speak to a man's face: "Greywater Ford. They cross at Greywater. The tall woman will send two riders ahead tonight, I swear it on my mother, I swear it—" And a man's voice answering, smooth as tallow: "And the reward will keep your girl in bread for ten years, Wicker. Ten years. Think of her kitchen."*

Ivo opened his eyes. The afternoon had not moved. Tam was still smiling at him, tearing bread, warm as a hearthstone, and the locket lay against his chest catching the thin mountain light, and inside it the girl with flour on her hands smiled out of a portrait painted two years before anyone had ever offered to buy a man's soul for her sake.

"That's good bread," Ivo said, and his voice did not shake, and he marveled distantly at that. "Thank you."

He stood. Walked to the fire. Told Denna, quietly, that they would move within the hour — and that she should have Pell and the brothers Osk walk behind Tam Wicker, and watch his hands.

She looked at him for a long moment. She had paid him for seven years precisely because he had never once been wrong, and she did not ask questions she suspected she did not want answered.

"If he's sold us—"

"He hasn't finished selling us yet. Move fast and we outrun the letter he sent."

"And Tam?"

Ivo looked across the camp to where the young man sat humming to himself, off-key, sharing out the last of his tobacco with the man whose life he had priced at ten years of bread.

"That depends," Ivo said, "on what kind of kingdom we're walking through."

Because the criers were true — Pell had not lied about that; liars don't tremble — and somewhere south of these peaks a king had signed a decree that made a traitor's silver the cheapest thing at any crossroads. And Ivo understood, standing in the smoke-smell of a dying cookfire with the first flakes of the storm beginning to sift down through the pines, that the mountains would not hold this trouble long. Trouble like this climbed. Trouble like this followed the trails he himself had spent seven years learning.

He touched the thorn-iron pendant at his own throat — the sigil of Veyra, worn smooth as river glass — and listened, as he did each night, for the god who had named him once upon a dark and given him a gift he had never wanted.

Silence. Only silence, and under it, very far away, that sound that might be weeping.

"Was this what you meant?" he asked the mountain, softly. "Or did I steal it, the way everything gets stolen in Thornfell?"

The snow thickened. Below them, miles south, a rider was already spurring for Greywater Ford with a letter in his coat. And above, on the highest crag where the clouds broke for a single breath, Ivo saw — or thought he saw — a vast shape wheeling against the last light, wings like torn banners, gone before his eye could hold it.

Something old had moved over Thornfell. And Ivo Kavanagh, who read the memories of stones and lockets and chains, found that for the first time in seven years, he was afraid of what he might be about to remember.
