## 2. The King's Decree

Corvess smelled of fish brine, tar, and too many people pressed into too little space. Ivo Kavanagh had been in the city three days and already missed the thin cold air of Thornfell, where the worst thing you could smell was your own fear.

He kept to the margins of the Salt Market, hood up, buying nothing. A man who bought nothing drew fewer eyes. He watched the stalls instead — the oyster-wives shucking with quick red hands, a boy hawking candied ginger, the fat spice merchant whose scales always lied by two grains on the buyer's side. Ordinary commerce. Ordinary lies. The kind of dishonesty that stayed honest because everyone knew about it.

Then the bells began.

They came from the Harbor Watch tower first, then the Bellhouse of Saint Verene, then every small chapel bell between, until the sound braided itself into one long iron complaint rolling across the rooftops. The market stilled the way water stills before a squall. Oyster knives stopped mid-stroke.

The criers came up from the quay in a line of six, wearing royal blue sashes over grey wool, each flanked by a soldier in the king's livery — deep green and gold, the stag-and-crown of House Aldmere. One climbed onto the fountain platform at the market's center, unrolled a scroll heavy enough to need both hands, and read in the flat, carrying voice of a man paid not to care what he said.

*"By decree of His Majesty, King Aldous III, rightful sovereign of Corvess and all her territories: it has come to the Crown's attention that certain persons within the realm possess unlawful affinities — arts of knowing, of binding, of unmaking, which threaten the peace and sanctity of the throne."*

Ivo's hand went still on his own belt buckle. He made himself breathe through his nose, slow, counting the gulls overhead like a man bored.

*"All such persons are hereby commanded to present themselves for examination at the Hall of Registers within thirty days. Those who do not shall be sought. Those who are found shall be seized. Those who resist shall be answered as traitors are answered."*

The soldier beside the crier shifted his halberd, and the morning sun caught the blade's edge, throwing a sliver of light across the crowd like a warning drawn in fire.

*"Reward is offered for true report of gifted persons: ten crowns silver per name verified, fifty per seizure accomplished. Let no citizen harbor what the Crown must examine. The King sees all. The King protects all."*

A beat of silence. Then the market exhaled and went back to its noise — but changed, the way a room changes when someone new enters it. Voices dropped. Glances slid sideways. An oyster-wife looked at the boy selling ginger a moment too long, and the boy noticed, and moved to the far end of his row.

Ten crowns a name. Fifty a body. In the Salt Market, where half the district lived on less than that in a season, the decree had just turned every neighbor into a purse waiting to be opened.

Ivo pulled his hood lower and walked.

* * *

He did not go straight to the safehouse. He took the long way, down Rope Street where the riggers' ropes hung like jungle vines, past the chandlery, through the covered fish auction hall where the smell alone could strip paint. Old habit. When you had something worth hiding, you never let anyone learn your corners.

The gift had come on him at nine years old, the day he'd picked up his dead father's hammer and felt the man's whole life pour through the handle — the sweat, the swearing, the particular joy of a joint fitted true. His mother had called it Veyra's touch. The silent god's mark, she'd said, proof he was seen even if the god herself never spoke. Seven years now the god had said nothing back. Seven years since the last time he'd prayed and felt anything answer except his own echo.

And now the crown wanted him for it. Not blessed. Not marked. Unlawful.

He reached the safehouse — a sailmaker's loft above the chandlery, rented under a name that belonged to a man who'd died at sea two winters past — and let himself in through the roof hatch out of pure spite for whoever might be watching the door.

Bren was sitting at the table, eating bread and hard cheese like a man with no cares, boots up, sword across his knees anyway. Bren Osgar was a head taller than Ivo, built like a door, with a beard going iron-grey early and a laugh that could be heard across a battlefield. They'd bled together in the Border Wars, shared tents, shared blood-feuds, shared the kind of silence between old soldiers that was more honest than talk.

"You look," Bren said around the cheese, "like a man who heard the bells."

"Everyone heard the bells."

"Everyone didn't take the long way here." Bren set down the food. "Sit down, Iv."

"I'm standing fine."

"Suit yourself." Bren leaned back until the chair creaked. "You've heard the rest, then? What the criers don't say?"

"The rest usually isn't good."

"No. It isn't." For a moment Bren's face did something complicated — guilt trying on the mask of practicality, and neither fitting quite right. "The Grey Company took a contract. Crown contract. Sealed, paid in advance, quarter's wages up front."

Ivo felt it start then, cold in the pit of his stomach, though he made himself ask. "What contract?"

"Hunting." Bren met his eyes and didn't flinch, which was its own kind of wound; Bren had never once lied to him in fourteen years, and was choosing not to start now, and somehow that was worse. "Finding them, Iv. The gifted. We're the ones doing the seizing."

* * *

The silence stretched long enough for the cheese to go stale on the table.

"Say it again," Ivo said quietly.

"You heard me."

"I want to hear how you say it."

Bren sighed and stood, pacing the little loft, ducking under the drying sails strung from the rafters. "Fifty men, Iv. Fifty mouths. Winter's coming and the Border Wars are done — nobody's hiring swords to stand around. The Company hasn't eaten crown coin in three years. Then this comes along, sealed by the king himself, wages guaranteed, indemnity against losses, pardon clauses for any member with— " He stopped himself.

"With what," Ivo said. It wasn't a question.

"With affinities." Bren turned to face him. "Any sworn brother of the Grey Company who presents himself voluntarily is exempt from examination. Registered, pardoned, protected. That clause was written in for us. I asked for it, Iv. Before you say anything — I asked for it *for you*. I told the Crown's clerk we might have men with small gifts among us, hedge-talents, nothing wicked, and I got them written out of the hunt. You walk into the Hall of Registers, give your name, get the king's pardon stamped on your skin if they do that sort of thing, and no soldier of Corvess can ever touch you again. You'd be *safe*, damn you. Safer than you've ever been."

"And in exchange the Company drags every other gifted soul in this kingdom to that same hall in chains."

"In exchange, fifty men eat through winter. And their families. And we don't tear ourselves apart wondering who'll inform on whom, because there's a lawful road right there, laid out, paved—"

"A lawful road." Ivo laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Bren. Do you know what 'examination' means? Have you seen anyone come back out of the Hall of Registers?"

"They're examined. Questioned. Catalogued. It's the king's own decree—"

"I've read things, Bren." He couldn't say *I read objects*, not yet, not even to Bren — the oath to Veyra sat on his tongue like a stone, and some habits of hiding went deeper than law. But he leaned forward, and lowered his voice, and gave his friend the truth sideways, the only way he could carry it. "Smuggling runs through the mountains. Things moving north out of the capital, fast, guarded. Prisoner wagons going in that never come back out. Whatever examination is, the ones who go in aren't walking around after. Something in that palace is hungry, and the decree is how it gets fed."

Bren stared at him. "That's rumor."

"That's freight manifests and guard rotations and the faces of the men driving those wagons. Rumor doesn't pay guards double to keep their mouths shut."

"You can't know—"

"Bren." Ivo stood. Fourteen years of brotherhood stood up with him, and he hated what he was about to do to it. "I have never once lied to you. Have I?"

"No." Flat. Certain.

"Then believe me when I tell you: this contract will end with the Grey Company as jailers. Worse. Whatever's being fed in that palace, our coin comes off the same plate. And the pardon clause—" he tapped the table, once, hard, "—the pardon clause isn't mercy. It's a leash. You don't exempt a man from the hunt unless you intend to use him in it."

Bren's jaw worked. "It's coin, Iv. It's just coin. Men have done uglier for less."

"That's exactly what frightens me. You know that's exactly what frightens me, and you're standing there saying it anyway."

For a long moment neither spoke. Below them, through the floorboards, the chandlery's customers murmured about the decree — a woman wondering aloud if the tinker who came through last month had odd eyes, a man laughing nervously about the reward, the laughter dying halfway through because nobody joined it. The hunt had begun before any hunter moved. It was already inside everyone's mouth.

"I came here," Bren said finally, heavily, "to offer you sanctuary. That was the whole of it. Come to the Company, swear in, take the pardon, and be my brother again under a banner that keeps you alive. That's all I wanted. That's all I *want*." His voice roughened. "I buried too many brothers to bury you too, Iv. Not you."

Ivo looked at him — at the iron-grey beard and the tired eyes and the sword laid across the table like an argument neither would finish — and felt something crack along an old seam. Loyalty wasn't a thing that broke clean, he thought. It tore the way canvas tears: slowly, loudly, all at once, and afterward there was no mending that held weight.

"If I take your pardon," Ivo said, "and tomorrow the Company puts chains on some girl in the lowlands because her bread rises wrong or her chickens won't die — what am I then?"

"A man who ate," Bren said simply. "Winter makes us all smaller than our principles. You know that better than most."

"I used to."

"And now?"

Ivo crossed to the window slit. Down in the street, six more criers were moving toward the Temple Quarter, soldiers flanking them, and behind the soldiers, at a distance, walked a seventh figure in plain grey — not a crier, not a soldier. Just watching. Marking who listened too closely to the decree. Marking who slipped away.

"Now," Ivo said, without turning around, "I think I need to be somewhere the crown's writ runs thin. There's a pass north of here, and a caravan leaving in two days that pays better than your pardon ever will."

"That caravan's run by smugglers, Iv. If the Company's hunting—"

"I know what the Company's hunting." He finally turned. "Which is why you should decide soon, old friend, which side of that hunt you'll be standing on when we meet again. Because we will meet again. Thornfell's not big enough for anything else."

Bren held out his hand — the old grip, the border-grip, wrist to wrist, blood-brothers since the mud at Cael's Ford. Ivo took it. Neither squeezed as hard as they used to.

"Thirty days," Bren said. "Hall of Registers. That offer doesn't rot, whatever you choose. It stays open till the frost closes the passes."

"Everything rots, Bren. Some things just rot slower than others."

He left by the roof hatch, dropping into the alley behind the chandlery, pulling his hood down as the bells began tolling again somewhere across the city — the decree being read a sixth time, a seventh, hammered into every ear in Corvess until ten crowns a name sounded like scripture.

Behind him, faint through the noise, he heard Bren call his name once — *Ivo!* — and then let it go.

He walked north, toward Thornfell, and did not look back.
