## 3. What the Chains Remember

They took him at the Salt Gate checkpoint, three days out of Corvess, where the mountain road narrowed to a throat of rock and the king's soldiers had built their fire barrels into a wall.

Ivo had seen the banners from half a mile off — the crowned stag of House Veylin snapping in the wind that funneled down from Thornfell's shoulders. He could have turned back. Could have taken the smugglers' path through the Ashen Steps, three days harder and twice as cold. But his feet had been pointed toward Corvess since Bren's face had surfaced in his mind like a stone dropped into still water, and so he walked forward, hood up, hands loose, and let the lie build itself around him: a wool merchant, a widower, a man with nothing in his blood but salt and patience.

"Name."

"Ivo Marsh."

The sergeant was young, with a raw pink scar splitting one eyebrow, and eyes that kept flicking to Ivo's hands. That was the thing about the hunt — it made everyone look at everyone else's hands. *Show me your palms. Show me what you've touched.* As if the gift lived in the skin, when it lived nowhere at all, or everywhere.

"Wool?"

"From the Thornfell weavers. Gray and brown, coarse stuff." He patted the mule's packs, which did indeed hold wool, because he had never been fool enough to cross a checkpoint carrying anything but exactly what he claimed. "My wife's brother sells it for me now. She died last spring."

The sergeant's expression softened by a degree. Grief was good currency; it always had been. He waved Ivo through the barrel line, and Ivo led the mule between two braziers, past the second gate —

— and stopped, because the chains were singing.

Not aloud. They never sang aloud. But the iron manacles hanging from the post by the guardhouse, six pairs of them, empty and waiting, were so loud with memory that Ivo's teeth ached. Iron held things. Iron remembered everything pressed against it, every wrist, every struggle, every prayer whispered into cold metal at dawn. Most objects only murmured. These were screaming.

He should have walked on. Seven years of silence from Veyra had taught him caution if nothing else. But his gift had never asked permission, not once, and as he passed the post his bare hand brushed one manacle in passing — an accident, or the shape an accident takes when something older than accident is steering.

The world folded.

*Cold. Snow in his beard, a boy's voice saying* mama, *a door kicked open in the dark, hands wrenched behind him, the bite of this very iron, and then the wagon, and then the palace, and then —*

— the draining. Gods, the *draining*. He saw it whole in a single terrible heartbeat: a chamber beneath the palace where prisoners knelt in a circle around a basin of black glass, and something poured out of them, visible in the way heat shimmer is visible, streaming from their mouths and eyes and ears, rising, coiling upward through a vent shaped like a crown. He felt what the manacle's last wearer had felt as it left him — not pain, worse, a kind of *evacuation*, the self scooped out like marrow from a bone. The man had been a fisherman. His name was Tallow. He had a daughter who laughed like breaking ice. And then he didn't have her anymore, didn't have anything, sat afterward in his cell smiling gently at the wall while his memories traveled up through the palace stone to feed —

—to feed *what?*

Ivo came back to himself standing in the trampled snow, hand still resting on the manacle, and the sergeant was staring at him with the scarred eyebrow climbing.

"You all right, merchant?"

"Just the cold," Ivo said. His voice came out level. He was proud of that, later. "Bad knee. Old wound."

"Mm." The sergeant hadn't moved. Behind him, two soldiers had drifted closer, casual as weather, hands near their swords. "Funny thing about bad knees. They make men touch iron they don't need to touch."

The trap closed the way well-built traps do: quietly, without malice. A soldier lifted the mule's saddlebag and found the thorn-iron charm Ivo had carried since Thornfell — a smuggler's luck piece, nothing more, but thorn-iron answered only to gifted hands, and everyone in the king's service knew it. The sergeant held it up between finger and thumb like a dead bird.

"Well," he said, almost kindly. "Ivo Marsh of nowhere. Let's see what you remember."

* * *

They shackled him with his own history. The manacles they closed on his wrists were old ones, reused dozens of times, and the wagon they locked him in was thick with the residue of everyone who had ridden it before him. He learned to keep his arms stiff and his hands fisted so the iron couldn't press its library into him, but iron is patient, and over four days on the road to Corvess it won again and again, feeding him scraps in the dark:

*A girl reciting a prayer to Veyra before the guards realized and gagged her.*

*An old man laughing, laughing, telling his cellmate that whatever they take, they can't take what matters, and then, weeks later, the same voice asking* what matters?

*A soldier — one of theirs — whispering into the dark of the wagon:* I don't think the king knows. I don't think the king knows what they're doing down there. *And the sound after that of someone weeping without noise, which is the worst sound there is.*

By the third day Ivo understood the architecture of it. The Crown Hunt wasn't cruelty for cruelty's sake. It was harvest. The gifted weren't being imprisoned; they were being *rendered*, their memories rendered down and piped upward like tallow into lamps, and somewhere above, in the high rooms of the palace, something was eating.

Something wearing the crown.

* * *

The interlude — the prisoner:

In the cell across from his, a boy of perhaps sixteen sat with his knees drawn up, rocking. They had taken him at the same checkpoint, a cooper's son caught humming a song that made bread rise, or so the guards joked. His name was Pell. He had not stopped shaking since Corvess.

"They come at night," Pell said, the fourth evening, through the wall. "You hear them coming? The ones with the glass basin? There's a smell. Like rain on hot stone. My da says—" He stopped. Started again, smaller. "My da doesn't say anything anymore. They took him first. Three months ago. When he came back he smiled at me and asked my name. He *asked my name*, and I told him, and he said 'that's nice,' like I was a stranger at market, and they let him go because there was nothing left worth keeping."

Ivo pressed his forehead to the cold stone between them. "Listen to me. What's your name?"

"Pell."

"Say it again. Every morning when you wake, say it. Say your mother's name, your street, the color of your door. Say them until they come, and if they come for you, say them while they're doing it. Names are anchors. Hold to them."

A long silence. Then, very small: "Do names work? Did they ever work for anyone?"

Ivo thought of seven years of silence from his god, of the locket on the pass, of every memory he'd ever read and every one he'd never be able to give back.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "But I'm going to find out what's taking them. And then I'm going to burn it."

Pell laughed — a wet, cracked sound. "You and what army?"

"Whatever's left when the truth gets loose," Ivo said, and heard the boy laugh again, differently, and hated himself for offering hope he couldn't yet weigh.

* * *

On the sixth day they came for him, but not for the basin — for the throne room, which frightened him more. Prisoners went down to the black glass. Only special prisoners went up.

They dressed him in a clean shirt, which somehow felt obscene, and marched him through corridors that grew quieter and richer as they climbed, until the air itself seemed to hold its breath. Two guards flanked a set of doors banded in silver. Beyond them, he could feel it — even without touching anything, he could feel it, a wrongness like a note played below hearing, a hunger with a shape.

One of the guards leaned close as the doors opened. "Kneel when the herald speaks. Eyes down. Speak only when spoken to." A pause. "And if the king asks you something strange — answer it straight. Last man who hesitated went below."

The throne room was vast and dim, lit by high windows the color of dirty ice. Courtiers stood in clusters far from the dais, the way people stand back from a fire that spits. And on the throne, beneath the great crowned-stag banner, sat King Aldous Veylin III.

Ivo had seen portraits. This was not the man in the portraits. The portraits showed a heavy-jawed, ruddy king with a hunter's confidence. The figure on the throne was gaunt as winter, his skin stretched over the skull like wet vellum, his eyes moving constantly, restlessly, as though reading a page no one else could see. And the crown —

The crown sat wrong. Not crooked — *wrong*, the way a word repeated too many times becomes wrong. It gleamed black-gold in the gray light, and as Ivo was pushed forward across the flagstones, he would have sworn — would have sworn on Veyra's silent name — that the crown shifted. Settled. The way a parasite adjusts its grip.

The herald spoke his false name. Ivo knelt.

"So." The king's voice was two voices braided together, one dry and human, one deep and resonant as a bell struck underwater. "The wool merchant who touches iron."

"Your Majesty—"

"*Look at me.*"

Ivo looked. Up close, the wrongness was worse. The king's lips were cracked and his breath rattled, and behind the restless eyes something else peered out — something patient and vast and *amused*, using the king's face like a mask hung on a hook.

"You read what things remember," the doubled voice said. "Iron. Stone. Bone. We have been looking for such a one. The others crumble too quickly. They give and give and there is nothing left, but you — you have been *hoarding*, haven't you? Seven years of quiet, and all that time, filling yourself up." The mouth smiled with the king's cracked lips. "Come. Touch the seal. Tell Us what We are."

A servant brought the royal seal on a cushion of cloth-of-gold — the great wax-stamp of state, handled by a thousand hands, dense with a century of memory. It was a test, or bait, or both. Every instinct screamed at Ivo to refuse, to kneel dumbly, to be dull and safe.

But the seal was a key, and beyond the key was the answer, and dread had been hardening in him for six days into something with edges.

He reached out and laid his palm flat on the ancient metal.

The world unfolded — and this time it did not fold into a manacle's small history but into an abyss. He fell through generations of oaths and decrees and wars, down and down, past the seal's honest surface, into what lay *beneath* the throne's authority like rot beneath gilt: darkness, and within the darkness a presence older than the dynasty, coiled through the crown's gold like a root through a coffin. It had been bound here deliberately — he saw the binding, saw robed figures and dragon-fire and words of unmaking half-spoken, a prison built in haste and hubris — and for generations it had slept, sipping, patient.

Now the vessel was failing. Now it was drinking deep. Now the harvest wagons rolled weekly, and the Hollow Regent — he knew the name suddenly, the way you know a word in a dream — the Hollow Regent grew fat on stolen selves and dreamed of unbinding.

And threaded through all of it, faint as a candle behind a wall, one voice, the true king's voice, worn almost to nothing, repeating like a man knocking on a door in a burning house:

*I am Aldous. I am Aldous. I am Aldous. Someone remember me. Please. Someone remember—*

Ivo snatched his hand back. He was breathing hard. The court had gone absolutely still, and the thing on the throne was watching him with an expression of terrible satisfaction.

"Yes," it breathed, in the bell-underwater voice, leaning forward so the crown's shadow fell across Ivo like a hand. "You *see* Us. Good. Oh, you will be useful, little reader. You will tell Us where the others hide. You will teach Us how gifts are hoarded, so that We may take them whole. And in exchange—"

"In exchange," Ivo said, and his voice did not shake, though everything in him did, "nothing. Because I know what you are now. And I know what can unmake you."

The smile froze. For one instant the king's own eyes surfaced — lucid, drowning, *terrified* — and the ruined mouth formed a single silent word: *please.*

Then the darkness surged back up behind the face, and the doubled voice rang through the throne room like a funeral bell:

"Guards. Take him below. Begin the rendering at moonrise — We want everything this one has been saving."

As they hauled Ivo up by his arms, he looked back once at the throne, at the hollow king and the thing wearing him, and made the only vow that had felt true since Veyra went quiet.

*Hold on,* he thought at the drowning man, at the boy in the cell, at everyone the wagons had emptied. *There is a fire in the mountains. I just have to live long enough to reach it.*
