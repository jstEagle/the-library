## 4. The Thing Wearing the Crown

They took the chains off in a room with no windows.

That alone told Ivo something. Every interrogation chamber he'd ever seen — and he'd seen his share, from the salt-cellars of Vask to the black houses under Corvess — wanted its prisoners to see the sky. Wanted them to remember there was an outside, a life that could be returned to them if only they confessed enough, wept prettily enough. This room had been built for something else. The walls were grey granite, unadorned, old beyond the palace's fashion. There was a table, two chairs, and a brazier burning some oil that gave light without warmth.

And there was the king.

Aldric III sat at the far end of the table like a man who had been set down there by other hands and left. He was not what Ivo expected. The criers and the coin-stamps showed a stern face, a jaw like a drawn bow, eyes that could pin a man to a wall. What sat before him now was thinner than his own legend. His hair was white where it should have been iron-grey — he was not yet fifty — and it hung lank against a skull that seemed to have shrunk inside the skin. His hands lay flat on the table, palms down, as if holding the wood still were all the work he could manage.

He wore no crown.

Ivo noticed that before anything else. A king receiving a prisoner, even a secret audience, even this — and his head was bare. There was a mark on his brow instead, a pale band of skin where hair had stopped growing, running temple to temple like a scar left by something heavy.

"Kneel," said the guard behind him.

Ivo knelt. Not because the guard said so. Because kneeling bought time, and time was the only currency he had left, and he meant to spend every coin of it looking.

The door shut. The guard's boots receded down stone. And then they were alone, the king and the smuggler, in a room with nothing on its walls and nothing in its air but the smell of cold oil.

"You are Kavanagh." The voice was dry, papery, the voice of a man reading words someone else had written. "The Thorn guide. The oath-sworn one."

"I am."

"They say you can read what things remember." The king's eyes came up, and for a moment Ivo's breath caught — because those eyes were *wrong*. Not cruel. Not mad, exactly. They moved like water finding cracks, always seeping somewhere else, never resting on him fully. "Is that true?"

Ivo weighed the lie and found it too expensive. "It is."

Something passed over the king's face. It might have been fear. It might have been hope. With a face that ruined, it was hard to tell them apart.

"Then you will read for me," Aldric said. "Not for my examiners. Not for my priests. For me." His hand lifted from the table with visible effort and pushed something across the wood.

The royal seal.

It was smaller than Ivo had imagined — a disc of dark gold the size of a plum, worn smooth at the edges, stamped with the stag-and-mountain arms of the house of Corvess. It should have hung from a chain around the king's neck. It lay on the table instead, set down like something the owner no longer wished to touch.

"I cannot wear it anymore," the king said. "I cannot hold it. But the law says the seal must be pressed to the hunt-warrants, and my chancellors say the warrants are false unless my hand does the pressing. So." The wet eyes slid away, found the wall, kept sliding. "So. Read it. Tell me what it wants."

*What it wants.* Not *what it has seen.* Ivo turned the phrase over once and felt the floor tilt beneath his understanding.

He reached out and took the seal.

* * *

The gift had rules. He'd learned them young, bleeding from his mother's kitchen doorframe after touching her wedding ring and falling into forty years of laughter and flour-smoke and a man's voice singing low. Objects remembered what touched them most. Iron held pain best. Silver held love. Wood held patience. And gold —

Gold held *power*. Gold remembered who had carried it, and how they had carried it, and what it had cost them.

Ivo closed his fingers around the seal and fell.

He fell through decades. Through the sweat-slick grip of Aldric's father signing peace with the hill clans, the seal biting into his palm hard enough to leave the stag embossed in flesh. Through a coronation, the seal pressed to a forehead slick with consecration oil while bells drowned the kingdom. Through war councils and famine ledgers and a hundred thousand small acts of authority, each one leaving its sediment in the metal like silt in a riverbed.

And beneath all of it — older than all of it, coiled through the gold the way rot coils through the heartwood of a tree — something else.

It noticed him immediately.

There was no better word for it. The memories he usually read were weather; they moved over him and past him and left him damp but whole. This was an *eye*. It opened inside the seal's long history, and it looked at him, and it was pleased.

*Ivo Kavanagh,* it said, though it had no mouth and made no sound. The words arrived the way a headache arrives, already formed, already behind his eyes. *The reader. We have waited for a reader.*

He tried to let go. His hand would not open.

The vision deepened, whether he willed it or no, and he saw what the seal had seen before any king touched it. A forge older than the palace, older than the city, older than the name Corvess. Hands — human hands, but wrong, the fingers too long, the knuckles bending backward — hammering gold around a core of something that was not metal. A binding, done in desperation by people who had run out of every other option. Chanting in a language that predated language. And a voice screaming from inside the cooling gold, a voice like wind through an empty hall, like footsteps in a house with no one home—

*They called us the Hollow Regent,* the thing said, almost fondly. *Because we regent. We govern what the kings can no longer carry. Their burdens. Their judgments. Their minds.* A pause, and then, softer, with terrible intimacy: *Yours would feed us well, reader. Seven years of silence. We could show you where your god went.*

Ivo wrenched himself back.

He came to on his knees beside the table, the seal skidding across the boards, his stomach heaving bile into his throat. The brazier flame had gone green for a moment — or he had imagined it — and the room was cold, truly cold, cold as the underside of a river.

Across the table, the king was watching him. And the watching was different now. Sharper. The wet drifting eyes had stilled, and behind them something leaned forward, interested, *hungry*, and Ivo understood with a clarity that felt like a blade between the ribs that he was not looking at Aldric III.

He was looking at the tenant.

"There," the king said, in a voice that was the old dry voice and yet not, layered now, doubled, a chord played slightly out of tune. "You have seen us. Good. The last priest who saw us screamed for three days before the quieting." The mouth smiled without any participation from the eyes. "You did not scream. Why did you not scream, reader?"

"Because screaming costs breath," Ivo managed, "and I may need mine."

The thing laughed through the king's throat. It was the worst sound Ivo had ever heard — not because it was monstrous, but because underneath the wrongness of it, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, there was a second laugh, hoarse and horrified and *trapped*, trying to rise and being shoved down like a drowning man pushed under by his own rescuer.

* * *

It happened between one question and the next.

The entity had been speaking through the king's mouth — questions about the Thorn passes, about the rebels in the high country, about how many gifted souls might hide in the smuggling routes — and Ivo had been answering in flat mercenary syllables, giving away nothing, buying seconds. Then the king's hand, which had lain palm-down on the table all this time, curled slowly into a fist. The knuckles whitened. The doubled voice stumbled.

"—and the northern road is watched by—by—" The face convulsed. Sweat sprang up along the pale band on the brow. "*By no one, the pass is clear, listen to me, boy, listen—*"

The eyes snapped into focus.

For one moment — one flickering, guttering moment, a candle flame cupped in a hurricane — Aldric III was entirely, lucidly present in his own face. Ivo saw the man the coin-stamps had lied about: exhausted past exhaustion, terrified past terror, and underneath both, stubborn as mountain oak. A king who had been fighting a war alone in his own skull for years, maybe decades, and had lost everything except the fighting itself.

"*Boy.*" The whisper cracked. His hand shot across the table and seized Ivo's wrist, and his grip was iron, desperate, the grip of a man catching a rope thrown over a cliff edge. "You read it. You know what it is. Don't — don't let them tell you it's madness, don't let the priests say it, it is *in the crown*, it has been in the crown since my grandfather's grandfather, it eats—" His breath hitched. "It eats a little more every year. My father lasted thirty years on the throne. I have lasted nineteen. My son—"

The fist tightened.

"My son would not last five. Do you understand? Whatever you do — whatever you decide you owe your brothers, your god, yourself — understand that every day that crown sits on a living head, it grows *stronger*, and the next wearer starts the war deeper in the hole." The lucid eyes bored into him, wet and blazing. "I signed the decree. I know I signed it. I do not remember signing it, do you hear me? I wake with blood on my warrant-hand and lists in my mind that I never wrote. It uses my hand. It hunts my own kind with my own seal because it *fears* your kind — readers, speakers, binders — the ones who could see it, name it, unmake it. That is why the hunt exists. Not treason. Not order. *Fear.*"

His voice dropped to something barely above breath.

"It lets me surface like this once a season, perhaps. Less now. It likes to let me see the water before it pulls me under again. Cruelty is a discipline it practices." A broken almost-laugh. "So I will spend it. All of it. On you, stranger, because you are the first soul in nine years who has touched the seal and lived to look at me afterward." He dragged himself half out of the chair, close, close enough that Ivo could smell the sickness on him, the strange hollow scent like an empty room. "There is a fire that can unbind it. Old fire. Dragon-fire. The old records speak of one dragon left in the Thornfell heights — Emberveil, the last of the caldera brood. If she lives. If she has anything left."

"The hunt," Ivo said hoarsely. "Your own soldiers are in the mountains. Bren's company holds every pass."

"Then outrun them." The king's eyes flicked toward the door and back, and the focus was already fraying at the edges, the pupil widening, the wet drift creeping in like fog reclaiming a clearing. "Go tonight. Go now. The east postern — the third guard, Tammas, he is mine, he has always been mine, he dreams true dreams and the thing cannot cloud a dreamer—" His grip spasmed. "Please. I have begged nothing from anyone in nineteen years. I am begging *you*."

And then the moment guttered out.

Ivo watched it go — watched the light drain out of the king's face the way tide drains from a strand, watched the shoulders settle back into their slack puppet-looseness, watched the doubled presence slide home behind the eyes like a key turning in a lock. When Aldric spoke again, the voice was smooth and papery and entirely sane.

"You have had a long audience, Kavanagh. Longer than most earn." The entity smiled through the king's mouth. "You will return to your cell. In three days, you will be examined properly, and you will serve the crown, and we believe — we *hope* — that you will prove very useful indeed."

The guards came back in. Hands took Ivo's arms. As they hauled him upright, the king turned away toward the windowless wall, and in the last half-second before the guards blocked his view, Ivo saw the king's hand move — small, hidden against the table's edge, where no eye was supposed to follow.

Two fingers tapped the wood. Twice.

*East postern. Tonight.*

They marched him back down the corridors, past cells where the drained prisoners murmured to walls, past the checkpoint chains that still whispered of everything they'd swallowed. Ivo walked among them with his heart slamming against his ribs and his mind already moving faster than his feet, mapping routes, counting hours, weighing a dying king's desperation against a silent god's seven-year absence.

The Hollow Regent feared readers. Feared them enough to build an entire hunt around that fear.

Which meant his gift — the curse he'd carried, the punishment he'd assumed — was the one weapon in the kingdom the thing in the crown could not afford to let live.

Somewhere north, past the passes his brothers-in-arms now held for crown coin, a dragon guttered in the dark.

Three days. He had three days.
