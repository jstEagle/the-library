## 5. Brothers in Coin

* * *

The rain had followed Ivo down out of the Thornfells for three days, like a hound that refused to be called off. By the time he reached Corvess it was falling in gray sheets, hammering the rooftops of the low city into a single unbroken roar, and the gutters ran brown with the ash the Crown Hunt had left behind.

He kept his hood up and his hands in his sleeves. A man who read memories through touch could not afford to brush against strangers — not now, when every third doorway bore the chalk mark of the king's examiners and every fourth door stood empty behind it. The city had the smell of a place holding its breath: wet wool, tallow smoke, fear gone stale in closed rooms.

The Drowned Bell sat where it had always sat, wedged between a rope-walk and a chandler's at the bottom of Tanner's Stair. Ivo told himself he was not going there. He told himself he was going for the stables out back, the ones Bren had once bought off a cheating farrier, the ones with the loose board in the north wall that any man of the old company could shift in the dark. Sanctuary by habit, nothing more.

The loose board was still there. So was the hand that caught his wrist as he slid through.

Ivo froze. Rain dripped from his hood onto knuckles scarred white with old rope-burns, and he knew those knuckles before he saw the face above them.

"Bren," he said.

"You're supposed to be dead." Bren's voice came out flat, which was how Bren sounded when feeling had gotten too large to speak around. "Checkpoint at Grey Ford burned two weeks ago. Four guardsmen dead. They put your description on the boards, Ivo. They put your *name* on them."

"Then you knew."

"I hoped," Bren said, and pulled him inside.

* * *

Bren had taken the corner room of the Drowned Bell — the one with two exits and a view of the stair — because ten years of selling violence had taught him that comfort was a thing you bought after survival. He shut the door, barred it, and stood looking at Ivo with the firelight jumping on his blunt features, and Ivo watched him do the arithmetic he'd seen him do before a hundred negotiations: what this costs, what this buys, who else knows.

"Sit down before you fall down," Bren said. "You look like something the river gave back."

Ivo sat. His body, which had been running on will and stolen bread since Grey Ford, chose that moment to remind him of its debts. His hands shook. He pressed them flat against his knees.

"They took me to the palace," he said. "After Grey Ford. Before I got free."

"I know. It's why you're alive. The examiners don't kill gifted souls, they—" Bren stopped himself, jaw working.

"They drain them." Ivo looked up. "You know that too. Don't you."

Bren poured wine without answering. Two cups. He set one in front of Ivo and drank from the other like a man washing out a taste he couldn't name.

"The coin's good," he said finally. "Better than anything we've earned since the Vellish war. The crown pays by the head, and the heads come quiet — mostly. We keep order. We keep the examiners from burning whole streets, which they'd do otherwise, because they're frightened and frightened men with writs are worse than soldiers." He said it the way he said all his justifications, level and reasonable, a wall built brick by careful brick. "Somebody runs the hunts, Ivo. Better men who remember what mercy looks like."

"And what do the heads buy?" Ivo's voice stayed low. That was the discipline of it, the terrible discipline — he did not shout. Shouting was for things that hadn't yet gone past shouting. "Do you know what happens to them after the examiners take them? Have you ever asked what the palace does with a cartload of empty people?"

"Empty—"

"I've touched their chains, Bren. Iron remembers everything that's been done to it." He watched Bren flinch; his oldest friend had never liked talk of the gift, even before the gift became contraband. "There's a thing in the palace. In the crown itself. It eats memory — that's what the examinations are *for*. Feeding. And the man wearing that crown is being eaten alive from the inside, one year of himself at a time, and no one in this kingdom is allowed to say so because the saying of it is treason."

Silence. Rain drummed the shutter. Downstairs, faintly, someone laughed at something, and the sound was obscene.

Bren set his cup down with exaggerated care. "Say I believe you. Say every word is gospel truth straight from your silent god." His eyes came up, and there was grief in them already, grief laid down like groundwork, like a foundation poured before the argument began. "What would you have me do? March forty men on the palace? Get them all killed and the kingdom handed to whatever comes after? Or walk away from the only steady coin we've had in five years and watch my people starve honest?"

"I'd have you believe me."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got left." Ivo leaned forward. "I'm not asking you to storm anything. I'm asking you to help me get out of Corvess — and then keep your mouth shut about what I've told you. That's all. That's the whole price."

Bren laughed, short and bitter. "That's the whole price. Silence. You want me to swear silence about treason while taking crown coin to hunt traitors." He rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment he looked older than his years, older than the war had made him. "Here's mine, then. I can get you over the border. Horses, papers, the Vellish road — I'll pay it out of my own purse and call it an old debt, and no one in the company will ever know you were here. You go north, or east, wherever gifted folk go to die quietly. And you swear to me you'll never speak of what you saw in the palace. Not to me, not to anyone. You vanish, and the world stays the shape it is."

"The world stays the shape it is," Ivo repeated. "With the king rotting in his own crown and half the country's souls drained into it like casks."

"Yes." Bren met his eyes and did not look away, and Ivo loved him for it and hated him for it in the same breath. "Because the alternative is a war we lose, and everyone I'm responsible for dies in it. You've never had forty lives sitting in your palm, Ivo. Don't tell me what I should feel about the weight."

"No," Ivo said softly. "I've only had my own. And I know exactly what it weighs, because they took most of it at Grey Ford and I felt every piece go." He stood, slow, knees protesting. "I won't swear your silence, Bren. Ask me anything else."

"Then I can't help you." But Bren's voice cracked on *can't*, and they both heard it.

* * *

They came downstairs together, because it could not be helped — the noise from the common room had changed, and Bren heard it the way a shepherd hears wolves. Voices raised. Chairs scraped. When they pushed open the door, half the company was on its feet.

Sera Tollen stood on a table. She was the youngest of the veterans, quick and sharp-faced, and she had Ivo's wanted bill crushed in her fist.

"—on the boards a fortnight and none of you thought to mention you'd served beside him? Kavanagh. *Oathman* Kavanagh. We drank with this man."

"We drink with a lot of people," growled Odo Marsh, arms crossed, planted between Sera and the stairs like a gatepost. "We're paid to stop drinking with some of them too. That's the work."

"The work." Sera spat the word. "My cousin went into an examination wagon in Marrowgate in spring. He came back smiling at walls. *Smiling at walls,* Odo. If that's the work, I want my share of it returned."

"Heads up!" Odo snapped, but other voices were rising now — Dace and the Ferrin twins backing Sera, young Pell looking from face to face like a man watching a fire decide which way to jump. Bren stepped forward and the room shifted around him the way water shifts around a dropped stone.

Enough. Ivo moved past him into the open floor, pushed back his hood, and let them see his face in the lamplight.

"It's me," he said. "Alive, as advertised. And before anyone decides what I am — a prize or a plague — hear what I am asking, because it's small." He let the silence stretch. "Bren has offered me a horse and a road out of this country. All I need is for no one in this room to remember seeing me. Can any of you manage that much forgetting?"

A ragged laugh went around, the kind that breaks tension and spreads it thin. But Odo wasn't laughing. Odo was looking at Bren.

"Captain. Tell us what you told him. Upstairs. About the road."

Bren's face went carefully blank. "That's between him and me."

"So there *was* something." Odo turned to the room, palms out, reasonable as a magistrate. "You all hear that? Our captain's cutting private terms with a hunted man while we hold crown contracts that name that man's face. What happens when the examiners ask us why Kavanagh slipped through Corvess under our noses? What do they do to companies that lie?"

"They don't ask," Ivo said quietly. "They examine. Ask Sera what examination means."

"Shut *up*—" Bren started, but the room had already tilted. He could feel it going, the way he'd felt a shield line start to fold — not all at once, but with a groan, a leaning, a terrible willingness in some part of it.

Sera jumped down from the table and stood beside Ivo. Dace followed. Then the Ferrin twins, moving together the way they always did, and young Pell last, red-faced, apologizing with his eyes to Bren even as he crossed the floor.

Eight of them. Out of forty-one.

Odo stared at the little knot of defectors as if they'd grown second heads. "You'd throw away ten years' brotherhood for a mad story about a talking crown?"

"Not for the story," Sera said. "For the smile on my cousin's face. For whatever made it."

Bren stood very still in the middle of his ruined room. Rain hammered the shutters. He looked at Ivo across the space where their friendship had been, and Ivo watched him choose — watched him reach for the officer's voice, the cold clean voice that ended arguments —

—and fail to find it.

"Get them out," Bren said at last, hoarse. "Tonight, before first bell, before anyone sober reports this. North gate, the chandler's cart, Odo has the key and won't use it against you — I'll see to that much." He turned away, shoulders square, and picked up his cup with a hand that was not quite steady. "Go find your dragon, Ivo. Since I know you'll do it whether I bless it or bury you."

Ivo paused at the door. "Bren. The debt I owed you—"

"Is paid," Bren said, without turning around. "It was paid the day you didn't swear."

Outside, the rain had softened to sleet, and far to the north, above the black teeth of the Thornfells, the clouds glowed faintly red — as if something old and burning were breathing beneath the stone, counting down the nights it had left.
