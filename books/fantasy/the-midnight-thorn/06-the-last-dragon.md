## 6. The Last Dragon

Three days into Thornfell, the snow began to speak.

Ivo had climbed these passes for a decade, guiding smuggler caravans through switchbacks that would kill a lowlander before he could cry out, and he had never heard the mountain make that sound — a thin, crystalline whisper, as though each falling flake were a syllable in a language older than stone. He told himself it was wind through the ice-choked ravines. He told himself a great many things since fleeing Corvess with the king's seal still burning behind his eyes.

The Hollow Regent. That was what it had called itself, coiled in the memory of a crown worn by nine kings across four hundred years. It did not need to call itself anything. It simply was, patient as rust, eating its way through the mind of a man who had once, perhaps, been kind.

And Emberveil was the only fire that could unmake it.

He climbed through the Grey Teeth — those shattered spires of black granite where even the smugglers refused to go — following instructions he had paid a blind herbwoman in a mountain hamlet to give him. She had taken his coin, pressed her palm flat against his wrist as if reading something there, and said only: *"You smell of thorn-iron and grief. She will know you. Follow the warm snow."*

Warm snow. He had thought it a madwoman's riddle until the third evening, when he crested a shoulder of scree above a hidden valley and saw it: a single drift, glowing faintly gold at its heart, melting even as fresh powder settled on it. Steam curled from it like breath.

Beyond it, the caldera opened in the mountainside like a wound that had never learned to close.

Ivo stood at the rim and forgot, for a long moment, how to breathe.

The basin below was vast — half a mile of ash-grey stone ringed by cliffs streaked with ancient fire-scars. Once, he understood, it must have glowed. Now it held only a dim amber light, guttering somewhere beneath the rock like embers under a dying hearth. And at the center of it, half-buried in her own accumulated centuries, lay the dragon.

She was enormous. That was the first thing, the almost comical thing — a body longer than the chapel in Corvess, wings folded like the sails of wrecked ships, scales the color of cooled bronze gone green-black with age. Moss grew along her spine. Lichen furred the edges of her jaw. A birch tree, impossibly, had rooted itself in the hollow between two of her dorsal plates and grown twenty feet toward the pale sky.

She looked less like a living creature than like a landscape that had briefly dreamed of being one.

"Emberveil," Ivo said. His voice cracked on the second syllable. He hadn't spoken aloud in two days.

Nothing moved. The steam rose. The warm snow fell.

He picked his way down into the caldera, boots sliding on glassy slag, and crossed the ash plain with his heart hammering against his ribs like a prisoner against cell bars. Up close she was worse — or better; he couldn't decide. Her scales were dull, pitted, some flaked away entirely to show grey hide beneath. Her eyes, when he finally stood before her head and dared to look into them, were filmed white as frost on a windowpane.

Blind. Or nearly.

"I know why you've come."

The voice did not come from her mouth. It came from everywhere — from the stone, from the steam, from the space behind his teeth. It was a woman's voice worn down to its grain, the way river water wears a bell to silence.

"Then you know what I'll ask," Ivo managed.

"I know what everyone asks." A slow exhalation, and heat rolled over him — not flame, just warmth, the last warmth of a furnace going cold. "The ones who remember I exist. They come for fire. They never ask if there is any left to give."

"There isn't." He said it gently. "Is there?"

Her head shifted — a movement like a hillside settling — and one clouded eye turned toward him with terrible precision, as if blindness had taught her to see some other way. "You're the one who reads objects. The memory-thief of the Thorn passes. Word crawls slowly up here, but it crawls."

"Not a thief."

"No?" Something that might have been amusement moved through the vast voice. "You take what is stored and carry it away in your mind. What would my mother's people have called that?"

"My mother's people didn't have a word for me at all." Ivo knelt in the ash before her, close enough to feel the heat radiating off her hide, close enough to count the lichen-furred seams of her jaw. "They had a word for what I'm asking, though. My grandmother told me stories. Dragon-fire doesn't burn things. It *unbinds* them. Curses, bindings, oaths written in blood — all of it comes apart in your flame. Nothing else can do it. Not sorcery. Not prayer." His throat tightened. "Not Veyra. Seven years silent, and even if she spoke, I don't think even a god can pull something like this out of a living crown without killing the man wearing it. But you can."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll climb back down the mountain, and within a year there will be no kingdom left to save. Just a crown walking around inside a skin, wearing a king's face, feeding on everything he ever loved." He paused. "And I suppose I'll keep guiding smugglers until they catch me again. Which they will."

The silence stretched. Somewhere high above, the snow whispered its crystalline language.

"You are either very brave," Emberveil said at last, "or very stupid, to kneel in front of a dying dragon and tell her she is the last hope of a kingdom that hunted her kind to nothing."

"The hunt for gifted souls started this autumn. Yours ended three hundred years ago. But yes." He bowed his head. "We have that in common, you and I. Both of us hiding in Thornfell while the world decides what we're worth dead."

Another silence — but a different quality to it now. When she spoke again, the vastness had narrowed, drawn closer, almost intimate.

"Come here, memory-reader. Put your hand on my brow, since that's your way of seeing. And then we will discuss the price."

* * *

The cost was not coin.

Ivo learned that in the first minute, when his palm touched the ancient scale above her eye and the memories came flooding — hers into him, unasked, a courtesy and a warning both. He saw skies blackened with wing-shadows. He saw her sisters fall burning over the lowland fields, and her brothers chained in salt-mines, and herself, young and furious, vowing never to land on human soil again. He saw centuries of solitude compress into a single image: a dragon alone in a cooling caldera, counting her heartbeats like a miser counts coins, knowing every one spent is one fewer.

When he surfaced, gasping, she was watching him with that uncanny blind sight.

"Now you understand what I am," she said. "Fire fed on nothing goes out. My flame has burned on my own reserves for three hundred years, and the reserves are done. To burn hot enough to unbind the thing you describe — an old binding, deep-woven, generations thick — I need fuel. Living fuel. Memories, freely surrendered, burned whole."

"Freely given," Ivo repeated. "Or it doesn't work?"

"It works badly. Stolen memories burn sour. They'd poison the flame, and the binding would laugh at it." A plume of steam rose from her nostrils. "It must be given the way a man gives blood. Knowing exactly what leaves him. Knowing it does not return."

Ivo sat back on his heels in the ash. Above them the caldera rim framed a scrap of darkening sky, and the first stars were coming out, indifferent as always.

"How much?" he asked.

"For a binding that old? Everything, near enough. Every treasured thing you carry. A life's worth." Her voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. "You will not be empty afterward. You will remember how to walk, how to speak, how to fight. But the *warm* things — the things that make a life feel like yours rather than anyone's — those go into the fire. And they do not come back."

He thought of his mother's face. Realized, with a lurch, that he was already struggling to hold it steady — that grief had blurred it over seven years of silence and hard roads, and now he was being asked to hand over even the blur.

He thought of Veyra's voice. One sentence, spoken over a boy of fifteen with an iron brand in his hand: *Ivo Kavanagh. You are seen.* Seven years of silence since, and he had lived on that single sentence like a man lives on one loaf through a winter.

"What happens to you," he asked instead, "when the fire's spent?"

A long pause. Snow drifted down into the caldera, melting to nothing a few feet above her back.

"Dragons do not die the way men die," she said finally. "We go out. Like lamps. There is a moment when the wick is bare, and then—" The vast shoulders shifted, and the birch tree growing from her spine shivered, shedding snow. "But that is my price to weigh, not yours. You asked what I would charge. Here is my answer: I will burn for you, memory-reader. I have been waiting three hundred years for someone to ask me for something worth burning for. But you must gather the fuel first, and you must gather it fast."

"How fast?"

"I have weeks. Not months. Perhaps three, if the winter is kind, and winters are never kind." The blind eye turned toward him, and he felt weighed, measured, found — something. "Go down the mountain. Gather what you love into a vessel that can hold it. Thorn-iron, if you can find it — old thorn-iron, forged cold. It keeps memories the way ice keeps fish. Then come back to me before the light under the snow goes out. Come back, and we will end a four-hundred-year parasite together, or we will both go out here in the dark and let the crown have the world."

Ivo rose. His legs were shaking, though not from cold.

"One more question," he said. "The memories I feed you. Do they suffer? Are they... aware, in the fire?"

Emberveil was quiet for so long he thought she might not answer.

"They sing," she said at last. "For a moment, before they become flame, everything freely given sings. It is the most beautiful sound in the world, and it is why I cannot bear to die without doing it once more." The great head lowered until her brow nearly brushed the ash. "Now go, Ivo Kavanagh. You are seen. Go and choose what you will lose."

The words hit him like a fist — *you are seen*, her words echoing a god's, and he stood in the dying light of the last dragon with tears freezing on his cheeks, unable to say whether they were grief or gratitude.

Behind him, as he began the long climb out of the caldera, he heard her begin to hum — low, tuneless, a furnace remembering music.

Three weeks, he thought. Three weeks to decide which pieces of himself he could live without.

And beneath the snow, faint but unmistakable, the warm gold light flickered — once — like a lamp running out of oil.
