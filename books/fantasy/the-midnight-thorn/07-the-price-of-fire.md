## 7. The Price of Fire

The caldera breathed.

That was the only word for it — the great bowl of stone at the mountain's heart rising and falling with a slow, tectonic rhythm, warm air rolling up its walls in a tide that smelled of sulfur and old iron. Ivo stood at the rim where the rock had split into terraces like a fallen staircase, and he understood that he was not standing on a mountain at all. He was standing inside a body.

She lay coiled in the caldera's floor, and his first thought was that the stories had lied to him. Emberveil was supposed to be vast enough to shade a valley. She was — but she was also dying. Her scales, which must once have burned copper and rose-gold, had gone the color of ash and rust, dulled by decades of neglect. Half her hide was bare bone where the scales had sloughed away. One eye opened when he descended the third terrace: a slit-pupiled amber lens, clouded at the edge like frost creeping across glass.

*Little oath-keeper,* said a voice that did not arrive through his ears. It arrived through the mountain itself, through the soles of his boots and the marrow of him. *Seven years since anyone climbed this far. Seven years, and now you come smelling of crown-iron and grief.*

"I come asking for fire," Ivo said. His voice sounded small in all that space.

*Everyone who comes asks for fire.* The great head shifted; gravel poured from her neck like water. *No one asks what it costs.*

* * *

He spent the first day simply listening.

Emberveil did not move much — could not, he came to understand. Her wings were sound. The left one dragged when she tried to fold it, and the membrane had torn along the trailing edge years ago and healed wrong, scarred into stiff ridges. She told him about it without self-pity, the way an old soldier talks about an old wound: as fact, as furniture.

*Dragon-fire is not flame,* she said, on the second day, as he sat against her foreleg and shared out dried mutton he'd carried up the pass. *Any beast can make heat. What I carry is older than heat. It unbinds. It unmakes bindings — oaths, chains, enchantments, things bound into other things.* A slow exhalation, and the air over the caldera shimmered. *Your king wears something bound to him. You want it burned away.*

"You know."

*I felt the Hollow Regent wake before your grandfather's grandfather drew breath. It has been eating its way up the spine of this kingdom for two hundred years, and no one has climbed here to stop it until a smuggler with a thief's gift.* The amber eye rolled toward him, and there was something in it that might have been humor, dry as bone. *The world has grown strange.*

"What do you need?"

And she told him.

*My fire is fed, not made. Every dragon burns on what we are given. In the old days, whole peoples gave freely — their harvests, their victories, the names of their dead sung into our keeping. Memory is the true fuel. Not the memory of the giver alone. Anything given freely, anything loved enough to hurt when it leaves.* The eye closed. When it opened again, the frost had spread further. *I am nearly empty, little oath-keeper. I have been living on dregs for thirty years. If you feed me, I can burn the crown clean — but I cannot carry the flame far anymore. My wings are done. Whatever fire you build in me must be used close, or soon, or both.*

Ivo looked out across the caldera, at the distant smudge of Thornfell's southern peaks, beyond which lay Corvess, and the palace, and the thing wearing the king like a glove.

"How far can you fly?"

*A day. Perhaps less.*

"Then I'll need to bring the throne room to you," he said, "or you to the throne room." He thought of Bren. He thought of the company, of coin and codes and the hunt. "Or I find men willing to open a road."

*Men,* Emberveil rumbled, and the caldera shivered with something like laughter. *First you will need to decide what you are willing to pay. Because you cannot give me what you do not love. And you, oath-keeper, hoard your memories like a miser counts coin.*

* * *

She was right, and he knew it before she finished speaking.

He went back down the mountain's shoulder to the cave where he'd cached his gear, and he took out the casket he had bought in Corvess from a fence who hadn't known what it was: thorn-iron, black metal veined with living briar-patterns, forged — the fence claimed — in the old days for carrying things too dangerous to hold loosely. Memories would keep in it. Emberveil had told him that much. Given memories could be stored, carried, held in reserve until the moment they were needed.

But they had to be *given*. That was the wound in it. There was no trick, no theft, no cleverness. His gift let him read what others had handled. This required the opposite: opening himself, choosing what he loved most, and letting it go — not setting it aside, not forgetting for an hour, but surrendering it root and branch, so that the memory passed out of him entirely and lived only in the iron.

He sat cross-legged in the cave mouth with the casket on his knees, and snow began to fall past the entrance in fat, unhurried flakes, and he did not move for a long time.

His mother's face.

It came to him easily, because he visited it often. Not the last time he'd seen her — she had been sick then, gray-lipped, frightened — but before. The kitchen in the lowland house with the crooked door. Flour on her knuckles. The way she squinted when she laughed, one eye almost closing, as if joy itself needed aiming. He had built his whole interior life around that image the way a cathedral is built around a relic. In the worst cells, in the coldest passes, in the seven silent years of Veyra's absence, he had gone back to that kitchen and stood in the doorway and watched her laugh.

If he gave it, he would never stand in that doorway again. He would know a mother had existed. He would know he had loved her. But her face would be a blank where a face should be, and the kitchen a room he could never enter even in dreams.

"Is there another way?" he asked the snow. The snow didn't answer. Neither, as ever, did Veyra.

He laid both palms flat on the casket's lid.

Giving a memory, he discovered, was nothing like reading one. Reading was trespassing — slipping into the grooves someone else's life had worn into an object. Giving was being read. The thorn-iron warmed under his hands and seemed to look back at him, patient as a confessor, and the only honest currency was willingness.

He thought of the flour on her knuckles. He thought of her voice saying his name — *Ivo, wash your hands, Ivo, mind the door, Ivo, my heart* — and then he stopped holding on.

It went out of him like blood from a opened vein. Not pain exactly; pain would have been simpler. It was the sensation of a room in him going dark, permanently, of a door bricking itself over while he watched. For one terrible instant he saw her whole — the squinting laugh, the crooked door, all of it blazing — and then the casket drank it, and it was gone.

He knew the facts still. Mother. Dead eleven years. Loved. But when he reached for her face, his hands closed on smoke.

He sat in the snow for a long while afterward, shaking, feeling the shape of the hole. It was smaller than he'd feared and larger than he'd imagined. He understood now why the Hollow Regent fed this way — why stolen memories drained prisoners into husks. Every memory taken unwillingly was theft. But even given freely, each one was an amputation. The difference was only that he had chosen where to cut.

*You wept,* Emberveil observed that evening, when he brought the casket down to the caldera floor and set it before her. Her nostrils flared over the thorn-iron, and deep in her chest something flickered — he saw it, a faint glow between her ribs like embers seen through a cracked stove door.

"Yes."

*Good. Freely given tears sweeten it. The Regent's meals taste of fear, and fear is gristle.* The glow pulsed once, twice, then steadied — a candle where a bonfire had been. *This will keep me alive another season. It will not burn a crown. You understand that.*

"One face isn't enough."

*One face bought you my attention. The crown-entity is old and deeply rooted. To unmake it I must burn hot and long, and to burn hot and long I need a store of memories that would shame a library.* The amber eye regarded him without cruelty. *Go home, little oath-keeper. Live your small life. Let the kingdom rot; kingdoms always do.*

"No."

*Then you will empty yourself.* She said it plainly, without drama. *Every treasured thing. Your first kill. Your first lover. The oath you swore to Veyra and the voice you swore it to — if you mean to feed me enough to reach Corvess and burn clean, you will give me everything you love, and I will tell you now, oath-keeper: what remains afterward is not nothing. But it is very little.*

Ivo looked at his hands. They were steady. That surprised him.

Seven years Veyra had been silent, and he had wondered every one of those days whether his gift was punishment — whether the god had turned away from what he'd become, a mercenary reading lockets for smugglers. If the silence meant abandonment, then the god's voice was already lost, and giving it would cost nothing.

But it wasn't lost. He knew it wasn't. Silence was not absence. Somewhere beneath the quiet, Veyra still held his name the way the casket held his mother's face — kept, not destroyed.

To give the voice would be to bet, finally and absolutely, that silence was all there was.

*You're doing it now,* Emberveil said softly. *Deciding which cuts to make first.*

"The god's voice goes last," Ivo said. "If it goes at all."

*Sensible. Save the deepest vein for last; miners do the same.* The dragon settled her ruined head onto her foreclaws. *We begin tomorrow. Bring the casket. Bring your courage, such as it is. And oath-keeper — eat well tonight, and sleep. Grief is heavy labor.*

* * *

For six days, the mountain heard him weep.

He gave the memory of his first snowfall, and the taste of honey-bread his grandmother made, and the name of the river where he'd learned to swim. He gave the face of Sera, the first woman he'd loved, and the sound of rain on the tavern roof the night Bren first called him brother. Each one went easier than the last, and each one cut deeper — that was the cruelty of it, that the hand grew practiced while the wounds grew mortal. By the fourth day he could not remember why he had ever wanted to be a mercenary. By the fifth, he caught himself staring at the casket and feeling nothing at all toward it, and that frightened him more than any memory of fear ever had.

On the sixth night, the glow between Emberveil's ribs had risen to a furnace-orange, and she could lift her head without dragging it, and she said: *Enough to burn a crown. Not enough to carry me far. Find your road to the palace, little oath-keeper. Find it fast — my flame gutters even as we speak.*

Ivo knelt in the caldera snow, hollowed and light, a house with its rooms stripped bare. Only one thing remained behind the locked door in him, glowing like a coal he dared not touch: a voice saying *Ivo*, in the dark, seven years ago, on the last night Veyra had spoken.

Below the mountain's southern shoulder, torchlight moved in the pass. Dozens of torches, moving in formation, in the disciplined pattern of soldiers sweeping for rebels.

Bren had found him.
