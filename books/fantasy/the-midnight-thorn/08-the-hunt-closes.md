## 8. The Hunt Closes

The mountain did not want them there.

Bren Ossler felt it in the way the wind came down the pass — not blowing at them but *against* them, a long cold exhalation from the throat of Thornfell, as if the range itself had drawn breath to spit them back out. Behind him, forty men of the Kestrels climbed in single file along a shelf of black stone, their breath pluming white in the grey morning, their boots crunching on scree that had never known a road.

Ahead, the caldera's rim showed as a jagged line against the sky, like the broken teeth of something vast.

"You're quiet, Captain," said Doss, falling into step beside him. The boy was twenty and eager and still believed the crown's coin was clean because it jingled.

"Save your breath for the climb."

They'd been three days in the mountains, following a trail that wasn't a trail — a smugglers' path Ivo Kavanagh himself had cut through these peaks years ago, before any of them knew what he was. Before the decree. Before the word *gifted* became a death sentence written in royal ink.

Before Bren had taken the coin.

He flexed his right hand inside its glove. It still remembered the tavern — the rain hammering the shutters, the whole company packed tight and shouting, the night they'd split like a dropped plate. Half had walked out with him into the wet dark, swearing the hunt was justice or at least wages. The other half had stayed with Marta Vane, who'd called him a bought dog to his face and meant it, and he hadn't been able to argue, which was the worst part. He'd just stood there with coin-heavy purse and empty hands while men he'd bled beside looked at him like a stranger wearing Bren's face.

And now here he was, climbing his dead friend's mountain to drag him out of it in chains.

Not dead, he corrected himself. Not yet. The scouts swore Ivo was alive, seen moving toward the high caldera with a thorn-iron casket strapped to his back like a pilgrim's burden. The palace wanted the casket more than the man. That alone should have told Bren something. In eleven years of soldiering he'd learned one iron law: when the powerful want an object more than a person, the person is in danger and the object is worse.

"Captain." Doss again, quieter now, nodding ahead. "Smoke."

There was. A thin grey thread rising past the caldera's rim, too steady for a campfire, too thin for a forge. Something between. Something *kept*.

Bren raised a fist. The column halted. Forty men stood breathing hard in the wind, and Bren looked up at the broken teeth of the rim and felt the mountain watching him back.

"Vane's half will come up the south path if we signal," Doss said.

"I know."

"And if the traitor runs?"

"He won't run." Bren started climbing again. "He never ran from anything in his life. That was always his problem."

* * *

They came over the rim at midday, and Bren ordered the company to halt at the edge because what lay below stopped even soldiers' tongues.

The caldera was a bowl of black glass a half-mile wide, floored in old lava gone smooth as a frozen lake. And in its center, coiled around a vent that breathed slow ribbons of heat, lay the dragon.

She was dying. There was no other way to see her. Emberveil — though Bren didn't know her name yet — was vast and ruined, scales the color of banked coals dulled by ash, one wing folded wrong, eyes half-lidded and filmed like an old woman's. Steam rose off her flanks. Where she breathed, the rock glowed faintly red, and where she exhaled, the air shimmered with a heat that made the far side of the crater waver like water.

Forty hardened men stood at the rim and none of them spoke.

"There," Doss breathed, pointing.

A figure moved near the dragon's head, small as an ant against her jaw. Dark hair gone shaggy, a lean frame Bren would have known blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea. The figure knelt, lifted the lid of an iron-bound box, and held his hands over it — and light rose out of the box, faint and gold and flickering, like fireflies trapped under glass.

Ivo fed the light toward the dragon's nostrils, and she inhaled it, and for one moment her scales kindled — truly kindled, coal-red to living orange — before dimming again.

"She's eating it," someone whispered. "Gods below, she's *eating his memories.*"

Bren had heard the rumors on the climb — smugglers' tales, madman's talk. That the king's mind was eaten hollow. That only dragon-fire could burn the thing in the crown away. That Ivo Kavanagh was paying for the fire with everything he was.

He'd dismissed it. You had to dismiss things, in this trade, or you couldn't sleep.

But he was looking at it now.

"Company," he heard himself say, "down. Slow. Weapons sheathed."

Doss stared. "Captain—"

"*Down.* If you draw steel on that creature I'll break your arm myself."

They descended into the crater in a ragged crescent, boots whispering on glass-smooth stone. The dragon's eye rolled toward them — one vast amber lens, filmed and ancient — and fixed on Bren with an attention that made his skin crawl, as if she were reading him the way Ivo read objects.

Ivo stood. Turned. And Bren saw what the mountain months had done to him: cheeks hollowed, eyes sunk deep and burning, a man being spent like a candle. The casket hung open at his feet, and around it the air itself seemed thick with residue — a shimmer, a scent of woodsmoke and rain and something sweeter, like bread baking somewhere far away.

Memories, leaking. Bren could almost taste them. That frightened him more than the dragon did.

"Hallo, Bren," Ivo said. His voice was rough as gravel. "You brought friends."

"You knew we were coming."

"I've known for two days." Ivo glanced at the dragon. "She smelled the steel. She says you smell of coin and fear."

"She talks now?"

"She always talked. I'm finally listening well enough." Ivo's gaze went past Bren to the forty men fanned out behind him, and something in his face closed, quietly, like a door. "So this is how it ends, then. Not a fight. Just arithmetic."

"It doesn't have to be anything." Bren spread his empty hands. "Ivo. Come down off this mountain. Put the casket down, walk out with me, and I'll swear to the palace you died in the snow. I'll swear it on my mother's grave. You disappear, and the hunt closes over your name like water."

"And the casket?"

"Goes to Corvess. That's the price. They want it more than they want you."

Ivo laughed — a short, terrible sound with no floor to it. "You don't know what's in it."

"No. But I know what the chancellor would pay for it, and it would set every man here for life."

"Look at me, Bren." Ivo stepped forward, and up close his eyes were frightening — lit from inside by something being consumed. "Do I look like a man who climbed three days through winter passes carrying a box of trinkets? Do I look like a traitor selling kingdom-secrets?" He knelt, gripped the casket's rim. "Look. Just look. That's all I ever asked anyone. Look."

"Bren—" Doss started, hand drifting to his hilt.

"Steady." Bren couldn't take his eyes off Ivo. "What happens if I look?"

"Nothing you can't survive." Ivo's voice gentled. "It's not my gift. It's hers." He nodded at the dragon. "Fire remembers what it's fed. She'll show you the truth of it, and the truth of it will hurt, and then you'll understand why I'm spending myself like this. Or you'll draw your sword, and we'll find out which of us the mountain likes better." He turned the casket toward Bren. The golden light spilled up over his scarred knuckles. "Either way — look first. Please. For the boys we used to be."

The wind moaned across the crater's rim. Behind Bren, forty men waited on his word, and half of them, he could feel it, already had arrows nocked under their cloaks — Vane's people had gotten word after all, or simply smelled treason the way dogs smelled storms.

Bren thought of the decree nailed to the Corvess gates. *All gifted souls to be seized for examination.* He'd read it aloud to his company once, in his parade voice, and called it duty.

He thought of the prison barges on the Sere, and the prisoners who came off them quiet and smiling and wrong, their eyes like rooms with the furniture sold off.

He knelt.

He reached out and laid both palms flat on the thorn-iron rim of the casket.

* * *

The world went gold.

Not light — *memory*. It poured into him like a river breaking a dam, and he was drowning in a life that wasn't his:

*A woman's hands kneading dough in a low-beamed kitchen, flour to the wrists, humming — and Bren knew with sudden violent certainty that this was Ivo's mother, and that Ivo had put her face into this box three nights ago, and that the box had eaten it. Gone. He could feel the shape of the absence, a hole in the world shaped exactly like her.*

*His god's voice — Veyra, the silent god — saying a name. Saying* Ivo. *Warm as a hearthstone. Then seven years of silence pressing down like snowfall.*

*A campfire, and Bren's own laugh, younger, ringing off the pines, and Ivo grinning across the flames at him —* gods, *he'd forgotten how young they'd been —*

*A shackled prisoner's last coherent thought, given freely:* tell someone.

*A king, lucid for one flickering moment, weeping:* get it out of me, please, whoever you are, get it OUT—

*Bren wrenched his hands back and vomited onto the black glass.*

When he could see straight again, he was on his knees and Ivo was holding his shoulder, and the dragon's amber eye filled half the sky of his vision, patient as a mountain.

"Now you know," Ivo said quietly.

Bren wiped his mouth. Around him, the company stood in stunned silence — those nearest the casket swaying, pale, some weeping without knowing why; the memory leaked off the iron like heat off a forge and touched everyone.

"Doss." His voice came out shredded.

"Captain?"

"Arrows down. All of them. Now."

A pause. Then the soft sounds of bowstrings slacking, bolts returned to quivers, one by one, like a long sigh running down the line.

But not all of them. Bren counted. Farther up the slope, maybe fifteen men hadn't moved — Sergeant Hale's lot, crown-sworn twice over, and Hale's hand was nowhere near his quiver. It was on his horn.

"Hale," Bren said, standing slowly. "Don't."

"The chancellor pays for the box and the traitor," Hale said. His voice was steady. His eyes weren't. "You've all just committed treason in front of witnesses, Captain. Only question left is who reports it first."

"That thing in the palace is eating the *king*," Doss burst out. "You saw—"

"I saw a story. I saw a box full of lights and a sick dragon." Hale raised the horn to his lips.

What happened next happened fast. Marta Vane's voice cracked across the crater from the southern rim — she'd come up the south path after all, twenty strong — shouting Hale's name, an arrow already loosed that buried itself in the glass a foot from his boot. The dragon exhaled, a long rolling wave of heat that staggered the line and buckled the scree, and in the chaos Bren got a hand on Hale's wrist and twisted until the horn dropped and skittered away across the stone.

But he was a heartbeat too late. Somewhere above, a second horn answered — one of Hale's outriders, posted on the high shoulder, signaling down the long miles toward Corvess. Three notes, rising. The alarm.

Bren stood in the ringing silence afterward, his chest heaving, and looked at Ivo across the smoking glass.

"They heard that in the capital," he said hoarsely. "Every garrison between here and Corvess just got woken up. You've got days. Maybe hours."

Ivo turned to the dragon, and the great head swung down to him, and something passed between them too fast and too strange for Bren to follow — but he saw Ivo's face change, saw grief and resolve arrive together like weather.

"Then we fly tonight," Ivo said. "Or she dies on this mountain, and the kingdom dies with her."

Behind them, the alarm horn echoed off the peaks a third time, fading northward — toward the palace, toward the thing in the crown, toward everything that was coming.
