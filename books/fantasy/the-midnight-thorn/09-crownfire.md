## 9. Crownfire

The palace of Corvess had never known darkness like this. Emberveil's wings blotted out the moons as she came down over the city, and every bell in every tower began to scream at once.

Ivo felt her descent in his chest — a pressure, a heat, the last coals of a fire older than kingdoms. The thorn-iron casket hung against his ribs on a strap of dragon-leather, and through it he could feel what remained of himself: his mother's face, his god's voice, seven years of small warm things he had fed into the flame's keeping. Not all of it. He had kept one memory back, pressed beneath everything else like a coal at the heart of a banked fire. He did not look at it. Looking would mean spending it.

"Gatehouse," Bren said beside him, sword drawn, rain running off his jaw. Around them, thirty of the company stood in the mud below the outer wall — the half that had chosen truth over coin, faces pale under the light of the burning watchtowers. "Sable and Doss have the postern. We go through the kitchens, up the servants' stair, and we don't stop for anything that isn't wearing armor."

"And if something is wearing armor?"

Bren's grin had no humor in it. "Then it stops."

They went over the wall on grappling lines while the sky above them filled with fire. Emberveil was old — older than the mountains, older than Veyra's silence — but even dying, a dragon is a catastrophe given shape. She wheeled once over the inner keep, and her breath turned the crown garrison's ballistae to slag and the men who worked them to shadows on the stone. Her scales, which Ivo had last seen dull as riverbed slate, glowed along her spine like a seam of ore catching sun.

*She is spending herself,* he thought. *Every pass of her wings is a year off her life.*

He climbed faster.

* * *

The palace fought them the way a drowning man fights: wildly, without order, with terrible strength. Crown huntsmen met them in the gallery above the great hall, and the marble ran red before Sable's shield-wall broke them. Ivo killed two men whose names he knew, whose funerals he had drunk at, and felt nothing he could name except a cold clarity — the same clarity that came when he touched an object and let its dead speak. There was no room left in him for grief. He had spent too much of himself already; what remained was mostly purpose.

In the second corridor they found the drained ones.

They lined the walls of the servants' passage — prisoners from the hunt, dozens of them, propped upright by chains, their eyes open and empty as winter ponds. The Hollow Regent had drunk them to the dregs and left the shells standing, the way a man leaves cups after a feast. Ivo passed among them and heard, faintly, the way he always heard: not voices, but residue. A lullaby. A debt repaid. A wedding. Scraps of lives, chewed thin.

One of them moved. A girl, no older than sixteen, fingers twitching against her chain as Ivo's boots rang past. Her lips shaped a word with no breath behind it.

He stopped. He should not have stopped. Every heartbeat mattered now; Emberveil was falling out of the sky somewhere behind him and the king's lucid hour was bleeding away. But he stopped, and he took the girl's hand, and he read the shackle — because shackles remembered, shackles had taught him the truth of this whole rotten hunt — and what poured into him was not memory but hunger. The entity's hunger, soaked into the iron like wine into bread. It was *close*. It was *awake*. And it knew, suddenly, that something carrying fire was walking toward it.

The girl sagged. Dead, or past anything he could call alive. Ivo closed her eyes with two fingers and kept moving, and told himself the shaking in his hands was only the climb.

"The throne room's ahead," Bren said quietly. He had seen. He said nothing else. That, more than anything, was why Ivo had come back to him.

* * *

The doors of the throne hall were oak and iron, tall as three men, and they were already open.

Of course they were. It wanted him to come in.

Ivo walked through alone. He had asked for that much, and Bren — who had argued every other point of the night, who had called him a fool with real feeling — had finally gone silent and given it to him. The defectors held the doors behind him. The casket strap bit into his shoulder. Somewhere above the palace, a shadow the size of a cathedral beat its wings lower, lower, answering a call only the two oldest things in Thornfell could hear.

The throne hall stretched away under vaults of black stone. Torches burned in their brackets, but their light bent strangely, curving inward toward the dais as if the room itself were an eye trying to focus.

King Aldric sat on the throne. Or the shape of King Aldric did.

He was thinner than he had been a month ago, when Ivo had knelt before him in chains and glimpsed the wrongness behind his eyes. Thinner, grayer, his skin drawn tight as parchment over a skull that was not quite the right shape anymore. And the crown —

The crown was black.

It had been gold once; Ivo had seen portraits in the lowland towns, seen the coins. Now it drank the torchlight and gave nothing back, and it had grown, he realized, roots. Thin filaments of darkness ran down from its band into the king's scalp, into his temples, vanishing beneath the skin like ivy into a wall. As Ivo watched, one of the filaments pulsed.

"You brought it," said a voice.

It came from everywhere — from the vaults, from the floor, from inside Ivo's own skull. Not Aldric's voice. Something using the shape of a voice, the way a thief uses a stolen coat.

"You brought my dinner wrapped in fire." The king's head tilted, an angle no neck should allow. His eyes were white, all white, and full of a vast and patient amusement. "Do you know how long I have waited, little reader? Your kind are so *nourishing*. Memories with salt on them. Fear seasons everything." The mouth smiled. "But you — you've been feeding yourself to a lizard. You've come to me *empty*. What do you imagine you can do?"

Ivo walked forward. One step. Two. The torches guttered as he passed, bowing toward the throne.

"I imagine," he said, "that freely given memories burn hotter than stolen ones."

And he unslung the casket.

* * *

Emberveil hit the roof of the throne hall like the fist of a god.

Stone exploded. Rain and moonlight poured through the wound, and the dragon poured through after them — too big, impossibly, folding herself into the space like a flame folding into a lamp, her wings shredding banners five centuries old. She landed between Ivo and the throne, and the heat of her body made the air itself shimmer and weep.

*Now,* she said, in the voice that was not a voice but a warmth behind the breastbone. *Open it. Open it and GIVE.*

Ivo opened the casket.

The memories rose like smoke given weight. He saw them as they left him — his mother at the washline, turning, laughing at something he could no longer recall her saying; the first time he'd touched a dead soldier's sword and heard its history and wept and thought, *this is holy, this must be holy*; the taste of bread shared with strangers in a mountain hut during the worst winter of his life; his mother's face again, because he had stored it twice, unable to choose which angle of her smile to lose. They streamed upward into Emberveil's open jaws, and her throat lit from within, gold going to white going to a color that has no name because nothing living has seen it since the world was young.

Dragon-fire does not burn wood or flesh. Dragon-fire burns *binding* — oath, enchantment, chain, lie. It burns the thing that holds a thing in place.

The Hollow Regent screamed.

Every torch in the hall went out. In the dark there was only the fire — a lance of unbearable dawn in Emberveil's jaws — and the sound, the terrible sound, a shriek that was not one voice but thousands, all the drained prisoners, all the kings it had eaten, all the minds chewed down to gristle across centuries of crowns.

*Ivo.* Emberveil's warmth brushed him, gentle as his mother's hand. *Your arm. Give me your arm.*

He raised it. The flame washed over him without heat, without harm, and in its light he saw the filaments clearly — saw where they rooted in the king, saw the seams of the binding, generations deep, layer upon layer of dark consent. And he saw the one thread that was not dark: a single filament of ordinary human terror, buried deepest, still alive. Still *him*.

Ivo seized the burning light in both hands — it let him, it was meant to be carried, that had always been its purpose — and drove it against the crown.

* * *

The world became noise and then became silence, and the silence was louder.

The Hollow Regent came apart the way a storm comes apart: all at once, violently, into rain. The filaments shriveled and smoked. The black crown cracked down the middle with a sound like a mountain's spine breaking, and the two halves fell ringing to the marble, and out of them poured a darkness so old it had forgotten how to be afraid — it simply dissolved, screaming its thousands of voices thinner and thinner until there was nothing left to hear.

King Aldric collapsed.

Ivo caught him before he struck the steps. The man in his arms weighed nothing. The eyes that fluttered open were blue again, and human, and utterly lost — a man waking in a house where every wall has been torn down, searching for the rooms he lived in and finding only sky.

"I had—" the king whispered. His fingers plucked at Ivo's sleeve, weak as a child's. "There was a garden. My wife had a garden. Why can't I—"

"She'll remember it for both of you," Ivo said, and did not know if it was mercy or a lie.

Above them, wings.

Emberveil rose through the broken roof into the rain, climbing, climbing, silhouetted against the moons — and then her fire failed. Ivo watched the glow die along her spine, scale by scale, like embers going out one by one in a grate. She stalled. She tipped. She fell, silent, enormous, graceful as grief, beyond the walls of Corvess, and the ground-shudder of her landing rolled through the city like a long exhalation.

The last dragon was done.

Ivo knelt on the marble steps of a freed throne, holding a hollowed king, listening to the rain fall through the ruined roof, and reached — without meaning to, without deciding — for the one memory he had refused to surrender.

It was still there. Warm. Whole. Waiting.

He did not open it yet. But he knew, with a certainty that had nothing to do with his gift, what he would find when he finally did — and who he would need to be, afterward, to carry it north into the snow.
