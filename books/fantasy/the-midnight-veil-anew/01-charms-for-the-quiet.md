## 1. Charms for the Quiet

There are three rules to surviving in Greyhollow, and I learned all of them the hard way, which is to say I broke each one at least twice before the lessons stuck.

Rule one: never let them see you hope. Rule two: never let them hear you pray. Rule three: never, under any circumstances, let them catch you doing magic.

I run a charm shop on Lantern Row, wedged between a chandler who waters his tallow and a cobbler who talks to his lasts like they're estranged relatives. The shop is called *Quiet Things*, and if that name sounds like a confession, well. Everything in Greyhollow is a confession if you squint hard enough. The city was built by people who had something to hide—heretics, debtors, one very ambitious goat thief—and it has spent two hundred years perfecting the art of looking harmless. We are a city of shuttered windows and polite silences. We are very good at being nobody.

I am the best nobody on this street.

My charms are famous—or they would be, if anyone could admit to buying them. Knotted threads against nightmares. Salt-and-iron sachets for doorways. Little wax seals pressed with a sigil no one recognizes anymore, sold as "old-fashioned luck pieces" to grandmothers who remember more than they say. Every item in my inventory works exactly as advertised: through craft, through suggestion, through the stubborn human refusal to stop believing in small protections. Not one of them requires the gift. That's the trick. That's always been the trick. A charm that works by magic is evidence. A charm that works by faith is just commerce.

The gift itself—the true thing, the thing I was sworn to use and then sworn never to touch—I keep folded up small inside my chest like a letter I'm afraid to open. Fifteen years now. Fifteen years since Veil went silent, since the god I gave my voice and years and sleepless vigils to stopped answering, and I walked out of the last chapel still standing and became this: a shopkeeper. A woman with clean hands and a locked cellar and a face designed to be forgotten.

I've gotten frighteningly good at it. The tax collector walks past my window without slowing. The watch captain buys candles from my neighbor and nods at me like I'm furniture. Even the rats seem to regard me as part of the architecture. Invisibility, it turns out, is not a gift you're born with. It's a discipline. You practice it the way monks practice vigil—except my vigil is performed in full daylight, in front of everyone, and the god I'm keeping it for hasn't spoken in fifteen years.

This morning started the way all my mornings start, which is to say with salt.

I throw a pinch across the threshold before I unbar the door. Old habit. Older than the law against it, older than the purge, older than the silence. It doesn't do anything—not really, not anymore—but it's how I tell the day I'm paying attention. Somewhere in the drowned dark behind the world, maybe something notices. Maybe not. Faith after silence is a strange animal. It stops being a conversation and becomes a practice, like sweeping a floor for someone who's moved out. You do it because stopping would mean admitting they're gone.

The morning market was loud in the way Greyhollow does loud: everyone talking, no one saying anything. Fish prices. Roof leaks. Someone's cousin arrested for "unlicensed gathering," which is what they call it when a hedge-witch picks herbs off crown land. The proclamation boards were freshly papered—I'd noticed that yesterday with the particular dread of a woman who reads official notices the way other people read weather—and the new sheets were thicker, blacker inked, crowned with the double-headed seal that means the order came down from the palace itself and not from some ambitious magistrate padding his numbers.

*By decree of His Majesty, all bearers of the unsanctioned gift shall present themselves for registration within thirty days. Those who conceal their nature commit treason against the crown and the realm's salvation. Informants will be rewarded. Harborers will be judged alongside.*

Salvation. That word was doing a lot of work, standing there in royal ink. Kings used to burn witches to save souls; now they registered them to save the realm. The vocabulary changed every few decades but the arithmetic never did: someone with power they didn't grant must be a threat, and threats are best handled early.

I read it twice, memorized it out of pure professional habit—you can't survive fifteen years underground without knowing exactly what the ground is doing—and went home to sell charms.

* * *

Midafternoon, the bell over my door rang, and in came a woman I knew only as Maren.

She came maybe once a month, always at odd hours, always alone, always with her hood up even indoors, which in Greyhollow is less a fashion statement than a lifestyle. She bought the same things every time: a nightmare-knot for her daughter, who dreamed of drowning; a doorway sachet; and once, memorably, a small wax seal she asked me to press with a symbol she drew herself—a circle broken by a single vertical line. She didn't explain it. I didn't ask. In this business, the questions you don't ask are the most valuable stock you carry.

Today she wanted the usual knot and something else. Something new.

"I need—" she started, and then stopped, and looked around my shop the way guilty people look around rooms, checking corners for ears that aren't there. There are no ears in my shop. I've checked. I check daily. It's rule three's lesser-known corollary: paranoia isn't a flaw in Greyhollow, it's maintenance.

"You need what?" I said, gently, the way you talk to a spooked bird.

"My son." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "He's six. He does things. Small things. He makes the fire lean toward him when he's cold. He found my lost ring by—by knowing where it was. He doesn't know he's doing it. But the neighbors have noticed. The neighbors always notice."

I stood very still behind my counter, hands flat on the wood where she could see them, and felt the old familiar vertigo. Because here was the truth I lived inside daily, the truth that made my whole trade possible and unbearable: half my customers weren't buying superstition. They were buying cover. They came to Quiet Things because a woman who sells harmless charms is exactly the kind of woman a suspicious neighbor expects to find, and as long as I looked like the safe kind of odd, they could hide their dangerous kind of odd inside my ordinariness. I was a decoy fire. People warmed themselves at my smoke.

And every month, Maren came and bought a knot for her daughter's dreams, and I took her coin, and we both pretended that was all it was.

"The boy needs to learn to stop," she said. "Can you teach him? I've heard—I've heard there are people who know how to quiet it. To fold it down small. Like folding a blanket."

There are. I'm one of them. Teaching a child to smother the gift is the first thing any sworn servant of Veil learns, back when there were sworn servants of Veil and learning things wasn't a capital offense. *The gift is a tide*, my teacher told me, a lifetime ago, in a chapel that doesn't exist anymore except in ash. *You cannot stop a tide, little wren. But you can teach it to come in quietly.* I taught four children, once. Before. I don't think about the before much. Thinking about the before is like pressing on a bruise to check if it still hurts. It always still hurts.

But that was then, when teaching was holy work. Now it was a rope with a noose at both ends: if I taught him and got caught, I died. If I refused and the boy's gift bloomed untrained, he'd do something obvious in front of the wrong person, and he'd die, and probably his mother too, and probably whoever they decided had harbored him, which would be me, because whose shop did she buy her charms from?

"I can't teach anyone anything," I said. "I sell knots and salt. You know that."

"I know what I know," Maren said, and her eyes over the edge of her hood were steady and desperate in equal measure, which is a combination I've seen so many times it has its own shelf in my memory, organized by year.

"Maren." I kept my voice level. Kind, even. Kindness is free and costs nothing if you don't follow it anywhere. "Take the knot. Take two. Hang one over his bed and tell him it keeps bad dreams out, and while he believes it, he'll sleep, and while he sleeps, he won't play with fire. Belief is a leash. Use it. And keep him away from water—he leans toward it, they always lean toward the element that frightens them—and don't let anyone see him *knowing* things. That's the tell. Fire can be an accident. Knowing cannot."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "You've done this before."

"I've sold a lot of knots."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

She counted out her coin—too much of it, the way scared people pay, as if generosity were another kind of ward—and I wrapped the knots in plain brown paper, no sigil, nothing identifiable, and put them in her hand, and for about ninety seconds everything was fine. Ordinary. Safe. The bell hadn't rung again, the street outside was its usual murmur of carefully unremarkable life, and I was just a charm-seller handing a mother a nightmare-charm, which is possibly the most legal transaction that occurs in this entire city.

Then the shadow crossed my window.

* * *

You learn shadows, in my line of survival. Most of them mean nothing. Cats. Carts. The chandler's apprentice hauling tallow with theatrical slowness to peek in my windows, hoping to catch me at something worth reporting, because informing pays better than candlework and everyone on Lantern Row knows it. I catalogued this one automatically: tall, moving wrong, moving *together*—three bodies moving as one shape, which is how hunters walk, shoulder to shoulder, unhurried, because hurry is for people who might fail.

Crown hunters don't hurry. Why would they? The law is a net and nets don't need to sprint.

They stopped outside my door. All three. The middle one consulted a folded paper, and I watched his lips move as he read, and I watched the other two look up at my sign—*QUIET THINGS*, painted in my own careful hand, the most innocuous words I could devise fifteen years ago—and I understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming, that the paper was a list, and my street was on it, and my door was being matched against it.

Maren saw them too. Of course she did. Mothers see everything; it's the one gift no law has ever managed to outlaw.

"Don't move," she whispered, though I hadn't. "Don't look at them. Don't look at me."

The door opened without the bell ringing—they'd flattened it with a palm, professionals, someone had trained them—and the room filled with grey cloaks and the particular smell of them, oiled leather and cold iron and underneath it something I couldn't name, something that made the hair on my arms stand up and my folded gift stir against its wrappings like a dog hearing its name. I hadn't felt it move in years. I'd nearly forgotten it could.

"Shopkeep," said the middle one. He had a voice like a ledger closing. "We're looking for a woman. Hooded. Comes here monthly."

"This is a charm shop," I said. My voice came out perfectly bored, and I want credit for that, because underneath the boredom my heart was trying to claw its way out through my collarbone. "Half the district comes here monthly. Grandmothers. Sailors' wives. The cobbler buys his wife a luck-seal every anniversary. Am I to give you names?"

He looked at me. Really looked—the long assessing look of a man deciding whether I was furniture or a problem. Furniture, he decided. For now. I watched the decision happen and made myself smaller inside it, made my hands busier than they needed to be among my thread and wax, made myself into exactly the harmless, slightly tedious tradeswoman my whole life had been rehearsing.

Maren stood three feet from me with her purchase in her hand, and I could feel her terror the way you feel weather coming. She had pulled her hood lower. She had angled her body toward the wall. She was performing invisibility with a skill that told me she'd been practicing far longer than I'd known her, and for a moment—just a moment—I let myself believe the performance would hold, because it had held for years, because it always held, because the whole rotten machinery of my survival depended on it holding—

The hunter's gaze snagged on her.

It was nothing. It was everything. It was the brown paper in her hand, and the way she held it—clutched, protective, the grip of a woman carrying something that mattered—and the way she stood, weight on her back foot, already leaning toward escape. Guilt has a posture. Fear has a posture. I know both intimately and I have drilled myself out of them, hour upon hour before my own mirror, until I could stand in a searchlight wearing a stolen uniform and look merely tired. Maren had not had fifteen years of mirror-drill. Maren had a six-year-old at home who leaned fires toward himself, and every night of her life she had gone to sleep knowing it.

"Hood down," the ledger-voice said.

Her hand rose halfway and stopped. Halfway. The exact midpoint between obedience and refusal, held there trembling, and I understood in that instant that she was going to refuse, that some line had been crossed inside her that had nothing to do with law or sense, that somewhere in the arithmetic of her fear the number for *my children* had finally outweighed the number for *my skin*.

"I'll need a reason," she said. Her voice shook. It also held. Both things can be true; I am living proof.

"Reason enough," he said, and unfolded his paper, and read her name off it.

Not a description. Her *name*. Which meant someone had given it. Which meant the list was not a dragnet—it was a harvest, gathered neighbor from neighbor, coin by coin, the informant's reward doing its work exactly as the proclamation promised. I thought of the proclamation's word again. *Salvation.* This was what salvation smelled like: oiled leather, and a mother's held breath, and my own silence.

Because here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I get, whether it's shallow or deep: I said nothing.

She looked at me. One second, maybe less. Over the hunter's shoulder, through the grey wall of him, her eyes found mine and asked the question that had no safe answer, the question I had spent fifteen years arranging my entire existence to never be asked. *Help me.* And I looked back at her with my furniture-face on and my hands busy among my harmless wares and my gift folded small and silent in my chest where it has been since the god went quiet, and I did nothing, because doing something meant burning down everything, and I had built my whole self on the belief that the fire could be avoided forever if I was only careful enough, only small enough, only quiet enough.

They took her at the door. Not gently. Not cruelly, either—that was somehow worse, the bureaucratic middle of it, the practiced efficiency, the way they turned her by the elbow like a door on a hinge. She didn't fight. I think she'd understood before I did that fighting was for people with somewhere to run, and there was nowhere, there had never been anywhere, there was only Greyhollow and its careful silences and me, standing in my shop full of quiet things, being the quietest thing in it.

At the threshold she turned her head. Just her head. The hunters didn't notice; it was a small motion, deniable, the last economy of a frightened woman.

"They'll come for him next," she said. Not to them. To me. "The boy. When they question her—when my daughter tells them about the fire that leans—he's next. You know what they do to children who can't stop."

I knew. God help me, I knew exactly. I had signed petitions about it in another life, in a chapel that burned, with a name I no longer answered to.

"Please," she said.

The door closed. The bell above it, crushed flat by the hunter's palm, swung once and settled, and the sound it made was the smallest sound I have ever heard, and the loudest.

* * *

I closed the shop. Locked the door. Put the shutters up in broad afternoon like a woman going into mourning, which I suppose I was, though not for Maren—Maren I had already failed to mourn properly by failing to act, and grief that arrives after cowardice tastes mostly of ash. No. What I mourned, standing alone in my dim shop among my knotted threads and sealed wax and honest salt, was the illusion. The fragile, painstaking, fifteen-years-in-the-building illusion that invisibility was safety. That smallness was armor. That if I made myself quiet enough, harmless enough, *nothing* enough, the tide would go around me and leave me standing.

The tide had come through my door. It had taken a woman from my counter, mid-purchase, mid-kindness, mid-*please*—and it had taken her using a list, and the list had names on it, and I would have bet everything I owned that my name was on it too, filed under *harborer* or *unregistered associate* or simply *watch further*, because that's how these things work, that's how they have always worked: first they take the obvious ones, and then they take the ones who served the obvious ones, and then they take the ones who watched.

I sat down at my workbench and looked at my hands. Charm-seller's hands. Clean, careful, competent at nothing that mattered. Fifteen years ago they had been other hands—they had woven wards and quieted storms and once, in the chapel's last hour, held a dying man's gift as it left him, folding it small the way you'd fold a flag, because no one else would and someone had to. Those hands were gone. Or rather, these hands were those hands, folded around a gift I hadn't touched in fifteen years, wrapped around a silence I had mistaken for absence.

Veil, I thought. Not prayed—praying is audible, praying has a shape, and rule two still stood even alone in a shuttered shop. Just the name, set down inside myself like a coin on a dead man's eyes. *Veil. Are you there? Are you anywhere? Do you see what they're doing in your name, in the king's name, in the name of salvation?*

Nothing. The same nothing as fifteen years. The same nothing as every dawn-salt and every midnight vigil I'd kept out of habit long after hope had stopped attending. My god was silent. My city was making lists. A six-year-old boy who leaned fires toward himself was going to be taken apart by men with ledgers for voices, and the only person in Greyhollow who knew how to teach him to fold the tide down small was me, and I had said *I sell knots and salt*, and she had paid me too much, and walked into the net.

I sat in the dark of my shuttered shop until the light went blue and then went gone.

And then I lit one lamp—just one, shaded, the way I do everything—and I went down to the cellar I have kept locked for fifteen years, and I stood in front of the door with my key in my hand, and I heard, from the street above, muffled and distant and unmistakable, boots. Several sets. Moving slowly along Lantern Row. Stopping. Starting again. Stopping.

Working door to door.

The purge hadn't reached my neighborhood. I'd told myself that for weeks, the way you tell yourself the cough is nothing, the way you tell yourself the water rising around your ankles is a flood *somewhere else*. But the tide was in the street now. It was measuring my door. And somewhere across the city, in a room I didn't want to imagine, a six-year-old was waiting for his mother to come home with a brown paper parcel, and she was never coming, and every hour I spent being nobody was an hour closer to the night they came for him.

My hand shook around the key. Not from fear. From something worse—from the sudden, terrible, fifteen-years-deferred understanding that the silence I'd been keeping wasn't safety. It was just a longer version of what I'd done today at my counter. I had been saying *nothing* to my god for fifteen years, and calling it devotion, and he had never once answered, and tonight, listening to boots count their way down Lantern Row, I finally understood that maybe he hadn't been refusing to speak.

Maybe he'd been waiting for me to stop hiding first.

The boots stopped outside my door.

I turned the key.
