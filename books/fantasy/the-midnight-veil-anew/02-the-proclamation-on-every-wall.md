## 2. The Proclamation on Every Wall

The boots outside my door belonged to a crier, which is the only reason I'm alive to tell you this.

I stood with my hand still on the lock, heart hammering out some kind of apology to no one, while a boy of perhaps fourteen unrolled a paper against my doorframe and began to read in the flat, bored voice of someone who has said these words forty times since dawn and will say them forty more before supper.

"By decree of the Crown of Veylmark: all persons bearing the unsanctioned gift shall present themselves for registration at the Greyhollow Assize within ten days. Registered gift-bearers will be assessed, catalogued, and assigned lawful employ. Unregistered gift-bearers will be treated as enemies of the realm."

A pause. The boy licked his thumb and turned the page.

"Harboring an unregistered gift-bearer is treason. Selling to one is treason. *Knowing* one—" he stumbled over the word, or maybe I did, "—and failing to report, is treason. The Crown thanks its loyal subjects. Long live the king."

He moved on down Lantern Row. I heard him at the baker's, then the cooper's, then fading toward the square, that same bored recitation, the same *long live the king*, delivered with all the passion of a laundry list.

Long live the king. That was the joke, wasn't it. Nobody had seen the king in three years. He'd fallen ill, they said, and ruled from behind closed doors through his ministers, and somewhere between his illness and this decree, the whole kingdom had decided that the cure for everything was to hunt down anyone who could light a candle without flint.

I didn't open the door right away. I stood there in my dark little shop, surrounded by honest charms—knots against nightmares, braided thread for safe travel, salt-and-iron trinkets that worked by craft and superstition and nothing else—and I listened to my own breathing and thought, very calmly, like a woman reading her own eulogy: *ten days.*

Then I opened the door, tore the proclamation off my frame, and read it myself, because I am a professional, and professionals check their sources.

*Assessed, catalogued, and assigned.* Lovely phrasing. You could almost hear the quill scratching it out, some clerk in the capital choosing words the way a butcher chooses cuts. *Catalogued* like jars of preserves. *Assigned* like mules.

And underneath, in smaller script, the part that made my mouth go dry:

*Registration includes declaration of the bearer's affinity, its strength, and any charms, wards, or talismans currently in the bearer's possession, whether self-made or purchased.*

Purchased.

I looked up and down Lantern Row, at the proclamations already nailed to every post and shutter and doorframe in sight, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the hunters dragged old Maren Colm away yesterday afternoon.

Every charm I had ever sold carried my maker's knot. It was how I signed my work—it's how every charm-seller signs their work; it's tradition, it's pride, it's *branding*, and I had spent fifteen years being proud of a signature that now functioned as a list of names.

Not a list of gift-bearers, mind. Most of my customers were exactly what they appeared: nervous brides, sailors' wives, grandmothers with arthritic hands. My knots worked on faith and good craftsmanship, nothing more. But scattered among them—the ones who came after closing, who paid in old coin, who never met my eyes—were the others. The hidden ones. Like me. And every one of them walked out of my shop wearing a little braided marker that said, in the language of knots: *made by Juniper Reyes of Lantern Row.*

If the crown wanted a census of the gifted, they wouldn't need diviners. They'd need only follow the thread backward. From charm to seller. From seller to ledger.

From ledger to names.

* * *

Maren Colm lived two doors down, above the cobbler's, and she had been coming to me for eleven years.

She was a weather-singer—not a dramatic one, nothing you'd put in a ballad. She could coax rain off a wedding day and talk frost out of a late planting, small mercies, garden-sized miracles. She paid me in honey and gossip and once, memorably, a goose. She called me "girl" though I was thirty-one, because she had known me since I was twenty and apparently age froze at the point of first acquaintance.

Yesterday they took her in the middle of the afternoon. Not even the decency of night. Four hunters in grey-and-gold, a paper with her name on it, and when she asked what she'd done, the captain said—and I will hear this until I die—"*Nothing yet. That's the point of registration.*"

They put her in a cart. She looked at my window as they passed. Just a glance, half a heartbeat, but I saw her see me standing there behind my glass, motionless, invisible, doing nothing, and I saw her understand it, and forgive it, which was infinitely worse than if she'd spat at me.

That night I hadn't slept. That night I'd stood at my counter and told myself there was nothing to be done, one woman against four hunters, and every word of it true, and none of it any comfort, because truth is not comfort, truth is just the thing you're left holding when comfort runs out.

Now there was a proclamation on my wall giving me ten days to decide whether Maren's glance would be the last thing I ever gave her.

* * *

I burned the ledger at midnight.

Not immediately. First I sat with it for two hours, which tells you something about me—I am a woman who needs to negotiate with her own decisions before committing to them. I sat at my kitchen table with the ledger open and a candle lit and a fire banked low, and I read names.

Dorrit Hale, weaver. Knot against fever, sold twice. Gifted? Maybe. Her son had the bright eyes. I'd never asked. Asking was dangerous for both of us; not asking was the courtesy we extended each other, the way you don't look directly at the sun.

The Fenwick brothers, boatwrights. Warding knots for their hulls. Both sworn quiet as stones, but their mother had been a river-caller, and gifts run in families like water finds low ground.

Tam—no surname given, a workhouse boy who bought a cooling-knot for his hands, paid in scavenged copper, hands that shook too much for a child of nine or ten. I'd told myself he was just a nervous kid. I'd told myself a lot of things.

Page after page. Eleven years of names in my cramped, careful hand. Some certainly innocent. Some certainly not. And here was the thing that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone: it didn't matter. Innocence wasn't a shield anymore; the decree made no distinction between a hedge-witch and a woman who talked to her bread dough a little too lovingly. Registration first, questions later—or possibly never, depending on how the assessment went, and nobody knew what assessment meant, and everybody could imagine.

So: burn it, and the names are safe. Burn it, and I'm safe, because a ledger is evidence, evidence is a confession, and I have spent fifteen years perfecting the art of leaving no confession behind.

But—

But if I fled Greyhollow tonight, and I left the ledger intact, and the hunters found it, everyone in it would hang. And if I burned it and stayed, I stayed blind—no clients, no network, no way to warn the ones who needed warning. And if I burned it and fled—

I got up, paced, sat back down. This is also part of the negotiation.

Here is what nobody tells you about hiding: it gets comfortable. Fifteen years of careful living builds itself into a shell around you, layer by layer, until one day you realize the shell has a name and a shop and a favorite teacup, and you cannot remember what it felt like to be the thing inside it. Sworn to Veil, the god of silence and thresholds and things-not-said, I had taken his silence and made it a career. I was so good at not-being-seen that I'd begun to mistake it for virtue.

The proclamation on my wall disagreed. The proclamation said: *your invisibility was always rented, and the rent just came due.*

I fed the ledger to the fire page by page, because feeding it in whole would smoke too much and smoke draws attention, and even my acts of defiance had to be discreet. That's the tragedy of a careful life. Even burning your sins, you burn them tidily.

Dorrit Hale's name curled and went orange and was gone. The Fenwick brothers. The boy Tam, whose cooling-knot I'd undercharged because his hands shook. Eleven years of my handwriting becoming heat, becoming nothing, becoming the only protection I could offer anyone anymore: *we were never here.*

When it was ash I sat back down and put my face in my hands.

Because here was the other stone in my chest, the one under the first one: the charms. Every gifted client I'd ever served wore my maker's knot, and the decree specifically demanded declaration of *purchased talismans*. Which meant that when the hunters came for Dorrit Hale—if they came for Dorrit Hale—she would be asked where she got her warding knot, and she would either lie badly or confess, and either way the thread ran straight back to Lantern Row, to a shop that officially sold only honest work.

My signature. My pride. My brand.

I had marked every one of them. Lovingly. With a steady hand. The way farmers mark sheep.

"You didn't know," I said aloud to my empty kitchen, in the tone of someone arguing with a lawyer who keeps winning. "Nobody knew. There was no law, no hunt, no—"

There had been whispers, though. For a year at least, whispers out of the capital: the king ill, the court strange, priests of the loud gods saying the silence of Veil was an omen. Whispers I'd ignored, because ignoring whispers was my religion's entire liturgy.

And underneath the whispers, deeper, older, the thing I really couldn't argue with: fifteen years ago I had felt my god go silent, and I had grieved, and then—this is the shameful part—I had gotten *on with it*. Built the shop. Made the knots. Learned to sleep. I had mourned Veil the way you mourn a distant father, with occasional letters never sent, and I had let the world convince me that grief was a season and seasons pass.

Tonight, watching his name—insofar as a silent god has a name, insofar as ash remembers ink—tonight I understood that his silence and the king's sickness might be the same silence. Might be the same *event*. I had no proof. I had a feeling in my sternum, which is where sworn mages keep their proof.

Fifteen years ago, Veil stopped speaking. Fifteen years ago, the king fell ill. And now the crown was harvesting the gifted like a starving man stripping a pantry.

I am not a scholar. But I can do sums.

* * *

Near dawn, someone knocked.

Three soft raps, spaced like a heartbeat. Then, after a pause, three more—the rhythm old Maren used when she came for her monthly charm, back when she was alive and free and two doors down instead of wherever the carts go.

I nearly opened the door on hope alone, which shows you how far gone I was. Hope is the most dangerous thing a hiding woman can carry; it rattles.

I looked through the shutter crack instead.

It wasn't Maren. Of course it wasn't. It was a stranger—a tall woman in a plain traveling cloak, hood back, hair dark with premature silver at the temples, standing very still in the grey pre-dawn light like someone accustomed to being watched. She had the posture of a soldier and the eyes of something worse. An inquisitor's eyes. I'd seen that look once, years ago, across a market square, and I had gone home and not left my shop for a week.

On her cheekbone: the brand. Fresh enough to still be angry-red. A traitor's mark, the order's own sigil turned against its bearer, burned in reverse.

An ex-inquisitor, at my door, before sunrise, the day after the decree. In the old stories this is where the wise woman bars the door and the story ends well.

I opened it anyway. Blame the ledger's ashes. Blame Maren's glance through the glass. Blame fifteen years of a silent god and the growing, terrible suspicion that he'd been waiting for me to stop hiding first—because when the woman spoke, her voice low and rough as gravel under boot, what she said was:

"Juniper Reyes. I need a warding charm that only a sworn mage can make." Her eyes flicked past me, into the dim shop, cataloguing everything the way soldiers catalogue rooms. "And you're going to want to hear what I know about why your god stopped talking."

Somewhere across the city, a bell began to toll—not the morning bell, the wrong bell, the alarm—and the woman didn't even flinch.

"Or," she said, "you can bar the door and burn the rest of your ledgers. But they're already pulling people out of the tannery district, and the list they're working from has your street on it."

I stood in my doorway, halfway between the life I'd built and whatever was coming for it, and I stepped aside to let her in.
