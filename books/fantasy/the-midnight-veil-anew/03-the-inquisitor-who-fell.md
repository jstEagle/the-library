## 3. The Inquisitor Who Fell

There is a particular sound a person makes when they realize the institution that raised them has decided to kill them. It isn't a scream. It's quieter than that—a small exhale, almost a laugh, the body's way of saying *oh* before the mind catches up.

I made that sound at half past nine on a Tuesday morning, standing in the interrogation wing of the Bastion Saint Verre with a confession writ in one hand and a pair of questioner's pincers I had no intention of using.

The child on the table was seven years old.

Her name was Maren Coyle. I knew it because I'd read her intake file the night before, twice, the second time slower, as if rereading might change the contents. Daughter of a tanner. Gift-bearer, unregistered. Her crime, according to the hunter who'd dragged her in, was making the well water taste like honey for three streets around Tanners' Row. Three streets of sweet water. That was what we were torturing children for now.

"Begin when you're ready, Vane," said Brother Calloway from behind the observation glass. His voice came through the speaking-tube all tinny and warm, the voice of a man watching something he found mildly diverting. "The Marshal wants the writ signed by vespers."

I looked at the pincers. They were good steel—Bastion-forged, blessed by three different chaplains, oiled every morning by novices who believed, sincerely, that the work they enabled was holy. I had oiled those pincers myself for six years. I had believed it too.

"Inquisitor Vane," Calloway prompted, when I didn't move.

"You know," I said, mostly to myself, "when I joined the Order, they told me the gift was a wound in the world. Something to be cauterized. They never mentioned it could look like a seven-year-old giving away free honey."

"The age of the vessel doesn't alter the corruption."

"No," I agreed. "It just alters how it screams."

Silence from the tube. The kind of silence that gets recorded in reports later.

Maren was watching me. She'd stopped crying somewhere in the night—I'd heard it through the wall, the small exhausted sobs tapering off into that terrible adult quiet children find when they understand no one is coming. Now she just watched, and her hands were curled into fists against the restraint straps, and I thought: *she's bracing. Seven years old and she already knows how to brace.*

Here is the thing nobody tells you about being an inquisitor: you don't fall in a single dramatic moment. You fall in increments, each one small enough to explain away. You tell yourself the third degree is mercy because it ends things faster. You tell yourself the ones who die in custody were guilty of something, probably, or would have been. You tell yourself stories until the day comes when someone puts pincers in your hand and points you at a child, and you discover there are no more stories left between you and what you're actually doing.

I set the pincers down on the tray. Carefully. The way you set down something you never want to pick up again.

"Sera." Calloway's voice had changed—not warmer, but closer, the way you speak to a horse that's spooking. He only used my first name when he wanted something. "Whatever this is, it passes. It passed for Duvall in '41, it'll pass for you. Unlock the door, sign the writ, take the penance rites. We'll call it doubt. Doubt is forgivable."

Doubt is forgivable. Disobedience is not. That was always the Order's arithmetic, and God help me, I had done the sums faithfully for twenty years.

"Maren Coyle," I said into the room, loud enough for the glass, "your gift made water sweet. That's the whole charge. And I'm supposed to make you confess to consorting with the Unseen because a writ needs a signature and signatures need crimes." I unbuckled her wrist strap. Then the other one. "I can't do that anymore."

She sat up slowly, rubbing her wrists, and looked at me with the flat, evaluating stare of a child deciding whether the new danger is worse than the old one. Smart kid.

"Calloway," I said to the tube, "tell the Marshal the writ goes unsigned. Tell him Sera Vane has resigned."

Then I picked up the girl, because the screaming started about four seconds after that.

* * *

You would think an order of professional interrogators would be easier to escape. In fairness, I made it further than most people would have—the training cuts both ways—but not because I was clever. Because I was *known*. Every guard on the west stair hesitated half a heartbeat when they saw my face, and half a heartbeat is a lifetime when your life depends on it.

I went out through the reliquary, which sounds dramatic and was actually just the only corridor with a window low enough to fit through while carrying a child. Behind us the Bastion's bells began their alarm pattern—three strikes, pause, three strikes—and Maren pressed her face into my shoulder and shook.

"Don't look back," I told her, which is easy advice to give when you yourself have spent twenty years looking forward so hard you never once checked what was behind you.

We got two streets. Two streets of Greyhollow's grey morning, fishmongers and cart traffic and ordinary people living ordinary lives, and then a hunter patrol turned the corner ahead of us and there was nowhere left to be clever. So I did the last thing an inquisitor should ever do.

I knocked on a stranger's door.

Not entirely a stranger. Not a friend either—that distinction matters, or mattered, or whatever it does now. A contact. One of the dozens of quiet names in the city's underside I'd cultivated over the years for exactly this purpose, though I'd always imagined I'd be knocking to *arrest*, not to beg.

The woman who opened the door was small, sharp-faced, with charm-wire braided through her shawl in patterns I recognized as protective sigils sold illegally on about four different street corners. Her eyes went from my face—still recognizable, still wearing half my inquisitor's coat—to the child in my arms, and her expression did something complicated.

"Inquisitor Vane," she said. "You've got nerve."

"I've got nothing else. Please. Ten minutes. An hour. I'll pay."

"With what? Your badge?" But she stepped aside, because underneath the sharpness there was something else, some old debt or old decency, and I filed that away too—*people help even the people who hunted them; remember that; let it hurt*—and then I was inside a charm-seller's cellar with a rescued child and the bells of my whole life ringing three-strikes-and-pause behind me.

The woman's name, I learned, was Juniper Reyes. Remember that name. It turns out to matter.

* * *

They branded me traitor by nightfall. I know because Calloway read the proclamation himself—I could hear his voice through Juniper's floorboards when the criers reached Lantern Row. *Sera Vane, once Inquisitor of the Second Chair, having embraced corruption and consorted with unregistered gift-bearers, is hereby stripped of office, sacrament, and protection. She is to be taken alive if convenient.* Alive if convenient. Twenty years of service, summarized in five words and a shrug.

Maren slept under Juniper's counter, curled around a warming charm shaped like a tin bird, and I sat in the dark of the cellar and tried to perform the exercise the novitiates called *inventorying the soul*. What do you keep? What do you release?

What I kept: my hands, which knew locks and knots and the pressure points that open doors. My name, briefly. A working knowledge of every safe route out of Greyhollow, because apparently I'd been planning my defection for years without telling myself.

What I released: everything else. The Bastion's stone corridors that smelled of candlewax and iron. The choir at dawn. Brother Calloway teaching twelve-year-old me to hold a questioner's stance—*spine straight, Vane, mercy lives in the eyes, never let them see yours waver*. Mother Superior Ilse, who pulled me out of the workhouse at nine and said, *you have a talent for truth, child, come serve it*. The Order wasn't my employer. It was my family. It was the only shape love had ever worn in my life, and I had just walked out of its house carrying one of its prisoners, and the bells were still ringing.

That's the vertigo they don't warn you about. Everyone imagines betrayal feels like anger. Mostly it feels like falling up—as if the ground you've stood on all your life was the thing moving, and now the sky is beneath you and there is nothing, nothing, nothing to stand on except the choices you make next.

And here is the part I am not proud of, the part I would only ever admit to paper that might burn: I hadn't defected for the child. Not entirely. Defiance is cheap in the moment; it costs nothing until the morning after. What kept me moving, what sent me creeping back into the Bastion that same night through the coal chute I'd memorized at nineteen, wasn't conscience.

It was a file.

* * *

The Grand Archive of the Bastion keeps its true records below the scriptorium, behind a lock that answers to three keys and a spoken phrase, and I want it noted for history that the spoken phrase was "the light of Veil preserves," which is the single most ironic password in Veylmark and always has been.

I took documents. Not many—weight matters, guilt matters, and I only had the pockets of a stolen lay-brother's robe. But I took the right ones.

The first was the intake ledger for the past season: names, ages, gifts, dispositions. Dozens of entries where there used to be a handful. The purge wasn't growing—it was accelerating, geometrically, as if ordered from above by someone in a hurry.

The second was correspondence between the Marshal's office and the palace physicians regarding the king's illness. Officially, King Aldous had suffered a wasting fever these fifteen years and seen no public face since. Officially, the inquisition's mandate had expanded in that same period from "heresy" to "unregistered gift-bearing" for reasons of public safety. I had never, in twenty years, been shown anything connecting the two facts.

But physicians write carelessly when they believe only other physicians will read. And in the margin of a supply requisition—requisitions, God preserve me, the humble requisition, traitor to kings since time immemorial—one Dr. Hessel had noted: *the Hollowing progresses. His Majesty requires forty vessels this solstice or the winter will not hold. The Marshal assures the quota will be met.*

Forty vessels.

I stood in the dark of the archive with that page shaking in my hand and understood, with the slow horrible clarity that comes only when a lifetime of small explanations collapses at once, that the purges were not fear. They were not policy. They were *harvest*. Every gift-bearer dragged from every home in Veylmark was going somewhere, feeding something, holding something up—or holding something *down*—and the crown's strange illness and the crown's strange hunger were the same creature wearing two faces.

I didn't understand it. I want to be clear about that. Understanding came later, bought at prices I haven't finished paying. What I had in that moment was smaller and worse: I had proof that everything I'd served was a lie, and no idea what to do with the knowing.

The third document was thin. A fragment, really, torn from some older record, in an archivist's cramped hand: *...the binding weakens generationally. The seal requires renewal. The god holds the door but the god grows silent. Seek the sworn one. Seek the one who—*

The rest was gone, eaten by damp or scissors or time.

*A god holds the door.*

I copied what I could onto my own paper, tucked the originals into the lining of my robe, and climbed back out through the coal chute into a city that had declared me dead in every way that counted. Somewhere across the rooftops, dawn was coming up the color of dirty pewter, and the frost on the gutters was thick—too thick for autumn, though I didn't yet know why, wouldn't know for weeks, and the not-knowing would cost us all.

* * *

Juniper Reyes was awake when I got back, which surprised me; I'd assumed charm-sellers kept coward's hours. She was sitting at her counter with a ledger open in front of her, and the look on her face when she saw me climb in through her own window with coal dust to my elbows was not gratitude.

"You went *back*." She said it quietly, which was somehow more alarming than shouting. "Into the Bastion. While they're hunting you. To get—" She gestured at my bulging robe.

"To get the truth. Which, incidentally, you're welcome for, since it turns out the purge you're hiding from is a harvest, and the king is the farmer."

Something crossed her face then—something old and raw, a grief with years of sediment on it—and she looked down at her ledger like it had suddenly become very interesting. I noticed, with an inquisitor's eye I couldn't seem to retire, that several pages near the front had been torn out. Burned, maybe, from the smell.

"That's not possible," she said finally. "Kings don't—"

"This king hasn't been seen in fifteen years. This purge tripled in a single season. There's a physician's note demanding *forty vessels* by the solstice." I laid the papers on her counter, one by one, like tarot cards. "I spent twenty years learning to read people, Reyes. Read these."

She read them. I watched her do it—the stillness spreading over her, the way her hand rose halfway to her mouth and then thought better of it. Under the counter, Maren stirred and murmured in her sleep, and the little tin-bird charm glowed faintly warm, and outside, the frost crept another finger's length up Juniper's window glass.

When she looked up, her eyes were wet and furious and, underneath it, lit with something I recognized from the mirror that morning. Purpose. The terrible kind. The kind that gets people killed and occasionally saves the world.

"Fifteen years," she said, almost to herself. "He went silent fifteen years ago. The same year."

"Who did?"

She closed the ledger. When she answered, her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman stepping off a ledge.

"My god."

I should have laughed. An outlawed charm-seller claiming a personal line to the silent god Veil—I'd heard a hundred mad confessions and this had the shape of every one of them. But I'd just read a physician's note about feeding forty souls to a king's shadow, and laughter wasn't available.

"So here's my offer," Juniper said, and pushed a scrap of paper across the counter—a warding diagram, complex, the work of a genuinely sworn mage, the kind of craft I'd spent my career classifying as contraband. "You want protection, inquisitor. Nobody in this city can make you a ward strong enough to survive what's coming. Except me. And I want—"

She stopped. Looked at the papers again. At the sleeping child. At the frost swallowing her window.

"I want to know what my god has been doing with his silence," she said. "And I think you're going to help me find out."

I looked at this small, dangerous woman in her shop full of honest lies, and I felt the vertigo tilt again—the ground gone, the sky below—and I heard myself say the words that would bind me to her longer and tighter than any oath the Order ever exacted:

"Well," I said. "Someone has to interrogate a god."
