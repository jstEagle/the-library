## 4. A Bargain in the Night Market

Three nights after the proclamation went up on every wall in Greyhollow, I learned that fear has a smell, and the smell is wet wool and candle tallow at two in the morning.

The night market didn't care about proclamations. That was its whole charm. It lived in the old tannery district, down where the streets forgot their own names, under awnings that had been illegal since before I was born. You could buy anything there if you didn't ask where it came from: pigeon pie of uncertain provenance, love potions that were just vinegar and confidence, knives with the serial filed off, and my charms—little braided knots of thread and bone beads that did absolutely nothing except make frightened people feel like they'd done something.

I told myself they were placebos. I told myself a lot of things those days.

* * *

The market had thinned to its skeleton crew by the time she found me. The fishmonger was packing up his ice. A fortune-teller two stalls down was telling a drunk man he'd die rich, which was technically true, since dead men don't spend anything. And I was haggling with a boy over a luck-knot when the crowd shifted the way water shifts around something sharp.

I saw her before she saw me—or rather, I saw what she was, because you don't survive fifteen years of hiding by trusting faces. She moved wrong for a civilian. Weight on the balls of her feet. Eyes doing the thing hunter's eyes do, sweeping corners, counting exits, cataloguing hands. Grey traveling cloak, hood back, hair cropped short and practical as a soldier's kit. A bruise going yellow-green along her jaw like a fading signature.

And beneath it, faint as a scar, the mark of the Inquisition burned into her left cheekbone—a brand half-scratched at, half-healed, like someone had tried to claw their own face off.

I knew that brand. Every gift-bearer in Greyhollow knew it the way you know the sound of your own heartbeat.

"Charm-seller." Her voice was low, roughened, like a blade drawn across stone. "The one called Vasquez."

"I'm called many things." I closed my luck-knot stall with deliberate slowness. "Most of them are lies. Some are flattering. None of them involve giving my name to strangers with Inquisition scars."

Something flickered across her face—not quite pain, not quite amusement. "If I wanted you taken, there'd be six men behind me and you'd already be bleeding."

"Convenient how traitors always say that."

"I'm not—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again with the careful diction of someone who had once been taught to interrogate and was now fighting every instinct in her body. "My name is Sera Vane. Formerly Senior Inquisitor, Third Circle, Order of the Unblinking Eye. Currently—" her mouth twisted around the word, "—nothing. Traitor. Runaway. Whatever the proclamations are calling us this week."

"Currently standing too close to my stall," I said, "which is a health hazard."

She didn't laugh. I got the sense laughter had been surgically removed from her somewhere along the way, and the wound hadn't healed clean.

"You sold a warding knot to Maren Ostwick," she said. "Two weeks ago. Copper wire, ash-oak bead, triple-braided."

The bottom dropped out of my stomach so fast I felt it in my knees. Maren. Sweet Maren, who mended lace and hummed while she worked and whose little gift was making flowers bloom out of season—who'd come to me scared and shaking, asking for something, *anything*, to keep the hunters' dogs from catching her scent.

"The knot didn't work," Sera said quietly. "They took her Tuesday."

"I know." My voice came out flat as a coin. "I watched."

* * *

Here's the ugly truth about guilt: it doesn't announce itself. It doesn't come in wearing black and wailing. It comes in quiet, in the space between one breath and the next, and it sets up housekeeping in your chest and never pays rent.

Maren had bought that knot because I sold it to her. Because I needed coin for bread and lamp oil, because I'd convinced myself that a placebo was kindness, that false hope was better than no hope at all. And maybe some part of me—the part still sworn to a god who wouldn't speak—had known, deep down, that the knot would fail, and sold it anyway because the alternative was admitting that I couldn't save anyone anymore. That I was just a woman with dead prayers and a market stall, watching the world burn one neighbor at a time.

Sera Vane stood there watching me realize all of this, and to her credit, she didn't gloat. She just waited, patient as winter, the way hunters wait.

"What do you want?" I asked finally.

"To trade."

"I don't have anything worth trading."

"You have exactly one thing worth trading." She reached into her cloak—and I'll be honest, every muscle in my body clenched expecting a knife—and drew out a leather folio, cracked and swollen with rain damage, stamped with the seal of the Third Circle. "Documents," she said. "Real ones. Not rumors. Names, dates, orders signed in the king's own hand. Everything the Inquisition knows about the Hollowing."

The word landed strange. Hollowing. I'd heard it whispered in the market, in the chapel ruins, in the spaces between conversations—*the king is ill, the king is changed, the king eats light*. Rumor dressed up in religion.

"And why," I said carefully, "would a former senior inquisitor give away her order's secrets to a charm-seller?"

"Because I read them." For the first time, something broke open behind her eyes—something raw and bleeding that she was holding shut with both hands. "Because I spent eleven years torturing people for the crime of existing, Juniper Reyes, and I told myself it was justice. I told myself the gift was corruption, that we were saving souls by burning bodies. And then they handed me a file on a nine-year-old girl and ordered me to do what we did, and I looked at her, and I looked at the documents, and I understood that everything I believed was built on top of something rotten." Her jaw tightened. "So yes. I want to trade. Your craft for my confession. Call it even."

"I don't take confessions. I'm not a priest."

"No," she said. "You're worse. You're a believer."

The word hit like a slap. Fifteen years of silence, fifteen years of praying into a void and getting nothing back but the sound of my own voice, and this woman—one look, one conversation—had put her finger on the exact wound.

"I'm not sworn to anything," I said. "Not anymore."

"Liar." She said it without malice, almost gently. "You braid your knots in the old pattern. Three-and-three, veil-wise. Only sworn mages braid veil-wise—it's muscle memory from the liturgy. You can leave the god, Juniper. The god doesn't leave you."

I should have walked away. Every instinct honed over fifteen years of survival said walk away, close the stall, vanish into the tannery maze, find a new city under a new name. But my hands were shaking, and not from fear.

"What kind of ward?" I asked.

* * *

We negotiated in the back room of a tea house run by a woman who owed me a favor and hated the crown more than she feared it. Sera laid the documents out on the table between us like a gambler showing her hand, and I will admit—I looked. Gods help me, I looked.

Orders of registration. Lists of names. Quotas—actual quotas, written in neat clerical script, like grain shipments or tax rolls. *Gifted persons conveyed to the capital, monthly allotment, condition: living.* And beneath it all, in a different hand, older, annotated in margins gone brown with age: sketches of the royal seal, diagrams of the crown itself, notes in a cipher I half-recognized from my swearing days.

"This is madness," I said. "This is—nobody signs quotas for people. You sign quotas for wheat."

"Now you understand why I ran."

"But the king—" I tapped the signature at the bottom of an order. "This says *by command of the monarch*. If the king's ill, if he's being manipulated, if there's a regency—"

"There's no regency." Sera's voice went very quiet. Very flat. "That's what I need the ward for. There's a ritual chamber beneath the palace. The 'illness' is performed there, monthly. The king sits the throne and the crown does the rest. And the purge isn't policy, Juniper. It's feeding."

The candle between us guttered, though there was no draft.

"Feeding," I repeated.

"The Hollow King." She pronounced it the way you pronounce a diagnosis. "It's in the files. Old ones, pre-Inquisition, sealed since before my grandmother was born. Something that wears the monarchy like a coat. Something that eats the gift right out of the land. Veilmark's magic is dying, charm-seller. Haven't you wondered why your charms have been getting weaker? Why the prayers go unanswered? Why the winter came early?"

I hadn't wondered. I'd been too busy surviving. But the words unlocked something—a memory of my own magic flickering like a bad lamp, of the silent god silence deepening year by year, of frost on my window in high summer three years ago and everyone laughing it off as freak weather.

Freak weather that kept happening.

"If it's true," I said slowly, "then the hunters, the registration, the quotas—"

"A harvest." Sera folded her hands on the table, and I saw them tremble, just slightly. Eleven years of torture, and her hands only shook when she talked about the truth. "Gift-bearers aren't being arrested, Juniper. They're being *delivered*. And the delivery schedule ends at midwinter solstice, when whatever's wearing the king finishes eating through the last of the seal and steps fully into our world wearing a crown."

The seal. The word snagged on something deep in me, some half-remembered fragment of liturgy: *the Veil is knotted, the Veil is shut, the Veil holds fast against the dark.* I'd recited it ten thousand times as a girl. I'd assumed it was metaphor. Everyone assumes the scary parts are metaphor.

"Why me?" I asked. "Why does a disgraced inquisitor need a charm?"

"Not a charm." She pushed up her sleeve, and there, wrapped around her forearm, was a band of scorched, blistered skin—the mark of something that had hunted her, caught her, and let her go. Or thought it had. "A ward. A real one. Sworn-mage work, veil-patterned, strong enough to hide a gifted signature from *that*. Because it found me once, and next time it won't be sloppy, and I am very tired of running."

"I told you. I'm not sworn. Whatever I was, I gave it up."

"You gave up nothing." She leaned forward, and the candlelight made her brand look freshly cut. "You can't give up a bond, Juniper. You can only stop using it. Which means you're the most dangerous woman in this city—a loaded weapon that thinks it's unloaded. And either you braid me that ward tonight, or in three weeks there won't be anyone left in Veylmark who remembers how."

Outside, somewhere in the frozen streets, a bell began to toll. Once. Twice. A third time—which meant hunters in the district, which meant doors being kicked in, which meant someone's mother or daughter or son was being dragged into the dark to feed a hungry throne.

Sera didn't flinch. She'd heard that bell before. She'd probably *been* that bell.

"So," she said. "Do we have a bargain, charm-seller? Your craft for my truth?"

I looked at the documents. At the quotas. At the cipher I almost recognized. At the woman who had spent eleven years breaking people like me and was now sitting in a tea house asking one of us to save her life.

Every smart, survival-honed part of me said *no. Walk away. Let the world burn; you've only got one skin.*

But Maren had bought a knot that didn't work. And somewhere out there, more Marens were buying knots every day, and the bell was tolling, and my god had been silent for fifteen years for reasons I was suddenly, terribly beginning to suspect were connected to a crown.

"One ward," I said. "And you tell me everything. Starting with why a sworn mage's bond matters to a thing that wears a king."

Sera Vane smiled, and it was the loneliest smile I'd ever seen.

"Because," she said, "the seal was tied by a god. And gods, charm-seller, can only be unknotted by what they've sworn."

Somewhere across the city, a child was screaming behind a door that was kicking in—and I would spend the next three nights learning exactly what she meant.
