## 5. The Boy Who Burned Rain

The rain in Greyhollow fell sideways that night, the way it always did when the wind came off the Graywater, and Tam Vetch was crouched behind a chimney on Widow's Rest Row trying very hard not to boil it.

That was the trick, see. Everybody thought rain was just rain. Cold, wet, harmless. But water didn't stay harmless around him. Water got *ideas*. He could feel it in his teeth when it touched his skin—a little hum, like the river whispering *come on, come on*—and if he wasn't careful, really careful, the rain would start steaming right off his shoulders and every hunter within three streets would look up and say, well, there he is.

So he sat under the eave of the tannery roof with his knees pulled up and let the cold soak through his shirt, because cold meant safe. Cold meant nothing boiling. Cold meant he was just another workhouse boy nobody wanted, which was the best thing a boy like him could be.

He counted chimneys to keep calm. That was Wren's trick. Wren, who shared her bread crusts even when the matron wasn't looking, who had this way of sitting perfectly still like she was listening to something nobody else could hear. *Count them,* she'd told him once, back when he'd first come to St. Brannoc's with burned hands and no name anybody would use. *Chimneys don't lie. There's eleven from here to the corner. Count them when your head gets loud.*

Eleven chimneys. One, two, three—

"TAM."

Matron Hedwig's voice carried up through the rain like a fishhook through water. Not angry. Worse than angry. Sweet.

"Tam, dear. Come down. You've left your supper."

Tam went very still against the wet slate. Supper. Right. The matron never called anyone dear unless someone was watching or something was wrong, and it was past supper hour and no one was watching, so.

He peeked over the ridge of the roof.

Two men stood in the yard below, holding lanterns up against the drizzle. They weren't the usual kind of man who came sniffing around St. Brannoc's—the drunk ones, the debt ones. These two stood too straight. Their coats were gray wool, good wool, and they wore the crown's badge on their collars: a circle with a bite taken out of it.

Hunters.

"Boys say there's a lad here burns his bathwater," one of them said. His voice was flat as a ledger. "Matron. We pay for names."

"And I've given you one," Matron Hedwig said. Her voice had gone smooth and oily, like she was haggling over linen. "Tam Vetch. Orphaned at six, no kin, no one to ask after him. Boiled the wash-tub clean through last spring. Scalded Polly's arm when she grabbed him. He'll be in the loft above the laundry, or on the roof—he goes up when he's frightened, like a cat."

"That's all we need."

Tam's ears were ringing. He watched the matron hold out her hand, watched one of the hunters drop coins into it—not many coins, he noticed, not even a full purse—and he felt something crack inside his chest, small and clean, like ice letting go of a branch.

She'd fed him. She'd bandaged his hands that first week, muttering about how clumsy he was. She'd called him *dear*.

And now she'd sold him for less than the price of a piglet.

He didn't cry. Crying made you breathe hard, and breathing hard made you hot, and hot made things happen. Instead he did what he'd taught himself to do since he was six years old: he folded everything down small, small, small, into the tightest little ball at the bottom of his belly, and he started moving.

* * *

Greyhollow's rooftops were their own country. Nobody down below looked up—that was the first law of the roof-country, and Tam had learned it the hard way, falling through a rotted skylight into a dyer's shop at age nine. The second law was: slate is slippery when wet, tile is slippery when dry, and everything is slippery when you're scared.

He ran anyway.

One, two, three—chimneys ticking past beneath his boots. Four was cracked; five had a rook's nest; six belonged to a house where an old woman sang off-key through the window, some lullaby about a shepherd and a star. Seven, eight, nine. The rain kept coming and Tam kept counting, because counting was a rope and he was holding on with both hands.

Behind him, below, lantern-light swung through the alley mouths. Voices. *Check the tannery. Check the drying sheds.* They moved fast for men in good coats.

He cut across the gap between the tannery and the chandlery—a jump he'd done a hundred times, four feet of empty air over a drop that would break a leg at minimum—and landed wrong. His foot skidded on wet slate and suddenly he was sliding, sliding, arms windmilling, and the only thing his hands found was the lead flashing around the chandlery's gutter, and the moment his bare skin touched the pooled rainwater in the gutter, the water *boiled*.

It happened in half a heartbeat. A puff of white steam, a hiss, and the whole street below must have heard it, because the voices stopped, and then—one of them said, quiet and certain:

"There. Steam. Roofline east."

Tam hung by his fingers off the edge of a building and understood, with a clarity that felt almost funny, that he was going to die tonight. Not maybe. Tonight. Because he couldn't stop the boiling, he'd never been able to stop it, and they'd follow the steam like hounds following blood.

So he climbed instead of dying. Hands burning—not scalded, his own gift never hurt him, which was somehow the cruelest joke of it—he hauled himself up onto the chandlery roof and ran harder, faster, past ten chimneys, twenty, out past the candle-shops and the rope-walks into the part of Greyhollow where the roofs got old and mossy and the streets below got narrow and mean. Lantern Street. Then Candle Lane. Then places without proper names, where the buildings leaned together overhead like conspirators.

His legs gave out somewhere above a cooper's yard. He folded down behind a mossy gargoyle—one of the old Veil-chapel gargoyles, salvaged and slapped onto a merchant's roof, its stone face worn blank and smooth—and he put his head between his knees and shook.

*Count them,* Wren's voice said in his head. But he'd lost count. He'd lost everything. Eleven chimneys, and then a hundred, and now there was just rain and dark and the sound of his own breath going ragged.

*I'm sorry, Wren,* he thought. *I'm sorry I didn't say goodbye.*

Because that was the other thing, wasn't it. Wren was still down there. Wren was in that workhouse with the matron who sold children for coin, and Tam was up here, and tomorrow—or tonight, if the hunters got bored and greedy—she'd be next. Wren, who was quiet and still and strange, who never burned anything, never boiled anything, never did *anything* that would get her named. But the matron had tasted coin now. The matron knew how easy it was.

*I have to go back.*

The thought arrived fully formed, absurd, impossible. Go back? Back past the hunters, past the lanterns, down into the mouth of the trap, for a girl who might already be—

No. No, he had to. Because Wren had shared her bread when nobody made her. Because Wren had held his burned hands in hers and not flinched, even though everyone else in St. Brannoc's flinched, even though touching him was like touching a stove. Because she was the only person in the whole gray city who had ever looked at him like he was a boy instead of a fire waiting to happen.

Tam got up. His legs wobbled. Below, the lanterns were spreading out, fanning across the district like spilled oil.

And that was when the roof under his feet groaned, tilted, and dumped him through a hole the size of a coffin into darkness.

* * *

He fell maybe twelve feet. It should have been worse, but something broke his fall—a stack of sacking, soft with rot—and he came down in a heap among crates and dust and the smell of wax and dried herbs and, underneath it all, something older. Something that made the hair on his arms stand up. Not danger exactly. More like... attention. Like walking into a room where somebody had just been praying.

Tam lay still and listened.

Above him, muffled through floorboards: footsteps. His own heartbeat. Rain drumming on the roof he'd just fallen through, drumming on the hole he'd have to figure out how to explain.

Then—light.

A lantern flared somewhere to his left, and a voice said, dry as tinder: "Well. Either the roof's finally gone, or the gods are delivering parcels again."

A woman stepped into view. Small, sharp-faced, maybe thirty, with dark hair escaping its braid and ink stains on her fingers. She held the lantern up and looked at him the way you'd look at a cat that had come in through your window—annoyance first, curiosity second, and underneath it, something wary and quick.

"You're bleeding on my inventory," she added.

Tam looked at his palms. They weren't bleeding. They weren't even red. But his skin was *steaming*, faint wisps curling off his knuckles where the rain had soaked through, and the woman saw it—he watched her see it—and he watched her whole body change. Every muscle deciding at once whether to run or fight.

He scrambled backward into the crates. "I'm sorry," he gasped. "I'm sorry, I'll go, I didn't—I fell, I wasn't—"

"Stop." She didn't come closer. She set the lantern down on a crate, deliberately, within his sight but out of his reach, and crouched so they were eye level. "Stop talking. Breathe slower. Your steam's getting worse the more scared you get, yes?"

He nodded, hating that she could read him so easily.

"So we do this in order," she said. "First question, and think carefully, because the answer decides whether I help you or throw you back out the hole: are they hunting you specifically, or are you just unlucky?"

"Hunters," Tam whispered. "Crown hunters. The matron sold me. For—" his voice caught on it, even now, even saying it to a stranger—"for coin."

Something moved behind the woman's eyes. Not surprise. Recognition. Like he'd confirmed a bill she'd already been expecting.

"How long have you had the gift?" she asked.

"I didn't steal it," Tam said quickly, the old reflex. "I didn't ask for it. It just—it happens. When I'm scared, or angry, or—" he gestured helplessly at his steaming hands, at the puddle near the wall that was starting to murmur and bubble, "—or when there's water. Mostly water. I can't stop it. I've tried everything. The matron used to make me sit with my hands in snow and it just melted and then it boiled and then—"

"Breathe," the woman said again, and then she did something Tam had never seen anyone do before.

She reached out and put her hand on top of his.

Her palm was warm and calloused and completely ordinary, and she held it there while his skin hissed and steamed under it, and she didn't flinch. Didn't snatch away. Didn't make the sign against witchery or spit or call for anyone. She just held on, steady, like his burning was weather and she'd dressed for it.

"Listen to me," she said. "My name is Juniper. This is my cellar. Nobody knows you're here yet, and nobody will, provided you can do one thing."

"What thing?" Tam's voice came out very small.

"Trust me faster than you're panicking." She almost smiled. It was a tired, crooked almost-smile, the kind worn smooth from use. "There's a basin behind you. Copper. I need you to touch it—but gently, with two fingers, like it's sleeping. Can you do that?"

"Why?"

"Because right now your gift is a kettle with no lid, and I'm going to teach you where the spout is." She tapped her own temple. "In here. Water doesn't want to burn, boy. Water wants to *move*. You've just been gripping it so hard it can't do anything but scream. So we give it somewhere to go."

Tam stared at her. Fifteen seconds ago he'd been ready to bolt through the nearest wall. Everything about this was wrong—strangers didn't help, strangers *sold* you, the matron had taught him that much with her sweet voice and her handful of coins. And yet her hand was still on his, still steady, and the steam coming off his knuckles had slowed without his noticing, slowed and thinned until it was barely a shimmer.

"The hunters," he said. "They'll find me. They followed the steam, they—"

"They followed the steam *up*," Juniper said. "Roofs run east from here toward the river. By now they're combing the cooperage yards wondering where their kettle-boy went." She rose, crossed the cellar, and pulled aside a hanging curtain of oiled canvas. Beyond it: a narrow cot, a stool, a candle stub, a child's blanket folded with military precision. A hiding place built by someone who'd been hiding a long time. "You sleep here tonight. Tomorrow we talk about whether you stay. Tonight you don't argue."

Tam didn't move. He was still crouched among the crates, coiled tight, and some stubborn animal part of him wouldn't uncoil, wouldn't believe, wouldn't—

"Here," Juniper said.

She filled the copper basin from a jug, set it on the floor between them, and stepped back. Well back. Giving him room, the way you'd approach a strange dog.

"Two fingers," she said. "Like it's sleeping."

His hands shook. Of course they shook. Every time in his life he'd touched water on purpose it had ended in scalding and screaming and the matron's switch, and here was this stranger asking him to do the one thing guaranteed to bring the hunters down on them both.

But she hadn't flinched. She'd put her hand on his steam and stayed.

Tam knelt. He touched the water with two fingers, feather-light, and waited for the hiss.

Instead, the water went *warm*. Just warm. Pleasantly, impossibly warm, like bathwater on a good day, like summer shallows, and it swirled once around the basin—a slow, lazy spiral, deliberate, almost playful—and settled.

Tam snatched his hand back and stared at it. At the basin. At her.

"It didn't boil," he said stupidly.

"No," Juniper agreed. "It danced."

And Tam Vetch, who had spent seven years being a disaster with skin, who had never once in his life touched water without ruining it, felt something crack open in his chest—something different from the crack of betrayal downstairs, something that hurt in a better way. His eyes stung. He scrubbed his sleeve across his face furiously before she could see.

"Sleep," Juniper said, and her voice had gone gentle in a way that suggested gentleness cost her something. "Nobody finds anybody in my cellar. That's rather the point of it."

She turned to climb the stairs, then paused, lantern light catching the side of her face.

"One more thing," she said. "The girl. In the workhouse. The one whose trick you were using—counting chimneys. You were going back for her, weren't you?"

Tam froze. "How did you—"

"You talk in your sleep eventually. Everyone does." She shrugged. "Get some rest. If she matters that much, we may yet do something about it."

She climbed the stairs, and the door closed, and the cellar went dark except for the candle stub and the faint warm glow of the basin, where the water still turned in its slow, contented spiral.

Tam lay down on the cot with his boots on, because boys who trusted fully woke up in cages, and he stared at the ceiling beams and counted them instead of sheep. Six beams. Seven. Eight.

Outside, far off, a bell rang once and stopped, like the city clearing its throat.

And down in the dark, in a stolen cellar under a charm-shop on a street he didn't know, Tam slept without boiling anything at all—for the first time since he was six years old.

He dreamed of rooftops, and of a girl sharing bread, and when he woke near dawn to voices above—Juniper's, low and sharp, and a second woman's, colder, clipped, like a blade being drawn—he sat bolt upright in the cot, heart hammering, and pressed his ear to the floorboards.

"—the king himself is the source of the purge," the cold voice was saying. "You don't believe me. Good. Look at the documents."

There was a silence. Then Juniper, very quietly: "Open the cellar door slowly. And whoever you are up there—you'd better pray he stays asleep."

The latch began to turn.
