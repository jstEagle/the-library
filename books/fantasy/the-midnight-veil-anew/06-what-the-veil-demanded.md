## 6. What the Veil Demanded

Tam slept in my cellar like a feral cat that had decided, provisionally, that the blanket was his. He'd curled up against the far wall, as far from the door and from Sera as he could get, one hand still curled around a stub of candle he refused to let anyone light. His breathing had the careful rhythm of someone who'd learned to sleep quietly so no one would notice him.

Sera sat on the third stair with her stolen satchel open in her lap, reading documents by lantern-light, because apparently traitors don't sleep either. She hadn't asked to stay. I hadn't offered. We existed in the cellar together the way two cats exist in the same alley—watchful, exhausted, mutually unimpressed.

I should have been sleeping too. Instead I was at my workbench, doing the thing I always did when the world cracked: working.

I was carving a warding charm for Tam—something small, something that would dampen the gift instead of hiding it, because hiding it hadn't worked for him and wouldn't. And because my hands needed to be busy or they were going to start shaking.

The wood was ash. It's always ash for me. Veil's tree, the old books say—the god who stands at the edge of things, who watches but does not speak, whose silence is not absence but *attention*. Fifteen years ago I would have known exactly which prayer went with this cut. Fifteen years ago my hands would have been warm.

Now I carved and felt nothing but the knife, and told myself that was enough.

*You're brooding,* said the part of me that sounded like my mother, may she rest and stop commenting.

*I'm working.*

*You're brooding with a knife. That's how you got the scar.*

I put the knife down. Fine. Fine. The night was long, the boy was asleep, the hunter was reading, and there was nobody left in Greyhollow who could hear me say it out loud:

I missed him.

Not in the way you miss a person. In the way you miss a wall that used to be there. For twenty-two years of my life—no. For seven years of it, the years that mattered, I had walked through the world with Veil beside me. Not a voice, never a voice; the silent god doesn't speak, not even to his sworn. But a *presence*. A pressure at the edge of every room. The sense of being watched over by something patient and vast and utterly without opinion, which sounds cold until you've lived with it, and then it sounds like the only warmth there is.

And then one morning, fifteen years ago, I woke up and the wall was gone.

* * *

I was nineteen when I swore myself. You don't choose Veil; that's what they tell you in the chapel-novices, and it's true. Most gods are courted. Veil *selects*, and nobody knows the criteria, and the priests—when there were priests—used to say that's the point. A god of watching cannot be watched back.

I'd been selling charms in Greyhollow's night market for two years by then, little honest things, luck-knots and fever-wards, the kind of magic so small the crown's men called it craft and taxed it instead of burning it. One market night a man came to my stall wearing grey, all grey, hooded, face like weather. He stood there while I finished a sale, and then he put one coin on the table—an ash-wood coin, which is not currency anywhere—and said, "You see the edges of things."

"I see people who want charms," I said, because I was nineteen and clever.

"You see them," he said, and touched the coin, and walked into the crowd, and the crowd closed behind him like water.

I dreamed of standing doors that night. Doors in fields, doors on cliffs, doors standing alone with nothing behind them, all of them slightly ajar, all of them breathing. In the morning my hands were warm all the way through, and when I went to the ruined chapel on Lantern Row—the old one, the real one, before the crown built their whitewashed version downtown—there was an ash leaf on the altar.

Nobody had put it there. I know because I'd swept that chapel myself for two years, hoping.

So I swore. There's no ceremony worth describing—a kneeling, a vow spoken into silence, the feeling of something enormous turning its attention toward me the way a lighthouse turns toward a ship. Not love. Not approval. *Attention.* The sworn call it the Watch, and it never leaves you. You can be in a cell, in a grave, in a burning building, and some quiet part of you knows: something sees me. Something knows where I am.

People ask what it's like, being sworn to a god who never speaks. I tell them: imagine the person who loves you best never says a word, but every time you look up, they're already looking at you, and they're glad. Now imagine it's the size of a mountain.

Seven years of that. I built my little life around it. Charms by day, chapel-sweeping by dusk, the Watch steady as a heartbeat.

Then, fifteen years ago, in the deep of winter, I woke at midnight knowing—knowing the way you know a limb is broken—that the Watch was gone.

I ran to the chapel barefoot in the snow. Nothing. No presence, no attention, no warmth in my hands. Just me, alone in a cold building, screaming silently at a god who had never answered and now couldn't even listen.

Three days later, the criers came through Greyhollow announcing that the king had fallen ill. A strange illness, they said. A wasting. The court mages were baffled. Prayers were requested.

I remember thinking, nineteen-going-on-twenty-six and hollowed out: *even the king gets prayers, and mine go nowhere.*

I didn't connect it then. Grief is stupid like that. It fills the whole house and leaves no room for noticing that the date on the proclamation matches the date on your own private apocalypse.

But tonight—sitting at my workbench with an ash knife in my hand and a hunted boy sleeping ten feet away and a disgraced inquisitor reading stolen papers on my stairs—I finally did the arithmetic.

Fifteen years ago, Veil fell silent.

Fifteen years ago, the king fell ill.

* * *

"You're making that face," Sera said.

I jumped hard enough to nick my thumb. She was standing at the bottom of the stairs, satchel closed, watching me with those inquisitor eyes that probably saw through brick walls and marriage vows alike.

"What face?"

"The one where you've just thought of something and hate it."

"Is there a face where I'm minding my own business?"

"Not on you." She came closer, glancing at the sleeping boy. "Talk or carve. Pick one."

I picked talk, mostly because my thumb was bleeding. "How long have you been in the order? The inquisition."

"Twenty years. Why?"

"The Hollowing." I used her word for it, the word from the documents she'd shown me. "When did it start? Officially. The king's illness."

She didn't answer right away, and I watched her decide whether lying to me was worth the effort. "Fifteen years ago," she said slowly. "Winter. The first recorded ritual—the court called it a treatment. Why?"

"And the purge of the gift-bearers? When did the order first draft registration laws?"

"Fifteen—" She stopped. I watched her do the same arithmetic I'd done, watch it land on her face like cold water. "That's not coincidence."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

I told her then. All of it, in a low voice, while the boy slept: the grey man, the ash coin, the doors in my dreams, the seven years of the Watch. The morning the wall vanished. The snow. The empty chapel.

"I spent fifteen years thinking I'd been abandoned," I said. "Or that I'd imagined the whole thing—that grief for a dead family had curdled into grief for a dead god, and I'd invented the Watch to fill the space. Do you know what that does to a person? Deciding your most sacred memory might be a symptom?"

"That's why you sell charms instead of using the gift," she said. Not a question. Inquisitors.

"Small magics only. Craft-magic, the kind that barely registers. Because if the Watch left me, maybe everything else would leave too, and I wasn't going to build a life on something that could vanish mid-prayer." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Turns out I was right to be scared. It *did* vanish. Everything. Except it didn't abandon me at all, did it? Something happened fifteen years ago. To the god. To the king. At the same moment. Same hour, maybe. Same—"

"Same binding," Sera said quietly.

We looked at each other across my workbench, hunter and hunted, and for once we were looking at the same thing.

"You felt him go silent," she said, "the day the king sickened. Which means whatever happened to the king happened to Veil. Or—" her inquisitor mind was running now, I could see it, terrible and magnificent, "—or they're the same event viewed from two sides. Juniper. What is Veil the god *of*?"

"Silence. Edges. Doors." My mouth had gone dry. "Thresholds. Keeping things *out*."

"And what does a kingdom have at its center?" Her voice dropped. "A crown. A sealed thing. A vessel. Every rite of coronation I ever studied has language about binding—'bound to the land,' 'bound to the people.' What if the crown of Veylmark isn't a symbol?"

The cellar felt colder. Tam stirred in his sleep and made a small hurt sound, and both of us went still until he settled.

"If you're right," I said, "then my god hasn't been silent for fifteen years. He's been *occupied*. Held. Used." The words came out of me like something torn. "All this time I thought I was faithless. I thought I'd failed some final test. I lit candles in an empty chapel for fifteen years talking to something that couldn't hear me, and the whole time he was—"

I stopped. My hands were shaking after all. So much for the knife-work theory.

Sera did something I didn't expect. She reached across the bench and put her hand flat over mine—not comfort, exactly; inquisitors don't comfort, it's against the union rules—but steadying. Grounding. The way you hold someone's hand when they're about to be sick.

"Then your faith wasn't wasted," she said. "It was interrupted."

"It was *stolen*," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference has a body, and that body is sitting on a throne in the capital eating magic off the bones of this kingdom."

* * *

I didn't sleep. Around the hour when even criminals and inquisitors concede the night is lost, I climbed the cellar steps to the shop above, unlocked the case where I keep the charms that aren't for sale, and took out an ash-wood coin.

The grey man gave me two, fifteen years ago. One I kept. One I buried under the chapel floor the week after the silence, because burying things is what you do when you can't scream loud enough.

I turned the surviving coin in my fingers. Ash wood, worn smooth now, still faintly warm—or maybe that was my hand. Maybe it had always been my hand. Fifteen years of not-knowing will make you doubt the warmth in your own palm.

"Here's what I know," I said out loud, to the coin, to the dark, to whoever was listening or wished they could be. "You chose me once. You watched me for seven years. And then something took you, and I have spent half my life apologizing to an empty chair."

The coin sat in my hand. The shop stayed dark. Somewhere below, a boy slept badly and a hunter read stolen pages, and outside, the city of Greyhollow slept its frightened, registered-or-dead sleep.

"So I'm done apologizing," I said. "If you're drowning, I'm coming in after you. If you're bound, I'm cutting the rope. And if the thing that took you is wearing a crown—" I closed my fist around the coin until the edges bit. "—then I'm going to walk into that throne room and take it off his head myself."

No answer. Of course no answer. Silence is his native tongue.

But for the first time in fifteen years, the silence felt less like a locked door and more like held breath. Like something on the other side of a very thick wall, listening as hard as it could, unable to say *yes*—and unable to say *stop*.

I put the coin in my pocket, next to my heart, where I used to feel the Watch.

Below the shop, Sera had gone back to her documents, and when I came down the stairs she looked up with an expression I was learning to dread, because it meant she'd found something.

"Juniper," she said. "There's a list in here. Names. Gift-bearers taken by the order over the past fifteen years—all of them, every purge since the king sickened. And there's a column I can't make sense of. Next to each name, a number. Rising, year over year. Small at first. Larger lately." She tapped the page. "They're counting something."

I looked at the numbers. I looked at the boy sleeping against the wall, whose gift boiled water and blistered skin, whose matron had sold him for coin, whose name—if the order ever learned it—would go on that list with a number beside it.

"They're counting fuel," I heard myself say. "And we're sitting in the pantry."

Sera closed the satchel slowly. Outside, far off, the first grey breath of dawn crept over Greyhollow's rooftops—and with it, thin as a knife through cloth, came the sound of hunters' boots in the street.
