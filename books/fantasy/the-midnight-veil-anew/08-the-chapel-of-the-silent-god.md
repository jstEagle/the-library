## 8. The Chapel of the Silent God

I have broken bread with torturers, slept in barracks built on confiscated land, and once, at nineteen, held a man's confession ledger while his hands were broken one finger at a time. I tell you this so you understand that shame and I are old acquaintances. We share meals. We know each other's habits.

But I had never once been afraid of it until Juniper found out what I'd done.

The chapel found us before we were looking for it. That's the way of ruined holy places—they don't announce themselves. One moment we were stumbling through a beechwood with Tam shivering so hard his teeth clicked like dice in a cup, and the next there it was: a roof gone to ribs, walls of grey stone furred with moss, and over the doorway a carving of two open hands, palms up, holding nothing.

The sign of Veil.

Tam stopped dead when he saw it. "Is this—" He looked at me. In three days of travel he had learned to be careful around me, the way you're careful around a dog of uncertain temper. "Inquisitors burned these places, didn't they?"

"Some," I said. "Most just rotted. Easier to let a god starve than to make a martyr of Him."

"Charming," said Tam, and went inside.

Juniper stood a moment longer under the empty hands. She does that—stands in doorways like she's asking permission from something only she can hear. Then she followed him in, and I stood outside in the cold feeling exactly what I was: the person who had helped empty every chapel like this one in Veylmark.

* * *

The interior smelled of pigeon and old incense, that sweet ghost of myrrh that no amount of rot quite kills. The pews had been dragged into a barricade decades ago by someone expecting soldiers who never came. Rain came through the broken roof in threads, silver in what little light remained, and pooled in the stone font where children had once been named.

Juniper went straight to the altar and did something I hadn't seen her do before. She knelt. Actually knelt, knees to the wet stone, and pressed her palm flat against the altar's face, and her lips moved without sound.

Tam watched her sideways while he wrung out his socks. "Does she do that a lot? Pray?"

"She's sworn," I said. "Sworn servants don't pray. They report."

"That's the saddest thing I've ever heard."

"You should meet my former colleagues."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then his face did the thing it did whenever he remembered what he was—the flicker down toward his own hands, the flinch from his own skin—and he pulled his sleeves down over his fingers and asked no more questions.

I busied myself making a fire small enough to hide, because hiding fires is an inquisitor's skill and I have few enough skills left that I might as well use them. By full dark we had soup made of rainwater and the heel of a loaf, and Tam was asleep against a pew with his boots still on, one hand curled near the flames as if even unconscious he needed to keep an eye on what fire might decide to do.

And then it was just Juniper and me and the sound of the roof dripping.

She sat across the coals from me, sharpening a paring knife on a whetstone she kept in her coat. She'd been doing it for an hour. The sound had a rhythm to it, patient and terrible, and I understood eventually that she wasn't maintaining the blade. She was deciding something.

"Sera." First time she'd used my name since the sewer gate.

"Yes."

"How long have you known me?"

"Three days."

"How long did you know about me before that?"

The whetstone stopped.

I could have lied. Lying was also an inquisitor's skill; I'd been better at it than most. But Tam was asleep ten feet away and Veil's empty hands were carved over the door and some part of me—the part that had refused the torture order, the part that got me branded—was tired, bone-tired, of carrying ledgers.

"Fifteen years," I said. "Give or take."

The knife went still. "Explain."

"There was a circle of sworn mages operating out of the Fenmark districts. Quiet ones. Healers, mostly. Ward-makers." My voice came out level, which felt obscene, so I made myself keep going. "The Order flagged them after the king fell ill. Standard protocol—all sworn gifts were suspect once His Majesty's condition began. I was twenty-three. I drew up the warrant. And I signed it."

"I knew those people." Juniper's voice was very quiet. Very flat. "Fenmark. Healers and ward-makers. That was my circle, Sera. That was *my* circle."

"I know."

"How long have you known?"

"Since the night market. Since your hands. A ward-maker trained in the old forms has a particular way of—" I gestured vaguely at my own fingers. "It's a signature. Like handwriting."

"And you didn't say anything."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I need you. Because I'm a coward. Because I thought if I told you later, after you'd trusted me with something real, it would hurt less." I heard myself and laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "That last reason is the worst one. It's the one I actually believe."

The coals ticked. Somewhere above us, pigeons shuffled in the rafters. Juniper set the knife down on the pew beside her—set it down carefully, deliberately, the way you put something away so you won't reach for it—and stood up.

"Say the rest," she said.

"The raid was in the spring. Eleven arrests. Your master, Odellin, resisted. He was—" I made myself say it plainly, because plainness was all I had left to offer her. "He was killed resisting. The others were taken to the Bastion. Two died inside the first year. The rest were registered and bound—gifts sealed, memories of the working-rites scraped. They live in Greyhollow now. Some of them buy charms."

The color left her face by degrees, like water going out of a lock. "You sealed them."

"The Order did. Under my warrant."

"So every charm I've ever sold, every customer who came to my shop because their gift was scraped out and they wanted *something* back—" Her breath caught. "That was me cleaning up your paperwork."

I didn't answer. There was no honest answer that wasn't yes.

She walked to the door. Not fast—she isn't a woman who does anything fast—but with the terrible unhurried certainty of someone leaving a burning building they've decided not to save. Tam stirred, blinked at the fire, at her, at me, and read the room the way children always do, instantly and without being told.

"What happened?"

"Go back to sleep," I said.

"Nobody says 'go back to sleep' in that voice." He sat up. He looked at Juniper, silhouetted in the broken doorway with the rain coming through the empty hands above her, and then at me, and I watched him make a choice too—a child's choice, but a brave one. He got up, crossed the nave, planted himself between us like a fence post, and said:

"No. Whatever it is, you both say it out loud or nobody walks anywhere. I'm not doing this again. I'm not waking up tomorrow in a workhouse cell wondering which adult decided my life while I was sleeping."

* * *

So we said it out loud. All of it, in the dripping dark, with Tam sitting cross-legged between us like a small furious judge.

Juniper came back inside because Tam wouldn't move from the doorway and she wouldn't step over him. She sat on the far side of the fire, as far as the chapel allowed, and she asked her questions in a voice with no warmth in it at all, and I answered every one.

Did I enjoy the work. (No. Enjoyment would have been easier to forgive.)

Would I have signed it again, knowing what I know now. (I wanted to say no. I said: I don't know, and I hate that I don't know, and I think that's why I have to tell people what I've done, so the not-knowing costs me something every time.)

Had I visited any of the sealed mages since. (Once. Odellin's widow. I stood across the street from her house for two hours and couldn't make myself knock.)

At that one Juniper made a sound—not a laugh, not a sob, something between, bitten off at the root. "Two hours. You stood there two hours, and Odellin's wife spent fifteen years thinking her husband's students had abandoned her. Because we were told to scatter. Because contact meant arrest for everyone. I *obeyed*, Sera. I hid for fifteen years and sold charms and told myself the silence was devotion, and meanwhile the woman who taught me half of everything I know thought I'd forgotten her."

"I know."

"You don't know. You can't. You broke things and I hid from the breaking and neither of us—" She stopped. Pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Neither of us gets to be clean. Is that what you're saying?"

"It's what I'm saying."

Tam had gone very still during all of this, hugging his knees, and when the silence stretched long enough to creak, he said, in the blunt tone of someone who has been hungry enough to have no patience for ceremony:

"My matron sold me. For coin. People who are supposed to protect you sell you, and people who hunt you sometimes feed you—I ate more in three days with you two than in a year at St. Verity's—and I've learned you can't sort people into good and bad. You can only sort them into honest and lying. She was lying. Right now Sera's the only one here being honest, and it looks like it hurts worse than lying, so maybe that means something."

Neither of us spoke. The boy had dismantled fifteen years of theology in four sentences and was now warming his hands with great concentration, as though he hadn't.

Juniper looked at him for a long time. Then at me. Then at the altar, at the empty hands, and I saw her jaw work around words she swallowed twice before one finally came out.

"Forgiveness isn't a feeling," she said slowly, like she was testing whether the sentence would hold weight. "My master used to say that. Odellin. He said the god doesn't ask you to feel mercy, because feelings come and go like weather. He asks you to *act* mercifully, and let the weather catch up if it wants to." Her eyes came back to mine and they were wet and hard at once, and I have never been looked at like that before or since. "I don't forgive you, Sera Vane. I want to be very clear about that. What I feel right now is fifteen years of grief with nowhere to put it, and it wants to walk out that door and never see your face again."

"I know."

"But." She breathed. "But you signed the warrant and then you refused the torture order, and I don't understand a person who can do both, and I think maybe I should stay long enough to find out. Not because I forgive you. Because Odellin would have stayed. Because staying is the act, and the feeling can catch up or drown itself, I don't care which." She picked up her knife and whetstone and sat back down, on her side of the fire, and began sharpening again, and the rhythm of it was almost exactly the same as before, except now I knew it was the sound of a woman choosing, stroke by stroke, not to leave.

"Thank you," I said.

"Don't thank me. It'll wear off by morning and then we'll both be embarrassed."

* * *

Tam fell asleep first, sprawled by the fire with the total abandon of the young, one hand still near the flames. Juniper took first watch, wrapped in her coat, facing the door. Neither of us mentioned sleep for me. Inquisitors learn to go without; it's one of the gentler things we're taught.

I lay on cold stone staring up through the broken roof at a sky rinsed clean by rain, and I did not feel forgiven, and I did not feel lighter, and I understood that I never would—that absolution wasn't a door you walked through but a debt you carried openly, in daylight, where people could see it.

Somewhere past midnight Juniper spoke without turning from the door.

"Odellin's widow. Maren. Does she still live on Tanner's Row?"

"Yes."

"When this is over—if we're alive, if any of this works—I'm going to knock on that door. And you're going to stand across the street for two hours again, and then you're going to cross it with me."

"All right," I said.

"And Sera. The documents you stole. About the Hollowing. Tomorrow, on the road—you start teaching me everything in them. Everything the Order knows about what's happening to the king."

A pause. Rain threaded down through the empty hands above the door, and I thought about how the god's symbol was two palms held open, offering nothing, asking nothing, waiting.

"There's a page I haven't shown anyone," I said. "It's why I really ran."

Juniper turned then, finally, her face half firelight and half shadow.

"The Order's deep file has a list," I said. "Names, ages, districts. Fifteen years of registrations, cross-checked against birth records from the year the king fell ill. Every name on it is a child born in the capital that same season." I heard my own voice drop, the way it does when the body knows before the mind does that the next words will change everything. "One of them has a mark next to it in the Lord Inquisitor's own hand. Not *registered*. Not *sealed*. A different word entirely."

"What word?"

"*Preserved.*"

Juniper stared at me. Behind her, the rain kept falling through the hands of a silent god, and somewhere south of us, in the city we'd fled, a workhouse girl was asleep in a cot she shared with three other orphans, dreaming whatever workhouse girls dream, not knowing her name was written in ink that had cost men their careers to hide.

"Wren," Juniper breathed. "The quiet one. The one who watches everything."

"You know her?"

"She's Tam's best friend." Juniper closed her eyes. "Oh, Veil keep us. Tam's going to burn the world down to get back to her."

"He may not have to," I said, and reached for the document case at the bottom of my pack, and showed her the page.
