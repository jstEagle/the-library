## 9. Winter Comes Early

The first frost came on the ninth day of Harvestmonth, which any farmhand with two teeth and a calendar could tell you was wrong by a full six weeks.

We were three days out of the chapel of the silent god and camped in a hollow beneath a stand of black pines when I woke to find my breath hanging in the air like a small ghost that had died of boredom. Tam was already up, crouched over the fire pit, glaring at it the way one glares at a mule that refuses to pull.

"It won't light," he said.

"Wood's wet."

"The wood's *frozen*, Juniper. It was dry bark yesterday. Bark doesn't do that overnight." He held a stick out to me as evidence, like a magistrate presenting a case. The stick crackled faintly when he flexed it, the sound ice makes in a puddle, in a pipe, in a chest.

Sera stood at the lip of the hollow, looking north. She'd been doing a lot of standing at lips of things lately—her wound had closed but left her moving like a woman carrying water she refused to spill—and when I joined her I saw what she saw: the hills to the north were silvered. All of them. Not dusted, not kissed, not whatever gentle word farmers use for the pretty kind of frost. Silvered. Coated. As if someone had poured white lead down every slope while we slept.

"That's not weather," Sera said.

"No," I said. "It isn't."

Here is what nobody tells you about magic, or what nobody told me until I was fifteen years deep in hiding: you can feel it in the land the way you can feel a hearth through the floorboards of the room above. Greyhollow sat on a thin patch of it, mostly drained, which is why the city felt the way it felt—like a house where the fire has been banked too long. But out here, in the wild places between towns, the gift ran close under the soil like a warm river. You could put your palm flat against the earth and feel it humming, patient and vast, the way you might feel a great animal breathing beneath you.

I put my palm flat against the earth.

The river wasn't there anymore. Or rather, it was there, but it was cold. Not winter-cold. Drained-cold. The hum had gone thin and reedy, like a voice singing at the far end of a long tunnel, and somewhere downstream of us—somewhere north and east, toward the capital—it was being *pulled*.

I sat back on my heels and did not say anything for a long moment, because there are times when saying a thing aloud makes it real, and I have always been a coward about real things.

"Juniper?" Tam said behind me.

"Eat your breakfast," I said. "We're walking faster today."

* * *

By noon we passed our first dead field.

I say dead, and I want to be precise, because crops fail all the time and farmers are a people who know grief the way sailors know water. This wasn't failure. The barley in that field hadn't withered or yellowed or bent under blight. It had gone black—stalk, husk, and root, black as if each plant had been drawn in charcoal by a child pressing too hard. And the black didn't crumble. When Tam snapped a stalk off and held it, it rang faintly, glassy, like a burnt log tapped after the flames go down.

"Shouldn't touch it," Sera said.

"Why? Is it cursed?"

"Worse. It's *hungry*. Everything the Hollowing touches gets hollowed. That includes fingers, if you're the type who goes around touching things." She looked pointedly at Tam, who put the stalk down with the exaggerated care of someone pretending they were going to anyway.

Wren walked ahead of us with her hood up. She had been quiet since the chapel—not frightened-quiet, which I knew well enough from my own mirror, but *weighing*-quiet, the silence of a girl doing sums with numbers she didn't ask for. Every mile south, the fields got worse, and every mile south, Wren looked at them a little longer.

"They're saying it's the king," she said finally, without turning around.

"Who's saying?"

"Everyone." She nodded up the road, where a line of figures had come into view—refugees, a whole ragged procession of them, carts and bundles and children tied to their mothers with rope so no one got lost. They were coming south out of the heartlands, away from the capital, away from the cold. "Listen."

And I listened, because you can't not. A road full of refugees is loud even when it's miserable—wheels and crying and the particular argumentative squabbling of people sharing fear the way they'd share bread. But threaded through it came fragments, carried on the wind the way gossip always is:

"—the shadow walks, I saw it, I'm not lying, it came up the lane like smoke and old Marta's cow just—"

"—froze solid in the *well*, in the middle of the day, and the water below still moves—"

"—the bells rang by themselves at midnight and then stopped mid-ring, mid-ring, I swear on my mother's grave, and nothing's rung since—"

"—the king's ill, they say, ill these fifteen years, and now whatever's ill in him is getting *out*—"

Fifteen years.

I kept my face pleasant and blank, charm-seller's face, the face you wear so people talk in front of you, and inside I was doing arithmetic of my own. Fifteen years since Veil went silent. Fifteen years since the king fell ill. I had spent a night in a ruined chapel turning those two facts over like stones, finding nothing underneath either one, and here was the whole countryside confirming the coincidence without knowing it was confirming anything at all.

A woman near the front of the procession caught sight of me—the only unhooded face on our side of the road, because hiding is habit and habit dies slow—and peeled off toward me with a toddler on her hip and desperation arranged into politeness.

"You there—miss—you sell charms, yes? I've seen your sort before, in Greyhollow markets." Her eyes flicked to my cart, to the little wooden sign Tam had painted for me as a joke: *HONEST CHARMS. MOSTLY HARMLESS.* "Have you anything against shadows?"

"Against—" I blinked. "Shadows generally, or one shadow specifically?"

She did not smile. That was the thing that undid me. A month ago, a question like mine would have gotten a laugh, or a scolding, or at least the tired courtesy strangers owe each other on roads. Instead she just gripped her child tighter and said, "One that comes at night. It stands at the edge of the lamplight and it watches, and everything it looks at goes cold. Our priest says pray. Our priest's church froze from the inside out."

I should have told her I had nothing. It would even have been nearly true; my stock was honest charms, craft-charms, knots and salts and iron nails, the kind that work because people believe they work, which is its own kind of magic, the only kind left to me legally. But my hands were already moving, digging into the cart, and what they came up with was an iron nail wrapped in red thread with a knot I tied the way I tie all my knots—wrong, deliberately, a flaw in the pattern, because Veil taught me that the holy hides in the imperfect—and I pressed it into her hand and said, "Keep it above the door lintel. If the shadow comes, don't look at where it isn't."

"Where it—isn't?"

"That's where it lives. In the part of the dark the lamp hasn't claimed." I heard myself talking and marveled distantly, the way you marvel when your mouth starts reciting something you didn't know you'd memorized. "Lamplight is a wall, mistress. Build it thick."

She paid me with a copper she couldn't afford and went back to the procession clutching the nail like an amulet, which, to be fair, it was.

Tam appeared at my elbow. "That was new," he said. "You sounded like—" He paused, hunting for it.

"Like someone who used to be sworn," Sera finished, from the other side of the cart. She said it mildly, but Sera's mild is a blade worn in a soft sheath. "You haven't prayed aloud since the chapel. Now you're handing out theology to refugees."

"I handed out a nail."

"You handed out a *cosmology*. Where does it isn't live, Juniper? Because that's not charm-talk. That's Veil-talk. Old doctrine. The kind they burned books over."

I looked north, at the silvered hills, at the thin cold thread of the gift running under my feet like a wire stripped bare.

"I don't know where I know it from," I said, which was true, and also the most frightening sentence I'd said in weeks.

* * *

We made Vetchford by evening, or what was left of Vetchford, which was most of the buildings and almost none of the warmth. The town huddled along a river crossing, and the river was frozen—not iced over the way rivers freeze in proper winter, edges first, politely, but frozen straight through, top to bottom, a single block of grey-green glass with fish suspended in it mid-swim, mouths open, like flies in amber. Children were walking on it. Their parents were letting them, which tells you how cold the town was, because a parent will risk anything for a warmed room, and there weren't any.

The inn had one fire going, fed by furniture, and charged a fortune to sit near it. We paid the fortune because Sera's wound ached in the cold—I could see her guarding the left side of her ribs the way you guard a cracked tooth—and because Tam hadn't stopped shivering since morning and wouldn't admit it, and because I wanted walls around the conversation we needed to have.

We got a corner table and a pot of something the innkeeper called stew with a straight face, and I laid it out the way I lay out goods at market: plainly, priced honestly.

"The purge isn't working," I said. "Or it is, but it's not the point anymore. Think about it. They've been hunting gift-bearers across the whole kingdom for months. Hundreds taken. Thousands. If the crown wanted us dead, we'd be dead, and cheaply—there are cheaper ways than hunter companies in every shire. But the land's draining anyway, faster wherever the hunts are thickest." I turned my cup slowly on the table. "They're not killing the gift-bearers. They're *harvesting* them."

Sera set down her spoon. Around us the common room murmured—refugees again, packed three families to a room upstairs—and her eyes did the thing they do when the inquisitor machinery behind them starts turning, grim and quick. "Feeding," she said quietly. "Something's feeding."

"On the gift itself. On the land's gift. On the people who carry it." I looked at Tam, and hated myself for it, and made myself keep looking, because he deserved to be included in his own danger. "On boys whose magic runs hot."

"So we turn around," Tam said. His voice was steady. His spoon was not; it rattled faintly against the bowl, and he stilled it with visible effort. "South is the capital. North is anywhere else. We go north, right? We get clear of the drain, we find some corner where it hasn't reached—"

"And then what?" Wren asked.

It was the first full sentence she'd volunteered since the chapel, and it landed in the table's center like a dropped coin. Tam stared at her. So did I, honestly.

"And then we live," he said.

"For how long?" Wren pulled back her hood. She had ash-brown hair and her mother's stubborn chin and the eyes of someone who had watched important people decide things her entire life without ever once being consulted. "Look at the river outside. Look at the fields. Whatever this is, it started in the capital and it's spreading outward like ink in water. There is no corner it won't reach. There's just sooner and later." She folded her hands on the table, workhouse-neat, a girl who had learned early that keeping your hands visible keeps them safe. "Sooner and later. That's the whole choice anyone's offering us."

"That's not fair," Tam said. "You're sixteen."

"So are you. Fair left the road a long time ago, Tam. It's back in Greyhollow, probably, frozen mid-step like the bells." But her voice gentled on his name, because Wren's sharpness has never once been aimed at Tam, not truly. "I'm not saying I want to march at anything. I'm saying pretending there's a north is a lie we'd be telling ourselves, and I've watched people die comfortably of lies my whole life. I'd rather die inconveniently of the truth."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a baby cried and was hushed.

Sera broke it, because Sera breaks silences the way she breaks everything else—cleanly, and regretting it slightly. "There's another consideration," she said, "and I've been chewing it since the chapel. The timing." She counted on her fingers, inquisitor-fashion, evidence laid out for a tribunal of three. "Veil falls silent. Same year, the king falls ill. Fifteen years pass. The king's illness worsens—the court physicians' notes confirm it, I stole enough of them—and in the same span, magic grows thinner everywhere. Ask any hedge-witch over forty. The wells of the gift are lower than they were. And now, in a single season, the drain accelerates past anything in the record." She looked at me. "Juniper. Your god didn't abandon you. Your god is *tied to this*. Whatever sits sick on that throne, your god is tangled in the same knot."

"You don't know that."

"I know patterns. Patterns were my trade." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, the kind you wear over a confession. "I spent ten years finding patterns in innocent people's lives and calling them guilt. I would give a great deal for that skill to be wrong now. It isn't."

I wanted to argue. I want to be honest about that; the wanting was enormous. Because if Sera was right—if Veil's silence and the Hollow King's rise were the same event—then my fifteen years of grieving had been grief over something I might have *done something about*, and every charm I'd sold in hiding, every careful invisible year, had been a sentry asleep at her post. Faith curdles fast when you suspect it was stolen rather than lost. It curdles into purpose, and purpose is heavy, and I wasn't sure I could lift it.

But the land decided the argument for me, the way land does.

It happened as we rose to take rooms. The common room door banged open and a man staggered in—a farmer by his hands, a ruined man by his face—and he stood dripping melted snow on the boards and said, in a voice pitched to carry no further than it had to and carrying anyway:

"It took the Hollis farm. Whole place. We went out at dusk because their chimney hadn't smoked all day, and the house was fine, the house was *warm*, and they were sitting at supper." He swallowed. "All four of them. Sitting at supper. Frozen. The bread frozen halfway to a mouth. And on the window, facing out toward the road—" His hand shook. He looked at the floor. He looked at all of us, forty strangers in a room, and chose honesty because there was nothing left that lying could protect. "There was a shape burned into the frost on the glass. Like a crown. Drawn from the *inside*."

The room made a sound I'll hear until I die—not a scream, not a gasp, but a low collective exhale, the sound of a hundred people deciding at once how afraid to be, and failing to decide low enough.

I felt it before I understood it: my magic, such as I had, the small sworn ember of it I kept banked under my sternum like a coal in ash—that ember *guttered*. Flickered. For half a heartbeat the world went flat and grey and utterly ordinary, a world with no gift running under it anywhere, a world like the one I'd pretended to live in for fifteen years—and I understood, with the clarity of the deeply frightened, that I hadn't been pretending. I'd been practicing. The whole kingdom was becoming Greyhollow. The whole kingdom was learning to be hollow.

Then the ember steadied, weakly, a candle in a draft, and I gripped the table and breathed.

"Juniper." Tam's hand on my arm. "You went white."

"Did I." I straightened. Across the room, the farmer was being given soup and brandy and space, and outside the window the frozen river gleamed under a moon that looked, tonight, like a coin rubbed thin.

Sera leaned close. "You felt it too."

"Yes."

"How much is left?"

I thought about the thread of warmth under the soil, thinner than yesterday. Thinner tomorrow. I thought about a nail above a door lintel and a shadow living in the dark the lamp hasn't claimed, and about a girl at this table who was right, damn her, about sooner and later.

"Not enough to hide behind," I said. "Not for long."

Wren was watching the fire, and the firelight moved on her face, and she said, so quietly I nearly missed it under the room's murmur: "Then we'd better reach the capital before it reaches us."

North lay the cold. South lay the throne. Between them lay four hundred miles of blackening fields, frozen rivers, and a kingdom learning, night by night, what stood at the edge of its lamplight.

In the morning, we turned south.
