## 10. The Heir in the Workhouse

The documents had been sitting in my pack for three weeks, wrapped in oilcloth, pressed between a spare shirt and a half-eaten heel of bread that had gone hard enough to qualify as a weapon. I'd carried them out of the Inquisition's archive under my coat, past two checkpoints and one very suspicious cat, and in all that time I had never once opened them past the first page.

I told myself it was caution. Caution is a respectable word. It sounds like wisdom instead of cowardice.

The truth was simpler: the first page was a list of names, and the second name on it was mine.

*Vane, Seraphine — Order of the Quiet Flame — clearance sufficient for retrieval of sealed lineage materials.*

I had signed myself into this. Not the smuggling — that came later, the night I refused the child with the gift and they branded me traitor in front of everyone I'd ever loved. But years before that, I had been the hand that carried these papers, the obedient instrument that filed away royal secrets so thoroughly that even I didn't know what I was filing.

Juniper found me staring at the pack by firelight, the night after we crossed the river and left the frozen fields behind us.

"You've been looking at your bag like it owes you money," she said. She was feeding twigs to the flames one at a time, as if each were a personal decision she wanted to savor. "Either open it or throw it in the river."

"The river's frozen."

"Then you're out of excuses."

She wasn't wrong. That was the problem with Juniper Vasquez — she was almost never wrong, and she knew exactly how annoying that was, and she did it anyway, every time, with the serenity of a woman who had already made peace with being insufferable.

Tam was asleep against Wren's shoulder, which was itself a small miracle. The boy slept like a soldier now, one eye always half-open, his scarred hands curled under his chin. Wren sat awake beside him, mending a sock with the grim competence of someone who had done a great deal of mending and very little of anything else.

I unwrapped the oilcloth.

* * *

Here is what I should explain about Inquisition archives: they are organized like a mind that hates itself. Everything is cross-referenced twice and indexed three times, and the truly important things are buried under deliberately boring titles. The document I pulled out that night was catalogued under *Miscellaneous Household Expenditures, Reign of Aldric II, Folio 114*, and if you read the first paragraph it really was about household expenditures — candles, mostly, an alarming quantity of candles for a palace that supposedly worshipped economy.

Read further, though. Read between the candle invoices.

Fifteen years ago — fifteen years and roughly four months, to be precise, because the Inquisition timestamps everything down to the hour — a midwife arrived at the palace gates at midnight, delivered a child, and left before dawn. The child was not the queen's. The child was not anyone's official business at all. But the expenditure folio recorded payment for her keep, routed through a laundry account, and the laundry account was administered by an order sister named Maren Cotch, and Sister Maren Cotch had, per the follow-up correspondence, been assigned to Greyhollow.

To a workhouse. Saint Verity's Home for Foundling Children, on Lantern Row, six streets from where Juniper sold charms to people who couldn't afford them.

And there was a name in the margin, written in the tight, joyless hand of an archivist who suspected someday someone would come looking: *the girl has her mother's eyes. Grey. Like the old king's.*

Wren was sitting across the fire from me when I read that line. Wren, who never asked questions about other people's business because she'd learned early that answers cost more than ignorance. Wren, whose eyes in the firelight were the exact grey of a winter sky, of old coin, of the portrait of King Aldric II that hung in every courthouse in Veylmark — the portrait they'd quietly taken down after his death, the one I'd stared at through ten years of training and never once thought about again until this moment.

"Say something," Juniper said. "You've gone the color of porridge."

I said nothing. I turned the page instead, because turning pages is easier than speaking, and there it was in the follow-up correspondence, dated fourteen years back: *Sister Cotch reports the girl thrives. She asks no questions. Payment will continue quarterly. If the succession question resolves otherwise, terminate the arrangement.*

Terminate the arrangement.

They hadn't. Which meant somewhere in the tangle of dead archivists and burned records and a kingdom sliding into unnatural winter, a quarterly payment had kept arriving at Saint Verity's for a fifteen-year-old girl with the old king's eyes — right up until, presumably, the Hollow King's hunters got around to reading their own mail.

"Wren," I said.

She looked up. Needle paused mid-stitch. She had that quality the quiet ones have, of giving you their whole attention without demanding anything back, and it made me feel like a man about to kick a dog that trusts him.

"How long have you been at Saint Verity's?"

"All my life." A beat. "Why?"

"Do you know why you were there? Who paid for you?"

Her needle went still entirely. Tam stirred against her shoulder but didn't wake.

"The matron said the church paid." Her voice was flat, careful. "Matron Hesk says a lot of things."

"When did Matron Hesk start saying things differently? When did the payments stop, or change, or get strange?"

"I—" And then I watched it happen, the moment a person's whole history rearranges itself behind their eyes. "Three weeks ago. She stopped letting me take the younger ones to market. She locked the east room. She asked me my birthday, which she's known since I was four, and she wrote it down, and she looked at me like—"

Like inventory, I thought. Like a ledger entry that had suddenly become valuable.

"She looked at me," Wren finished, "like she was measuring me for something."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, the wind moved through the blackened trees with that sound it made lately, less like wind and more like breathing.

"We're going back," Juniper said.

Everyone looked at her. Even Tam, surfacing from sleep, blinking.

"Back?" I said. "Greyhollow? The city with the checkpoints and the registration decrees and the hunters dragging people out of their beds? That Greyhollow?"

"Yes."

"Juniper, the purge lists are posted on every corner. Your face is on two of them. Mine is on four — I checked, it was flattering, better likeness than I expected—"

"And hers," Juniper said quietly, nodding at Wren, "is going to be on worse ones, if we don't move first. Because whatever's wearing the king killed the whole legitimate line fifteen years ago except for one baby, and somebody hid that baby in the cheapest workhouse in the poorest quarter of the city, and the only reason she's alive is that nobody important could be bothered to look down." She poked the fire. "People stop bothering to look down right up until the thing they buried climbs up and bites them. Then everybody looks down at once. We want to be gone before that happens. Or better — we want to be holding her hand when it does."

"That's a speech," I said. "That's a whole speech. You've clearly been saving it."

"I've been workshopping it, yes."

Wren hadn't said anything. She'd set down the mending, both hands flat on her knees, and she was looking into the fire with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it myself, once, the day the Order told me what I really was to them. It was the expression of someone doing arithmetic with their own life and getting an answer they don't believe.

"No," she said.

* * *

She said it eleven more times over the next two days, and I know because I counted, and because by the ninth time Tam had started counting aloud with me, which earned him a look that could curdle milk.

She said no on the road, walking north through country where the frost lay on the ground in patterns too regular to be weather — long straight lines, like something vast had dragged its fingers through the fields. She said no at noon, when Juniper tried the gentle approach and explained about lineages and legitimacy with the delicacy of a woman defusing a bomb by describing it. She said no at dusk, when I tried the honest approach and laid the documents out flat and let her read the archivist's marginal note about her own eyes.

She read it twice. Then she handed it back and said, "A name in a margin isn't a crown."

"It's more than most people get," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because it sounded like an argument when what it was, was fear. My fear. Because I had spent my entire adult life serving a throne, and I knew exactly what thrones did to the people set upon them, and some traitorous part of me wanted her to refuse just so none of us would have to walk back into Greyhollow and find out whether hope was real or just another word for bait.

"It's less than most people need," she said. "I watched important people decide everything my whole life. The matron decided who ate. The inspectors decided who stayed. The church decided who got taken to the chapel and who didn't come back." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening part. It got quieter and flatter, a blade being honed rather than swung. "Now you want to tell me I'm important too. That I'm the most important. And you expect me to thank you?"

"No," Juniper said. "We expect you to come anyway."

"Why?"

"Because the hunters are coming regardless, and out here there's no one to stand between you and them. Because Tam loves you and would follow you into a furnace, and I'd rather he didn't have to. And because—" she hesitated, and for once Juniper Vasquez, who always had the sentence ready, didn't, "—because I spent fifteen years hiding from something I thought was my fault, and it nearly ate me alive. Being found is terrible. I recommend it over the alternative."

That bought a silence, but not a yes.

It was Tam who broke it, that night, in the way Tam broke everything — accidentally, loudly, and with total sincerity.

"They'll burn her," he said. He was sitting apart, knees drawn up, and he said it to the fire rather than to Wren, which somehow made it worse. "The matron sold me for two crowns. Two. I heard her haggle. And Wren's worth—" he gestured vaguely at the documents, at the idea of her, "—worth whatever the king pays for a whole bloodline. They won't arrest her. You don't arrest a threat. They'll burn her and call it mercy and put up a notice."

"Tam," Wren said sharply.

"Well, it's true! Everybody keeps being polite at you like you've got time for it!" He was on his feet now, and his hands were shaking, and I saw Juniper's eyes flick to the nearest snow patch — assessing, always assessing, how far to the nearest water if the boy's gift boiled over. "I watched them drag Mrs. Ellery out of her shop. She made luck-charms. Little ones. Stupid ones. They burned her cart and her cat ran off and nobody — nobody did anything, because everybody was busy being invisible." His voice cracked. "I'm not being invisible again. Not for you. So say yes, or I'm going back alone, and I'll probably explode something, and it'll be your fault."

"That's the worst argument I've ever heard," Wren said.

"Yeah," he said, scrubbing his sleeve across his face. "Probably."

She was quiet for a long moment. Then she picked up her mending — the eternal sock — and stabbed the needle through it, and said, without looking up:

"If I do this. If. Nobody kneels. Nobody puts me on a horse I haven't approved. And when this is over — whatever 'over' means — I go back to a normal life. A small one. With people in it who don't decide things about me."

"Deal," said Juniper instantly.

"Agreed," I said, and meant it, and privately resolved to break it only if the kingdom required breaking, which — knowing kingdoms — it would.

* * *

We traveled by night after that, which suited the season, since the days had begun to arrive pre-darkened, the sun rising late and setting early like a laborer who'd given notice. The cold had teeth in it now. Real teeth. On the third morning we passed a well with the rope frozen solid in the air above the bucket, hanging in its own ice like a specimen in glass, and Tam touched it and whispered, "that's not possible," and none of us argued, because none of us had a better theory than *the king's shadow walks at night,* which was the refugees' theory, and refugees have excellent instincts about catastrophe.

Greyhollow announced itself by smell before sight — coal smoke and river rot and the particular staleness of ten thousand lives packed behind walls. From the ridge above the Lantern Row gate, the city looked unchanged, which is how cities lie. Bells were ringing vespers, ordinary and slow. Carts creaked. A dog barked at nothing.

Only the posters were new. Layered on every wall, three and four deep, fresh ink over older ink: REGISTER OR BE SEIZED. REWARD FOR REPORTING UNLAWFUL GIFT-BEARERS. And beneath the seal of the crown — a seal that had always looked to me like a keyhole, though I'd never let myself think so — the words BY ORDER OF HIS MAJESTY, IN HIS LONG AND RIGHTEOUS REIGN.

Long and righteous. The ink was barely dry and the sun was barely rising and already the lies had a head start on us.

"Saint Verity's is two streets in," I murmured. "There's a side entrance through the wash-yard, if Cotch hasn't changed the layout—"

"Cotch is dead," Wren said.

We all turned. She stood with her hood up, watching the gate, and her voice was steady in the way of a person standing on the last plank of a bridge.

"I heard the matron talking before I left. Sister Cotch fell down her own stairs two weeks ago. The same week the matron started locking rooms." She pulled her hood lower. "So whoever was paying for me either stopped paying, or started asking questions, and the matron answered one of those by killing the only person who knew what I was."

The arithmetic was ugly and simple. If Cotch was dead, then either the secret was safe — buried with the woman who kept it — or it had died *loudly*, and the matron had already sold what she knew to whoever paid best. Given that Matron Hesk had sold Tam for two crowns, I did not love our odds on the second possibility.

"There's another way to know," Juniper said slowly. "If the matron's already talked, the hunters will have been to the workhouse. There'll be a mark on the door, or a watcher, or the place will simply be empty. If they haven't been yet, it'll be ordinary. Terrible and ordinary, the way workhouses are." She looked at Wren. "You grew up there. Can you walk in like you belong?"

"I can walk anywhere like I belong," Wren said. "It's the only skill poverty teaches properly."

So we went in — Tam and I as laborers hauling a borrowed cart of rags, Juniper hooded and bent with a false stoop, Wren in front, spine straight, gait easy, belonging everywhere and nowhere. The wash-yard gate was unlocked. The yard was empty of children, which was wrong; Saint Verity's yard at that hour should have been loud with small bodies burning off the misery of breakfast.

Inside, the corridor smelled of lye and cold. And on the matron's door — the office at the end, the door with the little barred window — there was a mark chalked in white. Three strokes and a circle.

An Inquisition sigil. *Assessed. Cleared for collection.*

I knew the hand that drew it. I'd trained it.

"Oh no," I said, before I could think better of it, and Juniper's head snapped toward me.

"What?"

"The mark's fresh. Hours old, maybe less. And it's Kettering's notation — he was my second, once, before—" I swallowed. "If Kettering assessed the building and marked it cleared, he hasn't taken her yet. He's coming back. Tonight, probably, with a full detail, because a collection order for *this* prize doesn't travel light."

"Then we take her out now," Tam said, "before—"

From somewhere deeper in the building, a floorboard creaked. Then another. Then, unmistakably, close — a voice, dry and administrative and horribly familiar, saying:

"—and the girl's things are all accounted for, Matron. You'll be compensated for your cooperation. Where is she kept?"

Wren went white as paper. Tam grabbed her hand. And through the barred window of the office door, I saw the shadow of a man turn toward the corridor — toward us — and I did the only sensible thing left in a life that had recently run out of sensible things.

I kicked the door open, and said, "Hello, Kettering. Miss me?"
