## 11. Wren

I'd been minding my own business for fifteen years, which, if you asked anyone at Saint Ambrose's Workhouse for Foundling and Unfortunate Children, was the only thing I'd ever done well.

"Too quiet," Matron Coddle used to say, clicking her tongue like a clock that disapproved of itself. "A girl your age should be underfoot. Should be *wanting* things." She said it like wanting was a virtue and I was a moral failing. Maybe to her it was. Wanting things in Greyhollow got you taken, though—wanting got Tam boiled half a river and run out of the only home either of us had. So I didn't want. I watched. I waited. I kept my head down and my hands busy and I made myself useful enough to be left alone, which is the closest thing to freedom a workhouse girl ever gets.

And then four strangers came back into my life like a rock through a window.

* * *

They found me at the wash line, because of course they did. Sera Vane—the disgraced inquisitor with the burn scar crawling up her neck like ivy made of fire—stood at the gate with Juniper Reyes and a boy wrapped in three coats who looked like he'd been crying and running and crying again. Sera had papers. Actual crown documents, stolen from the inquisition's own vaults, and she held them the way you hold something that has already bitten you once.

"Wren," Tam said, and his voice cracked down the middle. "Wren, they say—I mean, there's this document, and it says—"

"It says," Sera said, in that flat inquisitor voice that probably worked wonders on prisoners and absolutely nothing on me, "that on the third night of the Hollowing, fifteen years ago, a midwife smuggled a newborn out of the palace. A newborn with a birthmark on her left shoulder blade. A newborn registered to a workhouse in Greyhollow under a name chosen from a list."

She looked at me when she said it. Everyone looked at me. Even the matron, who had come out to see what the fuss was and gone very still and very pale, in the way of a woman doing arithmetic about coin purses and hunters' bounties.

"The mark," Juniper said gently. She was smaller than I expected, the famous charm-seller, with ink-stained fingers and tired eyes. "May we?"

I didn't say yes. But I turned around anyway, which amounted to the same thing, and pulled my collar aside. I'd had the mark my whole life—a curling dark shape like a crescent moon tangled in briars—and I'd hidden it the way you hide anything that makes people look twice. Matron Coddle had called it a devil's kiss and scrubbed at it with lye soap every washing day until I was twelve, when she stopped, and I never knew why. Now I knew why. Lye doesn't come out. Neither does royalty.

The silence behind me went on so long I started counting stitches on my sleeve.

"Well," I said finally. "Congratulations. You've found her. The lost princess. She's hanging sheets."

* * *

Here's what nobody understood, standing there in the yard with their mouths open: they thought they were handing me the best news of my life. They thought they were giving me something.

But I had spent my whole existence watching important people decide everything. The matron decided who ate first and who got the thin gruel at the bottom of the pot. The city guard decided which streets were safe after dark, and then decided they weren't. The inquisition decided who had magic and who therefore deserved to die. The crown decided there was a purge, and the crown's illness decided the purge would be a harvest, and not one soul in that entire chain—not one—had ever once looked at a girl at a wash line and thought, *what does she want?*

I was eleven when the collectors came for the winter tithe and took the workhouse's entire coal supply because some lord three hundred miles away needed warmer toes. Forty children shivered through a Greyhollow January. Two of them coughed themselves into graves by spring. Nobody asked us. Important people had decided.

I was thirteen when a boy named Fitch was dragged out for having the gift—a little trick with sparks, nothing, less than nothing—and the neighbors who'd known him his whole life stood in the street and let it happen, because important people had decided magic was a crime, and it is astonishing what ordinary people will allow when someone else has done the deciding for them.

So no. When a disgraced inquisitor and a charm-seller and my best friend stood in front of me holding a birthmark up like a lantern and saying *you're the true heir, you could fix all of it*—

"No," I said.

Juniper blinked. "No?"

"No. I'm not doing it. Find another orphan with a shoulder." I picked up my basket. My hands were shaking, but the basket gave them a job. "You want to save the kingdom? Wonderful. Go save it. Why do you need me?"

"Because the seal is failing," Sera said. "Because by solstice the Hollow King walks free and every gift-bearer in Veylmark dies screaming, and the binding requires—" She stopped herself. Looked at Juniper. Juniper looked at the ground.

"Because it needs someone the land will actually answer," Juniper finished quietly. "The old blood. Your blood. It's not a lever I can pull alone."

"Then you'll fail," I said, and immediately hated how much it hurt to say it, and said it anyway, louder. "Plenty of things fail. Everything fails, eventually. That's what the world *is.* I'm not going to stand on a throne and be the next person deciding everyone's fate. I've seen what deciders look like from underneath. I'm not becoming one."

Matron Coddle cleared her throat. I swear on every saint the workhouse didn't believe in, that woman had been waiting fifteen years for a bounty opportunity, and here were four fugitives delivered to her gate. "Wren," she said sweetly, "perhaps you'd like to discuss this inside—"

"I'd like you to count the linens, matron," I said, "and notice there are four missing, and decide whether that's worth dying over today."

It's amazing what you can learn watching people instead of talking. I learned exactly where her greed lived and exactly where her fear lived, and I put my basket down between them.

"We're leaving now," I said. "All of us. And you never saw us."

* * *

We walked out of Greyhollow through the fish market, which smelled like regret and vinegar, and nobody spoke until the walls were behind us. Then Tam caught my sleeve.

"You can't just say no," he whispered. "Wren, they'll kill everyone. They killed—my rain, Wren, I *boiled a river,* and if they find me—"

"I know what they'll do."

"Then why won't you—"

"Because I don't want to matter!" It came out of me like something breaking a window to get free. A fishwife stared. I lowered my voice. "Tam. Every terrible thing that ever happened to anybody happened because somebody important decided something. If I stand up there, I'm just the newest somebody important. And someday some girl at a wash line looks up at *me* and gets her coal taken."

"That's not—"

"Isn't it?"

He didn't have an answer. He was fourteen; he wasn't required to.

Juniper fell into step beside me. She didn't argue. That was the thing about her that unnerved me more than Sera's scar or Tam's heat shimmering off his skin—she didn't argue, didn't plead, didn't do any of the things important people did when they wanted something from you. She just walked next to me, quiet as her god, and after two miles of frozen ruts she said:

"All right."

"All right?"

"You said no. So. No." She shrugged. "Come with us as far as the crossroads, at least. There's a field I want to show you. Then you can go home."

"There's nothing to see at a crossroads."

"There's something to see in a field."

* * *

The field lay a mile off the north road, and I'd have walked past it without a glance, because it looked like every other field in Veylmark that autumn: brown stubble, broken fence, gray sky pressing down like a lid.

Except it wasn't autumn anymore.

Juniper stopped at the fence rail and didn't say a word. I climbed up beside her because standing felt wrong and sitting felt worse.

The wheat was black. Not dead-black, not burned-black—*froze*-black, every stalk sheathed in ice that had grown inward from the middle of the field outward, in a perfect circle a quarter mile wide, as if winter had fallen out of the sky and landed in one spot. Beyond the circle the crops were merely dying, bent over like old men, but inside it the stalks stood straight and glassy and rang faintly in the wind, chiming against each other like a thousand tiny bells.

In the middle of the circle, half-buried, was a plow. Frozen mid-furrow. And beside it—

"They were working the field," Juniper said. Her voice was very even, the way voices go when they're holding something heavy. "Farmer and his sons. Three days ago. The frost took them where they stood. We buried them by the wall. Their neighbors wouldn't touch them—said the cold was a curse, and cursed men stay cursed."

I looked at the three fresh mounds by the stone wall, each marked with a stick and a strip of cloth, and I thought: *important people decided something,* and the decision landed here, in this dirt, on these men, and they hadn't even heard it being made. They'd been plowing.

"The Hollow King's winter," Sera said behind us. "It spreads from the capital. Another week, maybe two, and it reaches Greyhollow. After solstice, it reaches everywhere. Permanently."

Tam stood at the fence with his arms wrapped around himself, steam rising faintly off his shoulders where his gift leaked out of him like heat through a bad window, and even he looked cold.

"This is what 'eventually' looks like," Juniper said, still not looking at me. "You said everything fails eventually. You're right. But failure has a face, Wren. It has three faces, right there, under those cloths. Somebody's going to decide what happens to this land. The Hollow King has already decided his part. The question isn't whether someone decides. The question is whether anyone who's ever been cold gets a word in."

I gripped the fence rail. The wood bit into my palms and I let it, because pain is honest and I was tired of everything else lying to me.

"I watched important people my whole life," I said. "They took our coal. They took Fitch. They took Tam. They took *you*—" I nodded at Juniper's bare wrists, where a sworn mage's brands would have been if the law allowed her to keep them. "And none of them ever asked. Not once. Fifteen years, and not one of them ever asked what I wanted."

"No," Juniper agreed. "Nobody asked me either. That's rather the point of being poor."

"And you want me to become them."

"I want you to become *you.*" She finally turned, and her eyes were steady and sad and completely without pressure, which is the most persuasive thing in the world. "There's a difference. The ones who took everything never stood in a field and counted the bodies. You're counting them right now. That's the difference. That's the whole difference, and it might be enough."

The wind moved through the frozen wheat and it chimed, that horrible little bell-song, and I looked at the plow standing crooked in its last furrow, and the man who'd pushed it, under the stick and the cloth, and I thought about the matron's arithmetic and the collectors' ledgers and every sealed decree that had ever landed on Greyhollow like weather.

Important people decided. That was the order of the world.

But orders can be changed. I'd watched the workhouse girls do it a hundred times in small ways—who slept by the stove, who got the second helping—quiet mutinies of kindness arranged by the ones underneath. The deciding didn't have to belong to the top. Maybe it had only pooled there because nobody below ever reached up and *took some.*

Maybe a girl at a wash line could reach.

"I'm not doing it for the crown," I said finally. "Or the throne, or the blood, or any of it. I'm not becoming a queen. Queens are just matrons with better soap."

Juniper's mouth twitched. "Understood."

"If I come—if I *try*—it's for them." I nodded at the three mounds. "For the coal and the gruel and Fitch and Tam's river and every kid who froze while someone important decided. That's the whole contract. Break it and I walk."

"That," Juniper said, "is the finest coronation oath I've ever heard, and I include the actual king's."

Sera snorted. Tam laughed—a wet, hiccupping laugh, but a laugh—and the sound of it did something to my chest that I decided not to examine.

I climbed down off the fence. My legs felt strange, like they belonged to someone taller.

"So," I said. "Solstice. The capital. A door that needs my blood to open. What exactly does an heir *do* at a binding?"

Juniper and Sera exchanged a look—the particular look of people who know the answer and are deciding how much of it to survive telling me.

"That," Juniper said carefully, "depends on how much of the truth you can carry before the road tests it."

I picked up my pack. Behind us the frozen field chimed in the wind, three graves keeping their own silent counsel, and ahead the road ran north toward a city where bells had stopped ringing and something ancient wore a king like a coat.

"Test away," I said. "I've survived Matron Coddle's cooking. I can survive anything."

None of them laughed this time.

That should have told me something.
