## 12. The Hollow King's Hunters

Here's a thing nobody tells you about being cursed: it's patient. My gift sat in my chest for twelve years like a kettle left on a low flame, hissing, waiting. It never went away. It never got better. It just waited for me to be scared enough to need it.

The river crossing at Marlow's Ford was where it stopped waiting.

We'd been walking four days since the chapel, and Sera's shoulder had healed crooked from where the beam fell on her — she said it was fine, which in Sera-language meant it hurt like sin and she'd rather die than admit it. Wren walked beside me most of the day, quiet the way she was quiet, which wasn't empty-quiet like mine used to be but full-quiet, like a room with somebody thinking hard inside it. And Juniper led us down toward the water, humming an old hymn to Veil under her breath, off-key, because apparently even sworn priestesses can't carry a tune.

I remember thinking: this is almost nice. I remember thinking that.

You'd think I'd have learned by now.

* * *

Marlow's Ford wasn't much of a ford anymore. The Hollow King's winter had gotten there first and frozen the river solid — not pretty-frozen, not the way ponds freeze back home with the boys skating on them, but *wrong*-frozen, black ice with white veins running through it like something was trapped underneath and still trying to breathe. Juniper crouched at the bank and touched it and pulled her hand back fast.

"The gift's been pulled out of this water," she said. "It's not ice. It's just... absence."

"Cheerful," Sera said. "Can we cross it or can't we?"

"We can cross it. Carefully."

Wren stepped out first, testing the ice with each step like she'd done it all her life, and maybe she had, workhouse kids learn everything useful young. I followed, arms out, feeling like a cat on a fence rail. Halfway across, the ice sang under us. Not cracked. Sang. A long low note like a bell with a throatful of gravel, rolling up through my boots and into my teeth.

Juniper went rigid. "That's not the ice."

I know what you're thinking, and yes, I also know now what it was — the bells in the capital, frozen mid-ring, singing their one held note all the way out into the country. But standing on that dead river with the sound coming up through my feet, all I knew was that every hair on my body stood up and screamed *run*, and I'm not smart, but I've learned to listen when my whole skeleton votes at once.

"Hunters," Sera said. She didn't shout it. She said it the way you say *it's raining*. Then she pointed upstream, and I saw them.

Six figures on the far bank. They wore no livery at all, which is how I knew they were the real thing — the crown hunters we'd seen so far wore grey cloaks and badges and liked to be seen. These six wore nothing that marked them, moved like they'd been poured into their bodies rather than born into them, and the snow around their boots wasn't melting. It was *leaning toward them*, bending in the wind of them, the way fire leans.

"The Hollow King's own," Juniper whispered. "Not hunters. Harvesters."

"That distinction matters very little right now," Sera said, and drew her knife, because Sera solves problems with steel the way other people solve problems with words.

The first one raised its hand — I'm going to keep calling them *its*, because whatever looked out through those faces, it wasn't using the eyes — and the ice beneath Wren's feet turned white, then spider-webbed, then shattered upward in a bloom of shards that should have cut her to ribbons.

Except Juniper moved first. I'll never get tired of watching that, actually, even after everything. She threw both hands out and the shards hung in the air like a stopped rainstorm, trembling, and then she flung them back and two of the six went down. The rest didn't even slow.

"Wren, Tam, run!" she shouted. "Sera, with me!"

And here's the part I keep replaying, the part I'll be replaying when I'm old if I get to be old: I ran. Of course I ran. That was the plan. Juniper and Sera would hold them, Wren and I would make the far bank and get clear, and then — and this is the stupid, beautiful, doomed arithmetic of people who love you — they'd catch up later. Later always comes. Later is the lie everybody in a story tells themselves.

Wren made the bank. I was three steps behind her.

Then the tall one — the leader, I guess, though they all moved like parts of one animal — did something with its fingers, casual as snapping for a waiter's attention, and the river reached up.

Not the ice. The *river*. Underneath the dead ice, some last thread of living water answered that thing the way I answer Juniper calling my name, and it came up through the broken hole in a black rope thick as my waist, and it took Wren around the ankle and hauled.

She screamed. Short. More furious than afraid — that's Wren, even hanging upside down over a frozen river she sounded mostly annoyed — and then the rope of water started to drag her back across the ice toward the harvesters, and I heard the tall one speak for the first time. Its voice came out wrong, layered, like three men talking in one mouth slightly out of sync:

"The boy burns. The girl crowns. Bring both."

*Both.* Both of us. And Wren going away from me across that ice, and Juniper fifty yards back holding a wall of frost off herself with shaking arms, and Sera sprinting flat-out and too far, everyone too far, everyone exactly as far as they needed to be to not reach me in time.

Which meant it was just me. Me and the kettle in my chest.

* * *

Let me tell you about my gift, since I'm finally telling somebody.

It isn't fire. Everyone assumed fire, because I scalded the matron's hand once and boiled the wash-water twice and generally left a trail of blistered things behind me like a snail made of steam. But it isn't fire. Fire is a *thing*. What I have is heat itself — the motion of everything small and fast, the part of the world that never sits still. When I lose my grip, everything near me starts moving faster. Water boils. Air shimmers. Skin reddens, blisters, splits. Rain, when it caught me once in the workhouse yard, hit my shoulders and came off again as steam, and the matron watched that happen and sold me to the crown within the week.

Twelve years of holding it down. Twelve years of cold baths and clenched teeth and sleeping with my hands wrapped in wet rags, and every single night dreaming about what would happen if I ever let go, and every single morning waking up grateful I hadn't found out.

Standing on that ice, watching the water drag Wren away, I found out.

* * *

It didn't feel like power. I want that on the record. It felt like falling.

I stopped running. I turned around. And I opened my hands — I don't know why the hands, it just felt like the thing to do, like the gift lived somewhere behind my knuckles — and I let go.

The world went loud.

Every drop of water within sight of me heard me at once. I could feel the whole river — not the surface, the *whole* of it, every ounce from bank to bank and bed to sky, a mile of black water suddenly aware of a boy standing on its frozen skin telling it, without words, *wake up*. And it woke up angry, or maybe it woke up echoing me, because I was angry, angrier than I've ever been, the pure dumb animal anger of a kid who has watched every person he loves either leave or get taken and is *done*.

The ice didn't crack. It detonated. From me outward, a ring of white shrapnel, and the river underneath came up through it in a wall.

I heard someone screaming and realized distantly it was me, because it hurt. Nobody warns you about that either. Using your whole gift for the first time is like shouting after a lifetime of whispering — your voice tears. Mine tore everywhere. Heat roared up my arms and down my spine and out through my eyes, and I felt my own skin tighten and split along my forearms in lines like the river ice, and I didn't care, because the water had heard me.

The rope holding Wren boiled to steam before anyone touched it. She dropped onto the ice, rolled, came up running. The wall of river — my river, my boiling, shrieking, alive river — came down across the crossing in a curtain of scalding fog, and through it I felt the six of them, felt them the way you feel a splinter, wrong shapes in my new hot awareness, and I pushed.

Two of them simply came apart. I don't know how else to describe it. The water took them and they weren't built to be taken; they folded like paper in a fire, and the layered voices made one short harmonized note of surprise and stopped.

The tall one stood in the steam and looked at me. Even through the fog, even with my eyes streaming, I could see it wasn't hurt. It was *interested*.

"Yes," it said, in its three-mouthed voice, and it smiled with a face that had forgotten how. "There he is."

Then Sera hit it like a landslide.

She'd crossed half the ice while I was busy becoming a natural disaster, and she didn't use the knife — I think she understood, even then, that steel wouldn't matter to a thing like that — she just put her whole body between it and me, arms wide, the way you'd shield a candle from wind. The tall one moved its hand again, that casual snap, and something came out of it that wasn't water and wasn't cold exactly but was winter itself, the idea of winter, the long dark hungry version of it, and it went into Sera instead of me because Sera put herself in the way.

She made a sound I will hear until I die. Not a scream. Worse. A sound like a door closing.

They took her. Not killed — *took*. The tall one caught her as she fell, and the other three closed around it, and the last thing the leader did before the fog swallowed them all was look back at me through the steam and incline its head, almost politely, the way you tip your hat to someone you expect to see again.

Then they were gone, and Sera was gone, and the river fell back into its bed steaming, and I was standing in the ruins of the ford with my arms bleeding from a dozen split lines and Wren's arms around me saying my name, my actual name, over and over, and I couldn't stop shaking.

Juniper reached us last. She looked at the torn ice, the scalded banks, the two folded shapes already dissolving into grey slush, and then at my arms, and then she did the thing that broke me worse than anything the hunters did.

She hugged me. Gently, careful of the cuts, careful the way you're careful with something you've just realized is precious and dangerous in the same breath.

"You let go," she said.

"I didn't have a choice."

"Tam." She pulled back and held my face in her hands, and her eyes were wet and fierce and absolutely certain. "There is always a choice. You chose her. That's the only part that matters."

Behind her, Wren stood looking north, toward where the fog had swallowed them, and her jaw was set in a way I'd learned to recognize. It was the set of a girl doing sums she hated.

"They took her toward the capital," Wren said. "They'll want her alive. She knows where we're going. She knows what I am." Her voice cracked, just once, and she steadied it by force. "If the Hollow King learns the heir is already loose—"

"Then we don't let him learn," Juniper said. She stood, and I watched fifteen years of grief turn into something with edges. "The solstice is nine days out. They'll take her to him, because he feeds best on the ones who matter. Which means—" she exhaled, and I saw how much the next word cost her, "—which means they're taking us straight to the door we needed opened anyway."

"You want to follow the monster home," Wren said.

"I want to follow it home and take our family back."

I looked down at my arms. The cuts had already stopped bleeding; the skin around them glowed faintly pink, warm as fresh bread. Scars-to-be, mapped across my forearms in the same branching pattern as the river ice. Power and cost arriving together, like always, like forever.

But here's the strange thing, the thing I couldn't have believed a month ago: for the first time in my life, the kettle in my chest was quiet. Not gone. Never gone. Just — settled. Like it had finally been introduced to itself.

"Nine days," I said. And my voice came out steady, and hot, and mine. "Then let's go get her."

Far off to the north, past the hills, past the frozen fields, something vast and cold laughed — or maybe it was just the wind. We started walking anyway.
