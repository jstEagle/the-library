## 13. The Price of Survival

Fever has a color. I learned that the night after the river crossing, sitting in a collapsed shepherd's hut with Sera Vane burning three inches off my lap, watching her skin shift through shades I had no charm for. It starts gold—fever-bright, the body fighting. Then it goes copper. Then it goes the color of old bruises, and that's when you stop being a healer's apprentice and start being a witness.

I was never a healer's apprentice. That's the first problem.

The second problem is that I *am* a healer, technically, if you count fifteen years of selling charms that cured headaches by suggestion and bunions by placebo. My gift runs to small things: wards that keep moths out of wool, warmth stitched into gloves, luck bent just enough to matter. Quiet magic. Safe magic. The kind you can hide under a counter for a decade while the world pretends you don't exist.

Sera needed none of that. Sera needed me to reach into her chest, find the wound the hunter's blade had left festering along her ribs, and burn the rot out with enough raw gift to light a chapel—and I hadn't touched magic like that since the day Veil went silent, because magic like that is how they *find* you.

"Juniper." Tam crouched beside me, holding a bowl of melted snow he'd boiled clean without meaning to, which was its own quiet tragedy. "Her breathing's gone funny again."

It had. Shallow, fast, a whistle at the top of each inhale like wind through a cracked shutter. Wren knelt on Sera's other side, pressing cool cloths to her forehead with the methodical calm of someone who'd nursed workhouse fevers before and knew exactly which ones didn't come back from.

"She needs a physicker," Wren said.

"The nearest physicker is in Marrowford," I said, "and Marrowford has a registration office."

"So we go around it."

"And when she collapses on the road? When a patrol finds us carrying an unconscious woman with inquisitor tattoos and a boy who boils rivers?" I rubbed my eyes. Everything felt gray at the edges. The Hollow King's winter had been eating the land for six days now, and it ate something else too—I could feel it, a thinning in the air, like the world's own gift was being siphoned through a straw. My little charms came slower. Cost more. The land itself was running a fever, and mine was contagious.

Wren looked at me the way only a fifteen-year-old can look at a grown adult who is failing at their job: without judgment, but with full documentation.

"You're scared of your own magic," she said. Not cruelly. Just stating the ledger line.

"I'm *cautious* about my own magic."

"You froze two men solid on the road yesterday and then threw up behind a tree."

"That's called *conscientious objection*."

Tam made a sound that was almost a laugh and quickly converted into a cough so as not to be disrespectful near the dying. He was learning manners from Sera, who had approximately none.

The truth, which I kept in the same locked cellar where I kept everything else: yes. I was afraid. On the road, when the hunters cornered us, something had come out of me that I did not recognize—something vast and cold and *old*, wearing my hands like borrowed gloves, and afterward I couldn't stop thinking about the fact that it had been easy. That some part of me had known exactly how to do it. Fifteen years ago I'd sworn myself to Veil, the silent god, the god of thresholds and held doors, and my gift had always felt like His: small, careful, a candle cupped against wind. What I'd used on those hunters wasn't a candle. It was the whole house fire, and it had answered to me like it had been waiting.

You don't trust a door that opens that easily. You nail it shut.

But Sera Vane was dying on a pile of moldy hay, and the nails were starting to look like cowardice.

* * *

Night came down like a lid. The winter sky over the mountains had no stars in it—not clouded, *absent*, as though someone had reached up and taken them, and I tried not to think about what kind of appetite does that.

Tam slept by the door with his hands wrapped in wet rags, the way he always did now, as if his gift were a dog that might bite him in his sleep. Wren dozed upright against the wall, one hand still resting on Sera's blanket, because Wren did not know how to fully stop guarding things. And Sera burned.

I sat with my fingers on her wrist and counted her pulse the way you count money you don't have enough of. Too fast. Always too fast. The wound along her ribs had gone the bruise-color hours ago, and now there were dark lines crawling outward from it like ink dropped in water, and every hour I told myself *one more hour and I'll do it*, and every hour passed.

Because here is the arithmetic nobody teaches you about sworn magic: when you give your bond to a god, your gift stops being entirely yours. It becomes a loan. And Veil had stopped honoring the loan fifteen years ago—no answer, no presence, nothing but silence so complete I'd built a life inside it. Using deep magic without Him underneath me meant reaching into a well with no bottom and hoping my rope was long enough. Last time I'd hoped that, I'd frozen two men and vomited.

If I reached into Sera and the rope ran out halfway, I wouldn't kill her. Worse. I'd leave her hollowed—a thing the hunters could take alive, or a thing that woke up wrong, and either way it would be my name on it.

"You're doing the face," said a voice like gravel soaked in brandy.

Sera's eyes were open. Barely. Fever-glazed, but open, and focused on me with the last operational fraction of an inquisitor's mind.

"What face?"

"The one where you're deciding whether to save me." She tried to smile; it came out crooked. "I've seen it before. On priests deciding whether a confession is worth the paperwork."

"Don't talk. You need your strength for—"

"Burning the rot out. Yes. I know how this works, Reyes. I've *done* this works." Her breath whistled. "You should know. If it goes wrong. If you reach in and there's nothing of me left when you come back out—" She swallowed. "I signed orders. Years ago. You know that. Whatever you find in me, part of it earned the fever."

"That's fever talking."

"It's the only honest talking I've done in twenty years, so let me finish." Her hand found my wrist—gripping harder than a dying woman should be able to grip. "You're afraid of what's in you. Good. Be afraid. But don't confuse fear with unworthiness. The order taught us that trick. Fear feels like humility. It isn't. It's just fear wearing humility's coat, and meanwhile people die while you admire how pious you look refusing to act."

I stared at her. "You're insufferable even at death's door."

"Door's not death's yet. I checked. I'm very thorough about doors." Her eyes drifted half-shut. "Doors are sort of your god's whole thing, aren't they? Maybe ask him."

"He doesn't answer."

"Maybe you've been knocking too quietly."

She went under again after that, into whatever country fevers go to, and I sat there in the dark with her words rattling around my skull like dice in a cup.

Knocking too quietly.

Fifteen years of prayers whispered into a pillow so the neighbors wouldn't hear. Fifteen years of asking politely. Fifteen years of treating my god's silence like a verdict instead of a question.

* * *

I went outside.

The cold hit like a debt collector. Snow came down fine and dry and wrong—the Hollow King's snow, which fell without wind and never seemed to land, hanging in the air past all reason. In the distance, the mountains stood black against a starless sky, and somewhere among them, according to a dead man's map and a fever dream, there was a shrine.

I hadn't told the others yet. About the word. About the night after the river crossing, when I'd sat in the mud with blood on my hands and prayed the way drowning people pray—no words, no formality, just *please*—and something had answered. One word, arriving not in my ears but in the marrow of me, in the place where Veil had lived before He went quiet:

*Come.*

I'd told myself it was shock. Hypoxia. Wishful thinking dressed up as revelation. I was very good at telling myself things; I'd run a successful business on it for years.

But Sera's pulse was hammering under my memory of it, and the dark lines were climbing toward her heart, and I had run out of respectable lies.

So I stood in the falling snow that didn't land, and I did the thing I hadn't done in fifteen years.

I prayed out loud.

"Veil."

My voice cracked on it. Embarrassing. The god of thresholds, addressed by a charm-seller standing in a ruined hut's doorway, and I couldn't even manage a whole sentence.

"Veil. I don't know if You're there. I don't know if You've been there any of the fifteen years I spent talking to a wall and calling it faith." The cold bit through my coat; I welcomed it, because pain is honest. "I swore myself to You when I was nineteen. You gave me candle-magic and threshold-wards and a silence I thought was discipline. Then You went quiet, and I decided You'd left, and I hated You for it—quietly, respectfully, the way you hate a parent who's still paying the rent. And now there's a thing wearing a king like a coat, and it's drinking the whole world's gift through a straw, and my friend is dying, and the only magic big enough to save her is the kind that scares me, and I am *asking*—"

My voice broke again. Fine. Let the record show.

"—I'm asking if You're under there. Under the silence. Because Sera says maybe I've been knocking too quietly, and she's a disgraced torturer with the moral compass of a ledger, and she's also the most honest person I've ever met, and I don't know what that says about my life."

The snow hung motionless around me. The mountains said nothing. The starless dark said nothing.

And then—

Not a voice. Never a voice. But a *pull*, faint as a thread drawn taut somewhere behind my sternum, in the exact place where my oath had knotted itself fifteen years ago. A direction. A weight. The sensation of a door, far away, standing very slightly ajar.

*Come.*

The same word. Not shock. Not hypoxia. *Him.* Or what was left of Him, or what He had become, or whatever a god turns into after fifteen years of holding a door shut from the inside.

I laughed. It came out wrong—half sob, half something unhinged, the laugh of a woman discovering her dead father was actually just upstairs the whole time. Fifteen years of grief, and He'd been *drowning*. Holding the seal. Going silent so the thing behind the crown would have nothing of Him to drink. Every prayer I'd sent into that silence had landed on a back already bent under an impossible weight.

Faith, it turns out, is complicated when you learn the object of it was never absent—only sinking. Grief curdles into guilt. Guilt ferments into something hotter.

"Okay," I said to the empty, starless dark. "Okay. I'm coming. But first—"

I turned and looked back through the doorway, at the fever-light guttering over Sera Vane's ruined body, at Tam's rag-wrapped hands, at Wren sleeping upright because rest was a luxury for girls who weren't heirs to a stolen throne.

"—first I have to save a life. And I'm going to use the scary magic to do it. And if that's a sin against Your silence, then bill me."

The pull didn't change. But I swear—it could have been imagination, and I have built a career on knowing the difference, and it was not imagination—the thread behind my sternum warmed. Like approval. Like a hand briefly resting on a shoulder.

* * *

Healing magic, done properly, is not dramatic. No lightning. No choir. You reach into the wound and you *listen*—you find where the body's own song has gone discordant, and you hum the note it's missing until the flesh remembers the tune. I learned the theory from a herbwoman in my first year out of the novitiate, back when I still thought Veil's silence was a test I could pass by studying.

Doing it without a god underneath you is another matter entirely. Doing it means being the whole chord yourself.

I knelt in the hay. Wren woke without being called—of course she did—and took position at Sera's head without asking questions, because that girl absorbed competence the way sand absorbs water. Tam lit candles with a snap of his fingers, carefully, the flame small and obedient for once, and stood ready with boiled water and the clean rags, because everyone in this ridiculous found family of mine had decided to be useful tonight except fate.

"Hold her shoulders," I said. "If I lose the thread, she'll convulse. Don't let her bite her tongue."

"And you?" Wren asked.

"If I lose the thread," I said, "drag me off her and sit on me until I stop glowing."

"Glowing?"

"We'll see. Fingers crossed it's not glowing."

I put both palms flat over the wound, over the bruise-dark spread of it, and closed my eyes, and knocked.

Not quietly.

I went down through my own layers first—the shopkeeper's caution, the fugitive's flinch, the fifteen years of small safe candle-magic, all of it set aside like coats removed at a door—and beneath all that, at the bottom of the well, the thing I'd found on the road. The vast cold old current that wore my hands. I had nailed it shut. Now I pulled the nails out one by one and let it rise.

It rose the way dawn rises: slowly, then all at once, and suddenly there was light everywhere.

I heard Wren inhale. So. Glowing after all. Noted for future negotiations.

Then I stopped being in the room at all. I was in the wound—inside the fever-country, the landscape of Sera Vane's body at war with itself—and it looked the way battlefields look from very high up: rivers of heat, black tendrils of infection advancing like armies, her own strength massed in white ridges and losing ground inch by inch. And threading through all of it, unmistakable, the signature of the blade that had cut her. Hunter-steel, quenched in something that drank gift. Of course. They didn't just kill mages. Their weapons *ate* them on the way in.

Fine. Two could play at drinking.

I gathered the current—all of it, no rationing, no candle-cupping—and poured it into the black lines like boiling water down an anthill. The infection fought. It fought *hard*, wrapping around my gift and pulling, trying to drink me the way the steel had drunk Sera, and for one vertiginous moment I understood exactly what the Hollow King's hunger felt like from the inside, and I screamed somewhere far above my own body—

—and then the thread behind my sternum *held*. The pull. The door ajar. Something at the bottom of my well that was not me, bracing me, the way a spotter holds a climber's rope. Not taking over. Not commanding. Just *there*, the way He had always been there, before, under every small charm I'd ever sold.

Veil was holding my rope.

We burned the infection together. It took—by Wren's later accounting—most of the night, and by the end I was wrung out like laundry, nosebleeding onto Sera's bandages, shaking so hard Tam had to help me sit up. But the black lines had receded. The bruise-color had drained back toward gold. And Sera Vane, inquisitor, traitor, insufferable honest woman, opened her eyes at grey dawn and said:

"Told you. Knock louder."

Water of life, that woman. I'd have throttled her if I had the arm strength.

* * *

They let me sleep until noon. When I woke, the three of them were sitting in a row watching me like a tribunal, which is never a good sign.

"You said 'come,'" Tam said.

"I said lots of things. I was delirious."

"You said 'come' to somebody," Wren said, "and then you glowed, and then you said 'okay, I'm coming' right before the fever broke." She folded her arms with the terrible finality of a girl raised by accountants. "Coming to *where*, Juniper?"

And there it was. The question I'd been dreading, because the answer lived high in those black mountains, at the end of a thread of divine pull, in a shrine that appeared on no map I trusted—and because every mile toward it was a mile away from Greyhollow, away from the capital, away from the deadline ticking under midwinter solstice.

But also: the seal was failing. The god was drowning. The word hadn't been comfort. It had been a summons.

I sat up, wiped the dried blood from my lip, and told them everything—the word in the marrow, the thread behind the sternum, the rope held steady while I fought. I watched their faces do the arithmetic alongside mine: Sera grim and quick, Tam wide-eyed, Wren absolutely still in the way she got when something mattered.

"A mountain shrine," Sera said slowly. "On a direct line between here and the capital. Where a god sealed into a crown might keep... an anchor."

"You think it's a trap?" Tam asked.

"I think everything involving gods is a trap," Sera said. "I think we go anyway."

Wren looked at me last, longest. "Your god's been calling you for fifteen years," she said, "and he picked *now* to speak?"

"No," I said, and found, to my surprise, that I was smiling—terrified, exhausted, certain. "He picked *then*. Fifteen years ago. He just finally got loud enough for me to hear."

Outside, the false snow hung unmoving in the air, and the mountains waited, and the thread behind my sternum pulled gently, patiently, like a hand extended across a very long dark.

"Pack up," I said. "We're going to meet my god."
