## 14. The God Who Went Quiet

The word *come* did not leave me alone.

It sat behind my ribs like a swallowed coal. I'd spent fifteen years praying to a silence so complete I'd built my whole grief around it—grief is comfortable that way, once you furnish it—and now the silence had spoken one word and every piece of furniture in my head had been knocked over.

*Come.*

"Say it again," Tam said. He was walking backwards in front of me on the mountain path, which was exactly as safe as it sounded. "The god-word. Say it slow."

"It wasn't a word-word," I said. "It was more of a... a pull with grammar."

"So say the pull with grammar."

"Tam."

"I'm just saying, if a god talks to you, you don't get to keep it." He turned forward again just in time to not walk off the switchback, which I chose to attribute to divine intervention rather than luck. "Wren talks to me like that too. Like she's deciding whether to tell me something. It's very annoying."

Ahead of us, Sera leaned on Wren's shoulder and said nothing, because Sera had been saying nothing for six days, ever since the fever finally broke and left her looking like something a tide had given up on. The wound across her ribs was closed. What the hunters' blades had put into her—some tincture, some blessing gone wrong—had taken longer to leave. She walked like a woman counting her own steps to make sure they still belonged to her.

Wren bore her weight without complaint. She'd been doing a lot of that lately. Heir to Veylmark, smuggled into a workhouse at an age too young to remember it, and her first act upon learning of her royalty had been to ask if we could stop for Tam to sleep. I'd have followed worse monarchs off a cliff.

The mountain was called Hushfell, which even for a god of silence felt a little on the nose. Veil's old shrines dotted its lower slopes like fallen teeth—we'd passed three already, roofless, their altars furred with snow. But the pull didn't go to any of them. It went up. Past the treeline. Past the snowline. Into air thin enough that each breath felt borrowed.

"You're sure this is where it wants you?" Sera asked. Her voice came out sanded flat.

"It's been wanting me here since the river," I said. "I'm mostly just the legs."

"And if it's a trap?"

I considered lying to her, then remembered we'd done our honesty work in the chapel, all of it ugly, all of it necessary, and lies between us were supposed to be over. "Then it's a trap set by the only being who never once lied to me in fifteen years of silence," I said. "Which would almost be impressive."

Sera nodded slowly, like I'd confirmed something in a file she hadn't shown me yet. She kept doing that. Keeping things. I let it go. A woman who'd carried the inquisition's secrets in her bones for twenty years didn't shed them in a week.

* * *

We camped that night in the shell of the fourth shrine, the largest, its dome cracked open to the stars like an egg some enormous patient bird had abandoned. Tam built a fire from shrine-wood, which felt sacrilegious until I remembered that Veil had never cared about wood. Veil cared about quiet. And there was nothing quieter than four people who had all run out of things they could safely say.

Except Tam. Tam was never quiet.

"Do you think it'll look like something?" he asked, poking the fire. "When it talks. The god."

"I don't know."

"Sera says gods are just old magic that learned manners."

"Sera," I said, "says a lot of things."

"She does." Tam grinned at her across the fire. Sera, to my genuine astonishment, almost smiled. Almost. On Sera's face that was a festival.

Wren was asleep against a pillar, her breath fogging, her hands curled around the little tin charm I'd made her back when she was just a girl from the workhouse with cold feet—a warmth-knot, craft and no gift, worth maybe two coppers. She wouldn't trade it for a crown now. I found that I minded less about the crown than I'd expected.

I took first watch, because I couldn't have slept anyway. The pull was stronger at night, a thread drawn through my sternum, tightening toward the peak. Fifteen years ago, standing in a chapel much like this one, I had sworn myself to Veil with my blood on the altar-stone and my whole foolish heart wide open, and the god had taken it—taken all of us, my circle, nine sworn mages bound in service—and then, within the season, gone silent. One by one we'd lost the sense of it. One by one we'd scattered, or died, or in my case opened a charm shop on Lantern Row and learned to sell luck to people who didn't know what real luck cost.

I'd told myself a hundred stories. Veil had died. Veil had abandoned us. Veil had never existed and the swearing was theater and the magic was mine alone, misattributed.

None of those stories fit a god that said *come.*

* * *

Near dawn, Sera woke and sat beside me without asking. That was another thing the chapel had bought us: a shared tolerance for sitting in the dark near each other.

"The order has files on Hushfell," she said quietly, so as not to wake the others. "Old ones. From before my time. They call it the Anchor Mountain."

My stomach dropped through the frozen ground. "And you didn't think to mention this before we climbed it?"

"I thought to mention it now." She pulled her cloak tighter. "Juniper. When I gave you the documents at the night market, I read everything in that satchel twice. Most of it was purge orders, registry lists, requisitions. But there was one file I couldn't open. Sealed with the king's own signet, and beneath that—" she hesitated, and I watched a woman who feared nothing human hesitate— "beneath that, a mark I've only seen once before. On your chapel wall. The old sigil of the Veil-sworn."

The fire crackled. Somewhere above us, wind moved through stone with a sound almost like breathing.

"You think the king's office and my god are—" I couldn't finish it. The sentence felt like blasphemy in a language I no longer spoke.

"I think," Sera said, "that whatever answers you up there, you should ask it why the crown sealed a file with its symbol. And I think you should ask it before you swear anything else."

Always the inquisitor. Even disgraced, branded, half-dead, she interrogated. I wanted to be angry. Mostly I was grateful, which is a terrible thing to want to be around Sera Vane, because she never let gratitude soften a question.

"I'll ask," I said.

"Good." She looked up at the peak, white against the paling sky. "Because I've read enough confessions to know how they start. Nobody climbs a mountain to hear good news."

* * *

I went up alone. We argued about it—Tam argued loudest—but the pull was specific, and it was for me, and dragging three frostbitten companions up a death-slope to eavesdrop on my god struck even me as rude. Wren settled it by pointing out, with the terrifying practicality she must have inherited from whichever ancestor sat a throne, that someone had to stay with Sera anyway.

So: alone, past the last shrine, past the point where the snow lay blue and unbroken, up a spine of black rock that the wind had been carving at for ten thousand years. My lungs burned. My gift flickered under my skin like a lamp running low—the Hollow King's winter had been draining the land for weeks, and mages felt it first, the way sailors feel a storm before the coast does. Every spell I cast now cost double. I had stopped casting anything that wasn't keeping us alive.

The summit was a bowl of stone, and in the bowl stood a door.

That is the only honest word for it. Not a building, not a monument. A door—tall as three men, narrow, carved from a single slab of rock the color of a starless night, standing free on the mountaintop with nothing behind it. No frame. No wall. Just a door, upright in the wind, older than the shrines below it, older probably than the kingdom whose crown it had something to do with. Its surface was covered in knots—carved interlacings, rope-work patterns, loops threaded through loops, the whole history of binding rendered in stone.

And every knot was coming loose.

Not broken. Unraveling. Slowly, patiently, the way a sweater dies—one pulled thread at a time. I could see gaps in the carving where whole patterns had slipped free, and the gaps were not empty. They were *dark*, dark in a way that had nothing to do with light, and standing before them I understood two things at once, the way you understand falling:

This was a seal.

And seals do not unravel themselves.

"*Come,*" I had been told, and so I had come, and now I pressed my palm against the stone, which was warm—impossibly, obscenely warm, in all that cold—and I spoke aloud, to a mountain, like a madwoman.

"I'm here. You called me. So talk."

The answer didn't come as a voice. It came the way it always had, back when I was young and newly sworn: not sound but *knowing*, poured directly into the quiet part of me, the part I'd hollowed out and furnished with grief.

*I am holding the door.*

The knowing was faint. Strained. Like a voice shouted from underwater, or from under rubble. I gripped the stone harder.

"Veil." My voice cracked on the name, fifteen years of it. "Veil, where have you *been?*"

*Here.* And the knowing shuddered. *Always here. Since before your kingdom had a name. Your order swore itself to the door, Juniper. That was always the work. The shrines, the silence-craft, the vows—they were scaffolding. Reinforcement. Nine sworn hearts make a strong knot. Ninety make stronger. Generations of you, woven in.*

"My circle," I whispered. "The others. They didn't lose their gifts. They—"

*Were pulled into the weave. As you would have been.* A pause, and in the pause something that in a human voice would have been shame. *I could not warn you. To speak is to loosen grip. I have held this door for four thousand years with both hands, child, and I could spare neither of them for goodbye.*

The wind screamed past the summit and I barely heard it. I was seeing my own history torn up and re-laid: the swearing ceremony, the elders' solemn faces, the hymns about vigilance and watching—all of it a recruitment drive for eternal sentry duty. And the silence after—

"The king," I said. "Fifteen years ago the king fell ill. The same season you went quiet. Tell me that's coincidence. Lie to me if you have to."

The knowing that came then was so heavy I sank to my knees in the snow.

*The seal was knotted into the royal line long ago. It was practical. Thrones endure; thrones are guarded; a seal hidden in a crown is a seal no enemy thinks to attack. For centuries the kings of Veylmark wore me unknowing, and the door held.* The darkness in the unraveled carvings seemed to lean closer. *Then came a man who was not content to wear a crown. He wanted to open it.*

"The Hollow King."

*A thing from before the binding. What the door was made to shut away. It cannot pass through while the knots hold—but it can reach. It reached into the dream of a sleeping king and showed him wonders, and the king, poor fool, said yes. It has been wearing him ever since. Feeding. And every gift-bearer it takes, every mage burned in its purges, every drop of the land's magic it drinks—it pours into prying at my knots.*

Fifteen years. Fifteen years I'd grieved a god who had, at that exact moment, doubled its grip and stopped speaking to hold on. While I sold charms and hated the silence, Veil was drowning. While my circle scattered and died believing ourselves abandoned, our god was underwater, arms locked around a door, unable to call out.

"You should have let go," I heard myself say, and my voice was ugly. "You should have let the door break and told us. Told *someone.* We'd have fought. We'd have—"

*With what?* The knowing was gentle, which was somehow worse. *You have seen what it does with a fraction of itself loose. Now imagine all of it. Imagine the winter never ending, child. Imagine the dark behind this stone given a kingdom of bodies to wear, one after another, forever. I am one god. I chose the only arithmetic available to me.*

I knelt there and shook, and the shaking wasn't rage anymore, or not only. It was awe, the old kind, the kind the hymns used to describe and I'd never believed: the vertigo of touching something vast that knows your name.

"How long?" I asked finally. "How long can you hold it?"

Silence. Real silence, the kind I'd prayed into for fifteen years, and I nearly laughed at the cruelty of it.

Then: *Until midwinter. Perhaps. The solstice is when the king's rituals crest—when it feeds deepest. If the knots survive the solstice, I may re-tighten them. If they fail—*

The dark in the carvings pulsed, once, like a heartbeat behind a wall.

"—then it comes through," I finished. "Midwinter solstice. Weeks away."

*You asked me once,* Veil said, softer now, the knowing fraying at the edges like the knots, *in your novitiate year, why a god of silence needs servants. Do you remember what I answered?*

I did. I'd thought about it for fifteen years. *"A door holds better with hands on both sides."*

*Yes.* And beneath the exhaustion, beneath four thousand years of solitary grip, I felt something that undid me completely: hope. Small, and ashamed of itself, and alive. *I cannot leave the door, Juniper. I will never leave the door. But hands on the other side—that I may yet find.*

I stood. My legs had gone numb hours ago. Below me the world lay white and dying, the Hollow King's winter pressing down on fields and rivers and cities full of registered, hunted, burning people, and somewhere east of here a false king sat feeding, and in my pack Sera carried a sealed file with a god's sigil under a crown's signet, and she'd known, the way she always knew, to wait until I had the truth before handing me the rest of it.

"Weeks," I said to the door. "All right. Then you'd better teach me everything the Veil-sworn ever knew about knots."

The knowing that answered wasn't words at all. It was a pattern—vast, intricate, terrible—unfolding behind my eyes like a map of a country made entirely of rope, and I understood that I had just enrolled, at forty-one years old, in the oldest school in the world, and that graduation was scheduled for the solstice, and that the final exam was a god.

I started down the mountain at a run.

Sera met me at the tree line with the file already in her hands, and the look on her face said she'd been waiting all morning.

"There's something you need to read," she said.
