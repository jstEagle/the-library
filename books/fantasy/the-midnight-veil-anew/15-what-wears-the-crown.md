## 15. What Wears the Crown

I have been interrogated by better people than this fever.

That is not a boast; it is a complaint. The fever asks its questions over and over — *what did you sign, what did you burn, whose name was on the order* — and it never accepts my answers, because the answers are all the same: *all of them, everything, everyone*. Fever is a sloppy inquisitor. It has no technique. No patience. It simply turns you over and over like a page it cannot read.

But I am told I said things during the worst of it. Juniper says I recited the Inquisitorial Credo in my sleep, word for word, with the cadence of a woman who once loved it. Tam says I apologized to someone named Aldous eleven times. Wren says I wept, which I have officially denied, and will continue to deny, and if any of them bring it up again I will remind them that I once broke a man's arm for less.

So: convalescent. That is the word Juniper uses, gently, when she catches me trying to stand. Convalescent. As though I were a convalescing person rather than a half-sewn sack of meat propped against a chapel wall that isn't even a proper wall anymore, just three standing stones and a memory of a roof, somewhere in the hills south of Greyhollow where Veil used to be prayed to before praying got complicated.

The wound runs from my left shoulder down across my ribs. A hunter's blade, angled to open rather than kill — they wanted me alive, or they wanted Tam to watch me die slowly, and either way it worked out badly for their schedule. Juniper stitched it with thread she boiled in rainwater and a prayer she didn't finish, because her god is quiet and her hands are not. She did good work. I would know. I've seen a lot of bad work.

"Stop flexing," she says now, without looking up from the pot. She always knows. I'm beginning to suspect she can feel it through the floor, the way I twitch my shoulder to test whether the skin still belongs to me.

"I'm assessing."

"You're preening. There's a difference. Assessing is when you find out you're dying. Preening is when you find out you're not and pretend you meant to check anyway."

I lie back against the bedroll and glare at the hole in the roof, through which a grey sky is doing nothing in particular. Three days since the river. Three days since Tam boiled a crossing into steam and screamed the whole time, since I put myself between him and a hunter's second stroke and learned exactly how much a body can hold together and keep arguing.

Three days of fever-questions. And underneath them, all along, like a stone at the bottom of a well, one question I haven't asked aloud because I already know the answer, and knowing it changes nothing except everything.

What did I carry out of the Order's archive?

* * *

It was the last night. I want that understood — the last night, after the branding, after Aldous cut the sigil into my collarbone with a heated iron and told me I could still be saved if I came home, after I walked out of the Chapterhouse with my ears ringing and my whole life on fire behind me. I went back in. Through the servants' gate, past the novice dormitories where I had slept for twenty years, down into the deep archive where the sealed files live.

Why? Because I am, apparently, incapable of leaving well enough alone. Because I had spent my career believing that the Order kept secrets the way a body keeps organs — necessary, hidden, load-bearing — and that night, branded and bleeding, I finally wanted to see the organ itself.

The file was in a black case with no label. Black cases in the deep archive mean one thing: *the king's business*. Inquisitors don't open black cases. Inquisitors deliver them, bowing.

I opened it.

I should say something dramatic here — that my hands shook, that the candle guttered, that some animal instinct in me knew to be afraid. My hands didn't shake. I remember being annoyed by how steady they were. Twenty years of training does that; it takes the trembling out of you and leaves only the reading.

The file was called, in the old formal script, **THE HOLLOWING: MAINTENANCE SCHEDULE AND HARVEST LEDGERS**.

*Maintenance.* Like a roof. Like a bridge. And harvest ledgers — I knew that term, of course. Every inquisitor knows it. When a gift-bearer is taken in, their gift is measured, recorded, and *harvested* — drawn out through ritual apparatus into storage-vessels called reliquaries, officially for "safe containment of dangerous energies." We were taught the harvested gifts were destroyed. Purified. Rendered inert.

The ledgers in that file said otherwise.

They said otherwise in columns. Columns of names — I recognized some, gods help me, names from my own casework — and beside each name a quantity, and beside each quantity a destination. Not destruction. Delivery. To the capital. To the *Palace Rites*, under the seal of the Royal Physician, countersigned by the Lord Inquisitor himself. My teacher. The man who taught me that mercy without rigor is just cowardice wearing a kind face.

And then the maintenance schedule, which was the part that made my hands shake at last, four hours later, sitting in a ditch outside the city walls with the file inside my coat and no plan whatsoever.

Because the maintenance schedule described the king's illness. Described it as a *feeding cycle*. Described rituals — the same rituals I had seen performed twice a year in the capital, grand ceremonies of intercession, the whole court kneeling while physicians chanted over the sick king's bed — as *intakes*. Scheduled intakes. Quantities matched to the harvest ledgers. The king's mysterious wasting illness, the grief of the nation, the reason for every hard decree and emergency measure of the last fifteen years—

Was an appetite.

* * *

"So let me say it plainly," I tell them, on the evening I finally sit up without the room tilting. "Because I've been circling it for three days and Juniper tells me I circle when I'm ashamed, and I'm done being ashamed in front of children."

"I'm fifteen," Wren says.

"You're a child with excellent posture. Point stands." I shift against the wall; the wound pulls, I ignore it. "The purge isn't policy. It isn't fear. It isn't even cruelty, really — cruelty would at least be human. The purge is a supply chain. Gift-bearers aren't being killed for having magic. They're being killed so their magic can be taken somewhere first. We've been harvesting the kingdom's mages and shipping them to the palace to feed a thing that wears the king."

Tam has stopped pretending to whittle. Wren has gone very still, the way she does — stillness as armor, stillness as a workhouse skill, the talent of becoming furniture when important people start saying terrible things nearby. Juniper hasn't moved at all. She's feeding the fire one stick at a time, and each stick goes in with a precision that tells me she already knew, or guessed, and has been waiting for me to say it so she wouldn't have to.

"The king," Juniper says quietly. "The actual man."

"That's the part I can't fully answer." I hate saying it. I have built my whole self around answers. "The file treats the king and the thing using him almost interchangeably, but there are marginalia — physician's notes, decades old — that suggest a distinction. References to 'the host' and 'the resident.' References to the king's lucid intervals." I pause. "References to restraints."

Wren looks up. "He's tied down?"

"During the rites. Yes." I meet her eyes. She is the heir; she has a right to whatever this knowledge costs. "Whatever sits on Veylmark's throne, there may still be a man underneath it. Old. Drained. Possibly mad. Possibly begging."

"Or possibly dead years ago and nobody noticed," Tam says, too loudly, the way he says things when he wants the terrible version confirmed so he can stop dreading it. "Maybe it ate him all the way through."

"Maybe." I don't soften it. Softening is how the Order trained us to lie — wrap the blade in wool. "But if there's a man left, then the unbinding Juniper keeps talking about has two problems instead of one. Pull the thing off the throne, and you'd better hope there's someone left to catch."

Juniper sets down the fire-stick. "There will be."

"You sound certain."

"I'm certain about Veil. The rest we'll manage." She says it the way she says most true things — flatly, daring the world to argue. Then, to me: "The solstice. You said midwinter solstice. Say the rest."

Right. The rest. The arithmetic.

"The maintenance schedule runs on a cycle," I say. "Intake quantities increase each year — the ledgers show escalation going back fifteen years, matching the escalation of the purges. More harvests, bigger harvests, closer together. Which means the thing needs more to stay sealed in, or more to stay anchored, or—" I stop. Choose the honest sentence. "Or it's growing, and the seal around it is thinning, and the feeding is the patch job. Either way, the trend line ends at the same place. Midwinter solstice. The file calls it 'the Long Intake.' The largest rite in the calendar, scheduled for the longest night. Every reliquary in the kingdom gets delivered to the capital by then. Every drop of harvested gift in Veylmark, poured into one ceremony."

"And if it happens?" Wren asks. Her voice doesn't shake. Workhouses teach that too.

"Then whatever door the Hollow King is leaking through comes off its hinges entirely. The winter we've been walking through — the black crops, the frozen rivers, the shadows on the roads — that's leakage. That's a draft under a door. At the solstice, the door opens."

Silence, except the fire.

I look around at them and I feel something I can only describe as cold clarity, because warm clarity doesn't exist and anyone who claims otherwise is selling something. Here is what I see: a charm-seller with a silent god. A boy who scars himself every time he saves us. A girl who didn't ask to be royal and has said so, repeatedly, with feeling. And me — a traitor with a brand, a knife-wound, and a stolen file, who spent twenty years serving the machine that wrote that file and never once looked inside a black case until it was too late for everyone whose name was in it.

And here is what the clarity says: the enemy is not the hunters. The enemy is not the purge. The enemy is the throne itself, and it has been eating us for fifteen years, politely, ritually, with our own inquisitors carrying the plates.

"The solstice is nineteen days away," Juniper says.

"Nineteen," I confirm. "The capital is twelve days' walk for healthy people. We are not healthy people. So."

"So we leave tomorrow."

"So we leave tomorrow," I agree, and then, because the fever burned something loose in me and apparently it hasn't grown back yet, I add the last thing. The thing from the bottom of the file. "One more item. The final page wasn't a ledger. It was a contingency order. If the Long Intake fails — if the solstice rite is disrupted — the Order is instructed to stop harvesting and start burning. Every registered gift-bearer in the kingdom. Immediate. Total. They call it the Grey Mercy."

Tam sets down his knife. For a moment nobody speaks, and then Wren says, in a small, level voice that frightens me far more than shouting would:

"Then we can't fail."

No. We can't.

Outside, snow begins to fall — early, wrong-seasoned, tasting faintly of ash — and somewhere north, beneath a crown that no longer fits any head, something ancient rolls over in its sleep and waits for its longest night.
