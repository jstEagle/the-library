## 16. The City of Frozen Bells

I had imagined the capital a thousand times before I ever saw it. Mostly in the dark, on my back, staring at the workhouse ceiling while Tam snored two bunks over. I'd built it out of stories the older orphans told—the ones who'd come down from Veylmark's heart with accents like silver and memories of fountains you could drink from. In my head it was a city of gold roofs and singing bells, where nobody decided your whole life before breakfast, because everyone there was too busy being important.

The city I walked into on the third day of the thaw-that-wasn't had the gold roofs.

Everything else was wrong.

* * *

We came in through the corpse road, which is not as dramatic as it sounds—it's just what the carters called the old supply tunnel under the east wall, back when the capital still needed supplying. Sera knew about it because, as she put it with the particular flatness she saved for her worst confessions, "the Inquisition mapped every way into this city, in case we ever needed to get in without being asked." Juniper didn't say anything to that. She'd gotten very good at the specific silence that meant *I forgive you and I will never stop flinching*, which is a kind of forgiveness I understood better than I let on.

Tam went first with a lantern hooded down to a coin-sized glow. He'd stopped flinching from the flame somewhere on the road—I think after the river, after he boiled half a mile of it and came out the other side looking like something a god had sketched and then abandoned—so now fire sat in his hands the way a cat sits with someone who's earned it: warily, but staying.

Then me. Then Sera, moving like a woman carrying her own scaffold on her back. Then Juniper at the rear, and I noticed she kept her hand pressed flat against the tunnel wall the whole way, palm open, fingers spread.

"What are you doing?" I whispered.

"Listening," she said. "The walls used to hum here. Veil's seal runs under the capital like a root under a tree. You could feel it if you stood still long enough." Her hand dragged along the stone. "It's quiet."

"Quiet's good, isn't it?"

Juniper was quiet herself for three steps, which from her is practically an essay. "A heartbeat's quiet too," she said finally, "until it stops."

I decided not to think about that. I've always been gifted at deciding not to think about things. It's how I survived fifteen years in a workhouse without once wondering why no one ever came for me, why the matron looked at me sometimes like she was checking a lock, why my hair stayed black as spilled ink when every other orphan's went brown in the sun. Deciding not to think is its own kind of magic. It doesn't even get you hunted.

The tunnel ended at a grate, and beyond the grate was the city.

* * *

The first thing I saw was the bells.

Every story I'd ever heard agreed on one thing: Corvane never stops ringing. Dawn bells, market bells, warning bells, wedding bells, midnight bells just to prove the city was still awake and pleased about it. Tam's eyes went up and up and up when we climbed out of the grate into the shadow of the cathedral quarter, and his mouth made the small round shape it makes instead of words.

Because the bells were frozen.

Not silent. *Frozen.* The great bell of the cathedral hung mid-swing, tilted maybe ten degrees past vertical, and it did not fall. Neither did its clapper, caught a hand's width from the bronze, nor the smaller bells around it in their openwork tower, all of them arrested at different points in their arc like insects in amber. Frost furred them white. When the light hit, they threw rainbows across the square, beautiful and obscene, a chandelier made of a scream interrupted.

"They're not held up," Juniper said softly. She'd gone pale under her hood. "They're *paused*. There's a difference. Held means something's pushing against them. Paused means—" She swallowed. "Means time forgot them."

Sera said nothing. Sera had said almost nothing since the river, since Tam's gift had come out of him like a scalding flood and she'd thrown herself between it and the hunters' crossbows and taken two bolts through the shoulder and side. The wounds were closed—Juniper's doing, done badly and desperately at a roadside shrine—but Sera moved like each step was a negotiation. Still, when Tam drifted toward her, the way he'd started doing without noticing, she shifted so her good arm was between him and the street. Instinct now. Nobody commented on it. Commenting would have broken it.

"The bells rang when it happened," Sera said at last, quietly. "There's a report. The night the king 'fell ill,' fifteen years ago—the dawn bells began and never finished. The first stroke sounded. The second never came." She looked up at the cathedral tower. "I read that file a hundred times and thought it was a clerk's error."

"It wasn't an error," Juniper said.

"No," Sera said. "It was a symptom."

* * *

The people were worse than the bells.

I want to be honest about this, because everyone in the songs is honest about the battles and dishonest about everything else: the frozen bells frightened me, but the people broke something in me that I don't think grows back.

They moved. That was the horror of it—they weren't statues, they weren't dead, they *moved*. A baker came out of his shop at some internal signal and set loaves in his window and went back inside, and every motion was right except the speed. He moved like honey pours. Like something underwater. A woman crossed the square with a basket on her hip and stopped halfway to look at the frozen bell, and she looked at it for a long time—not seconds, minutes—and her face never changed. No wonder. No fear. Just the shape of looking, worn like a coat.

Children played in a gutter. Slowly. A ball rolled between them with the languid grace of a dream, and one boy reached for it and missed and reached again, and none of them laughed, and none of them cried, and I stood in a doorway with my fists in my pockets and hated the Hollow King with a completeness I had never managed toward anyone, including the matron who sold me out for less than these people's bread cost.

"They're not asleep," Juniper murmured beside me. She'd been watching too. "Sleepers dream. These are—" She searched for the word. "Dimmed. Like lamps turned down to save oil."

"To save *what*?" Tam asked.

But we all knew. We'd known since the shrine in the mountains, since Juniper came down from communion with her god looking like a woman who'd found her father drowning and alive. The Hollow King fed on the gift—the magic in the land, in the blood, in the people—and the winter was the feeding, and the purge was the harvest, and every gift-bearer dragged to the capital in chains had come here to be poured into that bottomless cold.

And the rest? The sleepwalkers, the dimmed?

"Collateral," Sera said. "Or储备. Or both." She coughed, wetly, and pretended she hadn't. Tam heard it anyway. He'd started hearing everything about her anyway.

* * *

We moved through the city the way you move through a temple—quickly, quietly, touching nothing.

That was Sera's plan, such as it was: the palace district lay at the city's heart, ringed by walls and by the wraith-guard, those tall cold shapes the refugees on the road had described as *the king's shadows*. We couldn't fight our way in; we could barely walk. But the Hollow King's own winter had emptied the streets of living patrols, and Sera knew the palace's service ways the way she knew her own scars, and so we went—four ghosts in a city of ghosts, following a disgraced inquisitor's memory through alleys where frost grew in patterns too regular to be weather.

I should say something about what it did to me, walking those streets. Because everyone else got to grieve a home they'd lost, and I had to grieve one I'd never had.

This was supposed to be mine. That's the thing I couldn't stop chewing on. Every fountain we passed, every gold-roofed spire, every cobbled square where slow children played slow games—my father had walked them. My father had ruled them. Somewhere in a workhouse ledger in Greyhollow there was a line with my name on it and a lie beside it, and above that lie, somewhere in a palace archive, there was presumably another line with my name on it and a crown beside it, and the distance between those two lines was fifteen years and everything.

I didn't cry. I want that recorded. Wren of the Greyhollow workhouse does not cry in occupied streets.

But when we passed a fountain—a real one, the kind from the stories, carved with fish that spat water frozen in mid-arc like silver rope—I stopped. And I put my hand in the water, and it was ice, and underneath the ice the water was still water, still moving, barely, the way the people were still people, barely.

"My mother carried me through this square," I said. I hadn't planned to say it. It just came out, low, to no one. "She must have. If the documents are true. Right here. With soldiers hunting her, she came through here with me wrapped in—something—and she gave me away to save my life, and she never got to see whether it worked."

Nobody spoke. Tam looked at his boots. Sera, who had signed papers that ended circles like my mother's, stared at the frozen fish with an expression I'd have paid money to unlearn.

Juniper took my hand off the ice and held it, her palm warm, callused from charm-work. "You're seeing it," she said.

"I'm seeing a graveyard."

"You're seeing your city. Underneath." She nodded at the fountain, at the water still moving under the ice. At the sleepwalking baker whose bread, when I really looked, had risen properly. At the children, slow but playing, still playing, still choosing the game. "It's all still in there. Dimmed, not dead. Everything he takes, he has to keep taking, because it keeps fighting back." Her grip tightened. "Fifteen years and it hasn't won. That's not a graveyard, Wren. That's a siege. And sieges end."

I looked down the long avenue toward the palace, where the spires rose black against a sky the color of dishwater, where the thing wearing my father's body sat drinking my city one soul-light at a time.

"That's the most comforting thing anyone's ever said to me," I told her, "and it was horrifying."

"I'm a charm-seller," Juniper said. "Honest goods only."

* * *

We sheltered that night—or what passed for night, under a sun that rose late and gray and set early like it was ashamed—in the cellar of a chandler's shop four streets from the palace wall. Sera chose it. "Safe houses," she said. "The Inquisition maintained dozens. This one's decommissioned, which means it's clean, which means it's forgotten."

Inside were crates of candles, a cot with one blanket, and a wall map of the palace district annotated in faded inquisitor's ink. Sera stood in front of that map for a long time, her bandaged shoulder sagging, reading her old life's handwriting.

"There," she said finally, tapping a mark behind the royal chapel. "Postern stair. It comes up inside the private wing. Three landings, one door, and the door is warded—" she glanced at Juniper, "—but not by anything current. The wards on that door are fifteen years old. They were laid by court mages before the purge."

"And?" Tam asked.

"And fifteen years ago," Juniper said slowly, "the court mages still served the king and not the thing wearing him. Their wards would know a Hollow-touched face. They might not know ours." She looked around at the four of us—herself charm-scarred and mortal-bound, Sera half-broken, Tam smoldering, me nothing at all, no gift, no claim I'd asked for, just a name on a wrong ledger. "We can pass."

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow we went up the postern stair, through the door, into the dark at the center of the siege, where my father begged inside his own skin and something ancient wore him like a glove.

I didn't sleep. I sat against the candle crates in the dark with Tam breathing slow across the room and Sera muttering fragments of penance in her fever-dreams, and I thought about my mother running through that square with me in her arms, and I thought about the water under the ice, still moving, still fighting.

They tell you important people decide everything. I'd believed it my whole life—that was my excuse for years, my comfortable little grave: *what could a workhouse girl do against a throne?*

But sitting in that cellar, I finally understood what Juniper had been showing me since the frozen fields. The Hollow King had taken a kingdom of millions and dimmed them all, and it *still* hadn't won, because under the ice the water kept moving. One girl had run through that square with a baby and beat an army. One god had drowned itself in silence for fifteen years and held a door shut with sheer stubbornness. Small things, refusing. That was the whole war.

Tomorrow I would walk into the palace and meet the thing that ate my family.

I found I wasn't afraid anymore. I was *furious*, and fury keeps much better than fear.

Across the room, Sera woke with a gasp, and Tam was there before I could move, his hand hovering over hers, not quite touching, waiting to be allowed. And in the doorway behind them, framed against the gray false dawn, Juniper stood utterly still, her head tilted, listening—to the walls, to the root of her god beneath the city.

"Wren," she said quietly. "It's humming again."

I stood up. Under the floor, under the frost, faint as a pulse through a fever—something vast, and drowning, and *awake*, had felt us coming.

"The seal knows we're here," Juniper said. "Veil knows we're here." Her voice cracked on the next words, and she let it. "He says *hurry*."
