## 17. The King in the Mirror

The palace of Veylmark had been described to me a hundred times by clients who'd seen it—gilded halls, singing fountains, windows of colored glass that turned the afternoon into church. Those clients were all dead now, or registered, or run. And the palace itself had stopped being any of those things.

We climbed through the servants' warrens with Wren leading, because it turned out the workhouse taught you to read buildings the way Sera read warrants and Tam read weather. She moved through the behind-the-walls corridors like she'd been born in them—which, in a way she hadn't but nearly had been, felt like a joke the world was telling at my expense. The walls were cold enough to bite. Frost grew inward here, thickening toward the heart of the palace instead of thinning at its edges, as if winter had decided the building was a wound and were packing it.

Tam's breath came out in little ghosts. He kept flexing his scarred fingers and I kept pretending not to notice. His gift was a banked fire under his skin now; I could feel it the way you feel a hearth through a floorboard. In this place, where every scrap of magic in the air had been drunk down to the dregs, he was the loudest thing alive.

"Left at the cistern," Wren whispered. "Then the long gallery. The king's chamber is above the throne room—my father liked to be *over* things."

"You remember him," Sera said. Not a question. Her voice had gone strange since the frozen bells, since she'd watched her own order's handiwork hanging in the air over a city of sleepwalkers.

"I remember a man who smelled of ink and let me sit on his boot once." Wren's jaw tightened. "I remember being four. I don't trust anything older than that."

The gallery was worse than the warrens. Portraits lined it—generations of Veylmark's kings and queens—and someone, or something, had gotten to them. The paint hadn't peeled. It had *frozen*, each face locked mid-expression, mid-blink, mid-smile, so that walking past them was walking past a crowd of people who had all been interrupted at the same moment by the same terrible thought. Ice had grown from the frames like lichen. In one portrait, a queen holding a lapdog had frost on her painted lips, and I would have sworn on any god you liked that her eyes followed us.

"They're not enchanted," I murmured, because Sera was giving me the look that meant *explain, charm-seller*. "They're drained. There was life in the pigment—old craft, the kind painters used to swear into their work. Something drank it."

"Everything here is drained," Tam said. His voice cracked on the last word. "It's like the whole city is a cup somebody already emptied."

*Somebody.* I kept my hand flat against the wall as we walked, feeling for what I'd felt at the shrine—the pull of Veil, faint and drowning, like a lantern at the bottom of a lake. It was stronger here. Stronger and more desperate. Fifteen years of silence, and now that I could feel him again, the only word for what came off the seal was *screaming without a mouth.*

We reached the door at the end of the gallery. Oak, iron-banded, tall enough for a giant, with the crowned stag of Veylmark worked into the metal. The stag's eyes had been frosted white.

Sera checked the corridor with the ease of a woman who had kicked in doors for the inquisition for twenty years. Tam put his back to the wall, palms out, ready to boil something. Wren stood very still, looking at the door the way you look at a grave you've only heard about.

"Whatever's in there," Wren said quietly, "it's wearing my father."

"Your father is in there too," I said. "That's the whole point. That's why we came." I didn't add what the shrine had shown me—that the man inside was barely a man anymore, that fifteen years of being eaten leaves little enough to rescue. Hope is a ration. You dole it out or you run dry before the road ends.

I pushed the door open.

* * *

The king's chamber was round, ringed with windows, and lit by nothing. No candles. No fire in the great hearth, though logs lay stacked there as if waiting for a servant who would never come again. The only light was the grey glow of the frozen city below, coming up through the windows like light through the surface of deep water.

The bed dominated the room—a vast carved thing with posts like trees, hung with curtains gone stiff with frost. And in it lay the King of Veylmark.

He was not what I expected, which was a monster. He was what I should have expected, which was worse: an old man. Gaunt. Grey-skinned, frost-rimed at the temples, hands folded on his chest like a recumbent effigy. He might have been dead except that his chest moved—shallow, slow, the breathing of someone who has forgotten how and is doing it out of habit.

His eyes were open.

One eye was brown. Human. Wet with terror.

The other was black all the way through, and it was watching us before we'd finished crossing the threshold, and it was smiling, and eyes can't smile, that's what makes them terrible when they do.

"Close the door," Sera breathed. "Slowly."

Wren made a sound I'd never heard from her—not the workhouse-flat voice, not the heir's resolve. A daughter's sound, small and young. "Papa?"

The brown eye found her. The whole body shuddered. His lips cracked apart—they were split and bleeding, frozen and thawed and frozen again, a hundred times over—and what came out was a rasp like wind through a keyhole.

"*Run.*"

One word, and it cost him everything. His back arched off the bed. The black eye flared, and the temperature in the room dropped so hard my breath turned to falling crystals, and then—

And then the Hollow King sat up inside him the way a puppeteer straightens behind a screen. The old body moved wrong. Too smooth. Too deliberate. Joints bending with a grace no seventy-year-old spine should permit, hands rising, turning, admiring themselves like a thief trying on stolen gloves.

"*Ah,*" it said. The voice was the king's voice and absolutely not the king's voice, the way a bell's note is the bell's even after the bell has shattered. "*The little sworn one. At last. I have wondered when you would find your way to me, Juniper Vasquez. You took your time.*"

"It knows my name," I said, mostly to keep my knees honest.

"It knows everyone's name," Sera said grimly. "It's been reading the purge lists."

"*I have been reading everything,*" the thing agreed pleasantly. It swung its borrowed legs off the bed and stood, swaying slightly, getting the weight of the body right. Up close, the king looked less like a man than like a coat that had been worn too long—stretched thin at the shoulders, frayed at the seams. "*Do you know how tedious it is, being a king? Everyone kneels. No one speaks honestly. Except you, little charm-seller. You have been speaking to my jailer for fifteen years without knowing it.*"

"My jailer," I repeated. My hand had drifted to my sternum, to the place where the bond lived, the silent knot beneath the silence. "You mean my god."

"*Jailer. Jailor. Gap-filler. Doorstop.*" The Hollow King spread its arms, and frost bloomed outward from its fingertips across the floorboards in feathering patterns. "*Your Veil bound itself into this crown three generations ago and called it mercy. Do you know what it is like to be pressed into a keyhole for sixty years? To feel a whole kingdom's gift seeping past you, drop by drop, and to swallow it because the alternative is me? Your god is not noble, girl. Your god is a cork.*"

"And you're what comes through when the cork rots," I said. "Yeah. I got the sermon at the shrine. Skip to the part where you offer me a deal, because I know you're going to, and I'd like to be home in time for the solstice."

Behind me, Tam made a noise that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Even now. Especially now. That boy.

The Hollow King tilted its stolen head. The brown eye rolled, wild, and the lips around the black eye's smile twitched—and I understood with a lurch that the king was still in there, still fighting for the mouth, losing. Fifteen years of fighting. Losing every time.

"*A deal,*" the Hollow King mused. "*Yes. You are a bargainer. It is your nature. You sold charms against bad luck to people who needed hope more than hexes. I have watched you. I watch everyone the Veil touches—it cannot help leaking, and I drink what leaks.*" It took a step forward. Sera's knife cleared its sheath with a whisper; the thing didn't glance at her. "*So here is my bargain, freely offered, no trick in it—I am old, girl, older than your kingdom, and I am tired of tricks the way your god is tired of holding. Walk away. Take your found family, your little heir, your burning boy, your traitor with her stolen papers. Go south, go anywhere. Open a shop. Sell your honest charms. Live.*"

"And in exchange?"

"*In exchange, I give you back your god.*"

The words went through me like a hook through soft flesh.

"*When the seal fails—and it fails at solstice whether you meddle or not—I will not devour the Veil. I will release it. Whole. Awake. Loud as it was the day it swore you. Every prayer answered, every silence ended. You will hear it again, Juniper Vasquez. Fifteen years of quiet, undone. All you must do is nothing. Nothing is such a small price. You have done nothing beautifully for fifteen years.*"

The chamber was very quiet. Somewhere far below, a bell that wasn't ringing rang anyway, in the way frozen things ring in dreams.

Because here is the truth I will set down plainly, since this account may be all that survives of me: for three full heartbeats, I wanted it.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of praying into a well and hearing nothing come back. Fifteen years of performing faith to a congregation of zero, of wondering if I'd dreamed the swearing, if the god had ever been real, if the silence was punishment or abandonment or simple death. Grief doesn't end. It just gets furniture. And here was a voice offering to take the furniture away, to give me back the one relationship in my life that had never been a transaction—

And that was exactly why I knew it for what it was.

"No," I said.

"*Think longer.*"

"Don't need to. See, you made a mistake in your pitch." I stepped forward, past Sera's warning hand, close enough to see the frost crawling the king's throat. "You called it *my* god. Like the bond goes one direction. But I've been doing some reading myself—Sera's documents, the shrine, the whole miserable archive. And here's what I learned." I met the black eye dead-on, which is like meeting a landslide dead-on, and did it anyway. "Veil didn't bind itself into that crown alone. It was sworn *through* its faithful. Generations of us. We're not the congregation. We're the mortar. And you can't cut one stone out of a wall and call the rest of it yours."

"*You are one woman,*" the Hollow King said, and for the first time the pleasantness thinned, and underneath it was something vast and cold and impatient, the voice of winter considering a single stubborn green shoot. "*One mortal. Your bond is a thread.*"

"Threads hold buttons. Buttons hold coats. Coats hold out the cold." I smiled, and I confess it was not a nice smile. "Also? You lied. Right there, in the middle of your generous offer. You said you wouldn't devour the Veil. But everything you take, you take *into* you. That's what you are. You're not a king, you're a hunger with a crown problem. If the seal breaks, you'll drink my god along with everything else, and you'll wear its voice answering prayers for a century before anyone notices the difference. So no. I don't accept. I don't walk away. I'm going to stand right here, in your borrowed bedroom, and on solstice night I'm going to pick the lock you've been rattling for fifteen years—and I'm going to do it *with* the thread, not without it."

Silence.

Then the king's body laughed. It was the worst sound yet—the laugh of a dying man forced through a puppet's grin—and midway through it broke, and the brown eye surfaced again, drowning, and the cracked lips shaped a different word:

"*Please.*"

"Papa—" Wren was moving before anyone could stop her, across the frost-feathered floor, and Sera lunged and caught her collar and held on, and Wren strained like a hound at a leash. "That's *him*, that's my father, we can't just—"

"Wren." I kept my eyes on the bed. On the body sagging back against the pillows, steam rising faintly where the Hollow King's grip loosened. "Ask him something only he knows."

She stopped struggling. Went still in Sera's grip. Then, in a voice pitched small enough for a deathbed: "The boot. When I was four. What did you say to me?"

The old man's chest heaved. His working eye squeezed shut. When he spoke, each word was a coin pried out of frozen mud: "Told you... a king who lets... a child sit on his boot... has already... given away... half the throne..." A wet, ruined sound that might once have been a chuckle. "You laughed... like bells..."

"Bells," Wren whispered. "The city's bells are frozen." She turned to look at me, and her eyes were blazing, and grief had finished its long work on her and become something with an edge. "Juniper. Solstice is two nights away."

"Two nights," I agreed.

"*Two nights,*" the Hollow King said through the king's slackening mouth, the black eye guttering back up like a lamp relit, "*and a door between us. Come then, little mortar. Bring your thread. I have waited three generations. I can wait two days—*"

The door slammed open behind us.

Not the wind. Not the wraith-guard, though I'd braced for them. A herald—one of the sleepwalking court, moving with that awful smooth purpose, a scroll of black ice unfurling from his rigid hands. He didn't see us. Sleepwalkers never saw us; that had been our one mercy in this city. But his voice filled the chamber anyway, hollow as a pipe, announcing to the empty room and the dying king and whatever wore him:

"*By decree of the Crown: the rite of the Long Vigil shall commence at solstice dusk. All registered gifts are summoned to the capital. Attendance is mandatory. Attendance is joy.*"

The scroll crumbled. The herald turned and walked away, footsteps precise as clockwork.

In the bed, the king began, very softly, to weep.

Tam looked at me. Sera looked at me. Wren looked at me, and her hand had found the frozen window frame, and beyond her the city sprawled, dark and silent, thousands of registered gifts already trudging toward the capital through the snow, summoned like moths to a flame that ate moths.

"Two nights," I said. "Then we'd better go find out how you unbind a god with a mortal's hands."

Above the bed, in the frost on the ceiling, patterns were forming. Feathers. Wings. A shape like a door, drawn in ice by no one, opening onto nowhere.

And through it, faint as a heartbeat, I heard—for the second time in fifteen years—my god say my name.
