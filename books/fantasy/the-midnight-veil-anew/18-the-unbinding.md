## 18. The Unbinding

The bells of the capital had been frozen mid-ring for three days, and on the night of the solstice they finally, quietly, shattered.

We heard it from the servants' stair beneath the royal chapel—glass-bright notes falling out of the dark like ice off a roof. Wren flinched. Sera didn't. Tam made a sound that was half laugh and half prayer, which I've come to understand is simply what Tam sounds like when he's terrified and trying to be brave about it, which is most of the time, which is most of us.

"That's it," he whispered. "That's the whole city's worth of gift, gone. It ate the last of it."

"Then there's nothing left to save but us," Sera said, "and we're not worth saving."

"Cheerful," said Tam.

"Honest," said Sera, which from her was the same as cheerful.

I pressed my palm flat against the cold stone and felt it—the pull, faint as a heartbeat through a wall. *Come,* Veil had said, weeks ago, in a fever-dream at a shrine in the mountains, and every mile since had been that word stretched thin. Now it wasn't a word anymore. It was a hand around my wrist, gentle and drowning, drawing me up through the palace's sleeping veins toward the throne room where a king was dying inside his own body and something older than kings was wearing him like a coat.

The corridors were full of sleepwalkers. Not dead—worse, kept. Courtiers standing in alcoves with candle stubs burned down to their knuckles, their eyes open and empty as winter windows. A chambermaid still holding a pitcher she'd been carrying since the frost came, water frozen solid inside it. The Hollow King didn't kill its people; it *saved* them, the way a miser saves coins. Every one of them had been someone's daughter once.

"Don't look at them," Sera murmured.

"I'm looking," I said. "Someone should."

She didn't argue. In the months since the river crossing, since the fever, since the shrine, since everything, Sera Vane had learned which of my silences were doors and which were walls. That was the strange grace of our friendship: two women who had spent their lives being silent for different reasons—a god, an order—and had finally found someone they could be loud with.

We climbed. The wraith-guard found us anyway.

They poured up the grand staircase like smoke deciding to be shapes—tall, crowned things, each one trailing tatters of whatever it had been before the Hollow King wore it hollow. Their faces weren't faces so much as suggestions of faces, and where they stepped, the warmth went out of the air like a snuffed lamp.

"Wren." Sera drew her stolen inquisitor's blade, the one with the sigils filed off, the one that had ended her old life. "You remember what I taught you?"

"You say something pious, then you hit them very hard."

"Good enough. Tam—the door."

Tam looked at the great double doors at the top of the stairs, oak and iron, sealed with wards that had probably been beautiful once and now hung slack as old nets. He rolled his shoulders. His hands were already steaming.

"I hate this part," he said, and put both palms against the wood.

What happened next was not fire, exactly. Fire is polite. What Tam does is boil the *idea* of warmth out of a thing until the thing forgets how to hold itself together. The wards shrieked—a high, glassy keening—and the oak went dark, then gray, then crumbled inward around a hole shaped like a boy's desperate hands.

"GO!" Tam screamed, and his voice cracked in the middle, and so did something across the landing, because three of the wraiths turned toward him all at once, hungry for the heat pouring off him like a beacon.

"No you don't." Sera met them on the stairs, blade low, moving with the economy of a woman who had spent twenty years learning exactly how much violence was required and refusing ever again to use more. Wren was beside her with a broken chair leg sharpened on the way in—I never did learn whose chair—and the two of them became a wall.

And I walked through the ruined doors alone.

* * *

The throne room had been beautiful once. You could tell by the bones of it: pillars like trees, windows tall as confessions, a floor of pale stone worn soft by centuries of coronation feet. Now frost grew up the pillars in black feathers, and the great windows were furred over with ice so thick the moon behind them was just a bruised glow, and the long winter the Hollow King had promised the world was already fully installed here, in this one room, like a showroom model.

The king sat on his throne.

Or rather—the king was tied to his throne, in the way a hand is tied to a body. I could see both of them now, the way you can't unsee once you've seen: the man, small and gray and folded into himself, lips moving around words that had no strength left to be sound; and the thing wrapped through him, vast and patient, its crown of shadow spreading up the wall behind the throne like root systems, like veins, like a map of everywhere it had already reached.

*Juniper Reyes.*

It knew my name. Of course it did. It had eaten the gift of everyone who'd ever spoken it.

*I know what you carry,* it said, and its voice was the voice of the king's cracked mouth, which was somehow worse than thunder. *The last sworn thread of the Silent God. The final knot of the seal. Come closer, little charm-seller. I have an offer.*

"You offered my god freedom if I walked away," I said. My breath fogged. "In the mirror-chamber. I heard you."

*I renew it. Walk away, and I will release the bond. Your god goes free—you go free. No more silence. No more servitude. Fifteen years of your life returned to you, unwasted. Is that not worth one mortal's comfort?*

And here is the thing I will not pretend, because I promised myself honesty somewhere around the frozen fields outside Greyhollow: for one full heartbeat, I wanted it.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of praying into a well and hearing nothing back. Fifteen years of being told—by doctrine, by doubt, by the smug faces of unsworn mages—that Veil had abandoned me, or never existed, or had never wanted me. One sentence from this thing and I could stop being the woman who gave herself away and start being a woman who belonged to herself. It dressed the offer in kindness because it had learned kindness was the fastest knife.

But I had also spent fifteen years listening to that silence, and I knew its texture. And this silence—the Hollow King's silence, the silence of a drained land and a stolen god—had a different grain entirely. Veil's quiet had always felt like a held breath. This felt like an exhalation after the breath was gone.

"You're not offering to free Veil," I said. "You're offering to free yourself. If the last knot walks away, the seal unravels, and you come through wearing whatever's left of the world."

*The seal fails regardless,* it said, with the terrible patience of deep water. *Midwinter has come. Feel it. Even now the knot frays. You cannot re-tie what gods could barely tie. But you can spend your thread buying your own peace. Everyone dies, Juniper Reyes. Most die having purchased nothing.*

"That's the first true thing you've said," I told it, and began the unbinding.

* * *

There is no book for it. There never was. When Veil bound itself into the crown generations ago, it did so silently, secretly, without telling its sworn ones—because a secret shared is a secret weakened, and the seal needed every scrap of strength it could hoard. So the only person alive who knew the rite was the god itself, and the god had been whispering it to me in fragments since the mountain shrine: *the knot, the knot, the knot.*

Here is what I understood, standing on the frozen floor while a dead-cold moon bled light through the windows:

The seal was not a chain on the Hollow King. It was a knot, and a knot needs rope. Veil had used itself as the rope—its divinity wound through the crown, through the monarchy, through fifteen years of agonizing silence—and even that was failing, because divinity stretches only so far before it tears.

But there was other rope in the room.

Me. Sworn at nineteen in a chapel that no longer exists, my gift knotted to Veil's, my identity braided into the god's, my protection—every ward, every charm, every quiet miracle of hiding that had kept me invisible for fifteen years—all of it one continuous thread running from my heart to the crown on that dying man's head.

To free Veil, I had to give the thread. All of it. Not spend it—spending implies you keep the coin. Give it. My magic. My sworn self. The version of Juniper Reyes who could make charms that worked and wards that held and lies that glowed faintly silver in the dark. After tonight, if I lived, I would be a woman who sold pretty knots and called them charms, and they would work exactly as well as knots do.

And here was the part Veil hadn't said aloud but I felt in the shape of the rite, the way you feel weather coming: the thread ran both ways. To give my bond freely, completely, I would have to walk it—inside it, down its whole length, through the collapsing seal itself, to the place where Veil was drowning and hold the door with my own bare human hands while the god re-knotted the lock.

Gods couldn't do that part. Gods are too large to fit through their own keyholes. But a mortal—a stubborn, frightened, charm-selling mortal who had spent her whole life making small things hold—might.

Behind me, the doors shuddered. Sera's voice, ragged: "JUNIPER! Whatever you're doing, do it FASTER!"

"Working on it!" I shouted back, and knelt on the frozen stones, and reached for the thread in my chest.

* * *

Pulling it hurt the way birth hurts, I imagine—not like injury, like emergence. The bond came loose from my ribs in coils of pale silver, and every coil took something with it: the memory of my swearing-oath, the warmth of Veil's presence from before the silence, the particular hum under my skin that had been mine since I was old enough to know it was unusual. I let it go. Coil after coil. Each one a small death, and I grieved each one properly, because I am a sentimental woman and always have been, and sentiment is just love that knows its own address.

The Hollow King screamed.

Not with the king's mouth—with the whole room, with the frost on the windows, with the black feathers of ice peeling off the pillars. *STOP.* The wraith-guard turned from the stairs, from Sera's blade, from Wren's sharpened chair leg, and poured toward me across the floor like a tide with a grudge.

"TAM!" Wren's voice, fierce and clear, the first time I'd ever heard the girl sound like royalty. "THE DOOR!"

The ruined doorway bloomed white-hot. Tam stood in it, arms out, bleeding steam from his split fingertips, boiling the air itself into a wall that the wraiths struck and recoiled from, shrieking, because they were creatures of stolen cold and he was a creature of terrible, generous heat.

"I CAN'T HOLD IT LONG!" he screamed. "IT'S EATING ME!"

"Then eat it back!" Wren shouted, absurd and magnificent, and I loved her in that moment with the clean love of a woman watching her family refuse to die.

I laid the last coil of myself on the frozen stones between me and the throne, and I stepped onto it, and I fell.

* * *

Inside the binding there is no up. There is no time, or there is all of it at once. I walked down the length of my own surrendered bond through a darkness that was not empty but *crowded*—fifteen years of prayers pressed against me like moths against glass, every charm I'd ever sold, every night I'd knelt in the cellar and asked *are you there* and gotten nothing.

At the bottom of the thread was the door.

It was enormous and it was failing, and Veil was wrapped around it like a mother around a child in a burning house—except inverted, except the god was the house and the child was the world. Veil saw me coming and I felt its recognition the way you feel sunrise: not a sight, a warming.

*You should not have come,* it said. Its voice was not a voice. It was the feeling of a held breath finally allowed to move. *This will take your gift entire.*

"It already did. I signed the paperwork." My voice sounded small and human and absolutely sufficient. "Re-knot it. I'll hold the door."

*A god cannot ask this of anyone.*

"You're not asking. I'm telling you. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point of me."

And I put my bare hands—my magicless, mortal, chapped-from-the-cold hands—against the door, against the seeping dark beyond it, against the thing pressing through, and I pushed.

The Hollow King pushed back. It knew my name. It knew my fears. It showed me the shop on Lantern Row I'd never open, the family I'd lose, the small gray ordinary life waiting for me on the far side of sacrifice, and asked if it was worth it.

"Yes," I said, and meant it, which surprised me more than anything else that night.

Because here is what I learned at the bottom of the seal, holding a door that gods could barely hold: the Hollow King feeds on the gift, on magic, on the divine spark—but it cannot digest a mortal choice. Freely given, fully meant, expecting nothing back. That currency means nothing to it. It slipped through its fingers like water, and it raged, and I held.

Behind me, Veil worked. I felt the god's attention pass over me like a blessing I could no longer feel as a blessing, only as warmth on my neck, and then the great knot began to close, loop by ancient loop, the lock re-forming around the screaming dark.

The door bucked. The dark clawed. My arms shook, my back screamed, my nose bled freely onto stone that existed in no geography I'll ever describe.

And I thought, absurdly, of Tam's hands steaming. Of Sera filing the sigils off her blade. Of Wren choosing to matter. Of the ledger I'd burned, and the guilt, and the road, and the chapel where forgiveness got chosen instead of felt.

*Let go,* the dark whispered, in a voice almost kind. *Everyone lets go eventually.*

"Not this one," I said.

And held.

Somewhere above me—far away, in a world of stairs and steel and steam—Sera was shouting my name, and Tam's wall of heat was guttering, and the solstice midnight was turning, turning, turning toward morning.

And in the dark beneath everything, with my bare hands on a god's door, I felt the knot catch.

Felt it hold.

Felt Veil's breath, for the first time in fifteen years, come free.
