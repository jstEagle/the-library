## 20. After the Long Winter

The winter broke on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Fifteen years of darkness, a hollow king, a god drowned inside a crown, and Tam nearly boiled alive twice in the process — and history chose to end it on a gray, unremarkable weekday while most of Veylmark was still asleep.

I know because I was awake. I hadn't slept properly since the solstice, and neither had anyone else in the palace, though "palace" was generous. It was more of a mausoleum that someone had tried to make cheerful with banners. I was sitting in a window alcove on the third floor watching the frost climb down the glass instead of up it, because apparently even ice didn't know what to do with itself anymore, when the first bell rang.

The bells had been frozen mid-ring for weeks. You could see them from the square — great bronze mouths hanging open, sheathed in ice, caught forever between one note and the next. The whole city had learned to live under that held breath. And then, at dawn, the ice cracked all at once, from tower to tower, a sound like the sky clearing its throat, and the bells fell into motion and finished the note they'd started a season ago.

It was the ugliest sound I'd ever heard. Clangor and rust and a century of pigeons objecting violently. People came out of their houses in nightshirts and stood in the falling slush and wept at it.

Tam cried too. He'd deny it. He cried so hard he set the washbasin boiling, which is how we knew his gift was settling — before the thaw, water near him just went cold and confused, like everything else.

And me? I stood in the corridor with my back against cold stone and thought: *so that's what an ending sounds like.* Ugly and loud and impossible to ignore. Like most truths, now that I think about it.

* * *

The king died eleven days later.

Not the Hollow King — that thing had been pushed back through its door and the door nailed shut with Juniper's whole life as the hammer. No. This was the man underneath, the poor wrecked body of Aldric the Fourth, who had spent fifteen years as a coat something else wore. When the seal re-knotted, whatever had been feeding on him let go, and what remained was just an old man with white hair and cracked lips and eyes that had been screaming behind glass for longer than I'd been alive.

He woke, briefly, on the third day. Juniper was there. She told me about it afterward in her flat, practical way, the way she talked about everything that mattered.

"He asked if the winter was over," she said. We were in the kitchen of the shop-that-didn't-exist-yet; she was mending a kettle by hand, no magic, because she had none left and kept pretending this was fine. "I said yes. He said, 'Then it was worth the price.' Then he asked who I was."

"What did you tell him?"

"Nobody," she said. "A charm-seller." She tied off the thread and looked at me over the kettle. "He smiled at that. Said nobody had ever saved him before. Only served him, or feared him, or used him."

The king lasted eight more days after waking. Long enough to ask forgiveness — from the kingdom, from the gift-bearers whose names filled the purge ledgers, from a boy called Tam whom he had never met and whose workhouse had been emptied by hunters wearing the king's own seal. He revoked the decrees with a shaking hand. He named no successor aloud, because everyone in the room already knew, and saying it would have made it real, and real things have to be governed.

They buried him under thawing ground. Half the city came. I stood at the back with my hood up, feeling like an impostor at my own father's funeral, which — technically.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about grieving a stranger: it doesn't wait until you know them. I grieved the man I'd never had — the father who might have come to the workhouse on visiting days, who might have known my name, who might have *mattered*. Instead I got a body in a box and a kingdom in my lap, and I was supposed to feel triumphant.

Mostly I felt cold. Though that could have been the weather. Everything was melting at once, and the streets ran brown with snowmelt, and the smell of wet stone and rot and green things starting was everywhere, and it was the best smell in the world.

* * *

The coronation was supposed to be grand. The council — what was left of it, which was mostly Sera's former colleagues looking sheepish and terrified, as well they should — planned a full day of procession and anointing and oaths in Old Veylmarki, and a crown weighing approximately as much as a workhouse stove.

I refused most of it.

This caused a constitutional crisis lasting four hours, which I'm told is a record. I told them: I grew up watching important people decide everything for everyone else, from behind a wall, through a window, at the bottom of a soup line. If my first act as queen is to disappear inside a ceremony, I've already lost. So we did it small. The chapel of Veil — the ruined one where Juniper and Sera had once nearly torn each other apart over old sins, now swept clean by Tam, who complained magnificently the entire time. Forty people. Juniper. Sera. Tam. The matron of my old workhouse, who cried and pretended it was dust. A girl named Odile from the laundry rooms who used to slip me extra bread.

No crown. They insisted on something, so they gave me a circlet of iron, plain as a horseshoe, forged from a melted-down hunter's brand. Juniper's idea. She has a flair for symbolism she pretends not to have.

The vow was short, because I wrote it myself and I am not a poet. I'll reproduce it here because someday some chronicler will get it wrong:

*"I was nobody, and you were everybody, and we survived each other. I swear to be ruled by you as much as ruling. The gift is lawful. The hiding is over. Nobody kneels."*

That last part caused a stir. Everyone knelt anyway, out of habit, and then stood up again very quickly, embarrassed, and it was the first genuinely funny thing to happen in Veylmark in fifteen years. Even Sera laughed, once, sharply, like a door slamming on better days.

The decree itself I signed that evening at a kitchen table, because thrones are for sitting and work is for tables. *The bearing of magic is no crime in Veylmark. All registers of the gift are burned. All hunts are dissolved.* The purge ledgers went into a brazier one page at a time, every name read aloud first by volunteers who worked in shifts for three days, because the dead deserved to be names before they were ash. Some pages took a long time. One woman read her son's name and then stood holding the paper for ten minutes before she could let it burn. Nobody hurried her.

Juniper stood beside me through all of it, hands folded, doing nothing, being everything. She's mortal now — truly, permanently mortal, no bond, no gift, no god on speed-call. You'd think she'd be bitter. She polishes the shop counter instead and says things like "craft holds what gift can't" and means it. But sometimes I catch her listening to a silence the rest of us can't hear, with an expression like someone waiting for a letter that may never come. I don't ask. Some doors you leave shut out of courtesy.

* * *

Sera left in the spring, which surprised exactly no one who knew her, and devastated exactly everyone who loved her, which was also everyone who knew her. These are the same people. That's what loving Sera Vane does to you.

She came to find me on the morning of her going, in the council chamber I was learning to hate slightly less. She looked better than I'd seen her look since the river crossing — color in her face, the fever-scar on her collarbone healed to a pale seam. She wore traveling leathers and the pardoning papers she'd never asked for, rolled up and unused, because Sera considered official forgiveness a formality extended to people who wanted to stay.

"The order's being rebuilt," she said, without preamble. "Reformed. Whatever word makes it palatable. They want me to lead it."

"And?"

"And I burned enough children's files to last several lifetimes, Your Majesty." She said the title like it tasted of iron. "If I build the new order, it becomes mine, and everything it does becomes mine, and I will spend the rest of my life flinching at my own shadow. I've done my time being an instrument. I'd like to try being a person somewhere quiet."

"So where's quiet?"

"The mountain shrine." She said it lightly, but her hand went to her side where the wound had been. "Where your god lives behind the door. Someone should keep watch. Not an order. Not soldiers. One person who knows exactly what's on the other side, and why it must never open again." She paused. "One person who owes it."

"You don't owe it," I said. "You were a weapon. Weapons don't choose."

"No," she agreed. "But I do now. That's rather the point."

We stood there in the gray light of the reforming kingdom, and I did the arithmetic I'd been dreading: Juniper staying, Tam apprenticing, Wren crowned, Sera leaving. The found family, found and immediately scattering. It seemed a cruel joke. It seemed, also, like how families actually work — gathered under one roof just long enough to learn each other's shapes, then carried outward like sparks.

"Will you come back?" I asked. "For midwinter, at least. Tam's making pudding. It boils itself. He's very proud."

Something crossed her face — the ghost of the laugh from the coronation. "Midwinter," she said. "I'll be there. Tell him to keep it away from the curtains."

She hugged me. Sera Vane hugged me. Briefly, awkwardly, like a sword trying to be a blanket, and then she walked out through the palace doors into the mud and meltwater and was gone up the mountain road before noon.

I watched her from the window until I couldn't. Then I went back to work, because the decree needed witnesses' signatures and the granary reports needed reading and a kingdom, it turns out, does not govern itself, no matter what the ballads will eventually claim.

* * *

Which brings me to tonight — the reason I'm writing this at all, ink freezing and unfreezing in the pot by turns because spring in Veylmark has commitment issues.

A rider came up from the south at dusk. Palace livery, half-frozen, half-mad with hurry. He brought a single dispatch, sealed with the shrine's mark, in handwriting I knew: small, precise, blade-neat.

*Door stable,* it said. *Seal holds. Watch routine.*

And beneath it, in different ink, added later, hastily, almost as an afterthought — except Sera Vane did not do afterthoughts:

*There are marks on the mountain path. Boot prints. Fresh. Coming up from the valley. Too many for pilgrims. I count thirty or more, armed, and they move like people who know exactly where they're going.*

*Someone remembers what's behind the door. And someone wants it.*

I sat with the dispatch for a long moment while the fire popped and Tam argued with Juniper in the kitchen about whether a self-boiling pudding constituted progress or arson.

Thirty armed strangers, climbing toward a door that a god died quietly to keep shut, fifteen years to the week since the seal was first tied.

I reached for my cloak.

I'm getting ahead of myself, though. There's pudding to deal with first, and a kingdom that won't hold its own breath while its new queen goes chasing up a mountain. But mark the date, whoever finds this: the winter ended, and the second fight began before the mud had dried.

Nobody tells you that part either. The thaw is just the beginning of the work.
