## 21. The Charm Shop on Lantern Row

The sign took me three tries, because I insisted on painting it myself and I have the artistic talent of a startled chicken.

**VASQUEZ & TAM — HONEST CHARMS.**

No stars, no crescent moons, no sigils of the old sworn orders. Just the name and the promise, in letters slightly too large, because Tam said small letters looked "suspicious," and he wasn't wrong. A charm shop that whispered was a charm shop hiding something. Ours didn't whisper. It sat on Lantern Row between a cobbler who sang off-key and a tea merchant who cheated at cards, with its door propped open from dawn until dusk, and anyone could walk in and watch us work.

That was the whole point. That had always been the whole point, back when it was illegal and I understood it, and now that it was lawful and most people still didn't.

"Juniper." Tam's voice came down the stairs, cracking in the middle the way sixteen-year-old voices do, like a door with a loose hinge. "The iron's hot and I can't remember if you braid clockwise or—"

"Counterclockwise for luck-charms. Clockwise for warding. You braided a warding knot into Mrs. Aldery's luck-bracelet last week and she won three coppers at dice and now she tells everyone my shop is enchanted."

"It *was* enchanted. Accidentally. By you."

"I fixed it."

"You fixed it by selling her a *second* bracelet."

I came up from the cellar where I'd been restocking tallow and found him at the workbench, sleeves rolled, hair tied back with a strip of leather Wren had given him because he kept setting his hair on fire. Not metaphorically. The boy had spent his whole life terrified of the thing in his blood, and now he used it to heat solder, and every few days he still singed something, and every time he apologized so earnestly that I wanted to shake him and hug him in equal measure.

"The second bracelet," I said, taking the iron from him before he could brand himself, "was honest craft. Copper, salt-ash, a knot tied with intention. If she wins at dice now, it's because she cheats, which is her own accomplishment and none of mine."

He grinned. He grinned a lot these days. There had been months—not long ago, though it felt like another life—when Tam hadn't smiled at all, when he'd flinched at his own hands. Some scars don't show. His did, actually, thin white lines up both forearms where his gift had boiled out of him at the river crossing, but those weren't the ones that mattered. The ones that mattered were healing.

"Customer," he said, nodding toward the front, where a woman stood examining our wall of wares with the particular suspicion of someone raised to believe charms were either fraud or heresy.

"Be honest with her," I murmured. "It's the only policy we've got."

She bought a sleep-charm in the end—a sachet of valerian and riverstone under waxed linen, stitched shut with a knot Tam tied while narrating every step aloud, because I'd taught him that honesty meant showing your seams. No magic in it at all. Just craft, and craft works, if you respect it. She left holding it like it might bite, and also like she might never let it go.

"That's the third one this week," Tam said after the bell above the door stopped swinging. "People keep buying things that don't do anything."

"They do things. Just not *miraculous* things. There's a difference between a miracle and a good night's sleep, and honestly, Tam, most people would take the sleep."

* * *

Lantern Row had been my street once, years ago, before everything—the purge, the winter, the frozen bells, the boy in my cellar, all of it. Back then I'd sold charms out of a satchel, moving stall to stall, invisible on purpose. Now I had four walls and a lease signed with the city of Greyhollow under a queen whose seal I'd watched being pressed into wax by a hand I'd first seen scrubbing workhouse floors.

Wren had offered me a shop in the palace district. She'd offered it with that flat, stubborn expression she gets, the one that says *this is happening whether you argue or not.* I'd argued anyway. Palace district customers wanted wonders. Lantern Row wanted knots that held, salves that soothed, and someone who'd look them in the eye. I'd spent fifteen years selling people comfortable lies about what magic could do for them. I intended to spend the rest of my life selling them the truth: that most of what ails you, craft can help, and the rest you carry, and carrying it is easier in company.

The bell rang again near midday, and I knew before I turned around who it was, because Tam straightened like a soldier and then immediately un-straightened like a boy trying not to look like a soldier, which is its own kind of tell.

Wren came in wearing traveling clothes. Not the crown. Never the crown, unless she absolutely had to; she'd told the court, in one of her first decrees, that the monarch of Veylmark would wear it for ceremonies and funerals and nothing else, and half the old families had fainted, and she had not cared even slightly.

"Your Majesty," Tam said.

"Wren," she corrected, for approximately the nine-hundredth time.

"Queen Wren," I said sweetly, just to watch her scowl. She scowled. It was a good scowl. Her father's face had been gentle even at the end, cracked-lipped and begging, and sometimes I caught the ghost of him in her features and had to look away. But the scowl was entirely hers. She'd earned it in a workhouse.

She drifted along the wall of wares, picking things up and putting them down. She did this every visit. She never bought anything. Once I'd asked her why she kept coming if she wasn't going to buy, and she'd said, "Because it smells like rosemary and burnt hair and it's the only place in the city where nobody needs anything from me," and I had decided never to ask again, because some answers you just hold onto.

"Sera sent this," she said finally, pulling a wrapped bundle from her bag and setting it on the counter. "From the shrine. She says the mountain passes are clear early this year. And she says—" Wren squinted at the note folded around it. "She says, and I quote, 'Tell Juniper her warding knots are still sloppy on the left side.'"

"Sera has been guarding a god-trap on a frozen mountain for eight months. She has time to critique my knots?"

"She has nothing but time. That's rather the point of a vigil." Wren unwrapped the bundle: dried herbs from the high slopes, the blue-flowered kind that doesn't grow below the snow line, and a small carved token of pale wood that I recognized, and didn't want to recognize, and picked up anyway.

A veil-token. The old symbol of the silent god. Sera had whittled it herself, presumably, because Sera whittles when she thinks, and Sera thinks constantly, relentlessly, the way other people breathe.

"She said to tell you one more thing," Wren added, more carefully now. "She said, 'Tell her the door is quiet. Tell her it's almost too quiet.'"

Tam had gone still at the workbench. He knew what the door was. We all knew what the door was. Some nights I woke up with my hands aching, remembering how they'd felt pressed against a seal that was coming apart at the seams, holding back a dark that wanted through, and knowing—I *knew*, the way you know a bruise—that my hands had no business being enough, and had been anyway.

"Quiet is good," Tam said. "Quiet means it's holding."

"Quiet means it's holding," I agreed. "Sera just likes to be dramatic. It's how she was raised."

But I put the veil-token in my pocket instead of in the drawer with the other curiosities, and I didn't examine why.

* * *

We closed at sundown, the way honest shops do. Tam swept. I counted the till, which held exactly enough for rent and flour and a little extra, which I put in the jar labeled **TAM — BOOTS**, because the boy grew like a weed and refused to accept charity, so I'd invented a business expense called "apprentice equipment" and he pretended to believe it, and that pretense was, in its way, one of the great loves of my life.

He lit the lanterns outside—we had a whole ritual now, him cupping a match against the wind, me holding the ladder—and when they caught, the row glowed the way it used to before the war, before the purge, before the winter. Other shops' lanterns answered down the street, one by one, and somewhere the singing cobbler was murdering a ballad, and a cart rattled past, and ordinary life went on being ordinary, which after everything we'd lived through seemed less like boredom and more like a miracle nobody had to bleed for.

"Juniper?" Tam said, halfway up the ladder, match in hand. "Can I ask you something weird?"

"You ask me weird things daily. Proceed."

"Do you miss it? The gift?"

I leaned on the ladder and thought about lying, and then thought about what a hypocrite I'd be, running an honesty shop.

"Sometimes," I said. "When I'm tired, mostly. There was a version of this"—I gestured at the shop, the light, the whole glowing street—"where I built it with magic. Where every knot I tied hummed. And some evenings I miss the humming." I shrugged. "But here's the thing I figured out, standing in a collapsing seal with my bare hands. The humming was never mine. It was borrowed. Every spell I ever cast, every charm I ever sold—it was all drawn on credit from a god who couldn't tell me he was drowning. I don't miss the debt. I like owning my own hands."

Tam considered this with the enormous seriousness he brought to everything, bless him. Then he lit the last lantern, and the flame steadied, and he said, "You own your own hands," like it was a proverb worth keeping, and climbed down, and stole a biscuit from the tin I'd hidden from him specifically, because found family apparently includes theft rights.

* * *

Later, when he'd gone up to his room over the shop—the room that used to be storage, with the window that faces east, because I'd learned the hard way what happens to boys who sleep without a view of open sky—I sat at the counter with the ledger and the lamp and Sera's little wooden token.

I turned it over in my fingers. Smooth wood, worn already at the edges from Sera's pocket. The symbol of a god I'd served for fifteen years and mourned for fifteen more minutes than that, before I learned there was nothing to mourn.

*I was never gone,* Veil hadn't said, that day in the binding, because Veil didn't say things, Veil was silence itself—but I'd felt it, felt the truth of it move through me like warmth through cold water. *Only drowning. Only holding the door.*

And since then: nothing. A year of nothing. Which was correct. Which was right. A god holding a seal has no breath to spare for a mortal woman selling honest charms on Lantern Row, and I knew that, and accepted it, and had definitely, completely, thoroughly made my peace with it.

Mostly.

I set the token down. I blew out the lamp. And in the dark of my shop, with the lanterns still glowing faintly orange through the windows and the cobbler finally, mercifully silent, I heard—or imagined I heard, and I want to state for the record that I am a woman of exacting honesty—something like a breath. Not a word. Not yet. Just the sense of attention, vast and patient and familiar, turning toward me across whatever distance lies between a mountain shrine and a shop counter, the way you feel someone looking at the back of your neck.

Tomorrow was the first of Thawmonth.

One year since the ice broke. One year since the bells of the capital rang again, unfrozen, mid-swing completing themselves at last. One year, and the door on the mountain was quiet, too quiet, and Sera—who missed nothing, who never had—had sent me a god's symbol with a warning wrapped inside a compliment.

I lay awake for a long time, listening to Tam snore through the floorboards and the wind come down off the hills, and just before sleep took me, the feeling came again, closer now, unmistakable, patient as winter and gentle as spring:

*Almost time.*
