## 22. The Veil, Anew

One year is a strange unit of measurement for a miracle.

The alchemists measure things in grains and drams, the tailors in ells, the astronomers in the slow crawl of stars. But grief, it turns out, measures itself in years. It takes a full turn of the seasons to learn that the thaw didn't just happen to the land—it happened to you, and you're still finding pockets of ice in yourself that haven't quite let go.

I was finding one now, on a cold bright morning in Lantern Row, holding a broom like it might defend me from my own thoughts.

"You're sweeping the same spot," Tam observed from the doorway of the shop, a mug of tea in each hand. He'd grown half a head taller over the winter, which seemed frankly rude of him. His sleeves were rolled up over forearms that still carried the pale shine of old scald-scars—scars he'd stopped flinching from months ago, mostly because Wren had told him once, with the blunt authority she was going to make a very interesting queen, that they looked like maps. *You've been places*, she'd said. He'd believed her. That was the thing about Wren; people tended to believe her, even when what she said was absurd.

"Am I?" I looked down at the flagstones, which were, indeed, immaculate. "I'm being thorough."

"You're being nervous."

"I'm never nervous. Nervousness implies something unexpected might happen. I run a charm shop on Lantern Row. The most unexpected thing that happens here is Mrs. Corvel haggling over candle-wicks, and I could predict that haggle syllable by syllable."

Tam set one mug on the counter inside and leaned against the doorframe with the particular patience of a sixteen-year-old who has decided his elder is being ridiculous and intends to enjoy it. "It's today," he said.

"I know it's today."

"The anniversary."

"Yes, Tam."

"The day Veil came back and talked to you in front of everyone, and you stood there in the square like a woman who'd seen a ghost, except the ghost was a god, and the god said your name—"

"It did not say my name. It said—" I stopped, because we had had this argument before, and because the memory of it rose up in me anyway, unbidden, the way it had been doing all week: standing in the thawing square of Greyhollow with half the city around us, lanterns lit for the dead, when every bell in the kingdom had gone silent at once—and into that silence, into *me*, had come a voice like snow deciding to fall.

*Juniper.*

Not an order. Not a summons. Just my name, spoken the way an old friend says it across a market street—glad, unhurried, as if no time at all had passed, though fifteen years of it had.

"They're expecting a speech," Tam said. "Wren's expecting you. Sera sent word from the shrine—well, Sera sent a note that said 'Tell Juniper to stop dithering,' which is basically a speech."

"Sera has always been a poet of few words."

"She also said," Tam added, digging the folded scrap of paper from his pocket and squinting at it, "'and tell her the knot holds.' Whatever that means."

The knot holds.

Three words, and there went my chest doing that tight, complicated thing again. Because I knew exactly what knot she meant. We all did. Every soul in Veylmark knew it now, though only four people alive understood it truly: the seal beneath the mountain shrine, re-knotted by a god's own hands while a mortal woman with bare human fingers held the door shut against the dark. The Hollow King was back behind it, and this time the lock was not failing. This time, for the first time in three hundred years, the god who kept it had help—and then, afterward, had rest.

Sera guarded the shrine now. She'd refused the new order's pardons, its titles, its tearful offers of reinstatement. *"I spent twenty years learning how to break things,"* she'd told Wren, in that flat inquisitor voice that fooled nobody anymore. *"Let me spend what's left learning to keep one thing whole."* She wrote to us twice a season. Her letters were getting longer. Last month's had contained a joke—a real one, about a mountain goat that kept trying to eat her dispatch satchel. I'd read it four times. Tam had pretended not to notice me reading it four times, which is the truest act of love a boy can perform.

"I'm not dithering," I told Tam. "I'm opening a shop. Shops open at nine. There will be customers. The world does not stop for anniversaries."

"The world literally stopped for a year," Tam said. "Frozen bells. Sleepwalking citizens. Rivers like glass." He shrugged. "I think it can spare you until noon."

He was right, of course. He'd learned that from Wren, or possibly she'd learned it from him—I'd never been able to untangle which of them had made the other braver. I put down the broom. I drank my tea, which had gone lukewarm because I am constitutionally incapable of drinking tea when it is at its best temperature. And then I took down my coat—the good coat, the one with the deep pockets, because a charm-seller never goes anywhere without at least three honest charms, and because old habits are the last thing the thaw ever reaches—and I walked out onto Lantern Row toward the square where, one year ago, everything had ended.

Or begun. Depending on how you counted.

* * *

The square had changed. Most things had.

Greyhollow in winter used to be a city holding its breath—shutters closed, eyes down, magic bled out of the streets like warmth from cracked windows. Now the shutters were open. There were stalls along the Row selling charms openly, brazenly, some of them working by craft the way mine did and some of them working by gift, which would have gotten their makers dragged into a black wagon two years ago and now got them nothing worse than a queue. A girl of perhaps ten was making little lights dance above her palm for a crowd of children, and nobody was arresting her, and nobody was even looking at her sideways, and every time I saw something like that I had to stand very still for a moment and let it be true.

Wren had done that. Or rather, Wren had signed the decree and argued down the council and appointed reasonable people instead of fearful ones—but what she'd actually *done*, what no decree could do, was stand up in front of a frightened country and say, in her quiet workhouse voice: *The gift was never the danger. What we did to each other out of fear—that was the danger. We are done with it.* People believed her. They always did.

She found me before I found her. She had a talent for that too.

"You're early," she said, falling into step beside me. She wasn't dressed as a queen today—she almost never was, unless the occasion demanded armor, and she considered armor and crowns to be the same category of garment. Just a grey wool dress and her workhouse boots, resoled twice. "Tam said you'd try to hide in the shop."

"Tam talks too much."

"He gets it from you."

"That is slander and also accurate." I glanced at her. Fifteen years in a workhouse had taught her a face that gave away nothing; the crown had slowly, grudgingly taught her a face that gave away everything. Right now it was giving away concern. "Don't look at me like that."

"Like what?"

"Like I'm a problem you're solving. I know that face. You used it on the grain tariffs."

"The grain tariffs were a problem I solved."

"And I'm not."

"No," she agreed mildly. "You're a person. Those are harder." She stopped walking, near the fountain at the square's edge—the fountain that had frozen solid mid-splash during the long winter and now ran free, loud, gloriously wasteful with water. "Do you want to talk about it?"

"There's nothing to talk about. A year ago a god spoke to me in front of a crowd. Today there'll probably be another crowd, and if the god feels like speaking, it will speak, and if it doesn't, we'll have some speeches and some lanterns and Mrs. Corvel will complain about the candle-wicks, and life will go on." I heard my own voice doing the thing it did—bright, brisk, the shopkeeper's patter laid over the truth like a cloth over a cage. I sighed. "That was a lie."

"I know," Wren said. "I let you finish it anyway. It seemed rude to interrupt."

We stood at the fountain's rim. The water threw cold light on both our faces.

"Fifteen years," I said finally. "Fifteen years I prayed to a silence. Do you know what I thought? I thought I'd failed. I thought I'd done something wrong in the swearing, or that I'd drifted, or that the gift they gave me was a loan the god wanted back. I built my whole self around that silence. The hiding, the little charms, the shop, the—everything. And the whole time, it wasn't absence. It was drowning." My hand found the old scar at the base of my thumb, the swearing-mark, faint now, nearly gone since the unbinding. "It held the door shut for three hundred years and couldn't spare a single word, and I took it personally."

"Anyone would."

"A better woman wouldn't."

"Yes she would," Wren said, with the flat certainty she'd once used on me about frozen fields and the duty of mattering. "A better woman would have taken it more personally. That's why there isn't one." Then, softer: "You're afraid it'll ask you for something today."

There it was. Laid out plain, the way she always managed, not cruelly, just truly.

"Everyone keeps saying the anniversary will be a celebration," I said. "But I keep thinking—it spoke once, after fifteen years. What if speaking reminded it what it was missing? What if it wants—" I gestured vaguely at my own chest, at the place where the bond had lived, the warm tether that had been cut away in the dark beneath the mountain so that a mortal could hold a door a god couldn't. "What if it wants it back?"

"Do you want to give it?"

And there was the question underneath the question, and I hated her a little for asking it, and loved her a great deal for asking it, because it deserved asking.

"I don't know," I said honestly. "Some mornings I wake up and reach for it, the way you reach for a limb that isn't there anymore. The magic's gone. All of it—even the raw gift, even the little tricks. I'm as ordinary as bread now. And some days ordinary feels like freedom, and some days it feels like—" I searched for the word.

"Grief," Wren supplied.

"Grief," I agreed. "Grief with good lighting."

She laughed—a short, startled laugh, the kind she still rationed like sugar—and then the bells began.

* * *

They rang at noon, all across Greyhollow, all across Veylmark: the bells that had hung frozen and silent through the Hollow King's winter, ringing now for the dead, for the thaw, for the year since. The sound rolled over the square and broke over the crowd like a wave, and thousands of faces turned upward—faces that had been sleepwalkers in the dark, faces that had lost someone to the wagons or the frost or the wraith-guards, faces that had simply endured, which is its own kind of heroism and the kind that never gets statues.

Wren gave her speech. It was short, because she was wise. She named the dead—not all of them, there were too many, but enough, a handful of names read slowly, letting each one be a person and not a number. She named Sera, who could not be there, and the crowd cheered the name of the disgraced inquisitor who had burned her order's files and carried the truth out of the capital in her own bleeding hands, and I thought, *if Sera hears about this cheering she will be insufferable for a month,* and I missed her so much it felt like weather.

She named Tam, who turned red and pretended to examine his boots.

She did not name me. Instead she did something worse, something I will never forgive and will treasure until I die: she simply stepped aside, and gestured, and let the crowd look at me—an ordinary woman in a good coat with empty pockets where the magic used to be—and the roar that went up was not for a hero, exactly. It was for the idea of a hero, which is safer, and kinder, and easier to carry.

And into the space after the roar, into the hush where the last bell-note was still dissolving into the winter air—

*Juniper.*

The same voice. Like snow deciding to fall. Like an old friend across a market street.

The crowd didn't hear it. I don't think any of them felt it, the way you feel a change in pressure, a door opening somewhere in a house. But I felt it everywhere at once—in the ground under my feet, in the water of the fountain, in the hollow at the center of me where the bond had lived and now lived a different kind of quiet.

*I'm here,* I said. Not aloud. I hadn't prayed aloud in years, and it turned out the habit lives in the bones, not the throat. *I'm here.*

*I wished to see you properly,* the god said. And then, impossibly, gently: *May I?*

I understood, in the way you understand things that arrive whole and wordless, that it meant something specific. That it was asking permission for something it had never once asked permission for in all the centuries of sworn servants and kneeling acolytes and prayers offered up like taxes.

*Yes,* I said.

The world went silver and still.

I don't know how to describe what standing in the presence of Veil is like, now that it isn't mediated by a bond, isn't filtered through the sworn channel, isn't *owned*. Before, the god's attention had felt like a hand on the back of my neck—loving, but a hand nonetheless, a hand that held. This was different. This was standing beside something vast in an open field, free to walk away, aware of it precisely because I was free. The silence of the god, I realized, had never been emptiness. Silence was simply what Veil *was*—the pause between heartbeats, the dark between stars, the held breath before the truth. I had spent fifteen years mourning an absence that was actually a presence wearing its native shape.

*You held the door,* the god said. *With your hands. Without power. Without me. No servant ever could have. No servant would have been allowed to try.*

*Someone had to.*

*No,* Veil said, and there was something in the voice I had never heard from it in all my years of service, something almost wondering. *No, Juniper. Someone had to choose to. There is a difference, and the difference is everything. The seal was made of obedience for three hundred years, and obedience frays. What you gave it cannot fray.*

The fountain ran. The crowd murmured. Somewhere a child laughed, and the sound passed through the god's attention like light through clear water.

*The bond is yours to take again, if you wish it,* the god said at last. *Rebuilt as it should have been from the beginning—not a chain, a companionship. Not service. Partnership. You would have your gift restored, and more than the gift. You would never be alone in the quiet again. I offer it freely, and I will take no for an answer, and I want you to know that both of those things cost me a great deal to learn.*

I stood in the silver stillness and I reached, the way you reach in the morning for a limb that isn't there. And I felt what it offered: the warmth, the tether, the gift returned, the end of the reaching. Every morning for the rest of my life, I would wake already held.

And I thought about my shop. About Tam's terrible handwriting on the inventory ledgers, and Mrs. Corvel's candle-wicks, and the girl making lights dance for children who clapped. About Wren's boots, resoled twice, and Sera's goat, and the letters getting longer. About a year of ordinary bread-mornings that had hurt in some places and healed in others and belonged, entirely, to me.

*Thank you,* I said. *No.*

The silence held me, and it did not tighten.

*Why?* the god asked. Not wounded. Curious. Gods, I was learning, are capable of curiosity; it may be the only honest emotion they have.

*Because I spent fifteen years being yours,* I said, *and it nearly killed me, and then I spent one year being mine, and it saved me. I know which one I want to keep.* I hesitated, then added, because it deserved honesty: *And because you asked. Nobody ever asked me before. That's the whole difference, isn't it? The chain and the companionship. One is given to you. The other you choose, every single morning, including the mornings it hurts. I choose the shop. I choose them. I choose me.*

For a long moment there was nothing but the fountain-sound and the far-off bells finishing their decay into silence.

Then Veil said—the first time in the history of gods and mortals, I would wager, the first time in all the long ages of oaths and altars and kneeling crowds:

*Thank you.*

*For what? For refusing you?*

*For showing me what the choice looks like,* the god said. *I have watched ten thousand mortals kneel. You are the first I have watched stand. It is a better view.* A pause, and then, with something that in anyone else I would have called mischief: *Also, the shop. I have seen it. The sleeping draughts are excellent. The love charms are honest nonsense, which is the only respectable kind.*

"They work by craft!" I said indignantly—and said it aloud, in the middle of the square, in front of several hundred celebrants and one queen who was pretending very hard not to listen. "Rosemary for remembrance, lavender for calm, iron for warding—it's herbalism, it's tradition, it's—"

"Juniper," Wren said, "who are you talking to?"

I looked around at the crowd, at Wren's carefully neutral face, at Tam grinning from the steps of the fountain like a boy who has caught his teacher talking to herself. The silver stillness was fading, folding itself away, but it left one last touch behind, feather-light, at the base of my thumb—where the scar used to be. Where, when I looked down, I found a mark I hadn't earned and hadn't asked for and hadn't sworn a single oath to receive: small, pale, shaped like a knot.

Not a brand. Not a bond.

A signature. The kind two equals leave on a finished piece of work.

*A friend,* I said, answering Wren. And I found, to my enormous surprise, that it wasn't a deflection. It was simply true. *An old friend. We're renegotiating the terms.*

* * *

The shop was warm when we got back, because Tam had remembered to bank the fire, and because Wren had quietly enchanted the kettle years ago and pretended she hadn't, and because some kinds of warmth you build yourself out of craft and habit and the people who show up.

We ate lunch on the counter among the unsold charms. Tam told a story about a customer who'd tried to return a luck-charm on the grounds that his luck was bad, and I explained for the hundredth time that luck-charms work by *attention*, they make you notice the doors that are already open, and Tam said "that's what I told him," and Wren laughed her rationed laugh and stole the last of the bread.

Later, alone, after they'd gone—Tam home to his lodgings over the bakery, Wren back to whatever queens do in the evenings (paperwork, probably; she claimed paperwork, but I suspected paperwork and secretly hoped for a little mild tyranny)—I stood in the doorway of my shop on Lantern Row and looked out at the lamplighters working their way down the street. Ordinary men with ordinary ladders, lighting honest flames.

My thumb throbbed gently, pleasantly, where the mark sat.

*I'll come to the shrine at midwinter,* I said into the evening air, knowing it would carry, knowing it was heard. *Sera makes terrible tea. Someone should bring the decent stuff.*

The lamp nearest my door flickered—just once, just slightly, the way a flame flickers when something vast and quiet passes close by it, glad.

*Bring the honey cakes too,* the silence said.

I smiled, locked the shop, and went home.
