## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The jar on the third shelf had been there for sixty-one years, and every morning Wren Okonkwo dusted it without looking at it directly.

This was not superstition. It was procedure. The guild's own manual—*A Practical Compendium of Impossible Trades, Ninth Edition*, its spine cracked like an old man's knuckles—devoted a full chapter to the handling of last words. You did not look at them. You did not listen to them, though they murmured behind the glass in voices like wind through reeds, sometimes pleading, sometimes furious, once—memorably—singing. You cataloged the jar's weight, noted any change in color (today: the amber of weak tea darkening toward rust), and moved on.

Wren moved on. She always moved on. That was her whole talent, if anyone had thought to call it one: she kept moving through the guild's vast inventory of the impossible without ever once being seen doing it.

The inventory room occupied the heart of the Guild of Impossible Trades, three floors below street level, where the lamplight came up green through filters of old bottle glass and the air tasted faintly of iron and lavender—the lavender being there to cover the iron, which was there because certain items wept. Wren had learned in her first year never to ask what the iron was for. She'd asked once. Master Corvane had looked at her the way a man looks at a dog that has unexpectedly spoken, and she had not asked again.

She worked down the shelves now with her ledger-book open across her forearm, writing small. Writing small was a skill. The guild's ledgers were finite things, bound in leather gone soft as earlobes, and the masters were forever insisting there was no room for extravagance of hand. *Compact, girl. Compact.* So Wren wrote compact, her letters stacked like coins, and in eleven years she had filled more pages than any clerk in the guild's history, and not one master could have told you what her handwriting looked like.

That was fine. That was fine. She had made it fine years ago.

*Item 4,417,* she wrote. *Jar, sealed wax over glass, containing last words, quantity unknown (est. 40–60), provenance: widow, eastern provinces, paid in full. Condition: restless but contained. Note: one voice has begun repeating itself. Recommend appraisal.*

The key came next—or rather, the absence of a lock for it. Item 4,418 sat in a velvet-lined box the size of a prayer book: an ordinary-looking key of blackened bronze, unremarkable except that it had been growing warmer each year since its acquisition, and except that every locksmith in three cities had sworn, some of them crying, that it did not fit any lock that existed yet. Not *nowhere*. Yet. The key was waiting, the way an answer waits for a question that hasn't been asked out loud. Wren had held it exactly once, early on, before she understood the rules, and it had lain in her palm like a sleeping bird, warm and patient, and she had thought—with a shame that still visited her at odd hours—that she knew exactly how it felt to be that key.

*Condition: warming,* she wrote. *Recommend continued storage away from doors.*

There were other things. There were always other things; the guild's wealth was measured in things that should not be, and the masters grew fat on the interest. A mirror that showed the room as it would look after everyone who loved you had died in it. A coin that always came up on the side you needed, which sounded like mercy until you read the provenance and learned what the previous owner had needed. A letter, sealed, addressed in a child's hand to *Whoever I Become*, delivered to the guild by no carrier anyone could name, with instructions that it be opened only when the addressee arrived—and the addressee had not arrived in forty years, though the letter had recently begun to smell of smoke, which Wren had flagged twice in her reports and both times been ignored.

She cataloged. She dusted. She recorded the jar's restlessness and the key's warmth and the letter's smoke-smell in her small, coin-stacked letters, and she knew—because she had tested this hypothesis with the rigor of a scholar and the patience of a ghost—that if she lay down on the floor of the inventory room and did not move for a week, the only person who would notice would be the cook, and only because someone else would have to fetch their own supper.

*You're not invisible, girl,* Master Corvane liked to say, usually while walking past her mid-sentence to greet a client whose boots cost more than Wren's yearly keep. *Invisible people don't make errors. You made one in March.*

It had been a comma. In March she had misplaced a comma, once, in eleven years, and it was the single fact of her existence that any master of the guild could produce on demand. Wren had privately decided that when she died, her headstone would read: *She Misplaced a Comma.* At least it would be something. Most days she suspected even that was ambitious.

She was halfway through the second floor of the vault—a crate of unspilled tears from a country that had never known drought, each vial labeled with a date and nothing else—when the bells above began to ring midnight, and something happened that had never happened in all her eleven years below.

Someone knocked at the guild's door.

Not the client's knock—three measured raps, palm-flat, the knock of money and manners. Not the desperate knock either, fists and weeping, the knock of people selling the unsellable. This was small. Soft. Four taps, spaced carefully, the knock of someone who had practiced being quiet so long it had become their whole manner of existing.

Wren stood among the unspilled tears and listened, and felt something in her chest turn over like a page.

The masters were asleep. The clients' wing was empty. The door wardens had gone to their cups hours ago, because nobody came to the Guild of Impossible Trades at midnight unless they were dead, drunk, or desperate, and none of those categories knocked politely.

Wren told herself she was going upstairs to fetch the warden. That was the procedure. Procedure was the architecture of her life, load-bearing and familiar, and she gripped it now the way a climber grips rock.

She went upstairs. She did not fetch the warden.

* * *

The front hall of the guild was designed to make visitors feel small: vaulted ceiling, portraits of dead masters whose eyes followed you with proprietary interest, a floor of black marble polished to the shine of still water. Wren crossed it in her stockinged feet because her boots would have sounded like gunfire, and stood a long moment with her hand on the bolt, listening to the four soft taps come again, patient as rain.

She opened the door six inches, with the chain on, because she was overlooked, not stupid.

The night outside was the wet gray kind that makes cities look unfinished. Standing in it, alone, was a child.

A girl, Wren saw—six, maybe seven, though something about her made age feel like the wrong instrument for measuring, the way you don't measure a flame in inches. She wore a coat built for someone three times her size, sleeves rolled into thick cuffs, and she was barefoot, and her feet were not dirty, which was somehow worse. Her hair was pale as unbleached linen, and her eyes—

Her eyes were the color of deep water over old stone, and when they found Wren's face through the gap in the door, the child did not flinch, did not beg, did not do anything a lost child in a strange city at midnight ought to do. She simply looked at Wren the way you look at a word in your own language printed in a book written entirely in another one.

Then she spoke.

"Ves tu mena il-velesse? Tu es doma-vesse?"

The sounds went through Wren like cold water through cloth. They were liquid and archaic and utterly unfamiliar, and yet—not. Somewhere far back in her skull, in the locked cellar of herself where she kept the things she didn't examine, something stirred and almost rose to meet the words, and she slammed that door so fast she heard it bang.

"I'm sorry," Wren said. "I don't—" Her voice came out wrong. "Do you know where your mother is?"

The child tilted her head. She tried again, slower, shaping each sound with the care of someone offering a gift across a wide gap: "Ilma. Mena Ilma." And then, pressing one small hand flat against her own sternum, then gesturing outward—at the city, the dark, everything beyond the doorstep—she said a longer phrase, gentle and grave, and though Wren could not translate a syllable of it, she understood it completely.

*I am what is left.*

The chain suddenly felt like it was strangling her. Wren closed the door, slid the bolt, and stood in the black marble hall with her heart going like a trapped bird, telling herself a dozen sensible things: fetch the masters, wake the wardens, this is not your business, you are a clerk, you are a comma, you are nothing that opens doors at midnight.

But underneath the sensible litany, quieter and older, another voice—the one she kept in the cellar, the one shaped like a key waiting for a lock—was saying something else. It was saying: *you understood her*. Not the words. The shape under the words. Eleven years of being unseen, and here at the threshold stood a child the whole world had evidently mislaid, speaking a language that no living person should know, and looking at Wren as if Wren were legible.

Wren unbolted the door. She would spend the rest of her life understanding that moment as the first crack in the careful smallness she had built around herself, the hairline fracture through which the rest of it—everything, the ledger, the road, the drowned harbor, the price of her own name—would eventually pour.

For now, she simply opened the door wide, crouched to the child's height, and said, "Come in out of the rain, Ilma."

And the child smiled, stepped across the threshold, and spoke one more sentence—soft, matter-of-fact, terribly clear—and although Wren still could not have rendered it into any tongue she owned, the meaning settled into her bones like frost forming:

*A debt has come to collect itself.*

Somewhere below them, in the green-lit dark of the inventory room, the key in its velvet box began, very softly, to hum.
