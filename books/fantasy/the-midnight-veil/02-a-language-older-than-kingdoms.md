## 2. A Language Older Than Kingdoms

The guild kept its oldest ledgers in the cellar beneath the counting room, in a vault that smelled of iron and cold stone and the particular sweetness of paper that had outlived its makers. Wren had been down there exactly twice in her six years of service—once to fetch a deed of sale for Master Corvel, and once, on her first week, to be shown the shelves and told, *you will not need these*.

She went down the third time at dawn, with the child's words folded into her memory like contraband.

The night before, she had sat cross-legged outside the nursery they'd improvised from a linen storeroom, listening through the door while Ilma talked to herself in her sleep. The child did not cry. She murmured—a low, rolling cadence that rose and fell like water finding its way downhill—and though Wren could not have translated a single syllable, she found she could not stop listening. It was the sound of something complete. Every language Wren knew was full of borrowed parts, patched and mended, but this one sounded whole, as if it had never been broken by another tongue's weight.

And it had done something to the candle flame. When the child spoke in her sleep, the flame leaned toward the door like a plant toward sun.

Wren told herself it was coincidence. Wren was very good at telling herself things; it was most of what being overlooked required.

Now she stood in the vault with a lamp held high, reading spines. The ledgers here were not arranged by year or by trade but by *weight*—a guild habit she'd never understood until that moment, when her fingers found a volume so heavy it took both hands to lift, bound in leather gone black as riverbed clay. No title on the spine. Only a number: **One**.

Ledger One. The first book the Guild of Impossible Trades had ever kept.

She carried it up two flights to the copying room, where the masters would not come for hours, and laid it open beneath the window's grey light. The pages were vellum, soft as cloth, crowded in an antique hand. And there, in the very first entries, written in a script older than any alphabet she'd been taught:

*Received: one promise of rain, in perpetuity, from the kingdom of—*

The word that followed stopped her breath. Not because she recognized it. Because she didn't—and yet her mouth shaped it without stumbling, the way a hand remembers a stair in the dark.

**Veles.**

She said it aloud, barely a whisper, and the lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

* * *

By midmorning she had filled eleven sheets of foolscap with her own careful transcription. The method was simple, the kind of work the masters thought beneath accounting for her time: every entry in Ledger One's first hundred pages was recorded in two columns—one in the old trade-script, which the guild still used for binding contracts, and one in whatever tongue the counterparty had spoken, preserved "for verification." The verification tongues were the treasure. They were how the guild proved a bargain had been understood by both sides, back when bargains were struck with rivers and winters as readily as with men.

Wren copied out the sounds Ilma had made in her sleep—the ones Wren could hold onto, the rising phrase she'd repeated most often—and set them beside the verification column.

Page forty-one. An entry dated, by the guild's reckoning, some nine hundred years past:

*Sold: the last harbor-light of Veles, purchased by the sea itself. Terms: the light will burn wherever Veles people are drowning. Paid in full.*

Beneath it, in the verification tongue, four words that Wren read three times before her hands stopped shaking.

They matched. Not approximately. Exactly—the same rise, the same fall, the same soft click deep in the throat on the second syllable that she had heard through a storeroom door at midnight. A child of perhaps five years, speaking a phrase recorded nine centuries ago, word for word.

Wren sat back. Dust turned slowly in the window light. Somewhere above her, the guild woke into its ordinary morning: Corvel arguing freight rates, Mistress Iva scolding an apprentice over a mislabeled jar of sighs, carts rattling in the yard. All of it suddenly thin, like paint over wood.

She began pulling atlases.

There were fourteen in the guild library, ranging from a pocket road-book to a surveyor's atlas the size of a table. She went through all of them, index by index, coast by coast, running her finger along every shoreline where the old entries said Veles should be—north of the Amber Reaches, east of the Salt Marches, a country of harbors and high moorland according to a dozen contracts that had bought and sold pieces of it across three hundred years.

No atlas contained it. Worse than that: the space where it should have been wasn't empty. On the newest atlas, a bay sat there. On the next oldest, a different bay, slightly larger. On the surveyor's atlas from two hundred years past, a bay again, differently shaped, with no towns marked, no roads, nothing but open water where the contracts spoke of moorland villages and a royal city called—she checked the ledger—*Vhelskaya-on-the-Weep*. Even the bays disagreed with each other, as if the maps couldn't settle on what lie should cover the wound.

It was as if someone had sold the country and every map had quietly agreed to forget what used to be there.

Except the guild's books. Except Ledger One, and its children—Two, Three, Seven, Eleven—each carrying forward entries in a language that no living person, according to every authority in this building, had ever spoken.

Except a child who had arrived at their door at midnight, barefoot, alone, speaking it fluently.

Wren gathered her foolscap, and her hands were steady, and her heart was not.

* * *

She lasted four hours before she broke her own rule about never being noticed.

It happened in the counting room, late afternoon, when the light came slantwise through the tall windows and turned the dust to drifting gold. Master Corvel was reconciling the quarterly accounts with Mistress Iva, and Wren was meant to be fetching ink. Instead she set her foolscap on the great oak counter between them, squared its edges, and said—in her best clerical voice, the flat one that invited no questions—

"I've a question of provenance regarding an entry in Ledger One."

Corvel's pen stopped moving.

That was the first wrong thing. Pens did not stop in the guild; money never slept, and neither did the men who counted it. But his pen hung above the page, and a drop of ink swelled at its nib and fell, blooming black on the ledger below, and he did not even blot it.

"Which entry," he said. Not a question.

"The harbor-light," Wren said. "The verification text is in a language I can't identify. I've checked every atlas in the library and none of them lists the country of origin. I thought perhaps the name had fallen out of use, and if you could tell me what it's called now—"

"Stop."

Mistress Iva had not raised her voice. That was the second wrong thing. Iva's gift was volume; she could scold an apprentice from three floors away. But she said *stop* softly, the way you'd speak near a sleeping animal, and she came around the counter and closed the counting room doors herself, both of them, and drew the bolt.

The gold light lay across the floor between the three of them like something spilled.

"Wren." Corvel set down his pen with exaggerated care, the way men do when their hands want to do something else. "Where did you hear that language?"

Wren had rehearsed a dozen answers on the stairs. She discarded all of them, because she saw in his face—actually saw, for the first time in six years, *saw* him—that lying would cost more than truth. "The child," she said. "She talks in her sleep."

Iva made a sound, small and involuntary, like a stitch tearing.

"You transcribed it." Corvel's eyes went to the foolscap. "You compared it against the ledgers."

"It matches entries from a country called—" Wren began, and got no further, because Iva's hand closed around her wrist, hard, and the mistress leaned in close enough that Wren could smell the camphor she wore against moths and see that her eyes were wet.

"Do not say that name," Iva whispered. "Not in this room. Not anywhere. Do you understand me? There are listeners for certain words, and they do not knock."

"Listeners." Wren laughed—it escaped before she could stop it, brittle and too loud in the bolted room. "Mistress, it's a country. It's in our own books, nine hundred years of entries, and it isn't on a single map, and a five-year-old speaks its language like it's the only tongue in the world, and nobody—*nobody*—will tell me why. I keep this guild's accounts. I have a right to know what we're holding."

"A right." Corvel almost smiled. It was the worst expression Wren had ever seen him wear. "Child, everything in this guild is held because someone paid for it to be held, and everything is released when someone pays for that instead. That is the whole of our trade. That is the only law older than us." He slid the foolscap across the counter and, deliberately, one page at a time, tore it into strips, then quarters, then confetti, letting it fall into the cold fireplace. "Your question has been answered. The provenance is sealed. The fee for asking was your afternoon; consider yourself fortunate it wasn't more."

"What happens to the child?" Wren asked. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "What are you going to sell?"

For a heartbeat—half a heartbeat—Corvel looked at her the way a man looks at weather coming in off the sea. Weighing it. Measuring how far off, how heavy.

"Nothing that concerns you," he said. "Go and see to the girl. Feed her. Keep her calm. That is all your part is, and if you do it well, no one need remember you at all."

No one need remember you at all. He said it like kindness. He probably even meant it as kindness—he had known her six years, and that was the shape of every compliment she'd ever gotten from him: *good work, Wren, no one noticed a thing.*

She gathered the torn pieces of her foolscap from the fireplace, because she could not help herself, because they were hers, because the words on them were the first true thing she'd ever dug out of the world with her own two hands. In the corridor outside, the guild went on being ordinary. Someone was laughing in the kitchen. The evening bell rang.

Behind the bolted doors, she heard Iva say, very quietly, "She knows too much already," and Corvel answer, "Then we move the sale up. Send word tonight."

Wren stood in the hallway with a fistful of shredded paper and a name like a coal on her tongue—*Veles, Veles, Veles*—and understood, with terrible clarity, that the curiosity she'd spent six years smothering had finally lit something that would not go out.

Whatever they were selling the child to buy, she had until the word reached whoever they sent it to.

She counted the hours left to her, the way she counted everything.

Three days, at most.
