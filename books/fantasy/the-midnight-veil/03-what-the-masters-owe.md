## 3. What the Masters Owe

The guild kept its secrets the way other houses kept dust: everywhere, unexamined, accumulating.

Wren had spent eleven years learning where the dust settled thickest. She knew which floorboards in the counting room creaked under a master's weight, knew that Master Corvelin's third cup of wine made him sentimental and his fifth made him careless, knew that the great iron-bound ledger was carried up to the council chamber on the last night of every month, when the moon was dark enough to hide whatever was written by its light. She knew these things because knowing them was her job—*inventory*, they called it, *cataloguing*, *the small work*—and because she had never been important enough for anyone to wonder what she did with her knowledge.

She told herself it was curiosity. She told herself it was diligence. She told herself a great many things while she waited in the cold of the linen closet off the council chamber with her ear pressed against a seam of old oak, and the child asleep three floors below, and her heart going like a bird trapped in a shuttered room.

They had taken the child from the guest room at dusk. Wren had watched two of the under-clerks carry her down the servants' stair as though she were cargo wrapped in blankets, and something in Wren's chest had gone tight and wrong. So when the masters convened past midnight—not the ordinary accounting, but the other kind, the kind that required the doors locked and the shutters drawn and salt poured in a line across the threshold—Wren did not go to bed. She went to the closet where the winter linens were stored, the one place no one entered between seasons, and she folded herself among the camphor-smelling sheets like a secret of her own.

Five masters attended. She knew their voices before she knew anything else: Corvelin, whose voice rolled like casks; Mistress Ivetta, dry as ledger paper; old Master Sable, who wheezed when he climbed stairs but never when he counted; the twins, Ferren and Fenn, who spoke so rarely in unison that hearing it now made the hairs on Wren's arms rise.

And a sixth voice she did not know.

It came from nowhere in particular—from the center of the room, perhaps, or from all of it at once—and it sounded like a conversation overheard through a wall, words arriving slightly after the mouth that shaped them should have moved. When it spoke, the candle flames leaned toward it the way flowers lean toward sun.

"The remainder," the voice said. "You have held the remainder for nine nights."

"She is fed and sleeping," Corvelin said, in the tone he used for reassuring difficult creditors. "The asset is undamaged."

*Asset.* Wren's stomach turned over slowly, like something settling at the bottom of a river.

"You understand the condition," the voice continued. "The debt matures. Veles was consumed entire—its grain, its gold, its griefs, its kings, its names. All but this." A pause that lasted too long, that seemed to happen somewhere behind Wren's eyes rather than in the room. "A country is not eaten in one sitting. What remains must be delivered. The child is the remainder. The child is the crumb on the plate, and the plate has been licked clean for thirty years, and we are patient, but we are not infinitely patient."

Mistress Ivetta's voice, crisp and careful: "The Guild of Impossible Trades honors its obligations. You will have delivery by the new moon. We ask only the agreed consideration."

"Agreed," said the voice, and every candle guttered at once. "The usual terms. Forgiveness of principal. And one favor, uncatalogued, redeemable at our discretion."

Wren heard Corvelin swallow. Even he, she thought. Even the man who signed for everything was afraid of an uncatalogued favor.

"Then we are concluded," Corvelin said quickly.

"We are never concluded," the voice said, almost gently. "We are only ever paid or unpaid. Goodnight, tradesmen."

The candles stood upright again. Someone began moving furniture. Wren stayed in the closet long after the footsteps faded, long after the salt was swept, her knees aching against the floorboards, one word ringing in her skull like a struck bell.

*Veles.*

The name the books remembered and no one else did. The name she had been forbidden to speak aloud. They hadn't forbidden it to protect *her*. They had forbidden it because the name was collateral, and you do not let the merchandise learn what it is worth.

* * *

She went down to the archive afterward, because her hands needed work and her mind needed proof. Grief without evidence was just fear wearing a better coat, and Wren Okonkwo had survived eleven years in this house by demanding evidence of everything, including her own feelings.

The oldest ledgers lived in the vault beneath the counting room, chained to their shelves not against thieves but against reading—the chains were short, and opening a volume wide enough to study meant kneeling in an awkward, hunched posture that the masters had surely designed to discourage exactly what she was doing. She knelt anyway. Her knees were already ruined for the night.

She found the entries in the second-oldest volume, in an ink that had gone brown as dried blood. The hand changed partway through, as though the original writer had been replaced—or as though the writer had simply stopped being someone who could hold a pen.

> *...the sale concluded at dusk. Veles entire: 40,000 souls (counted), 9 rivers (measured), 1 crown (unredeemed), the language entire (verified by native speaker, payment rendered). Buyer took possession at the border stones. Witnesses report no border stones remaining.*
>
> *Note: the buyer does not purchase land. The buyer purchases existence. Land remains; existence departs. Maps amended accordingly. Memories amended accordingly. Do not speak the name in the presence of the buyer. Do not write the name except in this book, which is bound in hide that forgets.*
>
> *Outstanding: one infant, female, smuggled out prior to conclusion. Location unknown. The buyer is owed the remainder. Search suspended — cost exceeds recovery value. Entry closed.*
>
> *Entry reopened, eleven years later: a child has arrived at the guild door speaking the pre-kingdom tongue. The remainder may have found us.*

Wren read it four times. The candle burned low. Somewhere above her, the guild slept the sleep of the well-paid, and the words sat there in brown ink being true.

*A country is not eaten in one sitting.*

She thought of the inventory she'd catalogued her first year—a jar of last words, purchased from a widow who could no longer bear to keep them; a key that fit no lock yet, sold with the promise that the lock would be built someday. She had thought those things strange and wonderful, the guild's little miracles. She had thought the masters were custodians of wonders.

But a jar of last words was only terrible if you understood where the rest of the speaker had gone. A key to a future lock was only innocent if no one had already bought the door it would open. Every marvel on the shelves, she understood now, had a shadow entry somewhere in these books—a debt, a remainder, someone, somewhere, walking around incomplete because the guild had sold a piece of them and called it trade.

The guild's wealth was not built on wonders. It was built on gaps. On the empty space where a thing used to be whole. And she had dusted those shelves for eleven years, grateful for the work, proud of her competence, invisible and glad of the roof—

No. Not glad. She examined that, turning it over like a coin of doubtful metal. She had told herself she was glad of being overlooked because the alternative—that she had given the best years of her life to a marketplace that traded in the missing pieces of the world—was unbearable.

The child. Ilma, if that was her name; Wren still didn't know it, had only heard her murmuring in that old tongue to herself in the dark, syllables like water over stones. Nine nights the masters had fed her and measured her and written *asset* beside her sleep. In twenty days, at the new moon, they would wrap her in blankets again and carry her down the servants' stair and hand her to a voice that leaned candles toward itself, and the last crumb of Veles would be swallowed, and the books would close, and no one would ever grieve because no one would remember there had been anything to grieve.

Except Wren. Wren would remember. Wren always remembered; it was her one talent, the small work, the cataloguing. Let her catalogue this: five masters, one debt, twenty days, one child.

She closed the ledger and chained it back to its shelf, and the chain's click echoed in the vault like a small door locking.

* * *

At dawn she brought the child bread and honey, because that was what you did for a person, and the masters had stopped doing it sometime around the moment they started saying *asset*.

Ilma sat on the guest bed with her knees drawn up, watching Wren with the flat, patient attention of a cat deciding whether the hand approaching it holds food or grabs. She took the bread. She ate it without taking her eyes off Wren's face.

"They're going to sell you," Wren said quietly. There was no point pretending otherwise; the child had been carried like cargo, and children knew what cargo felt like even when they lacked the word. "In twenty days. To—to the people who took your country."

Something flickered across Ilma's face—not fear. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone confirming a suspicion they'd carried so long it had worn smooth.

Then Ilma spoke, in the tongue of Veles, and pointed one small finger—at Wren's chest, then at her own, then at the window, eastward, where the sun was coming up over a world that had forgotten an entire country and gone on having breakfast.

"I don't understand," Wren said.

Ilma set down the bread. She reached out and took Wren's wrist—small fingers, surprisingly strong—and turned Wren's palm upward, and traced something in it with one fingertip. It tickled. Then Ilma looked up, and said one word in the old tongue, very clearly, the way you say a name.

And the word landed in Wren's ear and went *through* her, the way a key goes through a lock it was cut for, and somewhere deep under her ribs something that had been shut for thirty years shifted on its hinges.

Because she knew that word. Not its meaning—but its sound. She knew it the way you know a lullaby you heard before you could remember. She had heard it in dreams her whole life and never told anyone, because telling anyone would have meant admitting she dreamed at all, and Wren Okonkwo did not admit to anything that might make her seem less perfectly, safely invisible.

The child was still looking at her. Still holding her wrist. Waiting, with that terrible patience, for Wren to understand what she had just been told.

Downstairs, faintly, the guild bell rang the hour, and Wren counted the chimes against the days she had left, and understood that twenty was now the largest number in her life.
