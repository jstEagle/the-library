## 4. Theft of a Person

The decision did not arrive like thunder. It arrived the way rot arrives in timber—from the inside, quietly, until one day the whole beam gives way under a hand that only meant to lean.

Wren had spent three days telling herself she had misheard. That Master Corvane's voice through the wainscoting had said *apprentice* and not *asset*. That the pale figures in the meeting room had been discussing the sale of a *book* the child could read, not of the child herself. She was very good at talking herself out of things; it was, in its way, the guild's first lesson. Make yourself small. Make yourself nothing. A clerk who sees everything and says nothing lives long and dies comfortable.

But Ilma had fallen asleep against Wren's shoulder that evening in the counting room, her small fingers curled around the hem of Wren's sleeve as if it were a rope thrown to someone drowning, and something in Wren's chest—the careful smallness, the practiced nothing—gave way all at once.

They were going to sell her. The way they sold jars of last words and keys that fit no lock yet. The way they sold countries.

Wren sat very still so as not to wake the girl, and looked across the room at the master ledger where it lay chained to its lectern, and understood, with the terrible clarity of three o'clock in the morning, exactly what she was going to do.

* * *

She had picked locks since she was nine. It was the one skill the masters had never thought to notice, because noticing required looking, and looking at Wren had never once occurred to them in eleven years of service. She could walk into a room and walk out of it and leave no impression at all—not even on the dust, sometimes, though she'd never been sure whether that was the guild's magic or simply her nature.

The ledger's chain was old iron, older than the guild hall itself, and it parted for her picks with a sound like a held breath released.

Ilma woke when Wren lifted her, but the child said nothing—only wrapped her arms around Wren's neck and pressed her face into her collarbone, as if this were expected, as if she had been waiting all along for someone to finally pick her up and run.

"The back ways," Wren whispered, mostly to herself. "There's a stair behind the wine cellar that comes out by the coal chute. Nobody uses it. Nobody remembers it."

Ilma lifted her head. In the dark her eyes caught light that wasn't there, faintly, like coins at the bottom of a well. She pointed past Wren's left shoulder—at the wall—and spoke one word in that old tongue. It sounded like *seam*.

"There's nothing there," Wren said. "That's the foundation stone."

Ilma wriggled down, took Wren's hand with the absolute confidence of a child who has decided an adult is hers now, and led her to the wall. She put her palm flat against the stone and pushed, the way you'd push a door you already knew opened inward.

The wall swung.

Cold air poured through—a narrow passage that was not on any plan of the guildhall, lined with brick that glistened faintly as though the building itself were sweating. Beyond it, impossibly, came the smell of rain and river water, though the guild stood a mile from the Vell.

Wren stared. "How—"

Ilma tugged her hand. *Come.* Not in words. In the pull of small fingers and the urgency of someone who has seen doors close before.

Later—much later, on the road—Wren would understand what she was seeing: that the child perceived the seams of the world the way other people saw color. Every wall was fabric to her. Every border, every threshold, every place where one thing ended and another began—all of it stitched loosely, all of it negotiable. And perhaps, Wren would think, that was why Veles had needed her. A country is mostly seam. A country is the place where the land agrees to be itself, over and over, and someone must keep checking the stitches.

But that night she only took up the ledger, heavy as a sleeping animal under her arm, and followed a four-year-old through a door she could not see into a dark that swallowed them both.

* * *

The back ways of the guild were a labyrinth Wren knew better than anyone living. She had mapped them during years of errands nobody assigned: the coal chute with its rusted ladder, the dry cistern, the crawlspace behind the archive ovens where contraband inventory went to be forgotten. She moved through them now with the ledger hugged to her chest and Ilma on her hip, and for two hours she believed—really believed—that being invisible was enough.

Then, in the crawl beneath the east wing, Ilma went rigid in her arms.

"No," the child said—one of her few common-tongue words, and she said it the way sailors say *rocks*.

"What? What is it?"

Ilma pointed forward, into the black. And Wren saw it: light where there should have been none, seeping through a seam in the world that Ilma could apparently see and she could not—a hairline glow, gold-white, like a crack in porcelain filled with morning.

"It's a door," Wren breathed. "Someone's coming through a door."

She backed up. There was nowhere to go. Behind them the crawl bent toward the cistern, but she heard—felt—footsteps there too, unhurried, patient, the footsteps of something that had never once needed to hurry because everything eventually came to it.

The seam split open like an eye.

And out of it stepped her mother.

Not a memory of her mother. Her mother, whole and warm-smelling, in the gray wool coat with the mended elbow, her hair escaping its braid exactly the way it always had, her smile arriving a half-second late the way it always did—as though she had to remember how, each time, before her face agreed.

"Wren," her mother said. "Oh, my love. Look how big you've gotten."

Wren had been seven when her mother died. She had spent eleven years not thinking about it, which is its own kind of drowning, and now the water closed over her head all at once. The ledger slipped. She caught it against the wall, barely. Her legs wanted to fold. Every part of her that had been starved for exactly this—this voice, this coat, this half-second-late smile—leaned forward like a plant toward light.

"You died," Wren heard herself say. Her voice sounded very far away. "I was seven. You died."

"I know." Her mother's face did something complicated—grief worn like a borrowed garment. "It wasn't fair. None of it was fair, sweetheart. But I'm here now, and I've come to take you home. Both of you." Her eyes moved to Ilma, and the warmth in them changed temperature by a single degree. "Especially her. She's tired, isn't she? She wants to rest. Everyone from there wants to rest."

Ilma buried her face in Wren's neck. Her small body was shaking.

And it was the shaking that saved Wren. Because her mother—her real mother, the one in the grave behind the chapel at Marrow's End—had never once made a frightened child shake. Whatever wore the gray coat now, whatever had come through the seam smelling almost right, it had studied love the way a forger studies a signature: closely, competently, and without ever once feeling it.

"You're not her," Wren said.

"Wren—"

"You're wearing her." The words came out steady, and hearing them steady gave her the rest of her spine. "You're collectors. You erased a whole country and you're standing here in my mother's coat asking me to hand over a child, and I want you to know—" her voice cracked, and she let it, and kept going anyway— "I want you to know I see the seam. She would never have smiled like that. She smiled like she meant it or not at all."

Something flickered across the dead woman's face. For an instant the features slid, like oil on water—the coat suddenly too large, the smile suddenly a mechanism—and beneath it Wren glimpsed something tall and patient and hungry, something that had eaten a nation one memory at a time and found the meal insufficient.

Then it smoothed itself back into her mother's face, and that was somehow worse.

"You're invisible, little clerk," it said, in her mother's voice, gently. "No one will miss you. No one will look for you. We have never once needed to chase what no one can see."

"Then you've never been chased *by* one," Wren said, and turned, and ran into the dark with Ilma clinging to her and the ledger beating against her ribs like a second heart.

* * *

Behind her, the thing in her mother's shape did not pursue. That frightened Wren more than pursuit would have. Collectors did not chase; they waited at the far end of every road, because they owned the roads—or rather, because the roads owed them, the way everything owed them, the way the world was one vast ledger with their names written patiently in the margins.

She burst out of the coal chute into the alley behind the tanneries, gasping, Ilma's weight burning her arms, and stopped so abruptly she nearly fell.

The child was pointing again. Down the alley, at the brickwork beside the tanner's gate—another seam, glowing faintly, breathing almost, opening and closing like a gill.

Ilma looked up at her and said something in the old tongue, urgent, insistent, and then—straining, reaching for words that didn't fit her mouth—translated herself: "Door. To... home. Before they find the key."

Wren looked at the seam, then at the ledger under her arm, then at the child whose country had been eaten and whose stitches were coming loose, and understood that the choice she'd made in the counting room was only the down payment. The rest of the debt was about to come due.

"Home," she repeated, tasting the word. She had never once wondered where home was. That was the cruelest part of invisibility—you stop believing you came from anywhere at all.

Ilma took her hand. The seam widened, spilling out air that smelled of rye bread and river reeds and something older than either.

Somewhere behind them, unhurried, patient, footsteps began again.

Wren stepped through.
