## 5. The Road That Forgets Itself

The world outside the guild was wrong, and only Ilma seemed to notice.

She rode pressed against Wren's back on a borrowed mule, her small hands knotted in the woman's coat, and she watched the hedgerows trade places when no one was looking. It happened between blinks. A crooked oak would stand at the crest of a hill, and by the time they crested that same hill, the oak had wandered to the valley floor, or become an elm, or become a stone wall that had always been there, according to the sheep grazing beside it. The sheep did not mind. The road did not mind. Only Ilma's teeth ached, the way they always ached when the world told itself a lie.

*Vess-ah-loom*, she said against Wren's shoulder blade. *The road forgets itself.*

"I know," Wren said, though she didn't. "We're nearly to the crossroads."

They were not. The crossroads had been two miles back, and then it hadn't existed at all, and now there were three roads where the map in Wren's pocket promised one, all of them bending politely toward the low gray hills of the border country as if the land itself wanted them somewhere specific.

Ilma closed her eyes and listened with the part of her that was not quite her — the part that hummed like a struck bell whenever Veles came near. The old tongue lived in that humming. She could not say most of what she knew; her mouth was six years old and the words were older than kingdoms. But names she could carry. Names were small enough for a child.

*Sel-vane,* she whispered, and pointed left.

"That's not a word," Wren said.

Ilma pointed again, insistent. Left. The left road bent away from the hills, toward marshland and dark water, and every instinct in Wren's tired body said *no*. Ilma watched the woman wrestle with it — watched her jaw set, watched her glance back down the way they'd come, where the air had been faintly wrong since dawn, thickened, sweetish, like fruit going off.

Since the ones wearing faces had found them at the guild's edge, Wren had stopped arguing quickly. That was new. At the guild, Wren Okonkwo had argued about everything: inventory counts, candle rations, whether Ilma should sleep in the cellar or the attic. Now she went quiet at the wrong moments and gripped the ledger bag until its brass clasps printed crescents into her palm.

"Left," Wren said finally, hating it. "Fine. Left. If we drown in a bog, I want it noted that I objected."

Ilma almost smiled. Almost. Smiling felt like spending something she might need later.

They took the left road. Within an hour, the wrongness behind them thinned, then snapped like a cut thread. Ilma felt it go and let out a breath she'd been holding since the Collectors' footsteps had sounded in the guild hall — footsteps that had made no sound at all, only pressure, only the memory of sound.

Wren pulled up the mule under a rowan tree gone white with late berries. Her hands shook as she unwrapped bread.

"How," she said. Not *why*. Ilma liked that about her. Wren asked *how*, which meant she believed. "How do you know which way? You've never been here. You've barely been anywhere."

Ilma considered how much truth she could fit into a child-sized answer. She touched her own chest, then spread her fingers wide, taking in the hills, the marsh smell, the sky.

*Veles,* she said, in the tongue that made the rowan berries tremble. Then she pointed at herself. *Veles.*

"You're from there." Wren frowned. "But Veles isn't—"

She stopped. Ilma watched her stop. Watched the sentence die in her mouth like a moth hitting glass. Wren had tried to finish that sentence eleven times since midnight, and eleven times it had died, because the books said *Veles* and the world said *nothing*, and Wren Okonkwo trusted the books the way other people trusted their mothers.

"Isn't on maps," Wren finished, carefully, as if stepping over a gap in a bridge. "I know. Eat your bread."

Ilma ate. The road forgot itself behind them, rearranging, erasing their passage stitch by stitch. Somewhere far off, something patient followed the holes they left.

* * *

Odile's house was not a house. It was a boat that had given up on water and settled into the reeds at the marsh's edge, hull green with moss, chimney poking through the deck like a periscope. Smoke curled from it. A wind chime made of spoons clinked a warning that wasn't a warning.

The old woman who opened the hatch had a face like a riverbed — everything worn smooth except what had drowned. She looked at Wren first, unimpressed. Then at Ilma.

Then she sat down hard on a coil of rope.

"Where did you get that language," Odile said. Her voice had gravel and rust in it. "Say something else. Say it again."

Ilma said the first thing that rose in her, a greeting so old it predated greetings: *Sela-vune, dor-ah-mille.* Peace on your threshold, traveler.

Odile began to cry. Not gently. She cried the way a hull leaks, everywhere at once, and she pressed both hands over her mouth as if she could caulk it.

"I don't—" She swallowed. "I haven't heard that in forty years and I don't remember ever hearing it, do you understand me? They took it. I sold it. There was a man with kind eyes and a scale, and he weighed my memories of Veles like fish at market, and he paid fair, he paid *fair*, and I spent the money and now I have nothing but the shape of the hole." She wiped her face with a wrist scarred by old rope burns. "I remember the harbor. I remember the smell of the spice barges and my mother's voice calling me in for supper in that tongue, and I don't remember a single word she said. He let me keep the smell. Said smells weren't load-bearing."

Wren stood in the doorway with the ledger bag held like a shield. "You knew Veles. You traded there."

"Everyone traded there, girl, before everyone forgot there was a *there* to trade with." Odile looked at Ilma again and flinched, softly, as if the child were bright. "And now here's a little anchor walking around loose in the world with a clerk from the Impossible Trades, and the Collectors will already be counting the days. Sit down. Both of you. You'll want soup before the end of the world arrives, and it will arrive by Thursday."

It arrived early.

* * *

They stayed two nights. Ilma learned the boat's creaks the way she learned everything — instantly, permanently, helplessly. She learned that Odile slept sitting up, that she kept a tin box under the floorboard with a dried sprig of some flower she called *vessa-bloom* and could not explain, that she talked to the wind chime when she thought no one was awake.

On the second night, Ilma woke to voices and did not pretend to be asleep.

"—can't stay past tomorrow," Odile was saying. "The Collectors don't knock. They arrive wearing someone you loved and ask if they can come in, and your own mouth says yes before your brain is consulted. I've seen it done to a man in Ketterdam. His dead brother walked into his shop and by morning the shop belonged to nothing, the man belonged to nothing, and there was just an empty doorway with a draft coming through."

"I know what they look like," Wren said quietly. "One of them wore my mother."

The silence after that was long enough for Ilma to hear the marsh breathing.

"My mother died when I was four," Wren went on. "Fever. I remember the funeral. I remember the specific ugly hat my aunt wore. And this thing stood at the end of the corridor wearing her exact face, her exact voice, and it asked me if I'd missed her." A breath. "It knew I'd say yes. It was very patient about it."

"They're patient about everything," Odile said. "That's what makes them collectors and not thieves. Thieves take. Collectors wait for things to come due." Rope creaked; Ilma pictured the old woman leaning forward over the little stove. "You've read the ledger. Tell me — what does it say about Veles?"

"It says the debt was called in. All of it. Every acre, every name, every—" Wren's voice snagged. "Every memory. Sold to settle accounts the crown ran up over nine hundred years. Wars, mostly. Some bargains with things worse than wars. The last entry says *remainder outstanding: one (1) anchor, juvenile.*"

"One child," Odile said flatly. "A whole country boiled down to one child, and the Guild raised her in a cellar like stock. Like *inventory*." Something cracked — a log, kicked. "I carried barge freight up the Vessa river when it still had a name. I drank plum wine in a harbor town where they lit the water on festival nights so it looked like the sky had fallen in love with itself. Those people are *gone*, Wren. Not dead. Gone. And this guild you served, these masters you set tables for—"

"I know what they are now."

"Do you? Because you're still saying *now*, girl. *Now I know.* As if knowing is the work. Knowing is free. The work is what you do at three in the morning when the Collectors find you, and they will find you, because that ledger you're carrying has your handwriting in it now and handwriting is a scent they can follow across oceans."

Another silence. When Wren spoke again, her voice had changed — smaller, and somehow stronger for it, like a wire drawn thin.

"She talks in her sleep," Wren said. "Did you know that? In that language. Whole sentences. Last night she said the same phrase over and over, and this morning I found it in the oldest ledger — entry dated before the kingdom, before anything. It translates to *the door remembers the way home.*"

Ilma lay in the dark with her heart going like a bird in a box. She hadn't known she spoke in her sleep. She filed it away with the rest of what she couldn't yet say: the harbor, the new moon, the door standing open exactly once more. The words were too big for her mouth. But they were getting less too-big every day, and soon—

Soon she would have to tell the woman with the crescent-marked palms what home cost, and who was allowed through the door, and who was not.

"Sleep," Odile said at last. "Both of you. Tomorrow I dig out my old charts — the ones with the blank space where Veles used to be. Blank spaces are honest, at least. Maps lie prettier."

* * *

The Collectors came at dusk on the third day, and they came wearing kindness.

Ilma felt them before the wind chime did — a sweetness in the air, fruit going off, and then a knock at the boat's hull that was polite, unhurried, three soft raps like a friend.

Through the porthole she saw a figure standing on the marsh path. It wore the face of a young man with a crooked smile, mud-spattered boots, a courier's satchel. It looked harmless. It looked like someone's favorite cousin.

"Delivery!" it called pleasantly. "Package for Odile Vasq, formerly of the Vessa run! Just needs a signature, love. Just needs you to remember one small thing."

Inside the boat, Odile had gone the color of ash. She looked at Wren, and Wren looked at Ilma, and Ilma understood with terrible clarity that whatever happened next would happen fast.

"Back door," Odile whispered. "There isn't one. Make one."

Ilma looked at the hull of the boat — the old, tired wood — and saw what no one else could see: the seam. Faint as a scar in glass, running plank to plank, a place where the world's stitching showed. Behind the seam was the reed-flat, and beyond it the black water, and somewhere in the black water, far downstream, a drowned harbor holding a door that remembered the way home.

She put her hand flat against the wood.

*Sel-vane,* she whispered.

The seam opened like an eye.
