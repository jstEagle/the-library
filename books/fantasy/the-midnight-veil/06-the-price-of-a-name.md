## 6. The Price of a Name

The master ledger weighed more than it should have. Wren had carried ledgers all her life—inventory books, tax rolls, the guild's fat quarterly accounts—and she knew their heft the way a sailor knows rope. This one was different. It sat against her hip like something alive, like a sleeping animal that might wake angry if she moved wrong.

She had not opened it since the night of the theft. There had been no time. There had been Ilma's small cold hand in hers, and the Collectors' footsteps wearing her mother's voice down the guild's back stairs, and three days of mud roads and rearranged countryside with a smuggler named Odile who kept a knife under her tongue and a debt behind her eyes.

Now they were sheltered—truly sheltered, for the first time—in the loft above Odile's trading post, where the rafters hung with dried herbs and contraband candles and the air smelled of tallow and juniper. Ilma slept on a pallet of sacking by the window, curled around a carved wooden bird she would not let anyone else touch. Rain worked at the shutters. The lamp burned low and steady.

Wren set the ledger on the table between them and unlatched its clasp.

"You don't have to read it tonight," Odile said from the doorway. She was mending a net with hands that never stopped moving even when her voice did. "Books like that, they read you back."

"I've spent my whole life being unread," Wren said. "I'd like to know why."

She turned to the beginning, because beginnings were where the guild taught you to look, and because she was still, at heart, a clerk. The earliest pages were written in an ink that shifted color depending on the angle—a deep bruise-purple, then black, then the green of pond water. The script was the masters', but older, the hand of masters long dead.

*Received: one kingdom, entire, in settlement of outstanding balance.*

Her breath went shallow. She turned the page.

*Veles. Assessed: rivers (3), harvests (unnumbered), songs (innumerable), names (all). Paid in full by parties unknown. Remaining liability: one anchor, minor, female. To be collected when located.*

Below that, in a later hand—the current First Master's, she recognized his cramped, downward-slanting letters—someone had added:

*Anchor located. Buyer notified. Do not let the girl learn the word.*

The word. *Veles.* The word they had forbidden her to speak. Her mouth went dry around it now.

"Odile." Her voice came out strange, flattened. "You said you traded in Veles before it was eaten."

"I said I remembered fragments I was paid to forget." Odile did not look up from her net. "There's a difference."

"What did Veles smell like?"

That made the old woman stop. The net sagged between her fists. For a long moment there was only rain, and the lamp's small breathing flame, and then Odile said, quietly, as if reciting something she'd buried so deep she'd forgotten the digging: "Bread baking over river stones. And thunder, even on clear days. They said the country hummed to itself. You could feel it through your boots if you stood still long enough." Her jaw tightened. "Why do you ask me that? Why that question?"

Wren didn't answer. She was turning pages faster now, past decades of entries—debts of weather, debts of memory, a village sold twice, a sea bought and resold—and her fingers had gone cold, because clerks develop instincts for where things are filed, and some instinct older than training was pulling her toward the middle of the book, toward a date thirty-odd years past.

She found it on a page whose margins were stained with what might have been rain or might have been tears.

*Item received: one infant, female, born Veles. Bloodline confirmed—anchor stock, third generation. Smuggled out via the river gate by the mother. Toll assessed for passage beyond the border: one name.*

And beneath, in the same handwriting, almost tender, the way accountants are tender with figures:

*Toll paid: the child's name, entire. Balance owed by bearer: none. Note—bearer will pass unseen. Recommend placement where invisibility will cause no inquiry.*

Wren read it four times. The words did not change. That was the terrible thing about words; they stayed exactly what they were while everything around them collapsed.

"My name," she said. She heard her own voice from very far away. "It isn't that people overlook me. It's that they *can't*—" She stopped, started again. "I used to think it was me. Something wrong with me. I'd say a thing in a meeting and two minutes later the masters would propose it as if it were their own idea, and I thought—I trained myself not to mind. I got *good* at not minding. I built my whole self out of not minding."

"Wren—"

"That's not my name." She touched the page, the entry, the neat accounting of her own erasure. "Or it is, but it cost them nothing. It's a place-holder. A clerk's mark. My real name is *in here*, paid out like coin, and every time someone looked straight through me for twenty-nine years, that's why. That's the interest on the debt." She laughed, one dry note with nothing behind it. "I thought I was invisible because I was small. Because I was careful. Because I was good at being a shadow. I made a *virtue* of it. I polished it. Gods, I was proud of it."

Ilma stirred on her pallet. The child sat up, hair wild, eyes finding Wren across the dim room with that unnerving directness she had—as if she saw through skin to whatever was underneath. She said something in the old tongue, soft and questioning.

"She asks if you are hurt," Odile translated. Then, wry and sad: "I keep surprising myself, knowing that."

"I'm fine," Wren said automatically, and then, because the child was still watching, because Ilma was perhaps the only person alive who could see her clearly enough to catch the lie, she amended: "No. No, I'm not. But I will be."

Ilma slid off the pallet, crossed the room barefoot, and climbed onto the chair beside her. Small fingers pressed against the ledger page—not reading; Wren doubted the child could read the trade-script—but touching, the way you touch a scar. Then Ilma looked up at her and said one word, slow and deliberate, in the old language, and pointed at Wren's chest.

Odile had gone very still. "What did she say?"

"You tell me. You're the translator."

"I'm a smuggler with holes in her head." But Odile leaned closer, listening as Ilma repeated the word, and the color drained out of her face the way dye drains out of cloth in a river—gradually, then all at once. "She called you *krajica*. It means—it means homeland. Or it can mean *the one who stays*. It's both words. In Velesi they're the same word." She sat back hard. "Child, how do you know that word belongs to her?"

Ilma answered at length, gesturing between Wren and the ledger, between Wren and the window, between Wren and herself, and her voice rose with the frustration of someone trying to explain color to the blind, and finally she pressed both palms flat against Wren's cheeks and held them there, and Wren felt—she would swear to the end of her days she felt it—a low hum, like thunder on a clear day, like a country humming to itself through the soles of her boots.

"Oh," Wren said. "Oh, you can hear it too."

"The anchor blood," Odile whispered. "It doesn't wash out. It doesn't wear off. They took your name, girl, but they couldn't take what's underneath it. You've been carrying Veles around inside you your whole life without knowing its shape." She laughed, harshly. "That's why you could read those ledgers' oldest sections. That's why the old scripts came easy when the other apprentices struggled. We all wondered. We thought you were just—"

"Just a glorified clerk," Wren finished. "Yes. I know what they thought."

* * *

They talked until the lamp burned down to a thumb's width. Odile told her what she remembered of the river gate, of mothers paying passage in whatever currency they had—names, memories, years off their lives—and of the night the maps went blank and the roads bent away and everyone woke up unable to say what they'd lost, only that there was a hole in the world shaped like a country. Odile had been paid well to forget. She'd taken the money. She'd spent thirty years not asking herself what she'd bought with it.

"You couldn't have fought them," Wren said, when the old woman's voice cracked on some half-recovered face, some half-remembered name.

"No," Odile agreed. "But I could have *wondered*. Wondering's free." She looked at Ilma, asleep again now against Wren's side, the wooden bird clutched to her chest. "And now there's a child in my loft who is the last living piece of the thing I helped erase, and a woman who is apparently its walking heartbeat, and somewhere out in that rain are collectors who wear dead mothers' faces. So here's what happens next, and don't argue with me, I've argued with worse than you."

"Odile."

"They'll come for me first. Helping you was a breach; I signed papers once, stupid and young, and breaches get settled." She said it lightly, the way people say terrible things when they've decided to mean them. "When they come, you take the back stair, you take the girl, and you go to the drowned harbor. Don't ask me how I know about the harbor. Some fragments pay rent even after you forget the house."

"I'm not leaving you to be collected."

"You're leaving me to be *remembered*, which is different." Odile smiled, and it transformed her face into something younger, something from before the forgetting. "Thirty years I've been a hole in the world. Let me at least be a loud one. Now listen—the harbor door opens at the new moon, and only then, and only for Veles blood. You have four nights. And Wren—" she caught her wrist, grip iron despite her age, "—whatever the ledger says you owe, whatever they tell you you're worth, you were never invisible. You were *hidden*. There's a world of difference, and hidden things can be found."

The knock came an hour before dawn. Three knocks, unhurried, polite—and then a voice from below that stopped Wren's heart in her chest, because it belonged to the First Master, except the First Master was a week's ride away and this voice had a wet, borrowed quality, like speech played through a drowned throat.

"Madame Odile," the voice said pleasantly. "We understand you've been keeping broken accounts."

Odile was already moving—shoving the ledger into Wren's arms, pressing a folded scrap of paper into her palm, steering her toward the back stair with a firmness that permitted no discussion. At the top of the stair she paused. In the lamplight her eyes were bright and clear and entirely her own.

"Bread bakes over river stones," she said. "Thunder on clear days. Remember it for me, girl. That's all I ask."

Then she was gone down the stairs, and the front door opened, and Wren ran—through the herb-lofts and the candle racks, down the swaying back stair, out into the rain with Ilma's hand locked in hers and the ledger beating against her ribs like a second heart. Behind her she heard voices, courteous and terrible, and then a sound like a page being torn cleanly from a book.

She did not look back. Looking back was a luxury for people whose names hadn't been spent.

At the tree line she stopped, shaking, and unfolded the scrap Odile had pressed into her hand. On it, in a smuggler's blunt hand, were two words in the old tongue and, beneath them, a sketch: a drowned harbor, a door standing open in the water, and a moon with one bite missing.

New moon. Four nights.

Beside her, Ilma tugged her sleeve and pointed east, into the dark, and said—with the certainty of a child stating the obvious—one word Wren now understood in her bones, in the humming Veles-blood of her, in the name-shaped hollow where her true self had been sold away:

*Home.*
