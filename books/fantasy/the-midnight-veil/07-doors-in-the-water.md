## 7. Doors in the Water

Ilma counted the days by the way the water moved.

Not by suns, which came and went like guests who wouldn't commit to staying. Not by Wren's careful tally marks scratched into a stick, which Ilma had secretly corrected twice because Wren lost count whenever she was frightened, and she was frightened most of the time now, though she pretended otherwise with her jaw set like a door being barred.

The water told the truth. Water always did. It remembered everything it had ever touched, which was why the Collectors hated it, and why they had drowned half of Veles rather than let it keep speaking.

They were camped above a river that didn't know its own name anymore. Ilma could feel it trying — the little silver tongue of it lapping at the stones, tasting for a word that had been scraped off the world like wax off a seal. She sat on the bank with her feet in the shallows and listened until the river calmed, and then she said, in the old tongue, *you were called Vezha, and your mother was the mountain, and you are allowed to be tired.*

The river shivered. Somewhere downstream, a fish jumped that hadn't been there a moment before.

Behind her, Wren was doing her nightly ritual: reading the master ledger by candlelight, running her thumb down columns of debts as if she could pay them all herself through sheer stubbornness. She'd been doing this every night since Odile was taken. She read like someone looking for their own name in a list of the dead.

Ilma knew what Wren would find in there. She had known since the guild, since the night of the locked room, when the words finally came loose in her chest like a tooth that had been hurting for years. But knowing a thing and saying it were different trades entirely, and saying it meant the choice stopped being hers alone.

She wasn't sure she wanted company in that choice. That was the terrible part. She had been alone so long that alone had become a country too, and she was its only citizen, and part of her wanted to defend its borders.

But Wren had slept badly last night, muttering in a language she didn't know she knew. And this morning she had caught a spider's web on her sleeve and instead of brushing it away she'd looked at it for a long moment, at the way the threads held the light, and something in her face had gone soft and wrecked and recognizing.

Tonight, Ilma decided. Tonight she would say it.

* * *

The fire was small because fires drew things. Wren fed it one twig at a time, hoarding light like coin, and the ledger lay closed beside her with the weight of a sleeping animal.

"Ilma." Wren's voice was careful. It was always careful now, ever since the Collectors had come wearing her mother's face. "You've been watching me read it. Every night."

Ilma pulled her knees up. The old tongue rose in her throat first, the way it always did, the words for grief and debt and door — but she made it come out in Wren's language, slow and clumsy, like carrying water in cupped hands.

"You read the book wrong," Ilma said.

Wren blinked. "Wrong?"

"You look for Veles in it. You look and look. But Veles is not *in* the book." Ilma pressed her palm flat against the ledger's cover, and felt the hum of it, the way you'd feel a hive through a wall. "Veles is what the book is *made from*. All the pages. All the paper. A country is a lot of things, Wren Okonkwo. One of them is a promise everyone made together without deciding to. When the Collectors eat a country, they eat the promise. They leave the meat and take the meaning." She looked up. "This book is full of meaning nobody claimed. So they bound it into pages and sold it back to your guild, and your guild sold it onward, piece by piece, for two hundred years. Your masters never owned treasure. They owned leftovers."

The fire popped. Wren had gone very still, the stillness of a person standing at the edge of a floor that might not be a floor.

"Then what are you?" Wren asked quietly. "To them. To it."

And here it was. Here was the tooth coming out.

Ilma reached into her shirt and drew out the thing she wore on a cord around her neck: a small stone, river-gray, unremarkable, except that it cast no reflection in anything — not water, not glass, not Wren's eyes when Ilma held it up between them. She set it on the ground between them, and the firelight bent around it politely, the way crowds bend around royalty.

"When a country is eaten," Ilma said, "everything must go. Every word, every grave, every song. But a country cannot be fully eaten while one living thing still carries it. One anchor. One stone left unturned." She touched the cord at her neck again, though the stone lay on the ground. "My mother was the last keeper of the harbor records. When the Collectors came over the water, she put the whole remainder of Veles — every scrap they couldn't reach yet — into me. I was four years old. I remember it as being given a very heavy coat." Her voice did not waver; she had practiced not wavering for six years. "I have been walking ever since. Away from them. Carrying my country inside my ribs like smuggled bread."

"And if they catch you—"

"Then they take the coat off me." Ilma met her eyes. "And Veles dies forever. Not fades. Not sleeps. Dies. There will be nothing left to wake. Even the books will go blank, page by page, starting from the oldest. Your guild will burn through the night and by morning the ledgers will be empty and no one will remember why they're sad."

Wren's hand had found the ledger without her seeming to notice, pulling it close, guarding it. Ilma almost smiled. Two hundred years of leftovers, and the clerk guarded them like kin.

"But," Ilma said, and this was the harder half, "there is another ending. If I go home — if I cross back into Veles and stay — then the country lives. Slowly. Like a body knitting a wound. The rivers get their names back. The graves remember their dead. But an anchor does not get to move, Wren. An anchor is a thing that holds so other things can drift free. If I go in, I stay. Until I die there. Alone, in a country of returning ghosts, keeping the lights on so a place can remember itself." She swallowed. "I am nine years old. I have done the arithmetic many times."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for the fire to eat itself down to a red eye.

Wren didn't say *you can't*, which would have been kind and useless. She didn't say *we'll find another way*, which would have been a lie, and Ilma had learned to hear lies the way she heard rivers. Instead Wren picked up a twig and began drawing in the dirt — the harbor, Ilma realized. The drowned harbor of Veles, rendered from memory, from the coat of names she carried, though Wren couldn't possibly have known its shape.

"The door out," Wren said. "You mentioned a door. In the old ledger entries there was always a toll and a threshold."

"The harbor gate." Ilma crouched beside her and corrected the drawing with one finger — the breakwater curved the other way, the lighthouse had fallen toward the sea, not away. "It opens twice a year. Once at the harvest moon, once at the new moon after midwinter." She felt the words settle heavily as she said them. "The new moon is in five days. And it opens *outward* only. Things may leave Veles. Nothing returns through it. Except—" she hesitated, "except an anchor. The gate knows its own."

"So if you walk through—"

"I walk through and the gate shuts behind me and the sea closes over it and I am home forever." Ilma traced the little lighthouse in the dirt. "And if I don't, the gate rots shut for good, and I run until the Collectors catch me, and Veles dies anyway. Just later. Louder."

"There has to be—" Wren started, and stopped, because Ilma was shaking her head.

"I used to think that too. For the first year I thought it every day. *There has to be.*" Ilma poked the fire up. "But here is what I learned about doors, Wren. Doors are honest. People aren't. A door tells you exactly what it is: open or shut, in or out. It's people who stand in doorways pretending they're bridges." She looked at Wren sideways. "You've spent your whole life being a doorway nobody walked through. I can see it. You stand in rooms and the rooms forget you. I know that feeling from the inside. It is the loneliest architecture in the world."

Wren flinched as if struck. The twig snapped in her fingers.

"It's not a tragedy," Wren said finally, low. "It's just how I'm made. Some people are loud and some people are load-bearing."

"That's not how you're made." Ilma said it simply, the way children say devastating things. "That's how you're *priced*. There's a difference. I've read enough of the ledger through your shoulder to know the guild prices everything, even people. Especially people." She reached over and took Wren's hand — small fingers over calloused ones — and turned it palm-up. "Look. Really look. What do you see?"

Wren looked. Her palm was lined and ink-stained and ordinary.

"Nothing," she said.

"*Exactly,*" Ilma said, fierce suddenly, leaning in. "Nothing! Do you know what that means? Everyone else, everyone the Collectors have touched, has marks on them — debts written into the skin, tolls, liens, ownership stamps. I've seen them. Odile's hands were covered in paid-and-unpaid. Your masters' hands must be black with them. But yours are clean. Clean as unwritten paper." Her grip tightened. "You're not invisible, Wren. You're *blank*. And blank isn't nothing. Blank is where the story hasn't been written yet. Blank is the first page."

The fire crackled. Far off in the dark, the nameless river ran on, carrying its forgotten name toward the sea.

Wren stared at her open hand for a long, long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed — quieter, but with something underneath it that hadn't been there before, something with weight and edges.

"In the ledger," she said slowly, "there's an entry from twenty-two years ago. A birth. A crossing. A payment." She looked up at Ilma, and her eyes were wet and burning at once. "I haven't been able to make myself read past the first line. Because I think I already know whose birth it is."

Ilma's heart, the heavy-coated, country-bearing heart of her, went very still.

Five days, she thought. Five days to the new moon. Five days to the drowned harbor, where the sea kept the only door, and the door kept the only truth, and the truth kept them both.

"Then we read it together," Ilma said. "Whatever it says. We read it on the road, walking, because we can't stop walking now." She stood, brushing dirt from her knees, and pointed east, where the land fell away toward low gray hills and, beyond them, a coastline that three countries' maps refused to draw. "Because tomorrow we reach the salt marshes, and the marshes are where the Collectors keep their *market*—"

The fire died.

Not faded — *died*, snuffed flat, all at once, as if the night had leaned down and blown it out. And standing at the edge of the firelight, at the exact spot where the darkness gave way to darker, were three figures that had not been there a heartbeat before.

One of them stepped forward, and it had Wren's mother's face, and it smiled with Wren's mother's mouth, and said in a voice like a ledger closing:

"We'd hoped to do this gently, little anchor. But the market moves tonight — and you two are late for your *appraisal*."
