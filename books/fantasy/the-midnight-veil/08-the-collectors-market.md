## 8. The Collectors' Market

The border town was called Havel's Rest, though nothing in it seemed to rest. It clung to the edge of the map like a barnacle, all crooked lanes and shuttered windows, a place where smugglers drank and soldiers pretended not to see them. The kind of town, Wren thought, where a person could disappear.

Which was precisely the problem. She had spent her whole life disappearing without trying. Now she needed the opposite: she needed to be found—by the right people, on her terms—and she had no idea how to be visible on purpose.

Ilma slept against her shoulder on the floor of an abandoned dye-house, curled in a nest of burlap that still smelled faintly of madder root. The child's breathing was slow and even, her small hands slack around a scrap of cloth Odile had given her before the Collectors took the woman away. Wren had not let herself think about Odile for more than three seconds at a time. Three seconds of grief was survivable. More than that, and it swallowed her whole, and Ilma needed her whole.

She opened the master ledger across her knees by the light of a single stub of candle. The pages whispered under her fingers, that dry paper voice she had known since childhood, since the first time Master Corvel had put a quill in her hand and said, *You have tidy handwriting, girl. That is your entire value here.*

Even now, insult and comfort tangled together in the same memory.

The ledger lay open to the page she'd been circling for two days: the entry for Veles. Not the country's sale—that page was older, bound in something that wasn't quite leather—but the entry for its remainder. *Balance outstanding: one anchor.* And beneath it, in ink that shifted color depending on the angle of the light, the Collectors' standing offer. They had written it into the book itself, patient as mold.

*Terms negotiable at market.*

Wren looked down at the sleeping child and made the decision she had been walking toward since the guild's door closed behind them.

*All right,* she thought. *Let's go to market.*

* * *

Havel's Rest had a marketplace the way a wound has a scab—a hard crust over something raw. By day it sold turnips and tin pots and pirated songs. But there was a corner of it, behind the tannery, where the stalls sold stranger things: bottled weather, secondhand shadows, luck by the pound. Wren had heard of such places her whole life and dismissed them as guild legend told to frighten junior clerks.

Standing in that corner at dusk, she understood that everything frightening she'd ever been told had been true.

She set the ledger on a trestle table between two stalls selling pickled eels and mourning veils. Then she did the hardest thing she had ever done, harder than theft, harder than flight: she stood up straight, lifted her chin, and made herself look like someone worth noticing.

"I'm selling," she announced, to no one and everyone. "One master ledger. Guild of Impossible Trades. Complete holdings. Debts owed by the world itself."

The market didn't stop. That was the terrible elegance of this place—nothing stopped, nothing stared, everyone had seen worse. A woman haggling over a jar of captured thunder glanced at Wren once and went back to her argument. A man buying a shadow tried it on against his own and frowned.

But Wren felt it anyway, like a change in pressure. The way air feels before lightning.

They came at full dark.

She knew them instantly, and knowing did nothing to blunt the horror. There were five of them, arranged around her stall with the casual geometry of people who had never been ambushed in their lives. One wore the face of Master Corvel. One wore the face of the baker from the street where Wren had grown up, a woman who used to slip her day-old rolls through the window because she could see the thinness in Wren's cheeks when nobody else bothered looking.

And the tallest of them—the one whose hands were folded like a patient merchant's—wore her mother's face.

Wren had no memories of her mother. That was rather the point. But the ledger had shown her a portrait sketched in its margins, ink-stubbed and faded, a woman with high cheekbones and a stubborn mouth, and this creature wore that face the way you'd wear a coat taken off a hook. Correct in every line. Empty in every way that mattered.

"Good evening, seller," said the one wearing her mother's face. Her mother's voice, too—warm, low, amused. Where had they gotten the voice? From the ledger? From the country itself? "You've brought us something we've wanted for a very long time."

"The ledger," Wren said. Her own voice sounded small and flat in her ears, clerkish, and she hated it and leaned into it, because clerks were underestimated and she had been a clerk her whole life. "Complete records. Every debt the world owes. You know what it's worth."

"We do." The mother-faced Collector tilted its head. "And what do you want for it?"

"The debt canceled. All of it. The balance outstanding on Veles—closed. The child walks free, unclaimed, forever. No collector touches her in any lifetime."

Around her, the market continued its indifferent commerce, but the space around their little circle had gone strangely quiet, as if sound itself had decided not to get involved. One of the Collectors—the Corvel one—made a soft clicking noise, like scales being adjusted.

"That is a great deal of value," said her mother's face gently. "You understand we cannot simply give it away."

"Then we have nothing to discuss." Wren reached for the ledger.

"Wait." The word came out warm as bathwater. The mother-faced Collector smiled, and it was the smile from the portrait, the stubborn mouth curved upward, and Wren felt it land somewhere beneath her ribs with a force that frightened her more than any threat could have. "Before you walk away. Let us make you a proper counteroffer. You came to market. Hear the goods."

It gestured, and the market changed.

Not dramatically—nothing so vulgar as fire or transformation. But the pickled-eel stall became a fruit stall heaped with white cherries from orchards Wren had never seen and somehow remembered. The tannery smell lifted, replaced by woodsmoke and river water and bread baking in stone ovens. Somewhere beyond the stalls, bells rang in a pattern she knew in her bones and had never once heard.

"This is what remains," the Collector said softly, in her mother's voice. "This is what we hold. Veles. Not destroyed—*held*. In trust. Every field. Every harbor. Every grave and every cradle. We are not monsters, seller. We are creditors. We keep what we are owed in excellent condition."

The candlelight bent. For a moment Wren stood in a market square in another country entirely, one that existed nowhere on any map, and the cobbles under her feet were warm from a summer day decades gone, and an old man was arguing cheerfully about the price of fish in a language that Ilma spoke in her sleep.

Her eyes burned.

"You," the Collector continued, "were born there. You know this. You read it in the book. And here is what you may not yet understand, so we will be generous and explain: the debt works both ways. Veles owes us. But we—" the smile widened by a degree— "*owe Veles*. A country cannot simply die. It must be wound down properly, its accounts settled, its last assets distributed. Its last daughter restored to her inheritance."

"Last daughter," Wren repeated. Her mouth had gone dry.

"Wren Okonkwo. Born in the harbor district of Vellisar-on-the-Deep, in the spring of the last full year." The name landed on her like a hand on her shoulder—the first time in her life anyone had said it like it mattered. "Your parents paid a terrible price to send you out. Your visibility. Your place in the world's memory. You have spent twenty years unseen, unnoticed, overlooked. We can return every coin of it. Your name, restored. Your face, remembered by everyone who has ever forgotten it. And your homeland—" the Collector spread her mother's hands, and the ghost-market bloomed wider, bells and bread and river water— "alive again. Whole. On every map. In every mouth. All of it, if you simply complete the transaction as intended."

"And the child?"

"Is the remainder of an account," the Collector said, with the mild patience of a banker explaining interest. "Accounts must be settled. She comes to us, and everything else comes back to you."

The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

And Wren stood in the middle of the trap and let it close around her, because that was the thing about traps built from longing—they only worked if the bait was real, and oh, the bait was real. She could feel it. Twenty years of being furniture, of masters talking over her head, of lovers forgetting her name mid-sentence, of doing the work and watching others take the credit—all of it rose up in her at once like floodwater, and on top of the flood floated the image of a country coming back to life, her country, bells ringing, bread rising, her mother's actual laugh somewhere in it instead of this hollow imitation—

Behind the trestle table, in the nest of burlap, Ilma stirred and whimpered in her sleep. A small sound. Nothing.

Everything.

Because Wren had heard that whimper before, in a dozen variations, every night since the guild. It was the sound of a person dreaming about being alone. And Wren knew exactly what that dream was like, because she'd been having it her whole life, and she would not—not for maps, not for names, not for her mother's stolen smile worn by a thing that ate countries—she would not be the reason a child dreamed that dream forever.

"No," Wren said.

The market froze. Truly froze this time—mid-haggle, mid-step, a pigeon hanging in the air between wingbeats. Five Collectors turned their borrowed faces toward her.

"The ledger stays with me," Wren said, and her voice didn't shake, which surprised her; apparently there was a part of her that only worked when everything else was breaking. "And the child stays with me. Those aren't terms. Those are facts. What I'm offering hasn't changed: the ledger for her freedom. Take it or find me at the next market, and the one after that, because I will spend the rest of my life making this sale, and I am extremely good at paperwork."

Silence. Then the Collector wearing Corvel's face laughed—a dry, papery sound, genuinely delighted. "Oh, they wasted you at the guild," it said. "They truly did."

The mother-faced one studied her for a long moment. When it spoke again, all the warmth was gone, and somehow that was a relief—it was easier to refuse a cold thing than a warm one.

"You misunderstand the nature of markets, seller. We do not take refusal personally. We simply note it." It produced, from nowhere, a slip of paper and laid it on the trestle beside the ledger. "Our final position. The ledger for the anchor's freedom—or, should you decline, know that the drowned harbor opens at the new moon, and we will be there. So will our colleagues. All of them. Bring your paperwork."

Then the market exhaled, and it was just a border-town market again, turnips and tin pots and pirated songs, and the five of them were five strangers drifting into the crowd, wearing faces that belonged to other people.

Wren stood shaking, one hand pressed flat on the ledger, until a small weight collided with her hip. Ilma, awake, hair sleep-tangled, blinking up at her with those old, old eyes.

"They showed you home," the child said quietly, in the old tongue, and then—in careful, accented common, words Odile must have taught her—"I saw your face. It looked like the sky before rain."

"It's going to rain," Wren agreed. She picked up the slip of paper the Collector had left and read it twice. Then she looked east, toward the coast, toward a drowned harbor that would open in four days under a moon that wouldn't be there.

"Pack up, Ilma," she said. "We're going to need Odile's map after all. And we're going to need to be faster than the weather."

Because the paper in her hand was not a contract at all. It was a receipt—one itemized line, dated four days hence:

*Received in good faith: one keeper, pending delivery at the door out of Veles.*

They weren't waiting to buy anymore.

They were coming to collect.
