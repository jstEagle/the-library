## 10. The Ledger Reborn

The harbor fills at dawn now.

Not with ships—there are no ships yet, and may not be for years—but with light. It comes up over the drowned quays the way water comes into a dry channel, slow at first, then all at once, pooling in the streets of a city that spent twenty years forgetting its own name. The houses remember. I can hear them doing it, some mornings, a creaking and settling like an old man stretching after sleep, timber remembering it was oak, stone remembering it was quarried by hands that sang while they worked. Veles is re-knitting itself one thread at a time, and every thread hums.

I stand on the harbor wall where the door used to be—the real door, the one Ilma walked through with her bundle of letters and her loaf of bread still warm—and I watch my country practice being real.

My country. I am still not used to the possessive. For twenty-three years I was no one's daughter from no place in particular, a girl shaped like a gap in a crowd. Now I am the anchor of a nation, which sounds grander than it is. In truth it means I stand very still sometimes. It means when the Collectors come prowling along the edges of the world, looking for the seam they thought they'd sewn shut, they find me instead—a woman with no name to take, standing in a country with no debt left to collect—and they pass over us like weather.

That was the trick of it, in the end. The thing I paid them could not be taken back because there was nothing left to take.

I write this in the master ledger, or what remains of it. When I wrote my name into its pages at the drowned harbor—*paid in full: one Wren Okonkwo, purchased freedom of one Ilma of Veles, balance settled*—the book did not close. That surprised me. I had expected it to die with the old guild, to go up in smoke and guilt. Instead it shuddered, shed its binding like a snake shedding skin, and came out smaller. Thinner. Honest. The debts owed *by* the world are gone; the Collectors hold nothing on us now. What's left are only the debts we owe each other, which is a different kind of arithmetic entirely, and one I am learning slowly, in a hand that grows more confident by the season.

The first page reads: *Received into keeping, this ledger, by the nameless keeper of Veles.* Below it, in Ilma's careful child's script: *and her friends.*

There are more friends than I ever expected to have.

* * *

Ilma comes through the door on the new moon, as she always does, though the door is hers to open anywhere along the coast now—it simply likes the harbor best, and so do I. She arrives the way she arrived at the guild's midnight doorstep years ago: without warning, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and the sea, speaking first in the old tongue before remembering herself and switching to mine.

"You're standing in the same spot," she says, dropping her satchel on the quay. "You always stand in the same spot."

"It's a good spot."

"It's a stubborn spot." She is eleven now, tall for it, her braid threaded with river-glass beads that Odile would have called contraband and then stolen for herself. She digs in the satchel and comes up with bread wrapped in cloth, still warm, which should be impossible given the distance she's traveled, but the door has opinions about warmth and always lands her on the near side of fresh. "From Maren. She says you don't write enough. She says—" Ilma squints at the letter she produces next, reading aloud in a fair imitation of the smuggler's rasp, "'Tell our girl the roof on the east wing is fixed, the novices are less useless, and if she doesn't visit by midsummer I'm coming through that door myself, treaty or no treaty.'"

"Threats," I say, taking the letter.

"Promises. There's a difference, and Maren will teach it to you personally if you make her cross."

The guild is a guild again, though it bears little resemblance to the one I grew up in. The Impossible Trades still trade in impossibilities—a jar of last words sits on Maren's shelf, waiting for someone brave enough to open it; the key that fits no lock yet hangs by the fire, and last spring it fit a lock at last, opening a cellar full of things people had hidden from themselves—but the ledgers record gifts now alongside sales. Debts forgiven get their own column. The masters are gone; the ones who didn't flee when the Collectors' market collapsed stayed and were remade by the work, and the ones who came after were never taught that people can be inventory. They are taught instead by Ilma's letters, which arrive monthly and are read aloud in the common room like scripture, and which usually consist of three parts news from Veles, two parts insults aimed at whoever is slacking, and one part recipe.

She empties the rest of the satchel onto the stones between us: letters, a jar of honey, a child's drawing of the harbor with the door drawn as a bright rectangle, a pressed flower I don't recognize until I do—velestar, the blue one that grows only in our high valleys, the one the botanists say shouldn't exist anymore and yet does, spreading downhill year by year like a rumor nobody thought to stop.

"The valley farms took harvest," she reports, settling beside me with her legs dangling over the water. "Real harvest. Grandmother Sela cried. The bridge at Kostanj is rebuilt—well, mostly rebuilt, the middle keeps un-building itself, but everyone agrees that's charming. And the school opened. Thirty-one children." She holds up the number on her fingers, then folds one down. "Thirty-two now. A baby born Tuesday. First one born *inside* since the erasing. They named her after you."

"They couldn't have. I don't have a name."

"They named her after your *namelessness*," Ilma says, with the patience of the very young for the very dense. "The word for it. *Vetra.* It means 'the quiet one who stays.' Apparently it also means 'hearth.' Our language is efficient that way." She bumps her shoulder against mine. "So you're a hearth now. Congratulations."

I look out at the light filling the harbor and I do not manage to say anything for a while, and Ilma, who knows me better than anyone alive, lets the silence be what it is.

* * *

In the afternoon we walk the long road inland, the one that forgets itself a little less each season. There are still places where the country hasn't finished coming back—fields that lie flat and gray as uncut paper, a village whose church bell rings a silence—but they grow fewer. Ilma walks ahead of me with her arms spread along the fence posts, and where her fingers trail, the fences bloom.

"You could come out," she says, not turning around. It's an old conversation; we have it every visit, gently, the way you touch a bruise to check if it's healing. "Through the door. Maren would keep the ledger. You could see the sea from the other side. You could see *anything*. Nobody would erase you now. The debt's dead."

"I know."

"You could be somebody again. Somebody with a name."

And here is the thing I have learned, living in a country that chose to let itself die rather than sell its children, and then chose—because a nameless clerk refused to be invisible—that it did not have to die after all: I spent twenty-three years believing that being seen was something done *to* you. That recognition was a door other people opened or kept shut. But standing at that drowned harbor with the pen in my hand, writing myself out of the Collectors' reach, I discovered that visibility can also be something you *do*. I gave away the name they'd use to own me, and what was left over—what remained standing on the quay when the ink dried—was not a hole. It was a person. Just a person, unencumbered, the shape of me and nothing else, and it turned out that was enough. It had been enough all along. The guild just never taught me how to read myself that way.

"I am somebody," I tell her. "I'm the keeper of a ledger that forgives debts. I'm the hearth. I'm thirty-second on a list of names starting with a baby called Vetra." I catch up to her and take her hand, and we walk together through a field that is remembering how to be wheat. "Besides, someone has to stay. That was the whole bargain, little anchor. You bought the door. I hold it open."

"One day," she says, "you'll change your mind."

"Perhaps." I squeeze her hand. "But not today."

The bell of the half-remembered church rings behind us—not a silence this time, but an actual note, rusty and true—and Ilma laughs, that bright sound that started all of this on a midnight doorstep, and turns toward home.

Behind us, in the gray field, the first green shoots come up through the paper-flat ground, following in our footsteps like small, patient debts, being repaid.
