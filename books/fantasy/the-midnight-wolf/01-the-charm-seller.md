## 1. The Charm Seller

The bells of Saint Verity rang seven times before the fog lifted off the canal, and by the third stroke Marguerite Whitlock was already at her stall in Tallowmarket, laying out her wares the way a liar lays out her alibi: carefully, and where everyone could see.

Luck-knots first. She braided them from river-reed and red thread, three strands over two, two over three, whispering nothing at all while she worked — or almost nothing. A breath shaped into a word. A word shaped into a wish. Anyone watching would see only a woman's hands moving, quick and practiced, the way any weaver's hands moved. The magic lived in the space between the motions, in the pause where a knot tightened, and that pause was so small that even she sometimes pretended it wasn't there.

Twelve years of pretending.

She arranged the knots in a shallow basket lined with wool, then the fever-charms beside them: little pouches of grey linen stuffed with dried yarrow and a sliver of rowan wood, each one stitched shut with a single hidden stitch that held more than thread. Then the sleep-sachets, the colic-rattles for crying infants, the small tin charms sailors nailed above their bunks to keep the dreams from drowning them. Nothing grand. Nothing that would make an Inquisitor's list. If you wanted fire called down from a clear sky or a curse laid on your enemy's firstborn, Greyhollow had no one to sell you that — not anymore, not since the Sundering War had burned the great guilds down to their foundations and left the survivors to practice their art the way mice practiced theirs: quietly, in walls, at night, in the margins of ordinary lives.

Marguerite had made an art of the margin.

"Morning, Maggie," called Oren Petch from the fish stall two down, already gutting carp in a pink mist of scales and ice-water. "Cold enough for you?"

"Cold enough," she said, and meant it as a charm. He laughed, because he always laughed, because he half-knew and had decided long ago that knowing was a door better left shut. Half the market was like that. They bought her luck-knots before voyages and her fever-charms when their children burned at night, and they paid her in coppers and kindness, and they never once asked what it was she actually did between the stitches. Asking would have made it real. Real things could be taken away.

She sold forty-one knots before noon. She counted them the way she counted everything — twice, silently, the numbers kept like coins in a purse no one could see. Forty-one small wishes sent out into the city on red thread: safe passage for a bargeman, a fair price for a widow's goat, a mending for the crack running through the Petches' marriage that they'd never say aloud but had both, separately, come to her about. She never asked what they wished for. That was part of the bargain she'd struck with herself, the treaty terms of her smallness: they didn't ask what she was; she didn't ask what they needed. The knots went out blind, carrying whatever hope the buyer knotted into them alongside hers.

It should have been enough. Most days she told herself it was.

* * *

The trouble with market days was that they ended.

By late afternoon the tarpaulins came down, the fish-mongers hosed blood off their boards, and the square emptied back into its grey cobblestones, and Marguerite packed her unsold charms into the same canvas satchel she'd carried since she was nineteen and walked east along the canal wall toward the old guildhall, where the afternoon light came through broken windows in long amber blades and the dust motes drifted like snow that had forgotten which way was down.

The Hall of the Kindly Order had once been something. You could still see it in the bones of the place — the vaulted ceiling with its faded constellations painted by hands two hundred years dead, the great hearth big enough to roast an ox, the stone archway over the eastern door where someone had chiseled WE KINDLE WHAT THE DARK CANNOT HOLD and someone else, later, during the purges, had tried to chisel it back out again and only managed to blunt the words halfway, leaving a sentence that said nothing and confessed everything.

Now it was a ruin with a roof that mostly held, and it was hers to tend, and it was dying by inches, and so was the woman asleep in the chair by the hearth.

"Odile." Marguerite set the satchel down softly. "I'm back."

Her mentor didn't stir. Odile Vane had been the last full witch of the Kindly Order, keeper of its ledger and its lore, and in the twelve years Marguerite had known her she had been iron-spined and sharp-tongued and up before dawn to scold the pigeons off the roofline personally. Now she slept more each day. Slept through afternoons. Slept through conversations, waking mid-sentence to finish thoughts she'd started hours ago, as if time were pooling around her like water around a stone. The healers Marguerite dared consult — at a distance, disguised as questions about "an elderly aunt" — used words like *wasting* and *waning*, and Marguerite hated both words equally, because both were true.

She built up the fire without letting it crackle. There was a knack to that, a way of feeding green wood over embers so the flames stayed low and quiet, and she'd learned it young, because everything she'd learned young had been a variation on the same lesson: *be quiet, be small, be nothing the world can point at.* She put the kettle on the hook and checked the damp course of the north wall, where the frost got in every winter and pushed another seam of mortar loose, and she noted — as she noted every autumn, with the same dull dread — that this year the crack had grown long enough to slide a knife into.

The guildhall was coming apart. So was the Order, if two people and a locked box could be called an Order. So, some mornings, was she.

But there were good hours too. She watered the herb beds in the cloister garth where rosemary still grew stubborn through the cracks, planted by women whose names the ledger remembered even if no one else did. She swept the chapter room where the founder's portraits hung in their smoke-dark frames, all those severe faces in starched collars, and she'd invented stories for each of them over the years — which one had smuggled children out of the war-towns, which one had turned her gifts to healing plague-sores, which one had loved unwisely and written forty letters she never sent. The portraits never corrected her. It was, she supposed, a kind of company.

And there was the ledger itself. Great leather-bound thing, chained to its lectern now rather than to a wall, filled with the Order's accumulated craft in a cipher Odile had taught her letter by letter when she was nineteen and desperate and had nowhere else in the world to go. Fever-cures. Bone-setting. The proper way to bless a birth-room and the proper way to cleanse one after a death. Twelve years of study and she felt she'd barely gotten past the preface. The deep chapters — the ones Odile kept under separate lock, the ones with pages that smelled faintly of ash no matter how many times the book was aired — she had never been permitted to read at all.

"You'll know when you need to know," Odile had always said, in the years when she still said anything with certainty. "And may you never need to."

Marguerite thought about that sentence a great deal lately. It had begun to feel less like wisdom and more like a warning shot across a future she couldn't see.

* * *

That evening Odile woke near dusk, sharp-eyed for exactly an hour, the way a candle flares just before it goes out.

They ate barley stew at the long refectory table, just the two of them at a bench built for thirty, and Marguerite reported the day's takings — eleven coppers, four eggs, a bundle of candles from the Chandlers' Row widow who never let her leave without payment even when Marguerite tried to give the charms free — and Odile listened with her head tilted, and then, unexpectedly, asked a question she hadn't asked in months.

"How many knots today?"

"Forty-one."

"And how many took?"

Marguerite hesitated. This was the old game, the training game, the one Odile had played with her since her first year: the counting of takes, the honest accounting of how much of her whispered will had actually rooted in the work. When she'd been young the answer had been maybe one in five. Lately—

"All of them," she said. "Every one. I don't even have to press anymore. It just... goes where I send it."

Odile set down her spoon. For a moment she looked at Marguerite the way she looked at the frost-crack in the north wall — long, unblinking, measuring something against something else. Then she smiled, and it was a real smile, warm as banked coals, and also somehow the most frightening thing Marguerite had seen all day.

"When I was your age," Odile said, "a strong take was one in three, and I was considered gifted. My own teacher managed two in five in her prime, and they wrote songs about her. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That I count knots well?"

"Don't be clever." But there was no sting in it. Odile reached across the table and took Marguerite's hand, turned it palm-up, and studied it in the firelight — the calluses from the reed-work, the old burn scar from the lesson with the sealing-wax when Marguerite was twenty-two, the fine blue tracery of veins that Odile seemed, tonight, to be reading like text.

"There is power in these hands," she said quietly, "that has no business being in anyone's hands. Not yours, not mine, not any living member of our Order. And it is growing, child, and growing, and one day it will grow past what hiding can hold." Her thumb traced the lifeline and stopped, at a place where the line forked strangely, the way it had always forked, the way Marguerite had never thought to wonder about. "When that day comes—"

"When that day comes, what?"

Odile closed Marguerite's hand gently, folding the strange forked line away inside her fist, like a letter being sealed.

"Eat your stew," she said. "It's getting cold."

And she would say nothing more, though Marguerite walked her to her chair and tucked the blanket around her and stood in the doorway a long time after the old woman's breathing slowed into sleep. The firelight moved on the faded constellations overhead. Somewhere in the dark rafters a pigeon shifted its weight. The guildhall settled around them, stone by patient stone, the way old things do — not falling, exactly, just descending, slowly, toward whatever waited at the bottom of all descents.

Marguerite banked the fire. Locked the ledger. Checked the bolt on the eastern door, the one with the blunted motto, and stood her palm flat against the cold iron for a moment longer than checking required.

*Be quiet, be small,* she told herself, the litany of twelve years. *Be enough.*

The ache was in the word *enough*. She was thirty-one years old and she could not remember a single day when she had not performed her own diminishment — shrinking her voice, her step, her gift, her self, folding it all down and down until it fit through the eye of what Greyhollow would tolerate. And the terrible secret, the one she kept even from herself on most days, was that some part of her had begun to starve on it. Smallness was safety, yes. It was also a kind of slow burial, and she was doing the shoveling herself, grain by grain, and calling the grave a garden.

She blew out the candle. In the dark, before sleep took her, she heard the wind come off the northern hills and rattle the loose pane in the chapter room window, and she thought — not for the first time — that winter was coming earlier each year, arriving harder, staying longer, as though the season itself were gathering somewhere far off, waiting for something. As though the cold knew a name it hadn't spoken yet.

She dreamed, that night, of snowfields. White to every horizon, silent, enormous. And in the dream there was a voice — not hers, older than hers, a woman's voice worn smooth as river stone — saying words she could not quite catch, in a language she almost knew. She woke before dawn with the taste of frost in her mouth and dismissed it, the way she dismissed everything that didn't fit the small life, filed it under *strange* and moved on.

Market day came again, as market days do.

She braided her knots. Three strands over two, two over three. And as she worked, in the grey light before the stalls opened, she noticed the criers assembling at the fountain — the town criers in their crown-liveried coats, more of them than she'd ever seen gathered in Tallowmarket, and among them men she didn't recognize, men in dark uniforms with silver pins shaped like flames, standing very straight, saying nothing yet, waiting for something.

One of them looked at her stall. Looked at her baskets of charms. Looked at her.

He didn't ask anything. He just watched, the way you watch a door you've been ordered to remember.

Marguerite tied her next knot a little tighter than usual, and told herself, one more time, that it was nothing. That it was always nothing. That smallness was safety.

The bell began to ring.
