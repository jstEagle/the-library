## 2. The Proclamation

The bell rang for a long time.

Marguerite had heard Greyhollow's warning bell twice in her life—once when the river took the east bridge, once when old Master Corben's distillery caught fire and painted the sky orange over the Tanner's Row. Both times, the bell had rung fast and ragged, panic made into sound.

This bell was different. This one tolled slow and even, unhurried as a funeral, from the tower above the Provost's Hall, and it did not stop.

She stood in her doorway with a luck-knot half-finished in her hands, red cord wound around her fingers, and watched the street below fill with people the way a bowl fills with spilled water—slowly at first, then all at once. Neighbors came out in their aprons and their work coats. The baker's boy climbed onto the mounting block outside the farrier's to see better. Someone's dog barked once and was shushed by three people at once.

At the top of Cooper's Lane, where the street bent toward the market square, a crier appeared in the black-and-silver livery of the crown. He was flanked by two soldiers in the same colors, and he carried a scroll that looked heavier than paper had any right to look.

"By decree of His Majesty Aldric the Third," the crier called, and his voice carried strangely well, as if the morning itself were holding its breath to listen, "the Edict of Ash is proclaimed throughout the realm and all its holdings."

The name went through the crowd like cold water through cloth. Marguerite heard it repeated in murmurs—*Edict of Ash*—as though saying it might help anyone understand it. She already understood. She had understood since the soldier had stood watching her stall yesterday. Some part of her, some animal part buried under twelve years of careful smallness, had been waiting for this bell the way you wait for a tooth to finally break.

"All persons possessing the gift of sorcery, hedge-craft, charm-work, or any art forbidden since the Sundering War," the crier read, unrolling another length of parchment with ceremony that felt obscene, "shall present themselves for registration at any crown office within thirty days of this proclamation. Registration shall consist of declaration of gift, recording of name and residence, and marking by the registrar."

"Marking?" someone whispered near her. "Marking like cattle?"

The crier continued as if he hadn't heard, as if he'd been ordered not to hear anything ever again. "Those who register shall be permitted to pursue lawful trade under crown supervision, subject to inspection. Those who do not register within thirty days shall be declared *hunted*. Any citizen harboring, concealing, or aiding an unregistered gifted person shall share their forfeit."

He paused there. Marguerite realized, distantly, that the pause was rehearsed. He did it in every square in the city, this same pause, letting the word *forfeit* sit on the air like smoke.

"The penalty for the gifted who refuse registration," he said, "is death by fire. The penalty for those who shelter them is the same."

Nobody moved. That was the worst part—not screaming, not weeping, just four hundred people standing very still in the grey morning light while a man in silver-trimmed black read out the shape of their deaths. A woman near the fountain pressed her hand flat against her stomach, and Marguerite thought, absurdly, of all the fever-charms she'd sold that woman over the years, back when her youngest couldn't stop coughing through the winter months. The charms had worked. The woman had never asked how.

Now the woman's eyes found Marguerite across the crowd.

They had known each other eleven years. Her name was Marta. She'd brought Marguerite honey-cakes at midwinter. When Marguerite's mother died—when Elara Whitlock was carried out of the house on a shutter with her face covered, though everyone said fever—the woman had held Marguerite's hand at the graveside and not let go until the ground was full.

Marta looked at her now, and something in her face closed. Not hatred. Nothing so clean as hatred. It was recalculation—the swift, terrible arithmetic of a person measuring how much of her own life had been spent standing next to a fire that could burn her whole street down. Eleven years of honey-cakes and borrowed sugar and charms that worked, all of it being reweighed behind her eyes, and Marguerite watched every grain shift.

Then Marta looked away first, and pulled her daughter closer to her skirts, and Marguerite knew exactly what that meant too.

The crier rolled up his scroll. The soldiers' boots rang on the cobblestones as they turned. Somewhere behind Marguerite, a shutter banged closed, and then another, and then the whole lane began doing what frightened streets do: pretending to be empty.

She went inside and shut her own door, and stood with her back against it in the dimness of her little house, the luck-knot still tangled around her fingers. Her hands were steady. She noted that with a strange, floating detachment, the way you note the weather in a country you're about to leave forever. Steady hands. Good. Whatever came, she would need steady hands.

Thirty days.

She untangled the cord slowly. Then she crossed the room, knelt, and pried up the third floorboard from the hearth—the loose one, the one Odile had taught her to loosen with a fingernail so it would never look pried—and drew out her ledger.

It wasn't much to look at. A plain book bound in grey leather, its spine gone soft from handling, corners rounded by years of her thumb. Every charm she'd sold in twelve years was written inside in her own careful hand. Not names—never names, Odile had been strict about that—but descriptions. *Fever-charm, for the cooper's household, winter child. Luck-knot, red cord, for the fishwife before the season's opening. Warding sigil, chalked beneath a doorstep, renewed quarterly.* Twelve years of small mercies, itemized in a code only she could read, because a ledger was how you kept yourself honest. How you remembered whom you owed and who owed you. How you proved, to yourself at least, that what you did was craft and care, not the forbidden thing the law said it was.

She carried it to the hearth and knelt before the dead ashes, and for a long moment she didn't move.

Burning it felt like burning the proof that she had been good. That was foolish—she knew it was foolish, the ledger was evidence, nothing more, a list of crimes with the crime left out—and yet her hands hesitated over the cover the way they'd hesitate over a body.

"It's just a book," she said aloud, to the empty room, in the voice she used for frightened customers. "You're just a book."

She struck flint to steel anyway, because her hands were steady even when the rest of her wasn't, and touched the flame to the first page.

The grey leather caught reluctantly, the way old things do. She fed it kindling and watched twelve years curl and blacken. Fever-charms for children who grew up. Luck-knots for fishermen who came home. A birthing-blessing stitched into a christening gown, the only grand thing she'd ever made, for the miller's daughter who had named her baby Marguerite without asking permission. All of it going up in a column of sweet-smelling smoke that smelled, she thought, like the inside of a coffin.

When it was ash, she swept it into the cold hearth and mixed it in with last winter's cinders, and sat back on her heels, lighter and emptier both, the way you feel after vomiting.

Then she got up, packed her satchel with everything that mattered—which took almost no time at all, which was its own quiet grief—and went out the back way, up the hill, toward the guildhall.

* * *

The Guildhall of Saint Verene's Order stood at the top of Hollow Hill like an old dog too loyal to die. Its roof sagged in the middle. Its windows were leaded diamonds, half of them replaced with boards. Moss owned the north wall entire. Once, Marguerite knew from Odile's stories, forty gifted women had lived and worked here—healers, weather-readers, makers of wards strong enough to turn a raiding band at the war's end. Now it was a ruin with two occupants, and the city had agreed long ago to pretend it was a granary.

Odile was asleep in her chair by the cold hearth, as she was asleep more and more these days. She was small inside her blankets, birdlike, her white hair loose and thinning, her hands folded on her lap like two dried leaves. On the table beside her lay her cup of willowbark tea, gone cold, and her spectacles, and a letter she'd been writing for three days and had gotten no further than *To whoever remains.*

Marguerite hated how the sight of her hit, every time. Like a fist made entirely of love.

"Wake up," Marguerite said gently, kneeling to put fresh wood in the hearth. "There's news."

"I know." Odile's voice was dry and rusted but awake now, and when Marguerite looked up, the old woman's eyes were open and fixed on her with that unnerving clarity that illness never seemed to touch. "The bell. I heard the bell." She sighed, long and slow, the sigh of someone setting down something heavy she has carried a very great distance. "Sit down, girl. You've the face of a woman about to argue."

"I'm not going to argue." Marguerite stayed kneeling by the fire, lighting it, feeding it, giving her hands something honest to do. "I'm going to tell you we have thirty days to register or die, and then I'm going to tell you my plan."

"Register or die." Odile tasted the words like spoiled milk. "They've gotten more generous. In my day it was simply die."

"Odile."

"I'm listening. Tell me the plan."

"We go south." Marguerite laid it out the way she'd laid it out in her head all the way up the hill, each piece fitted to the next, because a plan was a kind of charm too and she needed it to hold. "Not tonight—tomorrow, before dawn. We take the pilgrim road as far as Thornbridge, we say you're my grandmother and we're going to your sister's funeral, and from Thornbridge we pick up the river. There are barges. Smugglers, mostly, but smugglers move people as easily as brandy if the coin's right, and I've been saving. There's land past the marches—freeholds, places where the crown's writ runs thin. Old bloodlines keep their heads down out there and nobody asks questions."

She stopped. The fire had caught properly now, throwing warm light up the old woman's face, and Odile was looking at her with an expression Marguerite couldn't read—a tenderness so raw it was almost anger.

"You've been planning this," Odile said. It wasn't a question.

"Since yesterday. Since the soldier at the stall."

"No." Odile shook her head slowly. "Since always. You've had a bag half-packed in your heart since you were thirteen years old, Marguerite. I've watched you keep it there." She unfolded one hand from her lap and reached out, and Marguerite took it, and the old woman's grip was still stronger than it had any right to be. "Child. I cannot walk to Thornbridge."

"You can. I'll carry you if—"

"I cannot walk to the gate," Odile said, with terrible gentleness, "and we both know it, and lying to me about it spends your strength, not mine. So hush, and let me spend mine telling you true things, because I haven't many days of true things left, and this—" her fingers tightened, "—this changes the count."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the ruin, wind moved through the broken cloister, stirring the dead leaves that no one ever swept anymore.

"What did the proclamation say, exactly?" Odile asked. "Word for word. The important parts."

"That the gifted must register within thirty days. Declaration, recording, and—marking. And that anyone who shelters us shares our forfeit." Marguerite's throat tightened around the last word. "Fire. For everyone. They said fire."

"Yes." Odile gazed into the flames. "That's how they did it after the war, too. Fire first, then the lists, then the hunting. Fire is theater—it teaches the crowds what to be afraid of. The lists are the true machinery. Names, girl. It always comes down to names." Her mouth twisted. "I wonder whose list we're on already. Because we are on one. Understand that. This edict didn't find us today. It's been looking for us for years. Today it merely announced itself."

"Then we run."

"You run." Odile said it flatly, without drama, the way she'd say *it's raining*, and it cut deeper than any argument could have. "I'm done running. I ran for twenty years after the war, Marguerite. I ran until running was the whole of me, and then I hid in this ruin and called hiding peace. I'll not spend my last season on a road, dying by inches in some smuggler's hold, so that you can watch. No. Here is what happens now. You'll leave me here, hidden, with Mother Cassia at the herbalist's cottage past the mill—we've arranged it already, she and I, weeks ago, because I am old but I am not blind, and I saw this coming the way you see weather coming. You'll go south alone."

"Alone." Marguerite heard her own voice crack on it and hated that. "Odile, no. I'm not leaving you to—"

"To die in my own bed, in the place I chose, with Cassia's poppy syrup when it gets bad? Child, that is not a fate. That is a *mercy*, and it is the last one I will accept from this world, so do not take it from me out of love, because that would be cruelty wearing love's coat." The old woman drew a breath that whistled faintly, and for a moment she looked every one of her years and more. "Besides. You'll need to travel light. Lighter than you know."

Marguerite stared at her. "What does that mean?"

For a long moment Odile didn't answer. She looked into the fire, and her face did something complicated—grief and guilt and a fierce, stubborn protectiveness all fighting for the same ground—and Marguerite felt the temperature of the room change, or imagined she did, a coolness moving through it like water under a door.

"There are things I kept from you," Odile said quietly. "Things your mother charged me to keep. Things I swore I'd carry to my grave, and I meant the oath when I made it, and I have hated myself for it every day since, and now—" she laughed, short and joyless, "—now the world has decided to make my cowardice irrelevant. Thirty days. Even secrets have deadlines."

"What things?" Marguerite's voice came out low. "Odile. What did you keep?"

The old woman turned and looked at her then, fully, and there was such sorrow in it, such bottomless, apologetic sorrow, that Marguerite's whole body went cold despite the fire.

"Not what," Odile said. "*Whom.* I kept a whom, child. And I think—I have always thought—that it may be time you met her."

She tried to rise. Her legs wouldn't take it; she waved off Marguerite's hands impatiently and pointed instead, with one trembling finger, toward the far end of the hall—toward the old chapter room, sealed these twelve years, its door locked since before Marguerite had ever set foot in the guildhall.

"In there," Odile said. "Under the third flagstone. There's a box. Bring it to me, and bring the key from around my neck, and then, Marguerite—" the old woman's voice dropped to almost nothing, and yet it filled the ruined hall the way the crier's voice had filled the square, "—then you must promise me you'll hear the whole truth before you hate me. Because you will want to hate me. And I will deserve it. But you will need me whole to survive what's in that box, and so you must promise."

Outside, far down in the city, a second bell began to ring—sharper, faster, wrong—and beneath the ringing, faint but unmistakable, rose a smell that did not belong to any ordinary morning.

Smoke.

Marguerite stood between the dying fire and the sealed door, the key cold against her palm, and felt the dread settle into her bones at last—not like a visitor, but like a tenant. Like something that had signed a lease.

"Promise me," Odile said again.

Down in the tanner's quarter, the smoke thickened, and somewhere a crowd was learning what the Edict of Ash meant in practice, on its very first day, before the ink was even dry.

Marguerite closed her hand around the key, and went to fetch the box.
