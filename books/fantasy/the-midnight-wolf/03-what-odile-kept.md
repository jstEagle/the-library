## 3. What Odile Kept

The guildhall had more doors than it needed.

Marguerite had known that since she was nine years old and Odile first set her to sweeping the upper corridor—the long one with the warped floorboards and the seven doors leading nowhere useful. Two opened onto closets. One opened onto a brick wall, bricked up so long ago the mortar had gone the color of old teeth. The rest opened onto rooms that served no purpose she could ever discover: a room with a single chair facing a window painted shut; a room of stacked, mildewed hymnals from a faith no one in Greyhollow practiced; a room that was empty except for a chalk circle on the floor, half-erased by decades of drafts.

*Old buildings grow strange,* Odile always said, when Marguerite asked. *Like old women. We keep doors we no longer remember opening.*

But old buildings did not usually have cellars whose stairs ended at a wall where no wall should be. And they did not have floorboards that rang hollow in patterns—*here, here, not here, here*—if you walked them slowly enough with your bare feet and paid attention the way twelve years of hiding had taught her to pay attention.

She started counting the morning after the criers came.

It was not panic that drove her. Panic was a luxury, like saffron or sleep; neither woman in that crumbling hall could afford it. It was something colder and more practical, the part of her mind that had spent a decade calculating exactly how much luck-knot wool to buy so the surplus never looked like stockpiling, exactly how many fever-charms a neighborhood could absorb before someone asked why she never caught anything herself. That part of her mind woke on the morning after the Edict of Ash and said, quietly: *Thirty days. Count what you have.*

So she counted.

The guildhall sat behind the tanner's quarter like an apology—a low stone building with a sagging roof, its foundation older than the city charter, older maybe than the war. Once it had held forty gifted women and men. Now it held two: Odile, who slept fourteen hours a day and woke confused more often than not, and Marguerite, who had inherited the building's secrets the way one inherits a coat too large in the shoulders, still growing into it, still tripping on the hems.

She began with the cellar.

The cellar smelled of cold earth and lamp oil and, beneath both, a faint sweetness she had never been able to name—as if something once stored down there had perfumed the stone itself. Her lamp threw her shadow huge against the far wall, the one that shouldn't be there. She pressed her palm flat against it. Mortar crumbled under her fingers in a fine grey rain, but beneath the crumbling surface the bricks were sound, fitted tight, laid with a care that said *hidden* rather than *forgotten*.

Someone had wanted this wall to look old. Someone had done a very good job.

"Odile," she said aloud, to the empty dark, "what are you hiding down here?"

The dark didn't answer. But somewhere above her, through the ceiling joists, she heard the creak of a floorboard—the particular slow drag-step of her mentor moving from bed to chair. Marguerite climbed back into the daylight and found the old woman sitting in the kitchen with her hands folded on her cane, watching the window as though the street beyond were a play she'd seen before and hadn't liked the ending.

"You're up early," Marguerite said.

"I'm up," Odile corrected. "Early is a matter of opinion." She turned her head. Her eyes were milky at the edges these days, clouded like river ice, but the look beneath the cloud was sharp as it had ever been. "You were in my cellar."

"I was checking the foundations."

"At dawn."

"The Edict gave us thirty days," Marguerite said. She kept her voice even. She was very good at keeping her voice even; she'd had practice. "I thought I'd spend them learning which walls we own."

Something moved across Odile's face—not guilt, exactly. Closer to grief wearing guilt's clothing. "Sit down, child."

"I'm not hungry."

"I didn't offer you food." The old woman's hand found the arm of her chair and gripped it, and Marguerite saw suddenly how thin that hand had become, how the tendons stood out like roots through shallow soil. "I offered you the truth, and you'll want to be sitting for it. Truths hit standing people harder."

Marguerite did not sit. Twelve years of obedience made her want to; twelve years of hiding had also taught her that the moment you let someone arrange you, they owned the shape of you. So she stayed standing, arms crossed, and waited.

Odile sighed, and the sigh seemed to cost her something vital. "Third door past the hymnal room," she said. "The one that opens onto the bricked wall. There's a loose stone at knee height, left side. Behind it, a key. The key opens the box under my bed. You've dusted under my bed a hundred times and never once asked about the box, and I have loved you for your discretion and cursed it in equal measure." She closed her eyes. "Go on, then. Bring it here. No point pretending anymore. It found us anyway."

*It found us anyway.* The same words she'd spoken when the criers came, as if the Edict were not a decree but a creature, something patient and hunting that had merely taken its time. Marguerite's skin prickled.

She went.

* * *

The loose stone came free easier than it should have, as if it had been loosened often, oiled by use. Behind it lay a key wrapped in oilcloth, black iron, heavy, its bow shaped like a knot of interlocking circles. Marguerite stood in the dead-end corridor holding it and felt her heart beating in her throat.

The box under Odile's bed was iron-banded oak, small enough to carry in two hands, light enough that its weight surprised her—it weighed almost nothing, as if the contents had been chosen for portability. Chosen by someone who expected to run. Or expected someone else to run, carrying it.

She set it on the kitchen table. Odile watched her without speaking. The key turned with a click that sounded, in the silent room, like a knuckle cracking.

Inside: a pendant on a leather cord, and a letter sealed with plain wax, unmarked.

The pendant was a tooth. A wolf's tooth, longer than her thumb, yellowed with age, set in a collar of silver worked into shapes she almost recognized—snowflakes, maybe, or stars, or the spaces between them. When her fingers closed around it, the metal was warm. Not body-warm. *Hearth*-warm, alive-warm, and beneath the warmth a faint pulse, steady as a sleeping heart, and frost bloomed across the silver under her fingertips in delicate ferning branches—

She dropped it. The tooth clicked against the table and lay there, innocuous, ordinary, cooling.

"What is that?" Her voice came out wrong. Thin. "What did you give me?"

"Read the letter first," Odile said. "Then ask me again. You may find the question changes."

The seal broke like a small bone snapping. The handwriting inside was Odile's—but younger, sharper, the strokes of a woman writing fast and frightened, ink blotted where the pen had trembled. Marguerite read it once in silence, then again aloud because her eyes refused to accept what the words said the first time.

*If you are reading this, then the hunters have come, or the girl has grown curious, or both—I always knew curiosity would outlast caution; it's why I chose her. Marguerite. If it is you holding this: forgive me. Forgive all of it.*

*You were eleven days old when your mother brought you to this door in the snow. Elara Whitlock carried you against her heart and would not put you down until the last moment, and then she knelt on our threshold and kissed your brow and gave you to me, and I have never in my life seen anyone carry such grief with such straight shoulders.*

*You are not my apprentice. I have taught you charms because charms were safe, because smallness was safety, because Elara begged me to keep you small. But the gift you carry is not yours. It was never mine to give and never hers to give either, and yet we gave it, because the alternative was worse. You are the vessel. What sleeps in you slept in the Wolf of Winter before you, and it has waited twelve years inside a charm-seller's daughter for the world to be foolish enough to need it again.*

*Burn this if you wish. Hate me if you must. But know this above all: every year of smallness I gave you was a shield, not a cage. Whatever comes now—remember that.*

There was no signature. Only a pressed sprig of winter jasmine gone brown between the pages, and beneath it, in a different hand entirely—elegant, slanted, familiar in a way that made Marguerite's chest hurt—a single line:

*Forgive me, little star. Grow anyway. — E.W.*

Her mother's handwriting. She knew it from three surviving letters and the flyleaf of a cookbook. She knew it the way you know your own name.

The kitchen was very quiet. The fire ticked. Outside, a cart rattled past, and somewhere down the street children were shouting about the snow that hadn't fallen yet but might.

"Say it," Marguerite said. "Out loud. All of it. Not fragments in a letter. I've read the letter twice and I still don't—" Her voice cracked clean through. She hated that. "Twelve years. Twelve years, Odile. Fever-charms. Luck-knots. You taught me to hide my gift behind *trinkets*, and the whole time—"

"The whole time," Odile said, and her voice was steady, which somehow made it worse, "the whole time I was teaching you to hide a bonfire behind a candleflame, yes. Because the last time the bonfire showed itself, it ended a war. Do you understand what I'm telling you, girl? Not a talent. Not a knack. The Wolf of Winter did not die at the Sundering. Wolves don't die. They go to ground. And your mother—" the old woman's breath caught, and for the first time her steadiness fractured, just at the edge, "your mother made sure it went to ground in the safest place she could find. Inside a newborn who would grow up loved. Who would grow up *ordinary*. Who would never once be tempted to listen to what slept under her ribs."

"Loved." Marguerite laughed, and the laugh came out jagged. "Is that what you call it? Raising a child on lies?"

"Yes," Odile said simply. "That is exactly what I call it. Love is mostly lies, child, well-meant ones. *You're safe. This won't hurt. I'm not going anywhere.* Half of those were lies in this house too." She reached out and closed her spotted hand over Marguerite's wrist, and her grip was stronger than it had any right to be. "Now. Ask your question again."

Marguerite looked down at the pendant on the table. At the tooth. Frost still lingered in the silver's crevices, refusing to melt in the warm kitchen air, glittering like something patient.

"What am I?" she whispered.

"A person," Odile said fiercely, and now the fierceness was a living thing, filling her shrunken frame, and Marguerite understood with a lurch that this—this fury on her behalf—was the truest thing the old woman had ever shown her. "You hear me? A person named Marguerite Whitlock, who burns her own porridge and cheats at cards and cries at weddings. That is who carries it. Not a vessel. Vessels are cups. Cups get emptied. You are a *woman*, and any man or crown or ancient power that thinks otherwise will learn differently, starting with me."

The words landed somewhere deep and bruised, and Marguerite found her eyes burning. She was angry—she was so angry, anger twelve years deep, at Odile, at the mother she barely remembered, at the whole conspiracy of love that had built a life for her out of smallness—and underneath the anger, shamefully, stubbornly, something else. Something like being held.

"You should have told me," she managed.

"When? At six, so you'd lie awake listening to it breathe? At ten, so you'd try to talk to it and wake it early? At fourteen—" Odile stopped. Looked away toward the window, toward the grey street. "At fourteen you already dreamed of it. Snowfields. You used to wake calling out about a wolf made of winter, and I'd sit by your bed till dawn with a fever-charm in each hand, praying to every god I'd stopped believing in that it wasn't time yet. It wasn't time. I decided it wasn't time. Old women get very few decisions, Marguerite. I guarded mine jealously."

"And now the criers are in the square," Marguerite said. "And it's time whether you decide or not."

"Now the criers are in the square," Odile agreed. "And here is what I know, the last of what I kept: the seal your mother helped make was meant to hold for a lifetime. Yours. Not mine. The unsealing requires things I can only half remember, and half-remembered rituals are how villages burn. So we do not unseal. We do not listen to the wolf. We run—we run tonight, ahead of the lists and the informants and whatever map Corvane is drawing in that fine head of his—and we stay small, one last time. There's a herbalist past the river fork, Hesta, an old sister of the guild. She'll take us in."

"Run where? Winter's coming. The roads will close. They'll close the *bridges*, Odile, they'll—"

"They'll close them within the week. Which means we cross tomorrow, or we don't cross at all." The old woman rose from her chair, unfolding joint by joint, taller than Marguerite had realized, taller than sickness had let her seem. She picked up the wolf-tooth pendant and held it out on its cord, dangling between them, turning slowly.

"Take it. It's yours—or rather, it's *its*, and it knows you. Don't wear it where eyes can see. Don't touch it when you're frightened, and never, never when you're angry. And Marguerite—" Odile's milky eyes fixed on her, and beneath the cataract-cloud something ancient and exhausted and terribly kind looked out. "Whatever you feel right now—betrayal, rage, all of it—keep it. Feel all of it fully. It's yours. It's the only thing in this house that has ever been entirely, uncomplicatedly yours. Guard it better than I guarded you."

Marguerite took the cord. The pendant settled into her palm, warm as a living thing, and the frost bloomed again across her fingers—delicate, beautiful, *wrong*—and this time she didn't drop it. She closed her fist around the tooth and felt that distant pulse answer, quickening, like something under the snow turning over in its sleep and beginning, slowly, to dream of waking.

Outside, far off toward the river, a bell began to ring. Not the market bell. Not the chapel bell. The alarm bell at the tanner's gate, ragged and urgent, and beneath it, rising on the wind, a smell that did not belong to any morning: smoke.

Odile went stiff as a struck match. "That's early," she breathed. "They said thirty days. That's *early*, that's—"

Down the street, a neighbor's door banged open. Marguerite saw old Marta Vey—the woman whose fevers she had charmed away every winter for a decade, whose grandchildren called her Miss Margie—standing on her step in her nightclothes, staring toward the smoke, and then turning, slowly, to stare at the guildhall. At her.

The look on Marta's face was not surprise. It was arithmetic. Ten winters of cured fevers, recalculating themselves into something else.

"Pack nothing you'd mourn," Odile said quietly behind her. "We leave within the hour."
