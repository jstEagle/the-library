## 4. The First Fire

The smoke told him before anyone did.

Tomas Vey was three miles downriver when he saw it—a thin grey column climbing out of Greyhollow's tanner's quarter, too straight and too patient to be an accident. Accidents leaned. Accidents scattered on the wind like a drunk leaving a tavern. This smoke rose the way a hangman's rope hangs: plumb, deliberate, official.

He cut the barge's engine—what passed for one, anyway, since the Sundering had killed most of the river's old workings and left men like him poling and sailing cargo past the city walls by muscle and cunning—and let the current take him sideways toward the reed bank. From there, half-hidden behind a curtain of winter-brown rushes, he could see the whole eastern face of the city: the tanner's quarter with its crooked roofs, the wall above it bristling with new watch-fires, and beyond it all, that single column of smoke standing against a sky the color of dirty pewter.

"Edict of Ash," said Wren beside him, spitting into the water. She was his cousin in the way that half the riverfolk were cousins—which meant her mother had slept with his uncle once, decades ago, and nobody had ever found a reason to stop calling each other family. She had eyes like chipped flint and hands scarred from thirty years of rope-work, and she had not taken her gaze off that smoke since it appeared. "Third one this week."

"Fourth," Tomas said. "There was one two nights back, out past the tannery. You were drunk."

"I was *celebrating*. We got the Corren shipment through." She squinted at the column, chewing the inside of her cheek. "That one's big, though. Big fire, big crowd. You can hear them from here if you listen."

He listened. The wind came off the city in slow pulses, carrying the usual river-smell of tar and rotting weed and tannery lime—but beneath it, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, came the sound. Voices. Not screaming. That was the worst part, he thought later; everyone always assumed the worst part would be the screaming. It wasn't. It was the murmur. Hundreds of people murmuring, the low collective sound of a crowd watching something done in their name, the sound of ordinary people deciding all at once to look at their boots instead of at each other.

"Come on," Wren said. "We're losing light. Cargo won't pole itself up to Millbrook, and I'd rather not be floating past the walls after dark with a hold full of unregistered grain."

Tomas didn't move.

"Tomas."

"That's Marisol's quarter," he said quietly.

Wren went still. He felt her looking at him, felt the shape of what he'd said settle between them like a stone dropped in still water. Everyone on the river knew about Marisol. Not because she made a secret of herself—the opposite, really. A hedge-witch couldn't hide much in a city that had been squeezing magic out of its cracks for twelve years, so she hadn't tried very hard. She healed. That was all. Fevers, broken bones, the creeping lung-rot that tanners caught from their vats before they turned thirty. She charged what people could pay and nothing when they couldn't, which meant she lived in a room above a chandler's shop with a leaky roof and a kettle that whistled three notes flat, and she was beloved by roughly four hundred people who would deny ever having met her if you asked them straight-faced.

She had saved his sister's life. That was the part Tomas kept circling in his head, unable to land on it properly, like a bird that couldn't find its branch. Lissa had been nine years old. Fever, the bad kind, the kind that came up the river every few summers and took children the way a flood takes fences—casually, indiscriminately, without malice or memory. Their mother had spent everything they had on physicians' tonics that tasted like pennies and did nothing, and on the fourth night, when Lissa stopped recognizing faces and started talking to someone in the corner of the room where no one stood, their mother had gone out into the dark and come back with Marisol.

Marisol had worked until dawn. Tomas remembered standing in the doorway—he'd have been fourteen, gangly, furious at the world in the way fourteen-year-olds are furious without knowing why—and watching this round, grey-streaked woman with ink-stained fingers hold his sister's burning hand and hum something low and wordless while frost-patterns bloomed across the window glass in midsummer. In the morning the fever broke. Lissa woke up hungry. Marisol refused payment, ate six of their eggs, complimented their mother's bread, and left a charm on the lintel that smelled faintly of juniper and stayed there for years, fading slowly like a held breath finally released.

"Maybe she got out," Wren said. It wasn't conviction. It was courtesy.

"Her roof leaks," Tomas said. "Her knees are bad. She's sixty-one years old, Wren, and she moves like a woman of seventy because she gave her strength away one healing at a time. Where exactly is she getting out to?"

Wren didn't answer. On the water, the current pushed at the barge's hull, patient, indifferent, the way the whole world seemed to be these days—pushing everything along toward whatever came next whether you were ready for it or not.

* * *

They moored at Millbrook at dusk, offloaded the grain to a factor with sweaty palms and a ledger he wouldn't meet Tomas's eye over, and by then the wind had shifted and brought the smell.

Everyone knew the smell. Twelve years ago, during the last of the purges after the Sundering War, the whole country had learned it: burnt hair and rendered fat and something sweeter underneath, wrong-sweet, the kind of sweet that stuck in the back of your throat for days. They'd burned the guilds then. The great houses of craft-magic, the battle-mages' colleges, the healing halls—all of it, in the name of peace, because the war had been fought with power and the peace was bought with the promise of never again. And for twelve years Greyhollow had pretended the promise was enough. People had whispered their little charms behind closed doors. Charm-sellers sold luck-knots in the market with a wink and a shrug, and buyers bought them with the same wink, and everyone agreed silently never, ever to say the true thing out loud.

Then the crown had posted criers in every square with the Edict of Ash, and the pretending had acquired a deadline. Thirty days. Register, or burn.

And now the burning had started early, because deadlines, Tomas had learned long ago, were things powerful men set for other people and ignored themselves.

He sat on the barge's deck with his back against the mast and a bowl of barley soup going cold in his hands, and he watched the glow over Greyhollow fade from orange to ember-red to nothing, and he thought about Marisol's kettle whistling three notes flat, and he did not eat.

"You're going to do something stupid," Wren said, dropping onto the deck beside him. She said it the way she reported weather. Matter-of-fact. Already planning around it.

"I'm going to finish my soup."

"You've been staring at that soup like it insulted your mother. You do that thing with your jaw when you're building up to something stupid. Your father did the same thing. Right before he tried to outrun the customs cutter at Blackweir Bend."

"He nearly made it."

"He sank a barge and lost us a season's profit, is what he did." She pulled her knees up. The lantern swung overhead, throwing their shadows long and wavering across the stacked crates. "So. What's the stupid thing?"

Tomas set the bowl down. He looked out at the dark water, at the far shore where the reeds whispered, at the black bulk of the city with its walls and its fires and its murmuring crowds, and he heard himself say it before he'd fully decided to say it, which was how most true things left his mouth:

"We start moving them out."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere down the bank, a night bird called twice and went silent, as if even the birds wanted to hear this clearly.

"The gifted," Tomas went on, and now that the door was open the words came faster, the plan assembling itself out of pieces he realized he'd been carrying around for weeks without knowing it. "The ones who don't register. The ones who can't—half of them are children, Wren, or old women, or fools who'd sign their own names just because a man in a crown's tabard asked politely. The Edict says register by month's end. Nobody's watching the river yet. They're watching gates, roads, the bridges. But the river—" he tapped the deck beneath them, "—the river's ours. Always has been. We run grain past the walls, we run brandy past the toll stations, we run anything that fits in a hold for the right price. So we run people."

"People aren't cargo."

"No," he agreed. "People are worse. People talk, people panic, people get seasick and ask questions and cry. But cargo doesn't look at you afterward and know what you did. Cargo doesn't get to live somewhere warm because of you." His voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and he hated it, and Wren had the grace to pretend she hadn't noticed. "Marisol pulled my sister out of a grave that fever dug for her. I was too young to do anything but stand in a doorway. I'm not doing that again. I'm not standing in any more doorways."

"It'll get you killed."

"Eventually, sure. Everything does."

"It'll get *me* killed. I'm implicated the moment you open your fool mouth, you understand that. Kinship's guilt under this Edict. They wrote it right into the law—harboring, aiding, conveying." She recited it flatly, the words of the proclamation she'd memorized the way she memorized channel depths and toll schedules. "'Any person who conveys, shelters, or conceals an unregistered gifted individual shall be held accessory to sorcery and liable to the same penalty.'"

"Liable to the same penalty," Tomas repeated. "Fire."

"Fire," Wren confirmed. Then she sighed, long and theatrical and entirely unconvincing, and reached over and stole his cold soup, and that was her answer. "You'll need more than one barge. And you'll need eyes inside the walls—someone who knows who needs moving before the hunters do. The hunters work off lists, Tomas. Somebody's making those lists. Which means somebody else has to be faster than the lists."

* * *

The eyes inside the walls turned out to belong to a boy named Ferren who ran messages between the tanner's quarter and the docks and had the particular talent of being so thoroughly unremarkable that no one ever remembered seeing him, which was its own quiet kind of magic and entirely legal. Ferren met them at the coal-wharf two nights later, in the hour before dawn when even the watch drank and dozed, and he climbed aboard without invitation and sat down among the crates like he owned them.

"They took her on Tuesday," Ferren said, without preamble. He was maybe fifteen, with a stutter that vanished completely when he talked fast, and he talked fast now. "Not the pyre—that was Wednesday, that smoke you saw. They took her Tuesday night. Registered informant on her street. Neighbor woman. Marisol had charmed her daughter's cradle two winters back, kept the baby sleeping through the night. That's the thanks." He picked at a splinter on the crate edge, splintering it further. "Inquisitor himself came for her. Corvane. Grey coat, grey eyes. Didn't shout, didn't break the door. Knocked. She answered. He was *polite*, Tomas. Ferren saw it through the shutter. He helped her down the stairs."

Tomas's hands had curled into fists without his permission. "Polite."

"That's the rumor up and down the streets. He knocks. He's courteous. He asks them to come along, and some of them do, because he's polite, because he looks tired, because—" Ferren swallowed. "Because kindness from a hunter is worse than cruelty. Cruelty you can hate. Kindness makes you think maybe it'll be different this time." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "It wasn't different."

The pyre had been built in the tannery yard, Ferren told them, because the ground there was already fouled and no one would complain about the smell. They'd given her a chance at the end—the standard offer, recited by a herald: confess your gift, abjure it, accept the binding-iron, and live. Binding-iron burned the gift out of a person like acid eating through cloth. Some survived it. What remained was a hollowed thing that flinched at raised voices and wept without knowing why.

"She said no," Ferren said. "Witnesses heard her. She stood up on the platform and she said, clear as church bells, 'I healed your children. Every one of you. And I'd do it again.' And then she spat at the herald's feet." His voice finally broke. "They lit it right after. Before the crowd could even decide how to feel about her. I think that's why they moved it to the tannery yard, actually. Smaller space. Less time for deciding."

Tomas stared out at the black water. The current moved past the hull in long slow ropes, heading downstream, heading anywhere but here.

"Ferren," he said. "How many are left? Unregistered. In the tanner's quarter, the weaver's rows, the riverside streets. How many do you know of?"

The boy wiped his face with his sleeve, businesslike, and became a courier again, tallying. "Forty I could name. Probably eighty I could guess at. There's a charm-seller works the market—Whitlock, the young one, sells luck-knots nobody believes in except everybody buys them. Old Odile from the guildhall, though she barely leaves her bed anymore. A lock-boy who talks to locks, though he's half-wild and won't stay put. A dozen hedge-healers. A fox-man—" Ferren paused, frowned. "Well. Half a fox-man. You'd have to see it."

"Eighty," Tomas said.

"At least. And the list grows daily, because fear makes informants faster than greed ever did." Ferren leaned forward, and in the pre-dawn grey his face looked suddenly older than fifteen, older than forty, the face of someone who had watched a neighbor turn on a neighbor and understood exactly what kind of winter was coming. "You're really going to do it. Move them out."

"I'm going to try."

"Where?"

Tomas hadn't answered that question yet, not fully, not out loud. But he'd spent two sleepless nights with the river maps spread across his bunk, tracing channels and oxbows and abandoned landing stages north of the city, and there was a place—one place—that kept drawing his eye. An old mill town two days upstream, dead since the Sundering broke its waterwheels, with empty houses and a defensible bend and a widow who owed him a favor and asked no questions. A place where eighty frightened people might become, for a little while, invisible.

"There's a place," he said. "Upstream. Call it a waystation. First stop on a longer road, if we find one." He looked at Ferren. "Can you get word to them? Quietly? No meetings, no gatherings, nothing the lists can catch. Just a whisper passed hand to hand: the river runs north, and there's room on it."

Ferren nodded slowly. "And if the hunters watch the water?"

"Then we get faster," Tomas said. "And smarter. And luckier." He almost smiled. "And if we're not lucky—if we lose someone, if we lose everyone—we'll still have been the ones who knocked politely on doors and offered a way out, instead of the ones who built the fire."

Ferren studied him for a moment. Then, unexpectedly, the boy grinned—a flash of fifteen showing through the grief like sun through cloud. "Marisol used to say the river remembers everyone who's kind on it. She said it's the oldest road there is, and roads keep faith better than people do."

"Marisol sounds like she was worth knowing."

"She was." Ferren stood, brushing splinters from his trousers, and paused at the barge rail. "One more thing. Word from the market—take it or leave it. The Whitlock girl. The charm-seller." He hesitated. "Corvane's men were asking about her yesterday. Not arresting. Asking. Writing things down. Like she's not just another name on a list. Like she's a page they've been looking for."

Tomas felt something cold slide down his spine that had nothing to do with the dawn air. "Why her?"

"Don't know. But the criers say registration closes in twenty-six days, and the polite man with the grey coat doesn't waste questions on small fish." Ferren hopped down onto the wharf, landed light as a cat, and glanced back up. "If you're going to move the gifted out, Tomas Vey, you might want to start with her."

He was gone into the coal-smoke and the greying streets before Tomas could answer, and Tomas stood at the rail a long time, watching the first light come up the river, thinking about a charm-seller he'd seen exactly once—a glimpse, months ago, of a young woman at her market stall arranging luck-knots with careful, practiced hands, wearing smallness like armor.

Twenty-six days, he thought. And somewhere in Greyhollow, a polite man in a grey coat was already asking her name.
