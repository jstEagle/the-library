## 5. Inquisitor Corvane

The charm-maker's shop smelled of dried lavender and fear.

Corvane stood in the doorway a moment longer than he needed to, letting the smell settle into his lungs. Fear had a hundred perfumes in Greyhollow—sweat under lavender here, bile under incense there, the copper tang of blood under everything if you waited long enough—but it always arrived first, ahead of the man or woman who wore it, like a dog running before its master's cart.

"Master Aldous Fenwick." He read the name from the registration ledger without looking up. "Registered gifted, class three, minor sympathetic workings. Luck-knots, weather-sense, mending charms." He let the pause stretch. "You came forward on the fourth day of the Edict. That was prompt of you."

Fenwick was sixty if he was a day, with ink-stained fingers and the stooped shoulders of a man who had spent forty years bent over a workbench making other people's lives marginally luckier. He wiped his palms on his apron twice in the space of a breath.

"I've nothing to hide, Inquisitor."

"No one does," Corvane said pleasantly. "That's rather the point of the Edict."

He walked the shop slowly while two of his men—Harrow, broad and silent as furniture, and young Pell, who still flinched at raised voices—took up positions by the door and window. The shelves were neat as scripture: bundles of knotwork in red thread, jars of fever-tea, small tin charms shaped like suns for children's cradles. Corvane touched none of them. He had learned long ago that touching things made owners defensive; looking at things made them talk.

"You mend broken crockery," he said. "Sympathetically. The pieces remember being whole, and you remind them."

"It's—it's a parlour trick, truly. My grandmother taught me. Harmless."

"Harmless." Corvane picked up a hairpin from the counter—not a charm, just a hairpin, dropped there that morning by some customer—and turned it over. "My grandmother taught me to whittle. Birds, mostly. She said my hands were too clever to be idle." He set the pin down precisely where it had lain. "Where were you during the Sundering War, Master Fenwick?"

The question landed the way he intended, soft as snowfall and cold as the same. Fenwick blinked. "I—I was a boy. Nine years old when the war ended."

"Mm. So you remember the burning years. The guild wars before that." Corvane drifted along the shelves. "Do you know what I remember? The smell of a city called Vaelmoor after the mage-council tore itself apart. I was eleven. We lived close enough that the ash reached us for three days. My mother hung wet linen in every window and told us it was fog." He smiled at a jar of fever-tea as though it had said something charming. "It was not fog."

"Inquisitor, I swear on my mother's grave—"

"Don't swear. Swearing is for people who think words are evidence." He turned and gave Fenwick his full attention, which was, he knew, a terrible thing to receive. He kept his voice gentle. "Tell me about your neighbors. Who else in this street works quietly? Not the registered—the unregistered. The ones who never came forward. The hedge-healers, the weather-readers, the ones who sell charms out of market stalls and call them 'keepsakes.'"

Fenwick's face did something complicated. Corvane watched all of it: the flicker toward the window, the swallow, the way the man's hands curled as if around something he wanted to protect.

"There aren't any," Fenwick said.

"Of course," Corvane agreed warmly. "There never are." He crossed to the counter and produced, from inside his coat, a folded sheet of paper—a list, though he held it so the light showed nothing through it but shadow. "Here is what happens now, Master Fenwick. Nothing. You will not be arrested today. Your shop stays open. Your neighbors keep buying your luck-knots." He laid the paper down between them, face-down, like a card in a game. "And every month, on the first, a clerk will call on you with questions like today's. And one day—and it may be a year from now, or five—you will answer one of those questions honestly, because you are tired, or frightened, or angry at someone, and you will give me a name. Perhaps without meaning to. Perhaps you'll only say *I don't know why she'd do that, she always seemed ordinary*, and that will be enough."

"I would never inform on anyone," Fenwick whispered.

"I believe you believe that." Corvane straightened his coat. "Most people would never inform. They simply inform. It happens the way rot happens—in the dark, a little at a time, until the whole beam gives way." At the door he paused. "The tea. Fever-tea. Whose recipe?"

"My grandmother's. Ordinary herbs. Yarrow, willow bark—"

"With a word spoken over the steeping?" He watched the man's eyes and saw the truth arrive there a half-second before the lie could form. "Yes," Corvane said softly. "That's what I thought. Good day, Master Fenwick. Keep your shop warm. Winter is coming early this year—I can feel it in my knees, and my knees are never wrong."

* * *

Outside, Greyhollow lay under a sky the color of old pewter. Market day, and yet the great square was only half-full; the Edict had done its quiet work already, thinning crowds the way frost thins late flowers. Criers had moved on to other quarters. On the guildhall notice-board across the lane, someone had nailed up the Edict's proclamation and someone else had nailed a dead rat over it, which Corvane found either blasphemous or hopeful depending on his mood.

"The rat's new," Pell said, following his gaze.

"Everything new is information." Corvane walked, hands clasped behind him, letting Harrow loom at his shoulder. "Someone hates the crown enough to spend a rat. Note the street. Someone in these shops is angry enough to be careless, and careless people talk to their friends, and friends talk to charm-makers, and charm-makers—" he almost laughed—"talk to me."

They took the long route back to the Inquisition house, past the tanner's quarter, where the air turned thick and sweet-rotten. Corvane slowed at the corner where the pyre-stake stood, its base scorched black from three burnings now. The wood was fresh; they'd rebuilt it higher this time, by order, so the crowds could see. Crowds had been thinning anyway. Fear worked better at a distance. Up close it curdled into something else—he'd seen it happen in Vaelmoor, in the last days of the war: fear turning to grief, grief turning to fury, fury turning to fire. The crown believed fear was a well that never ran dry. Corvane knew better. Wells ran dry. Then people started digging their own.

"Inquisitor." Pell again, younger, eager, holding out a leather folio. "Reports from the river districts. Three boats came in last night with cargo manifests that don't reconcile. And there's talk of a smuggler named—"

"Later." He took the folio anyway and weighed it in his hand without opening it. "What do you see when you look at that stake, Pell?"

The young man glanced at it. His throat moved. "Justice, sir."

"Liar," Corvane said, without heat. "You see a warning, same as everyone. Justice would have been catching her before she healed a smuggler's lung for free and vanished into the alleys. A warning is what you build when justice failed and you want the failure to look intentional." He handed the folio back. "Remember that distinction. It will save your soul, if you have one, and your career, if you'd rather have that."

Pell looked at him with an expression caught between admiration and alarm. Corvane had grown used to both. He was, he knew, regarded within the Inquisition as the crown's best hunter and its strangest—too patient, too soft-spoken, prone to standing in empty rooms for hours. They said he grieved what he hunted. They said it as an accusation. He had stopped correcting them years ago, because it was true, and because the truth made him better at his work than any amount of zeal ever could. Zeal burned hot and blind. Grief saw everything, because grief had already lost everything and so had nothing left to lose.

* * *

The Inquisition house in Greyhollow had been a merchant's mansion once, confiscated in the war years, its ballroom now lined with filing cabinets instead of mirrors. Corvane dismissed Harrow and Pell at the door and climbed alone to the top floor, to the room that was not on any inventory and whose key he carried against his chest like a reliquary.

Inside: a desk, a lamp, one chair, and on the wall, framed behind glass, a portrait.

Elara Whitlock, painted twenty-two years ago, when the Whitlock name still opened doors instead of graves. She sat angled toward the painter with her chin lifted, dark-haired, sharp-eyed, one hand resting on the arm of the chair as if she might rise at any moment and leave—leave the room, the city, the whole arrangement of the world—if it displeased her. The painter had captured that. Corvane had studied the portrait often enough to know it wasn't vanity in the pose. It was readiness. Elara Whitlock had always looked like a woman halfway out the door.

He poured himself a measure of the cheap plum brandy he kept in the desk drawer and did not drink it. He never drank it. He only liked knowing it was there, the way a man likes knowing the road home exists even when he has no intention of taking it.

"We were twenty-four," he told the portrait. "You remember. You pretended not to, but you remember better than I do—you remembered everything, that was your curse."

They had met at a lecture on resonance theory, of all the absurd venues, both of them too poor for the wine afterward and splitting the cost of bread and hard cheese on a bridge over the Grey. She had argued with him for four hours about whether magic was a muscle or a language, and she had been wrong, magnificently wrong, and he had known within the first hour that he would follow her argument anywhere it went for the rest of his life. He had never told her that. There had been a war coming, and then a war, and then an Edict, and somewhere in the grinding between those things she had chosen a side he could not follow—or rather, she had chosen no side at all, which the crown considered worse, and which Corvane privately considered the only honest position left standing.

She was nine years dead now. Fever, officially. Unofficially, no record at all, which was its own kind of sentence. Her daughter had been twelve when it happened. Marguerite. A quiet child, according to the parish rolls; apprenticed at fourteen to one Odile Vasska, a licensed apothecary of unimpeachable dullness. Corvane had pulled every record on the girl months ago and found exactly what a careful person would want him to find: nothing. No incidents. No registrations. No whispers in taverns, no anonymous letters, no debts, no lovers, no grudges. Twelve years of a life sanded down to smoothness.

No one was that smooth. Smoothness was a surface, and surfaces were made to hide things.

"You hid her well," he murmured to the portrait. "Or she hid herself. Or—" he swirled the brandy, watching the lamplight bend through it—"someone hid something in her. Which is the thought I keep circling, isn't it, Elara? You were never careless in your life. You were the most deliberate person I ever knew. And then in the worst year of the war, at the height of the purges, you retired to Greyhollow, married a wool merchant, and had a baby. Women flee wars. Women don't usually flee *toward* the city where the guildhall still smolders." He set the glass down untouched. "Unless the baby was the mission."

The portrait offered its half-risen silence.

He had gone to see the wool merchant once, in the early days, wearing a clerk's face. A bland man, dead of drink within two years of the wedding, leaving wife and daughter comfortably obscure. Comfortably. Corvane had interviewed the widow of a guild archivist last autumn who'd raved, near her end, about a sealing ritual performed in the final winter of the war—something the Wolf of Winter had done at the last, something hidden, something *kept*. He'd assumed senility mixed with legend. He no longer assumed anything. Legends, in his experience, were history with the bodies removed, and bodies had a way of surfacing when the ground froze.

A knock at the door. Harrow's particular double-tap.

"Not now."

"It's the list, sir. From the registrars. Thirty-day mark's tomorrow." A pause. "You'll want to see the count."

Corvane closed his eyes briefly. "Bring it in."

Harrow laid the folio on the desk. Corvane opened it and ran his finger down the columns—names, parishes, classifications—and felt the map assembling itself behind his eyes, street by street, the way it had for weeks. Four hundred and six names registered in Greyhollow. Perhaps a third genuine. Perhaps a tenth dangerous. All of them useful. Every confession he drew out, every neighbor's hesitation he logged, every shopkeeper who suddenly couldn't meet his eye—all of it went onto the great chart in the ballroom below, pins and threads and inked circles, until the city's secret circulatory system lay exposed like a diagram of veins under skin. He wasn't hunting witches. Witches were endless; cut one down and two grew back, quieter. He was hunting the pattern. Find the pattern, find the routes, find the safehouses, find the ones moving the gifted out ahead of the fires—and find, at the center of it all, whatever it was the old bloodlines had sent him north to dig out of Greyhollow's frozen ground.

His finger stopped on a name near the bottom of the page. A charm-seller. Market day stall, luck-knots and fever-charms, licensed under the apothecary guild for twelve years, no irregularities.

Marguerite Whitlock.

She had come forward after all. Registered, class two, minor workings. Prompt. Compliant. Perfectly, immaculately smooth.

Corvane read her line three times. Then he laughed—one short breath of it, more surprise than humor—and looked up at the portrait.

"She registered, Elara. Your daughter walked into the registrar's office and put her name on the crown's paper." He tapped the page. "Either she is exactly what she appears—a small talent hiding in plain sight, the oldest trick there is—or she wants me reading her file instead of searching her cellar. And I cannot decide which possibility I like less."

Because the truth was this, and he admitted it only here, in this room, to a dead woman and untouched brandy: he did not want it to be her. Twelve years ago he had stood at Elara Whitlock's funeral-orbit, far enough away to be a stranger, close enough to see a dark-haired girl of twelve holding her mother's cold hand and refusing, absolutely refusing, to cry where anyone could witness it. He had thought: *there is Elara all over again.* He had thought: *gods help whoever comes for that one.*

He rose and stood before the portrait, close enough to see the craquelure in the paint, the fine web of years spread across her lifted chin.

"They're sending me after the daughter next," he said quietly. "The bloodlines want something in Greyhollow, and every road I chart bends toward her door. So I'll go carefully. Slower than they'd like. Because if I'm wrong about her, I burn an innocent woman to warm the hands of men who deserve the fire themselves. And if I'm right—"

He touched the frame, once, lightly, the way you touch a headstone.

"—if I'm right, then whatever Elara died protecting is in that girl, and they mean to crack her open like a vault, and I am expected to hold the crowbar." He stepped back. His reflection ghosted faintly in the glass over hers, a grey man superimposed on a bright woman. "So. The daughter will be harder. Good. Hard things deserve patience."

Below, in the ballroom, the great chart waited with its web of threads, and one corner of it—Greyhollow's market quarter—had been left deliberately blank. He went down and stood before it for a long time, pin in hand, the lamplight shivering.

Then he wrote a single word on a fresh card and set it, unpinned, at the center of the empty space:

*Whitlock.*

Outside, far to the north, the first unnatural flakes of the season's early snow had begun to fall—though no man in Greyhollow yet knew it—and somewhere along the dark river road, a smuggler's boat was already cutting east through the reeds, carrying what the crown was hunting toward the place it would all come to a head.
