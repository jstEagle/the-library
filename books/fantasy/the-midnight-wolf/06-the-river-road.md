## 6. The River Road

Tomas Vane had smuggled a lot of things down the Ashwater in twelve years of running the river: untaxed brandy, unlicensed steel, letters that would hang their writers if read aloud, and once — memorably — a sleeping bear that had woken somewhere around the second lock and required an entire crate of honeyed pears to calm. He had never smuggled a person. He had certainly never smuggled a person who looked at him like Marguerite Whitlock was looking at him now, standing on the rotting dock at Miller's Bend with her mentor's arm over her shoulder and her whole face saying *I do not trust you, but I have run out of other options.*

"Two hours," he'd told her back in Greyhollow, pressing the note into her hand behind the charm-seller's shuttered stall. *Dock at Miller's Bend, midnight, come alone or don't come.* He hadn't expected her to bring Odile Fenn. He hadn't expected Odile Fenn to look like that, either — like a candle burned down to the socket, gray-skinned, swaying even in still air.

"She can't make the river run," Tomas said now, not unkindly. "Three days to Thornhaven, four if the patrols are thick. She'll die on my deck."

"Then we'll bury her at the bend," Marguerite said, "and I'll keep walking. But you're going to take us as far as she can go."

It wasn't a request, exactly, but it wasn't a threat either. It was something in between, delivered by a woman with ash still on her cuffs from burning her own ledger, and Tomas found himself — annoyingly — nodding before he'd decided to.

"The herbalist," he said. "Mother Hessel, at Willowmere. She owes me for a shipment of valerian root that never got taxed." He looked at Odile, really looked at her, at the fine tremor in her hands and the way her breath whistled faintly at the bottom of each exhale. "She's too far gone to hide well. You understand that. If hunters come asking—"

"They won't find her," Marguerite said. "Hessel hides people. That's half of what herbalists do."

"And the other half?"

Marguerite met his eyes. Her gaze was steady and dark and entirely closed to him, a door pulled shut against weather. "The other half is none of your business."

He liked her better for that, which was inconvenient.

* * *

They loaded Odile onto the *Kestrel* wrapped in fish canvas, because a body under fish canvas reads as cargo and cargo doesn't get questions asked at the toll-locks. Tomas had three crew: Doss, who could sleep through cannon fire; Wren, who couldn't sleep through anything; and old Maret, who piloted and pretended not to see everything, which was its own kind of skill. None of them asked why the cargo breathed. On the Ashwater, breathing cargo meant one thing only, these days, and nobody wanted to say it out loud where the reeds might be listening.

Odile slept in the hold among the crates, propped against a coil of rope, and woke twice in the night. The first time she called Marguerite's name in a voice like wind through a broken shutter. The second time she called someone else's — a name Tomas didn't catch, low and strange, a name that made the air feel colder when she said it — and then she laughed softly to herself and went back to sleep smiling, which frightened Tomas more than anything else that night.

Marguerite didn't sleep at all. She sat beside her mentor with a knife across her knees and watched Tomas work the tiller with eyes that never quite softened.

"You knew her," she said near dawn, when the mist came off the water so thick the banks dissolved. "Odile. Before."

"I ran goods past the guildhall for years. She bought things off me no honest woman needed. Moonwort. Grave-dirt candles. A book of sigils I stole without knowing what it was until she told me, and then I wished I hadn't known." He turned the tiller a hand's width. "She paid fair. She never once used magic on me, though I know she could have. That's more than most people manage."

"That's all?"

"That's all." He glanced at her. "You're checking whether I'm a risk."

"Aren't you?"

"Everyone's a risk, love. The trick is choosing which risks you take." He nodded toward the hold. "I took this one because of a hedge-witch in the tanner's quarter named Ilsabet Corren. She healed my sister's lung-fever when my sister was nine years old, and charged me two coppers and a smile. They burned her last week. So no — I'm not a risk. I'm a man doing arithmetic."

Marguerite was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed, some of the door opening a crack. "What did they burn her for?"

"A fever-charm. One fever-charm, worked for free, witnessed by one frightened apprentice who traded her name for his own safety." Tomas heard his own voice go flat and hard and let it. "So whatever you are, Miss Whitlock — and you're something, don't waste both our time denying it — you picked the right week to need a boat."

* * *

Willowmere was a day and a half upriver, a crooked cottage leaning against a willow so old its roots drank from the Ashwater itself. Mother Hessel answered the door before they knocked — small, brown as a nut, sharp-eyed — and took one look at Odile and said, "Bring her in, then, and don't dither on my step. Bad luck lingers where grief hesitates."

Inside smelled of every plant Tomas couldn't name and forty he could. Hessel settled Odile into a bed beneath the eaves, fed her something bitter from a black bottle, and shooed them all out of the room like cats.

Outside, in the garden where rows of feverfew stood sentinel, Marguerite stopped walking and didn't turn around.

"How long?" she said to Hessel's retreating back. "How long can you keep her?"

"As long as she has," the old woman said without turning. "Which is longer than you'd guess and shorter than you want. Go on. The river won't wait and neither will I."

Tomas expected argument. He expected tears, demands, some last desperate bargaining. Instead Marguerite stood very still among the herbs, and when she finally turned her face was dry and terrible and calm, and she walked back to the boat without a word, and Tomas understood that he had just watched a woman leave behind the only family she had left, on purpose, so that family might live a little longer.

He didn't say anything about it. Some things you carry silently, like ballast.

* * *

The trouble started at Cordon Lock, two days later.

There were six boats waiting, which was wrong — usually two, maybe three — and two men in crown gray at the lock-gate, checking papers with a thoroughness that hadn't been fashionable on the Ashwater since the war. Tomas felt Wren go tense beside him and put a hand on the boy's shoulder: easy, easy, nothing yet.

The gray men worked down the line. Cargo declared. Cargo poked. One boat ahead of them, a grain barge, got its whole deck torn apart over a rumor of hidden papers, and the master stood watching with the dead expression of a man recalculating his life.

"Toll and manifest," said the nearer gray man when the *Kestrel* slid up. Young. New to it. His eyes went over the deck and snagged — Tomas saw it happen — on Marguerite, sitting amidships with a blanket around her shoulders and a look of exhausted ordinariness arranged carefully over her face.

"Brandy and salt-fish," Tomas said, handing over the manifest. "Out of Greyhollow, bound for Thornhaven markets."

The young one barely glanced at it. He was still looking at Marguerite. "And her?"

"My sister," Tomas said, without a heartbeat's pause, and marveled a little at how easily the lie sat in his mouth, how natural his arm felt settling around her shoulders. "Widowed. Coming to stay with family. She doesn't travel well, as you can see, so if we could hurry along—"

"Name?"

"Whitlock," Marguerite said, and Tomas's blood stopped and restarted. Of all the names. She said it mildly, tiredly, like a woman naming a thing too common to matter. "Marguerite Whitlock."

The gray man frowned. Somewhere behind his eyes, a bell was ringing faintly, a bell connected to a list, to a proclamation, to thirty days and the Edict of Ash. Tomas watched him reach for it — watched the frown deepen, watched his hand drift toward the ledger chained to his belt—

"Her eyes are bad and her feet are worse," Tomas said loudly, "and if you've got a complaint about widows, friend, you can write it to the crown along with your next tax grievance."

The older gray man, farther up the line, called out: "Orrin! Leave the widow be. We've the barge yet."

A pause. A breath. Then Orrin stamped the manifest, muttered something about suspicious sisters, and waved them through, and the lock-gates groaned open, and the *Kestrel* slid through into the long green quiet beyond while Tomas's heart hammered against his ribs like a trapped bird.

They didn't speak until the lock was a mile behind them and the river bent away from the road.

"Whitlock," he said finally. "You gave him your true name."

"It's the best lie there is," Marguerite said, staring at the water. "Nobody believes a fugitive would use her real name. And half the kingdom is named Whitlock anyway. There were four families of them in Greyhollow alone." She paused. "You called me your sister."

"Habit. I've a lot of sisters. Most of them fictional." He kept his eyes on the channel. "That was sloppy, what you did. Clever and sloppy. Don't do clever-and-sloppy again unless I'm the one being clever."

"Noted." Something almost like a smile touched her mouth and vanished. Then, quieter, looking at the water instead of him: "Why did you lie? At the lock. You could have handed me over. There's likely coin in it now. There will certainly be more coin later, and you're a smuggler, and I'm told smugglers follow profit the way rivers follow gravity."

Tomas was silent for a while. The oaks went by. A heron lifted off the shallows, unhurried, ancient.

"When I was eleven," he said, "my sister coughed herself half to death, and a hedge-witch fixed her for two coppers. Nobody paid that woman anything worth dying for, and she died anyway, screaming, in front of a crowd that mostly looked ashamed." He adjusted the tiller. "I've been doing arithmetic ever since, Miss Whitlock. Two coppers against a life. It always comes out the same way. So no — I didn't lie for profit. I lied because it was cheap."

Marguerite looked at him then, really looked, and he watched her decide something — watch the scales tip behind her eyes, the door open another careful inch.

"What do you think I am?" she asked.

"Gifted," Tomas said. "More than a charm-seller, less than whatever the criers are wetting themselves about. Running from something specific, not just the Edict. And scared of it in a way that's older than this week." He shrugged. "But I figure you'll tell me when telling me costs less than not telling me. Until then, you're my widowed sister Marguerite, and my sister needs sleep."

She held his gaze a moment longer. "That's it? You won't press?"

"Pressing cargo makes it spoil." He nodded toward the stern where a bedroll waited. "Sleep. We make Thornhaven tomorrow, and after Thornhaven it gets complicated."

* * *

Thornhaven was complicated.

The safehouse was a tannery warehouse that smelled of lime and old hides, and the people inside it were — Tomas searched for the word and settled, eventually, on *catastrophe*. Sefa, the disgraced battle-mage, greeted Marguerite by insulting her boots and then her posture and then, comprehensively, her entire approach to personal defense, concluding that Marguerite would be dead within a week if left unsupervised. Pip, a child of perhaps eight who appeared from inside a locked storeroom that had been locked when Tomas arrived, informed Marguerite gravely that her heart-lock was "rusty but good iron underneath." Bramble, the shapeshifter stuck halfway between fox and woman, circled Marguerite twice, sniffed, and pronounced: "Winter. She smells of winter. Wrong season for it."

Marguerite went very still at that. Tomas watched the stillness, filed it away, and said nothing.

Supper was barley soup and stolen ham, eaten off crates, and it should have been miserable — fugitives in a hide-smelling warehouse with the Edict of Ash nailed to every church door in the valley — but instead it was loud, because Sefa argued with Bramble about whether foxes could taste sorrow (Bramble maintained they could, and that it tasted like chicken), and Pip demonstrated lock-picking on Sefa's belt pouch until Sefa noticed and threatened her with a lecture on warding theory, which Pip accepted as payment rather than punishment, and Wren laughed for the first time in a month.

Marguerite sat at the edge of it, half-in and half-out, and Tomas caught her watching them with an expression he recognized because he'd worn it himself once, years ago, arriving broke and hunted at this same warehouse: the look of a woman who had forgotten what it felt like to sit in a warm room with people who weren't afraid of her.

Afterward, when the others had bedded down among the hides, Marguerite stayed by the banked fire, turning something over in her hands. From the shadows Tomas could see it was a pendant on a leather cord — pale, curved, catching the ember-light like a tooth. Like a wolf's tooth.

"Won't sleep?" he asked, coming to sit nearby.

"Bad dreams lately." She closed her fist around the pendant. "Tomas. Why does a smuggler keep a safehouse full of mages and shapeshifters? What are you building here?"

He considered lying, decided against it, and didn't know why.

"Nothing," he said. "Yet. Just people who needed walls and had none. It keeps happening. I keep letting it." He looked into the embers. "My mother used to say the river doesn't choose what it carries. It just refuses to let anything drown that fights hard enough. I think I caught the habit."

Marguerite was quiet a long moment. The fire ticked. Somewhere overhead, Pip turned in her sleep and murmured to a lock in her dreams.

"If I tell you something," Marguerite said finally, "you'll wish you hadn't heard it. And you won't be able to unknow it. And knowing it will paint a target on this warehouse and everyone sleeping in it."

Tomas looked at her — at the frost-pale pendant in her fist, at the exhaustion carved under her eyes, at the door she was holding open against her own better judgment.

"Then keep it," he said. "For now. But hear me, Marguerite Whitlock, widowed sister of mine: whatever's coming downriver after you, it's already on the water. I saw the lock-gates today. Six boats, crown gray, checking faces. They're not hunting smugglers anymore. They're hunting something specific." He leaned forward, embers lighting the hard planes of his face. "So you've got until they arrive to decide who to trust. And when you decide—" he tapped his own chest, twice, "—remember the arithmetic."

She almost smiled. Outside, far off toward the city, a bell began to ring — wrong, ragged, alarm-pattern — and both of them went still, listening, as somewhere out in the dark the hunt moved one mile closer.
