## 7. Memories Not Mine

The fever came on the second night at the safehouse, and it did not come gently.

Marguerite remembered fragments of its arrival: Tomas's rough hand against her brow, the startled look he exchanged with the smuggler called Dree; the creak of a cot being dragged closer to the stove; the taste of willow bark tea gone bitter and cold in a cup she was too weak to lift. Then the fever folded over her like a wave closing above a swimmer's head, and the safehouse—the low-beamed room above the cooper's shop, smelling of sawdust and river damp—was gone.

What replaced it was white.

* * *

Snowfields.

Not the soft, civilized snow that dusted Greyhollow's rooftops in deep winter and melted by market day. This was an ocean of it, rolling to every horizon under a sky the color of old iron, wind-carved into ridges and hollows like something alive and sleeping. The cold here was not weather. It was a country. It had borders and laws and a language made of silence.

She stood in the middle of it, and she was not herself.

Or rather—she was wearing someone else's standing. The body she inhabited moved without her permission, turning to face the wind, and the motion had a grace hers had never possessed, a sureness in the knees, a looseness in the shoulders that came from years of walking wild places alone. She looked down at hands gloved in grey fur and felt—not saw, *felt*—a spear strapped across a broad back, and a heartbeat beneath her ribs that was slow and vast as a tide.

And inside her skull, a voice that was not a voice. A knowing that spoke without sound, the way you know your own name.

*The border holds.*

Marguerite tried to scream and found she had no mouth that obeyed her. She tried to run and her borrowed legs only turned, patient, toward the north, where the snow rose into mountains like teeth.

*It has held for sixty years. It will hold for sixty more.*

The voice was a woman's. Young, though ageless underneath—the way a river is young no matter how long it has run. There was loneliness in it so dense it had weight, pressing against the inside of Marguerite's chest like a hand.

*I have not spoken to another living soul in nine winters,* the woman thought, and the thought carried with it the memory of the last one: a face, wind-burned, laughing, already receding down a trail of footprints that the snow was patiently erasing. *They bring me supplies to the cairn at the valley mouth. They do not climb the last ridge anymore. I told them not to. It is safer for them. Safer is lonelier. I have stopped telling the difference.*

Marguerite understood, with a horror that had nowhere to go because she had no throat to scream from, that she was inside this woman. Not watching her. *Being* her. Feeling the ache of old frost-scars when the temperature dropped. Feeling the way the woman's eyes went, unbidden, south—always south—toward warmth she would never touch again.

Because the woman could not leave. Because if she left—

The snowfield shuddered.

Far to the north, past the mountain teeth, something turned over in its sleep beneath the ice. Marguerite felt it through the soles of her feet, through the bones the woman had walked on for sixty years, a vibration older than kingdoms: vast, hungry, and bound.

*There you are,* the woman thought at it, and there was tenderness in the thought, which was the most terrible thing yet. *Sleep. I am still here. I am still the door.*

The thing beneath the ice pressed back against the binding, once, testing—and the pressure came up through the woman's body like a black tide rising in her chest, filling her lungs, whispering in a language of hunger: *let me out let me out let me out let me—*

*No,* said the woman, gentle as a mother closing a door on a crying child. *I am sorry. No.*

* * *

The dream changed the way dreams do, all at once, without transition—one breath she was the woman on the snowfield, the next she was kneeling on stone floors in a circle of robed figures while torchlight guttered, and the air smelled of ozone and blood and scorched feathers.

A sealing ritual. She knew that word now—*sealing*—the way you know a word after you've heard it in a language you don't speak. She knew it the way the woman knew it, and the woman was here too, kneeling at the center of the circle, and she was dying.

*It was not supposed to be me.*

The thought came cracked, bleeding. Around the circle, faces swam in torchlight—young faces, terrified faces, guild robes scorched at the hems. The Sundering War was ending around them; even kneeling here, Marguerite could feel it through the stone, the whole world shaking itself apart somewhere beyond these walls, cities falling, the sky full of wings and fire. And at the center of it, the thing that had caused it—or been blamed for it, the memories tangled here, knotted, she couldn't pull them apart—the thing that had to be put away forever.

*The rite requires a guardian willing to be the lock,* the woman thought, and her thoughts were slowing now, thickening, like water going to ice. *Willing. That was the word they kept saying. Willing. As if I had a choice. As if anyone does, at the end of a war.*

An old man's hands pressed something into hers—a tooth, long and curved, yellowed ivory that burned cold against her palm. A wolf's tooth. *The focus,* his voice said, from outside her, muffled, urgent. *Hold it. Whatever happens, hold it, and think of the winter, and do not let go.*

*Do not let go.* She would hear those words again someday, Marguerite realized dimly, from another mouth, in another life, in a cottage that smelled of herbs. *Do not let go, child.*

But the ritual was going wrong.

She felt it going wrong the way you feel a stair missing in the dark. One of the circle faltered—someone screamed, someone else broke formation, a name cried out in a voice abruptly stilled—and the power they had been weaving together snapped sideways, all of it, a river bursting its banks, and instead of flowing into the binding it flowed into *her*, into the kneeling woman, flooding every channel of her body with cold and more cold until cold stopped being a sensation and became what she was made of.

*I am the seal now,* the woman thought, wonderingly, as her human heart stuttered and slowed. *I am the lock and the door and the winter both. They meant to bind it to the mountain. They have bound it to me.*

And then—this part came fragmented, shattered like ice on rock—she was changing. Fur where skin had been. Eyes that saw heat and sorrow and the shapes of names. Paws that left prints of hoarfrost. A shape vast and white moving through the snowfields that were suddenly, impossibly, hers, that answered her moods, that froze or thawed with her breathing—

*A wolf made of winter,* Marguerite thought, from wherever she floated inside the dying woman, and the words rang like a bell she had heard before, in a story, in a warning, in Odile's careful voice saying things she had never quite explained. *The Wolf of Winter. Oh gods. Oh gods, that's who—*

The wolf lay down at the edge of the bound thing's prison, curled around the mountain like a dog around a hearth, and began the long work of holding.

Sixty years. Nine winters without a voice. The cairn at the valley mouth. The last ridge no one climbed anymore.

*It is safer for them. Safer is lonelier.*

* * *

Then fire. Then screaming. Then—

The dream lurched again, and now the memories were coming faster, tumbling, a landslide, decades compressed into heartbeats. The wolf aging without aging. The wars below growing smaller, fading into legend, into nursery tales, into nothing. The woman inside the wolf growing quiet, then quieter, her name wearing smooth as a pebble under water—

And other hands. Newer hands. Hands that smelled of lavender and ink, trembling, working a rite of hiding rather than holding—pulling something out of the ancient, exhausted seal, some essential piece, some *memory*, winding it tight, folding it small—

*She cannot carry it forever,* a young woman's voice said, fierce and weeping. Elara's voice. *Mother*—no, not mother, not yet, but a voice that would become a mother's voice within the year—*she's carried it for two hundred years and she is coming apart at the seams. Someone else must hold a piece. Someone hidden. Someone the bloodlines will never find.*

*You understand what you're asking?* Odile's voice, younger, harder. *If we place it in a vessel, the vessel becomes the heir. Everything hunts heirs.*

*I'm asking my daughter to live,* Elara said. *Whatever else that costs.*

*She's not born yet.*

*She will be.*

Hands on Marguerite—on a newborn Marguerite, tiny and warm and screaming—and the sealed memory settling into her like a coal sinking into snow, hissing, going dark, going deep. And the last thing, the very last thing, before the dream released her: Elara Whitlock bending close, her hair falling around them both like a curtain against the world, whispering words into a newborn ear that the newborn would never consciously hear but that had waited twelve years in the marrow of her bones:

*Forgive me. Sleep, little one. And if you ever wake—find people worth waking for.*

* * *

Marguerite woke drowning.

That was her first coherent thought: *drowning*, except the water was wrong—it was dry, it was air, it was her own bedclothes twisted around her arms, and she was gasping at a ceiling of river-boat planks while her heart slammed like someone beating on a locked door.

She sat up too fast. The room tilted. Sweat had soaked her shift through, and the stove had burned down to embers, and somewhere in the building below a man coughed in his sleep, and none of it mattered, none of it registered, because—

Her hands.

Her palms were white.

Not pale. *White.* Frosted. A delicate crust of ice feathered across both palms and up her fingers, ferning patterns like frost on a windowpane in deepest winter, glittering faintly in the ember-light. When she breathed out, her breath bloomed silver in the midsummer-warm room.

Midsummer. It was midsummer. Tomas had complained about it just yesterday, wiping his neck, cursing the river mosquitos, saying the cooper's loft turned into an oven by noon. She could hear insects singing outside the shuttered window. The night was heavy and warm and smelled of green things.

And her hands were frozen.

"No," she whispered, and her breath fogged. "No, no, no—"

She scrubbed her palms against the blanket. The frost didn't melt so much as *retreat*, pulling back into her skin like something withdrawing from a losing fight, and where it went it left her flesh tingling, numb at the fingertips, alive with a feeling like distant bells. Under the numbness, deeper than the numbness, something else stirred. A patience. A vastness. An awareness at the bottom of her mind that had not been there twelve days ago—or that had always been there and had only now opened one eye.

*The border holds,* said a voice that was not her voice, in the quietest corner of her skull. *It has held for sixty years. It will hold for—*

"Stop." Her voice cracked. She pressed her frosted hands over her ears as if that could shut out something already inside. "Stop it. You're dead. You're a story. You're two hundred years dead and I am *Marguerite Whitlock* and I sell luck-knots for three coppers apiece—"

But even as she said it she heard how thin it sounded. How much it sounded like a woman kneeling in a circle of torchlight, insisting she had a choice.

The door creaked.

Tomas stood in the gap, a candle in one hand and a cup of something steaming in the other, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop dead. His eyes went to the window—checking, she realized later, whether hunters had come in the night—and then to her, and then to her hands, and she watched him see the last traces of frost sublimating off her knuckles in little wisps of white, watched his face go carefully, deliberately blank in the way of a man who had spent his life learning which things were safest not to see.

"You were calling out," he said quietly. "In your sleep."

"What did I say?"

He hesitated. "Nothing," he said, which was a lie, and they both knew it was a lie, and she loved him a little for telling it anyway. He set the cup on the floor by the cot—willow bark, by the smell, brewed fresh—and crouched down so their eyes were level, which was a thing he did sometimes that disarmed her completely, a smuggler's habit of meeting everyone straight on. "Fever broke about an hour ago. You want to tell me what happened?"

"I dreamed." The words came out of her before she could weigh them. "Someone else's dream. Someone else's—" *life*, she almost said, and strangled the word, and finished lamely, "—fever."

Tomas looked at her for a long moment. Candlelight moved on his face. Outside, the insects sang their oblivious summer song, and somewhere down the hall Dree snored, and the world continued being warm and ordinary, and Marguerite sat in the middle of it with winter in her hands.

"There's bread in the box," Tomas said finally, standing. "Eat something. And Marguerite—" He paused at the door, not turning all the way around. "Whatever you dreamed. Whoever dreamed it. You're not doing it alone in here. That's not how this works. That's not how any of this works."

He left before she could answer, which was a mercy, because she could not have answered. She looked down at her palms—ordinary now, pink, human, faintly chilled—and slowly turned them over, as if the answer might be written there.

On the left palm, in lines of frost that were already melting, already vanishing, the last of the dream had left its signature: a single print, small and precise, like the track of an enormous paw.

Downstairs, a floorboard groaned. Footsteps—more than one set, moving through the cooper's shop below, voices murmuring low and urgent. Dree's snores had stopped. In the walls, faintly, absurdly, unmistakably: the click of a lock turning, though no one had touched it.

And from the room at the end of the hall, a child's voice—Pip, the boy they'd brought in off the river two nights ago, the one who hadn't spoken since—saying clearly, conversationally, to no one visible:

"She's awake. I told you she'd be the one."
