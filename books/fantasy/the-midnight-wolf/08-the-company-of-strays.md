## 8. The Company of Strays

The safehouse was a tanner's warehouse that hadn't seen a hide in twenty years, squatting at the crook of the river where Greyhollow's walls forgot to be proud and settled instead for being old. Tomas had bought it with coin he'd earned moving brandy past checkpoints and never asked about twice. The roof leaked in three places. One of them dripped directly onto the chair he'd claimed as his own, which felt about right.

By the fourth night, he had six people sleeping under that leaking roof, and he still wasn't entirely sure how it had happened.

He stood in the doorway of the main room with a crate of dried herring under one arm and counted them like a man counting debts.

Marguerite sat by the fire they'd built in an old rendering pit—too much smoke for comfort, but the chimney drew well enough if you kept the wind-side shutter closed—and she was doing something with her hands that she probably thought looked like mending. It wasn't. She'd been working the same knot for an hour, over and over, a charm-pattern worn into her fingers by twelve years of habit. Luck-knots. Fever-charms. The harmless little weavings of a woman trying very hard to be no one. He'd watched her hands shake earlier when a cart rattled past outside, and watched her smooth them flat again before anyone could see.

Sefa Vane was sharpening a knife by the window, which was what Sefa did instead of talking, sleeping, or apologizing. She'd been a battle-mage once—the kind whose name got spoken in war colleges, according to Bramble, who loved telling stories about other people's downfalls. Then something had happened at Redford Bridge that Sefa refused to discuss, involving a general, a retreat, and eleven men she couldn't save, and now there was a price on her head that made Marguerite's look like a jaywalking fine. Her magic came off her like heat off a forge-stone even when she was doing nothing at all. Tomas had seen the candle flames lean toward her when she walked past.

Pip was in the rafters, because Pip was always in the rafters, small and quick as a squirrel, whispering to things that had no business listening. The locks listened. That was the thing about Pip—nine years old, maybe ten, nobody knew, including possibly Pip—and every lock within thirty feet woke up when the child came near, like dogs hearing their names. The padlock on the cellar door had started opening itself out of what Tomas could only describe as affection.

And on the far side of the room, curled on a pile of sacking with his tail—his actual tail, furred and red and ending in a white tip—wrapped around his knees, was Bramble.

Bramble had been stuck half-fox for four years now. Nobody knew why; shapeshifters didn't exactly publish their failures. One morning he'd tried to come back from fox-shape and only gotten halfway, and there he'd remained: a man's body, a man's voice, and a fox's ears, muzzle, amber eyes, and tail, all of it covered in russet fur from the collarbones up. He'd learned to laugh at himself before anyone else could. It was the saddest kind of armor, and Tomas respected it anyway.

"Stop counting us," Sefa said without looking up from her knife. "You do it every night. We're not going to multiply."

"You might," Tomas said, dropping the herring crate onto the table with a thump. "You seem the type."

That got him the look—the flat, gray, ex-battle-mage look that could curdle milk at forty paces—but he'd been carrying cargo past armed men since he was fourteen, and one sour woman's glare wasn't going to move him off his own floor.

* * *

Supper was herring, hard bread, and a pot of barley porridge that Marguerite had salted with the confidence of someone who had never actually cooked. It was terrible. They ate all of it anyway, because hunger is the great diplomat.

It was Pip who broke the fragile peace, as it usually was.

"Why don't we have a name?"

Tomas looked up from his bowl. "A name."

"The house." Pip gestured with a spoon, scattering porridge. "Safehouses have names. Everyone knows that. Smugglers name everything. You named your boat *Fat Lottie*. That's a stupid name but it's a name." A pause. "Sorry. It is stupid, though."

"*Fat Lottie* is a good boat."

"*Fat Lottie* is a barge with delusions."

From the corner, a dry rustle of laughter—Bramble, tail flicking. "The child has you there."

"I'm saying," Pip pressed on, with the relentless momentum of the young, "that if this is going to be a place, it should be called something. Otherwise it's just a leaky building where we happen to not die."

"It's not going to be a place," Sefa said quietly.

The room went still the way rooms do when someone says the true thing too soon.

She set her knife down on the table, point toward herself, which was a habit of hers that Tomas had decided meant either discipline or penance. "We stay two more nights. Maybe three. Then we split along the river road in pairs and scatter east before Corvane's map fills in. That was the plan. That's still the plan. Plans don't need names. Corpses get names."

"We haven't heard anything," Tomas said.

"We won't hear anything until it's too late to matter. That's how hunters work. Quiet, quiet, quiet, then fire." Sefa's eyes went to Marguerite, and softened—not much, a degree or two, but Sefa's face had a very narrow range and everyone had learned to read it. "No offense intended."

"Some taken," Marguerite said. There was no heat in it. She'd been quiet all evening, wrapped in whatever weather lived inside her now, frost-dreams and stolen memories. She turned her charm-knot over in her fingers. "But Sefa's right. I'm the reason the risk exists. If I moved on alone—"

"No," said three voices at once.

It surprised all of them, Tomas included. He heard himself say it again, slower: "No. You don't walk out of here alone. That's—" He stopped, because the sentence he'd been about to build was *that's how my sister died*, and he'd told that story to exactly one person in his life, a hedge-witch with smoke-colored hair, and she was ash in the tanner's quarter now.

"That's not how this works," he finished, lamely.

"A rousing argument," said Bramble. "You should write speeches."

Pip, mercifully, would not let the moment die of dignity. "So we agree we're staying together but not agreeing on anything else. Which means we're a family. Families argue. Old Marta next door used to say families are just people who can't stop arguing in the same house."

"We are not a family," Sefa said.

"You slept standing guard over her fever for two days," Pip said, pointing the spoon at Marguerite. "You didn't sleep. You stood there. Like a fence post. With knives."

"That was tactics."

"You cried."

"*Pip.*"

"You did! Right here. I saw from the rafters. Right—" Pip pointed at a spot on the floorboards near the fire, with the merciless accuracy of children everywhere, "—right about there."

Sefa picked her knife back up and resumed sharpening it with the air of a woman who had survived a war and would survive dinner. But her ears were red, and Tomas caught Marguerite hiding a smile in her sleeve—the first smile he'd seen from her in two days, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, but real.

* * *

The argument about the name lasted through supper and well past the washing-up, which was to say it became the evening's entertainment, and Tomas understood somewhere in the middle of it that this was the actual function of the argument—not the name, but the arguing. Stranded people needed friction or they went strange. He'd seen crews rot in silence on long runs. Better a fight about nothing than a slow drift apart.

"*The Foxhole,*" Bramble offered, gesturing at himself with both hands like a showman. "Obvious. Elegant."

"It's a tavern name," Sefa said.

"There are worse things to be than a tavern. Taverns are warm and full of lies and nobody asks questions."

"*The Locksmith's Daughter,*" Pip said.

"You're the locksmith," Tomas said.

"And?"

"You're also nine."

"Eight. Probably."

"*The Herring House,*" Tomas offered, mostly to watch their faces, and enjoyed the chorus of groans enough to almost regret the herring.

Then Marguerite, who had been silent so long they'd nearly forgotten her, said: "*Nothing.*"

They looked at her. Firelight moved on her face, and for a moment Tomas saw what he suspected very few people had ever been allowed to see—the whole architecture of her tiredness, and underneath it something stubborn and bright.

"We call ourselves nothing," she said. "That's the point, isn't it? Every guild has a name, and the crown burned the guilds. Every bloodline has a name, and look what they're doing with theirs. Names are how they find you. Names are how they claim you." She turned the luck-knot over once more, then set it down on the table between the bowls, finished after all these hours, small and tight and unbreakable. "Let's be the thing they can't put in a ledger. No banner. No charter. Nothing."

Silence held for a breath, then another.

"She's right," Sefa said at last, and sounded almost annoyed about it. "An unnamed company can't be outlawed. There's no edict against nothing."

"The Company of Nothing," Bramble tested. "Sounds like a troupe of failed actors."

"Better than *the Foxhole*."

"Nothing is wrong with *the Foxhole*—"

Pip climbed down from the rafters, crossed the room with the serious tread of a child conducting state business, and picked up the finished knot. Held it up to the light. "This is a promise-charm," the child said. Not asking.

Marguerite went very still. "...It's a luck-knot."

"It's a promise-charm. The locks told me. They talk about your knots sometimes." Pip said it the way children say impossible things, plain as bread. "Old ones. In the guildhall doors, before the doors were changed. They remember the pattern. It means *I hold what you hold*." Pip looked around the room, from face to face, ears—human ones, fox ones, none—taking inventory in a way no adult would dare. "So that's the name. Or that's the no-name. Same thing."

Nobody argued. Tomas found he couldn't have, even if he'd wanted to. His throat had gone tight around something that had no business being grief or hope and was somehow both.

"Well," said Bramble softly, tail curling around his feet. "Nothing it is."

* * *

Later, when the others had banked the fire and folded into their corners—Sefa upright against the wall with her knives, Pip in a nest of sacking near the rafters' ladder, Bramble curled fox-fashion with his nose under his arm—Tomas stepped out into the yard to check the river.

The night was cold for midsummer. Wrong cold, the kind that came off the water like a held breath. Downstream, the current ran dark and slow, and along the pilings of the old dock he could see, in the moonlight, the first thin skin of ice forming. In midsummer. He stared at it for a long time, and thought of Marguerite waking with frost on her palms, and said nothing to anyone.

When he came back inside, she was awake, sitting up by the dead fire, and she knew—he could see it in her face—that he'd seen the river. Neither of them said it. Some cargoes you carry without labeling.

"Tomas." Her voice was low, pitched under the others' breathing. "Why are you doing this? Truly. You could put me out tonight and be clear of it."

He considered lying. He was good at lying; it was most of his trade. Instead he sat down across from her, elbows on knees, and told the truth because the hour made liars of no one.

"When I was sixteen, my sister took a fever off the marsh water. Doctors wouldn't come—no money, wrong quarter. A hedge-witch came anyway. Didn't ask for coin. Sat up three nights drawing the sickness out of a child she'd never met, in a city that would burn her for it if they caught her at it." He looked at the cold hearth. "Her name was Wren. I saw the smoke today. Over the tanner's quarter. I knew before anyone said the name."

Marguerite reached out and touched the back of his hand, briefly. Frost-cold fingers, gentle as snowfall.

"So that's why," he said. "Not heroism. Debt. And—" he hesitated, then paid out the rest of the rope, "—and because I've spent my whole life being careful, and careful is just fear wearing good boots. I'm done with it. Whatever you are, whatever's waking up in you—we hold what you hold. Isn't that what your own charm says?"

For a moment she just breathed. Then, so quietly he almost missed it:

"The Wolf was alone at the end. I've seen it now, in the dreams. Snowfields, and no one beside her, and she chose it because there was no one to choose instead." Her jaw tightened. "There will be someone. This time. Even if it kills us."

"Preferably not," Tomas said. "I've grown attached to us."

That startled a laugh out of her—a real one, cracked and rusty, gone almost before it arrived, but he counted it like profit.

He was banking the fire again when Pip's voice floated down from the rafters, sleepy and certain: "Someone's coming up the river road. Two someones. Walking fast. The gate-lock told me—they always tell me when strangers touch them."

Tomas went still. Outside, the wrong cold pressed against the shutters, and downstream, unseen in the dark, the ice crept a finger's width further across the black water.

"How many did you say?" Sefa asked, already awake, already reaching for her knives.
