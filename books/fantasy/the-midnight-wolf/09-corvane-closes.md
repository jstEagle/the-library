## 9. Corvane Closes

The map had grown teeth.

Inquisitor Corvane stood before the great table in his quarters above the Ashgate garrison, candlelight pooling on vellum like wax on skin, and watched the thing he had built for three years finally begin to breathe. Greyhollow sprawled beneath his hand in ink and pin and thread—every district drawn to scale, every alley he'd walked himself rendered in a draftsman's neat hand. Red pins marked the registered. Black pins marked the dead. And now, threaded between them in silver wire, a pattern was emerging that no single arrest would ever have revealed.

A route. A river. A vein of silver wire running along the Vell from the tanner's quarter up through Cooper's Row, across the weavers' bridges, and terminating—always terminating—in the same seven streets at the city's northern edge, where the old guildhouses stood empty and the rents were cheap and the neighbors were poor enough not to ask questions.

He set another pin. Silver. A cooper's apprentice who had vanished from his bed eleven days ago, leaving behind a cold supper and a mother who swore her son had no gift at all, none, please, my lord, none.

"Everyone has something," Corvane murmured to the empty room. "That is rather the whole of it."

* * *

He worked as he always worked: slowly, courteously, and without mercy disguised as anything else. The crown wanted burnings; the crowds wanted spectacle; his junior hunters wanted glory. Corvane gave them almost none of it. He gave them ledgers.

The charm-maker brought in that morning was named Aldous Fen, sixty-one years of age, licensed under the old tolerance laws until the Edict of Ash revoked every license in the realm with a stroke of the Chancellor's pen. He sat in the interrogation room with his hands folded on the table like a man waiting for tea, and when Corvane entered, the old man looked up with eyes that had already made peace with whatever came next.

"Inquisitor." A nod, almost friendly. "You're younger than they say."

"They say very little about me," Corvane replied. He set down his satchel, took out paper, ink, a blotter. He arranged them with the care of a man setting an altar. "Master Fen. You registered within nine days of the Edict. Your declaration lists your gift as 'the warming of small rooms.'"

"It does."

"Is it true?"

Fen smiled, and there was something tired and fond in it. "It's true that I warm small rooms."

Corvane wrote nothing for a long moment. Then: "Your shop on Chandler's Lane sold fever-charms for thirty years. Luck-knots. Birthing blessings. The registry lists you as a hearth-magician, third order, unaffiliated with any guild." He turned the page. "Yet in thirty years, Master Fen, you never once took payment from the families on Weft Street. Not one coin. Forty households, all of them poor, all of them recipients of your charms—and never a ledger entry."

"The poor can't pay."

"The poor paid other charm-makers," Corvane said quietly. "Just not you. Why?"

Silence. The candle guttered and steadied.

"Because someone asked me to look after them," Fen said at last.

"Who?"

"A friend."

"A friend with a name?"

"A friend who believed that magic should go where it's needed, not where it's bought." The old man's hands tightened on each other, knuckles whitening, and Corvane saw—not for the first time—that fear was not what made these people lie. Love was. It was always love. "I won't give you a name. Burn me if you like. But she'll have moved on by now anyway, wherever she is. She was always good at moving on."

*She.*

Corvane closed his book.

"You may go," he said.

Fen blinked. "My lord?"

"I said you may go. Your registration stands; you've committed no crime the Edict recognizes. Selling charms isn't illegal for the registered." He rose, gathered his papers. "Though I'd advise you, Master Fen, to stop warming rooms for free. Generosity leaves tracks. Gratitude makes witnesses. In the coming months, the safest thing you can be is ordinary."

The old man stood slowly, watching him the way one watches a door that might be a trap. "Why let me go? They say you never let anyone go."

"They say many things." At the door, Corvane paused, his back to the prisoner. "Tell your friend—if you see her, if word reaches you—the roads north are watched now. All of them. Tell her there is no river crossing past Saltmere that I do not know, no smuggler on the Vell whose name I haven't bought. If she runs north, tell her to run faster."

"And if she doesn't run at all?"

Corvane opened the door. Down the corridor, two guards waited with the blank patience of men who had stopped wondering why their commander released more prisoners than he kept.

"Then she's braver than any of us," he said, "and it will break my heart, and I will still find her. Good day, Master Fen."

* * *

By evening, three more informants had come to the garrison, drawn by the proclamation nailed to the chapel doors: AMNESTY FOR INFORMATION. Any registered person revealing the whereabouts of unregistered gifted, or of those aiding their flight, would be exempted from seizure and granted crown protection for themselves and their kin.

It was a filthy instrument and Corvane knew it. He had written it himself, sitting up two nights, choosing each word the way a surgeon chooses a blade—small, precise, guaranteed to cut. Amnesty was cheaper than torture and yielded better intelligence; fear of neighbors did what fire could not, because fire only ended a voice while betrayal taught every other voice to sing. He hated the proclamation the way a good soldier hates a necessary war: completely, and without letting the hatred slow his hand.

The first informant was a fishwife from the tanner's quarter who reported smoke-smells and midnight comings and goings at a shuttered dye-house. The second was a boy of perhaps fourteen who had seen strangers carried through the reed-beds at low tide, wrapped in oilcloth like contraband brandy. The third said nothing useful at all, only wept and begged forgiveness from a God whose position on the Edict remained officially neutral, and Corvane dismissed her gently and sat alone for a while afterward, listening to the garrison settle into its nighttime rhythms.

Then he went back to the map, and followed the silver wire north, and stood before it until the candles burned low.

Seven streets. Empty guildhouses. Cheap rents and incurious neighbors. A smuggling route running goods upriver—brandy, salt, wool on the manifests, but the manifests were bought and paid for by men who never asked what else rode under the tarps. Someone was moving the gifted out of Greyhollow before the hunt could close, and they were doing it with the smooth competence of professionals, and they had chosen their ground well: the old guild quarter, where half the buildings still belonged to dead guilds and the other half to landlords too frightened or too greedy to count tenants.

But competence left patterns. Every route had a terminus. Every terminus had a keeper.

And somewhere in that knot of streets—his informants couldn't say which house, which cellar, which ruin—was the answer to the question that had brought him to Greyhollow in the first place, three years ago, when the Edict was still a rumor and his orders had been vague in a way that told him everything:

*Find the vessel.*

He touched the map, lightly, where the seven streets met the river road.

"I know you're there," he said softly. "I knew it before I came."

* * *

He dreamed of Elara that night. He dreamed of her often, though he had learned not to fight the dreams, because fought dreams only sharpened.

They were nineteen, and it was midsummer, and the orchards above the university town hung so heavy with plums that the branches bowed like courtiers. Elara Whitlock sat in the crook of the lowest bough with her skirts hitched up and her boots dangling, eating fruit with a knife and a total absence of manners, and she was laughing at something he'd said—he could never remember afterward what he'd said, only the laugh, bright and unguarded, a sound the world had not yet taught her to ration.

"You're staring, Corvane."

"I'm thinking."

"Dangerous. You'll hurt yourself." She tossed him a plum. He caught it badly, against his chest, and she laughed again. "Go on, then. Think out loud. That's what friends are for."

Friends. The word had been a wound even then, though he'd smiled through it, because he had understood early—before she ever spoke of it—that Elara Whitlock's heart had been promised elsewhere, or perhaps nowhere, or perhaps to something so large that no man could be asked to share it. There had been a gravity to her even at nineteen, a sense that part of her attention was always fixed on some horizon the rest of them couldn't see.

"What happens after graduation?" he asked instead. "You'll take a posting. Court mage, probably. You're brilliant enough. The Chancellery will fall over itself."

"And what will you do, lawman's son?" She bit into a plum, juice running down her wrist. "Follow your father into the provost's office? Count confiscated dice and settle tavern brawls?"

"Someone has to keep the peace."

"Someone has to *make* it first," she said, and her voice changed—not sharp, but serious, the way it always went serious when she spoke of the things she believed. She hopped down from the branch, landed light as a cat, and stood looking at him with the plum juice still wet on her chin and her eyes full of a future she thought was certain. "There's going to be a war, Corvane. Maybe not this year. Maybe not in ten. But the old bloodlines are hoarding, and the guilds are fracturing, and everyone who matters is choosing sides over dinner tables while pretending they aren't. When it comes—" she spread her hands, "—I know which side I'll be standing on. The side that says power belongs to everyone it lives in. Not to thrones. Not to bloodlines. To *everyone*."

"And if the realm says otherwise? If the crown outlaws it? If there's an edict someday, Elara, and the gifted must register or burn—"

She had gone very quiet. Somewhere in the orchard, wind moved through the leaves, and the afternoon light shifted, and he remembered thinking, absurdly, *she looks like a woman hearing her own death read aloud.*

"Then I hope," she said finally, "that the man keeping the peace remembers that he once shared plums with a witch. And that he asks himself, before he lights any fires, whether the peace he's keeping was worth what it cost."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It's a hope. Answers are for people who think the world can be settled. Hopes are for people who intend to keep trying." She held out her plum-sticky hand, and he took it, and her grip was warm and strong and brief. "Promise me something, Corvane."

"If I can."

"If it comes to it—if they put you on one side and me on the other—you'll remember the girl, not the witch. Whatever you have to do. Remember the girl."

He had promised. Nineteen years old and full of certainty, he had promised, and meant it, and believed that meaning it would be enough.

Twenty-six years later, he signed the warrant that sealed the guildhall of the Order of the Quiet Flame, and he wrote Elara Whitlock's name on the list of the unaccounted-for, and his hand did not shake, because he had spent twenty years teaching it not to.

* * *

They never found her body. That was the thing that kept the case open in his mind long after the war ended and the Sundering ash settled and the Edict of Ash rose from its ruins like a phoenix of the worst possible kind. The guildhall burned; the records listed forty-three dead; Elara Whitlock was not among the identified remains. Some said she'd died in the fire past all knowing. Some said she'd fled north. Some—the ones who whispered, the ones Corvane interviewed alone with the door shut—said she had been carrying something when she ran. Something the old bloodlines wanted. Something the Wolf of Winter had entrusted to the guild before its sacrifice ended the war, and that the whole rotten machinery of the postwar crown had been hunting ever since.

*A vessel,* the whispers said. *The memory, sealed in flesh. Find the vessel and you find the Wolf.*

And then, three years ago, a clerk cross-referencing the guild's surviving rolls against the civil registries found a line item that had been overlooked for twenty years: a birth record. A daughter. Born the winter before the guildhall burned. Mother: Elara Whitlock. Father: unnamed.

Marguerite.

Twelve years old when the trail warmed. Fifteen when Corvane picked it up. Now—a woman grown, somewhere, hidden in a city of ninety thousand souls, wearing a life like a mask over the thing beneath.

He had come to Greyhollow to find her. Not to burn her. The orders, when they finally came, explicit and signed by hands far above his own, had been precise in a way that chilled him worse than any pyre: *the vessel is to be recovered alive and intact.* No one at court would tell him what *intact* required. No one would tell him what would be done with the woman inside the vessel, whether the girl Elara had borne would survive whatever extraction the bloodlines intended, whether she was a person to them at all or only a jar with a lid.

He had asked. Once. Of the Chancellor's secretary, a grey man with grey eyes, in a grey room that smelled of ink.

The secretary had looked at him with mild surprise, the way one looks at a hound that has asked a philosophical question.

"Inquisitor. Your task is retrieval. What follows retrieval is above your station."

Above his station. Twenty-six years of service, and this was his station: to fetch, and not to wonder.

He had gone back to his map.

* * *

Dawn came grey over Greyhollow, and with it came the boy from the reed-beds again, breathless, clutching a scrap of oilcloth he'd fished from the shallows below the weavers' bridge. Corvane turned it over in his hands. Sturdy stuff. River-merchant grade. And stitched inside the hem, nearly invisible, a maker's mark: a fox curled around a key.

"Bramble," said one of his sergeants, consulting a battered ledger of known smugglers. "Half-blood shifter, works the Vell run. Reputed to move anything for anyone, provided the coin's right—or the cause. There's talk he's been carrying passengers since the Edict. Living cargo."

"Talk from whom?"

"A laundress. Registered. Nervous."

Corvane laid the oilcloth beside the map and drew the thread of the investigation tight: the fox, the river, the seven streets, the empty guildhouses. The picture was nearly complete now. One or two threads short of a noose.

"Amnesty for the laundress," he said. "Double rations for the boy. And ready the dawn detail—we'll want quiet boots and no torches. If the fox is moving passengers, he'll move them soon. Word of Ashgate's burnings travels fast among the frightened, and frightened people run to whoever offers a boat."

The sergeant hesitated. "And the vessel, sir? If she's among them—"

"Alive," Corvane said. "All of them alive, if it can be managed. Dead witches make martyrs and martyrs make recruits. Besides—" he rolled the map closed, and his voice, when it continued, was quieter, and only he knew how much it cost, "—Elara Whitlock's daughter has spent her whole life being hunted for what her mother hid in her. I intend to know exactly what we're taking her *from*. Wake the detail at fourth bell."

Outside his window, the city stirred into another grey morning. Smoke rose from a thousand honest chimneys. Somewhere in the northern streets, behind some shuttered window, a woman was waking who did not yet know his face, though her mother had known it once, under plum trees, in another world.

"Forgive me, Elara," Corvane said to the glass, to the city, to the girl who was no longer a girl. "You asked me to remember. I remembered everything. It changes nothing."

At fourth bell, the hunters moved out—soft-booted, torchless, converging on the seven streets where the silver wire ended—and Corvane rode at their head with his heart locked shut and the promise ringing in it like a bell that would not stop.

They hit the safehouse at dawn.
