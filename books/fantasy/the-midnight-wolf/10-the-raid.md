## 10. The Raid

The dream woke her the way a hand wakes you—gently at first, then with terrible urgency.

In it she had been standing in the snowfield again, the one that belonged to another woman's life, and the wolf made of winter had turned its great head toward her as if hearing something on the wind. *They are coming,* it had said, in a voice like ice shifting over deep water. *They are coming, and you are counting spoons.*

Marguerite opened her eyes in the narrow room above the safehouse kitchen, heart hammering, and listened.

Nothing. The creak of the old house settling. Pip's muffled snoring through the wall, punctuated by the soft clicks of whatever locks he kept beneath his pillow to lull himself to sleep. Somewhere below, the banked fire ticking as it cooled.

But the frost was already blooming across the windowpane inside the glass, feathering outward from where her palm pressed against the sill, and it was midsummer, and the night air through the gap in the shutter was warm as breath.

She sat up slowly. Her hands were cold. Not chilled—*cold*, the way a riverbed is cold, the way stone is cold in January. She flexed her fingers and watched silver light trace the lines of her knuckles like veins of ore before fading.

"Go back to sleep," she whispered to herself, the way Odile used to whisper to the guildhall when storms rattled its shutters. "There's nothing here but us."

It was almost true. It had been true for eleven days, since the raid in Greyhollow and the sewers and Bramble's half-fox face going grey in the lamplight of Sefa's cellar. Eleven days of learning the safehouse's rhythms: Tomas rising before dawn to walk the perimeter he refused to call a perimeter; Sefa drinking bitter tea and drilling Marguerite in breathing exercises that were supposed to teach control; Pip adopting her the way a stray cat adopts a household—without permission and entirely on his own terms.

Eleven days of pretending the dream-wolf was only a dream.

She lay back down. She did not sleep. And that was why, when the first crossbow bolt came through the window, she was already moving.

* * *

The bolt buried itself in the floorboards where her head had been, quivering, fletched with grey goose-feather. Grey goose—the crown's mark. Marguerite hit the floor and rolled, and even as she moved her body understood what her mind was still catching up to: the horses. She had heard no horses, but there had been a moment—a wrongness in the quality of the silence outside, the birdsong stopping all at once, the way the street held its breath—and her blood had known before her ears did.

Below her, the house erupted.

Sefa's door banged open hard enough to crack the frame. "Downstairs! Everyone downstairs!" The battle-mage's voice carried the flat calm of a woman who had held walls before, in another country, for another flag, back when holding things was still permitted. Marguerite heard Tomas's door slam, heard his boots hit the stairs three at a time, heard him say her name—not a question, just a confirmation that she existed and was moving.

Then the front door exploded inward.

Marguerite reached the top of the stairs in time to see the doorway fill with grey-cloaked hunters, six of them at least, lantern-light glinting off visored helms, and behind them the street beyond alive with more—torchlight, chain-mail whispers, the terrible orderliness of people who had done this many times and would do it many more.

"Hold," said the lead hunter, raising a gauntleted hand, and Marguerite felt the word land on the house like a hand on a birdcage. *Hold.* A binding-word. She'd read about them in Odile's forbidden books, the grammar of compulsion that licensed hunters were permitted to speak aloud because they wore the crown's authority like armor.

Sefa stepped into the doorway between them and everything else.

Marguerite had seen Sefa work small magics in the eleven days—warming spells gone sour, a ward scratched into a windowsill, the controlled little exercises of a woman keeping her edge sharp out of habit and grief. She had never seen this.

Sefa planted both feet in the threshold of the kitchen doorway, spread her arms, and *held*.

It was not elegant. There was no incantation, no sigil, none of the careful craft Odile had taught. It was raw force, the kind the war had been fought with, the kind that got battle-mages stripped of rank and exiled, and it poured out of Sefa like heat from an opened forge. The air in the front hall went thick and golden and bent, and the lead hunter's binding-word shattered against it like a cup dropped on stone.

"None of you," Sefa said quietly, "are coming through this door."

A crossbow answered her. The bolt stopped two feet from her chest, hung trembling in the thickened air, and dropped.

"None," Sefa repeated, "of you."

Marguerite took the stairs down two at a time. Tomas was already in the kitchen, hauling the bread-tray off the shelf, and Pip was there too—she didn't know how Pip was always already somewhere, the boy simply arrived at the center of things like water finding low ground. His eyes were enormous and perfectly calm.

"The cellar," he said. "Under the flour bin."

"There is no cellar under the flour bin," Tomas said. "I built this shelving myself. I've slept in this house four months. There's no—"

"There's a cellar under the flour bin," Pip said, with the serene certainty of a child informing adults about gravity, "and it doesn't want to be found, which is why nobody found it. But it'll let me. Doors talk to me." He was already dragging the bin aside, small hands scrabbling at the wall, and Marguerite saw what she would have sworn on Odile's grave had not been there ten seconds ago: a seam in the plaster, hairline-fine, shaped exactly like a door. "Old magic. Older than the Edict. Somebody hid this house a long time ago and the hiding got lonely and grew into the walls." He pressed his palm flat against the seam and whispered something too quiet to catch, the way you'd whisper to a dog you wanted to trust you.

The seam sighed open.

Cold air breathed up out of the dark—stone-and-earth cold, honest cold, not hers—and the smell of old straw and older secrets. Steps descended into blackness. Someone had left a lamp down there, decades ago by the look of it, and Pip's fingers found the striker on the third try and lit a flame that steadied like it had only been waiting.

"In," Pip said. "All of you. Quickly-quickly."

Tomas looked at Marguerite. In the chaos his face had gone very still, the stillness he wore when he was making a decision that would cost something. "Bramble's not in yet. Bramble sleeps in the loft."

"He'll come down with us—"

"Bramble doesn't hear well before sunrise. Fox half sleeps through anything." Tomas was already moving toward the back stair. "I'll get him. Go. Both of you. Now."

"Tomas—" Marguerite grabbed his sleeve. The frost leapt from her fingertips to the wool without her willing it, a rime of white spreading across his cuff like spilled milk, and she snatched her hand back as if scalded. He stared at the frost, then at her, and did the thing that undid her completely: he smiled.

"I know," he said. "Go."

He went up. The back stair groaned under his boots. And Marguerite stood in a kitchen full of broken light with a child who spoke to locks and a doorway held shut by the last battle-mage of a dead army, and she made herself go down into the dark because staying meant being caught and being caught meant every one of them burned.

That was the arithmetic of it. That was always the arithmetic. Stay together and you give them one target. Scatter and you give them many. She had spent twelve years believing the second sum, building her whole small careful life on it, and now she was descending into a hidden cellar behind a smuggler's found family and discovering that the first sum felt, horribly, like home.

* * *

The cellar was bigger than the house.

That was Pip's explanation for everything about it, delivered while Marguerite's eyes adjusted to lamplight: bigger than the house, older than the city, dug by someone who had needed to hide something larger than wine. Racks lined one wall, empty but for dust-shrouded bottles. Crates sat stacked against the far wall, and when Sefa came down the steps backward—still facing the door above, still pouring her force into the ceiling like a woman carrying a roof on her shoulders—one of the crates turned its head and blinked at them with amber fox-eyes.

"Bramble?" Marguerite breathed.

The shapeshifter unfolded from the crate in a rustle of straw, half-fox and half-man as ever, one ear swiveling toward the sounds of violence filtering faintly down through the floorboards. "Was sleeping in the wine rack," Bramble said, voice rough with sleep and indignation. "Someone put me *in a crate*—"

"You were in the crate when we came down," Pip said.

"I was resting in the crate. There's a difference. Where's Tomas?"

Above them, the house shook.

Dust sifted from the joists. Through the floor came a sound like a mountain clearing its throat—Sefa, spending something she could not afford to spend, and beneath it the splintering crack of timber giving way, and then voices, many voices, shouting words that made the lampflame gutter. Marguerite pressed her hands over her ears and felt the cold climb her wrists unbidden, frost feathering up her forearms, and this time she could not pretend it wasn't happening because Sefa was right there watching it happen with an expression of grim professional interest.

"Don't fight it," Sefa said, coming down the last steps, blood running from her nose, her voice stripped raw. "Whatever it is. Fighting it costs twice what using it does. I learned that holding a bridge for nine hours once. Don't fight it. *Shape* it."

"I don't know how to shape it. I don't even know what it *is*."

"It's yours. That's enough to start."

The trapdoor above them boomed—once, twice—and then a third impact that was not a boot but a battering ram, and the seam-door Pip had coaxed open began to close itself, plaster crawling over the gap like skin over a wound, and Pip yelped and slapped his palms against the shrinking gap.

"No no no, wait, *wait*—" The boy's voice cracked. "Tomas isn't down yet. If it seals—"

"Pip." Sefa's hand closed gently around the boy's wrist. "Look at me. Can you feel him? Through the door?"

Pip went still. His eyes lost focus, the way they did when he listened to things that weren't sounds. "He's... upstairs. Moving fast. There's a window—he's going out the window, onto the shed roof, and—" His face crumpled with relief so sudden and complete that Marguerite felt it in her own chest. "And Bramble's friend is with him. They're running. They're running along the rooftops toward the tannery lane and there are three hunters after them and—"

The trapdoor burst.

Not slowly, not with the dignity of siegecraft—it simply ceased to exist, blasted inward in a storm of splinters, and the grey cloaks came down the steps four abreast with crossbows leveled, and the lead hunter's voice rang out with the binding-word again, *HOLD*, and this time it was aimed down the stairs at all of them, at Pip and Bramble and the bleeding battle-mage and Marguerite, and Marguerite felt it take hold—

—and break.

Because the frost met it.

She hadn't decided anything. Later she would search the memory again and again for the moment of choice and find none, find only the sensation of the binding-word reaching for her like a hand closing around her throat and something ancient inside her answering the way winter answers a torch: not with anger, not with effort, simply with the fact of itself. Cold detonated outward from her body in a silent ring. The lamps went blue. Frost raced up the stone walls and across the ceiling in branching white lightning, and the binding-word shattered mid-syllable, and the lead hunter froze—not metaphorically, not rhetorically, but actually, truly *froze*, rime sealing his visor slits, his cloak stiffening into a carapace of ice, his next shout dying into a cloud of glittering crystals.

For one heartbeat the cellar was utterly silent except for the creak of freezing wood.

Then the hunter behind him screamed, "WITCH!"—the old word, the war-word, the word from the burned pages of Odile's books—and the stairwell filled with bolts and torchlight and the smell of pitch, and Sefa roared something in a language Marguerite didn't know and the far wall of the cellar *opened*, stone grinding aside to reveal the mouth of a sewer tunnel, black and reeking and blessedly wide.

"GO!" Sefa bellowed. "Everyone GO!"

They went. Bramble seized Pip under the arms and ran. Marguerite stumbled after them, her hands still burning-cold, frost trailing from her fingertips like the wake of a boat, and behind her she heard Sefa's footsteps stop at the tunnel mouth and knew the battle-mage was turning to hold the doorway one last time, alone, against however many of them remained.

"Sefa—" Marguerite turned back.

"*Go,* girl." Sefa didn't look at her. Golden light was gathering around the battle-mage's fists, and through the smoke of the ruined trapdoor Marguerite could see the hunters regrouping, could see the frozen one being dragged clear, could see, absurdly, a man in a plain officer's coat standing at the top of the stairs—not charging, not shouting, just *watching* her with an expression she couldn't read in the blue light. Watching her the way scholars watch texts. Watching her the way mourners watch graves.

"That's him," Bramble hissed from the darkness, gripping her arm with claws half-out. "That's the tea-drinker. That's Corvane. *Move.*"

She moved. Sefa slammed the stone door shut behind them and the last thing Marguerite saw of the cellar was Inquisitor Corvane's face, lit by witchfire, wearing the look of a man who has finally found the page he's been searching a whole book for.

* * *

The sewer swallowed them, and the city above did not notice.

They waded through channels of grey water by Pip's lamp, the boy holding it high like a tiny priest conducting some foul sacrament, calling out turns before Marguerite's mind could map them—left at the junction, right where the brick changed color, straight past the nest of rats who scattered from Pip with unusual respect. Bramble ranged ahead and behind in a low lope, ears swiveling, and twice stopped the column with a raised hand while boots crossed some grating overhead. Once, distant and distorted by stone, they heard a roar that might have been Sefa and might have been the collapse of the safehouse itself, and no one spoke about it, because speaking would make it one or the other.

Tomas found them at the second junction.

He came down a maintenance ladder slick with moss, hauling Bramble's other half—no, Marguerite realized, blinking—hauling *Odile's herbalist neighbor*, the stooped woman from the river road, whose name was Marta and who had, apparently, been sheltering in the safehouse attic for three days without anyone mentioning it to Marguerite. Tomas dropped into the channel beside her with a splash, soaked to the waist, a cut over his eyebrow bleeding freely, and his eyes found Marguerite in the lamplight with an urgency that made her stomach drop.

"You're hurt," she said.

"It's nothing. Are *you*—" He stopped. Looked at her hands, which she had wrapped in strips of blanket against the cold, and at the frost that had crept up past the wrapping anyway, silvering her knuckles like rings of ice. "What happened down there?"

"She froze a man solid," Pip reported helpfully. "Like a fish. Like a whole fish."

"Pip."

"What? It was amazing. It was the most amazing thing I ever saw, and I saw a lock once eat a burglar."

Marguerite looked at Tomas and waited for it—for the flinch, the recalculation, the subtle reclassification of her from person to problem that she had watched happen on market day in Greyhollow when the criers read the Edict, that she had spent twelve years engineering her entire existence to avoid. Even from friends. Especially from friends. Odile had taught her that much truth early: everyone you love will someday look at you differently, if you let them close enough to see.

Tomas wrung out his sleeve, wiped the blood from his eye, and said, "Can you do it again?"

She blinked. "What?"

"The freezing. Can you do it again, on purpose? Because Sefa stayed behind, and Bramble says the tea-drinker doesn't leave witnesses, and if they took her—" His jaw worked. "If they took her, I want to know what we're walking back into. And I'd rather walk in beside something like what I saw glow through that floor than beside a woman apologizing for it."

The sewer dripped. The lamp hissed. Marta, the herbalist, was watching Marguerite with sharp old eyes, and Bramble's tails had gone very still, and Pip was watching her the way children watch fireworks.

"They'll burn me," Marguerite said quietly. "You understand that now. What I am. What's in me. It's not charms and luck-knots, Tomas. It's not small. Whatever it is that I'm carrying, it's the reason they came tonight, and it's the reason they'll keep coming, and everyone near me is—" Her voice failed on the word she'd promised herself she wouldn't say, and said it anyway. "*Fuel.* That's the cost. This is what it costs, staying together. Bramble knows the price already. Sefa may know it now too. So don't ask me if I can do it again like it's a useful trick. Ask me if it's worth what it costs everyone who stands near me."

The question rang off wet brick and died.

And then Bramble laughed—a low, rasping, fox-laugh that echoed strangely down the tunnel—and the shapeshifter crouched beside her in the filthy water, amber eyes level with hers, and said:

"Little vessel. I was taken by hunters once before, in the border wars. Do you know what I learned?" The half-fox smile showed teeth. "Everything worth keeping costs fuel. Love costs. Loyalty costs. Staying costs. Leaving costs more—you just pay it slow, in installments, your whole life, the way *you've* been paying." The amber eyes flicked to the frost on her knuckles, unafraid. Curious. Almost tender. "Twelve years of smallness, and tonight is the first night anyone came for *you*. Feel what that means. We came for you. They came for you. Nobody in this sewer is standing near you by accident."

Marguerite couldn't answer. Something in her chest had frozen and cracked at the same time, like a river in thaw.

Tomas put his hand over hers—over the frost, bare-handed, unflinching, his skin flushing pink against the cold—and squeezed once.

"We need to keep moving," he said softly. "Before the hunt spreads. There's a lockkeeper south of the tannery who owes me, and a boat, and after that..." He glanced at Pip, at Bramble, at old Marta, at the darkness stretching away beneath the sleeping city. "After that we run. All of us. Together."

They moved. The lamp bobbed ahead, small and stubborn against all that dark.

And behind them, far above, unseen, a pigeon beat its way north through the summer midnight with a message tube on its leg—toward Ashgate Prison, toward a cell where a shapeshifter's riddle was already waiting on an inquisitor's desk, toward the name *Whitlock* written in a hand that had been patient for twelve years.

The hunt was not over.

The hunt had only just learned what it was hunting.
