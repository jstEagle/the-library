## 11. What the Fox Confessed

The cell beneath Ashgate was not built for comfort, and Corvane had made no effort to improve it. Stone walls sweated in the summer heat; a grate high above admitted a blade of grey light that crawled across the floor as the hours passed. The prisoner sat on the cot with her ankles chained to an iron ring and her tail—half-fox, rust-orange, unmistakably real even now—curled around one knee like a scarf she had forgotten she was wearing.

She had been in the cell for two days. She had not slept, or if she had, she'd done it sitting upright with her ears moving, tracking every footstep in the corridor above.

Corvane came down at the ninth bell carrying two cups of tea.

He had learned long ago that fear was a blunt instrument. It broke people, yes—but it broke them into fragments, and fragments gave you fragments of truth: names half-remembered, confessions shaped to fit whatever the torturer already believed. A frightened man told you what you wanted to hear. A comfortable man, a *surprised* man, told you what he actually knew, because he hadn't prepared defenses against kindness.

He set the second cup on the floor between them and sat down on the stool across from the cot, unhurried, arranging his grey coat about his knees like a man settling in for an evening by a fire.

Bramble watched him with eyes that were amber at the edges and brown at the center, and said nothing.

"Chamomile," Corvane said. "The kitchens had nothing better. I'm told it settles the nerves." He drank his own, demonstrating. "You haven't eaten since they brought you in. That's a choice, and I respect choices, but it makes my work harder."

"My work," the shapeshifter repeated. Her voice was rough-edged, a little hoarse, with something sly curled inside it even now. "That's what we're calling it."

"What would you call it?"

"Collecting." She lifted her chin. "You're collecting us. Like a man collects stamps, or teeth."

Corvane did not flinch. "And yet here is tea."

"Here is tea," she agreed. "Poisoned?"

"If I wanted you dead I'd have saved the crown the cost of the chains." He let that sit for a moment, then leaned forward, elbows on his knees. "Your name. Your real one, not the one your friends shout in alleys."

For a long moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she shrugged, one shoulder, the motion rippling oddly through a body that couldn't quite decide its shape. "Branna. My mother called me Branna. Bramble's what happened after."

"Thank you, Branna." He used the name deliberately, and saw the flicker in her face—the small disorientation of being treated as a person by the machine that had caged you. "Do you know why you're here? Not in this city. In this cell, rather than the pyre."

"Because the fox half of me isn't worth burning?" Her smile showed too many teeth. "Half a witch. Half a sentence."

"Because you were taken alive when taking you dead would have been easier, and I want to know why your friends fought so hard to keep you breathing." He set down his cup. "Tell me about the woman they're protecting."

* * *

She talked, at first, about nothing. About the weather in the river country, about the quality of prison bread, about a guard upstairs who cheated at cards. Corvane let her. He refilled her tea once, then again, and spoke only when silence threatened to become a wall instead of a door. He asked about Sefa and received a shrug. He asked about the child who spoke to locks and got a look of such pure, feral warning that it told him more than any answer: *that one matters; do not say her name again.*

It was nearly dusk when the light from the grate turned copper and Branna finally looked at him—not with the slyness, but with something tired underneath it.

"You knew Elara Whitlock," she said.

Corvane went very still. It was a practiced stillness, honed over years of interrogations, but he felt it cost him more than usual. "Everyone in Greyhollow knew Elara Whitlock. She sold charms on Wrenmarket like her daughter does now."

"That's not what I asked." The fox ears tilted forward. "I've been in cells before. I've been questioned before, back when questions still came with pliers. Men who are clean don't carry portraits."

He didn't answer. He didn't need to; his silence answered for him, and Branna read it the way her kind read tracks in snow.

"She was your friend," the shapeshifter said softly. "Before all this. And now you burn her world down around her daughter, and you tell yourself it's duty, and some nights you almost believe it." She picked up her tea and drank, watching him over the rim. "That's why there's no pliers. You can't torture someone who has her mother's chin. You'd see it every time you raised your hand."

"Careful," Corvane said quietly.

"Or what? You'll be careful at me?" But there was no triumph in it. She set the cup down and wrapped both hands around her own knees, folding herself smaller, and when she spoke again the slyness had drained away entirely, leaving something raw. "Inquisitor. I'm going to tell you something, because you'll hear it eventually anyway and I'd rather it come from me than from a screaming man in a cellar. And then I'm going to stop talking, and you can decide what to do with it."

"Go on."

"The woman you're hunting isn't a charm-seller." Branna's voice dropped, as though the walls might be listening—as though the walls *should*. "She doesn't have a gift. She has a passenger. Something old, sealed inside her before she could walk, sleeping in her blood." Her amber eyes held his. "They call it the Wolf of Winter. And everything your Edict has done—every fire, every name on your lists, every hedge-witch dragged out of her bed—it hasn't been about stamping out magic at all. It's about finding *her*. Because when the Wolf wakes, whoever holds her leash holds a war's worth of winter in one hand."

The candle flame between them bent, though there was no draft.

Corvane kept his face still, but beneath it, something shifted—a hairline crack running through masonry he had believed load-bearing for twenty years. He thought of the bloodline lords in their council chamber, their velvet voices, their insistence that the vessel be taken *alive*, their refusal to say what would happen to the girl afterward. He had told himself it was mercy. He had told himself many things.

"And where did you learn this word," he said at last, "Wolf of Winter?"

"Everyone in the old families knows it. It's a bedtime story for bastards like me—the guardian who ended the Sundering War, who walked into the ice so the rest of us could have spring." Branna smiled, and it was a terrible, gentle thing. "We just never expected the story to come knocking on our safehouse door smelling of fever-charms and river mud."

"Why tell me this?" Corvane asked. "You've given me the thing I've spent months hunting. Do you understand what you've done?"

"I've given you a story." She shook her head slowly. "I haven't given you a road. Ask me where she is. Go on. Ask."

"Where is she?"

Branna grinned, wide and sharp, and her shadow on the stone wall flickered—for half a heartbeat it had four legs and a brush tail, and then it was a woman's shape again.

"A fox knows many paths," she said, "but shows none to hounds. Here's what you get instead, Inquisitor, free of charge, because you bought me chamomile and didn't use the pliers:

*When winter walks, it wears no name.*
*Ask the ash what the ash became.*
*One keeps the door, one keeps the flame,
and what was buried calls its own—
not by blood, but bone for bone.*

Figure it out before the snow does. Because it's coming early this year, Inquisitor. I can smell it in my blood, and my blood is half wild thing." She lay back on the cot then, turned her face to the wall, and folded the rust-orange tail over her shoulder like a blanket. "We're done talking. Bring more tea tomorrow. The chamomile's decent."

* * *

He climbed the stairs slowly.

The corridor above was lamplit and ordinary: guards dicing, a clerk scratching at a ledger of names that grew longer every week. Corvane walked past all of it without seeing it and shut himself in his office, where the portrait hung on the wall behind his desk where no visitor ever stood to see it.

Elara Whitlock, painted the year before the Edict, laughing at whatever joke the painter had made, one hand raised to tuck grey-streaked hair behind her ear. He had been thirty-one. They had argued about poetry in the guildhall garden while her daughter—three years old, solemn as a magistrate—had tried to catch moths in cupped hands. He remembered Marguerite's small frown of concentration. He remembered Elara saying, *she'll break your heart someday, that one, she takes after me.*

He had burned four hundred and six names onto the Edict's rolls. He had stood at three pyres. He had told himself, every time, that order was the price of peace, that the Sundering War had taught the crown what unchecked power costs, that someone had to do the arithmetic of grief so no one else had to.

And all along, it had never been about order at all. The bloodlines wanted a weapon. They had used him as a hound to flush it, and he had run gladly, nose down, certain of the scent.

*What happens to the girl inside,* he had asked them, weeks ago, and Lord Alderyn had smiled like a man discussing crop rotation and said, *the vessel is the crown's concern now, Inquisitor. Yours is the finding.*

He looked at the portrait until the candle guttered.

Then he pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward him and began, methodically, to write—not a report, but a private ledger of his own. Everything Branna had said. Every inconsistency in the bloodlines' orders. The date the rivers had begun running cold in high summer. The riddle, transcribed exactly, because his instincts—which had kept him alive through two decades of hunting men and women cleverer than he—told him the fox had handed him the truth in the only container she could trust him to open.

*Not by blood, but bone for bone.*

He wrote it twice and stared at it.

Outside, far to the north beyond the city walls, the first stars were coming out—and among them, low on the horizon, a haze of white that he had taken, for the last three nights, for cloud.

It was not cloud. The weather-criers had begun whispering about it in the markets: snow in midsummer, falling in the high passes, falling wrong, falling *toward* something.

Corvane capped his ink, put on his coat, and took his cloak from its hook.

There was a herbalist who lived alone past the mill-road bend—an old woman who had slipped off his lists years ago because her magic was too small to bother with, because he had been a young hunter then and still believed small magics were harmless. Odile, the records said. Former guildmistress. Presumed retired.

Presumed. But the fox had spoken of one who *keeps the door*, and doors, in Corvane's experience, were always kept by the people everyone forgot.

He rode out through the north gate under the strange white stars, following the cold.
