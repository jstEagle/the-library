## 13. The Long Winter Falls

The first snow came on the eve of Harvestmoon, when the larches should still have been gold and the river should still have been running brown and slow past the willows. Tomas woke in the grey hour before dawn because the silence was wrong.

He'd grown up on the Vesselwater. He knew every voice the river owned—the mutter of it over the gravel shallows, the deep throat-sound it made under the ferry pilings, the way ice sang along its edges in late winter, thin and glassy, like a wineglass struck with a fingernail. He knew the sound of a river going to sleep for the season.

This wasn't that.

This was the sound of a river being *stopped*—muffled all at once, mid-word, as if someone had laid a hand flat across its mouth.

He rose from his bunk in the safehouse loft without waking Pip, who slept curled around a padlock like a cat around a kill, and went down through the cold rooms to the back door that looked out over the millrace. His breath fogged. That was normal enough for an autumn morning. What wasn't normal was that the fog hung in the air without moving, bearded on the doorframe, bearded on the fence rails, bearded on Bramble's empty coat-hook, each thread of vapor frozen into place like the whole yard had been caught in amber.

And the snow. It fell straight down. Not drifting, not swirling, not slanting in the wind, because there was no wind—just flakes descending in perfect parallel lines, unhurried, enormous, each one big as a thumbnail, falling with the gravity of something deliberate rather than something weathered.

Tomas stood in the doorway a long time, barefoot on stone that should have bitten cold into his soles but didn't, because the cold had gone somewhere else. All of it. Every scrap of winter in the world seemed to have been gathered up and poured into one direction: north. Toward the herbalist's cottage where Odile lay dying. Toward Marguerite.

He didn't know that yet, standing there. He only knew that the hair on his arms was standing up and that some animal part of him, the part that had kept him alive through nine years of smuggling runs past crown cutters and two winters shipwrecked above the Fens, was telling him to run.

He made tea instead. It was what you did when the world went strange—you put a kettle on, because a kettle was proof that ordinary things still worked. He was on his second cup when Sefa came down, took one look out the window, and said a word in Old Vessic that he didn't need translated.

"It's her," Sefa said. Not a question.

"It's weather."

"Boy." Sefa pulled a chair from the table, turned it, sat astride it with her arms folded over the backrest. She was fifty if she was a day, gray-shot and scarred, with the build of a woman who had once held a bridge against three companies and been cashiered for it. "I fought in the Sundering War's last year. I saw battle-mages pull lightning out of clear sky. I saw a siege-engine enchanter freeze a harbor at Kellsmoor so the fleet couldn't sail—and I want you to understand, that was forty ships' worth of water, and it took eleven mages working together, and it killed four of them." She nodded at the window, at the straight-falling snow. "That is not weather. That is one person. And she doesn't even know she's doing it."

* * *

Marguerite came back two days later, or rather they went and brought her back, because Odile had died on the second night and the herbalist, Wren, had sent a boy downriver with a message Tomas read four times before he understood it: *She's awake. She knows what she is now. Come quickly, and come kind.*

She looked smaller when she climbed down from the cart. That was the thing he noticed first—not the frost in her hair that didn't melt, not the way snow bent around her in the street like iron filings around a lodestone, but simply that she was smaller, as though the unsealing had burned away something structural. Twelve years of careful smallness, maybe. The pretending. Whatever Odile's last lesson had taken, it had left behind a young woman who walked like someone carrying a weight so vast it had passed through heaviness and out the other side into lightness.

Pip reached her first, the way Pip reached everything first, skimming across the frozen yard on feet that found purchase where no purchase was. The child stopped an arm's length away and cocked their head.

"You're cold," Pip said. "But not the bad way."

"Pip—" Tomas started.

"No, listen." Pip held up both hands, palms out, the way they did when explaining locks. "Everything's cold the bad way right now. But she's cold the other way. Like the inside of a lock in winter when the tumblers are waiting." They looked up at Marguerite with the frank, unafraid curiosity that no adult ever managed. "Are the tumblers waiting?"

Marguerite knelt. Snow gathered on her shoulders and did not fall off.

"I don't know," she said. "I think maybe I'm the lock. Or the key. Odile never finished teaching me which."

Tomas watched from the doorway and felt something shift in his chest that he would spend the rest of the winter failing to name.

* * *

By the fourth day, the rivers were gone.

Not low. Not frozen at the edges. Gone—the Vesselwater locked bank to bank in a single sheet of blue-green ice thick enough to walk a horse on, and every tributary with it, and the millrace, and the rain-barrels, and the dew. The smugglers' routes, the arteries Tomas had spent a decade learning to read like his own pulse, closed in a night. The whole economy of the river country—the grain barges, the salt runs, the quiet movement of people who needed moving—ground to a halt between one dusk and one dawn.

In the safehouse kitchen, the company argued about what it meant while the kettle steamed and the windows frosted from the inside out.

"It's grief," Bramble said. They had been quiet since Ashgate—quiet even for Bramble, who wore silence like a second pelt. Their fox eye and their human eye both fixed on the middle distance. "You seal something alive inside a person for twenty-two years and then you crack it open in one night. Of course it pours out. Of course it's winter. Grief is cold. Anyone who's buried anyone knows that."

"The crown won't see grief," Sefa said flatly. "The crown will see a weapon."

"The crown will see *her*," Tomas said.

They all looked at him. He hadn't meant to say it aloud, but now that it was out he found he couldn't take it back, and worse, he found he wanted to say the rest of it.

"I've been thinking of this wrong since the tanner's quarter burned. All of us have. We've been running her like cargo—valuable cargo, cursed cargo, but cargo. Get her upriver, get her over the passes, find a hole deep enough and keep her in it until the hunt blows over." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "There is no hole deep enough. Look outside. She isn't hiding from the storm. She *is* the storm. Every hunter, every bloodline sorcerer, every man with a torch—they're not chasing a fugitive anymore. They're chasing the reason the world went white. And you can't smuggle that. There's no blind corner in the world big enough."

Silence. Outside, the snow kept falling in its patient, parallel lines.

"So we stop running," Sefa said at last. It wasn't a question either.

"I don't know what we do," Tomas admitted. "But I know we can't run, because there's nowhere the weather doesn't go."

* * *

It was Pip who told him the truth he'd already half-known, later that same week, in the worst possible way.

The hunters had come to the district—not the safehouse itself, not yet; Corvane was too methodical for that—but to the streets around it, house by house, offering the amnesty, weighing neighbors in the balance of their fear. The company had agreed on rules: nobody out alone after dark, nobody to the market, nobody near the checkpoints. Rules kept them alive. Rules were the hull of the boat.

So when Tomas counted heads at supper and came up one short, the cold that went through him had nothing to do with the season.

They found Pip at the old lockhouse on the canal, two streets inside the cordon, sitting cross-legged on the frozen towpath in front of a door that belonged to a widow named Marta Hesse—the widow who had bought fever-charms from Marguerite for six years and then looked at her differently after the Proclamation. Two crown soldiers stood over the child with drawn truncheons, and a third held Marta Hesse by the arm, and Pip was talking, of course Pip was talking, because Pip talked to locks and doors and gates and apparently to armed men too, in the same confiding tone.

"—and the funny thing is, your manacles are third-quality work, look at the rivets, I could have those open before you finished shouting—"

Tomas came out of the dark the way he'd learned to come out of the dark, fast and loud, playing the drunk, playing the fool, playing anything except what he was. Sefa flanked him with a lamplighter's pole she'd claimed off a dead hedge-witch and carried like the officer's blade she used to own. It should have gone badly. By every law of odds and crowns it should have gone badly.

Instead, the canal ice spoke.

There was no other word for it. A sound rolled up through the frozen water beneath their feet—a long, groaning exhalation, the sound a ship's timbers make in a storm—and every lantern within fifty yards guttered blue, and the frost on the lockhouse wall bloomed outward in patterns that no frost had ever made: spirals, whorls, the branching shape of antlers, the suggestion—only the suggestion, but unmistakable—of a vast paw print. The soldiers' breath stopped being visible because the air around them had gone past the temperature of breath. One of them dropped his truncheon. Another began, very quietly, to pray.

And Marguerite stepped out of the alley mouth with snow streaming sideways around her in a wind that existed nowhere else in the city, and she wasn't doing it—he could see that, even then, he could see that she wasn't doing any of it on purpose. Her eyes had gone pale as shallow ice. Her hands were bare in the killing cold and unburned.

"Let them go," she said, and her voice had a doubled quality, like a bell struck with another bell hidden inside it. "All of them. Now."

They went. Soldiers, widow, everyone—scattered into the dark like startled birds, and the wind died, and the frost-antlers faded, and Marguerite stood alone on the towpath looking down at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger.

"I heard Pip," she said, when Tomas reached her. Her voice was just her voice again, small and shaking. "Across the whole city. Through the ice. I heard them, and then I was—" She swallowed. "I was *walking*, Tomas. I don't remember deciding to walk."

"I know."

"You should be afraid of me." She said it evenly, the way you state a fact you've checked twice. "Everyone should be. You saw what I am now. What I carry. You should put me out of the walls tonight and let the winter go where it goes."

Tomas looked at her—at this woman he'd hauled upriver in a fish cart, lied for, bled for, lost sleep over, planned escape routes around like the most precious contraband of his career. Cargo. He had thought of her as cargo, and the word curdled in his mouth now, and something older and larger moved in underneath it and settled in like it had always lived there.

"When my sister was sick," he said, "there was a hedge-witch in the tanner's quarter who sat up with her three nights running and never took a coin. They burned her anyway. First fire I ever watched." He stepped closer, close enough that the cold coming off Marguerite bit at his face, and he didn't step back. "I swore on the river that I'd never again stand on the far side of a fire watching someone burn. So no. I'm not putting you out of the walls. I'm not afraid of you, Marguerite Whitlock. Or if I am, it's the good fear. The kind you feel standing at the edge of deep water, knowing you're going to swim anyway."

"That's not devotion," she whispered. "That's stubbornness."

"In my family," Tomas said, "it's the same thing."

Behind them, Pip whooped, and the lockhouse door—which had been chained shut since summer—swung open all on its own, smooth as butter, just to show off.

* * *

On the ninth day of the wrong winter, the bells of Greyhollow rang out of pattern—three-and-one, three-and-one, the old siege signal that hadn't sounded since the Sundering War—and riders came down the frozen high road with the royal banner snapping in a wind that finally, finally moved.

Tomas stood on the safehouse roof with a spyglass and counted what the crown had decided one girl was worth.

Two thousand foot. A sapper train. Four of the new culverins on sledges, because the rivers were ice and the roads were snow-packed and the army had come *on* the winter, riding the very thing it feared, the way you'd ride a whale you meant to harpoon. Behind them, supply wagons in a line that vanished over the hills, and behind *those*, banners he didn't recognize—dark silk, silver devices, the colors of the old bloodlines who were supposed to exist only in genealogies and grudges.

"They've stopped hunting," Sefa said beside him, her breath pluming. "You understand that? This isn't a search anymore. This is a siege. They're not looking for where she's hiding." She lowered her own glass slowly. "They already know. Someone told them, or the winter itself told them—it doesn't matter. They've drawn a circle around this whole district and everything in it, and they mean to tighten it until she walks out to them or freezes with the rest of us."

"How long can we hold?"

"We?" Sefa glanced at him, and for the first time since he'd known her, the disgraced battle-mage smiled—a thin, fierce, almost happy thing. "Boy, we can't hold at all. Two thousand against six, with a child and a fox and a charm-seller. We can't hold a week."

Down in the yard, Marguerite stood in the falling snow with her face turned toward the approaching army, and the snow bent around her in a wide, reverent circle, and she raised one hand—not a weapon's gesture, not a spell-caster's gesture, but the open-palmed gesture of someone greeting something arriving from far away.

"She's listening to it," Pip said, appearing between them on the roof beam as children do, impossibly, silently. "The winter. It talks to her more every day. Last night it told her things about the mountains." A pause. "It says the army is afraid. It says the whole army is *cold and afraid*, and it keeps asking her what it's allowed to do about that."

"What did she tell it?" Tomas asked quietly.

Pip's mismatched eyes—human and fox—turned up to him.

"She hasn't answered yet."

Below, the first culverin sledge crested the ridge, and the sun flashed on its barrel like a needle finding a vein, and the true winter—the long one, the deciding one—came down on Greyhollow like a hand closing.

Tomas gripped the roof edge and understood, with a clarity that felt like frost forming, that the choice would not wait much longer: not hers alone, whatever Odile's ghost believed, but all of theirs. The storm had become a family, and the family had become a storm, and the crown was marching straight into the heart of both.

He went down to wake the others.
