## 14. The Crown's Bargain

The road to the capital had never felt so long, nor the city at its end so small.

Corvane had served the crown for nineteen years—long enough to know the smell of every corridor in Vhalcrest Keep, long enough to read the moods of ministers by the set of their shoulders, long enough to stop believing the marble was clean. It was never clean. The white stone drank smoke and secrets alike, and by midwinter it had gone grey again, as it always did.

He rode through the gates with Ashgate's ashes still under his fingernails and Bramble's riddle still lodged behind his teeth like a fishbone.

*What is kept warm by many hands and dies in one?*

He had turned it over for six days on the road north and south again. He did not like the answers that kept arriving.

They took his weapons at the inner gate, which was customary, and his boots' laces, which was not. A chamberlain with ink-stained cuffs smiled too much and said the Privy Council would see him "at their convenience." Corvane had learned to measure power by how long a man was made to wait, and he waited three hours in a room with no windows before the doors opened onto the council chamber—and onto something he had not expected.

There were seven seats at the long table. Six of them belonged to ministers he knew: the Lord Treasurer, the Master of Writs, the Marshal of the Interior, men whose faces appeared on coin and proclamations. The seventh seat sat at the head, above the crown's own crest worked into the wood, and the man in it wore no livery Corvane recognized.

He was old in a way that seemed less like age than like weathering—stone left out too long in bad air. His robes were plain grey wool, expensive only if you knew cloth, and his hands were folded on the table like a man at prayer who had long ago stopped expecting anyone to answer.

"Lord Inquisitor," the old man said. "Sit."

No name was offered. No minister introduced him. The Marshal of the Interior would not meet Corvane's eyes, and that told Corvane more than any introduction could have: this man outranked all of them, and none of them liked admitting it.

"I've come to report on the Ashgate action," Corvane said, standing. He had been offered a chair. He did not take it yet. Some habits were armor. "Forty-one gifted taken alive. Three resisted and were—"

"We know what happened at Ashgate." The old man's voice was dry as paper in a dry room. "We have known everything you've done since before you did it. Sit down, Inquisitor. This is not an accounting. This is a commissioning."

Corvane sat. The chair was cold.

"The Edict of Ash," the old man continued, "was never what you thought it was. You believed you were sent out to burn a plague from the realm's flesh. Magic is chaos; chaos killed a hundred thousand in the Sundering War; therefore magic must be cut away wherever it grows. A clean doctrine. Simple. I understand why it sustained you." He unfolded his hands. "It was always a lie, and you were always the instrument of it, and now the instrument has grown dull enough that we must speak plainly to it."

Around the table, the ministers studied their papers. One of them—the Master of Writs, a soft man with soft hands—had begun very slightly to sweat.

"Say what you mean," Corvane said.

"Good. Bluntness saves hours." The old man gestured, and a servant brought a chest to the table, iron-banded, old enough that its hinges wept rust. It was opened with two keys, held by two different ministers, which told Corvane the contents had been feared longer than any of them had held office.

Inside, on a bed of rotted velvet, lay a fragment of bone.

It might have been mistaken for ivory, or for river stone worn smooth. But when the lid came fully open the candles guttered—not blown out, *guttered*, leaning toward the chest like flowers toward sun—and a cold rolled across the chamber that had nothing to do with winter outside. Frost feathered up the inside of the chest walls. Corvane's breath hung white before him.

He knew what it was before anyone said it. He had spent half a year chasing rumors of it through burned villages and terrified safehouses. He had heard a shapeshifter speak of it in riddles over tea.

"The Wolf of Winter," he said quietly.

"What remained of her after the Sundering," the old man agreed. "Her body went into the ice at Cael Mora and the ice took it whole. Her power did not die with her. Power like that does not die. It waits." He closed the chest himself, and the candles straightened, and the room remembered it was warm. "For twelve years our agents have tracked where the guilds hid it. They hid it well—they sealed it inside a living vessel, a child, and scattered the records, and killed or exiled everyone who knew. Very thorough. Your Elara Whitlock's work, mostly. She was good at hiding things."

The name landed in Corvane's chest like a dropped stone hitting still water. He kept his face still. Nineteen years of practice kept his face still.

"She's dead," he said.

"Eleven years dead, yes. But her work survived her, and so did her daughter. Marguerite Whitlock. Charm-seller of Greyhollow. Apprentice to Odile Vasse, last ranking member of the Guild of the Quiet Flame." The old man recited it without looking at anything—a page in his head, well-thumbed. "Your map found her, Inquisitor. You simply didn't know what you'd found. You thought you were hunting a fugitive charm-witch. You were, in fact, walking past the most valuable object in the world."

"Object." Corvane heard his own voice go flat. "She's a girl."

"She's a *seal*," the old man said, "wearing a girl. And the seal is weakening. You've seen the signs yourself—you reported them. Frost in midsummer. Rivers freezing from the source down. Snow falling early and falling wrong. That is not weather, Lord Inquisitor. That is pressure. The vessel is cracking, and what is inside it is beginning to remember itself." He leaned forward for the first time, and his eyes were pale and patient and utterly without warmth, like a frozen lake over deep water. "Which means the moment of claiming is nearly upon us."

"Claiming," Corvane repeated. The word tasted wrong in his mouth. "You want me to bring her here. To the throne."

"To the *crown*," the old man corrected softly. "Yes. Alive. Unspoiled. The bloodlines have waited eleven generations for this. Do you imagine the Edict of Ash was written by frightened clerks? The war ended because one creature chose to give everything, and the old families watched her do it and thought: *what a waste, that such power should be spent rather than owned.* Every purge since, every burning, every registration list—it was pruning, Inquisitor. Weeding the field so the true harvest wouldn't be choked. We could not let the gift scatter among ten thousand hedge-witches and healers and charm-sellers. We had to gather it, compress it, terrify it into hiding until it concentrated. And it has. All of it. In one girl in Greyhollow who sells luck-knots at market."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere below the floor, the keep's great clocks ticked, indifferent.

Corvane looked around the table at the six ministers, and finally understood the shape of the last nineteen years. Every pyre he had lit had been a harvest. Every witch he had dragged to the ash-pits had been a weed pulled from a garden someone else intended to plant. He thought of the tanner's quarter, of the hedge-witch who healed children, of forty-one people caged at Ashgate whose names he had carried like stones. He thought of a portrait of Elara Whitlock that he had looked at more times than any living person should look at a portrait, and of the promise he had made to her once, in a garden, in another life, that he would protect whatever she needed protected.

"You're going to put her on a throne?" he asked. "A vessel? You'll make the Wolf a crown's weapon?"

"We're going to *empty* her and fill the crown," the old man said, with the mild tone of a man correcting arithmetic. "The rite is old. Older than the guilds. The vessel's memories, her self, her—call it soul, the guild-mages did—are stripped away in the transfer. What remains afterward is a body. Compliant. Empty. The power goes where power should go: to the seat that holds the realm together."

Something in Corvane went very quiet, the way a forest goes quiet before snowfall.

"And the girl," he said. "The actual girl. Marguerite Whitlock. Twenty-three years old. Likes honey in her tea, I'd wager. Has a laugh. Is afraid of things. What happens to her?"

No one answered.

The Lord Treasurer examined a document. The Master of Writs blotted a page that needed no blotting. The Marshal of the Interior discovered a sudden fascination with the ceiling's moldings. Only the old man met Corvane's eyes, and what was in them was not cruelty. Cruelty would have been easier. What was in them was *accounting*—the flat arithmetic of a man who had subtracted a human being years ago and never noticed the sum change.

"The vessel is the packaging," the old man said. "Packaging is discarded."

Corvane stood. He did not remember deciding to stand.

"You asked me," he said, "for nineteen years, to burn people. I did it because I believed the realm would break otherwise. I did it because I saw what unchecked power did at Cael Mora—I stood in that snow when I was a boy, Minister, I saw the fields that still won't grow. I made my peace with ash because I thought ash was mercy compared to war." His voice stayed level. He was proud of that, distantly, the way one is proud of a stranger. "But you're not preventing a war. You're *starting* one. You're going to carve the guardian out of a living girl and wear her like a ring, and when she fights it—and she will fight it, they always fight it—you'll call her a monster and burn her empty body in a square and call it justice."

"Careful," said the Marshal of the Interior, finding his voice at last.

"No," the old man said mildly. "Let him finish. It's instructive." He rose too, unhurried, and came around the table, and up close he smelled of cold stone and something faintly sweet, like flowers kept too long in a crypt. "Inquisitor Corvane. You are the finest hunter the crown has ever employed. You are also a grieving man who has spent nineteen years mistaking duty for penance, and grief makes instruments obedient. So I will tell you plainly what your choices are, because I respect instruments that know their edges."

He stopped an arm's length away. The frost-chill of the chest seemed to cling to him still, or perhaps it came off him naturally.

"Bring me the vessel alive within the month, and you will be rewarded beyond anything your rank can currently imagine, and the girl will be handled as gently as the process allows—which is to say, no more cruelly than necessary. Fail, refuse, warn, or waver, and I will declare you corrupted, and you will learn what we do with hunters who turn. There is no third path. There never is. That is the lesson of your entire career, if you had been paying attention."

"There's always a third path," Corvane said. "That's the lesson of mine."

Something flickered across the old man's face—surprise, quickly filed away, the way a clerk files a mislabeled ledger. "Then you've already chosen."

"I haven't chosen anything," Corvane said. "I'm going to Greyhollow. I'm going to find Marguerite Whitlock before your other agents do—because you have others, don't you, I can smell them on this room—and I am going to decide for myself what happens to the girl inside. Not the throne. Not the bloodlines. Me. I owe that much to her mother, and I owe considerably more than that to myself."

He walked out. No one stopped him. At the door he paused, hand on the iron ring, and did not turn around.

"Bramble," he said. "The shapeshifter you think gave us nothing. Gave me a riddle. *What is kept warm by many hands and dies in one?*" He let the silence sit. "I think I finally know the answer. I think you do too. I think that's why you're afraid."

He left the chest behind him, locked with two keys, breathing its cold into a room full of men who called a girl a package, and did not look back.

* * *

The stables at midnight smelled of straw and horse and honest filth, and Corvane breathed it in like a man surfacing from deep water.

His mare was saddled before the question fully formed, and once it formed it would not leave: *Where does a hunted woman run, when the roads are watched and the cities are maps in enemy hands?*

Not to the capital. Not to any garrison town. Back along the threads of the web he himself had woven—every safehouse he'd mapped, every smuggler's route he'd traced, every name in the ledgers Sefa had burned at Ashgate. He had built the net. He alone knew every knot of it. Which meant he alone could unweave it, strand by strand, ahead of the riders the bloodlines would send the moment his horse cleared the outer wall.

Because they would send riders. He had seen it in the old man's eyes. *Fail, refuse, warn, or waver.*

Corvane swung into the saddle. Behind him Vhalcrest slept beneath its grey marble, and somewhere in its heart a chest of bone waited for a girl who did not yet know she was a treasure, and riders would come at dawn, and the snow was falling early again, falling wrong, falling like a tide answering a moon.

He turned the mare north, toward Greyhollow, toward the woman he had spent a season hunting and a lifetime failing, and rode into the falling white.
