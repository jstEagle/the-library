## 15. Wolfkin

The snow had stopped falling on the world, but not inside Marguerite.

She woke each morning to the sound of it—soft, endless, a hush that lived behind her ribs now, where the seal used to be. Odile's unsealing had been partial, a door opened by inches rather than flung wide, and what came through it was not a flood of memory but a slow tide. Each night the tide rose higher. Each morning she woke with frost blooming on the window glass in patterns she didn't draw, and Sefa waiting downstairs with her arms crossed and her jaw set like a woman preparing to argue with the weather.

"Again," Sefa said, on the third day.

They trained in the cellar of the herbalist's cottage, among hanging bundles of dried yarrow and jars of tinctures gone cloudy with age. The space was small, earthen-floored, cold in a way that had nothing to do with the season outside. Marguerite stood at one end; Sefa stood at the other with a copper bowl balanced on a stool between them, filled with water drawn from the well.

"The point," Sefa said, "is simple. You freeze that water, you unfreeze it. Nothing else. No snowstorms, no ice sculptures of wolves, no—" she gestured at the ceiling, where frost had crept up through the floorboards overnight and hung from the beams like pale moss, "—whatever this is."

"I didn't do that."

"You didn't *mean* to do that. There's a difference, and it's the difference I'm trying to teach you." Sefa crouched beside the stool, elbows on knees. She was perhaps forty, hard-used, with a dueling scar that split one eyebrow and hands that never quite stopped moving, always half-forming sigils out of old habit even when no magic was wanted. "Battle-mages burn out young because they fight their power instead of riding it. I've seen gifted folk tear themselves apart trying to shove what they are back into the shape of what they were."

"And you think that's what I'm doing."

"I think," Sefa said, "that every time you close your eyes and try to push the memories down, the frost climbs another foot up the walls. So yes. I think you're fighting something that can't be fought. And I think you're going to have to stop."

Marguerite looked at the water in the bowl. It shivered at the edges already, skin forming over its surface like the first thin pane of winter on a pond. She hadn't done that either—or she had, without meaning to, the way a frightened animal sheds scent into the air around it.

"It's not mine," she said quietly. "None of it. The memories, the power, the—the wolf. It belonged to someone else. Someone who died so the war would end. And now it's sitting inside me like a lodger who won't leave, wearing my body, freezing my windows, dreaming my dreams in her voice. How am I supposed to just *accept* that?"

Sefa was silent for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its drill-sergeant edge.

"When the Order cast me out," she said, "they took my name off the rolls and burned my sigil-book and told everyone I'd died at Vell's Crossing. Officially, Sefa Marran is ash. What walked out of that court-martial was something new—something with her hands and her training but no name, no rank, no right to exist." She turned her scarred eyebrow toward Marguerite. "I spent two years fighting that. Drinking, brawling, running river cargo for men who paid me to forget I'd ever been anything else. And the whole time, my magic sat in me like a coal under wet cloth—smoldering, choking on itself, hurting me worse than any enemy ever had. Because it wasn't the casting-out that burned. It was the refusal."

"So what did you do?"

"I let myself be both," Sefa said simply. "The mage they killed and the smuggler who survived her. It wasn't tidy. It isn't tidy now. But the burning stopped." She tapped the rim of the copper bowl. "Freeze the water. But don't order it. Don't command it like a soldier. Ask it. Like you're asking someone you've only just met whether they're cold."

Marguerite almost laughed. Almost. Instead she knelt on the packed earth, laid her palms on either side of the bowl—not touching, because touching made things worse—and closed her eyes.

And there she was again.

The snowfield stretched white to the horizon, vast and silent as a held breath. The sky above it was the color of pewter, and the wind moved across the drifts in long low sighs, and somewhere beyond sight a wolf was singing.

Marguerite knew the dream by heart now. She had walked this snowfield a hundred times since Odile's cottage, since the unsealing, since the night the frost climbed her arms and wrote words in a language dead for three centuries. Usually she fought it—clenched against it, tried to wake, tried to scream herself back into her own skull. Usually the dream fought back, pressing down on her like deep water, until she surfaced gasping with ice on her palms.

This time, she did what Sefa asked.

She stood in the snow and did not run.

*You're here,* said the voice—that other voice, low and resonant, a woman's voice worn smooth by years of solitude. *You've come all the way in. I wondered if you ever would.*

"Where are you?" Marguerite called, and her breath hung silver in the frozen air.

*Everywhere. Nowhere. Wherever you carry me.* A pause, and then, almost shy: *I felt you fighting me. For months. Every time you pushed, it hurt us both. Did you know that?*

"No," Marguerite admitted. "I didn't know there was an *us* to hurt."

*There was always an us.* The voice came closer, or seemed to—the snow brightening ahead of her, the light gathering itself into a shape. *That's what your mother understood, and Odile after her, and why they hid me in you instead of destroying me. I cannot be destroyed, little vessel. I can only be carried. Or shared.*

The light coalesced, and Marguerite saw her.

Not a wolf—not entirely. A figure standing hip-deep in the drifts, wrapped in a cloak that might have been fur and might have been falling snow, with eyes the gray-green of sea ice. She was younger than Marguerite had imagined. Younger than Odile. Her face was ordinary in the way of people who had once been beautiful and then been alone too long to care.

"What's your name?" Marguerite asked. "Your real one. Before they called you the Wolf of Winter."

The figure smiled, and it was a tired smile, and a glad one, and Marguerite felt both of those things in her own chest as if they were hers—as if they had always been hers.

*Say it yourself,* the Wolf said. *You've known it since the unsealing. You've known it every time the frost wrote on the glass. Names want to be spoken, not kept.*

And Marguerite found that it was true. The name rose in her like a note rising in the throat before song, and she spoke it aloud into the silver air—

and the snowfield blazed.

* * *

She came back to the cellar on her hands and knees, gasping, tears freezing on her cheeks before they could fall.

Sefa was beside her instantly, gripping her shoulders. "Breathe. Look at me. Breathe."

But Marguerite was looking past her—at the copper bowl, where the water had frozen solid and then, impossibly, begun to melt again, turning gently, swirling in a slow spiral as though stirred by a patient finger. Around them the frost on the beams was receding, drawing back like a tide going out, leaving the wood dark and clean. The whole cellar felt lighter. Warmer. As though something enormous had finally exhaled.

"She has a name," Marguerite whispered.

Sefa went still. "Whose name?"

"Hers. The Wolf's. She was a person, Sefa. Before the war, before the sealing—she was a shepherd's daughter from the northern valleys, and she was lonely, gods, she was so lonely, and everyone she loved died in the Sundering and she carried the winter alone for sixty years because that's what guardians do, that's what she thought guardians *had* to do—" The words were tumbling out of her now, twelve years of silence cracking open. "She chose it. The sacrifice. Nobody forced her. She walked into the sealing ritual knowing she'd be forgotten, knowing the memory would need a vessel someday, and she did it anyway because she loved the world more than she feared being erased." Marguerite pressed a hand flat against her own sternum. "She's still in love with it. With everything. The crocuses coming up through snow. The smell of bread. She's been locked in the dark for three hundred years and she's still *in love with the world*, and I've been treating her like a disease."

Sefa eased back onto her heels. For a long moment she said nothing, and the melted water in the bowl turned slowly, catching the lamplight.

"My master at the Order had a saying," she said at last. "*Power that is resisted becomes a storm. Power that is welcomed becomes a servant.* He was a pompous old goat and mostly wrong about everything, but I think he was right about that." She studied Marguerite's face—searching, Marguerite realized, for signs of what? Madness? Possession? Something else? "Is she still there? Now? Can you feel her?"

"Yes." Marguerite closed her eyes, and this time reaching inward felt less like prying open a locked box and more like opening a door onto a warm room. "She's quiet. Resting. But she's—" A startled laugh escaped her. "She likes your scar. She says duelists who survive to grow old are rare, and beautiful, like lightning-struck oaks."

Something flickered across Sefa's face—too quick to name. "Does she now." She stood, brushed earth from her knees, and offered Marguerite a hand up. "Well. Then we'd better get on with it. If I'm going to train a three-hundred-year-old legend, I want dinner first."

* * *

It did not become easy. Nothing about it became easy.

But it became *possible*, and after the weeks of drowning, possible felt like grace.

The change was in the method more than the magic. Before, Marguerite had approached her gift the way she'd once approached a snarling dog—sideways, warily, hands raised, ready to retreat. Now she walked straight in. Each morning she and Sefa descended to the cellar, and each morning Marguerite closed her eyes and *asked*, and the Wolf answered—sometimes in dreams, sometimes in the sudden sharp clarity of waking thought, sometimes just in the way the frost formed: neat spirals now instead of claw-marks, ordered, deliberate, almost calligraphic.

She learned to freeze the copper bowl and thaw it. Then to freeze it in shapes—a rose, a key, a small perfect wolf no bigger than her thumb that trotted the rim of the bowl and made Pip shriek with delight the one afternoon he crept down to watch. She learned to pull cold out of things instead of putting it in: drawing the fever from Tomas's shoulder after a bad fall on the icy dock stairs, drawing the ache from Bramble's half-fox joints when rain came, drawing the grief, briefly, from her own chest on the nights when Odile's absence sat on her like a stone.

And she learned, gradually, to listen.

*Tell me about her,* Marguerite asked one evening, sitting cross-legged before the hearth while Tomas mended nets and Bramble dozed with one ear cocked. *My mother. Elara.*

The answer came like warmth spreading through cold fingers. *She came to me in dreams, as you do. Nineteen years old and furious. She'd found the old texts, the ones the bloodlines suppressed, and she marched into my snowfield and informed me that hiding me in some future child was cowardice dressed up as wisdom.* The Wolf's laugh moved through Marguerite like a shiver of delight. *I liked her immediately. She argued with me for six nights. On the seventh she wept, and asked me what I actually wanted, and when I told her—to be remembered, truly remembered, by more than one poor soul at a time—she swore she'd find a better way than I'd ever been given.*

"She did find one," Marguerite said softly. "It just took her whole life, and mine, and it nearly broke everything first."

*Most good ways do,* said the Wolf. *Yours will too, before it's done.*

Above the hearth, the fire flared green-white for a heartbeat—cold flame, winter-colored—and settled again. Tomas looked up from his nets, saw the color, and wisely said nothing. But his eyes met Marguerite's across the room, and held there a moment longer than casual, and she understood that he was keeping count. Keeping watch. Deciding, perhaps, how afraid to be.

She found she minded less than she expected. That was the strangest gift of the reconciliation: fear required distance, and there was no distance anymore. The Wolf was not a lodger. She was a hand slipped into another hand in the dark.

* * *

On the ninth morning of the new training, Sefa brought the copper bowl and a knife, and set both on the stool with a look that promised trouble.

"Today we test the edge," she said. "Control under pressure. I'm going to distract you, frighten you, maybe hurt you a little—I'll apologize in advance—and your job is to keep the water exactly as it is. Not frozen. Not boiling. Still. Can you do that?"

Marguerite looked at the bowl. The water lay calm, reflecting the low rafters, reflecting her own face upside down.

"I can try."

"Trying is the whole profession." Safa circled behind her. "Ready?"

She was not ready. Nothing could have readied her for what Sefa did next—which was, in fairness, nothing magical at all. Sefa simply leaned close to her ear and said, in a flat, careful voice:

"Inquisitor Corvane crossed the ford at Millbrook two days ago. He's asking after a girl with frost-scars on her palms. He has six hunters and a writ that lets him burn whatever stands between him and you. He knew your mother, Marguerite. Loved her, by some accounts. And he is coming *here*."

The water should have shattered to ice. Every instinct in Marguerite's body clenched toward panic—the old instinct, the animal one, freeze or flee—and she felt the cold leap up her spine like a spark up a fuse—

—and then the Wolf was there, steady as bedrock, and Marguerite did the new thing instead of the old thing. She breathed. She let the panic move through her like weather through a valley, arriving and passing, and she kept her eyes on the water.

Still. Unfrozen. Trembling only at the very center, a single ring widening outward, the record of a heartbeat that had stumbled and recovered.

"Good," Sefa whispered, and there was awe in it, poorly disguised. "Gods above. *Good.*"

Marguerite exhaled, long and shaking. "Was that true? About Corvane?"

"Half true. He crossed the ford, yes. The rest I invented." Sefa came around to face her, and her expression was grim. "But it'll be true soon enough. Men like him don't stop. Which means the question isn't whether you can keep a bowl of water calm in a quiet cellar." She nodded toward the window, where grey daylight fell through the frost-ferns—frost-ferms that now, Marguerite noticed, arranged themselves in patterns almost like writing, almost like welcome. "The question is what you do on the day the real Inquisitor comes through that door, and everything you love is standing behind you, and the whole winter you carry is begging to be let loose."

Marguerite looked down at her hands. No frost on the palms anymore. Just her own hands, chapped from well-water and mending, scarred at the knuckle from a childhood of small deceptions. Hands that had hidden things for twelve years and were learning, at last, to hold them openly.

"Then I suppose," she said, "we'd better practice until I'm ready."

She reached for the bowl—and stopped.

Because the water had frozen after all. Not by her will, and not against it. A single figure stood in the ice, tiny and precise as a woodcut: a woman in a traveling cloak, walking toward the viewer out of a storm of etched lines, and beneath her feet, fine as needlework, a line of frost-script that Marguerite could now read as easily as her own name.

*SHE IS COMING NORTH. AND SHE IS NOT ALONE.*

Marguerite lifted her head. Through the small cottage window, far off across the whitening fields, she could see the road—and on the road, a speck of movement, riders perhaps, or only shadows thrown by clouds.

The Wolf stirred within her, calm and grave as deep water.

*Winter remembers,* she said. *Now we must decide what it does next.*
