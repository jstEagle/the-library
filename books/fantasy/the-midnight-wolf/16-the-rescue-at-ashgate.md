## 16. The Rescue at Ashgate

Tomas had smuggled silk, salt, stolen relics, and once—memorably—a sleeping bear. He had never smuggled a person out of Ashgate Prison, and the difference, he told himself as he crouched in the reed-choked ditch below its eastern wall, was that silk did not scream when you squeezed it.

Ashgate had been a fortress before it was a prison, back when Greyhollow's kings still feared invasion from the river. Now it feared nothing but paper. That was the joke of the place: the crown had learned that walls were expensive and ledgers were cheap. Every gifted soul inside was catalogued, cross-referenced, and filed—their names inked in three copies, their crimes reduced to a single word, *gifted*, as if it were theft or treason instead of simply being born wrong.

Bramble was in there. Somewhere behind those grey stones, half-fox and half-broken, holding whatever riddle they'd wrung from her with teeth that were more fox than human now, if Sefa's sources were right.

Tomas looked down the line of his crew and felt the old riverman's fear settle into him like cold water in his boots.

Sefa stood nearest the wall, arms bare despite the frost, her disgraced battle-mage's sigils scarred over with ash where she'd burned them off rather than surrender them. She'd told him once that magic left a hole when you cut it out. Tonight she'd filled the hole with something else. Her eyes were steady as ballast stone.

Pip was beside her, small enough to be mistaken for a bundle of rags, fingers working restlessly against each other like they always did when there was a lock nearby. The child hadn't spoken since Bramble was taken—not from fear, Tomas suspected, but because Pip's words went to locks first and people second, and every lock in Greyhollow had been listening hard these past weeks.

And Marguerite.

She knelt at the edge of the ditch with snow gathering on her shoulders and not melting, which should have frightened him more than it did. Twelve days since Odile died. Ten since the winter had come down early and wrong, rivers freezing mid-current, frost crawling uphill against the wind. Nine since he'd watched her wake screaming with ice blooming across the floorboards under her hands, and understood that the cargo he'd sworn to protect was not cargo at all but the storm itself, given a girl's face and a smuggler's luck.

He wasn't afraid of her anymore. That was the strange part. Somewhere between the fever and the frost, fear had turned into something else, something with weight and direction, like a current you could finally read.

"You don't have to do this," he said quietly. "Any of you. I can go in alone."

Sefa snorted. "You'd be dead before the second gate."

"I'd be *caught* before the second gate," Tomas corrected. "Dead comes later, after the paperwork."

Marguerite smiled—a thin, tired thing, but real. "We're not doing this because we have to," she said. "We're doing it because Bramble would have done it for us. For any of us. For strangers." She looked up at the prison wall, at the smoke rising thin and greasy from its chimneys. "That's the whole point of us, isn't it? We're nobody's. So we're everybody's."

Tomas felt those words land somewhere beneath his ribs and stay there.

"Right," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he deserved. "Then let's go steal some people."

* * *

The plan was Sefa's, which meant it was elegant and slightly blasphemous.

Ashgate took its water from the river through an old culvert, sealed a generation ago with iron grating and forgotten by everyone except the people who lived outside walls and remembered everything. Pip could open the grate in ninety seconds. From there, a service passage ran beneath the cells—built, according to Sefa, for carting out the dead, because fortresses built for war were honest about what war cost, and prisons built for bureaucracy preferred to pretend.

The passage would bring them up into the records room. Not the cells—the records. Because Sefa had been very clear about this part, gripping Tomas's shoulder with fingers that trembled only slightly: "Names are how they hold us. You burn the ledger, and half those prisoners become unprosecutable. Warden can't execute what the crown can't prove exists."

"And the other half?"

"The other half we walk out the front door," Sefa said, "while everyone's running around shouting about fire."

It was a mad plan. It was also, Tomas admitted, the kind of plan that worked precisely because no one sane would try it.

They went in through the culvert at the hour when the watch changed and the guards' bellies were full of bad stew and worse ale. The river water came up to their waists and the cold of it was ordinary cold, honest cold, and Tomas found himself almost grateful for it—for one last stretch of being merely a man in a cold river, before the night became what it was going to become.

Pip reached the iron grating and laid both palms flat against it. The child's lips moved, whispering to it—Tomas had never asked what Pip said to locks, whether it was argument or apology or something like prayer—and the grate sighed open like an old man yielding his seat.

"Ninety seconds," Sefa murmured. "Show-off."

Pip grinned, and it was the first grin in ten days, and Tomas thought: *this is why we're here. Not for strategy. For the grin.*

* * *

The service passage smelled of lime and old sorrow. They moved by Marguerite's light—or rather, by her dark, because she'd learned in the safehouse weeks to pull the cold around them like a curtain, muffling sound and dimming torchlight in the sconces they passed, frost feathering along the walls in patterns too delicate to be anything but deliberate. The Wolf's power, worn gently. A guardian's trick, not a weapon's.

"She's getting better at it," Sefa whispered, awed despite herself.

"She's getting better at *not fighting it*," Marguerite corrected softly. "There's a difference."

The ladder to the records room ended at a trapdoor that was, thank all the patient gods of thieves, locked only from above and only with a simple bolt. Pip went up first, eased it aside, and peered out into a room lined floor to ceiling with shelves, and shelves, and shelves.

Ledgers. Hundreds of them. Names upon names upon names, going back past the Edict, past the Sundering War, past living memory—every gifted person the crown had ever found, ever registered, ever hunted, ever burned.

Tomas climbed up into that dry, papery silence and felt the weight of it press down on him. This room was the war. Not the fires, not the hunters—this. Ink and certainty, filed and cross-referenced.

Sefa stood in the middle of it with her eyes closed, breathing slowly, and when she opened them they were wet and furious.

"My name's in one of these," she said quietly. "Third shelf, probably. Battle-mage, Second Cohort, dishonorable discharge." She flexed her scarred hands. "I spent six years trying to burn it out of myself so it wouldn't be true anymore. And it was all just—" she gestured at the shelves, at the whole suffocating architecture of it, "—*paper*. Somebody wrote us down and decided that made us theirs."

"So we unwrite it," Marguerite said.

Sefa looked at her. Something passed between them then that Tomas couldn't follow and didn't need to—the mage who'd amputated her own gift and the girl learning to carry hers, recognizing each other across the distance.

"How much time do we have?" Sefa asked.

"Until the watch rounds again," Tomas said. "A quarter hour, maybe less."

Sefa nodded once. Then she raised her hands, and the scars on her forearms split open with light.

Tomas had seen her use magic exactly twice before—small things, desperate things, a shove here, a spark there. This was different. Fire poured off her in a slow, controlled tide, rolling along the shelves in waves she shaped with both hands like a woman combing hair, and the ledgers caught and burned and burned, and the room filled with the smell of scorched ink and the sound of names coming apart into ash.

She was crying as she did it. Laughing, too. Both at once, ugly and beautiful.

"Third shelf," she choked out, finding it, tearing it free whole and feeding it personally into the blaze. "That one's mine. *That one's mine.*"

Pip whooped—a wild, ringing sound that echoed down through the trapdoor—and started pulling ledgers off the lower shelves and hurling them into the fire like a child feeding ducks, shrieking with joy. Marguerite stood at the door with frost coiling off her fingers, keeping watch, and the cold pouring off her met the heat of the fire and made a shimmering wall of steam that hid the light from the corridor beyond.

"We've got maybe five minutes before someone notices the chimney smoke," Tomas called. "Sefa—"

"I know, I know." She swept her arms wide and the fire collapsed inward, drawing itself down into a core of white heat that she gathered like a ball of yarn and pressed against the stone floor, where it sank and spread and ate downward toward the foundations. "Let the warden explain *that* to his superiors."

* * *

The cells were the hard part, because the cells held people, and people are heavier than paper.

They came up out of the records wing into the main block, and the alarm had already begun—bells clanging, boots hammering, shouts of *fire* and *records* and *how in the hells*. Chaos, but organized chaos; Ashgate's garrison was trained, and trained men regroup fast.

So they didn't hide. That was the decision, made in the space of three heartbeats in a smoke-hazed corridor: they wouldn't sneak. They would walk.

Sefa went first, wreathed in flame that didn't touch her, and the guards who came around the corner took one look and decided their careers lay elsewhere. Pip ran the cell locks like a festival—palm to iron, a whispered word, a snap of release, palm to the next—moving down the row faster than Tomas could count, opening doors two and three at a time while prisoners stumbled blinking into the light.

"Go," Tomas kept saying, hauling dazed men and women upright, pointing them down the corridor toward the service stairs. "Culvert, follow the water, keep left at the fork. Go, go—"

And Marguerite walked the center of the block with the frost streaming off her like banners, and the prisoners looked at her—at this slight, gray-eyed girl wreathed in winter—and one old woman fell to her knees and whispered a word Tomas didn't know, a word from before the Sundering, and Marguerite reached down and lifted her up and said, "Not me. All of us. Keep walking."

Cell by cell. Lock by lock. Name by name.

They found Bramble in the deep cells, in a room with no window and a chair bolted to the floor, and for one horrible moment Tomas thought they'd broken her—that the creature huddled in the corner was all fox now, all animal terror, gone beyond reaching.

Then her head came up, one eye amber and one eye green, and she said in a voice rough as gravel:

"Took you long enough. I had a *riddle* saved for the inquisitor and everything."

Pip hit her at a dead run, and Bramble caught the child and folded around her, fur and limbs and laughter that broke halfway through into sobbing, and Tomas stood in the doorway and let himself feel it—one full breath of it, the thing they'd come for, the impossible thing, done.

"Can you run?" he managed.

"I'm a fox," Bramble said, wiping her face with a hand that was mostly paw. "Running is the entire personality."

* * *

They left through the front gate.

Not all of them—Tomas and Sefa and the freed prisoners went out through the culvert as planned, wading the freezing water with children and elders carried between them. But Marguerite walked out the front gate, through the courtyard, past the burning records wing, past guards who dropped their pikes and pressed themselves against the walls as she passed, trailing winter, and stood in the great gateway of Ashgate Prison and looked back at the dark blocks of cells stretching away behind her.

Every cell door stood open.

That had been Pip's doing, in the end—the child had broken from the group at the last moment and run the whole block one final time, opening everything, even the empty cells, even the punishment cells, even the warden's private lockups. *Leave them open,* Pip had signed, breathless. *All of them. Let them see.*

Let the crown come back to a prison with no doors that closed and no ledgers to say who belonged inside them.

Marguerite stood in that gateway a moment longer, snow falling around her in a column that had nothing to do with weather, and then she turned and walked out into the night, and the frost behind her sealed the gate shut in a skin of ice that would take sappers a week to crack.

* * *

They scattered through the city the way water scatters—by ones and twos, in cellars and lofts and the hidden rooms of people who owed Tomas favors or feared him slightly, which amounted to the same currency. By dawn, forty-one souls who had been filed and numbered were simply *gone*, unwritten, unowned.

They regrouped at the tannery loft at noon, exhausted, singed, alive. Someone had found bread. Someone else had found wine. Bramble sat wrapped in three blankets with Pip asleep against her side, refusing to let anyone treat her wounds until she'd finished telling the story of the riddle she'd fed Corvane, three times, embellishing further with each telling.

Sefa sat apart, looking at her hands. The scars on her forearms had faded—not gone, but lighter, like old burns healing years late.

"They'll come for us now," she said quietly, when Tomas sat down beside her. "Not hunt. *Come.* With the army. With everything."

"I know."

"And you did it anyway."

Tomas looked across the loft—at Marguerite asleep in the corner with frost melting off her lashes, at Pip and Bramble, at the freed hedge-witch teaching a terrified clerk's son how to breathe again, at all of them, this nothing-family of strays and smugglers and burned-out soldiers who had chosen, last night, to walk into a fortress for each other.

"No," he said. "*We* did it anyway. That's the difference. That's the whole difference."

Sefa was quiet a moment. Then she laughed, low and rough, and poured him wine.

Outside, the unnatural winter deepened—but word was already moving through Greyhollow's streets faster than any crier could carry it. *Ashgate stands open. The ledgers are ash. They left the doors unlocked for everyone.*

And far to the south, Tomas knew without knowing how he knew, a man in a gray coat was reading a report of the night's work, and setting down his pen, and beginning—slowly, terribly—to wonder whose side the fire had really been on.
