## 18. The Siege of Greyhollow

The snow came down like ash over Greyhollow, and Marguerite stood on the walls where she had first learned to be small.

Below her, the plain that had once been market ground and turnip fields and the crooked road to the river was a sea of tents. The crown's army had arrived at dusk two days past—pikemen in ranks like a fence built to cage the horizon, siege engines draped in canvas, cavalry banners hanging heavy with frost. Cookfires burned in their thousands, orange points scattered across the white as far as she could see, and the smoke of them rose straight up into the windless dark, a forest of gray columns standing between earth and sky.

She counted fires when she could not sleep. She always lost count somewhere past six hundred.

"They've stopped digging," said Sefa beside her, arms crossed against the cold. The battle-mage's breath steamed. "The sap trenches. They were advancing all yesterday, and now they've just... stopped."

"Because they're not here to take the city." Marguerite pulled her cloak tighter. The frost that lived under her skin these days made the cold feel less like an enemy and more like a relative—one whose intentions she no longer trusted. "They're here for me."

"A herald comes this morning," Sefa said. "Tomas heard it from the wall watch. Trumpets and a banner of parley, midmorning, at the south gate."

Marguerite looked out at the fires. Somewhere among those thousands of men were farmers conscripted off winter fields, boys who had never seen magic except as smoke rising from burning houses. And somewhere behind them, in a pavilion she couldn't see, sat whatever remained of the old bloodlines—the families who had ended the Sundering War by sealing its power inside a wolf, then spent generations making sure no one remembered there had ever been a wolf at all.

Until one of them remembered too much, and put it in a newborn girl.

*It found us anyway,* Odile had said, twelve days before she died. Marguerite carried the words now the way she once carried charms: close to the skin, warmed by her own body.

"I should go down to them," she said.

"You should eat something first," Sefa said flatly. "And you're not going anywhere alone. That's not a request. That's company policy."

Despite everything, Marguerite almost smiled. *Company policy.* Tomas's phrase, adopted by all of them, worn smooth like a stone passed hand to hand. It meant: *you belong to something now. It will inconvenience you.*

* * *

She walked the walls instead of going straight to the gate, because she needed to see them—the people inside the city the army had come to besiege for her sake.

Greyhollow had swollen in the weeks since Ashgate. Refugees had come in from the river towns first, then from the tanner's quarter burnings, then in a slow hemorrhage after the prison break, when every gifted person within three days' ride understood that the lists were burned but the hunters would not stop hunting. They filled the guildhall again—her guildhall, Odile's guildhall, swept clean and re-roofed by smugglers' hands—and spilled into the temple undercrofts and the old dye-works and any cellar with a door Pip hadn't gotten to first.

They lined the ramparts now, because everyone who could walk had asked to stand watch, and Tomas had decided it was kinder to give them a post than to refuse them.

Marguerite moved among them slowly, and they parted for her without being asked to, the way water parts around a cold thing dropped into it. She hated that. She kept walking anyway.

A woman from Millbrook sat wrapped in two blankets near the south tower, a boy asleep against her shoulder. Marguerite knew her face. Everyone from the river road knew her face: she was the herbalist's sister, the one who had hidden Odile's medicine in loaves of bread.

"How is he?" Marguerite asked quietly, nodding at the boy.

"Fever broke two nights ago." The woman's eyes went to Marguerite's hands—to the faint rime that gathered on her knuckles when she stood still too long, like breath on window glass. She didn't flinch. "He sleeps better when he can hear the walls humming, he says. Says someone up here keeps the cold polite." A pause. "That's you, isn't it? Keeping the cold polite?"

Marguerite looked down at her own hands. Since Odile's unsealing, the frost came and went with her moods, and lately, on the walls at night, it spread outward along the stone in fine fern-patterns—and the killing wind coming off the plains bent around the battlements instead of through them. The refugees had noticed. Of course they'd noticed. Children left offerings of bread crusts and buttons at the stairheads, the way children do, half joke and half prayer.

"The cold isn't mine," she said. It wasn't a lie anymore, exactly. It also wasn't the whole truth, and the whole truth was a country she was still learning to walk in.

"No," the woman agreed mildly. "But it minds you. That's more than most of us ever got anything to do." She shifted the boy higher on her shoulder. "Whatever they're asking for down there—don't let them have you cheap."

Further along, near the granary bastion, she found Bramble sitting on the parapet itself, legs dangling over the sixty-foot drop, fur ruffling in the wind. Half-fox still; the shapeshifter had not managed a full human shape since Ashgate, though the fox side had grown sleeker, more settled, as if captivity had taught Bramble which skin fit best.

"You'll fall," Marguerite said.

"I'm a fox on a wall. This is my natural habitat." But Bramble slid down and fell into step beside her, paws crunching frost. For a while neither spoke. Then: "Corvane's camp is the one with the gray standard, third ring from the river. I can smell his fire from here. Cedar and old paper." Bramble's ears flicked back. "He gave me tea, you know. When he had me. Asked real questions and waited for real answers. I keep thinking about that."

"He's hunting us."

"He's *mourning* us. There's a difference, and it's worse." Bramble looked out at the enemy fires. "I told him a riddle instead of your name. He let me go anyway—or rather, we stole you back, but he didn't chase hard, did he? You've thought about that."

Marguerite had. In the long sleepless stretches she turned it over like a charm-stone: the inquisitor who released prisoners, who resigned his commission, who rode north alone through the early snow. He had arrived with the army—that was certain; watchers had seen his gray horse at the bloodlines' pavilion—but he had not been seen giving orders since. A man suspended between lives. She recognized the geography. She had lived in it herself for twelve years.

Near the north gate, she found Tomas arguing quietly with the gate captain, and Pip perched on the winch housing above them, legs swinging, whispering to the great lock bolts the way other children whispered to pets. The locks adored Pip. They had begun, in the last weeks, to adore Marguerite too—she'd caught the granary door unlatching itself hopefully whenever she passed, presenting its mechanism like a dog with a stick. The whole city was becoming porous around her. Metal remembered winter. Stone remembered winter. Everything old enough remembered the Wolf of Winter, and everything that remembered leaned toward her like plants toward a window.

*Tomas* saw her and abandoned his argument mid-sentence.

"You walked the whole wall," he said. Not an accusation. An inventory. His eyes did a quick tally: hands, color, breathing, the frost-line on her sleeves. Twelve years a smuggler had made him a man who assessed cargo, and somewhere along the river road his assessments of her had quietly changed category, though neither of them had said so out loud.

"The herald," she said.

"Midmorning. South gate. They'll demand the vessel—you know they will. The bloodlines' sorcerer sent a pigeon even before the tents went up. Polite as a wedding invitation." Tomas's jaw worked. "We've got grain for six weeks, arrows for two, and about four hundred people who can't fight but won't leave. So before you get any noble ideas about walking out that gate: the answer is no. We don't trade family."

The word landed in her chest like a warm coal. *Family.* He said it so easily now. Six months ago he'd been a river runner lying about his cargo manifests, and she'd been a passenger lying about her name, and each of them pretending very hard to believe the other.

"Tomas." She kept her voice level. "What if walking out is how I save all of it?"

"What if it isn't? What if it's how you die for nothing while they take the city anyway?" He stepped closer, lowering his voice. "I've watched you since the safehouse. You think in exits because Odile trained you to—always the back door, always the small life, never be worth noticing. But that's not strategy anymore, Marguerite. That's just fear wearing strategy's coat. So." He crossed his arms. "Tell me the plan. The actual plan. And if the plan is 'give myself to them,' I'm going to pick you up and carry you to the granary and sit on you until spring."

Behind him, Pip snickered from the winch housing.

Marguerite opened her mouth—and closed it. Because the honest answer was that she didn't have a plan. She had fragments. She had the memory of the Wolf's sacrifice playing behind her eyelids every time she slept: a woman standing alone on a frozen field, pouring herself out into a seal because *someone had to*, because the war could only end if one person held all that power and held it forever, alone, unnamed, unremembered. The oldest story. The story the bloodlines were built on. The story they wanted to force her to finish, either by taking the Wolf's power for the throne or by destroying her and the memory both.

Every path they offered ran through the same country: *one person carries it.*

Alone or erased. Crowned or buried. Those were the only endings anyone had ever written for power like hers.

* * *

The herald came at midmorning, as promised.

They assembled on the south gate's barbican—Tomas, Sefa, Bramble, the gate captain, and Marguerite, with Pip officially forbidden from attending and unofficially present in the arrow-slit shadows overhead. Below, across fifty yards of trampled snow, the parley party waited: six riders under a white banner, and among them a figure in robes the color of deep water, silver-threaded, whom the whispers named as Master Illione of the bloodline sorcerers.

Illione's voice carried impossibly well across the snow, pitched as if the air itself were carrying it by appointment.

"People of Greyhollow! You shelter a thief. Within your walls hides the vessel that stole what belongs to the crown and the old blood—a power that ended one war and, unclaimed, will begin another. We do not come against you. We come against the theft. Deliver the vessel, and the army withdraws, the Edict is lifted, and every soul within these walls is pardoned. Refuse—" the pause was gentle, terrible, a hand laid softly on a shoulder, "—and we will dig you out. Every house. Every cellar. We are patient. We have waited three hundred years."

Silence on the walls. Marguerite felt four hundred hearts listening behind her, and the army's ten thousand in front, and between the two, the snow falling without wind, without sound, as if the sky were holding its breath.

Sefa leaned to her ear. "The Edict lifted. Pardons. He's offering them *you* as the price of everyone else's freedom. That's not a siege term, that's a knife with ribbon on it."

"I know."

"And?"

"And I want to hear him say the rest." Marguerite stepped forward to the battlement's edge, and let the frost rise.

She didn't call it up, exactly. These days it was less summoning than agreement—she inclined toward the cold the way one inclines an ear toward a friend mid-story, and the cold answered. White bloomed across the barbican stones beneath her feet. Her breath came out silver. Along the wall-top, ice feathered outward in patterns like ferns, like feathers, like the branching of rivers seen from very high up, and she felt the whole garrison feel it: the hush, the awe, the animal prickle of standing near something vast.

"Master Illione." Her voice surprised her—steady, and carrying nearly as well as his. "Three hundred years ago, your ancestors took the greatest guardian this world has known and sealed her away in the dark, alone, to end a war your own hoarding began. Then you outlawed magic so no one would grow strong enough to ask questions. Then you burned hedge-witches for curing fevers." She let the silence sit. "Now you offer pardons if the city gives me up. So answer one question, and answer it truly, where everyone can hear. When you take the vessel—what happens to the girl inside?"

Below, a ripple went through the parley party. Illione's serene expression did not change, which was itself an answer.

"The girl is a wrapping," he called back, kindly, horribly. "The gift is the gift. The bloodlines will honor what it carries."

*No one answers,* Corvane had written in his report—Bramble had told them; the riddle-giver kept strange accounts. *You ask what becomes of the girl, and the room goes quiet.*

"That's what I thought." Marguerite gripped the frost-laced stone. "Then hear our answer. Greyhollow keeps its people. All of them. Even the ones you wrapped in pretty paper."

She turned from the wall before her shaking hands could show, and the garrison behind her made a sound—not a cheer, not quite; lower than that, rougher, the sound of frightened people deciding together not to be alone in it.

* * *

That night she climbed to the top of the guildhall tower, because it was the highest point left inside the walls and because Odile had loved it, and because she needed to think where the thinking wouldn't wake anyone.

The city lay below her, banked fires and shuttered windows, four hundred refugees sleeping in cellars that Pip's locks guarded jealously. Beyond the walls, ten thousand cookfires. Between them, the dark plain where the snow kept falling, falling, falling—unnatural snow, tide-snow, snow that answered the seal Odile had cracked open in a dying woman's cottage. The whole world leaning toward her like the granary door.

And she stood there and finally admitted what she had been circling for days.

There was no version of this where she gave the bloodlines the Wolf. There was no version where she let the sorcerer unmake her, erase the memory, erase herself—she had stood at the edge of that offer already, in the ruins of the guildhall, and Tomas's voice calling her own name had pulled her back, and she would not go to that edge again. And there was no version where Greyhollow held out for six weeks against ten thousand and then won some impossible battle, because battles were just the old story with more corpses: one side seizes the power, one side destroys it, and ordinary people burn underneath either way.

One person carries it. One person is destroyed by it. Those were the only endings anyone had ever written.

Unless.

Her breath stopped silver in the air, and the memory rose—not the fever-dream fragments, but clear, the way it had come since Odile's unsealing: the Wolf on the frozen field, and around the Wolf, in the final moments before the sealing, the few who had stood with her. The memory had always blurred there. She had assumed the blur was damage.

Standing in the cold with the whole siege glittering below her, Marguerite suddenly wondered if the blur was a choice. If the ritual had never required one keeper at all—if it had only ever received one keeper, because one woman had stood forward alone, because that was how guardians were made to understand themselves: solitary, sacrificial, singular. Because the bloodlines had written the ritual that way on purpose, so the power would always have a single throat to hold, a single vessel to own.

*Power distributed cannot be crowned or burned.* She didn't know yet where the thought had come from—whether from her, or from the woman in the snowfields, or from something the two of them were slowly becoming together. It didn't matter. It was true the way the frost was true.

Share it. Not surrender it, not destroy it—*share* it. Break the seal open wide instead of wide-enough, and pour the Wolf not into one vessel but into every willing hand: Sefa who understood disciplined force, Pip whom metal already trusted, Bramble who wore two skins and could surely hold two natures, Tomas who had never once let cargo—or family—be taken from him. Even, perhaps, a gray-eyed hunter camped among the enemy with cedar-smoke and old grief. Pieces of winter in a hundred chests. Let the bloodlines try to seize a power that lived everywhere and nowhere, that belonged to no throne because it belonged to everyone who chose it.

It might fail. It might kill them. It might scatter her so thin she never found her way back to her own name.

But it was a third door, in a corridor that had only ever shown her two.

Down in the enemy camp, a single light still burned past midnight—the tent with the gray standard, cedar and old paper. Marguerite looked at it a long moment.

"Bramble says you let prisoners go," she murmured to the distant light. "Let's find out what else you're willing to do differently."

The frost on the tower stones bloomed outward, patient as handwriting, and far below, in the guildhall's warm hall, Pip woke suddenly from a dream of doors opening everywhere at once, and laughed out loud in the dark.
