## 21. After the Thaw

The river broke open on a Tuesday.

Tomas heard it from the *Kestrel*'s deck at first light—a long groan under the ice, like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and then a crack that ran the length of the Greywater and echoed off the city walls. By midmorning the channel was running brown and fast, carrying slabs of winter downstream to wherever winters go when they're done.

He stood at the tiller with his hands bare for the first time in weeks and felt the thaw move through him too, some knot behind his sternum loosening that he hadn't known he'd tied.

"You're smiling," Sefa said from the cargo rail, where she sat sharpening a knife that no longer needed sharpening. "It's unsettling."

"Am not."

"You are. Your whole face has come unstuck. It's very strange after four months of you looking like a man doing arithmetic he hates."

Tomas laughed—actually laughed, out loud, on the open water—and decided not to be embarrassed about it. The cargo hold was empty of refugees for the first time since midwinter. No families huddled among the barrels, no children whispering to locks so they'd behave, no half-fox curled in the warm corner by the stove. Just freight: honest grain, honest salt fish, honest bolts of wool. Goods that were only goods.

The Edict had been suspended eleven days ago. Proclamation posted in every market square, read aloud by criers whose voices shook a little, as if they couldn't quite believe the words either. *By order of the Regency Council, the Edict of Ash is suspended pending full review. All registrations voided. All warrants recalled.* Suspended—not revoked—and every gifted soul in Greyhollow understood the difference. A suspended blade could fall again. But it hung still, and still was more than they'd had in a year, and the city moved through the gap like a held breath slowly let out.

* * *

The guildhall looked worse than he remembered and better than he feared.

They came up from the docks together in the gray-gold afternoon—Tomas hauling a satchel of tools, Marguerite carrying a basket of bread and candles, Pip riding on Bramble's shoulders because the child had learned that walking was optional when you had a shapeshifter who didn't mind being furniture. Halfway there, Pip wriggled down and ran ahead, and the great doors of the guildhall opened before she reached them, swinging wide with an eagerness that made her shriek with delight.

"It remembers me," Pip announced, scandalized and thrilled. "Doors aren't supposed to remember."

"Doors are exactly the sort of thing that's supposed to remember," Marguerite said. "That's most of what a door is."

Inside, the hall smelled of sawdust and lime and, faintly, of old magic waking—the way a cold room smells different once a fire's been lit in it, even years ago. They'd spent three weeks repairing what the raid and the siege had broken: replacing the burned rafters, re-hanging the shutters, scrubbing soot from stone that had stood clean for two hundred years before the crown's men brought their torches through. The scorch marks wouldn't come out entirely. They'd voted—Pip insisted votes were how families did things—to leave them. A reminder, Sefa said, of what the walls had survived. Or maybe, Bramble said, just because some scars belong where they are.

New names were already going up beside the founders'.

The old carved lintel over the hearth bore them still: *ODESSA THE GATEKEEPER. HALLAM BRIGHTWATER. VESS OF THE NINE WINTERS.* Names worn shallow by centuries of thumbs touching them for luck. Below, on a fresh plank of pale oak that Bramble had sourced from a smuggler friend and Sefa had squared and sanded until it gleamed, the first new name waited for its carving:

*SEFA MARUN, WHO HELD THE DOOR.*

Marguerite had argued about the epithet for two days. *Who held the door* sounded, she said, like a tavern joke. Sefa had crossed her arms and said that holding the door against six witch-hunters with nothing but raw force and her own stubborn corpse for a barricade was exactly the kind of thing a person should be remembered for, and if Marguerite wanted poetry she could carve her own plank. In the end the epithet stayed. Sefa touched it every time she passed, quick and casual, the way you touch a talisman without admitting it's one.

Today they would carve the next name. Today it was Pip's turn, and the child had rehearsed the letters on slate for a week and refused all help, which meant the carving would take an hour and look like a spider had drowned in ink, and everyone would tell her it was beautiful, and it would be.

But the afternoon light was good and the day was theirs, and before chisels there was supper.

They ate at the long table Odile used to keep—hauled from the wreckage, its burn-scarred top planed smooth. Bread and hard cheese and river fish Tomas had caught that morning with his bare hands off the bow like a show-off, because he could, because the ice was gone. Pip talked with her mouth full about the lock she'd befriended in the cellar, a stubborn old thing that had apparently been lonely. Bramble sat with ears half-raised even in human shape, listening to three conversations at once the way foxes do. Sefa told a story about the war college that ended with her getting expelled and made the whole table laugh until Marguerite wiped her eyes.

And Marguerite—

Tomas watched her across the bread and candlelight and tried to put words to what was different, because something was, and he was a man who liked to know the names of things.

She was lighter. Not smaller—that had been his fear, in the dark weeks, that sharing the Wolf would hollow her, that giving away pieces of herself would leave less of Marguerite behind. He'd lain awake nights turning it over: what if the seal breaks and what pours out isn't shared but *drained*, what if they all wake holding splinters of a guardian while the girl who carried her alone for twelve years is gone?

Instead the opposite had happened. She laughed faster now. She slept whole nights. When she moved through the guildhall she moved like someone whose body fit her, whose name answered when called. As if she'd been a coat buttoned wrong for twelve years and someone had finally undone it and started again from the bottom.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up from her plate.

"I'm assessing cargo," Tomas said. "Habit."

"Mm." She looked up then, grey eyes catching the candleflame, and smiled the small crooked smile that he had privately catalogued, over months, as the truest thing in Greyhollow. "And what's the assessment?"

"Valuable," he said. "Fragile-looking. Isn't."

* * *

Corvane was in the ash-pits when they found him.

Tomas saw him first from the guildhall steps the next morning—a tall figure in plain wool, no white-and-grey livery anymore, kneeling in the tanner's quarter where the pyres had burned. He was on his hands and knees in the mud, and around him lay neat rows of something pale.

Seeds. He was planting seeds in ash.

Tomas went down alone. Not bravery—he'd stopped mistaking himself for brave somewhere around the night he'd realized Marguerite wasn't cargo but weather—but because someone should, and because he'd carried Corvane's list in his head since Ashgate, the list the man had loosed into the wind rather than feed to the fire, and a debt like that doesn't care whether you want to owe it.

"Inquisitor," he said.

Corvane didn't startle. He finished pressing a seed into the dark earth with one thumb, precise as a man signing his name, and looked up. Up close, without the commission, he seemed both older and lighter—like a building stripped of its banners, showing the honest stone beneath.

"Not anymore," he said. "Just Corvane. Or nothing, if you prefer."

"Tomas," Tomas said, though they both knew. "What are you growing?"

"Marigolds. Feverfew. Yarrow." Corvane brushed soil from his hands. "I asked the herbalist what grows in burned ground. She said these. She also said—" his mouth did something that was almost humor, "—that I should expect poor results my first year. That ash is generous with certain minerals and cruel with others. That grief makes impatient gardeners."

"She sounds wise."

"She sounds like a woman who lost friends to those pits and is letting me dig in them anyway, which is either wisdom or a very slow form of punishment. I've stopped trying to tell the difference."

They stood a while in the cold bright morning. The pits stretched away between the tanneries, a dozen long scars of blackened earth, and along their edges Corvane had already turned furrows—miles of them, by the look, turned by hand, by a man who used to command the crown's hunters and now owned a spade and a bag of seed.

"The bloodlines haven't given up," Tomas said finally. It wasn't a question and Corvane didn't treat it as one.

"No. A suspended Edict is a door left ajar; they'll push it again when they think the wind's right. And there are those in court who wanted the Wolf for the throne and don't much care that the Wolf now lives in—" he paused, and Tomas braced for *the girl*, for *the vessel*, for the old language— "in all of you. In a smuggler, a disgraced battle-mage, a child, a fox, and a charm-seller." Something flickered across his face that might have been wonder wearing a stern man's clothes. "Power distributed cannot be crowned. One of them said that to me, back when I was useful. He meant it as a warning. I've come to think of it as instructions."

"You could have run," Tomas said. "South, past the border. No one hunts a man with no rank."

"Yes," Corvane agreed mildly, and pressed another seed into the earth. "I considered it at length."

Tomas looked at the furrows, the seeds, the ruined ground being tended row by patient row, and understood that he was looking at the answer.

"Well," he said. "If you need help carrying water, the guildhall's got buckets."

Corvane looked up sharply. Whatever he'd expected—an accusation, a knife, at best a cold nod—it clearly hadn't been plumbing. For a moment the former chief hunter of Greyhollow looked like a man handed something fragile in a crowd, afraid to grip it.

"That would be," he said carefully, "acceptable."

Tomas nodded and walked back up the lane, and didn't look around, because some things can't bear being watched while they take root.

* * *

Market day came round again, as market days do, indifferent to wars and edicts and ancient guardians, concerned mainly with onions.

Tomas had errands and took them slowly, on purpose. The Greyhollow market had shaken off its terror like a dog shakes off rain: stalls reopened, voices lifted, the whole square running loud and ordinary. Here and there the new world showed itself in small ways. An apothecary openly labeling a tincture *strengthened*. A locksmith with a sign reading *enchanted fittings, fair prices*. Two old women arguing cheerfully over a luck-knot stall, and nobody crossing the street to avoid them, and nobody watching from corners with notebooks.

Marguerite's stall stood where it had always stood, third from the fountain, beneath the leaning awning.

Tomas bought nothing for ten minutes. He leaned against the fountain with a cup of cider and watched her work, because watching Marguerite work had become one of his quiet pleasures and he saw no reason to pretend otherwise.

She sold fever-charms to a mother with a sniffling toddler. Luck-knots to the fishmonger, who bought one every market day and always had mediocre luck and always came back. A warming-stone to the cobbler for his rheumatic hands. Small magics. Harmless ones—or rather, ones that had never needed to be harmless, that she had made small on purpose for twelve years, folding herself down and down to fit inside the space the law allowed.

Except now she didn't fold.

When the mother's toddler cried harder, Marguerite hummed a note under her breath—just a note, a thread of working—and the child hiccupped, giggled, and went calm as a pond, and Marguerite did it in the open, in daylight, in front of six witnesses, and the fishmonger didn't even stop haggling.

A young woman Tomas didn't know approached the stall afterward, twisting her cap in her hands, and spoke too low for him to catch. Marguerite listened, nodded, glanced across the square—and Tomas followed her glance to a second stall, where Sefa sat selling warding-sigils with the grim efficiency of a woman who'd found retail surprisingly satisfying, and beyond her, where Bramble charmed children by letting his ears go foxy when their parents weren't looking.

"They each hold a piece," Marguerite had explained once, on a night when the fire was low and the question had finally worked its way out of Tomas. "Not copies. Pieces. Sefa carries the Wolf's endurance—she says her old wounds ache less. Pip has its... delight, I suppose, its wildness. Bramble holds the loneliness, which sounds cruel, but he says loneliness held in company stops being a wound and becomes a compass. And you—" she'd looked at him then with an expression he'd been turning over ever since, "—you hold its patience. Its willingness to wait out a winter."

"And you?" he'd asked. "What piece did you keep?"

She'd smiled and spread her empty hands. "Me. I kept me. That was the whole point."

Now she caught him watching and raised an eyebrow, and he pushed off the fountain and ambled over, affecting the manner of a customer.

"Charms, is it? Heard the seller's dangerous. Carries an ancient guardian spirit or some such."

"Oh, terribly dangerous." She held up a luck-knot, red cord and river-glass, identical to ten thousand she'd sold before any of this began. "Though I'm told the guardian's been distributed. Something about a smuggling operation with an unusually sentimental cargo manifest."

"Best manifests are sentimental," Tomas said. He paid her—overpaid her, and she knew it, and let him, which was its own kind of intimacy—and closed her fingers around the knot before handing it back instead of taking it. "For you. You've had a year of everyone else needing luck."

She looked at the little charm in her palm for longer than such a small thing deserved. Then she tied it around her wrist, snug, and said, quietly, "Thank you, Tomas."

Around them the market roared its ordinary roar. Somewhere a fiddler started up. The thaw-water chuckled in the fountain, and the sun came through the parting clouds and lay across the square like a hand laid flat, and for a long unguarded moment Tomas simply stood in all of it, letting it happen to him.

* * *

Evening found them all back at the guildhall, because evenings did now, without anyone decreeing it.

Pip's name had been carved that afternoon—*PIP LANTERN, WHO OPENS WHAT IS SHUT*—in letters that wandered downhill and doubled back on themselves, and which Pip had inspected with the severity of a master appraiser before pronouncing them "good enough forever." Supper was stew and the last of the winter apples. Bramble fell asleep sitting up with his chin on Sefa's shoulder, ears twitching at dreams. Corvane came at dusk, awkward as a cat at a dog party, carrying seed catalogs, and Marguerite poured him wine without being asked, and he sat.

Later, when the others drifted to their beds in the rebuilt dormers, Tomas found Marguerite on the guildhall steps, wrapped in a blanket, watching the sky.

He sat beside her. Neither spoke for a while. The city below them was learning to be itself again—lamplight spreading window to window, a watchman's call that sounded bored instead of frightened, the smell of woodsmoke that was just woodsmoke, cooking, home.

"Winter will come again," Marguerite said at last. "The real kind, and the other kind. The bloodlines, the fear, whatever comes after suspension. It won't stay this gentle."

"No," Tomas agreed. "Probably not."

"But we held a piece of it. Of the worst thing. We held it *together*." She turned the luck-knot on her wrist. "Twelve years I thought the choice was hide or burn. Smallness or the pyre. And it turns out there was a door the whole time, and it wasn't even locked—it just needed more than one pair of hands."

"Pip could've told you that," Tomas said. "Locks are lonely. She's very insistent on the point."

Marguerite laughed, low, and pulled the blanket wider, and after a moment's deliberation pulled half of it over his shoulders too, because the night was cold and hoarding warmth was exactly the old mistake they'd stopped making.

Above the rooftops, the first stars were out. And among them, low on the northern horizon where the snowfields began, Tomas noticed something he'd have missed a year ago—a faint green shimmer, like light under ice, breathing slowly against the dark.

Marguerite saw it too. He felt her go still beside him, felt her recognition move through the quiet like a current.

"The north is waking," she said softly. Not afraid. Curious. "Something old, past the snowfields. The Wolf knew it once—I can feel the edges of the knowing, like a word on the tip of the tongue." She glanced at him, and her eyes were bright and steady and entirely her own. "Midwinter's coming again next year. We should decide, all of us, what we do about the things that remember us."

Tomas looked at the green light breathing on the horizon, and at the woman beside him who was herself multiplied, and at the lit windows of the guildhall behind them where his strange family slept.

"Well," he said. "We've got a year. And we've got each other."

"That," said Marguerite, "has historically been enough."
