## 22. The Names on the Door

The chisel fit badly in Marguerite's hand.

She had held it for the better part of an hour, turning it over in the firelight of the guildhall's great room while the others argued—affectionately, endlessly—about everything except the thing they had actually come to do. Pip wanted her name carved highest, "because locks opened first." Sefa maintained that rank should be determined by who had burned the most ledgers, which was Sefa. Bramble, half-fox and wholly contrary, insisted that names should be written backwards so that only foxes could read them from the street.

Tomas said nothing. He sat by the window with his mended hands folded, watching the snow come down over Greyhollow in long, unhurried sheets—the first midwinter snowfall in a year that felt like weather instead of omen. Every few minutes he glanced at Marguerite, and every few minutes she pretended not to notice, because if she met his eyes she would have to admit that her hands were shaking.

Not from cold. Not anymore. She hadn't been cold since the thaw, since the night she had opened the seal and let the Wolf of Winter pour out of her alone-ness and into everyone willing to hold a piece. Since then, winter had been only winter: beautiful, brutal, ordinary. She still woke some nights with frost blooming on the windowsill in spirals, but when she pressed her palm to it, it melted like any other frost, made by any other woman.

"Marguerite." Pip tugged her sleeve. The child was eleven now, or claimed to be; she had been claiming to be eleven for two years, and no one had the heart to audit her. "You're doing the thing where you stare at a tool like it's going to bite you."

"It might," Marguerite said. "Everything worth carving does."

Across the room, Sefa snorted. The battle-mage stood with her arms crossed before the great stone lintel above the guildhall door, the one that had survived fire and siege and twelve years of official forgetting. The founders' names were still cut deep into it, though the mortar around them had gone soft as old bread: **ODILE VAUNTE** at the top, then the nine others, each one a person the Edict had tried to erase and the city had quietly refused to forget. Below them, a band of blank stone waited, pale and clean where Bramble had spent three days with a wire brush, muttering curses in fox-tongue, scrubbing away the ash-pit grime until the granite remembered what it was.

"The stone's ready," Sefa said. "It's the carver who isn't."

"I'm ready."

"You've been ready for an hour. You're also still holding the chisel upside down."

Marguerite looked down. She was.

Laughter went around the room—not loud, but easy, the kind of laughter that had taken a year to grow and would take more than an edict to uproot. Tomas crossed to her, took the chisel gently from her fingers, turned it, and gave it back. His hands lingered near hers for a moment longer than the task required. In the year since the siege, since the snowfall that fell on the crown's army like a blessing and sent ten thousand men home wondering what exactly they had been ordered to fight, he had learned to read her silences the way sailors read water. This one, she knew he knew, was not fear.

It was grief, wearing its festival clothes.

* * *

They had buried Odile in spring, under the rowan behind the herbalist's cottage, with a luck-knot tied around the marker stone because Marguerite could not bring herself to leave the grave uncharmed even knowing charms were legal again. Odile had died as she had lived: giving away something precious at the last possible moment, and calling it a lesson. *You were never the vessel, child,* she had whispered, in the end, with frost already climbing her lips. *Vessels are empty. You were always full. We were just too frightened to look.*

Twelve years of hiding. Twelve years of smallness, of fever-charms and luck-knots sold to neighbors who half-suspected and never asked, of a life built deliberately small enough to slip through the world's fingers. And in the end it had all come anyway—the hunters, the fires, the war that lived in every heart that hoarded power. Marguerite had spent the first weeks after the thaw angry about those lost years. Then she had spent the next weeks understanding them. Fear was not cowardice. Fear was what love looked like when it had nowhere safe to go.

Odile had loved her the only way the times allowed: by burying the truth so deep inside a newborn daughter that even the bloodlines' sorcerers couldn't find it. It had nearly destroyed them both. It had also, against every odd, kept her alive long enough to choose otherwise.

Now the choice belonged to everyone.

That was the part that still made her hands shake. Not the power—power had turned out to be almost beside the point, once it stopped being a secret. But the sharing. On the night of the siege, standing in the ruined guildhall with the sorcerer's oblivion offered like mercy and Tomas's voice calling her back to her own name, she had done the thing no guardian in four hundred years had thought to do. She had opened the seal and asked, not commanded, *asked*: *Who will hold this with me?*

Sefa had stepped forward first, because Sefa never did anything second. Then Pip, who understood better than anyone that heavy things carried together weigh less than light things carried alone. Then Bramble, who said, "I've been more than one creature my whole life; what's one more," and meant it as a joke and wasn't. Then Tomas, wordlessly, the way he did everything that mattered. And last, arriving ash-smeared and half-dead from his ride north, having burned his commission and released his prisoners' list to the wind: Inquisitor Corvane, who knelt in the snow among the people he had hunted and said, "I knew your mother. Let me carry what I helped her hide."

Even now, a year later, Marguerite could feel them—the pieces of the Wolf distributed like bread at a table, warm in eight separate chests. Some nights she dreamed in snowfields, but the voice in the dream was hers and Sefa's and Pip's braided together, and the wolf that ran beside her was made of winter and company both. Power distributed could not be crowned. Could not be burned. Could not be buried in one girl's bones until the burial killed her.

She had learned that lesson at the edge of oblivion. She intended to carve it into stone.

* * *

Midwinter night came up silver.

By tradition—or by the new tradition, which was barely a year old and already treated as ancient—the city lit its lamps at dusk and left them burning till dawn, in defiance of the dark. Greyhollow had always needed its small rebellions. Now it had larger ones: magic worked openly in doorways, hedge-healers advertising fever-cures with hand-painted signs, a registered charm-maker's stall back on market day with a line out into the square. The Edict of Ash hung suspended, neither repealed nor enforced, a sword dangling over the kingdom by a thread the court dared not name. The old bloodlines had retreated to their estates to lick their wounds and count what remained of their claim on the Wolf's power, which was nothing, because there was no Wolf left to claim—only people, plural, unremarkable, impossible to crown or burn.

The guildhall had been rebuilt slowly, by hand, by anyone who came. Its walls were crooked in places where different crews had disagreed about plumb lines. Its roof leaked over the east corner and Bramble refused to fix it, claiming the drip "kept the moss honest." Its great room smelled always of woodsmoke and Sefa's terrible stew and Pip's lock-oil. Its door was new oak, iron-banded, and above it, set into the original lintel like a heart returned to a body, the founders' stone.

At moonrise they gathered in the snow outside it. Word had gone round, the way word does, and half the neighborhood came: the tanner's widow who bought fever-charms; the herbalist whose cottage had hidden Odile; Corvane, standing slightly apart as he always did, planting—of all things—a row of rowan saplings along the south wall earlier that day, one for each of the ash-pits he had ordered dug in another life. He had dirt under his fingernails and looked, Marguerite thought, more at peace than she had ever seen him. Grief, weaponized into duty, had finally been disarmed into gardening.

"All right," Tomas said quietly, and handed her the mallet.

The crowd hushed. Snow ticked softly against the lanterns. Marguerite set the chisel's edge against the blank stone below ODILE VAUNTE and the nine founders, below the space where Odile's own second carving would go—because they had voted, weeks ago, after much argument and one incident involving Pip and a stolen chisel, that Odile belonged on both lists: founder and keeper, the woman who hid the truth and the woman who gave it back.

She carved TOMAS first, because he had carried her when she was cargo and stayed when she was storm. Then SEFA, who held doorways. Then PIP, who spoke to locks and to loneliness. Then BRAMBLE, who wrote their own name with a flourish that took twice the space and included, at Marguerite's raised eyebrow, a small defiant fox. Then CORVANE, and the crowd murmured at that, and Corvane bowed his head as the letters went in, and someone—the tanner's widow, of all people—said loudly, "He plants trees now. Let him stay," and the murmur became something gentler.

Then ODA VAUNTE, and Marguerite's eyes blurred, and Tomas's hand settled warm between her shoulder blades until she could see again.

And then the stone was blank below all of them, waiting, and the shaking came back into her hands, and this time she let it.

"There's one more," she said. Her voice carried further than she expected; it always did now, winter listening politely whenever she spoke. "The founders' guild had a rule. Every keeper added their name. But keepers before us carried alone, and names carved alone are just markers on graves. So we changed the rule."

Sefa stepped up beside her. Pip climbed onto a crate. Bramble flowed up the wall and perched on the lintel, tail curling, ears forward. Tomas put his hand flat against the stone beside hers, and beneath her palm she felt the faint familiar hum of him holding his piece of the Wolf, steady as a hearthstone.

"We're not writing our names," Marguerite told the crowd, to the snow, to the city, to whatever watched from the dark between stars. "We're writing *its* name. The one that was buried in one person for four hundred years. Tonight it belongs to whoever wants to remember it. That's all remembering ever had to be—a thing held in common."

She set the chisel below the last letter and carved, slow and sure, the true name of the Wolf of Winter.

It was not a grand name. That was the joke of it, the ache of it, the mercy of it. Four centuries of dread and legend, and the name was short and human and old: **VESNA**. A woman's name. A person, once, lonely as any of them, who had walked into a sealing ritual expecting to be erased alone and had instead been passed, hand to hand across generations, waiting for someone foolish enough to share her.

As the last stroke fell, the snow stopped.

Not unnaturally—not the wrong early snow of the long winter, nothing that made the blood run cold. It simply paused, the way a crowd pauses, the way a room pauses when a name is spoken that everyone suddenly recognizes. The lanterns burned straight. Somewhere in the dark, Marguerite felt eight hearts hold the same breath, and felt the breath release, and felt—everywhere at once, in her chest and in Tomas's and in the child's and in the fox's and even, faintly, in the gardener's—something vast and patient finally, finally set down its burden.

Then the snow resumed, soft as a blessing, and the crowd exhaled all together, and Pip shouted, "Do mine next! Carve PIP IS ELEVEN!" and the moment broke open into noise and warmth and Sefa declaring the stew was burning, which it was, which it always was.

* * *

Later, when the lanterns had burned low and the neighbors had gone home and the others were arguing sleepily over cards in the great room, Marguerite stood alone before the door and read the stone by lamplight.

Founders above. Keepers and company below. And beneath them all, small and true:

VESNA — HELD IN COMMON

Frost crept up the lintel in gentle spirals, and melted when she touched it, and she laughed at that—at herself, at the years, at the girl who had once believed safety was a life built small enough to hide. Safety was this: a crooked hall and a leaky roof and eight people carrying one name, and a city learning, lamp by lamp, that power hoarded is a wound and power shared is a hearth.

Behind her, the door creaked. Tomas leaned in the frame, card-hand still fanned absently in one fist, watching her watch the stone.

"They'll ask you to speak tomorrow," he said. "The council. About reopening the guild proper. Taking apprentices."

"I know."

"Will you?"

Marguerite looked at the names a moment longer—her family's names, her own name, the old wolf's name resting easy among them—and smiled.

"Yes," she said. "But they should know the first lesson won't be magic. It'll be this door. Everything else is just remembering how to share."

Out past the rowan saplings, the snow went on falling over Greyhollow, and it was only snow, and it was enough.
