## 1. The Charm-Seller's Quiet

There are three rules to selling charms in Vell, and I'd learned all of them the hard way, which is to say: expensively.

One — never promise a miracle. Miracles attract attention, and attention in Vell is a disease you catch from other people's eyes.

Two — never let the charm actually work too well. A luck-charm that wins someone a fortune gets talked about. Talk gets repeated. Repetition reaches ears that don't buy charms; they collect the people who make them.

Three — never, under any circumstance, hum.

I was breaking rule three when the girl came to my stall.

It wasn't really humming. It was more like the memory of humming, a vibration that lived in the bones of my jaw and only escaped on quiet mornings when the market was still waking up and my guard was still asleep somewhere behind my ribs. Swordspeech, my mother had called it, back when she called it anything at all. The language that lives in steel and answers when spoken to. Twelve years since I'd said a word of it aloud, and it still sat under my skin like a cat that hadn't decided whether it lived here or was merely waiting for me to die so it could have the chair.

"Excuse me." The girl was maybe ten, maybe eleven, wearing her brother's coat over her own dress because her brother presumably no longer needed his. Her eyes were red-rimmed in the way eyes get when crying has been done privately, in advance, so as not to inconvenience anyone. "Are you the charm woman?"

"I'm a seller of curiosities," I said. "Charms is a strong word."

"Mama says yours work."

"Mama," I said, "is generous."

She put both hands on the edge of my table, which was against the rules of my table, and looked up at me with the particular gravity children reserve for asking adults to lie to them convincingly. Behind her, the market was assembling itself out of fog and commerce — fishwives arguing with fishmongers, a tinker setting out his pans with the clangor of a man who hated pans, the baker's boy delivering loaves with the grim efficiency of someone paid by the loaf and not by the smile.

"My da's going down into the mines tomorrow," she said. "Third shift. The one where people don't come back up."

I set down the copper ring I'd been polishing. Third shift. Everyone in Vell knew about third shift. The mine had been running wrong for two years — seams collapsing, lamps guttering in air that should have been good, men coming up gray-faced and saying the dark down there felt *crowded*. The company said gas. The company always said gas. Gas was a tidy explanation for things that didn't want explaining.

"He believes in the deep ones?" I asked carefully. Belief was a currency in Vell, and like all currency, it could be taxed by the wrong authorities.

"No." She shook her head hard. "He laughs at them. But Mama made him take a saint's medal anyway, and he laughed at that too, and then last night I heard him tell Mama he'd take *anything*, even a heathen charm, if it meant—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again in a smaller voice. "If it meant coming home."

Well. There it was. The oldest sales pitch in the world isn't made by the seller at all. It's made by fear, and fear never once had to offer a discount.

I looked at the girl for a long moment, and I looked at the little row of charms laid out on velvet gone gray with handling — knotted threads, iron nails wrapped in red wool, river stones drilled through with holes I swore I'd drilled naturally, and the small tin tokens stamped with nonsense sigils that were nonsense right up until they weren't.

Here is what nobody tells you about selling fake magic: some of it isn't fake. Here is the second thing nobody tells you: you can't choose which parts are which.

The knots I tied were just knots. But I tied them with a rhythm, and rhythms are a language, and languages are doors. The words I murmured over the river stones were market-vendor patter, pure theater — but sound carries differently through stone than through air, and something in the stone listened the way stone does, patiently, the way it has listened since before we invented impatience. And underneath all of it, under the patter and the knots and the honest fraud of it all, the swordspeech purred in my jaw like a banked fire, and every morning I told it the same thing I'd told it for twelve years:

*Not today.*

Today it almost didn't listen.

Because here's the truth I kept folded up smaller than any charm in my inventory: the girl's father was going to die without help. Not might. Was. The mine was sour with old bound magic, magic the crown had squeezed out of the gifted generations ago and piped underground like sewage, and that kind of trapped power curdles. It makes the dark crowded. And there was exactly one thing I carried that could settle it — a word, three syllables, spoken to the iron in the rock itself — and speaking it would light me up like a festival lantern in a city that had spent thirty years teaching itself what happened to lanterns.

So instead I picked up a knot-charm. Red wool, seven turns, tied at dawn while reciting the prices of fish.

"This one," I said. "For miners. You tie it around his left wrist, not the right, and he doesn't take it off, and he doesn't tell anyone what's in it, because charms that get bragged about lose their temper."

"What's in it?"

"Nothing that will interest a witchfinder," I said, which was true, and also the closest thing to a prayer I'd said in years.

"How much?"

"For you? Two coppers."

She had five clutched in her fist, I could see, sweaty and counted in advance, and I watched her do the arithmetic of a child who has already learned that money is love made portable. She put four on the table.

"It's two," I said.

"The extra's so it works harder."

Gods preserve us from the logic of children, which is airtight and insane. I took the two coppers, pushed the other two back, and watched her face decide whether to be offended or grateful. Grateful won, barely.

"Tell your father," I added, as she turned away, "that the third shift is long but it ends. Everything ends. That's most of what charms are, when you get down to it. Something ending sooner than it would have otherwise."

She nodded solemnly, having understood none of it, and ran off into the fog with her brother's coat flapping, and I sat back down at my table of harmless nothing and told the fire under my skin *not today* again, quieter this time, and pretended that was the same as being safe.

* * *

The procession came through the market an hour after full light, which was late for witchfinders, and lateness, I learned later, meant they'd already been somewhere else that morning.

You heard them before you saw them. Boots, mostly. Witchfinders marched in step, all of them, in a city where nobody else had ever managed to agree on anything, and the sound of forty boots hitting cobblestones in unison travels through the ground and up through the soles of your feet and settles somewhere behind your stomach like a swallowed stone. Then the white-and-gray cloaks came around the corner of the chandlery, and the market did its trick — the one I'd watched a thousand times and never gotten used to. Every conversation bent smoothly sideways. Every eye found a fascinating patch of empty air. The whole market became a congregation of the deeply, aggressively innocent.

I joined them. I'm not proud of how good I'd gotten at it.

They were escorting a cart. On the cart was an old woman.

I knew her. That's the part I keep circling like a tongue finding a cracked tooth. Maren, who sold dried herbs two stalls down and three rows over, whose rosemary was famous within a radius of perhaps forty feet and unknown beyond it, who had once haggled me down on a bundle of sage and then slipped an extra sprig in anyway because she said my cough sounded terrible and she wasn't having my death on her conscience. Maren, who was eighty if she was a day, being walked up onto the cart by two witchfinders with the gentle firmness of men handling furniture they'd been instructed not to scratch.

Her wrists were bound. Her chin was up. She was looking straight ahead, past all of us, past the bent conversations and the fascinating patches of air, and her lips were moving. Praying, maybe. Or counting. Or doing what I did every morning, telling some stubborn thing inside her *not today*.

The captain of the procession stopped at the mouth of the market row and read from a paper. His voice had the flat, rehearsed quality of a man who had read this exact sentence enough times that it had worn smooth as a river pebble.

"By order of the Crown of Vell: the woman Maren of the Salt Row stands accused of the crime of gift. Evidence having been presented, testimony taken, and judgment rendered, she is remanded to the pyre at evening bell. Citizens are reminded that concealment of the gifted is itself a crime. Citizens are reminded that vigilance is a form of loyalty."

Citizens are reminded. We were reminded constantly. It was practically the city motto. *Vell: Reminded Since Forever.*

Maren's herb stall sat abandoned, a basket of rosemary toppled, the fragrant bundles scattered across the cobbles like something spilled. One of the witchfinders' auxiliaries — a local man, hired flesh, the kind of neighbor who makes himself useful to wolves because the alternative is being eaten — kicked the basket aside and began nailing a notice to the stall's post. ACCONDEMNED, the notices said, in letters tall enough to read from a distance, because shame should scale.

And here is the thing I did, the thing I need to write down plainly so that I can't later soften it into something else: I sold a charm.

A man came to my table while the cart was still in sight — a cooper, big hands, the smell of oak shavings — wanting a knot-charm for his daughter's wedding, and I sold it to him. I smiled. I quoted the price. I tied the knot with the rhythm that was almost a language, and I said the patter that was almost a prayer, and the whole time, at the edge of everything, the boots were marching Maren of the Salt Row out of the market she'd worked for fifty years, and the swordspeech was rising in my throat like floodwater, pressing against my teeth, offering me — it always offered, that's the obscenity of it, it *offered* — a word. Just one word. Spoken to the iron shackles on her wrists, it would open them like a hand unclenching. Spoken to the axle of the cart, it would seize it solid. Spoken loudly enough, to all the steel in the square at once, it would turn forty swords into forty rattling snakes and give the whole crowd something to remember besides their own silence.

Twelve years ago I would have needed someone to talk me out of it.

Now I needed someone to talk me into it, and everyone nearby had developed a sudden fascination with wedding knots and the price of fish, and so the word stayed where it lived, under my skin, and I tied the cooper's knot, and I took his silver, and I said *blessings on the house*, and the cart rolled out of hearing, and the market exhaled and resumed being a market, because markets are honest that way — grief is bad for trade, and trade must go on, and the dead are dead whether you watch them go or not.

Except I did watch. That's the joke of it. Hiding isn't the same as not seeing. I've spent twelve years perfecting the art of seeing absolutely everything while appearing to see nothing, and I can report that it works flawlessly and costs exactly what you'd expect.

* * *

Evening bell came, and with it the smoke.

Vell's pyre-ground was on the hill above the Tanner's Quarter, close enough that on burn nights the wind carried the smell across half the city, and far enough that everyone could pretend the smell was tallow, or a chimney, or dinner gone wrong. Nobody pretended well. We all just agreed to pretend badly together, which is the civic religion of Vell in its purest form.

I closed my shutters. I ate bread and hard cheese standing up, because sitting down to eat felt like admitting I planned to stay anywhere. I counted my coppers. I re-inked the false ledger that showed my inventory as buttons and ribbon, because buttons and ribbon hang no one.

And then I sat by the window with the shutter cracked one inch — one inch, the width of self-forgiveness — and watched the glow on the hill, orange and steady, like a second, worse sunset.

Somewhere in my chest, under twelve years of careful silence, the swordspeech shifted. Not humming now. Not purring. Just present, the way a scar is present, the way a debt is present. It didn't say anything. It never said anything unless I spoke first. That was the bargain my mother had bought with whatever it cost her, kneeling in a cold room making me swear an oath I wasn't old enough to understand: the gift would sleep, and I would live, and living would be enough.

Living was enough. I'd built a whole life on the foundation of enough. Enough coppers. Enough customers. Enough inches of shutter to watch the world through.

But tonight, watching the hill burn an old woman who had once given me free rosemary, enough started to feel less like a floor and more like a lid.

Because here's what I couldn't stop calculating, with the merchant's cold arithmetic I'd sharpened over twelve years: Maren had been careful. Maren had been *careful*. Fifty years of careful, dried herbs and haggling and a stall so boring it was practically camouflage, and they'd come for her anyway. Which meant careful wasn't a shield. It was just a slower way of being found. Which meant the difference between Maren and me wasn't safety. It was only time, and the quality of our pretending.

And beneath that thought, quieter, in the place I didn't look: *you could have done something.* Not the big something — not forty swords turned to snakes, I wasn't that brave anymore, maybe I'd never been. But a word to the cart axle. A stumble in the road. A delay, an hour, a chance. Small magics. Deniable magics. The kind of thing that looks like luck.

That's what I sold other people, after all. Luck. Little mercies dressed as commerce.

Just never for anyone who was already on the cart.

The glow on the hill faded toward embers, and I closed the shutter fully, and I told the thing under my skin *not today* one final time, and for the first time in twelve years, I heard how it sounded. Not like resolve. Like a door being locked from the inside, by someone alone, in a house that was getting smaller every year.

I went to bed. Sleep took its time arriving, and when it did, it brought the mine with it, and the crowded dark, and a girl's voice saying *the extra's so it works harder,* and I woke twice before morning.

Morning came gray and cold, and I opened my shutters and set out my charms and prepared to be nobody for another day.

Which is when the knocking started — not a customer's knock, casual and greedy, but a heavy, failing knock, a fist that hit the wood twice and slid down it, and when I opened the door at dawn with my heart already climbing my throat, there was a man crumpled against my threshold in armor gone brown with old blood, and burned into the back of his clenched right hand, glowing faintly, unmistakably, was a nine-pointed star.

Every child in Vell knew that star. It was on the forbidden lists. It was in the cautionary rhymes.

It was the sigil of the Ninefold Court, which had been ash for thirty years —

— and he was looking directly at me, and he knew.
