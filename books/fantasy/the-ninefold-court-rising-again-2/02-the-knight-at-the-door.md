## 2. The Knight at the Door

The trouble with luck-charms is that they only work when nobody believes in them.

I've built twelve years on that principle. A charm is a knot of string and a copper bead and whatever dried flower the market had cheap that week, and it works because people who carry a little hope walk a little taller and step out of the way of carts. The magic, if you can call it that—and I do not, not where anyone can hear—is all in the customer. Which is why my charms are honest. They cost two coppers and deliver exactly nothing except courage.

The swordspeech under my skin delivers considerably more, which is why it stays under my skin.

I was restocking the shelf of small protections—against envy, against bad roads, against mothers-in-law, priced accordingly—when the bell over my door rang wrong. Not the bright double-note of a customer. A single dull clang, like something falling against it.

He came through sideways, one shoulder first, the way men do when their bodies have stopped taking orders. Grey cloak gone dark at the hem with something that wasn't rain. He got three steps into my shop, looked at me with eyes the color of wet slate, and said, "You're her daughter," in the tone other people use for *thank the gods*, and then his legs quit.

I caught him—or caught enough of him to steer the fall away from my display of anti-grief charms, which would have been an omen too far even for Vell's market tolerance. He was heavy in the way of armor worn so long it becomes part of a man. Under the cloak: a breastplate scored by three deep lines, old work, and beneath that, blood coming from somewhere I didn't want to look.

"Shop's closed," I told him, mostly on principle, and bolted the door.

His right hand was clenched like he was holding something invisible. When I pried the fingers open—he let me, after a moment of resistance that was more courtesy than strength—I saw the sigil burned into his palm. Nine interlocking rings around a central point, seared into the skin in fine silver-white lines, the kind of scar that isn't made by iron but by oath.

I knew what it was the way you know a face in a crowd you've spent your life avoiding. Everyone in Vell knew the shape, though officially we'd been taught it as the mark of traitors, the emblem of the Ninefold Court that tried to make itself king above the king, thirty years ago. Children drew it in the dust to dare each other. You scrubbed it off fast.

Nobody carried it burned into their hand unless they wanted it there forever.

"No," I said, to him, to it, to the morning generally. "Whatever this is, no."

"You're her daughter," he said again, and coughed, and the cough had blood in it. "Oscarina."

The name went through me like a blade finding the gap in armor. Nobody had said that name aloud in twelve years. My mother had died with it behind her teeth. I had paid good money to a records clerk to make it vanish from every ledger in the city, and here was a stranger bleeding on my floor handing it back to me like change.

"It's Oscar," I said. "Just Oscar. And you've got the wrong shop. Charms and sundries. Luck, not—not whatever that is." I nodded at his hand as if the sigil were a spilled jar of something unpleasant.

"The Court fell," Aldric—I learned his name later; he gave it in pieces between coughs, the way men give up things they no longer need—"the Court fell because there was no heir left to speak for it. Nine seats. Nine voices. The king cut them down one by one, and none of them thought to—" another cough —"none of them thought anyone would need to come after. Pride. We were proud."

"We?"

"I served the third seat. The Voice of Rivers." His eyes found mine and held, and there was something in them worse than pain: certainty. "Your mother was the ninth. The last voice. Before she took the Last Oath and went silent, she spoke a naming. I heard it. I was sworn to carry it until—" his hand tightened around mine, grip surprisingly strong, "—until I could spend it."

"Spend it on someone else," I suggested. "There's a whole city out there. Try the temple district. Try anyone whose floor hasn't got blood on it yet."

The bell rang again. Three sharp notes, quick, the rhythm I'd known since childhood and dreaded since adolescence: the knock-code of the watch, or worse.

Through my shop window, I saw them come around the corner from Tanner's Row. Two witchfinders in grey-and-crimson, hoods back, faces bare—the new fashion, faces shown so the city could see that witchfinders were just people, neighbors really, nothing to fear unless you were something to fear. Behind them, four watchmen with net-poles and irons. And behind them, trailing at a respectful distance, the procession had picked up followers, because in Vell a burning was the best entertainment going and everyone liked to be able to say afterward that they'd seen it start.

They weren't coming for me. They couldn't know about me. They were sweeping the market street door to door because a witchfinder had dreamed a dream, or read a chicken's entrails, or simply woken up hungry, and now the street would be combed like hair before a hanging.

Ser Aldric saw my face and understood everything at once, the way dying men are permitted one clarity.

"They'll find me," he said. "And then they'll find what I carry."

"What do you carry?"

"Nine names." He pressed his burned hand flat against my chest, over the sternum, and I felt the sigil's heat through my apron like a coal wrapped in cloth. "And yours among them. By the authority of the third seat and the rite of succession, I name you—"

"Don't." The word came out of me with force I hadn't authorized. "Don't you dare—"

"—heir to the Ninefold Court," he finished, "in the sight of the nine and everything that remembers them. Rise when your Court calls you."

The heat sank in. I swear to you it did—it went through skin and bone and settled somewhere behind my navel like a swallowed ember, and every sword-blade within earshot—which in a market street means every nail, every hinge, every knife in every butcher's stall—hummed once, softly, a chord I hadn't heard since my mother's hands covered my ears.

Outside, the knocking started. Proper knocking now, fist on wood. "Open in the name of the crown!"

* * *

Here is what I want to explain about the next ninety seconds, because afterward I heard versions of it told in six taverns and all of them were wrong.

I did not decide anything. Decision implies options weighed, a mind choosing among paths. What happened was simpler and more humiliating than decision: the knocking came, the door shook, and twelve years of careful silence cracked straight down the middle because a dying man had put a coal in my chest and the swordspeech rose to meet it like water rising to its own level.

The lock on my door was iron. Iron listens to me. It has always listened to me, the way some dogs listen only to one voice in a household, and I have spent twelve years never saying a word to it above a whisper.

I said a word to it.

Not aloud. That was the horror and the relief both—that the speech doesn't require breath, only intent shaped the way blades are shaped, and the iron heard me anyway, and the lock threw itself open with a crack that sounded like a snapped bone, and the door swung wide to reveal me standing over a bleeding knight with the sigil of the Ninefold Court shining faintly silver on my own hand now, transferred like grease, like guilt.

The lead witchfinder was a woman perhaps ten years older than me, sharp-faced, calm-eyed. She looked at the door. She looked at the lock, hanging crooked. She looked at my hand.

"Well," she said, in the tone of someone confirming a suspicion rather than discovering a surprise. "That's convenient."

Behind her the crowd had gone quiet in the specific way crowds go quiet when the entertainment arrives early.

"Charm-seller," she said, reading my sign. "Oscar. Just Oscar?" There was a ledger somewhere with my false name in it, I realized, and this woman had read it, and the false name had never once fooled anyone who mattered. "Step aside from the body, if you please."

"He came in wounded. I haven't touched him beyond—" Beyond branding myself with a dead court's sigil. Beyond being named heir to a council of corpses. "—first aid."

"Mm." She stepped past me with the unhurried confidence of someone who has never once been told no, and knelt by Aldric. Her gloved fingers found the wound under his breastplate, and she frowned. "This man has been running from us for eleven days. Do you know what he carries, charm-seller?"

"A lot of blood, apparently."

She smiled without warmth. "Names. He carries nine names, and the crown pays a bounty on each that would buy your shop, your street, and the street behind it." She rose. "So you'll understand my disappointment at finding him in your shop, under your protection, with his sigil fresh on your skin."

"I don't know what you mean. This is a birthmark."

"Show me your other hand."

And here is where I should tell you what I did, and I will, but first understand: there was no plan. There was a calculation of the crudest kind—if she takes me, I die; if she doesn't take me, I might live—and underneath the calculation, rising up from wherever the swordspeech lives, something that had waited twelve years in the dark and was done waiting, and it did not consult me.

Every blade on the street sang.

Not loudly. Not dramatically. But every knife and scythe and hinge and horseshoe-nail from the well to Tanner's Row hummed a single clear note, and the sound went through the crowd like cold water through cloth, and horses shied, and the witchfinders' net-irons rattled in their holders like teeth chattering, and Ser Aldric Vane laughed—a terrible, delighted, bloody laugh—from the floor of my shop.

The sharp-faced woman spun toward me. Her hand went to something at her belt that wasn't a weapon exactly, more a device, a cage of wire and glass. "You," she breathed, and for one moment the professional calm cracked and I saw what lived under it: hunger. The particular hunger of a woman who has hunted whispers for years and just watched one take solid form.

I have one talent besides the swordspeech, and it is this: I am very good at knowing how things break. It's part of the same listening. So when I looked at her wire-and-glass cage, I saw immediately that the left strut was fatigued, badly soldered, one bad day from failure regardless of anything I did.

I gave it a bad day.

One note, focused, pitched to the frequency of tired metal. The cage shattered in her hand with a pop of glass, and while she stared at her bleeding palm I did the second thing, the unforgivable thing, the thing that ended my life as Oscar the charm-seller forever:

I pulled the sword.

Not mine—I've never owned a sword, owning one is asking for questions. Ser Aldric's, from under his cloak, drawn by nothing visible, leaping from its sheath across the shop to hang in the air between me and the doorway, humming like a struck bell.

"Anyone else," I said, and my voice came out doubled, mine and the steel's together, "want their fortune told?"

* * *

We went out through the back, over the wall, through Widow Maren's yard (sorry, Widow Maren), and down into the undercity through a grate I'd always known about and never needed. Aldric lasted as far as the second junction tunnel. Then his legs quit for good, and I sat with his head in my lap in the dripping dark while the sounds of the hunt faded above us—whistles, boots, the crowd dispersing disappointed, a herald already calling the news: *a witch awakens in the market, the crown offers reward.*

He was smiling. That was the worst part. Dying men shouldn't smile like that, like a man setting down a load he's carried up a mountain.

"Told you," he whispered. "Told the third seat, before she died. Told her the daughter would rise." He fumbled at his cloak and pressed a leather packet into my hands, tied shut with cord gone soft with sweat. "Vaults. Nine vaults, hidden when the Court fell. Tokens. Each seat keeps... keeps a token..." His eyes were losing focus. "Find them before the crown does. The crown is looking. It has been looking for twelve years, ever since it felt the oath break—"

"Ever since what broke?"

But his eyes had gone past me, fixed on the tunnel ceiling as if reading something written there. "It wears the king," he said. "Understand me, girl. The thing on the throne—it is not the king. The king is dead. Has been dead for years. Something else wears the crown, and it feeds, and it hunts the gifted because we are—" his voice failed, recovered thinner, "—we are food. Your mother knew. Your mother sealed you away from it. The Last Oath was a shield, but shields are also cages, and cages are also..."

He trailed off. I shook him. Gently, then less gently.

"Cages are also what?" I demanded. "Also *what?*"

"...leashes," he murmured. And then, with the last of his breath, in the formal cadence of a knight completing an obligation decades overdue: "Heir of the ninth seat. Daughter of the last voice. The Court calls. Rise when it calls you. Forgive me for... the weight of it."

He died there in the undercity with the water dripping and my hands full of a dead man's packet and a dead court's name, and above us the heralds were still crying the reward for the witch of the market street, and the sword lay across my knees, quiet now, patient, like it intended to wait out my objections.

I sat in the dark for a long time. Long enough that the damp soaked through my skirt and the packet's cord printed itself into my palms. Somewhere in the tunnels, water counted out the seconds of my old life ending.

Twelve years, I had believed hiding was safety. Twelve years, and it had taken one dying man ninety seconds to prove that my invisibility was never mine—it was loaned, revocable, and the interest had just come due.

I untied the cord. Inside: nine slips of vellum, each bearing a name in different hands, and a map of Vell's undercity marked with symbols I didn't recognize yet—but would learn to dread, and to love.

The topmost slip, in my mother's handwriting, said: *If you are reading this, I am sorry. Start with the river.*

Above me, faintly, through stone and earth and the bones of the city, every bell in Vell began to ring.
