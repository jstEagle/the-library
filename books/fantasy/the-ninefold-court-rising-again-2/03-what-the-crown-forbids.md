## 3. What the Crown Forbids

The undercity of Vell smelled like wet stone and old lies, which was fitting, because I'd spent twelve years perfecting both.

Ser Aldric Vane had died at my threshold with the Ninefold Court's sigil burned into his palm, and I had run. Not bravely, not strategically—run, in the way a rabbit runs, all instinct and no dignity, down through the trapdoor behind my charm-shelf that I'd never once used until that night. The tunnel dropped me into Vell's second skin: the drowned quarter where the city's founders had dug for silver and found only water, then kept digging anyway out of spite.

I'd been down there three days. Three days of sleeping in a smuggler's storeroom among barrels of salted eel, eating hardtack that could double as roof tile, and listening to the drip-drip-drip of water that sounded suspiciously like a countdown.

My name was on lists now. Witchfinders didn't burn shopfronts; they burned people, and they liked to do it slowly, with paperwork first. Every hour I sat still, the noose drew tighter. But every road out of the undercity passed through streets where someone might look at my face twice, and I had learned long ago that being looked at twice was how you ended up ash.

So I did what I do best. I hid. And I hated myself with tremendous efficiency.

It was on the third evening that the girl found me—which is to say, she'd probably been watching me since the first night, because nothing in the undercity stayed lost for long except hope.

"You're her," said a voice from atop the eel barrels, "the one the knight bled on."

I didn't scream. I want credit for that. Twelve years of practice will teach you to keep your face still even when your heart tries to climb out your throat. I looked up at the silhouette crouched above me—a small person, wiry, wrapped in a coat that had been stolen from someone at least a foot taller.

"And who," I said, "are you supposed to be?"

"Nessa." She dropped down, landing light as an accusation. Up close she couldn't have been more than fifteen, sharp-faced, with eyes the color of pond ice and hair hacked short by someone who clearly hadn't been paid to care. "Nessa Quick. Well. That's not really my name, but it's the one I like."

"Wonderful. A thief with a fake name."

"A thief with a *gift*." She held up her hand, and between her fingers, something glimmered—not light exactly, more like the air forgot what it was supposed to be doing. It lasted half a heartbeat before she closed her fist. "Small spark. Nothing worth burning over. Yet."

"Keep it hidden," I said, sharper than I meant to. "That's not advice, that's a survival strategy."

"I know." Something flickered across her face—too old, too tired for fifteen. "I saw them take the tallow-chandler's boy last month. He used to make his candles float while he worked. Just a little. Just for fun." Her voice went flat. "They burned him on Wexford Green. He was eleven."

I said nothing. There wasn't anything to say that wasn't a lie or a confession.

"But you—" Nessa leaned closer, studying me the way she'd study a lock. "You're different. Aldric Vane came to *you*. The whole undercity heard he was looking for the heir. And now the witchfinders are turning this quarter inside out, so either you're the heir, or you're the luckiest dead woman in Vell."

"The knight was delirious."

"He named you heir to the Ninefold Court. On purpose. In full sentences." She tilted her head. "Delirious men don't do grammar like that."

I should have sent her away. Instead I heard myself ask: "What do you know about the Court?"

And Nessa smiled, showing a chipped tooth, and said the words that would ruin every comfortable lie I'd ever told myself:

"Not enough. But I know who does. Come on. Old Maren's expecting you—well, she's been expecting *someone* since the knight died, and you're the only someone left."

* * *

Maren lived below the lowest street, in a chamber behind the flooded aqueduct where the city's oldest bones were stacked like firewood. She was ancient in the way of things that refuse to stop—small, bent, blind in one eye, with hands spotted like a leopard's and steady as a surgeon's. When we entered, she was already pouring tea into three cups, which meant Nessa had told her, which meant everyone knew everything, which meant my remaining lifespan in this city could be measured in hours if I was unlucky and days if I was lucky.

"Sit, child," Maren said, in a voice like gravel poured slow. "You've got your mother's shoulders."

Every hair on my arms stood up. "You knew my mother?"

"I knew *all* of them." She pushed a cup toward me. "Sit. Drink. This story's long, and you've been starving yourself on ignorance for twelve years. It shows."

I sat. I drank. The tea tasted like iron and rosemary, and I would later learn it was laced with something that calmed the shakes, which was thoughtful of her, because what she told me next would have shaken me apart otherwise.

"What do you know about the Ninefold Court?" Maren asked.

"Tyrants," I said automatically. The word came easy, worn smooth from use. "Nine sorcerer-lords who ruled Vell with fear. They hoarded magic. They enslaved the gifted and murdered anyone who resisted. Then the good king broke them, and there was peace."

Maren laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry as old paper catching flame.

"Peace," she repeated. "Yes. Let me tell you about peace, Oscar Underhill. Do you know what Vell was like under the Court?"

"Enslaved."

"It rained bread." She leaned forward, one eye glittering. "Not literally—the grain-charms weren't that strong—but near enough. The Court's nine seats weren't thrones, girl, they were *duties*. Nine bearers, each holding one thread of the kingdom's weave. The Voice, who spoke law into stone. The Smith, who bound strength into steel. The Tidecaller, who kept the harbor safe and the rivers honest. The Greenwarden, whose gardens fed the city through three famines. The Shield, the Lantern, the Roadmaker, the Keeper of Names, and the First Seat, who held them all together." She ticked them off on her fingers like a rosary. "Magic wasn't hoarded. It was *shared*. Any child born with a spark was brought to the Court and taught—not chained, taught. The gifted served terms, like soldiers. They built the aqueducts. They healed the plague-years. They made Vell the city kings in other lands wrote home about."

"That's not—" My voice cracked. "The histories say—"

"The histories say what the crown paid them to say." Maren spat into the fire. "Your king—your *good king*—declared magic treason twenty years ago. Do you know why? Because the Court refused him one thing. One. He wanted the gift bound to bloodline—his bloodline—so his heirs could rule with power and no accountability. The Court said no. Magic belonged to everyone who carried it or no one. So he called it treason, and he broke them seat by seat, and he wrote himself a pretty history where tyrants fell and peace began." Her voice dropped low and vicious. "And then he started hunting. Every gifted soul in Vell, dragged out, tested, burned or bound—and their power siphoned into the crown itself. You think the witchfinder processions are punishment? Child, they're *harvest*. Every pyre feeds the thing on the throne. Why else do you think the fires never stop? Why else does the hunt grow every year, hungrier and hungrier, when every history book swears the magic was already destroyed?"

The room seemed to tilt. I gripped my cup hard enough to whiten my knuckles.

"The tallow-boy," I whispered. "Eleven years old. He wasn't punished. He was—"

"Harvested," Maren said. "Like wheat."

I thought of the old woman from the market, days ago, dragged past my stall while I arranged my charms and looked at my hands. I thought of the way the crowd always watched the burnings, quiet, hungry, grateful it wasn't them. I thought of twelve years of telling myself that hiding was the same as surviving, that silence was the same as safety.

All of it—every year, every suppressed word, every charm sold by candlelight—hadn't been prudence. It had been complicity wearing a very convincing disguise.

"My mother," I said, and stopped, because my throat had closed.

"Was the Court's last true Voice," Maren said gently. "Swordspeech ran in her line—the old gift, the first gift, the one that speaks to the blade and through it. When the purge came, she did the only thing she could. She silenced you. Bound your own voice against you, so the testers would find nothing. And she hid you in plain sight, selling trinkets in a market full of people too frightened to look closely." Her good eye fixed on me. "She bought you twelve years, Oscar. The question is what you'll buy with them."

I set the cup down because my hands had begun to shake, and I am nothing if not graceful under pressure.

"This is a lot," I managed. "This is—I sell *charms*. Luck-charms. They work by coincidence and confidence, mostly. I'm not a—"

"You're the heir," Nessa said quietly, from the shadows by the door. She'd been silent so long I'd forgotten her. "Aldric Vane didn't bleed out on your floor for a mistake."

"Aldric Vane was dying, and dying men grab at anything."

"He grabbed at *you*." She stepped forward, chin up, that pond-ice stare unblinking. "You can keep pretending. You're good at it—you've had practice. But the witchfinders are going door to door in this quarter right now, and they'll find you eventually, and when they do, all your pretending will get you is a slower pyre." She spread her small, clever hands. "Or you can find out what you actually are. There are vaults, Oscar. Nine of them. The Court hid their tokens before the end—seats, memories, pieces of the old power. Aldric's notes are already moving through the undercity, and they point to you."

"Maren," I said desperately, "tell her I'm not—"

But Maren was watching me with that one glittering eye, and what she said was: "Grief keeps, child. Guilt keeps better. But inheritance—" she shook her head slowly, "inheritance rots unless you pick it up. Your mother didn't silence you to keep you small. She silenced you to keep you *whole*, until the day the world needed the whole of you. Look outside these walls. Tell me that day hasn't come."

I opened my mouth to argue—to say something wry and deflecting, something about eel barrels and hardtack and the general unfairness of being a shopkeeper drafted into destiny—

And that was when the bells started.

Not the hour-bells. The hunt-bells—the deep triple toll from the Citadel that every soul in Vell learned to dread before they learned their letters. Through the stone walls came the other sounds too: boots, many boots, and the creak of the winches that lowered the witchfinder lanterns into the undercity tunnels.

"They've sealed the stairwells," Nessa breathed, suddenly all business. "They're sweeping level by level. That's not a search—that's a *harvest*. Someone told them where to look."

We looked at each other, and the same thought landed on us both: three days ago, a dying knight had collapsed in a charm-shop in the market district, naming an heir. Word traveled. Words always travel in Vell, faster than anything except fire.

Maren moved with shocking speed for a woman shaped like a question mark, pressing a bundle into my hands—bread, a vial, and a folded scrap of parchment that smelled of smoke.

"Aldric's notes. First vault's under the old Tidecaller's chapel, down by the Saltgate. Go now, go deep, and don't stop for anyone you can outrun." She caught my wrist, her grip iron despite the spotted hand. "One more thing. The harvest isn't random anymore. They're not hunting the gifted generally, girl—they're hunting *you* specifically. The crown knows its heir walks free. Whatever wears the king's face wants the last swordspeaker found before she finds her seats." Her grip tightened. "Do you understand me? You are not running from the fire anymore. You *are* the fire, as far as they're concerned."

Somewhere above us, something heavy slammed against a door, and voices shouted in the cadence of men who enjoyed their work.

Nessa grabbed my sleeve. "Chapel's a mile through the flood-tunnels. I know the way. Can you walk fast and panic quietly?"

"No."

"Perfect. Neither can I. We'll be terrible together." And she grinned—that fierce, chipped-tooth grin—as the first lantern-light spilled orange down the far tunnel, and took off running, pulling me with her into the dark.

Behind us, Maren stood in her doorway with her three teacups, one of them still full and steaming for a guest who would never come back, and began calmly singing a lullaby in the old tongue—the kind of song, I realized much later, that the Court's Voices once used to wake the stones.

Ahead of us, the flood-tunnels swallowed our footsteps, and somewhere in the dark ahead lay a chapel, a vault, and the first piece of an inheritance I had spent twelve years pretending I didn't owe.

The bells tolled again. I ran.
