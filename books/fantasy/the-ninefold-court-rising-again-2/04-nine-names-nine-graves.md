## 4. Nine Names, Nine Graves

Aldric's notes were written in the handwriting of a man who knew he was dying and resented every second it cost him. The letters slanted hard to the right, as if the words were trying to run off the page before his blood dried on them.

*The first vault lies beneath the old tannery in Corvers Row. Look for the door that isn't. It answers to blood of the line.*

Blood of the line. Which raised several questions I didn't have time to ask a dead man, chief among them: *what line?* I'd been an Underhill for twelve years, and before that an orphan of convenient paperwork. My mother had left me three things—a name she told me never to speak, a scar on my palm I'd always assumed came from a kitchen knife, and a lullaby in a language that made my teeth itch when I hummed it.

I'd spent twelve years assuming the scar was the most interesting item on that list.

"You're doing the face," Nessa said.

I looked up from the notes. She was sitting on my counter—my counter, in my shop, which she had annexed sometime around dawn with the entitlement of a cat claiming a sunlit windowsill. She'd been sleeping in my back room for four days now, ever since Ser Aldric Vane bled out on my floorboards and witchfinders burned half of Tanner's Lane looking for us. Four days of stolen bread, sharp opinions, and a girl who could make candle flames lean toward her like flowers toward the sun.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you swallowed a ledger and it's disagreeing with you."

"I'm deciding whether to break into a vault under a tannery based on instructions from a corpse."

"Decided yet?"

I folded the notes into my coat. "Obviously not, or I'd be gone already."

She hopped off the counter and grabbed her threadbare cloak. "Good. I hate waiting."

"Nessa—"

"You were going to say it's too dangerous." She tied the cloak with a jerk. "You said that about leaving the shop. You said that about the tunnels. You said it about eating, once, which was honestly deranged. So save your breath. You'll need it for whatever's under the tannery."

There are moments when you realize you've stopped leading and started merely walking at the front of a small parade. That was one of them.

* * *

Corvers Row had been respectable once, back when Vell still pretended the river district wasn't rotting from the edges inward. Now the tanneries ran three days out of ten, the smell of lime pits and cured hide hung over everything like a curse nobody had the energy to lift, and the workers moved with the particular shuffle of people who had stopped expecting anything to improve.

The old tannery sat at the row's dead end, its roof sagging like a tired shoulder. According to Aldric, it had been abandoned for thirty years—which meant, in Vell terms, that someone was probably living in it, and that someone would object to visitors.

*Look for the door that isn't.*

I walked the building's perimeter twice while Nessa kept watch with the nervous energy of someone who had never kept watch over anything larger than a stolen pie. On the north wall, between two buttresses, there was a stretch of stone that bothered me. Not visibly—it matched the rest, same mortar, same weathering. But it bothered me the way a wrong note bothers you in a song you've known since childhood. The way certain silences bother me. The way, for twelve years, my own voice had bothered me, humming under my skin like a blade held too close to a whetstone.

Swordspeech isn't like other magic. I can't explain that to anyone who doesn't carry it, but I'll try anyway. Other gifts are things you do. Swordspeech is something you *hear*—the world is full of edges, and every edge has a name, and if you know the name you can ask the edge to be something else. Steel wants to cut. Stone wants to hold. Wood wants to burn or float or grow, depending on its mood. Everything talks, if you're cursed enough to listen.

And the wall in front of me was saying, very quietly, *no.*

Not *nothing here.* Just *no.* A refusal so practiced it had worn smooth, like a lie told so often it becomes indistinguishable from truth.

"There," I said.

Nessa peered at the blank wall. "It's a wall."

"It's a door pretending very hard to be a wall."

"How do you know?"

"Because I'm having an argument with it."

She gave me a look—the look she'd been giving me more often lately, equal parts awe and alarm—and wisely said nothing.

I pressed my scarred palm against the stone. The old woman in the market had taught me that charms work best through skin; it was the one piece of honesty in a trade built on misdirection. But this wasn't charm-work. This was the other thing, the thing I hadn't let myself do in public since I was seventeen, the thing Aldric's dying grip on my wrist had dragged back into the light whether I consented or not.

*Open,* I said—not aloud, not even close to aloud, just the intention shaped the way steel likes it, blunt and honest. *You were made to open. Open.*

The stone shivered. Mortar dust sifted down like pale snow. And then a seam appeared where no seam had been, thin as a hair, widening into the outline of a door, and the door swung inward onto darkness that smelled of cold stone and, faintly, impossibly, of my mother's kitchen.

I stood very still for a moment.

Twelve years, and no one had warned me that grief keeps a filing system. That a smell could reach down through all the years and all the careful forgetting and hand you back a Tuesday evening: bread on the table, lamplight low, my mother humming that teeth-itching lullaby while she wrapped something in oilcloth and told me, again, *never say your name, never say it, promise me.*

"Oscar?" Nessa's voice, small in the doorway. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing." My voice came out rough. "Old memory. Come on."

* * *

The vault was smaller than I'd expected—barely a chamber, really, cut from the living rock beneath the tannery foundations. No treasure. No arsenal. Just a stone pedestal in the center, and on it, a wooden box the size of a prayer book, and above the pedestal, carved into the rock in letters deep enough to cast shadows:

**MAERWEN OF THE NINTH SEAT. VOICE OF THE SWORD. KEEPER OF THE LAST OATH.**

I read it three times. The words didn't rearrange themselves into anything less impossible.

Voice of the Sword. Keeper of the Last Oath. I knew those phrases—I'd known them all my life without knowing I knew them, the way you know a scar's shape by touch. They were in the lullaby. They were in the words my mother murmured over my palm the night she cut it, the night of the Last Oath, the night before she put me on a cart to Vell and vanished from history.

"Oscar," Nessa whispered. "Whose name is that?"

"A dead woman's," I said, because it was true, and because the truer answer—*possibly my mother's mother, possibly worse, possibly a family tree I've been standing on my whole life without looking up*—was more than I could say out loud in a room that smelled like bread and lamplight.

The box was plain oak, unremarkable except that it, too, said *no* when I reached for it. Everything in this kingdom said no. No magic. No names. No questions about why the king hadn't aged in ten years, according to the whispers Aldric had traded with his last breaths. Vell was built on *no*, stacked brick by brick, and we all lived in the shadow of it calling it safety.

My blood was the key—that much the notes had made clear. I drew my belt knife, hesitated only long enough to feel ridiculous, and opened the old scar across my palm.

The pain was familiar. That was the worst part. Twelve years and the wound remembered being made.

I pressed my hand to the box.

Warmth bloomed under my palm—not the warmth of wood near fire, but the warmth of a hand clasping yours. And then the vault was gone.

* * *

I stood in a hall I had never seen and knew intimately. Nine thrones on a dais of black granite, each different—oak and iron, glass and bone, one grown from living white birch with its roots trailing down through the floor into darkness. Banners hung from rafters high enough to hold weather. And on the steps below the ninth throne, a woman with grey-streaked black hair was kneeling, wrapping something in oilcloth, humming.

I knew her face. Of course I knew her face. I saw it every day in my own reflection, older and wearier and missing the laugh lines.

"Mama?" My voice cracked in the middle of the word, seventeen years old again.

She didn't hear me. Memory, Aldric's notes had said—*the tokens remember their bearers. You will see what they saw. It is not a kindness.* He'd underlined that twice.

The scene shifted like a hand turning a page. Same hall, different light—storm-light through high windows. The woman stood now, and she was shouting, and around her nine figures blurred at the edges as if memory couldn't quite afford to keep them all in focus.

"—sworn, Maerwen! Sworn on the Court itself!" A man's voice, ragged with fury. "The king has declared us traitors and you want to *negotiate*?"

"I want to survive," my mother said, and her voice was swordspeech spoken plain, each word with an edge on it, and I felt it pass through me like cold water. "There are children in this court, Davorin. There are people who cannot fight. If I go to him and offer the Oath—if I bind myself, bind the gift, show him we can be silenced without slaughter—"

"He'll take your silence and come for our throats anyway."

"Then I'll have lost nothing but my voice."

"And if you're wrong?"

She was quiet for a long moment. In the storm-light her face did something complicated—grief and resolve wrestling to a draw.

"Then I'll have bought time," she said finally. "Time is all any of us have ever had. Time to hide what matters."

The scene turned another page. Night. Fire on the horizon, though I couldn't tell if it was the city burning or just sunset lying. My mother knelt before a child—young, dark-haired, maybe six years old, clutching a rag doll—and held out her own palm with a knife resting across it.

"This will hurt," she told the child gently. "But after, no one will ever find you. No one will ever hear what you are. You'll be safe, little bird. Safe and silent until the world changes."

"Will the world change?" the child asked.

My mother smiled, and it broke my heart, because it was the smile I'd spent twelve years failing to forget, and because I could see she didn't believe her own answer.

"Someday. And when it does, you'll speak your name, and every sword within a hundred miles will sing for you."

The child laid her small hand on the knife.

The memory ended.

* * *

I came back to myself on the vault floor with Nessa shaking my shoulder and my own voice making sounds I didn't recognize—raw, rhythmic, half-sobbed syllables of the lullaby, the actual words of it, spilling out of me for the first time since I was six years old.

"—Oscar! Oscar, stop, you're scaring me—"

I stopped. I lay on cold stone and breathed and stared at the ceiling where nine shadows might have been, once, carved by hands that believed the Court would last forever.

The box sat open on the pedestal. Inside, on a bed of faded blue velvet, lay a token: a small sword wrought in silver no longer than my finger, its blade inscribed with a single word in the language of the lullaby. When I lifted it, it fit my palm exactly, scar to hilt, as if it had been measured against my hand before I was born.

*Maerwen of the Ninth Seat,* the wall had said. *Keeper of the Last Oath.*

My grandmother, maybe. Or—given the child in the memory, given the knife, given the vow that had lived in my palm for twelve years—

Or my mother herself, before she threw away her seat and her name and became simply a woman with a cart and a terrified daughter.

"She bound me to save me," I said aloud. The words tasted strange. True things usually do, at first. "The oath in my hand—it's hers. The Last Oath. She took it to the king, or meant to, and she put a copy in me so they'd never... so I'd never..."

Nessa crouched beside me. For once she didn't have a sharp remark loaded. Instead she said, quietly: "Did it work? Are you safe?"

I laughed. It came out wrong.

"No, Ness. I'm heir to a murdered court, breaking into graves with a thief, while a dead man's crown hunts anyone who glows. It worked for exactly as long as it took a knight to bleed out on my floor." I closed my fist around the silver sword. "But she tried. Gods help her, she tried."

Eight tokens remained. Eight names carved into eight walls somewhere in a kingdom that had erased them. And I understood now—really understood, in the marrow where the swordspeech hummed—what Aldric had handed me along with his blood and his sigil. Not a quest. An inheritance. Nine dead voices, each with a memory to give and probably a price to ask, and every one of them fitting my hand like family.

Outside, far off toward the palace hill, a bell began to ring. Not the hour-bell. The hunt-bell—the one that meant witchfinders had found something, somewhere, and were burning it.

Nessa and I exchanged a look.

"They know about the vaults," I said.

"How can you know that?"

"Because I'm starting to think like the thing that killed them." I tucked the token inside my coat, over my heart, where it sat warm as a held hand. "Come on. We have eight graves to rob before they do."

We climbed out of the dark into the lime-stink afternoon of Corvers Row, and behind us the false door sealed itself smooth as skin, keeping its silence the way this whole city does—

until someone bleeds on it, and asks it properly by name.
