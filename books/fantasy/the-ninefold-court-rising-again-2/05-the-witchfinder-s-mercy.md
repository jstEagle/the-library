## 5. The Witchfinder's Mercy

The Hallow family's house smelled of bread and rosemary, and I stood in their kitchen at dawn with a warrant in my hand and ash already on my mind.

That's the thing they don't tell you about the work: you smell it before it happens. Twelve years of burning leaves a residue on the imagination. I could stand in any room in Vell—bakeries, nurseries, chapels—and see exactly how it would go up. Where the fire would catch. Which window would blow out first. It is not a gift. I want that understood. It is a professional deformation, like a butcher who can't look at a lamb without weighing it.

"Herrin Hallow," I said to the man of the house, because the warrant said so and because names matter when you're about to take everything else away from someone. "You're accused of bearing the gift. The penalty is fire. You may confess, which shortens things considerably, or deny, which doesn't."

He was a cooper by trade, broad-shouldered, with flour on his knuckles where he'd been kneading before we knocked. He looked at his wife. His wife looked at the floor. Between them, half-hidden behind her mother's skirts, stood a girl of perhaps seven with her father's jaw and her mother's habit of looking down.

"Deny," Herrin Hallow said.

"They always do," said Wexler, my junior, from the doorway. He was picking at something under his fingernail. He'd been picking at it since the Ashwick burnings last month, and I had begun to suspect it was not dirt but conscience, though he'd have denied it with his whole chest if I'd named it aloud.

I set the warrant on their table, next to the bread. There was a ritual to this, and the ritual mattered, or told myself it did. A witchfinder who skips the forms is just an arsonist with paperwork.

"The accusation comes from your neighbor, one Doss Merrow," I continued. "Who reports that your daughter, Tilly Hallow, made a broken cart wheel roll uphill."

"It rolled downhill," Herrin said. "It rolled downhill into our yard and she stopped it with her foot. Doss Merrow is a liar and owes me money for a barrel stave."

"Probably all true." I said it mildly enough that Wexler glanced up from his fingernail. "And yet here we are."

Here we are. The two most honest words in this city, and I've built a career on them.

* * *

Let me tell you how a burning actually goes, since the pamphlets get it wrong and the songs get it worse.

First there is the reading of charges, done loudly, in the square, so the crowd understands this is justice and not murder. The distinction is thin as communion wine, but the crowd needs it, and frankly so do I. Then the condemned are bound—not cruelly, I insist on that much; rope chafing is not part of the sentence—and led to the stake, which stands in Cindermarket because the stones there hold heat and the drains were designed for it, back when the city still pretended to plan ahead.

Then the oil. Then the torch.

Then the noise. The noise is the part no one warns you about. Not the screaming—the screaming stops sooner than you'd think, and I'll say nothing else about that—but the crowd. They make a sound like hunger being fed. I have heard it at executions, at festivals, at the fall of a famous man from a high balcony. Vell loves an ending. Give them one and they will love you for it, briefly, the way you love a knife only while it's cutting.

Herrin Hallow denied his gift to the very end, which was either brave or honest or both, because here is what I knew and what he didn't: I had read the girl. That's the technical term, *read*, and it's mine—I invented it, or at least I use it as if I did. You take the accused's hands and you wait, and if there's anything buried in them, anything humming, it leans toward you like a plant toward light. Most people are empty. That was the surprise of my early years, how empty most people are, how thoroughly the crown's twelve-year purge has scoured the talent out of Vell until what remains is rumor and grudge and neighbors settling debts with accusations.

Tilly Hallow was not empty. Tilly Hallow had a spark in her small enough to hide in a thimble, warm as a struck match, and when I took her hands during the reading of charges—standard procedure, the child examined too—she looked up at me with absolute trust, because I had been gentle, because gentleness from someone in my uniform reads as safety, and she let me feel every ember of what she was.

She had made the wheel roll uphill. Or downhill and then stop it, same thing—the point is she touched it and it obeyed. A nothing-gift. A party trick. In the old days, before the Court fell, a child like Tilly would have been registered, tutored, maybe given a token and a title she'd grow into. Now she was kindling.

Her parents were empty. Both of them. Clean as washed plates. The gift hadn't come from either line, or had skipped generations, or had simply happened, the way weather happens.

I reported the family as charged anyway. I want that on the record, wherever records go after they're burned: I reported them knowing two-thirds of them were innocent, because the warrant named three and my superiors count heads, and a captain who keeps returning incomplete numbers starts attracting questions she cannot afford.

But between the reading and the stake, I did one small thing. One.

* * *

"You'll want to say goodbye properly," I told the mother, Maren Hallow, as we waited in the holding pen behind Cindermarket. "The child especially. Take your time."

Wexler gave me a look. He's not stupid, Wexler. Dangerous quality in a subordinate. But he also owed me his career twice over and knew it, so he turned and inspected the wall with great diligence.

Maren Hallow held her daughter and whispered to her, and I stood close enough to hear none of it and far enough to see everything, which is the witchfinder's whole art distilled. And when Maren pressed something into the child's hand—a little cloth bundle, a mother's foolishness, a biscuit or a button or a saint's medallion—I turned away and spoke to the guard on the pen's far gate about the state of his boots.

When the guards came to fetch the family for the procession, there were two children in the pen.

I know how that sounds. I know exactly how it sounds, because I've rehearsed the accounting a hundred times in the dark hours: *one child spared, one child put in her place*. The place belonged to a beggar girl called Pip who worked the market crowds and whose mother had died the winter before and whose description, conveniently, matched Tilly Hallow's within a hair—same age, same coloring, same underfed frame. Pip had been on the census rolls as unclaimed. No one would come looking for her. No one ever came looking for girls like Pip.

Except someone had to be standing in the pen, and Pip was, and when the guards counted heads—three, matching the warrant—Pip went to the stake wearing Tilly Hallow's name and Tilly Hallow's fear, because I had whispered to her beforehand that if she cried quietly and didn't fight, her death would be quick, and I am ashamed to report that she believed me, and more ashamed to report that I kept my word. The oil was fresh and the wind was right. She went fast. Quicker than some.

And Tilly Hallow walked out the far gate of the pen in a servant's smock I'd arranged, under the arm of a woman I trust, bound for a farm two provinces east where the crown's writ runs thin and the census is a rumor. I paid for the arrangement out of my own salary. I have paid for four such arrangements now. I keep the tally in my head, because paper burns, and I have learned what fire does to paper.

Four lives bought with one. That's the ledger. That's how I sleep, on the nights I sleep: *four for one, four for one*, over and over, a rosary made of arithmetic.

It doesn't work. I'm telling you honestly, as a professional in the field of self-deception: it doesn't work. Pip's face comes anyway. Pip trusted me too.

* * *

After the burning, there is the report, and after the report, the palace summons, because the Hallow case had drawn attention. Three gifted in one household, even falsely—it was the kind of arithmetic the crown liked, and the crown has developed an appetite lately. The summons came sealed with black wax, and black wax means the Office itself, and the Office means the old man's chamber above the Hall of Judgment where nobody goes unless summoned.

I went. Obviously. You don't decline black wax.

The antechamber smelled of tallow and something sweeter underneath, something like flowers left too long in a closed room. Two other captains waited, both senior, both looking at the floor the way people look at floors when the alternative is looking at each other and admitting shared terror. I took my turn quickly, which I've learned to do. Hesitation reads as guilt. Confidence reads as loyalty. Neither is true, but the crown grades on presentation.

Inside, the Lord Overseer sat behind a desk older than the dynasty, and beside him, on a cushioned chair too ornate for sitting, sat the Visitor.

We're not told what the Visitor is. We're told the Visitor speaks for the crown directly, without intermediary, which everyone understands to mean without humanity. It wore robes of deep blue and a mask of pale porcelain, featureless except for the suggestion of eyes—two shallow dents, like thumbprints in dough. Its voice came out level and pleasant and wrong, the way a well-tuned instrument played by someone with no heart sounds.

"Captain Reyne." It knew my name before introduction. Of course it did. "Your Hallow operation was efficient."

"Thank you, Your Grace."

"One child escaped your custody, we understand. During the transfer."

My pulse did something complicated. "A clerical confusion regarding identity, Your Grace. The condemned girl was delivered to the stake as warranted."

"Yes." The porcelain mask tilted, a degree, no more. "The condemned girl. Tell us, Captain—when you examined the Hallow child, what did you feel?"

*Feel.* Not *find*. Feel.

"Residual warmth consistent with low-grade gift," I said. "Nothing actionable."

"Nothing actionable," the Visitor repeated, tasting the words. "You have a talent for classification, Captain Reyne. Things are never quite what they are in your reports. They are *manageable*. They are *clerical*. They are *nothing actionable*." A pause, long enough to hang a man in. "This is why we value you. And why we watch you."

I kept my spine straight through sheer stubbornness. "The Office has my full service."

"The Office has your obedience," the Visitor corrected gently. "Service is a choice. Obedience is a leash. Do you know what we do with leashes, Captain Reyne? We pull them. Occasionally we admire them. Very rarely—" the mask leaned toward me, and beneath the sweetness of its voice I heard something vast shift its weight, like a ship changing course, like a mountain settling, "—we cut them, and see what the animal does with freedom. You would not enjoy that experiment. Neither would the animal."

"No, Your Grace."

"We have a task for you. There is a swordspeaker in Vell."

The words landed in the room like ice dropped in hot fat. Swordspeech. The gift that hadn't been seen in a generation, the voice that could command steel and unmake oaths, the thing the old Court's last champions had used and the crown had spent twelve years making certain died out. Every witchfinder learns the name the way seminary students learn the devil's.

"She surfaced today," the Visitor continued. "Briefly. Publicly. In the Sootmarket district. A knight died speaking her inheritance, and she answered him with power, in front of witnesses. Witnesses who are now being collected." The mask regarded me. "Find her, Captain Reyne. Bring her to us alive. This is not a request dressed as a request. This is the leash, pulled once, gently, so you remember its weight."

"And if I find I cannot deliver her?"

The Visitor rose. It was taller standing than sitting, which shouldn't be possible, and its shadow fell across the desk and across me, and the shadow moved a half-second after the body did, the way a reflection lags in stirred water.

"Captain," it said, almost kindly, "you have been spending the crown's coin on farmhouses in the eastern provinces. Four of them, we believe. Lovely country, this time of year. Full of orphaned girls with new names." The porcelain dents that served it for eyes held steady on mine. "Serve, and those arrangements remain clerical confusions forever. Be served instead—be *useful* in the deeper sense—and perhaps we discuss other arrangements. We know what hums in you, Ilsa Reyne. We have known since your examination year. You bury a candle in your chest and call it professionalism. Bring us the swordspeaker, and we will teach you what the candle is for."

I walked out of that room with my legs working through pure discipline and my mind doing the arithmetic it always does, the rosary, the ledger: *four for one, four for one.*

Except now the ledger had a new column, and the new column had my own name in it, written in a hand I recognized as mine.

* * *

That night I sat in my rooms above the Hall with a map of Vell spread across my table and a cup of wine I wasn't drinking, and I told myself the story I always tell, the good one, the reasonable one: *I am the mercy in this machine. Without me the burnings would be crueller, the counts higher, the Pip-hedges torn up. Someone reasonable has to hold the leash so it isn't held by monsters.*

It's a fine story. It has held my weight for twelve years, the way a bridge holds a cart—creaking, but holding.

But tonight, for the first time, I noticed the crack in the span. Because the Visitor had said *we know what hums in you*, and it had said it like a promise, and the terrible thing—the thing I will admit here in the dark where no one can hear—is that some small buried part of me, the part with the candle in it, had *leaned toward the light*.

Like a plant. Like a match. Like Tilly Hallow's hand in mine, trusting.

Somewhere in the Sootmarket tunnels, a swordspeaker was running with a dead knight's inheritance in her blood, and I was the hound they meant to loose on her, and the worst of it—the truly unforgivable part—is that I wanted to meet her. Gift to gift. Mirror to mirror. To ask her the question I've never had anyone to ask:

*What would you have done, in my place?*

I pinned the first marker to the map. Sootmarket. And began, reasonably, to hunt.
