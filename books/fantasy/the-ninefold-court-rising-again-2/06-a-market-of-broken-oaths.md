## 6. A Market of Broken Oaths

Brann the oath-breaker lived above a shuttered forge in Copperlane, where the chimneys had gone cold so long ago that sparrows nested in them like they'd won the war. I knew the address because Aldric's notes were thorough and because Nessa, who claimed to know every roofline in Vell the way other people know their own teeth, had drawn me a map on the back of a stolen prayer card.

*The smith. Don't let his hands fool you,* she'd written underneath. *They're gentle until they're not.*

I stood across the street for a quarter hour rehearsing opening lines like a girl about to propose marriage. *Good morning, I'm the heir to a murdered council, your old boss's ghost is in my pocket, please help.* No. Too much. Start smaller.

The truth was I'd been carrying one of the court-tokens for three days now — a sliver of silver shaped like a feather, warm against my sternum, whispering things to me at night when I wanted to sleep. It had belonged to a woman called Maren of the Third Seat, and it remembered her laugh, which was worse than remembering her screams would have been. Screams I could file under history. Laughter felt like an accusation.

So: Brann. The Court's smith. The man who had forged nine thrones and then spent forty years refusing to say their names.

I crossed the street before my courage finished cooling.

* * *

The door was oak, older than the building around it, and it opened before I knocked — which told me he'd heard me pacing out there for fifteen minutes and had been enjoying the show.

He was not what I expected. I don't know what I expected. Fire, maybe, given the profession. What I got was a man built like a door himself, wide and scarred and gray to the eyebrows, with hands so knuckled they looked like a map of somewhere unpleasant. He wore a leather apron gone soft as cloth from years of disuse. Behind him, the dead forge sat cold, and every surface of the room was covered in hinges.

Hinges. Hundreds of them. Brass, iron, copper, arranged in rows like a man cataloguing every way a thing can be made to open and close without ever doing it himself.

"Whatever you're selling," he said, "I don't want it."

"I'm not selling."

"Everyone in Vell is selling something." His eyes went over my coat, my satchel, the market-dust still on my boots. "Charms, are we? Luck-baubles. Little brass lies." Something moved behind his face that was too tired to be contempt and too sharp to be grief. "Get out."

"Aldric Vane sent me."

That landed. I watched it land — watched the name go through him like a nail through green wood. He didn't step aside, but he stopped trying to close the door, which from this man was practically an embrace.

"Aldric," he said. "Is he—"

"Dying. Probably dead by now." I said it flat because if I didn't make myself say it flatly I wasn't going to say it at all. "Witchfinders took him in front of my shop. He gave me something first. A sigil. A name." I hesitated. "Nine names, actually."

For a long moment the only sound was a sparrow rearranging itself in a chimney somewhere overhead.

Then Brann laughed. It was the worst sound I'd heard all week, and I'd heard a woman being dragged through a market square by her hair, so that was saying something.

"The Ninefold Court," he said. "Twelve years, and someone finally comes knocking. And it's a charm-seller with mud on her boots." He turned away, leaving the door open, which I took as permission or a test. "Come in, then. If you're going to disappoint me, do it where I can sit down."

* * *

His workshop was a museum of refusal. I counted at least six hammers on the wall, each one polished, each one hanging exactly where a working smith would hang it, and I understood that he kept the room ready the way a widow keeps a chair. He hadn't touched the forge. He'd just never let anyone take it apart.

"You were the Court's smith," I said. "Aldric's notes call you Brann Ironhold, Forgemaster of the Fifth Seat."

"Aldric's notes should mind their own business." He lowered himself onto a stool with the care of a man whose body had been billing him for decades. "Yes. That was me. Another life. Another man, near enough — the one I was died the same day the rest of them did."

"Not all of them did," I said. "Not immediately. There were survivors. People who hid. I'm—" I stopped. This was the part I'd rehearsed, and it fell apart in my mouth anyway. "I'm one of them. Sort of. My mother was Court-born. I have the gift. Swordspeech."

His eyes came up and actually looked at me for the first time. They were pale gray, furnace-colored even cold, and they went through me like a proof-mark through bad steel.

"Swordspeaker," he repeated. "The last one. Aldric named you heir, didn't he. That's why you're here. You've come to collect the smith."

"I haven't come to collect anything. I've come to ask for help."

"No." He said it the way he probably said everything: once, completely, no seam for argument to find. "No, girl. Find another way."

"There isn't another way. There are nine tokens scattered across Vell and I can't open the vaults alone. The rite needs—"

"I know what the rite needs. I forged the locks." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the lamplight caught the burn scars up both forearms like old lace. "You want to hear why the answer is no? Sit down. No — stand, you'll want to run after."

And he told me.

* * *

His daughter was named Liss. She was nineteen when the purge began, apprenticed to the Court's healers, Fourth Seat line, a gift for mending bone the way other people mend socks. Brann loved her the way furnaces love air — completely, dangerously, needing her to keep burning.

When the king declared magic treason, the Court met for the last time. Nine seats, nine voices, one decision to make: fight, flee, or fold themselves into the city and hope the storm passed over.

"They chose to hide," Brann said. "All nine of them. The great guardians of the realm, the balance of the kingdom, and when it came to it they chose their own skins. They broke the oaths they'd sworn to people like my Liss. They sealed their vaults, salted their traces, and scattered into the dark like ash on water." His voice never rose. That was the terrible thing about it. It stayed level as an anvil face. "But hiding takes money. Papers. Safehouses. So they sold the ones they couldn't protect. Names. Addresses. Gifted families, healer-lines, children — handed to the witchfinders in exchange for quiet roads out of the city."

The room got very small.

"Liss was on the list," he said. "Her teachers wrote her name down themselves. Her own masters. To buy three days' head start." He looked at his hands. "Three days. They bought three days, and it didn't even save them. The crown found every last one within the year. Sold their souls for nothing."

The sparrow in the chimney had gone quiet, as if it too were listening.

"So when you come to me wearing their inheritance," Brann said, "asking me to help raise their Court again — understand that I watched those people choose survival over honor, and I watched it cost my daughter her life, and I have spent twelve years telling myself the world was better off with them in the ground. And I almost believe it." He looked up. "Almost. That 'almost' is the most dangerous thing left in this house."

"I'm sorry," I said, because there was nothing else true to say.

"Sorry's cheap. Everyone in Vell is sorry." He stood, joints cracking like green branches. "Go home, charm-seller. Sell your luck to someone who believes in it."

* * *

Here is what I should tell you about swordspeech, since nobody in Vell remembers: it isn't casting. There's no gesture, no incantation, no circle of chalk. Steel remembers what it has cut. Every blade ever forged carries the shape of its making and the weight of everything it's done, and a swordspeaker can hear that memory and speak back to it — wake it, bend it, remind a sword of better purposes. My mother used to say we don't command metal. We gossip with it.

I hadn't spoken aloud in twelve years. Not a word of power, not a syllable. My oath held my voice shut the way a scabbard holds a blade, and I had told myself for twelve years that the silence was safety, that silence was wisdom, that silence was the price of breathing.

Standing in Brann's doorway, with his hand already on the latch, I understood that silence had also been a kind of lying. To him, and to everyone, and mostly to myself.

"Your daughter's name was Liss," I said.

He froze.

"She was a healer-line. Bone-mender. Nineteen." My heart was going hard enough to shake my ribs. "And you blame the Court, and you have every right, but you also blame yourself, because you were the smith and you taught half those people everything they knew about steel and secrets, and you weren't there. You were here. With your cold forge and your hinges."

"Don't," he said quietly.

"And I know what that's like." I reached down and drew the knife from my boot — the plain one, market-legal, the one I'd carried for twelve years and never once spoken to. "Because I stood in a market square two weeks ago and watched them drag an old woman past my stall, and I did nothing, and I have done nothing for twelve years, and I told myself it was the same as being safe."

"Girl. Put it away."

"No." My hand shook. I let it. "You want proof I'm theirs? Fine. But I'm not proving anything to you. I'm proving it to me."

And I spoke.

One word. A fragment — the smallest honest piece of swordspeech I owned, the word my mother used to hum over kitchen knives, the one that means roughly *be well-used* in a language that doesn't have words so much as it has invitations.

The knife woke.

It didn't glow. It didn't sing, exactly — though the room rang faintly, like a struck bell heard through a wall, like every hinge on every shelf suddenly remembering it was iron once, ore once, star-stuff once, long before anyone thought to make doors out of it. The blade turned in my grip, slow and easy as a fish in current, edge catching lamplight, and I felt its whole short history pour through my palm: the forge it was born in, the rope it cut, the bread it sliced, the twelve dull years of never mattering.

*I know*, I told it, in the wordless way. *Me too.*

Then I let it sleep and slid it back into my boot, and the silence afterward was enormous.

Brann had gone white. Not frightened-white — I know frightened; I sell it in small doses every day. This was different. This was a man looking at something he'd buried alive and finding it still breathing.

"That word," he whispered. "Where did you learn that word?"

"My mother. Sera Underhill. Voice of the Ninth Seat." I swallowed. "She bound my gift when I was fourteen to keep the witchfinders off me. The oath is still on me. That was one word through a locked door."

He sat back down. Slowly, the way old men sit down when the floor has become untrustworthy.

"Sera's girl," he said. "Sera's *girl*. She used to bring you to the hall, do you know that? Little thing, always chewing on something. Liss carried you on her shoulders once, through the lower gallery, showed you the nine thrones." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, I could see him hate it. "You were four."

"I don't remember."

"No," he said softly. "You wouldn't. That's the cruelty of it. We remember everything that broke us and nothing that held us together."

We stayed like that a while — him on his stool, me in his doorway, the dead forge between us like a third party neither of us wanted to introduce ourselves to.

Finally he said, "Why now? Twelve years of quiet, and now you knock on my door with a dead knight's errand. Why?"

Because of the old woman in the square. Because of Maren's laugh in the token against my breastbone. Because Aldric bled out on my floor calling me heir while witchfinders kicked my door in. Because the king is a corpse and the crown is eating my city and I am so tired of mistaking hiding for living.

"Because I did nothing," I said, "for a very long time. And it turns out that wasn't safety. It was just shame with better lighting."

Something shifted in his face. Not forgiveness — I wasn't owed that, and he knew it, and mercifully he didn't pretend otherwise. But the door inside him, the one he'd hung and hinged and painted shut, creaked.

"The fourth vault," he said at last, grudging as gravel. "Under the Saltgate customs house. It's mine — my lock, my making. If anyone can open it, it's me, and if anyone else tries they'll lose fingers." He pointed a scarred finger at me. "But hear me, heir. I'm not doing this for the Court. Their bones can rot. I'm doing it because whatever wears the crown killed my daughter same as they did, and I want it to hurt."

"That's reason enough," I said. "It might be the best one anyone's given me yet."

* * *

Nessa was waiting in the alley, perched on a barrel like a gargoyle with opinions, and she read the answer off my face before I reached the corner.

"He said yes." She grinned. "Ha. I owe Tammy six coppers."

"How do you—"

"Everyone owes Tammy six coppers. It's how you know Tammy." She hopped down, falling into step beside me, and then slowed, sniffing the air like a cat does. "Oz. What's that smell?"

"What smell?"

"Like — hot iron. Like a forge lit for a second and put out." She frowned at me. "It's coming off *you*."

I checked my sleeves. Nothing burning. Nothing smoking. But she was right — there was a smell clinging to me, faint and clean and unmistakable, the smell of metal that has recently been awake. Of work honestly done.

"It's nothing," I said.

It was not nothing, and we both knew it, because halfway down Copperlane the bells started.

Not the hour bells. Not the alarm bells either — those ring fast and angry. These tolled slow, deliberate, spaced like footsteps, and every soul on the street stopped moving at once, the way a field stops when a hawk's shadow crosses it.

From the direction of the palace came a sound I'd never heard and recognized instantly anyway, the way you recognize your own name shouted in a dream: a horn, deep and wrong, bending the slow bells out of tune.

Nessa grabbed my arm. "What is that? What's happening?"

Down the length of Copperlane, at every window, candles were guttering — not blown out, but *bending*, all of them leaning toward the palace like iron filings toward a lodestone. Somewhere beneath my feet I felt it too, faint but rising: the pull. The crown, drawing breath.

And every hinge in Brann's workshop, three streets behind us, must have been trembling on its screws.

"They know," I said. "Someone spoke. Someone listened. They know a swordspeaker is in the city."

The horn sounded again, closer now, and under it — very faint, very far — I could hear the witchfinders' drums starting up, and Nessa's hand tightened on my arm as she said the thing I was already thinking:

"Oz. They're not searching anymore. They're coming straight for us."
