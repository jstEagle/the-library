## 7. The Thing in the Throne Room

They summon me at the hour when Vell's bells forget themselves.

I'm in the Ash Quarter, knee-deep in paperwork that smells of smoke and tallow, when the courier arrives with a summons sealed in black wax — no crest, just the impression of a crown pressed so deep into the seal it's nearly torn through. Captain Ilsa Reyne, it says. Come at once. The king desires an accounting.

The king has never desired anything from me personally. In eleven years of service I've received three commendations, two reprimands, and one form letter thanking me for my discretion regarding an incident in the Tanneries that I have worked very hard to stop remembering. All signed by chancellors. All stamped by clerks. The king himself is a rumor we work under, like weather.

So when the summons comes, my first instinct is not awe. It's arithmetic. Who did I disappoint? What did I burn that I shouldn't have? I run the ledger of my last month while I dress — the family in Cooper's Row, the boy I let walk out the back door while his parents' house went up behind him, the reports I've filed with numbers slightly kinder than the truth. Somewhere in those small mercies there may be a hook with my name on it.

"You look like a woman going to her own funeral," says Doss, my second, holding the door.

"Funerals are honest, at least. Everyone knows why they're there."

He laughs. He thinks I'm being wry. I'm being accurate, which in Vell passes for wit if you deliver it dryly enough.

* * *

The palace rises out of the city's heart like a tooth someone forgot to pull. Black granite veined with something pale — marble, they say, though I've never believed it. Veins don't run uphill. The whole structure leans inward at the top, subtle as a held breath, and every witchfinder learns in their first year not to look directly at the spires for longer than courtesy allows.

I've delivered prisoners here forty times. I've stood in the antechambers, the counting rooms, the corridor of masks where petitioners wait to be told no in expensive language. But I have never been past the third door, and I have certainly never been summoned alone.

The guards at the gate check my papers twice, then a third time without papers, simply looking at me — long, flat looks that make my skin prickle in a way I refuse to name. There's a word for what I am. I've spent thirty years making sure the only person who knows it is dead.

Inside, the palace is warm. That's the first wrong thing. It's high summer and the stone halls should be cool as a cellar, but the air sits heavy and fevered, and the candles burn without flickering. Flames that don't move. I stare at one for three full strides before I catch myself.

"Captain Reyne." A chamberlain glides up beside me — I didn't see him approach, which means either I'm getting old or he practices. He wears grey on grey, and his voice has the smoothed-over quality of a man who has said the same sentence for years. "His Majesty will receive you in the presence chamber. You'll forgive the informality of the venue; he finds the great hall drafty."

"Of course."

"He'll ask you about the swordspeaker."

I keep my face still through sheer professional habit. "There's no swordspeaker, my lord. The gift died with the Court."

"Mm," he says, in the tone of a man filing my answer somewhere I can't reach it. "He'll ask anyway."

We walk. My boots sound wrong on the stone — too loud, then too quiet, as though the floor can't decide how much of me to acknowledge. Portraits line the walls: kings going back nine generations, each more faded than the last, except the most recent ones. Those are vivid. Too vivid. The current king's portrait hangs at the corridor's end, and I make the mistake of meeting its painted eyes.

It was painted, according to the plate beneath it, twelve years ago.

* * *

The presence chamber is smaller than I expected, and rounder, and the ceiling curves down until the room feels less like a hall and more like the inside of a bell. Candles everywhere. No windows. And at the far side, on a throne that's really just a high-backed chair grown grand by loneliness, sits the king.

I bow. I've bowed to magistrates and chancellors and once, memorably, to a visiting duke whose wig was on backwards. I know how to fold myself into respect. But this bow comes out slower than I intend, because some animal part of me has looked at the man on the throne and does not want to bend toward him.

"Captain Reyne." His voice is dry paper. Kind, almost. "You've served the crown eleven years."

"Eleven years, Majesty. Seven as witchfinder, four as captain of the Second Company."

"And in that time you've found—" he pauses, tasting the word— "so many. So many gifted souls, carried gently out of the city's wounds."

Carried gently out. I know what he means by that. I have written those reports myself, in my own hand: *subject removed from the population.* I taught myself to write it without flinching, and mostly I succeeded.

"The work continues, Majesty."

"It does." He smiles, and it's a good smile, warm as a hearth, and that's the second wrong thing, because I realize I cannot tell how old he is. His face is unlined. Not young — unlined, the way a river stone is smooth, worn rather than unworn. The portrait in the corridor shows a man of fifty with a soldier's weathering. The man on this throne could be that portrait's son, or its ghost, or nothing at all. Twelve years of rule, and not one year of age on him.

My mouth has gone dry. I am Ilsa Reyne. I have held burning houses steady with my own two hands and pulled children out of them. I do not frighten at old men.

But this isn't an old man. This is a thing wearing the memory of one.

"Your last report interests me," he says. "Cooper's Row. A family of four. Two confirmed bearers, both... removed. And yet your tally lists four souls processed and three accounted for."

The room gets very quiet. Even the candles seem to lean in.

"There was a child, Majesty. A girl of six. She showed no sign of the gift."

"No sign," he repeats. "Or no sign you wished to find?"

Here it is. The hook I felt coming all morning, and it's worse than insubordination — it's the truth standing naked in front of the throne. I could lie. I've lied to better men than this. But lying to this thing feels like lying to a well: the words just drop, and drop, and you never hear them land.

"She showed no sign," I say. "I judged the risk low. The company needed the example made, not the child dead."

Silence. Long enough that I count my own heartbeats. Nine. Ten.

Then the king laughs, soft as ash settling. "Judgment. Mercy, even." He rises, and his shadow rises with him — and there it is, the third wrong thing, the thing I will be chewing on for the rest of my life. His shadow moves a half-breath after he does. He lifts his hand; the shadow lifts its hand a heartbeat later, like a servant who resents the instruction. When he steps forward, the shadow lags, drags, catches up in a rush that makes my eyes water.

No living man's shadow hesitates.

"Do you know what the gift is, Captain?" He circles me slowly. The candles gutter as he passes them, then steady. "The chancellors call it treason. The priests call it sin. The mob calls it devilry. They're all wrong. The gift is a debt. Magic lent to flesh, and flesh must repay. The Court understood this. The Court *hoarded*, hoarded the way misers hoard coin, nine thrones sitting on nine vaults of borrowed fire while the kingdom starved for warmth." His hand settles on my shoulder, light as a moth. It's cold through my uniform. "I corrected the ledger."

Twelve years ago the Ninefold Court fell. Every history agrees on that much. The histories differ on everything else, and I suddenly understand why: because whatever happened in that fall is still happening, still paying itself out, in candle flames that won't dance and shadows that come late.

"And my part in the correction, Majesty?" I ask. My voice holds. I'm almost proud of it.

"Exemplary." He returns to the throne, and the shadow follows, sullen. "Which brings me to the matter of the swordspeaker."

"Majesty, the gift is extinct—"

"Do not lie to me." The temperature doesn't drop, but everything in the room goes *still* in a way that heat and cold have nothing to do with. "A knight died in the Lamplighters' Market three nights past with a sigil burned into his palm. A charm-seller vanished from her stall the same hour. A thief named Nessa Carrow left the undercity the same night. Coincidences are for mathematicians, Captain. You will find her."

"I don't even know her face."

"You do." He smiles again, that hearth-warm smile, and now I see the hearth is full of something other than fire. "You've seen her already. In Cooper's Row. You looked at a child's mother and let the family burn around her because you thought mercy was a thing you could spend privately." The words land like coins dropped down a well. "You were wrong. Mercy is a debt too, Captain. Everything is. And debts come due."

I should kneel. I should protest. I should do any of the ten thousand things a loyal officer does when her king speaks madness. Instead I hear myself ask the question that has lived under my ribs since I was nine years old, since my grandmother whispered a word over a broken cup and made it whole and was taken away for it, since I buried that word so deep inside me that sometimes I convince myself it was never there:

"And if I refuse?"

The king tilts his head. And the crown — the iron-and-gold thing I've seen in portraits all my life, plain as a horseshoe, famous for its plainness — moves.

Not falls. Not shifts. *Moves*, settling on his brow the way a cat resettles, and the shadow on the floor behind him spreads wide as a spilled tide, and something speaks. Not the king. Through him, around him, using his mouth the way a bad musician uses a borrowed fiddle.

**"Ilsa Reyne."**

My name in a voice like a door opening onto winter.

**"Bearer. Hider. Keeper of a word you swallowed at nine years old. You think you serve the hunt. You ARE the hunted who learned to hold the torch."**

My knees nearly go. I lock them. Thirty years of discipline, spent in one moment just to stay upright.

"What are you offering?" I whisper. Because it is an offer. I know an offer when I hear one; I've made enough of them over prisoner's tables.

**"Legitimacy. Registration instead of pyre. Your gift — spoken aloud, trained, honored, woven into the crown's new order. No more hiding. No more burying grandmothers' words in your chest like seeds you're afraid of. Bring me the last swordspeaker, and you will never again flinch when magic is named in your hearing."**

Every suppressed thing in me leans toward that voice. That's the horror of it — not that I'm tempted, but how *little* temptation it takes. Thirty years I've told myself I stayed clean out of principle. One minute in this room and I know the truth: I stayed hidden because hiding was all I was allowed. Given permission, given honor, given *legitimacy* — I would take it. I want to take it. Some starving thing behind my sternum is already reaching.

And underneath the reaching, cold and clear as well-water, the observer I've always been takes notes: *the shadow lags. The candles don't flicker. Nothing here is alive, and it is promising you life.*

I think of the girl in Cooper's Row, walking out a back door while her house burned. I spared her. Or I told myself I did. Perhaps I just deferred her, the way you defer a payment.

If I bring this crown a swordspeaker, what happens to her? What happens to Nessa Carrow? To every hider and bearer in this city who survives because the hunt is blind in the places I keep it blind?

And if I refuse — what happens to me? Worse. Worse, and pointless, and my gift dies with me unsaid, and the crown finds another captain, one with fewer scruples and colder hands.

"Yes," I hear myself say.

The word leaves my mouth and the room exhales. The candles flare. The shadow spreads across the floor and touches my boot — cold, patient, a signature being taken.

**"Wise,"** says the crown, through the king's smiling mouth.

*I'll control it,* I tell myself, walking backward out of the presence chamber, bowing to a thing that isn't a king, keeping my face arranged into loyalty. *I'll play the hunt slow. I'll steer it wrong. I've deceived better men than this for lesser reasons.*

*I'll control it.*

The doors close behind me. In the corridor, I pass the portrait painted twelve years ago and finally understand why its eyes followed me on the way in: the man in the painting is older than the thing on the throne. Time flows the wrong direction in this palace. It drains upward, into the crown.

In my pocket, my fingers find the stub of chalk I carry for marking doors, and I realize my hand is shaking — and that beneath the shaking, faint as a pulse, the old word my grandmother whispered is stirring for the first time in thirty years.

The crown woke it when it spoke my name. It knows what I carry. It will use what I carry.

And somewhere out in my city, in the dark under Vell, a charm-seller who talks to swords is running out of places to hide.

I straighten my uniform. I put on my captain's face.

I go hunting.
