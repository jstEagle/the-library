## 8. Salt and Ash

The second vault was under a tanner's yard, which meant I spent an hour elbow-deep in lime pits and came out smelling like something even the gulls wouldn't claim. The third was behind the altar of a chapel dedicated to a god nobody had worshipped in two hundred years, which at least had the courtesy to smell like nothing at all.

The tokens, though — those smelled like everything.

I don't know how else to put it. You open your hand and there's this small weight of metal, no bigger than a plum stone, and it breathes. Not literally. Mostly not literally. But when I held the second token — a ring of braided silver that had belonged, if Aldric's notes were right, to the Court's tide-speaker, a woman named Maren Salt who could argue rivers into new beds — I felt the sea. Not remembered it. Felt it, cold and enormous and utterly indifferent to me, rolling through my bones like I was a hollow thing someone had left on the beach.

"Put it in the bag," Brann said.

"I'm holding it."

"I can see you're holding it. Put it in the bag before it holds you back."

Nessa, crouched by the chapel door with her ear pressed to the wood, snorted. She'd been doing more of that lately. Snorting. Smiling crookedly. Eating like a person who expected there to be food tomorrow. It's remarkable what a few weeks away from soldiers will do for a child's disposition, even a child who can set tapestries alight by accident.

"The fourth token," she said, "is in Greyharbour."

"Aldric's notes say Greyharbour," I corrected. "Aldric's notes also said the third vault was 'unwatched,' and we left that chapel through a window while three witchfinder outriders walked up the lane. So forgive me if I take his geography as a suggestion rather than gospel."

Brann grunted. He'd been grunting for three weeks straight, which was an improvement over the first week, when he'd been actively hostile. The smith had come around to tolerating me the way a man tolerates weather — resentfully, and with an eye always toward shelter. His daughter's name still sat between us like a stone neither of us would pick up. I'd spoken swordspeech in front of him once, one fragment, honest and terrible, and he'd decided that whatever I was, I wasn't the Court that killed her. Just its heir apparent, which was apparently worse and better in roughly equal measure.

Greyharbour was two days south along the coast road. A fishing town, Aldric's notes promised, salt-stained and unimportant, where the Court's fourth seat — the oath-keeper, a man named Dovan Ree whose whole gift was making promises true — had hidden his token beneath a lighthouse before the purge came for him.

An oath-keeper's token. I turned that over in my mind the entire ride south. A man whose magic was honesty. What must it have cost him, I wondered, to hide instead of fight? To make himself small?

I knew exactly what it cost. That was the trouble.

* * *

We reached Greyharbour at dusk, and my first thought was that it was too quiet.

Fishing towns aren't quiet at dusk. They're loud with the particular industry of men who've spent all day being lied to by the sea and have opinions about it. But Greyharbour's docks were half-empty, its nets hung slack and dry, and the people in the streets moved with their heads down, quick and silent, like a household tiptoeing past a drunk father's chair.

"They know," Nessa said quietly.

"Everyone always knows," Brann said. "That's the problem with living under a crown. Knowing changes nothing."

We took a room above a chandlery because it was the only building with a lit window, and the chandler — a broad woman with rope-burned hands named Tally Voss — looked at our coins, then at our faces, then out the window at the darkening street, and said only: "Third floor. Back stairs. If you hear bells, don't use the front."

"Bells meaning what?" I asked.

She gave me a look like I'd asked what water meant to a fish. "Meaning run, girl. Same as everywhere else."

That night I sat by the window with Aldric's notes and the lighthouse in view across the harbour, its lamp turning slow and steady, and I tried to feel like a woman gathering the remnants of a dead court to save a kingdom. What I actually felt was tired, and damp, and increasingly certain that the quiet in those streets was the quiet of held breath.

Around midnight, Nessa crept into my room without knocking, which was becoming her signature move.

"It's the soldiers," she whispered. "Down at the tavern. I heard them through the floor. They're not staying the night." Her eyes were huge in the dark. "They're waiting for morning. They're going to do it at dawn, when everyone's at the water."

"Do what?"

"You know what."

I did know. I just hadn't let the knowing all the way in yet. There's a trick to survival — twelve years of practice taught me — where you keep the worst knowledge in a separate room of your head and visit it only when necessary. I'd built that room door by door, brick by brick, until I could walk past it in the dark without flinching.

Nessa stood there in the dark and kicked the door off its hinges.

"The lighthouse," I said. My voice sounded far away. "The token's under the lighthouse. If they burn the town—"

"Then we go now," she said. "Tonight. While they're drinking."

"And if they've posted watchers?"

"Then we go anyway." She said it simply, the way children say impossible things, because nobody has yet taught them that impossible things require hesitation.

I got Brann up. He didn't argue, which frightened me more than arguing would have. A man who has already lost a daughter knows the arithmetic of these nights, and he moved through the dark chandlery with a grimness I recognized from mirrors.

* * *

The lighthouse stairs were narrow, spiraling, and stank of old oil and older gull. Halfway up, Brann stopped at a seam in the wall, ran his thumb along it, and said, "Here. Stone doesn't match. See the mortar? Newer."

He pried it loose with a knife blade and his considerable opinion about walls. Behind the stone was a cavity, dry despite the sea air — oath-keeper's work, I'd learn later; Dovan Ree had sworn the wall to keep his hiding place safe, and the wall had kept faith for twenty years — and inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, lay the fourth token.

It was different from the others. Where Maren Salt's ring had breathed like the sea, this thing — a small iron key, plain, almost ugly — breathed like a held word. When I touched it I didn't feel anything so much as *understand*: the absolute, crushing clarity of every promise ever made to me and every promise I'd ever broken, laid out in a row like bodies.

"Oh," I said. "Oh, that's — that's a lot."

"Give it here," Brann said, and when I passed it to him his face went grey and he handed it back within four seconds. "No. You carry them. Apparently you're the one who gets to suffer."

Which is when the bells started.

Not church bells. Alarm bells, ragged, from the town below, and beneath them a sound I'll hear until I die: screaming, thin and high, carried off the water.

We went up the lighthouse stairs two at a time and burst onto the gallery, and Greyharbour was burning.

They hadn't waited for dawn after all. Or maybe dawn was just a formality and the drinking had simply gotten ahead of schedule. Torches had gone into the net-houses first, then the row houses near the quay — the poor streets, the streets closest to the water where the fishing families lived — and the wind off the sea was doing the rest, pushing the fire inward, house to house, street to street. Soldiers moved through the smoke with hooked poles, dragging people out of hiding and shoving them toward a line in the market square. Some went to the line. Some went to the flames. I watched a soldier decide which, for an old man who couldn't run fast enough, with the casual economy of someone sorting laundry.

"There," Nessa said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Oscar, there are people on the pier. They ran the wrong way. They're trapped between the fire and the water."

She was right. Maybe thirty souls clustered at the end of the long wooden pier, hemmed in by burning warehouses behind them and black water ahead, and the fire was coming along the pier itself now, eating the planks plank by plank.

Every instinct I owned — twelve years of them, well-fed and well-trained — screamed the same word. *Away.* Down the far side of the lighthouse, out the north road, gone, alive, safe, invisible. The instinct had kept me breathing since I was nineteen years old. It deserved some loyalty.

But I had Maren Salt's ring in one pocket and Dovan Ree's key against my breastbone, and the ring was *singing*. I swear to you it was singing. The tide-speaker's last argument, held in silver all these years: *the sea is not your enemy unless you treat it as one.*

And the key was saying something quieter. Saying: *a promise made in fear is still a promise.*

Saying: *you swore you'd gather them.*

I hadn't. I'd never actually said the words aloud. I'd let Aldric's dying hands press the sigil into mine and let everyone assume.

The key burned cold against my skin and I understood that it knew. Oath-keepers always know.

"Brann," I said. "The boats at the north dock. Get as many launched as you can. Nessa—" I looked at the girl, thirteen years old and already carrying more death than any child should, and I made myself say it: "You stay with me or you go with Brann. Choose fast."

Her chin came up in that way that was going to get her killed someday, probably gloriously. "With you."

"Then listen. The fire wants to spread. Fire is just a hunger, and hunger can be argued with." I pulled the ring from my pocket. "I'm going to try something I've never done, speaking a dead woman's gift through a dead woman's token, and it might not work, and if it doesn't we swim. Understood?"

"No," Nessa said honestly. "But go."

* * *

I want to tell you that I stood on the burning pier like a figure from a ballad, arms raised, hair streaming, and commanded the sea.

What actually happened was I ran down the lighthouse stairs, along the smoking quay with embers stinging my face, out onto the pier with the heat peeling paint off the posts around me, and I grabbed the rail and screamed at the water like a madwoman, because that's what it was. Swordspeech isn't spells. It's argument. You speak to things in the grammar of their own nature and you *insist*, and the insisting costs blood and breath and sometimes sanity. My mother's voice could make steel remember it was ore. Mine, when I let it out — which had been twice in twelve years, counting the fragment for Brann — could make iron obey me, briefly, expensively.

The sea was not iron.

But the ring was the sea's, in a way, and Maren Salt had spent a lifetime learning its grammar, and her token was warm in my fist like a hand gripping back, and so I opened myself to both — the ring's memory and my own voice — and I spoke to the water.

*I know you're vast,* I said. *I know I'm asking small. But there are thirty people on this wood and the wood is failing, and I am asking you to be their floor. One hour. One hour of holding, and I will never call you enemy again. I am the ninth seat of the Ninefold Court and I am asking, not commanding, because you deserve to be asked.*

The sea paused.

I felt it pause — felt the whole grey immensity consider me the way a mountain considers weather — and then the water rose. Not violently. Almost politely. It swelled up along the pilings, wrapped the pier's end in a deep cushioned swell that lifted the burning planks clear of the worst heat, and lay there, flat and solid as a road, while the trapped crowd stared at it in disbelief.

"GO!" I roared at them, in plain human speech, because miracles are wasted on the confused. "ONTO THE WATER! IT'LL HOLD! GO!"

They went. Fishermen first, hauling children, then the old man I'd seen sorted into the wrong pile, limping, weeping, stepping barefoot onto black water as if it were stone. The sea held. The sea held and held, cold and patient under their feet, while Brann's commandeered boats came around the breakwater one by one.

I paid for it, of course. Swordspeech always collects. By the time the last boat loaded, my nose was bleeding freely, my knees had the structural integrity of wet bread, and Nessa was holding me upright by the belt with both fists, dragging me backward off the pier as the fire finally ate through behind us.

"You did it," she was saying, half laughing, half sobbing. "You absolute lunatic, you *did it*—"

"We're not done," I managed, because the market square was still full of soldiers and the fullness of my heart did not extend to them.

* * *

Here is what I learned about fighting alongside a smith and a thief-child while bleeding from the nose: it works better than it should.

Brann fought the way he hammered — economically, brutally, with a total lack of interest in elegance. He waded into the square's edge and started breaking polearms and the men holding them, roaring something about Greyharbour, about his own hometown that he'd never see again, about all of it. And Nessa — Nessa stayed behind me, as agreed, but her little gift woke up anyway, sparks skittering off her fingers into the wet straw, and the fires that had been chasing the townsfolk *hesitated*, curled back, confused, and bought us the seconds we needed.

I didn't kill anyone. I want that recorded somewhere. I swung a boat hook, I broke a wrist, I used three words of swordspeech to weld a squad's boot buckles shut, which is exactly as ridiculous as it sounds and bought us ten glorious seconds of six armed men hopping in circles. But I didn't kill anyone, and whether that's virtue or cowardice I still haven't decided.

By full dark the soldiers had withdrawn to the headland to regroup, the town's center was ash, and perhaps two hundred people of Greyharbour huddled on the shingle beach and in Brann's borrowed boats, watching their homes burn and looking, in increasing numbers, at me.

I hated it immediately.

"You're the one," said the old man, kneeling in the sand in front of me, which made me want to sink through the shingle into the sea and keep going. "The water. You talked to the water. You're the Court."

"I'm nobody," I said. "I'm a charm-seller from Vell."

"You're the one the sea listened to," said a fisherwoman, arms around two children. "Nobody's talked to the sea in twenty years. My grandmother told stories. The Ninefold Court — the sea used to answer *them*."

"Well," I said, "it answered once. Under duress. With extensive bribery."

Nobody laughed. Two hundred exhausted, homeless people stared at me with a hope so heavy I could feel it physically, pressing on my shoulders like wet wool, and I understood with perfect clarity that something had just happened to me that I had not consented to.

They weren't looking at me as a woman anymore. They were looking at me as a direction. North, out of the ashes, toward the person the sea had carried their grandparents' stories about.

"They'll follow you now," Brann said quietly, beside me. He was cleaning blood off his knuckles with a rag, methodical, not looking at me. "That's how it starts. You pull one family out of a fire, and by spring you've got an army, and by summer somebody's put a crown on you whether you want it or not. I watched it happen to them." He nodded at the dark, at the idea of the nine thrones. "Watched it happen to all of them. Started exactly like this. Small. Somebody saved, somebody grateful."

"I don't want it."

"Neither did some of them." He tied off the rag. "Wanting has nothing to do with it. That's the trap. Belonging arrives before you're ready, and leadership arrives before you're asked, and one day you look up and people are dying for you and you can't send them home, because there isn't one anymore. You built them a reason." He finally looked at me, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before — not forgiveness, nothing so generous, but recognition. Grief recognizing grief. "Get used to the weight, girl. Or drop it now, tonight, and let them find someone else."

I looked out at the beach. The fisherwoman had made a space for her children against a dory's hull and was feeding them hardtack like it was a feast. The old man was telling the story already — I could see his hands moving, shaping the wave, shaping the woman who'd called it — and even now, even hours old, the story was getting bigger than the truth. That's what stories do. That's what I'd spent twelve years refusing to become.

Nessa appeared at my other side, soot-faced, holding out the token bag. "Maren's ring is warm," she reported. "And the key is... humming? Is humming bad?"

"Everything about this is bad," I said, and took the bag, and slung it over my shoulder where it belonged, where it had apparently always belonged, and hated how right the weight felt.

Four seats. Four voices. Five graves still waiting.

"Tomorrow we go north," I told the beach, loudly enough for the nearest fifty to hear, because apparently I was doing this now. "There are five more tokens and a crown that needs breaking, and I won't pretend the road is anything but ugly. Anyone who follows me follows into that. Nobody's swearing anything tonight. Sleep."

The old man smiled like I'd said something profound. The fisherwoman bowed her head. Somewhere in the dark, someone had already started calling me the Sea-Speaker, and the name would reach Vell before I did.

Nessa leaned against my arm, boneless with exhaustion, and murmured, half-asleep: "Told you we'd need the boats."

And in the token bag against my hip, Dovan Ree's iron key hummed against Maren Salt's silver ring, two dead voices keeping each other company, and I would have sworn — oath-keeper's witness — that the sound they made together was laughter.

Because the fifth vault, according to Aldric's notes, wasn't in a cellar or a chapel or a wall.

It was in the palace.
