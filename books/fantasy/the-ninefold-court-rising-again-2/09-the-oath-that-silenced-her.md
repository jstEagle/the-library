## 9. The Oath That Silenced Her

The fourth token had been a coin of green glass, warm as a living hand. The fifth was a sliver of bone that hummed when I held it, like a tuning fork remembering its note. But the sixth—the one Brann pressed into my palm in the grey light before dawn, his face carved from something older than grief—was different.

It was a ring.

Small. Silver worn to the color of rain. The kind of ring a woman might wear every day until it became part of her finger, and I knew whose finger it had been part of before I even touched it, because my mother's hands were the first thing I remembered and the last thing I dreamed.

"Where did you get this?" My voice came out flat. Careful. The voice of a charm-seller who has never wanted anything in her life.

"The Court's vaults weren't all hidden by knights," Brann said. "Some were hidden by mothers." He set it down on the table between us and stepped back like a man retreating from a lit fuse. "I found it years ago, in the wreckage under the palace. I kept it because I didn't know what else to do with it. Now I know."

Nessa was asleep against the far wall, curled around her own knees like the world might still be argued with if she just held herself tightly enough. She'd slept badly since the soldiers. We both pretended not to notice how she flinched at sparks now—flint striking steel, a log collapsing in the hearth. I'd talked her back from the edge with swordspeech humming through her blood, and neither of us had said one honest word about it since.

I picked up the ring.

It should have been cold. Everything else in the undercity was cold; the stone drank warmth like a debt collector. But the ring lay in my palm like a sparrow, small and impossibly alive, and I felt the memory inside it stir the way a sleeper stirs when you say their name.

"I don't have to do this tonight," Brann said. It wasn't kindness. It was procedure—he'd learned, somewhere in twelve years of hating himself, that memory-tokens don't care whether you're ready. "But you'll want to be sitting down."

I sat down.

* * *

The world went white, then wet, then warm.

I was standing in our old house above the chandler's shop, the one I hadn't thought about in twelve years because thinking about it felt like pressing on a bruise to see if it still hurt. It hurt. Candle-smoke and tallow and the particular smell of my mother's hair—rosemary oil, cheap, applied generously. Rain on the shutters. Night.

My mother stood at the table with her back to me, and she was younger than I remembered, or maybe I was older than I remembered being, and either way she looked tired in the way that women look tired when they've been carrying something heavy for so long they've forgotten it isn't part of their spine.

She was talking to someone I couldn't see.

"She's six," my mother said. "Six years old. You're asking me to bind a six-year-old."

A voice answered from the shadows by the hearth, and I recognized it with a jolt that nearly knocked me out of the memory: Ser Aldric Vane, whole and hale and thirty years younger, his armor dented but his voice steady as a plumb line. "I'm asking you to save her, Maren. There's a difference, and we both know which side of it we're standing on."

"They took the Vael family yesterday. All of them. Even the babe." Her hands were flat on the table, braced, as if the world might tip. "They burned them in the square and called it justice, and you come to my door asking me to cut my daughter's voice out of her."

"Not out. *Bound.* There's a difference there too."

"Is there?" She turned, and I saw her face, and I made a sound that no one in the memory could hear because I wasn't really there—I was a ghost haunting a night that had already happened. My mother's face. Gods. I had spent twelve years unable to picture it clearly, and here it was in merciless detail: the crease between her brows, the chapped lips, the eyes that were mine exactly, except hers were red-rimmed and mine were dry only because I'd forgotten how.

"She has the gift," Aldric said gently. "You know what that means now. You know what he's doing with them."

"He," meaning the king. Meaning the crown. Meaning the thing I would spend twelve years not knowing about while it wore a dead man's face and fed on my city.

"She's strong," my mother said, and there it was—not pride, not exactly. Terror wearing pride's clothes. "Stronger than me. Stronger than—" She stopped. "If I bind her, will it hold?"

"With the Last Oath? It will hold for a lifetime."

"And the cost?"

Aldric was quiet for a moment. In the hearth-shadow his face was unreadable, and I understood, watching him, that he had already decided what he was willing to lie about. "There is no cost," he said. "Only silence."

My mother laughed. It was an awful sound, short and cracked, the laugh of a woman who has run out of everything except the ability to recognize a lie. "Ser Aldric. I was the Court's last voice. Do you truly think I don't know what an oath costs?"

*I was the Court's last voice.*

The words went through me like a blade finding the gap in armor. Twelve years I'd believed my gift was some accident of blood, some stray ember. My mother hadn't just been gifted. She had been the ninth seat. The last voice of the Ninefold Court. And she had never told me, because telling me would have been the same as painting a target on my small back.

"She'll hate me," my mother said quietly. "When she's grown. When she wonders why she's empty. Why words feel like they're behind glass. She'll hate me for it."

"Yes," Aldric said. "Probably."

"Good," my mother said. "Hate keeps you looking. Hate means she'll ask questions someday, and questions are safer than answers. Answers get you burned."

* * *

The memory shifted the way memories do—no door, just a change of light—and suddenly I was in my own bedroom, in my own small bed, and I was six years old again and awake when I should have been asleep.

My mother sat beside me. She was holding the ring.

"Do you remember the game we play?" she asked. Her voice was gentle, the storybook voice, the goodnight voice. "The one where your words are birds?"

"You put them in a cage," I said, six years old, drowsy, trusting. "So the cats can't get them."

"That's right. And tonight we're going to build the cage for real. And it won't be a game anymore, and I need you to be very brave, my love, because building it will hurt, and after it's built you won't remember why it hurts. You'll just feel quiet. Can you be brave for me?"

Little-Oscar—me, her, the girl I had been—nodded. Six-year-olds are brave the way animals are brave: without understanding the bill.

What followed, I watched from outside myself, and I will not describe it fully because some things belong to the girl in the bed and no one else. I will say only this: my mother held my face in her hands and spoke words in a language I now understood was swordspeech—not the battle-cant, but its root tongue, the older speech beneath it, the one that doesn't command steel but commands the self. And the oath she spoke was long, and it was beautiful, and it was terrible, and every word of it was a nail.

And at the end of it, she said: *"By my name and by my seat, I bind your voice until you speak your true name freely, or until the crown itself calls it. Let silence keep you. Let silence save you."*

Until you speak your true name freely.

Or until the crown itself calls it.

I hung on those words like a drowning woman on driftwood, because I heard them, I *heard* them, the second clause, the one my mother must have hoped I'd never need to understand. The oath wasn't just a cage. A cage has bars and a lock and sits where you left it. This was a leash. It ran, thin and invisible, from the center of my chest all the way across the city to the throne room, and whatever wore the crown could pull it.

Could call my true name out of me like a fisherman hauling a hooked line.

Could find me. Anytime. Anywhere. Had perhaps always been able to, and simply hadn't bothered yet—or worse, had been waiting, patient as rot, letting me gather the tokens, letting me walk the road it wanted walked.

The memory ended. I came back to the undercity gasping, the ring clenched so hard in my fist that the band had bitten a line into my palm, and Nessa was shaking my shoulder saying my name—my false name, my safe name, *Oscar, Oscar*—and Brann was standing over me with a bucket of water he clearly hadn't decided whether to use.

"It's a leash," I said. My voice sounded scraped. "The oath. It's not just hiding her—it's a leash, and the crown holds the other end."

Brann went very still. "Maren wouldn't have—"

"She would." And the awful thing, the thing that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone, was that she *would*. Because my mother had been given two choices that night—bind her daughter or lose her to the pyre—and she had chosen the option with a flaw in it, a flaw she'd built in deliberately, a thread left loose so that someday, somehow, her daughter might follow it home. She hadn't muzzled me to erase me. She'd muzzled me the way you muzzle a dog you're smuggling past soldiers: praying it holds, knowing it might not, leaving one seam undone.

I thought about her hands on my face. I thought about the smell of rosemary oil. I thought about twelve years of wondering why words felt like they were behind glass, why I flinched from my own reflection some mornings, why I had built a whole life out of almosts and maybes and charms that worked by luck instead of truth.

I was furious at her. I was furious down to the marrow, furious enough to break things, furious that she'd decided for me, furious that she'd died before I could scream at her—

—and underneath the fury, so close to it that I couldn't tell where one ended and the other began, was something that broke my heart in half: she'd done it while crying. I'd seen it. In the memory, holding my sleeping face, speaking the nails into place, my mother had wept silently the entire time, tears running off her chin onto my blanket, and she hadn't stopped speaking, not once, not for a single word, because stopping would have meant starting over and starting over would have meant losing her nerve and losing her nerve would have meant losing me.

Tenderness and fury, braided together so tight I couldn't tell which was strangling which.

"She loved you," Brann said quietly. He'd lost a daughter too. He knew the arithmetic of these things—the terrible exchange rates parents pay. "That's what it was. However much it looks like a cage from in here."

"I know." I turned the ring over in my palm. Inside the band, so faint I'd missed it, was an inscription in the root-tongue. I read it aloud without thinking, translating the way my mother had taught me before the cage went up, the way you learn a thing so deep it survives its own burial:

*"For Oscarina. May she forgive me, or may she live long enough to be worth forgiving."*

Nessa made a small sound. Brann closed his eyes.

Oscarina. My true name, spoken in silver, carried twelve years in a thief's pocket, waiting. Not a secret anymore—not entirely—but still mine, still unspoken, still locked behind the last bar of the cage. Freeing it would mean breaking the oath. Breaking the oath would mean facing whatever pulled the leash.

And then, from somewhere far above us—up through the layers of the undercity, up through the market and the streets and the palace hill—a bell began to toll.

Not the hour bell. Not the alarm.

The calling bell. The one that hadn't rung in living memory, the one the old texts said was rung only when the crown summoned every bound thing in Vell to come home.

The leash, pulling taut.

Somewhere above us, the crown had learned my mother's name.
