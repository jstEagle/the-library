## 10. Hunters in the Undercity

The tunnels under Vell smell like everything the city wants to forget.

I've been down here three days now, and I've catalogued them all: tannery rot, grave-dirt, the sweetish reek of the fungus farms, and underneath it all, faint as a hymn through a wall, old magic. The undercity is soaked in it. Before the purges, this was where the Ninefold Court kept its roots—literally, I'm told. There are stones down here that still remember being sung to.

My gift remembers too. That's the problem with a suppressed talent: it doesn't die, it just gets hungry. Every hour in these tunnels it presses against the inside of my ribs like a cat locked in a box, and every hour I press back, because that's the arrangement. Serve, or be served. I made my bargain twelve years ago and I have renewed it every morning since, the way you renew any vow worth keeping—deliberately, with full knowledge of what you're choosing not to be.

Today's renewal is going poorly.

Because she's close. The swordspeaker. Oscar Underhill, charm-seller, ghost, the last heir of a dead court—and the one name on the crown's list that matters more than all the others combined. I've tracked her for two weeks across half the kingdom, following a trail that should have been invisible and wasn't, because here's the thing nobody tells you about people who hide: they get very good at hiding from everyone except themselves. She flinches at witchfinders. She walks wide around processions. And her magic leaks. Not much—a drop here, a shiver there—but to someone like me, someone who has spent twelve years learning to smell suppression because I live inside my own, she might as well have left a lantern burning in every room she passed through.

Right now the lantern is two hundred paces ahead of me, moving east along the Old Processional toward the flooded cisterns.

I signal my squad to hold. Four witchfinders, hand-picked for loyalty over sense, which in the crown's service is the same thing. They wait in the dark behind me like well-trained hounds, and I tell myself the familiar lie: *I'll take her alone first. Cleaner that way.*

It isn't cleaner. It's just mine.

* * *

I catch up to her at the edge of the cistern, where the tunnel opens into a vaulted dark and black water lies flat as a mirror. She's standing with her back to me, which no competent fugitive does, which means she heard me coming and decided to stop running.

Good. Running is exhausting for both of us.

"Captain Reyne," she says, without turning. Her voice is low and even, market-day pleasant, and I hate how much it sounds like mine. "You walk loudly for a woman whose whole job is quiet."

"I walk exactly as loudly as I intend." I stop ten paces back. Close enough to talk. Far enough that if this goes wrong, I'll have time to regret it properly. "Oscar Underhill."

"Now that's a name I haven't answered to in years."

"It's the name on your mother's petition. Filed the winter before the purge. I've read it eleven times."

That makes her turn. In the grey light seeping down through a grate somewhere above, I can see her face properly for the first time—not the charcoal sketch in the crown's files, but the living version, tired and sharp-eyed and younger than her reputation. There's ash dusting her sleeves. Salt in her hair. The coastal town, then. She'd pulled people out of that fire; my reports said so, written by men who couldn't understand why anyone would do that.

"You read petitions," she says. "For fun?"

"For ammunition." I keep my hands visible, away from my blade. Not mercy. Tactics. "Your mother tried to register your gift legally. A binding license, supervised practice, the whole apparatus. The Court would have approved it. The king refused." I let that sit between us. "She didn't muzzle you out of fear, Underhill. She muzzled you out of paperwork."

Something moves behind her eyes—grief, fury, recognition, all the things I've spent twelve years training out of my own face. "And you? What did they promise you, Captain? Or did you just wake up one day loving the smell of burning?"

"They promised me nothing." The truth comes out flat and ugly, the way truth does when you've polished it too long. "They offered me a choice. Serve, or be served. Do you know what 'served' means, in the crown's vocabulary? It means the way they served the Ashford family last month. Father, mother, two children. The pyre was lit before dawn so the smoke wouldn't trouble the morning markets."

"And you chose to light it."

"I chose to carry the torch," I say. "There's a difference. It's small. It's the only difference I have left."

She studies me the way she must study her charms—for flaws, for cracks, for the place where the luck runs out. "Why are you alone? Where's your squad?"

"Two hundred paces back, doing exactly what I told them." Which is true, and also a confession, and we both know it. A loyal witchfinder doesn't dismiss her squad to talk privately with the kingdom's most wanted woman. "I wanted to make you an offer before they arrive. The crown's offer. Kneel, and live."

"The crown doesn't let gifted people live."

"The crown lets *useful* gifted people live." I hear my own voice take on the cadence of the sales pitch I've rehearsed in the dark, and I despise it, and I deliver it anyway, because that's the job, because that's always been the job. "Registered, bound, leashed—but alive. You'd hunt your own kind, Underhill. You'd burn the ones who run. And in exchange, you'd never see the inside of a fire. Your friends in the undercity—the thief, the smith—they'd be left off the lists. That's the offer. It's generous. It's more than I got."

"And what did you get, Captain?" Softly. Too softly. "What did they give you for twelve years of carrying torches?"

The water behind her doesn't move. My gift does—it surges against its cage, and for one unguarded moment I almost answer honestly. *They gave me permission to survive. They gave me a reason for the hunger. They gave me a crown-shaped hole to pour myself into so I'd never have to look at what I actually am.*

"They gave me order," I say instead. "Vell burns fewer children than it used to. I count that as a victory. You'd understand if you'd ever had to choose."

"I did choose." Her voice doesn't rise. That's the terrible thing about her—that everything in her stays level, like deep water, like a drawn blade not yet swung. "Twelve years ago. I chose silence. I sold charms that worked by accident and I watched them drag an old woman through the market and I told myself hiding was the same as safety. So don't lecture me about choices, Captain. I know exactly what they cost. I'm paying the interest."

For a moment—one long, treacherous moment—we simply stand there, two women in the dark, mirrors angled at each other. Both of us buried our gifts alive. Both of us built lives on top of the graves. She dug hers up. I put a career on mine and called it service.

I could kill her now. Ten paces, one thrown knife, done. The crown would give me anything I asked. A title. Land. Maybe even the thing I've never said aloud in daylight: legitimation. Recognition. *Permission.*

Instead I hear myself ask, quietly, genuinely: "Does it ever stop hurting? Keeping it down?"

Her expression shifts—surprise, then something worse, something like pity. "No. But it stops being shameful, once you stop pretending it's a wound. It's just... you. Loudly, permanently you."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It is. It's also free."

Behind me, boots on stone. My squad, obedient, punctual, arriving exactly when I told them to—which means I have perhaps forty heartbeats before this becomes an arrest, or a fight, or something I can't take back.

"Kneel and live," I say one final time. "Or stand and burn. Those are the options, Underhill. I didn't write them. I just enforce them."

She looks at me for a long moment. Then she smiles—small, sad, absolutely certain.

"No," she says. "Those are *your* options. You keep offering them like they're the world's. But somebody wrote them, Captain. Somebody wearing a crown that hasn't aged in twelve years. Have you looked at him lately? Really looked?"

The words land harder than any blade could, because I *have* looked. I've stood in that throne room and felt his shadow move wrong and told myself it was candlelight. I've heard the crown speak in dreams I refuse to remember. And here is this woman, hunted and filthy and alone, saying aloud the thing I've been strangling in my chest for months.

"He's dead," she says gently, cruelly. "Isn't he. You know it. You've known it, and you've carried his torch anyway, because carrying it was easier than asking who you're really serving."

"Enough." My voice cracks on it. I hate that. I hate her for hearing it.

Forty heartbeats. Thirty. My squad will round the corner and find me standing ten paces from the target, talking, and either I arrest her or I explain why I didn't.

So I do the thing I will spend many sleepless nights examining afterward: I step aside.

Not far. Just enough. Just enough that the angle of my body, the tilt of my lamp, the half-second delay in my shout—all of it adds up to a corridor of shadow she can slip into, and she sees it, because of course she sees it, she's been reading people's cracks for twelve years professionally.

"Run," I tell her. "East tunnel floods at midnight. Don't be under it."

"Why?" she asks. And it's not gratitude in her voice. It's the question itself, honest and awful: *why are you doing this, when you'll do the opposite tomorrow?*

"One heartbeat," I say. "That's all I'm giving you. One heartbeat of not choosing them. Tomorrow I'll choose again, and I might choose wrong. But tonight—" I turn my face away, toward my approaching squad, toward the crown, toward everything I've fed myself for twelve years. "Tonight I want to know what it feels like."

She goes. Silent as her namesake, gone into the dark before my men round the corner, and when they find me I'm kneeling at the water's edge examining footprints that lead nowhere useful, and I report a lost trail, and my voice doesn't shake, and I add another morning to the ledger of renewals.

But walking back up through the tunnels, my gift presses against its cage, and for the first time in twelve years I don't press back quite so hard.

And in the dark behind my eyes, the crown whispers—patient, amused, *hungry*—and offers me a promotion.

I haven't said yes yet.

That word *yet* is going to keep me awake for the rest of my life.

* * *

Three days later, summoned again to the palace, I kneel on cold marble while the king—who has not aged, whose shadow bends toward the candles rather than away from them—looks through me with eyes like coins left on a dead man's face.

"Captain Reyne," the revenant-voice says, warm as a fever. "You found her. And you *let her go.*"

"I lost her, Majesty. There's a difference."

"There is indeed." The crown glimmers, and beneath the glimmer something turns over, slow and vast, like a leviathan deciding whether a minnow is worth swallowing. "Bring me the swordspeaker, and I will forgive the difference. Bring me the swordspeaker, and your gift will never be hungry again."

I bow my head. I feel the yes forming in my mouth like a stone.

And somewhere beneath the city, in tunnels that flood at midnight, a woman with ash in her hair is gathering nine names against the thing on this throne—and I realize, with a clarity that feels almost like hope and almost like horror, that I already know which side of this story I'm going to end up on.

I just don't know yet how much of me will be left when I get there.
