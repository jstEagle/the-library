## 11. The Sixth Seat Is Empty

The sixth vault was under a tannery, which felt like the Court's idea of a joke from beyond the grave.

Eight seats of the Ninefold Court had been hidden with ceremony—beneath chapels and ancestral estates, behind locks that sang when you touched them wrong. The sixth was beneath a vat of rotting hides in Sallowside, guarded by nothing but a smell that could strip paint and a door so ordinary I'd walked past it twice before Brann's notes made sense. *Where trust died, bury it where no one will look for honor.*

I stood in the dark with a lantern and tried to make my hands stop shaking. Nessa waited above ground because I'd asked her to, and she'd agreed too easily, watching me with those sharp thief's eyes that saw more than I wanted them to. She knew I wanted to be alone for this one. She didn't know why.

The truth was simple and I hated it: five tokens had felt like gathering. This one felt like digging up something that should have stayed buried.

The lock on the tannery door wasn't locked at all. It swung open at a touch, which was somehow worse than any ward would have been. The sixth bearer hadn't wanted to be found—or had wanted it so badly she couldn't bear to guard herself against it.

* * *

The vault was small. No throne-shaped alcove like the others, no carved seat waiting for its token. Just a stone shelf, a dead man's writing scratched into the wall, and a box of black iron sitting in the center of the floor where anyone could reach it.

I knelt. The lantern threw my shadow huge against the wall, over the scratched words. Old Vellish, the formal script they used for oaths and indictments. My mother taught me enough to read funeral inscriptions, and this was a funeral inscription if ever I'd seen one:

*I opened the gates. Let whoever finds this know what I bought with them.*

My name was Maren Solle. I held the Sixth Seat—the Seat of Thresholds. Doors, gates, crossings, all the ways in and out of a kingdom. For nine generations my line kept the borders honest and the city's ways open to the gifted and the plain alike. I was trusted with every lock in Vell.

I am the reason you can read this.

I sat back on my heels. The lantern hissed. Somewhere overhead, the tannery workers stamped and cursed, living their lives directly on top of the worst thing the Court had ever done.

"Go on," I said out loud, because the dark deserved it. "Tell me."

I put my hand on the iron box. The other five tokens had warmed at my blood like cats recognizing a hand. This one went cold. Cold as a well in winter, cold pulling at me through the cut I made on my thumb before I could think better of it—and then the memory took me the way the others had, except this time I'd have given anything to be anywhere else.

* * *

Maren Solle was forty years old and tired of being reasonable.

That's how it started. That's what nobody tells you about betrayal—it doesn't arrive wearing horns. It arrives wearing your own voice, saying *someone has to be practical here.*

I watched her walk the palace corridors at night, a broad woman with ink-stained fingers and the particular slouch of someone carrying weight she hasn't told anyone about. The king was dying—that much was true even then, though not the way the histories said. His body was failing and his fear was growing to fill the space his strength left behind. And he had come to her, quietly, alone, because he trusted the Sixth Seat more than any of the others.

*They mean to replace you,* he'd said. *All nine of you sit there deciding the kingdom's fate while I lie awake wondering when you'll decide mine. The Court was meant to advise kings, Maren. It has become a second crown. And a kingdom cannot wear two crowns.*

And she had believed him. That was the poison of it. Not ambition—not greed. She believed him because he was afraid, and because she was tired, and because half the Court's arguments did sound like power talking itself into permanence. She told herself she wasn't destroying anything. She told herself she was restoring balance. Balance was the whole doctrine of the Ninefold; surely unbalancing an unbalanced thing was balance too.

So on the night of the Long Equinox, when the Court gathered in their hall beneath the palace to renew the rite that bound magic to guardianship, Maren Solle left the western gate unlocked.

I stood inside her memory and screamed at her. *Don't. Don't do it.* But memory is a locked room and I was only a visitor, hammering on the glass while she walked away down the corridor with her keys heavy in her pocket and her heart doing the steady work of self-justification. *It will be a bloodless correction,* she told herself. *The king will curb their excesses. There will be negotiations. No one needs to die.*

Soldiers came through her open gate like water finding a crack.

What followed wasn't a negotiation. What followed was the purge's first night, and Maren heard it—heard everything—from her post at the thresholds, because the Seat of Thresholds hears every door. She heard the First Voice die trying to hold the hall doors shut. She heard the Third Seat beg, and the Fourth Seat fight, and the Fifth Seat run, and none of it mattered because she had already sold the only thing that mattered: the way in.

She ran to the king to demand he stop it. And I felt what she felt when she found him—not dying, not frightened, not even really there. A body on the throne with something already moving into it, something patient and hungry that had been waiting years for a gate to be opened, and had used her fear the way she'd used her keys.

The crown had never been the king's. The crown had been *waiting*. And it needed a traitor, because hollow things cannot open doors for themselves.

Maren Solle understood, in the last hour of her life, exactly what she had bought with her unlocked gate. She came down here, to the ugliest place she could find, and sealed her token in iron—iron, the one metal that mutes gift—and wrote her confession on the wall in a dead language, and sat down to wait for the soldiers who were already hunting every seat of the Court.

The last thing she thought, and I felt it land in my chest like a dropped stone, was: *Let it be found by someone strong enough to hate me properly.*

* * *

I came back to myself on the floor of the vault with my cheek against cold stone and the lantern guttering, and I lay there for a long time not crying, mostly succeeding.

Five tokens had been a quest. Clean lines. Dead heroes, stolen history, a crown that murdered the good and true. I'd built a story out of Aldric's notes and Nessa's grin and Brann's grief, and the story had a shape: *they were good, the king was a monster, restore what was broken.*

But the monster hadn't come from outside the walls. The monster had been *invited*, by the sixth-best person in the kingdom, on the worst night of the kingdom's life. The purge hadn't begun with the crown. It began with a woman who was tired and reasonable and certain she could control what she unleashed—which, I thought grimly, wiping my face, sounded familiar to approximately everyone I'd met lately, including a witchfinder captain and, if I was honest, a charm-seller who'd spent twelve years telling herself that hiding was neutral.

The Court hadn't been tyrants. That part of the story held. They'd been guardians, flawed and arguing and human, and they hadn't deserved what came for them. But they also hadn't been clean. Power never is. The rite they renewed every equinox bound magic to service—but binding is a kind of leash, and leashes are held by someone, and every guardian eventually wonders whether the leash would fit better around a different neck. Maren had wondered. The king's whisper had found exactly that wonder and pried it open.

And the revenant—Aldric had called it a hollowed thing feeding on bound magic, and I'd imagined it as a parasite that had taken the king. Wrong. It was born that night. Born from a broken trust, wearing a dead man's face, sustained ever since by the oath Maren broke. The crown didn't just feed on the city's magic. It fed on the *shape of that betrayal*, renewed nightly, the open gate remembered forever in the thing's hollow core. Which meant—

Which meant Reyne's records, if she'd dug deep enough, would show a king who stopped aging twelve years ago. Which meant whatever the crown was, it had been completing something for a very long time. Which meant the rite I was assembling token by token wasn't just restoration.

It was a closing of a gate.

I got up. My knees cracked. I picked up the iron box and it was light, absurdly light, and inside lay the sixth token: a key. Of course. A plain bronze key, worn smooth, the kind that opens a thousand ordinary doors. It didn't warm to my blood like the others. It just lay there, cold and honest, waiting to be carried by someone who understood what it had cost the last person who held it.

"I don't hate you properly," I said to the empty vault, to Maren Solle wherever she was. "I'm sorry. I think I understand you instead, and that's worse."

The wall-script caught my eye again as I turned to go. Beneath the confession, smaller, almost hidden—a final line I'd missed:

*If the crown still stands, it is because no one has dared ask it the question it cannot answer. Ask what the king swore. Ask what was never said aloud. The oath it feeds on has a hole in it, and the hole is shaped like a word.*

I read it three times. The hole is shaped like a word.

I climbed the stairs with the key cold against my sternum, and pushed open the tannery door into grey afternoon light and the reek of hides—and stopped, because Nessa wasn't alone.

She stood pressed against the wall with a boy of maybe fifteen holding her arm, both of them white-faced, and between them and the street stood two of Brann's people—one of the smith's journeymen, and a woman I'd seen once hauling crates in the undercity. Behind them, filling the alley mouth and spilling into the lane beyond, were thirty people. Forty. Dockhands and dyers and a grandmother with a bread knife, and old Tam with the ruined lungs who owed me two coppers for a cough-charm that had never worked.

They weren't a mob. Mobs surge. These were standing very still, all of them looking at the tannery door, at me, the way you look at a door you've decided to knock on even though you're afraid of who answers.

"They heard it," Nessa said quietly. "Last week, at Brann's forge. Your swordspeech. It carries, Oscar. It carries farther than you know." She swallowed. "They've been coming here since dawn. They say—" Her voice cracked and steadied. "They say if the Court's heir is gathering the seats, they want to stand with her. They want to *help*."

Forty faces in the stink of the tannery, and every one of them waiting for me to be worth following.

I looked down at the bronze key in my hand—the key of the woman whose reasonable, tired betrayal had killed them all once already—and I heard the wall-script again. *Ask what the king swore.*

There was a hole in the crown's oath, shaped like a word.

And now, apparently, there were forty people who thought I knew what to do next.
