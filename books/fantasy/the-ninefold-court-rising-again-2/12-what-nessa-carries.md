## 12. What Nessa Carries

I want it on the record that I never asked to be anybody's anything.

Not a thief's daughter — well, I did get that one by birth, no refunds. Not the Court's little spark-bearer. Not Oscar Underhill's shadow, her lookout, her girl-with-the-hands-that-itch. And certainly not the person standing in a burned-out granary outside Vell with six soldiers coming through the doors and something in my chest rising like floodwater behind a bad dam.

But let me back up, because stories that start at the worst part are usually lies told by people who want your pity.

Three days after Saltmere burned, we were moving north along the coast road, and I was learning what it meant to travel with Brann the smith, which mostly meant carrying things. Brann did not believe in walking light. Brann believed in iron. He had a mule named Duchess who had opinions about him that I shared, and between us we hauled his forge-tools, three days of salted fish, and a crate of half-finished charms Oscar kept fiddling with like a woman knitting in a house fire.

Oscar herself walked ahead, hood up, hands loose at her sides. I'd started watching her hands without meaning to. When she thought nobody was looking, they moved — small patterns in the air, like she was plucking strings only she could hear. Swordspeech, Brann called it. The old language of making and unmaking, spoken with fingers instead of mouth. Twelve years silent, and still her hands couldn't help talking.

"You stare at her like she's bread," Brann said, falling into step beside me.

"I stare at everyone."

"You stare at her like she's *bread you're not allowed to eat*." He said it kindly, which was somehow worse. "It's not hunger, girl. It's recognition."

"I don't have anything worth recognizing."

Brann grunted. Brann had a whole vocabulary of grunts, each one a small sermon. This particular one meant: *you are lying and I am too tired to argue.* He'd been doing a lot of that grunt since Saltmere. We'd pulled forty-one people out of the fire there. He counted them every night on his fingers, and every night he arrived at forty-one, and every night he looked like he'd hoped the number had changed.

The fourth vault was supposed to be under a chapel two miles inland. The chapel was now a black tooth in a field of ash, because apparently when the crown can't find a thing it burns everything around the thing until the difference stops mattering. Oscar stood over the wreckage for a long time with her jaw doing that thing it did when she was swallowing words instead of speaking them.

"Nothing left," she said finally.

"There'll be others," I said. "You've got five tokens. Five out of nine. That's—" I did thief-math, fast and cheerful, because cheer is also a kind of armor and I've worn mine so long I've forgotten it's there. "That's more than halfway. That's practically a majority. You could found a government."

"A government of dead people."

"Dead people are famously easy to govern."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was becoming my full-time occupation, harvesting those almosts, and I hated how much of my day it took up, and I hated more that I'd have done it for free anyway.

We made camp that night in the ruined granary — stone walls, good sightlines, a roof that was maybe thirty percent roof. Oscar set the five court-tokens in a circle on a flat stone, the way she always did, and I watched them do their slow breathing glow, and I felt my gift lean toward them like a dog toward meat it knows it shouldn't touch.

Here is what I haven't told you yet, because I've been telling this story the way I tell all my stories — sideways, with jokes bolted over the load-bearing walls:

My gift used to be nothing.

A candle-flame trick. A warmth in my palms on cold nights. I could make a dying ember remember it was fire. Street performers in Vell could do more with pigeons. For twelve years my entire magical output fit inside a matchbox, and I liked it that way, thank you very much. Small gifts don't get you burned. Small gifts get you warm hands and a place near the fire.

But since the night Oscar spoke swordspeech in Brann's shop and the city heard it — since that word went through me like a bell through water — my matchbox gift has been growing. Every day a little. Like it heard something bigger call its name and decided it was done being small.

Nobody else had noticed. Or if they had, they were being polite about it, the way people are polite about a cough that might be consumption.

I noticed. I noticed the way an addict notices, which is to say I noticed and lied about noticing, even to myself, especially to myself. Because here's the truth underneath the jokes: it felt *good*. The warmth in my palms wasn't warmth anymore. It was a tide. And tides don't care what they carry off.

*

They came an hour before dawn, which is professional hours, which meant these weren't garrison rabble. Six of them, moving quiet through the ash-field, and I only woke because my gift woke first — a cold prickle down my spine like someone walking over my grave before I'd gotten around to dying in it.

Six soldiers. One ex-smith with a hammer, one charm-seller with a sword she barely used, and me, whose greatest prior achievement was lifting a purse off a drunk bishop.

The arithmetic was not encouraging.

"They've found us," I whispered, shaking Oscar's shoulder. Her eyes opened already awake, which I later learned was just how she slept — one foot in the world, one hand on a blade.

"How many?"

"Six. Coming from the east door and splitting — two around the south wall."

Oscar was up and moving before I finished. She woke Brann with two fingers on his arm, and Brann came awake the way anvils come awake, all at once and with intent. No panic. No argument. They'd done this before, the two of them, some older version of this same bad morning, and there was a terrible comfort in watching people be competent at catastrophe.

"Duchess," Brann muttered, loading the mule with hands that never hurried and never stopped. "Out the west gap. Girl, you're on the mule."

"I'm on the—"

"You're on the mule."

Now, I have been underestimated by better men than Brann, and I know all the flavors of it. But this time the insult landed differently, because the shameful part was that he was right. My job, in any fight, had always been to be elsewhere during it. That was the whole architecture of my life: small, quick, gone.

So I took the mule's lead. And that is why I was standing in the west doorway when the seventh soldier stepped out of the dark directly in front of me.

Seventh. There were supposed to be six. Thief-math, my pride and joy, my cheerful armor — wrong by exactly one man with a crossbow.

He saw me. I saw him. Duchess saw him and, traitor that she was, shied sideways and ripped the lead rope out of my hand, leaving me alone in a doorway with a loaded crossbow and a tide rising in my chest.

"Hands," he said. "Where I can see them."

And here is where I should tell you I raised my hands. Here is where the sensible version of me, the version that survived twelve years in Vell by being smoke, puts her palms out and says something disarming and lives to make jokes about it later.

Instead, I looked at the crossbow, and I looked at the granary behind me where Oscar was buying our escape with steel and fury, and I felt the tide rise past my wrists, past my elbows, up into the place where I keep the parts of myself I don't show anyone — and I thought, with a clarity that still makes me sick to write down:

*I'm tired of being small.*

He fired. I'm still not sure of the order of events. Some part of me wants to say I moved faster than the bolt, but that's a flattering lie and I promised myself I'd stop telling those. What actually happened, as best as I can stitch it together: the bolt came at my chest, and my gift came out of me like a wave hitting a seawall, and the two met in the space between us.

The bolt stopped. Hung. Turned to slag mid-air, dripping orange onto the frost.

The soldier looked at the molten metal raining between us. Then he looked at me. Then he did the bravest or stupidest thing available to him, which was to draw his sword and charge.

And I — Nessa, pickpocket, matchbox witch, girl who once apologized to a cat for stepping on its tail — reached out with both hands and *unmade him.*

I didn't burn him. Fire would have been cleaner. I reached into the warm living machinery of him — the breath, the blood, the stubborn animal hum of a heart doing its work — and I remembered it differently. I remembered it as stillness. As rest. As stopped.

He fell the way clothes fall when there's no one in them anymore.

The silence afterward was enormous. It had edges. I stood in the doorway with my hands still raised, still open, still reaching, and the tide didn't go back down. That was the thing nobody warns you about. I'd expected release. Instead the power stood there inside me like a guest who's decided the house is theirs, turning in a slow circle, admiring the rooms, wondering aloud what else it could take apart. The dead soldier's warmth was fading out of my palms and I wanted more of it. That's the sentence I keep circling. *I wanted more.* Somewhere in me, under the horror, a voice with my own face was asking: what else could I stop? What else could I remember into rest? How big could I get?

The granary went quiet too. Oscar came out of the dark with blood on her sleeve that I prayed wasn't hers, saw the body, saw me, and stopped like she'd hit glass.

"Nessa," she said carefully. Carefully, the way you speak to someone standing on a ledge. "Hey. Look at me."

"I'm fine," I said, and my voice came out wrong, layered, like two speakers playing the same words slightly out of sync. The power talking over me. Or through me. Hard to say whose voice was whose anymore. "See? Fine. He was going to shoot me. It was self-defense. It was—"

"It was yours," Oscar said. "It counts. All of it counts." She took one step closer. The tide in me rose to meet her, hungry, and I watched her see that. Watched her read it in my stance, my eyes, whatever my face was doing. And then she did something I will think about for the rest of my life.

She raised her hands. Fingers moving. Speaking to me in swordspeech.

I don't know the language. I want to be clear about that. Twelve years in Vell's gutters teaches you cutpursing and lying and which drains are walkable, not ancient tongues of making and unmaking. But I didn't need the grammar. The words weren't information. They were *shape* — and my gift recognized shape the way a drowning woman recognizes a rope, without translation, without permission, without any dignity whatsoever.

Later, Oscar told me what she'd said. Three phrases, repeated, cycling, her fingers never stopping while she talked me down in ordinary speech over the top of them:

*Small. You are small. Be small.*

Which sounds cruel until you understand that she wasn't diminishing me. She was giving me back the matchbox. She was saying: *this power is bigger than you right now, and the only way to survive it is to be smaller than it, humble before it, until you grow a soul big enough to hold it.* She was handing me my old cage and calling it a shelter, because tonight, it was.

"Talk to me," she was saying, closer now, close enough that I could see her hands hadn't stopped their patient spelling. "Tell me something true and small. Anything."

"He wet himself," I said. The soldier. It was true; the dead do that. "When he died. Nobody ever puts that part in songs."

"Good. Keep going."

"He had straw on his boot. He was a farm boy. Somebody's son is going to not come home and it's going to be my fault and I *liked* it, Oscar, for one second I liked it—"

"I know." Her voice cracked on it, just barely, just enough to let me hear the twelve years underneath. "I know exactly how much you liked it. That's why I hid for twelve years, Nessa. That feeling is the door, and everything ugly in the world walks through it wearing your face." Her hands spelled on. *Small. You are small.* "But it isn't you. Say it. Say it's not you."

"It's not me."

"Louder. Make yourself believe it, because the power won't wait for you to catch up."

"It's not *me*!"

And the tide broke. Not gently — there was nothing gentle in it — it went out of me in one long shudder, down through my feet into the frozen ground, and I dropped to my knees in the doorway next to a dead man I'd made, and Oscar knelt with me and held the back of my neck the way you hold someone who might fly apart, and we stayed there while the sky did its gray-to-gold business overhead like none of it mattered.

Somewhere in the middle of it Brann appeared, looked at the body, looked at us, and said nothing. His silence was its own kind of kindness. He simply picked up the soldier's crossbow, unloaded it, and set it aside with the respect you give a tool, not a threat.

*

We buried the farm boy at dawn because Brann insisted and Oscar agreed and I owed him that much. Brann dug; I couldn't make my hands work a shovel. Oscar stood at the head of the grave and said words over him — not prayers, she doesn't pray, but names. "Somebody's son," she said. "Straw on his boots. Scared enough to wet himself. He came hunting witches and died at the hands of one, and neither of those facts cancels the other." Then she filled in the grave herself, first shovel-full alongside Brann, so I wouldn't carry the digging on top of everything else.

I sat against Duchess afterward and shook for a while. The dignified account would be "processing grief." The accurate account is that my body had discovered what my gift had spent, and presented the bill.

Oscar sat down next to me. Close enough that our shoulders touched.

"I saw you," I said. "In the doorway. When you looked at what I did. You weren't scared *of* me. You were scared *for* me. Why? You've killed. You told me about Saltmere."

"Killing with a sword is a conversation between two people," she said. "What you did was a monologue. There was no one to answer you back, no risk, no cost except the ones you choose. That kind of power doesn't ask questions. That's what frightened me." She turned her hand over, palm up, showing the faint scars across it where swordspeech had burned its way out of her over the years. "Twelve years ago I stood where you're sitting, and I liked it too. For about a minute. And then I spent twelve years paying for the minute." She looked at me straight on. "I will not let you spend twelve years, Nessa. Whatever it costs me."

"That's a stupid promise. You've known me a month."

"Yes," she said. "And look how much trouble you've caused already."

I laughed, and it hurt, and laughing while it hurts turns out to be its own kind of magic — the cheap kind, the human kind, the kind I'd recommend to anyone.

Brann called from the road that the mule was loaded and the coast was clear, and we got moving, because staying near graves is bad for the appetite and worse for the morale.

I walked at the rear, as usual, keeping watch on our backtrail. And it was there, in that ordinary duty, in the plain work of watching a road, that I saw it: far behind us, at the edge of the ash-field, a single rider who didn't ride like a scout or a courier or a farmer. She rode like someone reading the ground. Like someone following a thread only she could see — a thread, I realized with a coldness that had nothing to do with my gift, that led from a dead farm boy's boots straight back to us.

Witchfinder grey. Moving unhurried, the way you move when you already know where the story ends.

I turned to warn Oscar, and found her already looking back at me, her expression telling me she'd seen it too — and that whatever was coming, it had been coming for longer than either of us knew.
