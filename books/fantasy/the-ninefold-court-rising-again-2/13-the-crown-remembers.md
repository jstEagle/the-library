## 13. The Crown Remembers

The crown had begun to whisper to me in my own voice.

That was the part I couldn't tell anyone, though telling anyone would have required having anyone, and Captain Ilsa Reyne had spent eleven years making sure she had no one. A witchfinder with friends is a witchfinder with a weakness, and the crown's inquisitors—my inquisitors, I corrected myself, *my* witchfinders, as if ownership made it better—assembled their dossiers on each other with the same diligence they applied to heretics. I knew which of them drank. Which of them prayed too loudly. Which of them had once wept over a pyre and sworn it was smoke in his eyes.

None of them knew that when I was alone in my quarters at the Bastion, I took off my gloves and let a candle flame lean toward me like a cat toward a warm hand.

None of them knew that the crown whispered.

It didn't speak words, exactly. Words were what it used on the king, or so I imagined—I'd only been summoned before him four times in three years, and each time I'd come away with the sensation of having been read rather than received. With me it worked differently. It worked through me. It would be midnight, and I'd be sharpening a blade or copying an order, and I'd hear myself thinking thoughts that weren't shaped like mine. *The girl in the undercity district has a gift,* my own mind would observe, calm as a ledger entry. *The baker's son on Corrow Street hums when he kneads. The widow Tessel's hands heal what they touch, slowly, badly—but they do.*

I had never noticed any of those things. But once the thought arrived wearing my voice, I could not unhear it, and within a week there would be an order on my desk bearing my own seal, instructing me to investigate precisely those addresses.

I told myself it was instinct. Good witchfinders developed instincts; that was why they were good. I told myself the crown merely *amplified* me, the way a lens amplifies light, and that the light was mine to begin with.

I told myself a great many things at midnight. That was the hour when telling-myself was most effective, because the alternatives were not sleeping, and not sleeping led to listening, and listening led to places I did not go.

* * *

The summons came on the morning after the Saltmarket fire, delivered by a boy who wouldn't meet my eyes, which meant the palace clerks had already decided what the summons was about before I read it. *His Majesty requires the presence of Captain Reyne. Immediately. Alone.*

*Alone* was new. The first three audiences had been ceremonial—the crown parading its witchfinder captain before the court like a well-trained hawk. The fourth had been different: the king alone behind a screen of shadows, asking questions about a swordspeaker rumor in the undercity, his voice coming out slightly wrong at the ends of sentences, like a musician playing slightly behind the beat.

This fifth time, the throne room doors opened before I knocked.

"Captain." The word came from the king, but the pause before it belonged to something else. "You have failed to deliver the swordspeaker."

"I have located her," I said. "She moves through the undercity. She's gathered allies—a smith, a thief-girl, possibly others. I will have her."

"You will have her soon." He sat very still on the throne, stiller than any living man sits. Kings fidget. Kings shift their weight, scratch, adjust, perform the human business of occupying a body. This one simply occupied his, the way furniture occupies a room. His face hadn't changed in—I did the arithmetic without wanting to—years. I'd assumed good health, good blood, the preservation of wealth. Wealth preserved everything else. Why not faces?

"The crown grows hungry," he said, and then, in a different cadence, one I recognized with a lurch because it was almost my own: "Bring her, and you will never burn. Bring her, and the gift you hide beneath your gloves will have a name, a place, a law. Bring her, and you will be reasonable *forever*, Captain Reyne."

My hand went to my glove before I could stop it. A tell. In front of the throne, a tell.

"How—" I began.

"We see everything the crown touches," he said, and smiled, and the smile arrived a half-second late, like thunder after lightning. "Go. Soon, Captain. The city is nearly ripe."

I walked out of the throne room on legs that behaved admirably, down stairs that behaved, across courtyards that behaved, all the way back to the Bastion where I shut my office door and stood with my back against it and shook for the length of ten slow breaths.

Then I did what I always did with fear. I turned it into work.

* * *

Here is the confession I have never made aloud: I became a witchfinder because I am gifted.

Not powerfully. Not beautifully. When I was nine years old, my mother caught me holding a kettle of boiling water steady with my bare palm—not unharmed, just *unbothered*, the heat pooling against my skin like a polite guest—and she went white as flour and beat me for it, and then wept, and then taught me the two rules of Vell: hide it, and hate it. Hate it loudest. The safest place under a purge is at the front of the mob.

So I hid it, and I hated it, and I rose, and somewhere along the way the hating stopped being a performance and started being architecture. You build a life out of a lie for long enough and the lie becomes load-bearing. Pull it out and the whole house comes down on whoever's standing inside.

I told myself I was the reasonable one. Every purge needs its reasonable ones—the officers who insist on evidence, the captains who demand trials before torches, the woman who spares a child here and there when no one's watching, sliding a boy named Tam out of a condemned house through a cellar hatch because his gift was small and his eyes were his grandmother's and someone, once, must have done the same for me. I kept a private ledger of my mercies. I balanced it against my fires and called the account honest.

Standing in my office with my heart still hammering from the throne room, I looked at that ledger properly for the first time in years.

It did not balance. It had never balanced. A mercy is not a payment; it's a bribe you pay yourself.

And the crown knew about my gift. The crown had known, and had said nothing, for years—had let me serve and burn and serve and burn while it sat on its secret the way a lender sits on a debt. That wasn't tolerance. That was a collar being fitted slowly, link by link, so the wearer never feels the weight until the leash snaps taut.

*Soon, Captain.*

I pulled off my glove and held my palm over the cold grate until a thread of warmth answered it, small and stubborn, twelve years suppressed and still alive, like a coal banked under ash. My gift was the same age as my service. Both of them had been waiting.

"Right," I said to the empty room, in my own voice, and for once I was certain it was mine.

Then I went to find proof. Because if I was going to commit treason, I intended to do it with documentation.

* * *

The palace archive sits beneath the west wing, three floors down, and is guarded by two men who owe me favors—one because I lost his brother's file, one because I found it again when it suited me. I signed the ledger with my own name. Treason with documentation, as promised. If this went wrong, I wanted history to record that Captain Reyne had at least been thorough.

I told the guards I was auditing the reign's financial records for the treasury commission. Nobody audits anything in Vell, so the lie was flawless.

The archive smells of dust and tallow and old decisions. The personal records of the royal household are kept in the sealed eastern stacks, accessible only by writ of the Lord Chamberlain—which is why I went in through the maintenance gallery instead, the narrow brick passage built for the people who used to tend the palace's heating vents back when palaces admitted they needed tending. I had walked that gallery twice before, both times hunting evidence against others. It is remarkable how much of my profession is simply knowing which corridors everyone forgets.

I wanted the physicians' rolls. Royal physicians keep the most honest records in any kingdom—they are paid to notice, sued if they don't, and hanged if they flatter wrong. If the king had aged even one day in the last decade, a physician would have noted it, because a physician who doesn't note the king's aging eventually notes the inside of his own cell.

The rolls lived in a run of grey boxes marked by year. I pulled the oldest first and worked forward, kneeling on the cold stone with a hand-lantern, reading entries in a succession of cramped, careful hands:

*Year of the Long Frost. His Majesty in excellent humor, appetite robust. Examined for the traditional complaint; prescribed the usual. No change of note.*

*Year following. His Majesty in excellent health. Remarkably so.*

*Year following that.* Remarkably so *again, and the handwriting has begun to press harder into the page, as if the physician were digging in.*

Then a gap. Then a box whose label had been scraped clean, and inside it, loose pages that should never have been together:

A death certificate. Dated twelve years ago. Cause of death left blank, cause of death *left blank*, which in royal medicine means either poison or politics, and the signature at the bottom was Physician-Attendant Corvel, who I knew had retired to a country estate, and whose retirement, I now understood, had been purchased rather than earned.

Beneath it, a memorandum in the Chamberlain's hand, unsigned, undated, but the paper matched the certificate's stock:

*The succession is unsettled and the realm fragile. The crown cannot pass. The crown will not pass. Arrangements continue. Speak of this to no one. There is nothing to arrange that the crown does not arrange itself.*

And beneath that, a final sheet—not a document, a scrap, the kind a clerk uses for practice—and on it, in a shaking hand, the same sentence written four times as if the writer couldn't believe it either:

*The king is dead. The king is dead. The king is dead. The king is dead.*

Twelve years ago.

Twelve years ago, a king died in this palace and something decided the paperwork was optional. Twelve years ago, something put on the crown and sat down and began issuing orders, and the orders grew hungrier, and the purges began, and a woman named Underhill took her daughter somewhere far from Vell and bound the child's voice with an oath so deep the child grew up believing her own silence was her nature.

Twelve years. The swordspeaker had been hiding exactly as long as the corpse had been reigning. Two fugitives from the same night, running in opposite directions—one into silence, one into service—and every fire I'd lit in between had been lit by the thing that killed us both, in a sense, on the same winter's night.

I knelt there among the grey boxes and laughed. It came out wrong, thin and airless, the laugh of a woman discovering the floor of her entire life had been a stage.

Every heretic I'd burned. Every family. The child I spared, weighed against the children I hadn't. All of it in service of a dead man's chair, warmed by something that ate loyalty and excreted orders. I had told myself I was the reasonable one. The reasonable one. Reasonable people check their premises occasionally. I had checked mine never.

I copied the certificate. I copied the memorandum. My hand shook on the fourth line of the scrap—*the king is dead*—and I let it. Some things should be written shakily.

Then I heard the door at the top of the stack stairs open.

* * *

I did not panic. Panic is for amateurs and honest people. I closed the box, set the lantern behind me, and stepped into the shadow between the shelves with my copied pages folded flat against my ribs, and I waited.

Footsteps. Two sets. Clerks' soft shoes and boots with military heels. Voices, low.

"—the Chamberlain wants the eastern stacks cleared by week's end. Everything older than the accession burned."

Burned. Of course. The crown was tidying. The crown had realized, perhaps, that its Captain Reyne had gone quiet and thoughtful lately, and thoughtful servants get audited too.

"And the physicians' rolls?"

"Especially the physicians' rolls."

They passed within arm's reach of me. One of them carried an empty sack. They moved on toward the far stacks, and I slipped out the maintenance gallery with my heart going like a drum in a procession, and I climbed, and I walked out into the evening streets of Vell, and I stood in the crowd outside the Bastion gates breathing ordinary air and understanding that I had perhaps three days before the crown finished eating its own history—and that whatever window I had for treason was the same window the crown had for finding me.

I should go to the undercity. That was the thought that arrived, unwrapped, uninvited, wearing my voice but not my hesitation. The swordspeaker. Oscar Underhill, heir of the Ninefold Court, currently hiding in the tunnels beneath the city I had spent a decade setting alight from above. She would kill me. She ought to. And yet.

And yet she was the only person alive with a reason to want the crown destroyed more than I did, and unlike me, she hadn't spent twelve years helping it.

I turned the copied pages over in my coat one more time. *The king is dead. The king is dead.* Four times in a dead clerk's shaking hand, and now a fifth time, alive, in mine.

Tomorrow, I would take the maintenance galleries down. Tomorrow, I would find the tunnel entrances the witchfinder maps pretended not to show. Tomorrow, I would walk into the dark under Vell and offer the last swordspeaker the one thing I had left that was worth anything: the truth, delivered by the hand that had done the burning.

Assuming she didn't kill me on sight.

Assuming I deserved better than that.

Assuming, most frightening of all, that when I finally stood in front of her and said *I can help you*, some buried, banked, twelve-years-suppressed ember of me wouldn't mean it as a bargain—but as an apology.

I pulled my gloves on tighter and went home to write my own name in a place the crown would never think to look.
