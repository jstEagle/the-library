## 14. The Gathering of the Nine

There are three ways into the palace, according to Brann, and two of them involve bribing men who would sell their own mothers for a cask of wine. The third involves a drainage grate behind the kitchens that smells like the entire city's regrets.

We take the third. Of course we take the third.

"Charming," Nessa says, hauling herself up through the slime-slick tunnel, her voice echoing off stone that hasn't heard an honest word in years. "I've been in gutters with better manners."

"You *are* a gutter thief," I point out.

"I was. Now I'm a revolutionary. There's a difference. Revolutionaries get killed with dignity."

Brann grunts from behind us, which is his way of laughing, or praying, or wishing he'd stayed in his forge hating me in peace. He carries the tokens wrapped in wool and oilcloth against his chest, the way you carry something alive. Seven of them now, recovered from seven vaults across half the kingdom—each one a small dead weight that hums when my blood gets near it. The eighth we found shattered in a riverbed outside Saltmarsh; I keep its pieces anyway, because I have learned this year that broken things are not the same as finished things.

The ninth token—the Court's own seal, the one Aldric died pressing into my hand—we have carried since the beginning. It has never once gone quiet. Some nights I swear it counts my heartbeats, patient as a creditor.

The drainage passage climbs, which offends me on principle. Sewers should flow downhill toward freedom, not up into the belly of the palace. But climb it does, and then there is a door—a plain iron thing with no lock, because who locks a door to nowhere—and Brann sets his shoulder to it, and the dark beyond breathes out at us.

Cold. Old cold. Stone-cold.

And underneath it, faint as a memory of a bell: singing.

"Do you hear that?" Nessa whispers.

"No," says Brann.

I say nothing, because what I hear is swordspeech—not words yet, but the shape of them, the way you hear a crowd before you hear any single voice. Twelve years I kept my gift muzzled, and now it rises in me like a dog scenting home, straining toward that buried song.

*Steady,* I tell myself. *It's just a room.*

It is not just a room.

* * *

The hall beneath the palace was never on any map I was raised on. The histories they taught us in Vell said the Ninefold Court met in splendor above ground, in a hall of glass and gold, until the righteous king burned it down. What we stand in now is nothing of the kind.

It is a circle. Nine thrones set in a ring around a floor of pale stone, no dais, no elevation—no throne higher than another. The ceiling has fallen in one place, letting in a shaft of grey light from somewhere far above, and dust hangs in it like the whole room is holding its breath. The walls are carved floor to vaulting with names. Thousands of names. Not kings and heroes—I look for the familiar ones and find none—but small names. Miller's names. Midwife's names. Names of people who were never written down anywhere else, and someone thought they should be.

"They weren't rulers," Nessa says quietly. She's stopped at the ring's edge, suddenly afraid to step onto the pale floor. "Everyone said—they sat above the law, they—"

"Everyone said a lot of things." My voice comes out rougher than I mean it to. "Look at the floor, Nessa. No steps. Nobody sat higher than anybody. That's the whole point."

Brann walks the circle slowly, touching each throne as he passes. He was the Court's smith once; he knew these seats when they held living people. His hand lingers on the seventh, and something crosses his face that I pretend not to see. His daughter's name is carved on the wall somewhere in this room. I felt it when we entered—a tug in my chest, like a hook set gently in me. The hall knows its dead. It is simply waiting to see if we do.

"Well," I say, because someone has to, and apparently it's always going to be me. "Let's wake them up."

* * *

Setting the tokens on their thrones should feel ceremonial. Mostly it feels like setting out dishes for guests who are going to criticize your cooking.

Each throne has a hollow in its armrest, shaped to its token. Brann made these—he admits it grudgingly, in the way he admits anything—with his own hands, forty years ago, and his fingers still remember the fit. The first token settles into its cradle with a sound like a struck coin, and the pale floor under our feet warms by exactly one degree.

By the fifth, the singing I heard in the tunnel has resolved into something almost like speech. By the seventh, the dust in the light-shaft has begun to move against the draft, drifting inward, toward the circle, the way smoke leans toward a fire.

Nessa grabs my sleeve. "Oscar."

"I see it."

"The stones are—"

"I see it."

The floor of the hall is not pale stone. Or rather, it is, but pale stone over something else, and the something else has started to show through—veins of light spreading from each throne like frost blooming across a window, reaching for the others, reaching for the ninth seat where I stand with the Court's seal sweating in my grip.

"Last chance to walk away," I say. Neither of them moves. Brann folds his arms. Nessa gives me a look that says she'd sooner die in an interesting place than a boring one, which is, I suppose, why I love her.

I set the ninth token in its hollow.

The hall inhales.

Every vein of light flares at once, every name on the walls brightens like coals breathed upon, and the singing becomes voices—nine of them, layered and distinct, rising out of the thrones themselves, out of the stone, not *in* the room but *of* it, the way heat is of a forge. They do not appear. There are no ghosts, no figures, no beloved faces. The dead stay dead. What remains is their voices in the stone, the way a bell keeps the shape of every note it ever rang.

The first voice comes from the eastern throne, deep and slow, like bedrock settling.

**WHO SPEAKS THE RITE?**

My mouth goes dry. "Oscar Underhill," I manage. "Daughter of—" and I stop, because the true name catches in my throat like a fishhook. The oath. Always the oath. "Oscar Underhill," I finish, smaller.

**UNDERHILL,** the voice repeats, and there is something in the way it says it—recognition, and grief, and a question mark hanging off the end like a loose thread. **THE LAST VOICE'S CHILD. GROWN NOW.**

"Yes."

**GROWN IS NOT READY.** A pause like tectonic patience. **BUT THE CROWN MOVES, AND WE ARE WHAT REMAINS. SPEAK THE OPENING, SWORDSWITCHER. LET US SEE IF YOUR MOTHER TAUGHT YOU ANYTHING WORTH BURYING HER FOR.**

So I speak it. I don't know where the words come from—except that I do know, they come from under my skin, from the oath-shaped silence my mother carved into me twelve years ago, from the place where my gift has been living all this time like a tenant paying rent in whispers. Swordspeech, spoken aloud in a dead tongue for a dead council, and the hall takes each word and rolls it around like wine.

When the opening ends, the silence afterward is enormous.

Then the second voice speaks—from the south, quicksilver, amused even in death:

**THE SMITH'S HANDS SET US HERE AGAIN. GOOD. TELL HIM HIS JOINTWORK STILL HOLDS.**

Brann makes a sound like a man swallowing a shout. "Forty years," he rasps, "and they're critiquing my joinery."

**TELL HIM ALSO,** the voice adds, gentler, **THAT WE KNOW WHY HE STOPPED BUILDING.**

He turns away. I let him.

The third voice is a woman's, worn smooth as sea glass, and it speaks directly to Nessa, who jumps a foot in the air. **LITTLE SPARK. YOU SHOULD NOT BE ABLE TO HEAR US.**

"She hears fine," I say.

**THEN SHE HAS MORE IN HER THAN SHE KNOWS, AND THAT WILL COST SOMEONE. EVERYTHING DOES.** The voice turns to me, and I feel it settle on me like a hand. **WE HAVE WATCHED YOU CARRY OUR TOKENS, UNDERHILL. SEVEN VAULTS. TWO BURIALS OF YOUR OWN TEARS. YOU THINK YOU HAVE BEEN COLLECTING RELICS.**

"Haven't I?"

**YOU HAVE BEEN ASSEMBLING A DEBT.** And now all nine voices speak at once, not in unison but in accord, the way a chord is many notes agreeing: **THE RITE THAT CAN BIND OR BREAK THE CROWN REQUIRES NINE LIVING VOICES TO COMPLETE IT. WE ARE NOT ALIVE. WE CAN ONLY LEND THE FRAME. THE STRENGTH MUST COME FROM THE LIVING—AND THE LIVING DO NOT GET TO CHOOSE WHETHER THE PRICE IS FAIR.**

The warmth drains out of the floor so fast I feel it in my teeth. "Price," I repeat. "What price?"

**WE DO NOT SET THE PRICE,** says the first voice, almost kindly, which is worse. **THE RITE DOES. AND THE RITE KNOWS WHAT EACH OF YOU HOARDS MOST CLOSELY.**

The veins of light pulse once, slow, like a heartbeat deciding whether to continue. In the shaft of grey light, the dust has stopped moving entirely, suspended, listening.

Nessa's hand finds mine. Her fingers are ice. "Oscar," she whispers, "what do we hoard?"

I think of Brann's forty years of unforgiveness, heavy enough to forge armor from. I think of the spark in Nessa that has grown every week since Saltmarsh, hungry and bright and more than she can hold. I think of a name I haven't spoken in twelve years, folded up small and hidden so deep that sometimes I forget it isn't mine to lose.

"We'll pay it," I hear myself say. "Whatever it is. We'll pay."

**NO,** the nine voices say together, and for the first time they sound less like judges than like mourners. **THAT IS WHAT THE LAST COURT SAID TOO.**

The lights dim to embers. The thrones wait. Somewhere far above us, through fallen ceilings and guarded halls, a crown that eats cities shifts on a dead man's head—and I understand, finally and completely, that we have not gathered the past to help us.

We have invited it to collect.
