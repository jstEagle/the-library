## 15. A Price for Every Voice

The dead, it turns out, are terrible negotiators.

I'd imagined—when I set the nine tokens on their thrones in that drowned hall beneath the palace, when I spoke the opening words of a rite I'd only half-learned from a dead knight's notes—that they would answer the way the dead do in stories. Reverently. Gratefully, even. *Thank you, last swordspeaker, for waking us after twelve years of silence. Please proceed to victory.*

What actually happened was this: the hall lit up like a struck bell, every stone remembering its own ringing, and then each throne began to speak at once, and none of them said anything I wanted to hear.

"Name your price," said the first voice, and the second, and the third, until all nine were saying it in overlapping harmonies, some of them warm as banked coals, one of them cold as a coin left out overnight.

"I don't have anything," I said. "That's sort of my whole situation."

The voices did not laugh. Dead courts have no sense of humor, or perhaps they simply spent it all before dying.

* * *

Let me back up, because the hall deserves describing, even if describing it feels like trying to describe a dream you're still inside of.

We'd come down through a crack in the palace foundations that Nessa swore she'd known about since she was nine years old, which meant either she was lying or she'd been robbing royalty longer than I'd been selling charms. Brann went first, because Brann always goes first—he's a mountain that decided walking downhill is a personality—and I went last with the tokens wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, where they'd been humming for three days like a hive deciding whether I counted as flower or threat.

The Court hall had not collapsed so much as been *abandoned mid-sentence*. Nine thrones on a raised dais, each carved from different stone—river granite, white marble gone grey with damp, one of them something dark and glassy that made my teeth ache to look at. Banners hung in ribbons from the walls, the sigils faded but legible: the wave-and-anchor of Court Tidewalker, the crossed hammers of Court Emberhand—Brann's old court, though he wouldn't look at that throne—the nine-pointed star above the central seat, which had been the Court of Voices itself.

Dust everywhere. Twelve years of dust, thick as snowfall, undisturbed except by rats and one very indignant owl that flapped out through a broken window when our lanterns found it.

And under the dust, magic. Not the starved, guttering sparks people smuggled through Vell's markets in charm-bottles. This was the old kind, the deep kind, pooled in the foundations like water in a cellar. My skin had been prickling since we passed the second archway. The swordspeech under my sternum—the thing I'd kept muzzled for twelve years—had been humming louder with every step, and now it was practically singing, straining against the Last Oath my mother had laid over my voice like a hand over a mouth.

"Right," Brann said, setting his lantern down. "Which throne takes what?"

"All of them," I said. "Aldric's notes were clear. Each token returns to its own seat."

"And then?"

"And then I speak the rite, and the Court wakes, and presumably tells us how to kill the thing wearing the king."

Nessa was already halfway up the dais, because Nessa treats sacred architecture as furniture. She touched the river-granite throne and yanked her hand back like it bit her. "It's warm."

"They're all warm," I said. "They've been waiting."

That should have been my first warning. Waiting things are never patient things.

I unwrapped the tokens one by one. Eight of them—we'd never found the ninth, and I was beginning to suspect the ninth was the crown itself, or the hole where the crown's wearer used to be a person. A wave-carved shell for Tidewalker. An iron hammerhead no bigger than my thumb for Emberhand. A feather of green glass. A ring of braided hair that I refused to think too hard about. Seven more, each one humming its own note, and when I set the last one in its place and stepped back, the whole dais exhaled.

Not wind. *Exhaled.* The dust rose off the thrones in a single breath, hung in the lantern light like the hall was showing us its memory, and settled into nine faint shapes—no faces, no details, just the suggestion of figures seated where figures used to sit.

Then: "Name your price." Nine times. All at once.

* * *

"Price?" Brann's voice came out flat. He'd gone the color of wet ash, which for Brann means terrified. "Oscar, what price?"

"I don't know. Aldric's notes ended before—" I stopped, because one of the shapes had turned toward me. The central one. The Court of Voices, or what was left of it, and when it spoke alone, its voice sounded like every voice I'd ever failed to use.

"Swordspeaker. You opened the rite. You will hear the terms."

"I'm listening," I said, and hated how small it sounded.

"The Court does not wake for free. Magic is not charity. Every seat demands a price from the living who would raise it—not blood, not gold. Those are cheap. We ask for what you actually are."

The hammerhead throne spoke next, and its voice was furnace-deep and familiar in a way that made Brann make a sound like he'd been struck. "Emberhand asks its smith's line. Brannoc Ashvane. Forgive the traitor."

"No," Brann said immediately.

"You haven't heard—"

"There's nothing to hear." He was gripping his hammer with both hands, knuckles white. "Maren Vale opened the gates. Maren Vale gave the king the names. My daughter died in the first burning because Maren Vale wrote it down neat and proper who carried the gift and who didn't. There is no version of forgiveness that doesn't spit on her grave."

"She was also the sixth token," I said quietly. "She was also Court. She betrayed you and she died for it and her token has been sitting in a vault for twelve years next to yours, and if you can't find it in yourself to forgive her, this throne stays cold and the Court stays dead and the crown keeps eating this city."

Brann looked at me like I'd suggested he forgive the fire for eating his house.

"That's the price, isn't it," he said finally. "That's the whole rotten shape of it. You want me to make peace with the worst thing that ever happened to me, or the good guys lose."

"I didn't make the rules."

"No," said the Emberhand voice, almost gently. "But you will have to live inside them."

* * *

The tide-shell throne turned to Nessa, and I watched the girl straighten up with the particular bravado of someone about to be brave about the wrong thing.

"Tidewalker asks its heir. Nessa Quick, thief of the undercity, carrier of the spark. Surrender the growth."

"My growth?" Nessa's hand went to her chest, where that little blue flicker lived under her skin. In the lantern light I could see it moving now, restless, brighter than it had been two weeks ago. Brighter than it had been the night she killed those soldiers, the night I'd talked her back from the edge of something with a hunger in it I recognized from mirrors. "This is mine. It's the only thing that's ever been mine."

"It is yours," the tide-voice agreed, "and it is growing faster than you are. What you felt on the night you took your first life—that was the beginning of an appetite. Left to ripen, it will carry you the way floodwater carries a cart. Surrender the growth and keep the gift. Keep the candle. Lose the wildfire."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the seat stays empty, and you keep the wildfire, and we will all discover together what you become."

Nessa looked at me. Sixteen years old, all elbows and defiance, and underneath it the same terror I'd been carrying since I was younger than her: *what am I, if I'm not what the power wants?*

"Don't let me talk you out of it," I said, which was a lie, because everything in me wanted to tell her to refuse, to run, to keep her wildfire and damn the Court. But I'd seen her eyes go wrong that night in the alley. I knew exactly where that road ended, because I'd spent twelve years standing at its entrance, holding the door shut with my own silenced name.

"It's not forever?" she asked the throne.

"The growth, no. The surrender—yes. Some prices are paid once and carried always."

Nessa took a long breath. "I'll pay it. After. When it matters. Not now, standing here like a—like a tithe being collected."

The tide-shape inclined its head, which I chose to read as agreement rather than ominousness. I was going to regret that reading.

* * *

Then all nine shapes turned to me at once, and the hall got very quiet, the way a forest gets quiet before something large moves through it.

"Voices asks its heir," said the center throne. "Swordspeaker. Daughter of Sera Underhill, last voice of the Ninefold Court. Give up the name you hide behind."

The world tilted slightly. I put a hand on the dais rail.

"Oscar is mine," I said. "It's the name I built. It's twelve years of surviving."

"It is a wall you live inside," the voice said. "You were named Oscarina Underhill, and your mother bound your true name beneath the Last Oath to hide you, and you have hidden so well you have hidden from yourself. To complete the rite—to unbind the king, to break the crown—you must break her oath. You must speak the name aloud, plainly, in this hall, before these seats. Not the shortened thing. Not the safe thing. The whole truth of what you are called."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the rite fails. Slowly, then all at once. Look."

And I looked, and saw what I'd been refusing to see: the shapes were thinner than they'd been a moment ago. Fainter. The warmth in the stones was draining away like heat from a corpse. The rite wasn't holding because *I* wasn't holding—I was standing at the door of my own self with my arms braced against the frame, and the whole ceremony was bleeding out through the gap.

"Give us a minute," I told the thrones, which was absurd, they were thrones, but nobody laughed, including me.

* * *

Brann sat down heavily on the steps below the Emberhand throne, hammer across his knees, staring at nothing. Nessa paced. The hall dimmed another degree.

"I can't," I said to both of them, or maybe to myself. "Don't you see? The oath isn't just hiding my name. It's the last thing my mother gave me. She bound my voice to save my life and I've been angry at her for twelve years and I *still*—if I break it, I'm saying it wasn't enough. That her sacrifice was just a delay. That everything she burned herself hollow to protect was something I'd trade away the first time it became useful."

"Or," Brann said, without looking up, "you're saying her daughter finally got strong enough to not need hiding."

"That's worse."

"Aye. Usually is." He rubbed his face with a scarred hand. "You know what the worst part is? I understand the throne. Forgiving Maren Vale—it's not for her. It's for me. Carrying that hate twelve years has done more damage to my hands than the forge ever did. And I still can't do it. Not yet. Maybe not standing here. Maybe I need to say it at her grave, ugly and slow, and mean it on the third try instead of the first." He looked up. "Does the rite take installments?"

"I don't know," I said. "I genuinely don't know anything except that it's dying while we argue."

He was right about the hate, though. I could hear it. And I could hear the other thing underneath it, the thing the oath had been muffling along with everything else: my own voice, my real one, wanting out so badly it hurt. Twelve years of *Oscar*, convenient and small, and the whole time there was a girl named Oscarina Underhill folded up inside me like a letter never sent.

Nessa stopped pacing. "The lights are going out," she said. "Look at the thrones."

She was right. One by one, the faint warmth above the seats was thinning to nothing. The tide-shape was nearly gone. Even the center voice, when it spoke again, sounded distant, underwater:

"Swordspeaker. The rite ebbs. Name yourself, or lose us for another twelve years—and the city will not survive another twelve years of the crown."

My mouth opened. Closed. The oath clamped down like a jaw, and I felt it clearly for the first time—not just a silencing but a *leash*, and somewhere at the far end of that leash, a hand. The crown's hand. My mother had hidden me so well she'd accidentally given the enemy a rope tied to my throat.

If I spoke the name, I broke the leash. If I broke the leash, the crown would feel it snap, wherever it sat on whatever wore the king.

There would be no more stealth after that. No more gathering quietly in the dark. The moment I said *Oscarina Underhill* aloud in this hall, every witchfinder in Vell would know exactly where the last swordspeaker stood.

"Oscar," Nessa said softly. "Whatever you decide. Decide fast."

I looked at the empty ninth throne. I looked at my friends—one frozen in grief, one frozen in fear, both waiting on a woman who'd spent twelve years perfecting the art of waiting.

The rite flickered like a guttering lamp.

And then the doors at the far end of the hall boomed—once, twice—iron on stone, and a voice I knew better than I wanted to carried down the stairwell ahead of bootsteps and torchlight:

"Last chance to kneel, Underhill. I brought company."

Reyne. Here, now, while the rite was half-born and dying, while Brann couldn't forgive and Nessa hadn't surrendered and I couldn't say my own name.

The dead thrones went silent around us, and I swear on every charm I ever sold, in that silence I heard the crown laughing.
