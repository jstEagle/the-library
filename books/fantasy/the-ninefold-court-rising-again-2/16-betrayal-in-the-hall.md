## 16. Betrayal in the Hall

### Ilsa

I had burned eleven families before I learned to stop counting.

Not stopped counting the dead — I'd never been allowed that luxury, the ledgers saw to it, every name in my own hand in my own ink. No. I'd stopped counting them as *families*. That was the trick the crown taught you early, the one they didn't put in any oath: a family is nine people with a hearth between them, but a *case* is a number, and numbers don't scream.

Tonight's case was number forty-one, and it was still breathing when we found it, which was unusual.

"Captain." Sergeant Dole, at my shoulder, torch held high enough to catch the undercity damp and hiss at it. "Tunnels fork ahead. Scouts say the left branch."

The scouts said the left branch because the crown told them to say the left branch, and the crown said it through me, and I had signed the order myself two days ago in a hand that shook only a little. The crown knew where the swordspeaker was going. It had known since the fourth vault burned. It simply preferred that I do the walking — I'd come to understand that much about it. The thing on the throne did not like to leave its chair. Chairs are for sitting; thrones are for being *unable* to rise.

"The left branch," I agreed.

Forty-two men and women behind me, most of them true believers, three of them like me — gifted, leashed, fed just enough of the crown's permission to keep their gift from eating them alive. I could feel the other three the way you feel another fever in a sickroom. We never spoke of it. Speaking of it was how you ended up as case number forty-three.

The tunnels under Vell smelled of river rot and old candle smoke, and beneath both, faintly, something sweeter — magic unbound, leaking up from the ruined Court hall like wine through a cellar floor. Twelve years I'd walked past that sweetness without letting myself taste it. Tonight I let myself taste it, and my suppressed gift turned over in its cage like a dog hearing its name after a decade.

*Soon*, I told it. *One way or the other, soon.*

Because here is what I had learned in the king's presence chamber, kneeling on stone while his shadow bent the wrong direction: the crown does not keep bargains. It keeps *debtors*. And I had read the archives — actually read them, which made me dangerous in a way no witchfinder with a clean conscience ever is — and I knew what the debtors were for.

They were for feeding. All of us. Every gifted soul in Vell, harvested slowly, politely, with paperwork. The purges were never about safety. They were about *portion control*.

So yes. I was leading forty-two people to their deaths, or to something worse than deaths, and I intended to make sure exactly none of them arrived.

Dole would call it treason. He'd be right. I'd spent twelve years telling myself I was the reasonable one — the one who spared the child in the ash-house, the one who filed the merciful reports, the one who believed she was holding the leash rather than wearing it. Reasonableness is a very comfortable room. The trouble is that someone else holds the key, and eventually they come to collect rent.

We reached the threshold of the Court hall an hour past midnight.

I heard them before I saw them — voices raised in the old rite, echoing up out of the dark like bells rung underwater. Nine voices, some of them long dead, and among them one living voice that made the stones themselves lean in to listen. Swordspeech. I'd heard it once, as a child, before the purges, from a woman my mother paid to mend our neighbor's fence by singing to the iron.

My gift broke its cage.

It wasn't dramatic. That's the horror of it. No lightning, no transformation. Just warmth flooding into a limb that had been cold for twelve years, and the sudden unbearable knowledge of everything I might have been if I'd been born twenty years earlier, or braver, or anything at all besides what I was.

"Captain?" Dole. Torchlight on his honest, stupid, loyal face. "Orders?"

I looked down the steps into the hall. The swordspeaker stood among the thrones with her hands full of light. Beside her, a thief-girl with a spark like a struck match, and a big grey smith with a hammer across his shoulders, and half a dozen others — survivors, strays, the ragged congregation that forms around anyone carrying something worth following.

Case forty-one was down there. So was everyone I'd ever pretended not to burn.

"Orders," Dole repeated.

I drew my sword.

"Yes," I said. "Here they are."

### Oscar

The rite was failing, and it was failing because of me.

Nine tokens on nine thrones, nine voices rising out of the stones, each of them asking for a price I hadn't yet agreed to pay. Give up the hidden name. Surrender the safe life. Become legible. And every moment I stalled, the light guttered lower, and somewhere above us the crown drank deeper from Vell's veins, and the bells overhead tolled the hunt's endgame like a countdown nobody had asked for.

"You have to choose," Nessa whispered. She was white-faced, clutching her own token — the sixth seat, the traitor's seat, which Brann had finally, terribly, forgiven. "Oscar. The stones are dimming."

"I know they're dimming."

"They're going *out*—"

"I *know*—"

Which is when the witchfinders came down the stairs.

Forty of them, maybe more, torchlight pouring into the hall like something spilled. And at the front of them, Captain Ilsa Reyne, whom I had last seen in a tunnel with her sword point resting an inch from my throat and mercy in her eyes like a coin she couldn't decide whether to spend.

Everyone in the hall went still. Even the dead voices hushed. There is a particular silence that falls when armed men enter a room where magic is happening, and it is the silence of a held breath.

"Surrender the swordspeaker," Reyne called down, "and the rest of you walk out."

Nobody moved. Brann shifted his grip on the hammer. Nessa's spark flared along her knuckles. And I did the arithmetic I always did — odds, exits, how many I could shield with swordspeech before the steel reached us — and came up short, the way I always came up short, twelve years of hiding having taught me nothing except better ways to lose.

Then Captain Ilsa Reyne turned around and cut down her own sergeant.

I will see it until I die: the torchlight wheeling, Dole's face opening in astonishment rather than pain, the sword coming back out of him red. Reyne stepped over his body onto the stairs so that she stood between her soldiers and us, alone, and her voice when she spoke again was not the voice of a captain at all. It cracked straight down the middle.

"The crown is not the king," she shouted, to her own witchfinders, in the voice you use for a confession. "The king has been dead twelve years. I've seen the records. I've seen *him*. You're not serving a kingdom — you're serving a meal ticket, and you're the meal!"

For a heartbeat, nobody breathed.

Then a young witchfinder near the rear — a boy, gods help me, he couldn't have been older than Nessa — lowered his torch. And another followed. And then the front rank surged forward, but not toward us, toward *her*, and the stairwell became a slaughter in the space between one tolling bell and the next.

"GO!" Reyne screamed over the crash of it, not looking back. "Finish the rite! I'll hold the door!"

Brann grabbed my arm. "She's buying us minutes, girl. Don't waste them grieving."

"I'm not grieving," I said, which was a lie, because grief is what happens when someone spends themselves on your behalf and you can't give it back.

The rite was dying on the thrones. I ran to it, palms flat on the ninth throne's armrest, and poured everything I had — every swallowed word, every year of silence — into the stones.

And the dead began, slowly, to answer.

### Ilsa

There were perhaps ten of them left standing when the last of my witchfinders fell, and I was leaning on my sword in a spreading pool that I understood, distantly, to be mine.

A blade had gone through me below the ribs — Halloway's, I thought, Halloway who'd trained under me for six years, whose wife I'd once quietly removed from a purge list. Fitting. Everything in this city came back around eventually, like water through the cisterns.

I sat down on the bottom step. It seemed important to sit down properly rather than fall down badly. A captain has standards.

The swordspeaker came to me. Of course she did. They always do, the ones worth following — they come to the wounded even when the wounded tried to kill them, because that is the entire difference between us, laid out plain: she walks toward the hurt and I walked away from it for twelve years and called it duty.

"Reyne." She knelt. Her hands hovered over the wound without touching, as if asking permission. "Why?"

"Because I read the archives." My voice was going. I could hear it going, thinning out like smoke. "And because I spared a child once, in the ash-house on Corvel Street, and I told myself it meant something, and then I burned the next family anyway. Redemption isn't a coin you save, swordspeaker. It's one you spend. I should have spent mine years ago." I laughed, and it hurt, and I laughed anyway. "Too late. But purchased. Write that down somewhere."

"Tell me what you know," she said. "About the crown."

Ah. Yes. The reason I was dying on a staircase instead of running, though gods knows I wouldn't have gotten far.

"Its power is the king's oath." I gripped her wrist. My fingers were already cold; I could see her noticing, and being kind enough to pretend not to. "Every oath sworn to the crown feeds it — that's the harvest, that's the purges' real purpose, fear makes oaths, oaths make food. But the revenant itself, the thing wearing him—" I coughed, and tasted copper. "It's *bound*. Bound by the king's original oath. The first one. The one he swore when they crowned him, before the rot. It was never spoken aloud — it lives inside the binding, unfinished, and as long as it stays unspoken, the crown owns the corpse and the corpse owns the magic and the magic owns all of us."

"How do I break it?"

"You don't." I smiled at her. My eyes were failing; hers swam. "But you can. Your oath — your mother's oath, the Last Oath, the silence. It's woven from the same thread. Old law, binding-oath, crown-law. Break yours truly, completely, at the right moment — speak what you've never spoken, the whole of it, your true name and the vow's undoing together — and the resonance will tear the king's oath loose too. One silence ending unmakes another." My grip tightened. "You'll have to mean it. Half-meant oaths just tickle the thing. You must stand in front of it and *mean it* with everything your mother bound and everything you buried, or you'll die in that throne room for nothing, and so will the girl, and so will the smith, and so will my forty-two cases."

"The smith is alive," she said. "He's holding the door now. Your door."

"Good man. Terrible hammer etiquette." Darkness was coming up from underneath me like water filling a boot. I let my head rest against the cold stone of the step. "Swordspeaker. Oscar Underhill. Yes, I know the name, the crown sang it to me for weeks, it thinks names are levers." I found her face one more time, blurred gold in the torchlight. "I was almost reasonable, once. Tell them that. Or don't. Honestly, tell them whatever gets the city free. I won't be checking references."

Her hand closed over mine.

"Ilsa Reyne," she said formally, clearly, in the cadence of an oath — and I realized she was giving me the one thing the crown had starved me of for twelve years. Witness. Someone saying my name as though it mattered. "Witchfinder. Who chose."

"Who chose," I agreed.

And that was enough. That was, gods forgive me, genuinely enough.

* * *

### Oscar

She died between one toll of the bells and the next, with the battle-noise above fading and the rite's light strengthening behind us, as if the stones themselves had waited respectfully for her to finish.

Nessa found me there, kneeling in a stranger's blood, and put her small fierce hand on my shoulder and said nothing, which was the correct amount.

"She gave us the way," I managed. "The king's original oath. It's still unspoken — it's the anchor. If I break mine truly, at the right moment, it tears his loose too."

"Can you?" Brann asked quietly, from the doorway, hammer bloody, breathing hard. "Truly? In front of the thing? With everything?"

Twelve years of silence rose up in me like a wall. Every swallowed word, every market-day smile, every night I'd lain awake listening for boots on the stairs. Somewhere in that silence was my mother's voice, binding me, loving me, muzzling me to save me — and somewhere deeper was the name underneath the name, the one I hadn't spoken since I was fifteen years old, the one that would cost everything I'd built my survival out of.

Above us, the bells changed their rhythm. Faster now. Urgent. The hunt's endgame, the crown drinking deep, the palace shaking dust from its rafters.

"The throne room," I said. I stood. My legs held. "It ends tonight, in the throne room, with my name."

I looked down at Ilsa Reyne one last time — case number one, redeemed too late but redeemed — and then I took up my token, and the nine voices in the stones rose around me like a tide coming in, and we started climbing.

Behind us, far below, the last of the witchfinders' torches went out.

Ahead, the palace waited, and the thing in it that had been waiting longer.

The ninth throne's voice spoke through the stone at my hip, dry as old paper, and it sounded almost amused:

*"Late, little heir. But purchased."*
