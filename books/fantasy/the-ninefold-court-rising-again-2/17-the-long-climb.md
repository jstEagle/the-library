## 17. The Long Climb

The bells began at dusk, and they did not stop.

I had heard Vell's bells my whole life—wedding bells, market bells, the sour midnight clanging that meant a fire in the Tanners' Row. These were none of those. These were the hunt-bells of the old stories, the ones mothers in the undercity still used to frighten children who wouldn't sleep: *when the great bell rings nine times, the crown comes to collect.* They rang now in no pattern I could read, overlapping and arrhythmic, like nine hearts all failing at slightly different speeds.

"They're not calling soldiers," Nessa said. She stood at the mouth of the tunnel where it spilled out behind the tanneries, her face lit orange by the burn-barrels the witchfinders had set along the embankment. "They're calling magic. Listen."

I listened. Under the bronze there was something else—a low note, felt more than heard, the way you feel a storm before it breaks. The same hum I'd carried under my skin for twelve years, except this one came from everywhere at once. From the stones. From the river. From every charm and ward and half-remembered blessing the city had ever swallowed.

The crown was drinking.

"It's pulling everything toward the palace," Brann said. He'd said little since we left the hall of the Nine, and what he said came out flat as hammered iron. He carried his forge-hammer over one shoulder like it weighed nothing, which for Brann it nearly didn't. "All that power's got to go somewhere. It goes up."

"Then so do we," I said.

He looked at me then—the first real look he'd given me since the hall, since the voices of the dead Court had asked him to forgive a traitor and he had walked out rather than answer. His face was soot and grief and forty years of holding things together with rivets and refusal.

"You understand what you're asking," he said. "That thing up there ate a king. It ate nine courts. You're three people with a bag of rocks and a sword that talks."

"The sword doesn't talk," I said. "It listens. That's worse."

Something that might have been a smile crossed his face and died of exposure. "Aye. Worse." He shifted the hammer. "Well. I've buried everyone else I ever swore to. Might as well see how the last one goes."

Nessa laughed, high and too bright, the laugh of someone who has recently killed a man and hasn't decided yet whether that makes her a monster or just tired. "Charming group," she said. "Really. If we survive this, someone should write it down."

*Someone will,* I thought, and did not say it, because I had learned in the last month that prophecy is mostly just confidence wearing a nicer coat.

We went up.

* * *

The palace of Vell sits on the highest ground in the city the way a tumor sits on an organ: not built there, grown. Twelve years of the revenant's reign had changed it. When I was small my mother pointed it out across the rooftops and called it a grey wedding cake; now it looked like something that had been eaten and spat back out. The white stone had gone the color of old teeth. Gargoyles had melted into shapes that were almost faces but refused to commit. And the whole mass of it hummed with the drained magic of a kingdom, a sound I could feel in my fillings.

The lower gates were guarded, naturally. But witchfinders guard against intruders coming *in*, and the crown's hunger was pulling every able body outward—to the burning districts, to the tunnels where Reyne had once hunted us, to the markets where the last few gifted souls in Vell were being flushed out by the bells. So we came at the palace from above, across the slate roofs of the chancellery, and dropped through a ventilation shaft that Brann pried open with a patience that suggested he'd been breaking into better buildings than this for most of his life.

"You were the Court's smith," I whispered as we climbed down into the dark. "How do you know the servants' ways?"

"I built the locks on some of them," he said. "Court business. Doors that open for the right blood." He paused. "Most of them don't work anymore. Try not to bleed on anything."

The upper reaches of the palace were wrong in a way I don't have clean words for. The corridors were too long and also too short—you'd walk twenty steps and arrive somewhere that should have taken fifty. Portraits hung at angles that made your neck ache to look at them. And everywhere, seeping up through the marble like damp, that humming, growing stronger with every stair.

Halfway up the second flight, Nessa stopped walking.

"It knows we're here," she said.

"How can you possibly—"

"Because I can feel it *looking*." She pressed a hand flat against the wall. Her spark—that little thief's flicker she'd spent a lifetime hiding—was standing up on end like hackles. "Oscar. It's not draining the city because it wants power. Power's just what it eats. It's draining the city because it's making room. For us. It's clearing the table."

I wanted to tell her she was being dramatic. I wanted very badly for her to be being dramatic. But my own gift had gone strange in the last hour—not silent, never silent anymore, but stretched thin, like a wire pulled taut between me and something enormous. Every time the bells tolled, the wire sang.

And then the corridor ahead filled with light, and I stopped wanting anything except to live.

* * *

They came around the corner in white and gold—witchfinder livery, but wrong, the white stained grey at the hems as though the color itself was being drunk out of it. Six of them. Their eyes were the worst part. Not cruel. Empty, in the specific way of people who have handed their insides over to something and are waiting politely for instructions.

"Brann," I said. "The hammer."

"Aye."

What happened next took maybe ninety seconds and cost me more than I've let myself count since. Brann met them in the narrow stair like a door slamming. He fought the way he forged—heavy, economical, each blow placed where the metal wanted to break. Nessa's spark flared and caught two of them in a snare of light that wasn't quite fire and wasn't quite lightning, something new, something that frightened me more than the soldiers did because I recognized the shape of it: power feeding on its own user, growing teeth.

And I drew my sword and spoke to it.

Not the fragments I'd given Brann in the market, months ago. Not the careful, rationed syllables of a woman spending coins she couldn't replace. I spoke the way I had spoken as a girl, before the oath, before the silence—whole sentences of swordspeech pouring out of me like water finding a hole in a dam, and the blade woke in my hands and sang back, and the two of us together were briefly, terribly beautiful.

Three soldiers fell. The other three broke and ran, and I let them, because I had learned that much mercy from watching Reyne die.

When it was over, the corridor was quiet, and Brann was sitting on the stairs with his hand pressed to his side, laughing softly.

"That's the first time," he said, "in thirty years, that fighting felt like honest work."

"You're hurt."

"I'm *old*. There's a difference, and I'll thank you not to confuse them in front of the girl." He waved off my hands and stood, and swayed, and lied about it convincingly enough that I chose to believe him, which is a decision I will be paying interest on for the rest of my life. "Come on. The throne room's at the top, and if that thing finishes eating before we get there, none of us will need to worry about a little blood loss."

We climbed.

* * *

The last stretch of the palace was the oldest part, the pre-Court keep, stone blocks the size of coffins laid by hands that hadn't needed mortar. Our footsteps echoed wrong—each one arriving a half-beat after we made it, as if the building were repeating us back to itself, checking our shape against something.

The bells outside had stopped. In the sudden silence the hum was deafening, and I realized with a lurch that it wasn't ambient anymore. It had direction. It was flowing past us, upward, a river running uphill through the walls, and we were wading against the current of a kingdom's stolen soul.

Nessa grabbed my sleeve. "Oscar. Look."

She was staring at the wall, at the seams between the ancient blocks. Light was moving in them—not reflected, *generated*, a slow golden pulse traveling upward like a heartbeat in a throat. And in each pulse, if I let my gift listen instead of my eyes, I could hear... voices. Faint. Familiar in a way that scraped something raw in my chest.

"The tokens," I breathed. "The nine voices. The crown isn't just hoarding what it took from the city. It's holding the Court itself. All this time. It's been keeping them."

"Keeping them or *keeping* them?" Nessa said. "There's a difference between a prisoner and a pantry."

I didn't have an answer. Somewhere above us, something vast turned over in its sleep, and dust sifted down from a ceiling that had held for four hundred years and was suddenly unsure.

Brann put his hand on the wall, gently, the way he touched metal he respected. For a moment the pulse beneath his palm stuttered, slowed, matched itself to him—and I swear the old man smiled at whatever it said.

"What did it—" Nessa started.

"Nothing," Brann said. "Old friend saying hello. Come on." He turned and started up the stairs, and his stride was steady, and I was grateful enough not to notice that he was favoring his left side, that the blood on his shirt had spread, that honest work or not, ninety seconds in a stairwell had opened something that laughter couldn't close.

I noticed later. I notice everything, later. It's the curse of survivors—we get perfect hindsight and no refunds.

* * *

The doors to the antechamber of the throne room were black oak banded in iron, taller than three men, carved with the sigils of courts that no longer existed. Brann stopped before them and laughed again, short and bitter.

"I hung those bands," he said. "Young man's work. Show-off's work." He ran his thumb along the sigil of the Smith's Court, burned into his own hand these many years, twin to the mark on mine. "The day I finished, the ninth seat told me iron was honest because it only does what you make it do. I believed her." His jaw worked. "Then her court burned, and I learned iron lies too. Everything holds until it doesn't."

"Brann—"

"She asked me to forgive the traitor. In the hall. The sixth voice." He wasn't looking at me. He was looking at his own scarred hand. "I walked out. I've been walking out for thirty years, girl. It's the only skill I've kept sharp." He turned, and his eyes were wet and furious and calm, all three at once, which should not be possible on a human face and yet. "So hear me. Whatever's up there—if it offers you a seat, a bargain, a version of the story where you come out clean—remember that clean isn't a thing power sells. It sells *useful*. And useful always bills you later."

"Noted," I said. "Any other wisdom? We're on a schedule."

"Aye." He pushed open the doors. "Don't die stupid. Die expensive, if you have to die at all."

Beyond the doors, the antechamber opened like the inside of a bell—a vast round vault of stone, and hanging in its center, suspended on nothing, the crown.

Not the king. Not yet. Just the crown, turning slowly, drinking the river of gold that poured up through the floor, and I understood in one sick flash that everything we had climbed past—the guards, the wrongness, the bells, the twelve years of ash—had been scaffolding around this one object, this ring of metal that had decided, somewhere in its hollow history, that it would rather rule forever than belong to anyone even once.

"There you are," I said, and my swordspeech came out without permission, and the word for *enemy* and the word for *home* in the old tongue are separated by a single breath, and I have never been sure which one I said.

The crown stopped turning.

Every torch in the antechamber guttered. The hum became a chord, the chord became a voice, and the voice was warm and reasonable and knew my mother's name.

Behind me, boots on stone. Many boots. The doors we had come through were full of white and gold, and at their center stood a witchfinder captain who should have been dead, whose wound I had watched close like a liar's promise, whose eyes were not empty at all—they were full, horribly full, of everything she had ever suppressed.

"Well," said Captain Ilsa Reyne, in a voice that was only half hers. "You're a difficult woman to kill, Oscar Underhill. Fortunately, I'm no longer the one doing the killing."

And Brann stepped forward, hammer rising, and said, "Get her up the stairs, girl. This bit's mine."

I have replayed the next ten seconds ten thousand times, and they never go any differently. The crown screamed. Reyne moved like something puppeted. Brann swung, connected, and did not move fast enough afterward—and I was halfway to him when Nessa's arms locked around my chest and the stairs appeared where a wall had been, a hidden stair, an escape, and I was screaming words I won't write down while the last friend of the old world held the doorway of the new one with a hammer and thirty years of unforgiven grief.

He was smiling when the light took him. That's either a mercy or an obscenity; I haven't decided.

Then the stair swallowed us, and the climb went on, and every step upward felt like carrying him, and Reyne, and my mother, and the nine dead, and the whole weight of a kingdom that had forgotten its own name—

—and at the top of the stairs, at last, the throne room doors stood open, and the thing wearing my king was waiting, patient as rust, and it said:

"*At last. The heir arrives. Sit down, child. Your court has been holding your chair for twelve years.*"

The ninth seat was not empty.

It was occupied—by a coat of arms I knew from a locket I had buried with my mother: the Underhill crest, the last voice of the Court, stitched in gold thread that was still, impossibly, wet.
