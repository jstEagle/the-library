## 18. The King Who Was Not

The throne room doors were taller than my whole shop, and I want you to understand that my first coherent thought upon seeing them was: *someone is going to have to dust these.*

Twelve years of hiding does something strange to a person's courage. It doesn't make it smaller. It makes it *specific*. You learn exactly which fears you can carry and which ones will crack your spine, and you arrange your life around the load-bearing ones like furniture in a narrow room. Fear the witchfinders: yes, daily, manageable. Fear the dark undercity: no, that one stays by the door where it can't follow. Fear dying in a palace corridor with bells tolling and Brann's blood still warm on your sleeves—

Well. Apparently that one was coming along whether I liked it or not.

Nessa leaned against the wall beside me, her hands still trembling from whatever she'd done to the last squad of guards. Not shaking the way she'd shaken after the first time, in the coastal town. This was a steadier tremble, the kind you get when your body has decided terror is just another chore. "There'll be more inside," she said.

"There's always more," I said. "That's rather their whole strategy."

Brann was dead three floors below us, and I hadn't had time to grieve him properly, and I suspected — with the cold clarity that swordspeech lends me at the worst possible moments — that grief was going to have to wait its turn like everyone else in this kingdom. The smith had gone down swinging a hammer he'd forged for a Court that broke his heart, buying two women time to climb. He'd said something at the end. I couldn't remember what it was. That's the cruelty of it; the dying always say things, and the living never get to hold onto them, because the living are too busy not dying.

"The tokens are humming," Nessa said.

They were. All eight of them — nine counting the traitor's blackened disc, which hummed in a lower register, like a note held underwater. I'd strung them across my chest on Aldric's chain, and they sat against my sternum now like nine small hearts that weren't mine, beating out of time with each other and with me.

The king's original oath, Reyne had said, bleeding out on the threshold behind us. The revenant is bound by words no one has spoken since. Find them. Unsay them. Or say them — she hadn't been sure, and neither was I, and there was no time left to be sure about anything.

I pushed the doors.

* * *

The throne room of Vell was built for a court of nine hundred, back when courts were things a kingdom could afford. Now it was empty except for banners gone grey with age, pillars furred with dust like frost, and — at the far end, beneath a window of stained glass depicting some ancestor or other triumphantly stabbing something — the throne itself.

And the king.

He was seated. Of course he was seated. Kings are always seated; it's how you know them. But even at forty paces I could see what Reyne must have seen in that audience hall months ago, what she'd carried out of there like a stone in her chest until it killed her: the wrongness. The corpse wore its crown the way a coat rack wears a coat. Its skin had the waxy evenness of candle-tallow, and its eyes were open and lit from within by a light that had nothing to do with seeing. And its shadow —

Its shadow stood up.

I don't mean it was cast oddly. I mean the shadow rose off the floor behind the throne like smoke poured backward, gathered itself into a shape roughly man-shaped but longer, jointed wrong, and looked at me. Shadows don't have faces. This one had something adjacent to one.

"Oscar Underhill."

The voice came from everywhere — the pillars, the glass, my own teeth. It was the king's voice, or had been once, before whatever wore it thinned it out like broth stretched over too many bowls. And beneath the royal cadence there was something else, something I recognized with a lurch, because I'd spent twelve years listening for it in market crowds and midnight footsteps:

Magic. Old magic. Bound so tight it screamed.

"Swordspeaker," the thing said, and the corpse's mouth moved a half-second late, like a puppet remembering its lines. "Daughter of Maren Underhill. Last voice of the Ninth Seat. Come all this way. Do sit."

"I'll stand," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Standing suits my temperament."

"You always were your mother's daughter." A dry sound that might have been laughter rattled through the room. The corpse did not laugh. The corpse only breathed, because corpses on thrones are apparently expected to keep up appearances. "She stood too. At the end. Did you know that? They gave her the same choice I'm about to give you, and she chose to burn rather than bend. Very moving. Very wasteful."

My mother burned. I'd known it the way you know the sun rises — as fact, not weather. Hearing it described as a *choice* made something in my chest go white-hot, and the tokens against my sternum flared warm in answer, eight small voices saying, in eight dead tones, *we felt it too.*

Careful, I told myself. That heat is exactly what it wants. Anger makes people predictable, and predictable people kneel or charge, and both are easier to kill than someone thinking.

"What do you want?" I asked.

"To talk." The shadow drifted down from the throne's dais, flowing across the flagstones toward me without disturbing a single mote of dust. "You've climbed through my palace. You've buried my witchfinder captain — ah, yes, I know about Reyne. Sentimental of her. Foolish. She could have had everything." The shadow circled me, slow, and I turned with it because letting it get behind me seemed unwise in a way I couldn't articulate. "And you've collected my Court's little toys. Eight seats' worth. May I see?"

"No."

"Of course not. You're a stubborn one. They're all stubborn ones, the ninth seat." The corpse's head tilted, tallow skin catching the grey window-light. "Let me tell you a story, Oscar Underhill, since stories are what your kind trades in. Once there were nine rulers who shared a kingdom between them, and every one of them believed the magic belonged to *them* — to their line, their gift, their judgment. Nine kings and queens, each certain the other eight were the problem. Do you know what that looks like from the throne? It looks like chaos wearing ceremony. It looks like a fire that agrees to stay in the hearth only so long as everyone keeps agreeing. Every generation, a border war. Every decade, a purge of somebody — never called a purge, of course. Called *balance*."

"That's a tyrant's history," Nessa said from the doorway. Her spark was rising; I could feel it like heat off a forge. "Aldric told us—"

"Aldric told you what the winners of an older war wrote down," the shadow said gently, almost kindly, and that kindness frightened me more than anything else in the room. "Child, I *was* there. I sat behind the ninth seat for thirty years. I watched the Court of Nine decide whose villages starved. I watched them bind a river spirit because it flooded the wrong fields — the wrong *lords'* fields. Magic in nine pairs of hands is nine chances for ruin. Magic in one pair of hands is ruin with a schedule."

"And so you took the one pair," I said.

The pause was very slightly too long.

"So the kingdom took it," the corpse said. "So the *oath* took it. I am the oath's servant, swordspeaker, nothing more. Your mother knew that better than anyone. Ask her why she ran instead of fighting. Ask her what the Last Oath cost her."

I didn't ask. Mostly because I couldn't speak for a moment. The thing in the throne room knew about my oath. Knew about the leash Reyne had hinted at, the cord running from my silenced throat to the crown's cold hand. Which meant it could pull that cord whenever it wished. Which meant every second I stood here listening was a second it was deciding *when*.

But here's what twelve years of selling luck-charms teaches you: when the mark starts telling you a story, the story is never for your benefit. Listen anyway. There's always a seam.

"Say your piece, then," I managed.

* * *

And oh, it was good. I'll give the thing that. Whoever — whatever — had assembled this temptation had studied at the feet of masters, or possibly *was* the master.

"The eighth seat stands empty," the shadow said, and the stained-glass window behind the throne seemed to brighten, though the sky outside was storm-dark. "It has stood empty twelve years, waiting. The ninth seat burned. But seats are not people, swordspeaker. Seats are *offices*. And offices can be refilled." The corpse rose. It moved badly, joints creaking like a ship in weather, and yet it descended the dais steps toward me with terrible patience, and I made myself not step back, because backing away from kings is how you end up kneeling. "Give me the tokens. Speak the rite. Take the eighth seat beside me — take the ninth, if the dead insist — and we will restore the Court. Not the squabbling oligarchy your knight mourns. A true Court. Ordered. Eternal."

"Ruled by a corpse," I said.

"Guided by continuity." The corpse stopped six feet from me. This close, I could smell it: cold ash and old incense and underneath both, faintly, the copper stink of bound magic — the same smell as the vaults, the same smell as my own silence. "You think I offer you tyranny? Tyranny is what you've been living, girl. Twelve years of burning grandmothers in market squares while you sold fake charms and hated yourself. THAT is what ungoverned fear makes. I am offering the alternative. Magic under law. Gifted under guard. No more children dragged from their beds because a neighbor envied them. No more Reyne choosing which child dies today. Structure. Sanctuary. Safety." The corpse's dead eyes fixed on mine, and within them that inner light flickered, and for one vertiginous instant I saw something else looking out — something vast and patient and *hungry*, wearing the argument like a glove. "You could have made magic safe forever, Oscar Underhill. Safe by making it lawless no longer. One word. One seat. One oath."

And here is the part I'm not proud of.

For a moment — a long moment, three heartbeats at least — I wanted it.

Not the power. Power has never tempted me; I'd seen what my gift cost my mother and I'd priced myself out of that market at eleven years old. What tempted me was everything wrapped around the offer like ribbon. *Sanctuary.* No more flinching at boots on cobblestones. No more calculating, every morning, whether today was the day the humming under my skin got me killed. A world where Nessa's spark wouldn't be a death sentence. A world where I could say my own name in a crowd without checking who stood downwind.

The thing had taken everything I'd lost — home, safety, my mother, my name — and offered it back to me in exchange for kneeling. That's not corruption's usual shape. Corruption usually comes dressed as ambition. This came dressed as *relief*, and relief is so much harder to refuse.

"You're quiet," the shadow murmured, pleased. "They're always quietest right before—"

"You killed Brann's daughter."

The words came out flat, and the room went still.

"Sixteen years old," I continued, and now the anger wasn't heat anymore; it was a blade, and blades are cold, and cold is where I live. "Gifted with mending — she could knit bone with her hands. Your witchfinders took her because the Court's fall made giftedness treason, and you let that law stand because a frightened kingdom harvests easily. You stand there offering me sanctuary and you *built the need for it*. You don't want to end the hunt. You ARE the hunt. The fear is the crop, and I am standing in your field being invited to become the scarecrow."

The corpse smiled. It was the worst thing I had ever seen, that smile — lips moving over teeth that had been dead too long to be white.

"There it is," the shadow said softly. "The refusal. Every ninth seat refuses first. It's traditional. Your mother refused too — beautifully, at length, quoting the old rites. Then we spoke her true name aloud, swordspeaker, and the oath she'd hidden behind cracked like river ice, and she burned anyway, because a broken oath cannot be re-sworn, only *paid for*." The shadow leaned close, and its non-face filled my vision, and the bound magic underneath the room screamed louder, and I understood suddenly, completely, what Reyne had died trying to tell me. "I know your name, Oscarina Underhill. I have known it for twelve years. Shall I say it? Shall I pull the leash your mother fastened so lovingly around your throat, and show you what your silence was FOR?"

Eight tokens blazed against my chest. The ninth — the traitor's black disc — went cold as grave-dirt.

And the corpse opened its mouth to speak my name.

* * *

Later — if there is a later — I will try to explain what happened next, and I will fail, because language was designed by people who never had a dead king inhale to steal their soul. But here is my best attempt:

The tokens sang.

Not the hum. Not the memory-voices that had haunted me through seven vaults and a burning town. They *sang* — eight notes in harmony and a ninth grinding dissonance beneath, and the sound hit the throne room like a wave hits a seawall, and the corpse staggered, and the shadow recoiled, and in that half-second of grace I heard my mother's voice in the song, clear as market-day, saying the thing she'd said when she bound me:

*"One day you'll speak your name, my love. Make sure it's yours when you do. Don't let them say it for you."*

The corpse recovered. Of course it did. Dead things have infinite patience and nothing to lose, which is a combination no living creature should ever face.

"Then we do it the hard way," it said, almost fondly. "You always do it the hard way, ninth seat. It's what makes you worth harvesting."

Behind me, the great doors boomed — soldiers, reinforcements, the hunt's endgame arriving precisely on schedule. Nessa shouted something. The shadow rose tall as the pillars.

And I reached for the oath.

Not to break it — I didn't know how, not yet, not fully, the words Reyne had given me turning over and over like a key I couldn't fit to the lock. But I found its edge, the place where my mother's love had sealed my voice shut, and I pressed against it from inside, the way I'd pressed for twelve years without knowing I was pressing.

The binding *held*.

But it rang.

And in the ringing, beneath the corpse's name-stealing breath and the soldiers' boots and Nessa's rising fire, I heard it — faint, frayed, unmistakable: the king's original oath, still alive somewhere in these stones, still unspoken-complete, waiting twelve years for a voice to finish it or undo it.

Mine.

If I could find the words.

The corpse lunged, and I ran — not away, but *inward*, toward the dais, toward the throne, toward the place where the ringing was loudest, while the doors burst open behind me and the last chapter of Vell began.
