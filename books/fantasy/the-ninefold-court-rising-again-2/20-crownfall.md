## 20. Crownfall

The dead king's spirit hung in the air like a held breath, and I understood—finally, stupidly, at the end of everything—that it *was* a held breath. One breath. That was the price the crown's breaking had already paid without asking anyone's permission. The rite had torn the revenant loose from its anchor, and what remained of the man who had worn that anchor was given exactly one breath to spend.

He spent it looking at me.

"Speak," he said, and his voice was dust and bells, "and I will finish what my silence began."

* * *

Let me back up. Because in stories—and I have written enough bad ones to know—the moment before triumph is always rendered in clean strokes. The hero stands. The villain falls. The light breaks. What actually happened in the throne room of Vell, in the hour before dawn on the last day of the revenant's reign, was that I knelt in broken glass with blood running into my eye and nine voices screaming in the stones around me, and I had no idea if any of this would work.

The revenant had lost its king. That much Reyne's dying gift had given us: the thing on the throne was bound by an oath the real king had never spoken aloud, an oath locked behind his teeth when he died—or when he was made to die—and so long as it stayed unspoken, the hollow thing wearing his shape could wear it too. My mother's oath had been the key. *Oscarina Underhill,* I had said, twelve years of silence ending in four syllables that tore something out of my chest and left a hole shaped like my own name. The binding had ripped free of the corpse's throat like a hook from a fish's jaw.

But unbinding is not destroying. The revenant still stood.

It stood the way a coat stands on a hook. There was nothing inside it anymore except habit and hunger, and the hunger turned toward me with the whole weight of the room.

"You could have been mine," it said. It had the king's voice now, or rather it had the memory of a voice, the way a glove remembers a hand. "You could have had the eighth seat. The ninth. Magic made safe forever."

"Safe," I said, spitting blood. "You've been eating the city for twelve years and you call it *safe.*"

"I called it order." Something moved beneath the corpse's skin, wrong, like eels under ice. "Your Court called it balance, and look where balance got them. Burned. Buried. Forgotten. I offered them law and they wanted freedom. Freedom is just power with no one holding the other end of the rope, swordspeaker. I am the only one who ever held it honestly."

And here is the terrible thing, the thing I will carry to my grave along with everything else: he wasn't entirely wrong. That was the trick of the crown, I think. It never lied. It only told you the truth in the shape most useful to itself.

Behind me, Brann lay where he had fallen on the stairs, and I would not let myself look at him yet. Nessa stood at my shoulder with her hands burning—not with fire, with that dangerous silver light she carried, the one I'd talked her back from twice—and the nine voices in the stones were chanting something old enough that the words predated the kingdom, predated the city, maybe predated language's good manners.

*One breath,* the voices said. *One breath remains.*

I turned back to the spirit hanging above the throne.

"What do I do?" I asked him. And then, because honesty had gotten expensive but it had also gotten me this far: "Please. I don't know how this ends. I only know how it started."

Something in the ruined face softened. Kings are not supposed to soften. But then, he hadn't been a king for twelve years; he'd been a hostage in his own body, watching the thing that wore him burn children in his name. Twelve years. The same twelve years I'd spent hiding. We had both been silent, in our ways. His silence had just cost more.

"The rite needs nine voices and a tenth to bind them," he said. "I was meant to be the tenth. I was meant to speak the closing and shatter the crown upon the stone. Instead I let fear seal my lips, and my silence became a door, and something walked through it." His gaze flicked to Nessa, to the stones, to me. "You have gathered the voices. You have paid the prices—they demanded, and you paid, more than they had any right to ask. Now you need only a tenth voice willing to be temporary."

"Temporary," I repeated.

"Spirits given breath must give it back," he said, with the ghost of dry humor—the ghost of a ghost of humor. "It is the one tax even kings cannot avoid."

"And if the tenth voice refuses?"

"Then the crown endures, diminished, hungry, patient. Crowns are very patient, swordspeaker. It is nearly all they are."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about magic: it is mostly administration.

All that terror, all that wonder, all those years of hiding my gift under my tongue like a coin I was afraid to spend—and at the end of it, saving the world came down to standing in the right place, saying the right words, in the right order, while nine dead people watched me work.

I took my position at the center of the hall. The tokens sat on their thrones in a ring—the smith's hammer-token, the singer's bell, the healer's cup, the traitor's cold gray stone, all nine, each one humming now with a voice that had once been a person with bad knees and favorite foods and opinions about weather. Each one had demanded a price. Each price had been paid, though two of them—Brann's forgiveness of the traitor, given over his daughter's grave-memory, and my own surrender of the name I hid behind—still ached like teeth.

Nessa took her place at the ring's edge. She was shaking. So was I. We looked at each other, and I did the thing I had learned to do with her in the tunnels, the thing that had pulled her back from the edge of her own power twice: I opened the swordspeech. Not as a weapon. As a hand held out.

*Together,* I said, in the language that lived under language.

*Together,* the girl answered, and her light steadied.

"Begin," said the dead king.

And the nine voices spoke.

I will not transcribe it. Partly because the rite is not mine to give away—it belongs to the stones now, and to whoever comes after me, and they'll find it when they're ready—but mostly because human letters weren't built for it. Imagine nine instruments playing nine songs that should not harmonize, and then imagine the moment they do. Imagine that moment lasting three minutes. Imagine standing at the center of it while your bones learn they are instruments too.

My part was small. That's the joke of it. After everything—the vaults, the fire, the climb up through a palace that was trying to eat the city's magic out from under its own floors—my part was to hold the ring steady while the ninth voice joined the other eight, and to keep speaking my true name like a nail driven through the rite's center. *Oscarina. Oscarina. Oscarina.* Each repetition cost me something I can't describe. Not pain. More like paying off a debt in installments of self.

The revenant screamed.

It didn't fight, exactly. Fighting implies strategy. It simply *clung*, the way ivy clings, the way grief clings, pouring itself against the walls of the rite and finding them closed. And as the ninth voice rose—as the ring completed—the corpse on the throne began to come apart, not in flesh but in claim. Every wall it had touched dimmed. Every drain it had sunk into the city's veins sealed shut with an audible sound, like a hundred locks turning at once.

"It will flood back," the dead king warned, from somewhere above and inside the music. "Twelve years of taken magic, returning to a city that has forgotten what it is. There will be fires. There will be miracles. There will be children waking up with gifts and no one to teach them whether to use them." A pause. "That was the mistake I made. I feared the flood more than the drought, and my fear crowned that thing. Do not make a king of your fear, Oscarina Underhill."

"I don't want to be a king," I said, through gritted teeth, still chanting.

"No," he agreed, and there was warmth in it, awful, undeserved warmth. "I believe that is precisely why it will work."

The crown was still on the corpse's head. The rite needed it shattered on the stone—on the ancient flagstone at the hall's center, the one with nine seats carved around it, the one I knelt on now. But the corpse was ten feet away and the rite held my hands full of invisible rope.

"One breath," the king said. "Mine to spend as I choose."

And the spirit of Aldous the Third, last true king of Vell, the man whose cowardice had built all of this and whose death had fed it for twelve years—came down out of the air and into his own body one final time.

For a heartbeat, the corpse had a face again. Tired. Human. Ashamed.

"I am sorry," Aldous the Third said—to the room, to the stones, to the burned and the buried and the little family Reyne had failed to save, to me. "To everyone. Especially to the ones I will never meet, who inherit the flood."

Then he lifted the crown from his own head—his dead hands moving with living purpose—and brought it down against the stone with every ounce of a king's regret.

* * *

The sound was not a sound.

I have tried, since, to describe it. In my drafts I've called it a bell, a crack, a scream, a chord. None of it is right. The closest I can come is: it was the sound of a word being unsaid. Twelve years of theft and terror and hollow reign, and the crown hit the flagstone and the world *unsaid* it.

Then the flood came.

Magic hit Vell like a wave hits a seawall. I felt it pass through the palace—through me—like standing in a river that has decided, after years of drought, to remember it is a river. Every bounded charm in the city broke open at once. Every spell the witchfinders had suppressed, every gift the crown had siphoned, every drop hoarded in the revenant's black reservoirs—all of it came home.

And it was terrifying.

I want to be honest about that, because the ballads—I can already hear them being butchered in taverns—will skip this part. The flood was not golden light and birdsong. The flood was a baker's wife in the Lamplight Ward waking up levitating three feet above her bed and screaming. It was the river changing course six inches and flooding two streets before anyone understood why. It was lamplighters' torches flaring green, cats talking in the Fishmarket (badly; cats are poor speakers), the great bells of the palace ringing themselves with no hand near them, a low ominous thrumming under the whole city like the ground clearing its throat. Somewhere north of the temple district, a fountain ran upward for eleven minutes. Nobody has explained that one to me yet, and I suspect nobody ever will.

People woke to a world where the impossible had stopped asking permission. Some wept. Some barricaded their doors. Some went out into the streets and lit candles and danced, because their grandmother's stories had come back and the stories said the flood meant the Court would rise again—and they were half right, which is the most dangerous amount of right there is.

In the throne room, the corpse finished falling apart. Gently, almost. Dust accepting an invitation. The shards of the crown lay scattered across the flagstone, and they were beautiful, which felt like an obscenity—thin crescents of dark metal, each one still faintly warm, each one still faintly *wanting*. I could feel them wanting. That was the first lesson of my new life: power does not stop wanting things just because you've won.

Nessa helped me stand. Her hands had stopped burning. She looked older. She looked nine years old. Both at once.

"Is it over?" she asked.

I started to say yes. I genuinely did. And then I saw her face, and I saw the shards, and I heard the city outside—the bells, the shouting, the singing, the crying—and I thought about a woman named Ilsa Reyne who had died at a threshold buying us minutes, and a smith named Brann lying cold on the palace stairs because every ally is a cost, and nine voices in the stones waiting patiently to see what I would do next.

"No," I said. "It's just finally begun."

* * *

We carried Brann down ourselves. There was no ceremony available and none needed; Nessa held one arm and I held the other, and the palace staff—who had emerged from wherever servants hide during apocalypses, pale and blinking—took off their hats as we passed. He was the Court's smith. He had forged the tokens. He had refused me, forgiven a traitor, saved two fools on a staircase, and died with his hammer still in reach. If the new age builds anything worth having, it will build it on Brann's shoulders, and I intend to tell everyone so until they're sick of hearing it.

Dawn found me alone in the wreck of the throne room. Nessa was asleep against a pillar, exhausted past dignity. The nine thrones stood empty, their tokens quiet now, resting. And in my hands I held the crown's shards, gathered into a fold of cloth, because someone had to and because leaving them on the floor felt like leaving a loaded crossbow in a nursery.

They hummed against my palms. Faintly. Patiently. Even broken, even dead, the crown remembered how to want.

Outside, Vell was waking into its first ungoverned morning in twelve years. I could hear it—a thousand small wonders and a thousand small disasters, all of them unregulated, all of them alive, all of them now, somehow, in some way I had not agreed to and could not refuse, *mine to answer for.* The most powerful person in the city, the whispers were already saying in the halls below, the last swordspeaker, the breaker of crowns. The woman who freed the magic. The woman to watch.

A knock came at the shattered doors. Then footsteps—hesitant ones, many of them. When I turned, the doorway was full of people: servants, soldiers without officers, market folk who'd climbed the palace steps in their nightclothes, a child with new light coming off her fingertips like frost. Dozens of them. Looking at me.

Not at the empty throne. At me.

An old man at the front—the baker from the Lamplight Ward, I'd learn later, the one whose wife had floated—cleared his throat and asked the question I would spend the rest of my life answering:

"What do we do now?"

I looked down at the shards in my arms. I looked at the nine empty thrones. And I thought: *not a throne. Never again a throne.*

"We talk," I said.
