## 21. The Ninth Seat

They wanted to give me a throne. I want that on the record, carved somewhere, so that in fifty years when the ballads get it wrong—and they will, because ballads always do—someone can point at this and say: *she said no*.

The argument had been going for three weeks by the time I was invited to it, which tells you everything about how Vell works now. The city council met in the Guildhall, in a room built for merchants to shout about grain tariffs, and they shouted about me the same way. I sat in the gallery like a woman waiting for her bread ration while aldermen who'd spent twelve years pretending magic didn't exist debated whether to hand it a chair.

"Order," said Magistrate Corven, for the eleventh time. Nobody gave him any.

"The point," Alderwoman Sessily Vane said—I'd learned she was some cousin of Ser Aldric's, twice removed, with his jaw and none of his patience—"the *point*, Magistrate, is that Underhill shattered the crown. She holds its shards. She commands swordspeech, which the histories say could level walls. And you propose we simply... ask her nicely?"

"I propose," Corven said, "that we not replace one uneaten king with another."

I raised my hand. It took them four minutes to notice.

"For what it's worth," I said, "I don't want the crown. Any of it. I've been carrying the shards around in a sack and honestly it's been terrible for my back."

Silence. Then, from somewhere near the doors, Nessa's unmistakable snort.

"You find this amusing?" Sessily Vane demanded.

"I find all of this amusing," I said. "A month ago every one of you would have reported me to Reyne's witchfinders for selling a charm that actually worked. Now you're fighting over whether to worship me or leash me. I'd like to propose a third option."

Corven rubbed his eyes. "Go on."

"Nobody rules. Nobody is ruled. We build a Court—but not the old one. Not nine thrones above a kingdom. Nine seats below it, holding up the floor."

I'd rehearsed the speech on the walk over, standing outside a cobbler's window where a mannequin wore boots I couldn't afford. It came out better than rehearsal usually does, mostly because I stopped trying to be eloquent and just told them the truth: that the old Court fell because power answered to no one; that the crown endured because it answered to nothing at all; that the only thing I'd learned from twelve years of hiding and one year of war was that a gift without accountability is just a slower way of becoming a revenant.

"The Ninefold Court rises again," I finished, "but it kneels. To the city. Every seat swears an oath of guardianship, answers publicly for what they do, and can be unseated by vote of the other eight plus the magistrates. Including mine. *Especially* mine."

More silence. Then Sessily Vane leaned forward slowly, like a woman seeing land after a long time at sea.

"And who," she said, "would sit these seats?"

*

We argued about that for two more weeks. Of course we did. This is Vell.

The first seat went to Brann—or rather, to Brann's memory, because the man himself was ash on the palace stairs, and the seat became a promise: that the smiths and makers would have a voice, and that forgiveness was work the living still owed. His apprentice Torv took the oath with Brann's hammer across his knees and cried through the whole thing, which set the tone more than any ceremony ever could.

The second seat went to Sessily Vane herself, to everyone's surprise including hers, because someone had to speak for the families the witchfinders had broken and she was angry enough and honest enough to do it without flinching.

The third seat—the healers' seat—went to a woman named Maren Ostrewe, who had spent the purge sewing wounds shut with ordinary thread and lying beautifully to witchfinders about where her patients came from. She accepted with the expression of someone agreeing to a second root canal.

The fourth seat went to the undercity. They elected a rat-catcher named Doss, on the grounds that he knew every tunnel and owed nobody anything. He showed up to the first session smelling faintly of lamp oil and won the room in ten minutes by asking the sharpest question anyone had heard all year.

The fifth seat went to the scholars, and here I put my foot down. The universities nominated three men who wanted to *study* the gifted rather than stand beside them. I stood up in their hall and asked, very politely, whether any of them had ever been dragged out of their beds at dawn. When the silence got uncomfortable, I suggested they think about it longer. Eventually they sent a woman named Pell Ashk, who'd been quietly expelled years ago for writing a sympathetic history of the Ninefold Court. She arrived at our first meeting with a stack of notes and an apology for nothing.

The sixth seat—

The sixth seat was the hard one. That was the traitor's seat, the empty one, the wound in the middle of everything. The voices in the ruined hall had demanded Brann forgive the betrayer, and he hadn't lived long enough to manage it, and I don't think the debt died with him. For a long time we considered leaving the sixth seat vacant forever, a monument to broken trust.

It was Pell Ashk who argued against that. "If we leave it empty," she said, "then betrayal has no voice but memory, and memory only says *never again*. Someone has to hold the seat that asks the ugly questions. Someone has to be the one who wonders if *we're* the traitors yet." So we swore in a retired harbor-warden called Ivet Crane, whose gift was small and strange—he could tell when a person was lying—and whose first act was to look directly at me and announce, "You're still hiding something." He wasn't wrong. But we'll come to that.

The seventh and eighth seats filled themselves in ways I didn't orchestrate and couldn't have: a farmer's cooperative sent a weather-speaker named Jut Halloway who talked to rain the way other people talk to horses, and the dockworkers chose a salt-witch grandmother called Old Brey who terrified two aldermen into submission before lunch on her first day.

Which left the ninth.

*

Nessa found me on the roof of my shop the night before the convening. She climbed up the way she climbed everything, badly and with commentary, and dropped down beside me with two cups of cider and no invitation.

"They're saying you'll take the ninth seat tomorrow," she said.

"They're right."

"They're also saying you'll take it as queen. Half of them want that. Half of them are terrified of it. A few want both, which is the most Vell thing I've ever heard."

I drank my cider. Below us the market square lay quiet, the stalls shuttered, the burn-scars from the purges finally scrubbed off the cobbles. Somewhere a night-watchman sang to himself, badly.

"When I broke the oath," I said, "when I said my name out loud in that throne room—it wasn't just words, Ness. Swordspeech doesn't work that way. You don't just say a thing. You *give* it. I gave my hidden name to the air and the air's been holding it ever since, and everyone who walks through that square walks through a piece of me. I can feel them. All of them. Do you understand what that means?"

"That you're powerful?"

"That I'm *responsible*. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point." I turned to look at her. Fifteen years old, scarred from the soldiers, carrying a gift that had nearly eaten her alive and a steadiness that had nothing to do with magic. "That's why I'm taking the ninth seat. Not because I earned it. Because if I don't bind myself to something, there's nothing left to stop me except my own good intentions, and I've met my good intentions. They're exhausting and occasionally monstrous."

"So swear the oath, then."

"It's not that simple. The oath binds the seat to the city. But the ninth seat—" I looked out at the dark. "My mother held the ninth seat once. Last voice of the Court. She bound my own throat to keep me safe, and she never got to explain why, and I spent twelve years being furious at her for a sacrifice. If I take her seat, I take her weight too. And the weight includes the fact that the oath I swore yesterday isn't the same oath she swore thirty years ago. Mine has teeth in it. Mine lets the city bite back."

Nessa was quiet for a while. Then she said, in the flat, practical voice she used when she thought I was being stupid:

"Good. Your mother loved you so much she broke you to save you. That's love with no accountability. You're doing it better."

I laughed until my ribs hurt, and then, because she was fifteen and deserved it, I cried a little, and she pretended not to notice, and passed me the rest of the cider.

*

The convening happened at dawn, in the ruined hall beneath the palace—not because I wanted to keep meeting in a crypt, but because the dead had earned the right to watch. We'd cleaned it up as best we could. Torv had forged new brackets for the old thrones, plain iron instead of gold. Maren had grown moss and flowering vines along the cracks with a touch, softening the stone. The nine voices that lived in the walls stayed quiet, but I felt them listening, the way you feel a sleeping dog shift its weight.

Nine seats. Eight occupied.

I walked to the last one barefoot, because my mother had gone barefoot to her oaths and I figured I owed her that much imitation. The hall smelled of cold stone and cut flowers. Every seat was full behind me: Torv with his hammer, Sessily with her jaw set, Maren looking resigned, Doss smelling of lamp oil, Pell with her notes, Ivet watching me with those lie-detecting eyes, Jut with rain-sense prickling at the high windows, Old Brey radiating the specific menace of a woman who has already decided how this ends.

I stood before the ninth throne and did not sit.

"Before I take this seat," I said, and my voice carried the way swordspeech carries, filling the hall without effort, "you should know what you're getting. My true name is Oscarina Underhill. Daughter of Maerin Underhill, ninth voice of the Ninefold Court. I hid for twelve years. I watched an old woman dragged through the market and did nothing. I broke an oath to kill a dead king. I am not clean. I am not wise. I am the most dangerous thing in this room and possibly in this city, and I am asking you—all of you—to hold me to account for that for as long as I live, because the alternative is what's buried under that rubble."

I pointed at the collapsed corner of the hall, where the crown's ashes were mixed into the mortar of the repairs. We'd built it in. Let the thing that ate a kingdom hold up a floor.

Then I knelt. Not to the thrones. To the assembled seats. To the living.

"I swear to guard and not govern. To answer when called and yield when voted. To spend my gift for the city's sake and account for every spending. And if I forget—if power makes me forget—remind me. Loudly. Publicly. Without mercy."

Old Brey snorted. "And if we don't remind you?"

"Then we've all failed," I said, "and we deserve what comes."

I sat down. The stone was cold through my skirts. Nothing dramatic happened—no thunder, no voices rising, no light. Just nine people in a ruined room, tired and scared and stubborn, agreeing to try.

Except. As I settled into the seat, I felt my mother's presence in it, faint as warmth from a cup just emptied, and I would have sworn—though Ivet would call it a lie—that I heard her say, not in words but in the shape of feeling:

*Finally.*

*

Afterward, in the grey light of full morning, the nine of us climbed the long stair out of the crypt and emerged into the palace forecourt, where a crowd had gathered—hundreds of them, maybe thousands, because word travels fast in a city that spent twelve years learning to whisper. They'd heard the rite was done. They'd come to see what rose from the ground.

What they saw was nine dusty, exhausted people blinking in the sun, and a woman in the center of them who'd spent half her life invisible and was now, permanently, unbearably *seen*.

Someone started clapping. Then more. Then a lot of them, and a few cheers, and one voice near the front calling out, clear and carrying: "Long live the Ninth Voice!"

"No," I called back, before the echo could settle. "No 'long live.' No crowns, no reigns. Say it properly: *long serve*."

A beat of confusion. Then someone laughed—a real laugh, surprised and delighted—and took it up: "*Long serve!*"

And the crowd caught it, thousands of voices, the sound rolling off the palace walls and down into the streets where the markets were opening and the children were running late and the whole enormous, ordinary machinery of Vell was grinding back to life around us.

Nessa squeezed my hand. Ivet, beside me, murmured, "That was true, at least. What you said down there."

"Most of it," I admitted.

"Most of it?"

I looked out at the sea of faces, the city I'd fled and fought for and finally, cautiously, let myself belong to. "There's one thing I haven't told any of you. One thing the crown's records showed Reyne, and Reyne showed me, in the last minutes she had. Something about the ninth seat. About what my mother really did with it, all those years ago—and why the crown wanted a swordspeaker so badly in the first place."

Pell Ashk went very still. "Oscarina. What is it?"

I took a breath, and beneath my feet I felt the stones of Vell listening, waiting, the way they'd waited twelve long years for me to speak.

"There's a tenth thing under the city," I said. "And it's waking up."
