## 22. Swordspeech, Sung Plainly

The market of Vell reopened on a Tuesday, because Vell has always believed that grief is no excuse for missing a good trading day.

I had a stall again—same corner of Copperlane as before, same crooked awning that leaked when it rained and dripped when it didn't, which in this city amounts to the same weather. But the sign was new. Nessa painted it herself, tongue between her teeth, letters wobbling with the confidence of someone who has recently killed a man and discovered she's allowed to have opinions now:

**UNDERHILL CHARMWORK — HONEST MAGIC, FAIR PRICES.**

"Honest," I'd said, when she showed me. "You want me to put *honest* on a sign. In writing."

"You're the one who shattered the crown," she said. "I figure you've earned one honest thing."

The charms sold differently now. That was the strangest part—not that they worked, though they did, properly, humming with small true magic instead of the luck-shaped nothing I used to peddle—but that people knew. Twelve years I'd hidden under the word *charlatan* like it was armor, and it turns out the whole city had known, or guessed, or simply hadn't cared enough to ask, and the only person my secret was ever really kept from was me.

A woman bought a warding charm for her roof against storm-fire. A man bought a finding-charm for his brother's wedding ring, lost down a drain, and I spoke the word into it quietly—the first syllable of swordspeech, the one that means *come home*—and he walked away holding it like it might bite him and grinning all the same.

Then there was the girl.

She couldn't have been more than eight. She came to the stall three days running before she worked up the nerve to speak, and when she did, she did it the way children do important things: too loudly, in front of everyone.

"You're the swordspeaker."

"I'm Oscar Underhill," I said. "Charm-seller. Occasional hero, according to the ballads, none of which get the weather right."

"My mam says your voice could cut steel."

"Your mam exaggerates. It could cut rope. Steel takes a full breath and a bad attitude." I leaned on the counter. "What's your name?"

"Maren."

"And what do you want, Maren?"

She looked at her feet. Then at my hands. Then, with the terrible directness of the young: "The hum. I hear it. In my teeth. Mam says it'll go away if I don't listen, but it doesn't go away, it just gets louder when I'm cross."

I knew that hum. I had spent twelve years pretending mine was tinnitus.

"Show me your hands," I said.

She put them on the counter, small and chapped and trembling slightly, and beneath her skin—faint, but unmistakable, like a candle behind cloth—something answered me. Not swordspeech. Something older and softer, an earth-gift maybe, stone or seed or salt. The Court would have had a seat for it once. The Court will again, if we do this right.

"Maren," I said, "that sound in your teeth isn't going away. It's not supposed to. It's yours."

Her eyes went wide and frightened, the exact expression I must have worn at her age while my mother pressed a finger to my lips and taught me the first line of silence.

"But—" she whispered, glancing at the crowd, at the witchfinder-empty scaffold still standing at the far end of the square because the city couldn't decide whether to tear it down or keep it as a warning, "they burned people. For it."

"They burned people for lots of things. They burned people for reading. Didn't stop anyone from learning their letters, did it?" I came around the counter and crouched to her height. "Here's what I know about being afraid of your own voice, Maren. It works. It keeps you alive. And then one day you're thirty-four years old and you realize you can't remember what you sound like, and somebody has to die before you find out." I smiled, so she'd know it wasn't a threat. Mostly. "So. Do you want to learn one word? Just one. Small one. Nothing that cuts anything except silence."

She nodded so hard her braid bounced.

I took her hands. I let my gift rise to the surface where I kept it these days—not caged, not loosed, just *available*, the way you keep a knife in a kitchen—and I sang the opening syllable, the one every gifted child should learn first and the old Court never bothered teaching because they were too busy teaching heirs and heraldry.

It means *you are safe here.*

Maren's lips moved around it clumsily, and the charm-rack behind us chimed all at once, every warding-stone ringing like tiny bells, and half the market turned to look, and nobody ran, and nobody screamed, and a fishmonger two stalls down called out, "Oi, do that again, my wife loves bells."

That's it. That's the whole miracle. No crown shattered, no revenant unmade, no kingdom saved. Just a girl learning that the noise in her teeth had a name, and a city that heard magic in daylight and responded by asking for an encore.

Nessa appeared at my elbow as Maren ran home to tell her mother, glowing like a struck match. My apprentice—because that's what she was now; she'd appointed herself and I'd failed to object fast enough—watched the girl go with an expression I recognized. Wanting. Fear of wanting.

"You're going to teach loads of them, aren't you," she said. Not a question.

"I'm going to teach whoever shows up," I said. "It's less work than hiding."

"And the Court? The real one? The nine seats?" She'd been there when I swore the new oath. She'd taken a seat herself, the youngest seat in the history of the thing, and had spent two weeks pretending she wasn't terrified of it. "People keep asking when we start *ruling*."

"We don't. That's the entire point." I straightened the charm-rack. "Ruling is how the last lot ended. We guard. Somebody asks for help, we help. Nobody asks, we mind our own business and sell charms."

"That's not very dramatic."

"No," I agreed. "Thank the Nine for that."

* * *

I visited Brann's grave on a Thursday, because Thursdays were forge-days and he'd have hated me coming on a day I could be working.

They buried him up on Ash Hill, where the city puts its honored dead, which would have made him furious. Brann Odo, oath-breaker, Court-smith, father, had spent his whole life insisting he belonged nowhere near honor. He'd wanted a plain stone and no inscription and to be left alone, and instead the city gave him a marble marker and a crowd at the funeral and, eventually, because I couldn't stop myself, an inscription:

**HE FORGAVE. LATE, BUT HE FORGAVE.**

He'd have thrown something at me for that too. Worth it.

The rite had demanded his forgiveness—the traitor's forgiveness, the sixth seat's forgiveness, the man whose betrayal opened the gates—and Brann had given it with his daughter's name in his mouth and tears cutting tracks through forge-grime, and three weeks later he'd stood on the palace stairs with a smith's hammer against soldiers in crown-livery and held the line long enough for Nessa and me to climb. He didn't see the end. Sometimes I think that was mercy. Sometimes I think he earned the right to see it, and the world shortchanged him, and the least I can do is visit.

I sat on the grass beside the stone and told him about the market. About Maren and her earth-gift. About the fishmonger who liked bells. About the way the scaffold at Copperlane finally came down last week—not torn down, not kept, but *taken apart*, plank by plank, by volunteers, and the wood given to the widow Aldane to repair her roof, which felt like the kind of arithmetic Brann would have approved of.

"You were right, you know," I said. "When you refused me. When you said the Court's last act doomed your daughter and you'd not lift a hammer for its ghost. You were right about everything except the ending." I picked at the grass. "We weren't clean. We weren't good. Half of us were tyrants with better manners than the king, and the sixth seat proved the rot went all the way down. But it turns out a thing can be rotten and still worth rebuilding. Like a house. Like a city. Like me."

The wind moved through the hillside graves. It didn't answer. Dead men rarely do, whatever the stones claim. But I'd gotten in the habit of talking to him anyway, and I found that saying things out loud—even to grass—was a discipline I never intended to give up. Twelve years of silence teaches you the value of wasting words.

On my way down the hill I stopped at the second grave. Smaller stone. Newer.

**ILSA REYNE — WITCHFINDER CAPTAIN — SHE TURNED AT THE THRESHOLD**

No dates. Nobody knew them all, and Reyne would have lied about at least one anyway.

I'd hated her for most of a year, and understood her for about four minutes, and mourned her ever since, and I still haven't sorted out what order those are supposed to go in. She'd hunted me through the undercity with a patience I almost admired, offered me the crown's bargain with a sincerity I couldn't dismiss, and let me walk—one heartbeat too long, one heartbeat that cost her everything, because somewhere in that heartbeat she'd already decided. Then she'd led the witchfinders straight to the Court hall, and turned on them at the threshold, and bought us the hour we needed with the only coin she had left.

Dying, she'd grabbed my wrist and told me the truth that broke the revenant loose: *the king's original oath, still unspoken.* The binding-word the corpse-king had swallowed twelve years ago, the vow that anchored the crown to its meat. She'd known for weeks. She'd sat in the throne room wearing the crown's attention like a collar and dug through the archives anyway, planning her defection one stolen document at a time, telling herself she was still in control right up until the moment she chose to lose everything instead.

"She thought she was the reasonable one," I told her grave, the way I'd told Brann's. "And the worst part is, she nearly was. There's a version of this story where you and I sit down across a table and work it out like civilized women, and nobody burns, and nobody climbs those stairs." I crouched and touched the cold marble. "But you chose the version where you burn your own ladder. I don't know if that makes you the best of us or the worst. I think you were both. I think most people are."

The city below went about its afternoon. Somewhere a hammer rang—an actual smith, a living one, taking up work that had been waiting.

* * *

My mother's grave was hardest, so I saved it for last, and then, being me, I circled it twice before I could make my feet stop moving.

They'd laid her in the quiet yard behind the Temple of the Ninefold, among the other dead of the purge—unnamed mostly, marked with tokens instead of names, because names had been dangerous back then. But hers had a name now. I'd carved it myself, badly, with a mason's chisel and no talent whatsoever:

**MAERWEN UNDERHILL — VOICE OF THE COURT — SHE BOUGHT HER DAUGHTER TWELVE YEARS**

Twelve years. The exact span of the false king's reign. The exact span of my silence. I used to think that was coincidence. I don't anymore. I think my mother knew precisely how much time she was purchasing and paid the price without haggling.

The memory-token had shown me the night of the Last Oath. I'd relived it in the fourth vault with Nessa's hand on my shoulder, watching my younger self watch my mother kneel in candle-smoke and speak the binding into my throat: *silence until the danger passes, silence until she chooses, silence sworn to service rather than fear.* Except oaths decay when the swearer dies, and hers had curdled in the dark, and what was meant as shelter became a leash the crown could pull—which is exactly what the revenant tried to do, in the end, in the throne room, yanking my mother's dying vow like a rope to haul me to its side.

It almost worked. Let the record show. There was a moment—I've told the Court this part honestly, because an oath of service doesn't leave room for flattering yourself—when the eighth seat glowed warm as a hearth and the ninth seat glowed warmer, and the thing wearing the dead king said *rule beside me, restore the Court, make magic safe forever by making it lawless*, and some starved animal in my chest leaned toward it like a plant toward light. Everything I'd lost, offered back with interest. Belonging, weaponized.

What saved me wasn't courage. It was the memory of my mother's face while she bound me—terrified, resolute, *loving*—and the understanding that she'd never once offered me safety at the price of myself. Sheltered, yes. Hidden, yes. But always, underneath the silence, she'd been teaching me the words anyway. Every lullaby was a lesson. Every hushed rhyme was swordspeech in disguise. She muzzled me and armed me in the same breath, trusting me to know the difference when it mattered.

So I broke her oath. The final act of obedience was disobedience. I stood in the throne room with the revenant's shadow pooling around my boots and I spoke my true name into air that hadn't heard it in twelve years:

*"Oscarina Underhill. Swordspeaker. Daughter of the Court's last voice."*

And the binding tore free—from my throat and from the dead king's both, one sentence doing the work of a revolution—and the rest you know, or think you know, depending on which ballad singer you've been drinking with.

I knelt at her grave now and put my palm flat on the earth.

"Hi, Mam."

The wind came through the temple yard and stirred the late-blooming jasmine someone had planted along the wall. Nessa, probably. That girl cannot leave a memorial unflowered.

"I rewrote the oath," I said. "You'd want to hear it. You'd probably want to edit it—you never met a clause you couldn't improve—but it's done, it's sworn, nine witnesses and everything." I took a breath, and gave her the last line, the one that replaced *silence*, the one I say every morning now like brushing my teeth:

"*Not silence, but service. Not hiding, but keeping. My voice for the city that feared it, my name for the ones who had none.*"

Something in the yard shifted—not dramatically, no ghosts, no voices in the stones; the Court's dead had finished their business and gone wherever dead courtiers go—but the jasmine moved once against the wind, and I choose to believe that was her, and I choose to believe she approved, and if that's sentimentality then I've earned a little.

I stayed until the light went gold. Then I stood, and brushed off my knees, and did the thing that still surprises me every single time: I walked out of the graveyard into the open street, in daylight, past people who looked at me and *knew my name*, and I wasn't afraid.

* * *

The market was closing when I got back—awnings folding, stalls creaking shut, the fishmonger arguing cheerfully with no one about bell-enthusiasm—and Nessa was waiting at our corner with her arms crossed and the particular posture of someone bearing news.

"There's a delegation," she said. "From Casterhale. Arrived an hour ago. Six of them, riding crown-road, flying no colors."

"Casterhale. The eastern provinces. What do they want?"

"That's the thing." She handed me a folded note, sealed with plain wax, unsigned. "They asked for you by name. Your *full* name. Oscarina. Which exactly four living people know, and none of them live in Casterhale."

I read the note twice. It was short. It was polite. It was terrifying in the way that only very polite things can be.

*To the Swordspeaker of Vell: the Ninefold Court was never only yours. Three seats east of the mountains still remember their oaths. We would speak of what comes next—before others, less patient, arrive to decide it for you.*

"Well," I said.

"Well," Nessa agreed.

I looked down Copperlane as the lamps came on, one by one, the honest light of honest flames in a city that had stopped flinching from fire. Behind me, our stall. Our crooked awning. Our sign with its wobbly, confident letters. Ahead of me, six riders with no colors and a sentence I hadn't written.

Twelve years ago, a woman bought her daughter's life with silence. Tonight, apparently, the bill for the rest of the world had come due.

"Nessa," I said, rolling the note closed, "how do you feel about a trip east?"
