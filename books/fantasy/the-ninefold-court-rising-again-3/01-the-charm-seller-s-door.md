## 1. The Charm Seller's Door

The trick to surviving Vell was to be forgettable in a way that felt like kindness. Tamsin Villalobos had learned it young, and she practiced it daily, the way other people practiced prayers.

She opened her shop at the same hour every morning, rain or frost or the gray drizzle that Vell called summer. She hung her sign—*CHARMS, MENDED, SMALL GOODS*—at the same angle. She sold luck-knots braided from river grass, buttons sewn back on with thread charmed only by patience, and little cloth stars stuffed with dried lavender for children who couldn't sleep. She never sold anything that worked too well.

That was the whole art of it. A charm that actually *worked* got noticed. A charm that mostly didn't got you called a sweet old fraud by soldiers who might otherwise have asked harder questions.

Tamsin was not old. But she dressed old, moved old, kept her brown hair pinned under a gray scarf and her eyes down and her voice pitched somewhere between apology and boredom. Fifteen years of it. She could count on one hand the number of times she'd laughed out loud since her mother burned, and she'd counted them carefully, because laughter was the kind of thing the Ninefold Court's enforcers noticed—a woman who laughed had something loose in her, something unguarded, and unguarded things were what the sweeps came for.

Magic-bearers died in Vell. That wasn't a law so much as weather. Everyone knew it the way they knew winter came: some years it took your neighbor, some years it passed over, but you built your life around it all the same.

So Tamsin sold knots that were just knots, and she was careful even with herself. When she felt the gift stir—that warm pressure behind her breastbone, like a second heartbeat with opinions—she pressed it down. She hadn't spoken a true word of beast-speech in fifteen years. She'd buried it so deep she sometimes managed to believe there was nothing there at all.

Almost believe it.

* * *

The cats were the problem. The cats, and once, mortifyingly, a mule.

There was a one-eared tom who haunted the alley behind her shop, gray as dishwater and missing half his dignity along with his ear. He sat on her back step most evenings while she dumped wash water, and he watched her with the flat gold patience of his kind.

She knew she shouldn't. She knew exactly how it would look if anyone saw—a charm-seller crouched in an alley, lips moving at a cat. People had been reported for less. People had been burned for less, though usually they'd done more.

But some evenings, when the light went amber and the street emptied and the watchmen's boots faded toward the tavern district, she let herself have three seconds of it. Three seconds, no more.

*"Cold night,"* she'd say—not aloud, not with her mouth, but in the way that cats understood, a pressure of meaning shaped like words.

And the tom would blink slowly, which among cats is a whole conversation, and answer: *"Warm stone. Later."*

That was all. That was everything. It cost her nothing and it nearly killed her every time, because afterward she'd stand in the dark with her hands shaking, feeling the buried thing turn over under its stones like something dreaming, and she'd think: *one day this will get me ashed, Villalobos, and you'll deserve it.*

The mule had been worse. A carter's mule, stuck in the mud outside the grain gate last autumn, whipping itself into a panic while soldiers shouted and men pulled ropes uselessly. Tamsin had been three streets away and had felt the animal's terror arrive before any sound did—a hot spike of it, right through the wall she'd built.

She'd walked past. She told herself she walked past. But somewhere between one step and the next, without deciding anything, she had sent out one small word—one word, calm, green-colored, the shape of *steady*—and the mule had stopped screaming so fast the carter crossed himself.

Nobody looked at Tamsin. Nobody ever looked at Tamsin; that was the entire architecture of her life. She had gone home and been sick into her basin and lain awake until dawn listening for boots on her stairs.

They never came. She'd gotten away with one word.

She never used two again.

* * *

Market days were the worst, because market days meant crowds, and crowds meant animals, and animals meant noise—the real noise, underneath the human one, the constant low murmur of beasts thinking their small thoughts. Pigeons muttering about crumbs. Dogs narrating their grievances. Horses doing sums about weight and distance and whether the man on their back deserved them.

Fifteen years ago, before her mother burned, that murmur had been as natural to Tamsin as hearing. Now she kept it walled off behind iron discipline, and on market days the wall strained, and she went home with a headache like a nail driven behind her eyes, and she considered that the fair price of staying alive.

On this particular market day—the last ordinary day, though of course she didn't know it yet—a woman bought a lavender star for her son and haggled Tamsin down two coppers. A soldier bought a luck-knot without smiling, which was normal; soldiers never smiled at luck. And a gull landed on her windowsill and stared at her for a long time with an expression she chose not to interpret.

*"Nothing,"* she told it firmly, inside the wall. *"I'm nothing. Move along."*

The gull left. Gulls always did. It was the leaving that kept her alive, and she knew that, and she hated it a little more each year, the way you hate a medicine that works.

* * *

Night came down on Vell the way it always did—early, wet, smelling of coal smoke and the canal. Tamsin banked her fire, counted her till out of habit (eleven coppers, two bits of silver, a button someone had paid with by mistake), and was reaching for the candle to carry upstairs when the knock came.

Three knocks. Soft ones.

Her hand stopped above the candle.

Nobody knocked at night in Vell. Night knocks belonged to the enforcers, and enforcers didn't knock softly—they knocked the way avalanches arrived, and usually they didn't bother knocking at all. A soft knock at night was a desperate person, and desperate people were dangerous, and Tamsin had spent fifteen years perfecting the art of being nobody's rescue.

She stood very still and listened with the part of herself she kept locked.

Nothing. No heartbeat of dog or horse nearby, no watchman breathing around the corner. Just the rain starting up against the shutters, and beyond the door, small and quick—

A heart. Small, fast, frightened. Human.

"Shop's closed," she called, in her bored old-woman voice. "Come back at dawn."

Silence. Then the knock again, softer, almost apologetic.

Tamsin closed her eyes. Every sensible instinct she owned lined up in a row and said *no*, in fifteen different voices, in her mother's voice loudest of all—*Tamsin, love, we don't see things, we don't hear things, we are gray as pigeons and twice as dull.*

She opened the door anyway.

The child stood in the rain with no coat and no shoes, maybe eight years old, maybe younger—it was hard to tell, because the face was wrong. Not damaged-wrong. *Old*-wrong. Children's faces are all future; this one had already spent its future somewhere else and come back. The eyes especially. Gray eyes, deep-set, patient in a way no living child is patient, looking at Tamsin the way you look at a place you've been before.

"You can't be here," Tamsin said quietly. "Whatever it is, whoever sent you—go home."

The child didn't move. Rain ran off its chin. It raised one small fist and held it out, and only then did Tamsin see what it clutched, had clearly been clutching for hours, knuckles white and rain-swollen around it.

A scrap of cloth. Fine linen, gone gray with road dirt, hem torn where it had been ripped rather than cut.

Embroidered across it, in thread that had once been silver:

A nine-branched tree, rooted in a crown.

Tamsin's body understood before her mind did. Her knees went loose. Her mouth filled with the taste of copper and woodsmoke, fifteen years old, the taste of the square where they'd made her mother kneel on a platform with straw piled around her feet—and stitched into the sleeve of her mother's good dress, hidden under the cuff where only family would know to look, the same nine-branched tree. The same crown.

The sigil of the Old Court. Of the true line. Of the king everyone said was dead, or worse than dead, and whose name you did not say indoors or out.

"Where," Tamsin heard herself say, "did you get that."

The child looked up at her through the rain. Its mouth opened.

And no sound came out at all.

Not silence like refusal—silence like a room with the furniture taken out. The child tried again, throat working, and produced nothing, and its ancient gray eyes filled with a frustration so vast and so weary that it looked, for one horrible moment, like grief wearing a small borrowed body.

It held the scrap of cloth higher, offering it, insisting.

Tamsin should have shut the door. She knew that the way she knew fire burns. Somewhere down the street a dog started barking—no, not barking, she realized with a cold drop through her stomach, not barking at all—the dog had gone silent mid-bark, and so had every other sound of animal life on her street, as if something had drawn a line through the night and everything with fur or feathers on the far side of it was holding its breath.

Waiting.

The child stood in the rain, mute, holding the sigil Tamsin had last seen going up in smoke with her mother's sleeves, and its old gray eyes said, as plainly as any words she'd refused to hear in fifteen years:

*I know what you are.*

Tamsin Villalobos, who had survived by being nobody, stepped back from her own doorway and heard herself say the fatal, unforgivable, inevitable thing:

"Get inside before someone sees you."

The child slipped past her into the dark of the shop, small and rain-cold and silent as a held breath—and behind them both, out in the wet street, every cat within three alleys began to sing at once.
