## 2. A Language Older Than Kingdoms

The child did not sleep.

Tamsin knew this because she didn't either. She lay on her cot behind the bead curtain, listening to the small body breathing on her floor pallet—too evenly, too deliberately, the breathing of someone pretending to sleep the way she had pretended to be ordinary for fifteen years. Around midnight the rhythm changed. By the time the bells of Saint Vess rang the hour, she could hear the child's eyes being open in the dark.

She got up. She lit no lamp. She sat on the edge of her cot and said, quietly, "You can stop pretending. I invented pretending."

The silence from the floor was a different kind of silence then. Not performance. Calculation.

"Here's what happens," Tamsin went on, keeping her voice at the level of a woman talking to herself, which was safe, which was invisible. "In the morning, you eat bread I'll give you because it's cruel to send anyone out hungry, even trouble. Especially trouble. Then you go back to whatever door you came from, and I go back to selling luck-knots to people who don't believe in luck, and neither of us remembers the other. That's the arrangement. That's the only arrangement there is."

The child sat up. In the gray wash of light through the shutters, their face was still wrong—still old, still arranged like something that had been folded and refolded so many times the creases had become permanent.

"You have your mother's hands," the child said.

Tamsin stopped moving.

Not the words themselves—the voice. The voice was not a child's voice. It was layered, faintly, the way river water over stone is two things at once: a boy's pitch carrying something deeper beneath it, something with weight and years and a strange formal music to it, as though the child were pronouncing each word for the first time and remembering how.

"Your mother," the child repeated, patiently, as if Tamsin were the slow one. "She tied knots too. But hers sang."

"My mother is dead." Fifteen years, and the sentence still came out of her like a splinter leaving flesh.

"I know," the child said. "I was there."

Tamsin stood up so fast the cot frame knocked the wall. She was across the room before she decided to move, crouched down, gripping the child's shoulders—not hard, she made herself not hard—and searching the old eyes for the trick, the con, the soldiers' trick, the priest's trick, any of the hundred ways the powerful had of fishing for people like her with bait shaped like memory.

"Who told you about my mother?" Her whisper shook. She hated that it shook. "Who sent you? Was it a man with gray gloves? Did he give you the cloth?"

"No one sent me." The child tilted their head, considering, the way a bird considers. "I came. I don't know all of why. There was a garden. There was a woman laughing, and the laugh sounded like—" The child pressed a hand flat against Tamsin's sternum, gentle as a falling leaf. "It sounded like here."

Tamsin stepped back. Her heart was doing something stupid and loud.

"Sleep," she said. "Eat in the morning. Then go."

But she did not sleep, and when the gray hour came she found herself in the kitchen cutting bread she'd sworn not to cut, and the child sat at her table with the scrap of embroidered cloth smoothed flat between their hands like an altar cloth, watching her with those eyes, and Tamsin understood with cold clarity that she was already lost, because she was buttering the bread thick, the way you do for someone you intend to keep.

* * *

She should have asked questions. She asked one instead, the worst one.

"What's your name?"

The child chewed slowly, swallowed, set down the bread with terrible care. And looked at her the way people look out windows during executions—as if the answer were happening somewhere far away and they wished they weren't watching.

"They took it," the child said.

"Took your name."

"Took everything with a shape. The name was a shape. The memories are shapes." The child's small fingers curled around the edge of the table. "Most of me is gone. What's left is—" The brow furrowed; the child reached inside themselves visibly, groping for a word, and Tamsin watched a five-year-old face perform the labor of a drowning man. "What's left is the part he hid."

"He," Tamsin said. Her mouth had gone dry. "He who?"

And the child opened their mouth and spoke.

One word.

Tamsin never learned afterward how to write it down. The letters they used for Old Vellish in the archive-books couldn't hold it; the sound wasn't built of air the way other sounds were. It began somewhere below hearing and rose through the bones of the world, and it meant—or Tamsin believed it meant, in the way you believe things your blood tells you before your mind agrees—*king*. Or perhaps *the crowned one*. Or perhaps simply *him*, the way a dog means its whole sky when it says its master's name.

Every animal within three streets went still.

Tamsin felt it happen. That was the horror of it—that she *felt* it, felt the word pass through her shop and out into Vell like a stone dropped into still water, and felt the ripples come back: the sparrows under her eaves freezing mid-preen, the miller's dog two doors down stopping its endless barking mid-bark so abruptly the silence had edges, the rats in her walls—her constant, chattering neighbors—going quiet as graves. Three streets of living things holding their breath at once, because a child at a kitchen table had said a word older than the walls they lived behind.

Then, slowly, breath returned. The sparrows resumed. Somewhere a gull screamed once, confused, and stopped.

The child looked up at her, and their face crumpled—not old anymore, suddenly, but exactly as young as it looked, frightened and wet-eyed.

"I'm sorry," they whispered, in a normal child's voice now, the deep layer gone. "I didn't mean to say it out loud. It just—when you asked—he pushed up. He wants to be remembered. He's so tired of being forgotten, and he's *so scared*, and I'm the only place left where he fits."

Tamsin sat down across from the child very slowly, because her legs had informed her they were done negotiating.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of keeping her head down and her gift buried and her ears closed, of letting the cats' chatter wash past her like weather, of being nobody, and in one night a child had walked through her door wearing her mother's sigil and speaking the forbidden tongue like a first language, and every animal in three streets had knelt.

She thought about the proclamation boards. *Magic is sedition. Sedition is ash.* She thought about the gray-gloved men who came through the lower quarters twice a year, smiling, counting. She thought about her mother's feet, bare on the scaffold, because they'd taken her shoes so she'd feel the winter.

"Get your coat," Tamsin heard herself say.

The child blinked. "You're sending me away?"

"No." She was already pulling the bolt on her front door, checking the street, empty, gray, safe for eleven more seconds. "I'm getting us off this street before every hawk and hound in Vell finishes deciding where that word came from. Move."

* * *

They ended up at the tannery pond on Corver Street, which was ugly, reeking, and overlooked by nothing—which was why Tamsin liked it. She bought chestnuts from a cart with coins she couldn't spare and sat the child on the low wall and fed them the way you feed a fire you've decided to let burn.

"Say it again," she said. "Slowly. Just to me. No one else is close enough to hear."

The child hesitated, then spoke the word again, softer, and this time Tamsin listened *underneath* it, the way she'd been taught before she was seven, the way she'd sworn never to do again. And underneath the sound she found the rest: not just *king*, but a whole compressed knot of meaning, a memory folded so tight it had become a syllable. A coronation. Oaths spoken in a ring of standing stones. Something entering through the vow like water through a cracked seal. A crown whispering. A king going quiet from the inside out, and one piece of him—one small screaming piece—being carried out by loyal hands and hidden, hidden in the only vessel small and unremarkable enough to pass unnoticed through a city full of eyes.

A child.

"Oh," Tamsin said aloud. It came out very small. "You're not cursed. You're not even really—you're *remembering*. You're a piece of King Aldric that somebody smuggled out of the palace."

The child nodded, and then their face did the folding thing again, and when they spoke it was half them and half the deep voice, both exhausted.

"He hid me in himself first. Inside his own forgetting. The Court eats memories, so he fed me to himself like poison in meat, and then the rebels cut me loose and ran." The child looked down at the chestnut in their palm. "I don't remember their faces. I remember running. I remember a door that smelled like knots and dried lavender, and my feet knowing it before I did."

Tamsin's throat hurt. Lavender. Her mother's trick, kept alive in Tamsin's own bundles without her ever asking herself why.

"Why my door?" she asked, though some part of her already knew, some part that had been flinching since the moment she saw the embroidered sigil, since she'd seen the child's face in the lamplight and felt the sick lurch of looking at a reflection slightly wrong.

The child looked at her for a long time. Then they reached up and touched her cheek, once, with flour-dusted fingers.

"You have her face," the child said simply. "The queen's. The one he loved. The Court took her out of him too, but they missed one laugh, and the laugh has your shape." A pause. "I followed the shape."

Above the tannery pond, every gull on the roofline turned its head toward them at once—a dozen white faces, perfectly silent, perfectly attentive—and Tamsin's skin crawled with the knowledge of what that meant. The word was still traveling. Things with feathers and things with teeth were still passing it along, and not all of them were loyal to children.

She stood, pulled the child off the wall, and held their small cold hand harder than gentleness strictly required.

"New rule," she said. "From now on, when he wants to speak, you tell me first. We pick where and we pick when, and until I figure out what in nine hells we're going to do with a king in a coat three sizes too big—you let me do the talking."

The child nodded solemnly. Then, at the end of Corver Street, where the fog was beginning to lift off the gutters, they stopped dead and pointed with the whole arm, the way children point, at a line of soldiers moving through the morning market—not patrolling, not smiling, but going stall to stall, showing something to the vendors.

Showing a drawing.

Even at this distance, Tamsin could see what the drawing was of.

It was a child.
