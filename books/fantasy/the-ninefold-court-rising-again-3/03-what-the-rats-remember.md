## 3. What the Rats Remember

Tamsin had not spoken to the rats in fifteen years.

She thought about them the way a lapsed priest thinks about prayer—knowing the words were still in her, coiled somewhere beneath the tongue, patient as rot. Every morning she passed them on her way to open the shop. They lived under the tannery steps and along the drainage cut behind the fishmonger's row, fat and quick and utterly indifferent to her, because she had made herself indifferent to them first. That was the trick of it. A gift unused goes quiet, but it never dies. It waits. It learns to be ashamed.

Ember was asleep on the floor pallet when Tamsin knelt at the back door with a heel of bread broken into crumbs.

She hadn't decided anything. That was what she told herself. She was only feeding the birds, like any woman might. Only scattering crumbs for the gulls that wheeled over Vell's rooftops in their gray hundreds, screaming their grievances at the dawn. Anyone could do this. Anyone could crouch at her own back step and say—

The word caught in her throat like a fishbone.

She said it anyway. One word of the Old Tongue, small as a coin dropped down a well: *come.*

The silence that followed was worse than any sound.

Every gull above the lower quarter stopped screaming at once. Tamsin felt the stillness spread outward from her door like oil on water—the pigeons freezing on the chimney pots, the sparrows going tight and bright-eyed in the ivy, the cats along Fishergate lifting their heads mid-step. Three streets of animals, listening. Fifteen years of careful gray life, undone by one syllable.

A gull landed on her railing. Its eyes were wet-black and ancient, and behind its ancientness Tamsin saw something she had forgotten existed: attention. The whole blunt animal weight of it, turned toward her like a lamp.

*You speak,* the gull said, and its voice in her mind was salt and gravel and the memory of drowned things. *After so long. You speak, and you are afraid.*

"I'm not—" she began, out loud, then caught herself and shaped the answer the old way, thought pressed into meaning without words. *I'm not afraid.*

*Liar,* said the gull, comfortably. *But you feed us. So we will tell you what you ask.*

Behind her, inside the shop, Ember stirred. Tamsin didn't turn around. She kept her eyes on the gull and asked the only question that mattered, the one that had been sitting in her chest since the knock at her door two nights ago:

*Is there anything hunting a child who speaks the Old Tongue?*

The gull's head tilted. Around the street, the other birds shifted, feathers rustling, passing the question along the way wind passes through wheat. When the answer came, it came not from one voice but from many layered together—gull and pigeon and sparrow, even the rats emerging now from the drainage cut, whiskered faces lifted toward her step.

*Yes.* A pause, the way there is always a pause before bad news. *There is a thing that hunts voices. It wears men. It has worn them for years beyond our counting. When a voice speaks the Old Tongue anywhere in Vell, the thing turns all its worn men toward the speaking, the way a body turns toward a wound.*

*How does it hear?* Tamsin asked.

*Rats hear everything,* said the oldest rat, climbing onto the step itself, unafraid, which should have frightened her more than the answer did. *We have heard the crown whisper through nine mouths at once. We have heard it call itself a court. And we have heard its soldiers in the barracks, these three nights past, given new orders: sweep the lower quarters. Door to door. They search for a child.*

Tamsin's hands went cold around the bread.

*When?*

*Tonight,* said the rat. *Perhaps tomorrow. The soldiers do not know exactly what they seek. Their captains do. The captains carry glass vials and write down every child in the quarter, tallied against some list that makes them sweat.*

The gulls screamed again—all at once, resuming their ordinary noise—and Tamsin understood that they had released her, that the conversation was over, that she had gotten what she'd paid for. She sat back on her heels in the doorway and listened to Ember breathing inside, slow and small, and felt fifteen years of discipline crumble like wet paper.

She had fed the child. She had let the child sleep under her roof. And tonight soldiers with glass vials would come up Fishergate counting children against a list.

"Right," she said aloud, to no one, in plain trade-tongue. "Right."

***

She named the child over breakfast, because you cannot keep calling someone *the child* in your own house and expect them to stay human.

"Ember," Tamsin said, setting down the bowl of barley porridge. "Because you're small and hot and you'll burn my whole life down if I don't watch you every moment."

Ember looked at the bowl. Looked at her. And then—there was no other word for it—the child *smiled*, briefly, crookedly, like someone trying on an expression remembered secondhand.

They ate in silence for a while. Then Ember set down the spoon, laid one small hand flat on the table, and spoke—not the Old Tongue this time, but fragments of trade-tongue, halting, assembled wrong, the grammar of someone translating from a deeper language:

"Garden. There was—a garden. White flowers." The child frowned, digging. "A woman laughing. Not—" Ember touched their own throat, frustrated. "Not me-laughing. Her-laughing. I remember her laughing and I don't know her name."

Tamsin went very still. "Where was the garden?"

Ember shook their head. Then, slowly, they raised one hand and closed it in the air above the table—an unconscious gesture, grasping something that wasn't there—and whispered a single Old Tongue word that Tamsin felt in her teeth before she translated it.

*Crown.*

"And?" Tamsin kept her voice level, the way she kept it level with customers who haggled. "What about the crown?"

Ember's eyes came up, old beyond years, and the child said, in trade-tongue, with terrible clarity:

"The crown whispers. All night. Nine voices. It never stops whispering." A pause. "He cannot sleep. The king cannot sleep. I remember him not sleeping."

The porridge turned to ash in Tamsin's mouth. King Aldric had been dead—or gone, or enthralled, depending which tavern rumor you believed—for longer than Ember had been alive. But the child spoke of *him* the way you speak of a headache: present, constant, close. As if the child were carrying a piece of the man around like a stone in the chest.

"You're not a child," Tamsin said quietly. "Are you. You're something else wearing a child."

Ember considered this with grave seriousness. Then shook their head, and touched their chest, and offered the closest thing to an explanation the Old Tongue could give in small hands:

"A piece. Of him. Hidden." The child's brow furrowed, searching for words. "The crown ate most. This piece ran away. Ran until it found—" and here Ember stopped, and looked at Tamsin's face for a long moment with an expression she couldn't read, "—a door."

"I'm not a door."

Ember shrugged, the shrug of someone who has seen empires and is not impressed by denials.

Outside, boots were already in the street.

***

Tamsin heard them before she saw them—she had always heard things early, another inheritance from her mother she'd spent a lifetime pretending not to have. Boots, many, moving up Fishergate in pairs. Not marching. *Sweeping.* Stopping at doors. Knocking with the flat unhurried rhythm of men who would knock whether you answered or not.

She moved through the shop killing light, shuttering windows, heart going hard. Ember stood in the middle of the floor, calm as furniture.

"We can't be here," Tamsin whispered. "Do you understand me? If they find you—"

Ember pointed at the floorboards. Then, when Tamsin didn't follow, pointed again, more insistently, and said one Old Tongue word: *below.*

"There is no below. This is a shop. Below is a cellar with pickles in it."

Ember walked to the corner where the floorboards had rotted soft two winters ago, pried one up with fingers far too strong, and looked at Tamsin expectantly. Beneath: darkness, older stone, the smell of Vell's buried bones—because everything in this city was built on something older, and everyone knew it and nobody said it.

And in the darkness below, Tamsin heard—felt—small voices stirring. Rats. Dozens of them. Awake, attentive, waiting.

*We heard you speak this morning,* they said, in their many-threaded whisper. *Come down. We have watched the soldiers' routes since midnight. We can keep her safe. Keep them safe. If you ask.*

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of harmless charms and luck-knots and trading small words with street cats when no one watched, and now the rats were offering her sanctuary in the bones of the city, and soldiers were four doors away, and a fragment of a devoured king was standing in her shop holding up a rotted floorboard like an invitation.

Tamsin looked at Ember. Ember looked back, patient, certain, eyes full of someone else's memories.

"They're coming," Tamsin said. "Soldiers. They'll tear this place apart."

Ember nodded. Then the child smiled that borrowed smile again and said, clearly, in trade-tongue, the most frightening sentence Tamsin had ever heard:

"That's why we need the rebels. You know where they are. You dreamed about them last night—I heard you dreaming."

The knocking started next door.

Tamsin took the child's hand, lifted the floorboard wide, and stepped down into the dark with her gift singing in her blood and her mother's ashes rising in her throat—and the last thing she saw before the board closed over them was the shop door swinging open, and the shadow of a soldier filling it, and on the counter where she'd left it, the luck-knots she'd been braiding for tomorrow's market, still half-finished, still waiting.
